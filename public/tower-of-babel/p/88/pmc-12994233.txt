Reimagining community health nursing: a qualitative participatory action research study of nurse-led primary healthcare innovation in rural Saudi Arabia
Authors: Faihan F. Alshaibany, Bader M. Almutairy, Waleed M. Alshehri, Abdulaziz M. Alodhailah, Thurayya Eid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural primary healthcare (PHC) systems face persistent challenges related to access, continuity, and responsiveness, particularly in geographically dispersed communities. Nurses constitute the backbone of rural PHC delivery in Saudi Arabia, yet their leadership role in driving locally grounded innovation remains underexplored.AimThis study aimed to examine how nurses lead and sustain primary healthcare innovation in rural Saudi Arabia using a participatory action research (PAR) approach.MethodsA qualitative PAR design was employed with 12 registered nurses working in rural PHC centers. The study unfolded across three iterative cycles of planning, action, observation, and reflection. Data were generated through focus group discussions, in-depth interviews, reflective field notes, and documentation of implemented actions. Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, with participants actively involved in interpretation and refinement of findings.ResultsFour interconnected themes were identified: (1) leading from the frontline through locally grounded innovation; (2) co-creating solutions with patients and communities; (3) working within rural constraints through adaptive practices; and (4) organizational enablement as a condition for sustainability. During PAR Cycles 2 and 3, nurses implemented and refined ten practice innovations targeting continuity of care, patient engagement, workflow efficiency, documentation burden, and team learning. Sustainability was strongest when innovations were selective, simple, and embedded into routine workflows and protocols.ConclusionNurses in rural Saudi Arabia act as key agents of primary healthcare innovation through incremental, relationship-centered changes grounded in everyday practice. Participatory action research offers a robust framework for strengthening nurse leadership, aligning service improvement with community realities, and supporting sustainable rural PHC transformation. Policy and organizational strategies should explicitly enable nurse-led, participatory innovation to advance equitable community health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04510-5.

Introduction

Primary healthcare (PHC) is widely recognized as the cornerstone of effective, equitable, and sustainable health systems, particularly in rural and underserved settings. Globally, strong PHC systems are associated with improved population health outcomes, reduced health inequities, and more efficient use of healthcare resources [1, 2]. However, rural communities continue to face persistent challenges in accessing comprehensive and responsive primary care, including workforce shortages, limited infrastructure, geographical isolation, and sociocultural barriers that constrain service utilization [3]. These challenges are especially pronounced in rapidly transforming health systems, where rural populations risk being left behind amid modernization and technological advancement [4].

Health system reform in Saudi Arabia has accelerated under Vision 2030, positioning primary healthcare (PHC) as the central platform for improving population health, strengthening preventive services, and reducing dependence on hospital-based care. Recent national initiatives, including healthcare clustering and the accelerated transformation programme, have emphasized community-oriented services and quality improvement within PHC settings. Empirical studies demonstrate ongoing efforts to assess community health needs and redesign service delivery to enhance responsiveness and continuity of care. Despite these advances, rural regions continue to experience variability in service accessibility and engagement, indicating the importance of locally adaptive approaches that complement national reforms [5]. Despite substantial investments in healthcare infrastructure and workforce development, rural and remote regions continue to experience disparities in service availability, continuity of care, and community engagement [6]. Cultural norms, population dispersion, and contextual differences between urban and rural settings further complicate the implementation of standardized PHC models [7]. Addressing these challenges requires locally grounded, context-sensitive approaches that move beyond top-down policy directives toward more participatory and adaptive forms of innovation [8].

Health system reform in Saudi Arabia has accelerated under Vision 2030, positioning primary healthcare (PHC) as the central platform for improving population health, strengthening preventive services, and reducing dependence on hospital-based care. The national healthcare clustering model and the Accelerated Transformation Programme have restructured service governance, enhanced integration across care levels, and emphasized quality improvement within PHC settings [9]. Recent empirical work assessing community health needs within Saudi PHC demonstrates ongoing efforts to redesign services to enhance accessibility, continuity, and responsiveness at the local level [10]. While these structural reforms have strengthened leadership, governance, and accountability mechanisms, rural regions continue to experience variability in service accessibility, workforce distribution, and community engagement. These contextual differences highlight the importance of locally adaptive, practice-based innovation that complements national reform agendas rather than replacing them [11]. In rural settings, nurses frequently function as frontline providers, coordinators of care, health educators, and trusted community intermediaries [12]. Their sustained presence within communities positions them uniquely to identify unmet health needs, adapt services to local realities, and foster culturally responsive care practices. However, despite their critical contributions, nurses’ leadership in driving PHC innovation, particularly in rural contexts, remains underexplored and often underrecognized in both policy discourse and empirical research [13].

Emerging international literature highlights the growing importance of nurse-led innovation in strengthening primary healthcare systems, improving access, and enhancing care quality in underserved communities [14, 15]. Nurse-led initiatives have been shown to improve chronic disease management, preventive care uptake, patient satisfaction, and health literacy, particularly when nurses are empowered to participate in decision-making and service redesign [16]. Although nurse-led innovation has been widely documented internationally, the mechanisms through which such initiatives operate within rapidly reforming healthcare systems and culturally distinct rural environments remain insufficiently examined. Understanding how national reforms translate into everyday frontline practice therefore requires context-sensitive investigation [17]. There is a clear need for contextually grounded research that captures how nurses in rural Saudi communities conceptualize, enact, and sustain innovation within primary healthcare systems [18].

International participatory and community-engaged primary care studies have shown that frontline health professionals contribute to service redesign when involved in structured reflective processes [19]. Research from Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom demonstrates that rural nurses improve continuity, navigation, and chronic disease follow-up through locally adapted micro-interventions rather than large-scale restructuring [20]. These findings suggest that innovation capacity often exists within routine practice but requires facilitative structures to become visible and sustainable.

While Saudi health clusters have strengthened structural leadership, governance integration, and quality oversight within PHC, less is known about how these reforms are enacted within everyday rural nursing practice. Understanding how frontline nurses interpret, adapt, and operationalize reform strategies at the community level requires participatory and practice-oriented inquiry. Such an approach acknowledges both the progress achieved through system transformation and the contextual challenges that shape implementation in geographically dispersed regions.

Participatory Action Research (PAR) offers a methodological framework well suited to addressing this gap. Rooted in principles of collaboration, reflexivity, and social change, PAR actively involves participants as co-researchers in identifying problems, generating knowledge, and implementing solutions [21]. Unlike traditional research approaches that position practitioners as passive subjects, PAR recognizes frontline healthcare professionals, such as nurses, as holders of experiential knowledge and agents of transformation [22]. In primary healthcare settings, PAR has been increasingly employed to support practice-based innovation, enhance professional empowerment, and promote contextually relevant change [23].

Within rural health contexts, PAR is particularly valuable because it foregrounds local voices, acknowledges structural constraints, and facilitates iterative cycles of action and reflection [24]. For nurses working in rural Saudi Arabia, PAR provides a platform to articulate challenges embedded in daily practice, negotiate institutional limitations, and co-create innovative strategies aligned with community needs and cultural values. Moreover, PAR aligns closely with contemporary calls for health system transformation that emphasize co-production, workforce engagement, and community partnership as essential drivers of sustainable improvement [25].

Despite its potential, the application of PAR to nurse-led PHC innovation in Saudi Arabia remains limited. Existing studies in the region have predominantly employed descriptive or cross-sectional designs, focusing on workforce perceptions, service utilization, or patient satisfaction, with minimal exploration of participatory processes or action-oriented change [26]. While Saudi health clusters have strengthened structural leadership and service integration, less is known about how these reforms are enacted in everyday nursing practice within rural settings. Participatory and practice-oriented inquiry is therefore needed to understand how frontline nurses adapt national strategies into locally workable care processes and identify remaining operational challenges [27].

This study seeks to address these gaps by employing Participatory Action Research to examine how nurses lead and sustain primary healthcare innovation in rural Saudi Arabia. By centering nurses’ experiential knowledge and engaging them as co-creators of change, the study aims to generate practice-relevant insights into the processes, challenges, and enabling factors underpinning community-oriented PHC innovation. In doing so, it contributes to the growing body of nursing and health services research that positions nurses not only as care providers, but as critical leaders in reimagining community health and advancing equitable primary healthcare systems.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a Participatory Action Research (PAR) design to explore and support nurse-led primary healthcare (PHC) innovation in rural Saudi Arabia. This cyclical structure enabled responsiveness to contextual realities within rural primary care settings and supported the co-creation of knowledge grounded in nurses’ practice-based expertise, service processes, and operational realities. PAR is a collaborative and action-oriented methodology that integrates systematic inquiry with collective reflection and practice change, emphasizing the active involvement of participants as co-researchers rather than passive subjects [1, 2]. The approach was selected to align with the study’s aim of understanding how nurses conceptualize, enact, and sustain innovation within their everyday practice while simultaneously fostering locally relevant improvements in community health services.

Consistent with established PAR frameworks, the study followed iterative cycles of planning, action, observation, and reflection, allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent phases of inquiry and practice development [3]. This cyclical structure enabled responsiveness to contextual realities within rural primary care settings and supported the co-creation of knowledge grounded in nurses’ practice-based knowledge and operational realities.

Study setting

The study was conducted across rural primary healthcare centers located in geographically dispersed regions of the central and southern areas of Saudi Arabia. These regions are characterized by travel distances to referral hospitals ranging from approximately 60 to 120 km and a high reliance on PHC services for preventive care and chronic disease management. The centers serve populations including older adults, individuals with diabetes and hypertension, maternal–child health clients, and families with limited access to specialist services. In these settings, daily operations are largely nurse-led, with nurses assuming multifaceted roles that include triage, chronic disease follow-up, health education, referral coordination, screening, and community outreach. Variability across centers in staffing levels, digital infrastructure, and referral coordination mechanisms provided contextual diversity for examining nurse-led innovation processes.

PHC centers in these areas are predominantly nurse-led in daily operations, with nurses assuming multifaceted clinical, educational, and coordination roles. Participants and recruitment

Participants were registered nurses working in rural PHC centers who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) a minimum of one year of experience in primary healthcare, (2) current employment in a rural or remote PHC setting, and (3) willingness to engage in participatory inquiry and reflective activities. Nurse managers and frontline nurses were both eligible, provided they were directly involved in service delivery or local practice improvement. Data adequacy was determined through thematic sufficiency, defined as repetition of interpretive patterns across multiple data sources and cycles, rather than numerical saturation alone. Representation across sites ensured variability in service organization, leadership structures, and community engagement contexts.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants with diverse levels of experience, roles, and community engagement, consistent with PAR’s emphasis on inclusivity and representation of multiple perspectives [4]. Recruitment was facilitated through coordination with PHC administrators and informational sessions held at participating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Participants collaboratively refined discussion topics, reviewed coding categories, and negotiated interpretation during reflection meetings. Disagreements were discussed until consensus was reached, and final themes were endorsed collectively. The research team facilitated analysis but did not determine themes independently.

Participants were distributed across multiple rural PHC centers within the central and southern regions of Saudi Arabia, ensuring representation of contextual variation in staffing patterns, referral structures, leadership responsiveness, and community expectations. Data adequacy was determined through thematic sufficiency rather than numerical thresholds. Repetition of core interpretive patterns was observed across interviews, focus groups, and reflective logs after approximately ten participants completed full PAR engagement cycles. Continued participation of remaining nurses enhanced depth, confirmability, and contextual representation rather than introducing substantially new analytic categories.

The PAR process unfolded across three iterative cycles over a defined study period.

All interviews and focus groups were audio-recorded with permission and transcribed verbatim. Where necessary, data were translated from Arabic into English using a forward–backward translation process to preserve meaning and cultural nuance.

Interview guide

The semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this participatory action research study by the research team, informed by the study aims and relevant literature on rural primary healthcare innovation and nursing leadership. The guide was reviewed by two qualitative research experts for clarity and content coverage, then piloted with two rural PHC nurses (not included in the study sample) to assess comprehensibility and flow; minor wording and ordering revisions were made accordingly. Consistent with PAR principles, prompts were iteratively refined across cycles to capture emerging priorities while maintaining core domains. The final English interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Researcher positionality

The research team consisted of academic nurses with experience in community health and quality improvement. None held managerial authority over participants. Reflexive journals were maintained to monitor assumptions related to leadership and innovation, and facilitation emphasized shared dialogue to minimize hierarchical influence.

Data analysis

Data analysis was iterative and concurrent with data collection, consistent with PAR methodology [6]. A reflexive thematic analysis approach was employed to identify patterns of meaning across data sources while remaining attentive to context and participant voice [7]. Analysis proceeded through familiarization with the data, initial coding, theme development, and iterative refinement through participant validation and group reflection.

Crucially, participants were involved in collective sense-making, reviewing emerging themes during reflective sessions and contributing to interpretation. This collaborative analytic process enhanced credibility and ensured that findings authentically reflected nurses’ experiences and perspectives.

Trustworthiness and rigor

Methodological rigor was ensured through strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [8]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement, triangulation of data sources, and participant validation of findings. Dependability was supported by maintaining an audit trail documenting methodological decisions and analytic processes. Reflexive journaling by the research team strengthened confirmability by making assumptions and positionality explicit. Thick description of context and participants supports transferability to similar rural PHC settings. Confirmability was strengthened through independent coding review and consensus discussion between research team members and participants during reflective meetings. Transferability was supported through detailed contextual description, while credibility was reinforced through prolonged engagement across three PAR cycles and iterative member checking.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional review board prior to study commencement. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was ensured through anonymization of transcripts and secure data storage. Given the participatory nature of the study, attention was paid to power dynamics, mutual respect, and shared ownership of knowledge throughout the research process, consistent with ethical principles underpinning PAR.

Results

Reported effects reflect participants’ observations and experiential assessments rather than measured clinical outcomes.

Characteristics of participants

A total of 15 nurses working in rural primary healthcare (PHC) centers were approached and invited to participate in the Participatory Action Research (PAR) project. Of these, 12 nurses consented and engaged across the PAR cycles, yielding a participation rate of 80%. Recruitment and data generation proceeded until data adequacy and thematic sufficiency were achieved, operationalized as (i) repetition of core meanings across interviews, focus groups, and reflective logs, and (ii) no substantively new insights emerging that altered the analytic structure. Initial thematic stability was observed after approximately 10 participants had completed the full cycle of engagement, with continued participation of the remaining nurses supporting depth, confirmability, and representation across sites.

Participants were registered nurses employed in rural PHC services within Saudi Arabia. The sample included 8 females and 4 males, with ages ranging from 25 to 49 years. Years of PHC experience ranged from 2 to 18 years, representing early-career, mid-career, and senior practitioners. Nurses held diverse functional responsibilities, including chronic disease follow-up, triage and acute presentations, immunization, maternal–child care, health education, and community outreach. All participants were actively involved in improvement activities within their clinics and were willing to engage in reflective and action-oriented inquiry consistent with PAR.

Thematic analysis

Analysis generated four overarching themes and twelve subthemes describing professional agency, community partnership, contextual adaptation, and organizational enablement of nurse-led innovation. Collectively, the findings demonstrate how nurses identify local problems, mobilize trust and relationships, navigate rural constraints, and translate small changes into sustainable practice improvement through participatory cycles of action and reflection.

At the center of the findings is “Leading from the frontline: nurses as initiators of locally grounded innovation,” capturing how nurses used lived knowledge of community needs to define priorities and drive change. Surrounding this is “Co-creating solutions with patients and communities,” reflecting participatory engagement and cultural responsiveness. Enveloping these themes is “Working within rural constraints,” which details the structural and logistical barriers shaping innovation feasibility. Framing all themes is “Organizational enablement and the conditions for sustainability,” highlighting how leadership, resources, and institutionalization determined whether innovations endured beyond the project period. Themes, subthemes, and descriptions

Leading from the frontline: nurses as initiators of locally grounded innovation

This theme captures how rural PHC nurses initiated innovation from within routine clinical work rather than through externally imposed programs. Participants described innovation as emerging from repeated exposure to unmet needs, missed appointments, delayed referrals, uncontrolled chronic conditions, and preventable deterioration. Importantly, nurses did not frame innovation as technology-driven disruption; instead, they described it as practical redesign of everyday processes to protect continuity of care and improve community outcomes.

Nurses articulated a strong sense that living “close to the realities” of rural communities gave them an advantage in recognizing what did and did not work in practice. Their descriptions reflected leadership enacted through actions, problem identification, proposing adjustments, persuading colleagues, and iterating solutions, rather than through formal titles.

Seeing gaps through everyday practice

Participants repeatedly described noticing gaps through patterns that accumulated over time, patients returning with the same uncontrolled symptoms, repeated missed follow-ups, or families unclear about care plans. These patterns acted as “signals” that current processes were not aligned with community realities.

You keep seeing the same patient coming back worse, not because they don’t care, but because the system is not fitting their life. (N2)

You keep seeing the same patient coming back worse, not because they don’t care, but because the system is not fitting their life. (N2)

In rural areas you learn quickly, if follow-up is not organized, the patient disappears until complications happen. (N6)

In rural areas you learn quickly, if follow-up is not organized, the patient disappears until complications happen. (N6)

For several nurses, recognizing these gaps created moral urgency. They described a shift from passive acceptance (“this is rural”) to proactive problem-solving (“what can we change today?”).

Reframing “Innovation” as small, doable change

Participants emphasized that rural innovation needed to be small-scale, feasible, and immediately implementable. Nurses described building change through micro-interventions: restructuring triage flow, creating reminder systems, organizing brief counseling scripts, or standardizing handover notes. These actions were perceived as more realistic than large-scale reforms that required resources beyond local control.

Innovation is not a big project here. It can be a small change that stops a problem from repeating. (N9)

Innovation is not a big project here. It can be a small change that stops a problem from repeating. (N9)

We don’t have everything, so we innovate with what we have, simplify, organize, and follow up. (N10)

We don’t have everything, so we innovate with what we have, simplify, organize, and follow up. (N10)

This reframing reduced intimidation and increased participation across the team. Nurses reported that once innovation was defined as “small improvements,” colleagues became more willing to engage.

Professional agency and accountability

Across accounts, nurses positioned themselves as accountable for community outcomes, often linking their professional identity to advocacy and continuity. They described an internal sense of responsibility to “close the gaps” created by distance, referral delays, and limited services.

If we don’t lead here, who will? We are the ones seeing the community every day. (N5)

If we don’t lead here, who will? We are the ones seeing the community every day. (N5)

Sometimes the patient’s outcome depends on whether the nurse follows up, not on the prescription. (N8)

Sometimes the patient’s outcome depends on whether the nurse follows up, not on the prescription. (N8)

This agency was strengthened during PAR cycles, as nurses saw that their actions led to visible improvements (e.g., fewer missed follow-ups, clearer patient understanding, smoother clinic flow), reinforcing confidence to lead further changes.

Co-creating solutions with patients and communities

This theme reflects nurses’ descriptions of participation not as a research activity but as a practical necessity for successful rural PHC innovation. Nurses highlighted that interventions fail when they assume urban access, individual decision-making, or health literacy patterns that do not reflect rural contexts. As a result, nurses described innovation as requiring trust-building, cultural tailoring, and shared problem-solving with patients and families.

Building trust as an innovation resource

Trust was repeatedly framed as the foundation for successful education, follow-up, and adherence. Participants described trust not as abstract rapport but as a resource that enabled sensitive conversations, engagement with lifestyle change, and acceptance of referrals.

In rural places, if there is no trust, nothing moves. The patient listens first because they trust you. (N1)

In rural places, if there is no trust, nothing moves. The patient listens first because they trust you. (N1)

People don’t always trust the system, but they trust the nurse who stays and follows them. (N11)

People don’t always trust the system, but they trust the nurse who stays and follows them. (N11)

Trust was developed through continuity, respectful communication, and visible effort, such as calling patients after missed visits or checking on medication barriers.

Cultural tailoring and respectful communication

Nurses described adapting health messages to local norms, including family involvement, gender dynamics, and community expectations. They emphasized that culturally respectful communication was not optional, it determined whether interventions were realistic and acceptable.

If you speak in a way that doesn’t respect the culture, even correct advice will be rejected. (N4)

If you speak in a way that doesn’t respect the culture, even correct advice will be rejected. (N4)

Sometimes you need to involve the family, because decisions are not only individual. (N12)

Sometimes you need to involve the family, because decisions are not only individual. (N12)

Participants described using locally meaningful examples and avoiding judgmental language to reduce resistance and preserve dignity.

Shared problem-solving and patient partnership

Nurses described patients and families as partners in designing feasible solutions, choosing appointment times aligned with transport realities, agreeing on simpler self-care plans, or identifying barriers to referral attendance.

When we ask them what is possible, the plan becomes real. (N2)

When we ask them what is possible, the plan becomes real. (N2)

We stopped assuming. We asked: what prevents you from coming? Then we changed the process. (N6)

We stopped assuming. We asked: what prevents you from coming? Then we changed the process. (N6)

This collaborative approach was perceived as strengthening uptake and reducing “paper plans” that look good in guidelines but fail in rural life.

Working within rural constraints

This theme captures the structural pressures shaping rural nurse-led innovation. Participants consistently described constraints as real and persistent, limited staffing, equipment shortages, intermittent services, complex referral pathways, and documentation burden. However, nurses did not describe constraints as total barriers; instead, they framed innovation as “working inside limits,” prioritizing what could be improved locally while advocating for broader system support.

Resource Scarcity and Role Expansion

Participants described chronic staff shortages and expanded responsibilities requiring them to switch rapidly between triage, education, chronic disease checks, immunization, and administrative tasks. This role expansion created fatigue but also stimulated adaptive strategies to maintain service continuity.

One nurse can be doing triage, vaccination, education, and documentation in the same hour. (N7)

One nurse can be doing triage, vaccination, education, and documentation in the same hour. (N7)

We are expected to cover everything, so we must organize the work smarter. (N10)

We are expected to cover everything, so we must organize the work smarter. (N10)

Innovation often focuses on workflow redesign to protect time for high-value care (e.g., structured patient education, follow-up calls).

Distance, transport, and referral barriers

Distance emerged as a central determinant of health access. Nurses described missed appointments and delayed referrals as being driven less by “noncompliance” and more by transport limitations, cost, work obligations, and fragmented coordination between PHC and hospitals.

Referral is not just sending a paper. If the patient cannot travel, the referral is meaningless. (N5)

Referral is not just sending a paper. If the patient cannot travel, the referral is meaningless. (N5)

Sometimes patients wait until they are very sick because reaching the hospital is hard. (N3)

Sometimes patients wait until they are very sick because reaching the hospital is hard. (N3)

Nurses described innovation efforts aimed at strengthening referral navigation, clear instructions, follow-up calls, and coordination with receiving facilities when possible.

Digital friction and documentation burden

Participants described technology as both an enabler and a constraint. While electronic systems supported tracking, nurses reported that documentation requirements often competed with time for relational care. Intermittent connectivity, system complexity, and duplication of forms were described as barriers to sustaining innovations.

You spend time documenting the care more than delivering it. (N9)

You spend time documenting the care more than delivering it. (N9)

When the system is slow, the work becomes delayed, and patients get frustrated. (N1)

When the system is slow, the work becomes delayed, and patients get frustrated. (N1)

Nurses argued that digital tools should be redesigned to support rather than burden frontline work, particularly in resource-constrained rural settings.

Organizational enablement and the conditions for sustainability

This theme reflects how organizational culture shaped whether nurse-led innovations were legitimized, protected, and sustained. Participants emphasized that innovation required more than individual motivation; it depended on leadership responsiveness, psychological safety, training opportunities, and embedding changes into routine processes.

Leadership responsiveness and psychological safety

Nurses described that supportive leaders enabled experimentation and reduced fear of blame if changes did not work perfectly at first. In contrast, punitive or indifferent environments discouraged initiative and reinforced “just follow the routine.”

If leadership supports you, you can try and improve. If they blame you, you stop suggesting. (N6)

If leadership supports you, you can try and improve. If they blame you, you stop suggesting. (N6)

Integrative summary of findings

Overall, the findings indicate that nurse-led innovation in rural Saudi PHC is an adaptive, relationship-centered process shaped by frontline insight, community partnership, structural constraints, and organizational enablement. Nurses led change by identifying practice gaps, reframing innovation as feasible micro-improvements, and mobilizing trust-based relationships with patients and families. However, sustained innovation was constrained by rural resource scarcity, distance and referral barriers, and documentation burden. Organizational culture, particularly leadership responsiveness, psychological safety, training access, and formal integration into routines, emerged as the critical condition that transformed isolated initiatives into sustained practice improvements. Together, these themes illustrate how PAR can surface locally grounded solutions while strengthening nurses’ leadership role in reimagining community health in rural Saudi Arabia.

Participatory action research cycles: actions implemented and refined

Consistent with the participatory action research (PAR) design, findings from Cycle 1 (problem identification and collective reflection) informed a series of nurse-led practice innovations that were implemented during Cycle 2 (action) and subsequently evaluated and refined during Cycle 3 (reflection and adaptation). These actions were intentionally small-scale, locally feasible, and grounded in nurses’ everyday experiences of delivering primary healthcare (PHC) in rural settings. Rather than aiming for system-wide reform, participants focused on practical modifications to workflows, communication practices, and care coordination processes that could be enacted within existing resource constraints.

Across sites, nurses collectively identified recurring problems related to missed follow-up, fragmented referral pathways, limited patient understanding of care plans, clinic congestion, and documentation burden. In response, ten core actions were developed, tested, and refined through iterative cycles of feedback and adjustment (Table 3). The PAR process enabled nurses to assess not only whether an action “worked,” but how and under what conditions it was acceptable, sustainable, and meaningful in rural PHC practice.

Actions targeting continuity of care and follow-up

Several actions focused on improving continuity of care for patients with chronic conditions and those requiring referral to secondary or tertiary services. Nurses identified missed appointments and incomplete referrals as persistent contributors to avoidable deterioration and patient frustration. In Cycle 2, participants implemented structured follow-up reminder pathways, combining brief phone or SMS reminders with standardized scripts that clarified appointment purpose and logistics. Reflection in Cycle 3 revealed that reminders were most effective when they included a barrier check (e.g., transport, work obligations), prompting refinement of the script and adjustment of reminder timing to late afternoon hours.

Similarly, a referral navigation checklist was introduced to address gaps between referral initiation and patient attendance. Nurses reported that early versions of the checklist were too detailed and time-consuming; refinement focused on essential elements such as required documents, directions, and expected timelines. Adding a brief post-referral follow-up call improved closure of referral loops and reduced uncertainty among patients. These actions collectively shifted nurses’ role from referral “senders” to active navigators of patient trajectories, reinforcing continuity as a nursing responsibility rather than an administrative task.

Actions enhancing patient understanding and engagement

Another set of actions targeted patient understanding, health literacy, and engagement, areas nurses identified as central to effective rural PHC but often undermined by time pressure and competing demands. In Cycle 2, nurses introduced teach-back micro-counseling, limiting education to two or three key messages and asking patients to restate information in their own words. Reflection revealed that rigid scripts were less effective than adaptable messaging, leading to refinement through pictorial cues, simplified language, and selective involvement of family members when appropriate.

Complementing this approach, nurses implemented community-tailored mini health education sessions in waiting areas, focusing on high-priority topics such as diabetes self-management, hypertension warning signs, and immunization. Cycle 3 refinements shifted session timing to peak attendance periods and introduced interactive elements, improving participation and perceived relevance. These actions reinforced nurses’ identity as educators and community partners, while demonstrating that brief, well-timed educational interventions could be embedded without disrupting clinic flow.

Actions addressing workflow efficiency and documentation burden

Participants consistently identified workflow congestion and documentation burden as barriers to both care quality and innovation sustainability. In response, nurses co-designed a quick triage flow redesign, introducing a fast-track pathway for stable refill and screening visits. Reflection during Cycle 3 led to refinement of eligibility criteria and the addition of brief safety checks to prevent clinical risk. Nurses reported smoother patient flow during peak hours and reduced congestion, particularly when roles were clarified during morning huddles.

To address documentation challenges, a simplified documentation template was developed, emphasizing problem–plan–follow-up structure and reducing duplication. Refinement focused on minimizing required fields and clarifying role responsibility for completion. Nurses reported spending less time on documentation and experiencing improved continuity during handover. These actions illustrate how innovation in rural PHC often centers on protecting time for relational and clinical work, rather than adding new layers of activity.

Actions supporting screening, family involvement, and team learning

Additional actions addressed preventive care and workforce development. Opportunistic screening prompts were introduced at triage to increase uptake of blood pressure and risk screening, then refined to focus on high-yield measures and designated screening periods to avoid overburdening staff. Nurses also implemented family-inclusive counseling options, recognizing the central role of family in rural health decision-making. Refinements clarified consent and privacy boundaries, ensuring patient autonomy while leveraging family support.

Recognizing that innovation capacity varied by experience level, participants initiated peer learning huddles to share successes, challenges, and next steps. Cycle 3 refinements introduced rotating facilitation and action-focused agendas, strengthening collective ownership and spreading effective practices across sites. These actions supported professional confidence and normalized reflective practice within routine work.

Embedding and sustaining nurse-led innovations

During Cycle 3, participants critically examined which actions were feasible to sustain amid workload fluctuations. Rather than attempting to maintain all initiatives, nurses collectively identified a core bundle of actions (follow-up reminders, referral navigation, teach-back counseling, and triage flow redesign) for formal embedding. Sustainability strategies included integrating actions into routine checklists, assigning role-based responsibility, posting visual prompts, and incorporating practices into orientation for new staff.

This deliberate narrowing process reflects a key PAR insight: sustainability in rural PHC depends on selectivity, simplicity, and institutional alignment, rather than proliferation of initiatives. Embedding changes into everyday routines transformed innovation from an “extra task” into an expected component of nursing practice.

Discussion

This study provides insight into how nurses interpret and enact innovation within everyday rural primary healthcare practice. Rather than demonstrating intervention effectiveness, the findings illuminate processes through which frontline staff identify problems, negotiate solutions, and embed feasible changes within routine care. The findings demonstrate that nurse-led innovation in rural PHC is not primarily driven by technological advancement or large-scale reform, but by contextually grounded, relationship-centered micro-changes embedded within routine care. By positioning nurses as co-researchers and change agents, this study advances understanding of how frontline professionals translate experiential knowledge into meaningful and sustainable improvements in community health services.

Nurses as frontline innovators in rural primary healthcare

A central contribution of this study is the illumination of nurses’ leadership as emergent, practice-based, and morally grounded, rather than formally designated. Participants did not conceptualize innovation as an abstract or externally imposed activity; instead, it emerged from repeated exposure to unmet needs, preventable deterioration, and system gaps encountered in daily rural practice. This finding aligns with international evidence suggesting that frontline nurses are uniquely positioned to identify inefficiencies and initiate change due to their sustained proximity to patients and communities [28].

Importantly, nurses reframed innovation as small, feasible, and iterative, emphasizing adjustments to workflows, communication, and follow-up rather than comprehensive system redesign. This challenges dominant innovation discourses that privilege scale and novelty, and instead supports models of incremental, embedded innovation as particularly suited to rural and resource-constrained contexts [29]. The PAR process reinforced nurses’ sense of professional agency, transforming tacit problem recognition into explicit action and reinforcing accountability for community outcomes [30].

Participation, trust, and co-creation with communities

The findings underscore that participation is not merely a methodological feature but a practical necessity for effective rural PHC innovation. Nurses described trust as a foundational resource enabling patient engagement, adherence, and continuity, particularly in settings where geographical distance, cultural norms, and historical experiences shape health-seeking behavior. Consistent with literature on relational continuity and cultural safety, trust functioned as both a prerequisite and an outcome of nurse-led innovation [31, 32].

Co-creation with patients and families allowed nurses to tailor interventions to local realities, including transport limitations, family decision-making structures, and variable health literacy. This participatory orientation enhanced feasibility and acceptability, mitigating the common gap between guideline-based care and lived experience in rural settings [33]. The study therefore reinforces calls to reconceptualize community participation not as consultation, but as shared problem-solving embedded in care delivery [34, 35].

Navigating rural constraints through adaptive innovation

Consistent with prior rural health research, participants identified persistent structural constraints, including workforce shortages, expanded role expectations, fragmented referral pathways, and documentation burden [36, 37]. However, rather than framing these constraints as insurmountable barriers, nurses described innovation as adaptive work within limits. This adaptive stance aligns with complexity-informed perspectives on healthcare systems, which emphasize local sense-making and flexible responses over standardized solutions [38, 39].

The PAR actions demonstrated that innovation in such contexts often aims to protect time for high-value relational and clinical work, such as follow-up, education, and navigation, rather than introducing additional tasks. Efforts to redesign triage flow, simplify documentation, and streamline referrals illustrate how nurses prioritize continuity and accessibility as core dimensions of quality in rural PHC. These findings contribute to the limited empirical literature on how rural nurses actively reshape systems to mitigate structural disadvantage [40, 41].

Organizational culture as a determinant of sustainability

While individual motivation and professional commitment were evident, sustainability of innovation was shown to depend critically on organizational enablement. Leadership responsiveness, psychological safety, and access to training emerged as decisive factors influencing whether nurse-led initiatives were maintained or faded under workload pressure. This finding is consistent with evidence linking supportive leadership and learning climates to successful quality improvement and innovation adoption [42, 43].

Notably, nurses emphasized that sustainability required selectivity and institutionalization, rather than maintaining multiple parallel initiatives. The deliberate narrowing to a core bundle of actions reflects a sophisticated understanding of implementation fatigue and resource limitation. Embedding practices into routines, protocols, and orientation processes transformed innovation from discretionary effort into normalized practice, an insight highly relevant to policymakers and managers seeking scalable rural PHC improvement [44, 45].

Methodological contributions of participatory action research

Methodologically, this study demonstrates the value of PAR in capturing the dynamic, relational, and iterative nature of nurse-led innovation. By integrating action with reflection, PAR enabled participants to evaluate feasibility, acceptability, and impact in real time, rather than retrospectively. This approach contrasts with descriptive qualitative studies that document perceptions without supporting practice change, and with experimental designs that may lack contextual sensitivity [46, 47].

The collaborative analytic process enhanced credibility and relevance, ensuring that findings reflected nurses’ priorities rather than researcher-imposed frameworks. In the Saudi context, where hierarchical structures may limit frontline voice, PAR provided a structured yet flexible space for professional empowerment and collective learning. This study therefore, contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting PAR as a rigorous and ethically grounded approach for health systems research and workforce development [48, 49].

This study should be interpreted as an exploratory participatory case study of practice processes rather than an evaluation of intervention effectiveness.

Implications for primary healthcare policy and nursing practice

The findings have several implications. First, they highlight the need for PHC reform strategies that explicitly recognize and support nurse-led innovation, particularly in rural settings. Policies emphasizing digital transformation or centralized planning should be complemented by mechanisms that enable local adaptation and frontline leadership. Second, investment in leadership development, mentorship, and protected time for reflective practice may yield significant returns in service quality and continuity. Finally, embedding participatory approaches within PHC governance could strengthen alignment between national reform agendas and community realities.

The findings suggest innovation functioned as relational professional work rather than technical redesign. Nurses framed responsibility for continuity as a moral commitment shaped by proximity to patients and resource limitation, highlighting tensions between institutional standardization and situated professional judgement.

Limitations

This study involved a purposive sample of nurses drawn from multiple rural PHC centers across different regions rather than a single site. The sample size was appropriate for participatory action research, allowing in-depth engagement across iterative cycles of planning, action, and reflection. The findings are not intended for statistical generalization; rather, transferability depends on contextual similarity, including rural service structures, workforce roles, and community dynamics.

Second, participation required a willingness to engage in reflective practice and innovation activities, which may have introduced self-selection bias. Nurses who volunteered may have been more motivated, confident, or positively disposed toward change than those who declined participation. Consequently, the study may underrepresent perspectives of nurses who experience greater disengagement, burnout, or resistance to innovation.

Third, as action and inquiry occurred concurrently, attribution of observed changes to specific PAR actions cannot be established with certainty. The early effects reported reflect process-level signals and participant perceptions rather than formal outcome evaluation. Longer follow-up periods and mixed-methods designs incorporating quantitative indicators would be needed to assess sustained impact on patient outcomes, service utilization, or cost-effectiveness.

Fourth, the collaborative nature of PAR raises the possibility of social desirability and confirmation bias, particularly during group reflection and collective analysis. Although reflexive journaling, triangulation of data sources, and iterative member validation were employed to enhance credibility, participants may have emphasized positive experiences or underreported challenges.

Finally, the study was conducted within a specific sociocultural and health system context, shaped by Saudi Arabia’s rural geography, primary care organization, and ongoing health reforms. While this context strengthens the study’s relevance for national policy and practice, it may limit direct applicability to settings with substantially different governance structures or workforce roles.

Conclusion

This participatory action research study demonstrates that nurses in rural Saudi Arabia are active leaders of primary healthcare innovation, capable of translating experiential knowledge into practical, sustainable improvements in community health services. Through iterative cycles of reflection and action, nurses identified locally salient problems, co-created solutions with patients and communities, and embedded feasible innovations into routine practice. Innovation was not characterized by technological disruption or large-scale reform, but by incremental, relationship-centered changes that addressed continuity, access, workflow efficiency, and patient understanding within existing constraints.

The findings highlight that the effectiveness and sustainability of nurse-led innovation depend on organizational enablement, including supportive leadership, psychological safety, targeted training, and integration of new practices into institutional routines. Participatory approaches such as PAR provide a powerful methodological and practical framework for surfacing frontline insight, strengthening professional agency, and aligning health system reform with community realities.

As Saudi Arabia continues to advance primary healthcare transformation under Vision 2030, recognizing and investing in nurses’ leadership at the community level will be essential to achieving equitable, responsive, and sustainable care, particularly in rural and underserved regions. Future research should build on these findings through longitudinal evaluation of nurse-led innovations, inclusion of patient-reported outcomes, and comparative studies across rural and urban PHC contexts.
