A low-dose self-amplifying mRNA vaccine encoding HPV16 E6/E7 induces potent T-cell immunity and antitumor protection in mice
Authors: Rui Xing, Shanshan Wang, Yixin Wang, Geng Chen, Rui Zhou, Xuelian Wang, Li Ma, Yongjun Yang, Sheng Li, Ming Zhao, Yinwei Zhang, Yuanyuan Fu, Fujie Cui, Gao Meng, Bin Zheng, Changyun Xiong, Youru Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV), particularly high-risk types such as HPV16, is associated with several malignancies, including cervical cancer. Existing therapeutic vaccines targeting HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 show limited immunogenicity and high production costs. Self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA) vaccines offer a promising alternative by enabling robust antigen expression at low doses. This study evaluated the immunogenicity and antitumor efficacy of a novel saRNA vaccine, JJ-saRNA-HPV01, targeting HPV16 E6/E7 in a preclinical mouse model.MethodsJJ-saRNA-HPV01, encoding an HPV16 E6-linker-E7 fusion protein, was encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNP). Physicochemical properties (size, PDI, encapsulation efficiency, and zeta potential) were characterized, and in vitro expression was confirmed in 293 T cells by Western blot. Female C57BL/6 mice were immunized intramuscularly with single or double doses (0.1–5 μg). Immune responses were assessed by IFN-γ ELISpot, CD8⁺CD69⁺ T-cell activation, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte analysis. Antitumor efficacy was evaluated in TC-1 tumor-bearing mice, with prophylactic and long-term protection tested by tumor challenge and re-challenge. Statistical tests included one-way and two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with Mantel–Cox test.ResultsJJ-saRNA-HPV01 elicited potent, dose-dependent, E7-specific CD8⁺ T-cell responses in the mouse model. In therapeutic TC-1 models, both 0.1 and 1 µg doses markedly inhibited tumor growth (TGI = 93.0 and 95.7%) and improved survival (p < 0.001), with the 1 µg dose achieving complete regression and 91% survival. Prophylactic vaccination provided 100% protection and cured mice rejected tumor re-challenge, confirming durable E7-specific memory. Mechanistically, vaccination increased intratumoral CD8⁺ infiltration with limited CD4⁺ recruitment, elevated IFN-γ⁺ effector T-cell frequencies (p < 0.001), and reduced PD-1 expression on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by 33–55% (p < 0.05), indicating partial reversal of T-cell exhaustion and establishment of a more immunoactive tumor microenvironment.ConclusionsJJ-saRNA-HPV01 induces potent antitumor immunity at low doses (0.1–1 μg), by promoting T-cell infiltration, enhancing IFN-γ secretion, and downregulating PD-1 expression. Its dual prophylactic/therapeutic efficacy, dose-sparing advantage, and long-term protection support clinical translation in HPV-associated cancers, particularly in resource-limited settings. Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is etiologically linked to approximately 5% of all human cancers, including cervical, vulvar, vaginal, penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers [1, 2]. Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common malignancy in women worldwide, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually, of which more than 94% occur in low- and middle-income countries [3–5]. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) types, particularly HPV16 and HPV18, is the principal cause of these malignancies, with HPV16 alone accounting for 50–60% of cervical cancer cases globally [5, 6].

HPV-driven oncogenesis is mediated by continuous expression of the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7, which inactivate the p53 and retinoblastoma (Rb) tumor suppressor pathways and promote malignant transformation [6–9]. As E6 and E7 are constitutively expressed in infected and transformed cells but absent in normal tissues, they represent ideal targets for therapeutic vaccination [7, 9, 10]. Several HPV therapeutic vaccine platforms–including peptide, protein, DNA, RNA, viral vector, and cell-based approaches–have entered clinical development [11–13]. However, these strategies are often limited by modest immunogenicity, the requirement for high antigen doses, and complex manufacturing, underscoring the need for a more potent, scalable, and safe vaccine platform.

Self-amplifying messenger RNA (saRNA) vaccines have emerged as a versatile and promising platform for both infectious disease prevention and cancer immunotherapy, leveraging intrinsic RNA self-replication to amplify antigen expression and elicit robust cellular and humoral immune responses [14–17]. The self-amplifying capacity of saRNA is conferred by the alphavirus nonstructural proteins nsP1–nsP4, which form a replicase complex that mediates RNA amplification via nsP4-driven RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) activity [18–21]. This complex drives intracellular RNA amplification and sustained antigen expression, leading to potent and durable immune activation at doses one to two orders of magnitude lower than those required for conventional linear mRNA vaccines [21, 22]. Consistent with this concept, early proof-of-concept work showed that self-replicating RNA could function as a cancer vaccine platform, with an SFV replicon encoding a model tumor antigen inducing antigen-specific CD8⁺ T-cell responses at doses as low as 0.1 μg, complete tumor protection at 1 μg in a prophylactic setting, and prolonged survival in a therapeutic tumor model [23]. In the HPV field, related alphavirus replicon-based strategies have also shown potent activity in the TC-1 E6/E7 model; notably, an SFV-based DNA-launched replicon encoding HPV16 E6/E7, delivered intradermally with electroporation, achieved therapeutic tumor control at sub-microgram doses, with efficacy reported down to 0.05 μg [24]. Together, these findings support the rationale for evaluating low-dose replicon-based HPV vaccination while highlighting the continued need for clinically practical and scalable saRNA platforms.

In this study, we developed and characterized JJ-saRNA-HPV01, a self-amplifying mRNA vaccine encoding an HPV16 E6-linker-E7 fusion antigen. To benchmark its performance, we evaluated JJ-saRNA-HPV01 against multiple controls, including the non-replicating mRNA counterpart JJ-023, the irrelevant reporter RNA-LNP control JJ-0020, and blank LNP, depending on the experimental context. JJ-saRNA-HPV01 was designed to enable sustained antigen expression and to elicit strong cellular immune responses at low doses (0.1–1 µg). The present work assessed its immunogenicity, antitumor efficacy, and underlying immunological mechanisms in both therapeutic and prophylactic murine models.

Methods

Mice

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Hangzhou Medical College, Hangzhou, China, and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility (25 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12–h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology, Hangzhou Medical College.

Animal welfare and safety monitoring

Mice were monitored at least once daily for general condition and clinical signs using a standardized welfare rubric. Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days using an electronic caliper, with more frequent monitoring when tumor growth accelerated. Injection sites were inspected for erythema, swelling, ulceration, ecchymosis, or impaired limb use, and the tumor inoculation site was examined for ulceration, necrosis, or other abnormal changes in tumor appearance. Systemic observations included general health status, body weight, food intake, activity/behavior, and signs of distress such as labored breathing or coat changes. Transient local inflammation was occasionally observed within 24 h after injection and resolved by the following day; no persistent injection-site reactions were noted. Humane endpoints were applied in cases of excessive tumor burden (tumor volume > 2000 mm3), ulceration or rupture, or signs of distress. No overt treatment-related systemic adverse clinical signs were observed during routine welfare monitoring.

Self-amplifying RNA vaccine and controls construction

The self-amplifying RNA vaccine candidate, designated JJ-saRNA-HPV01, was designed in-house based on our patented anti-HPV self-replicating RNA platform (Chinese Patent No. CN118725049A). The vaccine construct was generated by inserting a codon-optimized HPV16 antigen cassette into a Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV)-based self-replicating vector backbone. Codon optimization was performed using host-biased synonymous recoding, with removal or replacement of sequence features predicted to reduce RNA instability and adjustment of GC content to improve expression; the optimized open reading frame had an average GC content of approximately 59% (range 57%–61%) [25].

The antigen cassette encoded a 335–amino acid fusion polyprotein arranged as tPA signal peptide–E6–furin cleavage site–E7–MITD. The N-terminus contained a tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) signal peptide (MDAMKRGLCCVLLLCGAVFVSPSSAR) to direct the fusion protein into the secretory pathway. This was followed by mutated, non-oncogenic derivatives of HPV16 E6 and E7, separated by a furin-sensitive cleavage motif (GRKRRS). The E6 and E7 sequences were engineered as non-oncogenic derivatives by introducing point mutations in regions associated with oncogenic activity. Specifically, E6 carried the mutations C63G, C113G, and I135T, whereas E7 carried the mutations C24G and E36G, as described in our Chinese patent [25]. To enhance antigen presentation, a C-terminal MHC class I trafficking domain (MITD) was fused downstream of E7 [26]. For construct generation, the optimized antigen cassette was cloned into the self-replicating vector using SalI and XbaI restriction sites, and the resulting plasmids were verified by restriction digestion and sequence confirmation prior to in vitro transcription [25].

For comparative immunogenicity assessment, a non-replicating mRNA control (JJ-023) was generated to express the identical antigen cassette (SP1-HPV16 E6(mut)– Endosite–HPV E7(mut)- MITD). As a negative control for antigen specificity, an EGFP-expressing negative control (JJ-0020) was engineered to express an 852–amino acid reporter fusion protein comprising Firefly luciferase and enhanced green fluorescent protein (FLUCQEGFP-6H). Antigen and control coding sequences were synthesized and sequence-verified prior to in vitro transcription and lipid nanoparticle formulation.

saRNA-LNP synthesis and formulation

saRNA constructs were synthesized by in vitro transcription (IVT) from linearized plasmid templates. Plasmids were linearized using BspQI (New England Biolabs), purified using a magnetic bead-based DNA cleanup kit (Vazyme, Cat# N411–02-AA), and quantified by NanoDrop spectrophotometry. IVT was performed using a T7 RNA polymerase kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) in the presence of nucleoside triphosphates and a co-transcriptional capping reagent (Syngene Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Jiangsu, China). DNA templates were degraded by DNase I (Vazyme Biotech) and the resulting saRNA was purified by lithium chloride precipitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA quality and integrity were assessed by A260 absorbance and agarose gel electrophoresis before storage at − 80 °C.

For LNP formulation, a lipid mixture of Dlin-MC3-DMA, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 was prepared in anhydrous ethanol at a molar ratio of 50:7:40:3. The saRNA was diluted in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 4.0) and combined with the lipid phase using an MPE-L2 microfluidic mixer (Aitesen, Suzhou, China) at a 3:1 (aqueous:ethanol) volume ratio. The resulting saRNA-LNPs were buffer-exchanged into 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) containing 10% (w/v) sucrose using 100 kDa Amicon ultrafiltration units (Millipore, USA). Encapsulation efficiency and RNA concentration were determined using the Quant-iT™ RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Cat#R11490, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (Nanolink SZ901, Linkoptik, Zhuhai, China).

In vitro transfection and western blotting

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 0.25 × 106 cells/mL (2 mL per well; 5.0 × 105 cells/well; ~ 5.2 × 104 cells/cm2) 24 h prior to transfection. Cells were transfected with 2 μg of the indicated mRNA formulations using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 24–h post-transfection and lysed for protein extraction. Protein expression was analyzed by standard Western blotting. The HPV16 E6-E7 fusion protein was detected using an anti-HPV16 E7 antibody (GeneTex, Cat#GTX133411), and GAPDH was used as a loading control.

Tumor model and experimental design

The TC-1 murine tumor cell line was obtained from Immocell Ltd. Across TC-1 challenge studies, blank LNP denotes empty lipid nanoparticles without encoded RNA, whereas JJ-0020 denotes an irrelevant reporter RNA-LNP control used where a platform-matched negative control was required.

To evaluate dose-dependent immunogenicity in vivo, mice were assigned to seven treatment groups (blank LNP, JJ-0020, and JJ-saRNA-HPV01 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, or 5 μg; n = 6 mice per group per cohort) and studied in two independent terminal cohorts. In the post-prime cohort, mice received a single intramuscular immunization on day 0 and were euthanized on day 14 for splenocyte collection. In the post-boost cohort, mice received intramuscular immunizations on days 0 and 14 and were euthanized on day 28 for splenocyte collection. Splenocytes from both cohorts were analyzed by IFN-γ ELISpot, whereas flow cytometry was performed only on splenocytes from the post-boost cohort. All intramuscular injections were administered into the right quadriceps (right thigh; quadriceps femoris muscle). Blank-control animals received 50 μL of empty LNP formulation on the same dosing schedule as vaccine-treated groups, whereas JJ-0020 control animals received JJ-0020 RNA-LNP at 30 μg per dose.

Therapeutic efficacy study: Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old; total n = 30) were inoculated subcutaneously with 2.5 × 105 TC-1 cells. When tumors reached approximately 50–100 mm3, mice were randomized and treated by intramuscular immunization on days 0 and 14 according to the study design. At the time of randomization, three animals were excluded prior to treatment allocation according to prespecified criteria because tumor volumes fell outside the target window, yielding 27 evaluable mice (blank LNP, n = 6; JJ-saRNA-HPV01 0.1 μg, n = 10; JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg, n = 11). Tumor growth and survival were monitored through day 30.

Prophylactic tumor challenge (preventive efficacy): Naïve female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomized into four groups (blank LNP, JJ-0020, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 0.1 μg, and JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg; n = 6 mice per group; total n = 24) and immunized intramuscularly twice, with a 14-day interval between the prime and booster doses. Sixty days after the second immunization, mice were challenged subcutaneously with 2.5 × 105 TC-1 cells. Tumor growth was monitored for 35 days after challenge or until humane endpoints were reached.

Tumor re-challenge study: To evaluate long-term recall protection, a separate re-challenge study was performed. In the initial phase, female C57BL/6 mice bearing TC-1 tumors were immunized with JJ-saRNA-HPV01 at 0.1 μg or 1 μg on days 0 and 14 (n = 15 per vaccinated group). Following the initial challenge phase, mice that achieved complete tumor regression were eligible for re-challenge, and six tumor-free survivors from each vaccinated group entered the re-challenge phase. A separate blank LNP comparator group (n = 6) was included in parallel as the negative control for secondary tumor challenge, resulting in a total of 18 mice in the re-challenge experiment. On day 45, mice were re-challenged by subcutaneous inoculation of 2.5 × 105 TC-1 cells into the flank contralateral to the initial inoculation site, and tumor growth was monitored for 35 days thereafter. Blank-control mice were euthanized on day 73, whereas mice in the JJ-saRNA-HPV01 0.1 μg and 1 μg groups were euthanized on day 80; splenocytes harvested at these terminal timepoints were subjected to IFN-γ ELISpot analysis.

TC-1 tumor microenvironment studies were conducted using 24 mice distributed across four groups: blank LNP control, JJ-0020 control, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 0.1 μg, and JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg (n = 6 mice per group). Tumors were harvested at the following time points: Day 15 (JJ-0020 control and blank LNP), Day 27 (0.1 μg group), and Day 30 (1 μg group). Harvested tissues were then allocated for either flow cytometric analysis or multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF).

ELISpot assay

Spleens were harvested aseptically and mechanically dissociated through a 70-μm cell strainer into PBS to generate single-cell suspensions. Cells were pelleted (400 × g, 5 min), subjected to red blood cell lysis (2 mL RBC lysis buffer, room temperature, 5 min), and lysis was quenched with 8–10 mL complete RPMI-1640 (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS). Cells were washed twice (400 × g, 5 min), resuspended in complete RPMI-1640, counted, and adjusted to 4 × 10⁶ cells/mL for downstream assays.

For ELISpot assays, splenocytes were plated at 2 × 105 cells per well in 96-well PVDF membrane ELISpot plates (Millipore, Cat# MSIPS4W10) and stimulated for 24 h with HPV16 E6 or E7 peptide pools (GenScript) at a final total peptide concentration of 10 μg/mL for each pool. DMSO, used as the peptide solvent, served as the negative control. ELISpot plates were coated with rat anti-mouse IFN-γ capture antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 14–7313–85) and developed using biotinylated rat anti-mouse IFN-γ detection antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 13–7312–85), followed by streptavidin-HRP (BD, Cat# 554,066) and AEC substrate (BD, Cat# 551,951). Spots were quantified using a Mabtech IRIS™ automated ELISpot reader (IRIS 52).

Flow cytometry analysis

Splenocytes from the post-boost cohort were used for systemic immune phenotyping. Freshly isolated single-cell suspensions were prepared as described above, stained with Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BD Biosciences, Cat# 565,388), blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend), and surface-stained with antibodies against CD8α (FITC; BD Biosciences, Cat# 553,030) and CD69 (PE; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 12–0691–82) to quantify activated CD8⁺ T cells.

For tumor-infiltrating leukocyte analysis, TC-1 tumors were excised, minced, and digested for 45 min at 37 °C in RPMI-1640 containing collagenase IV (1 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 17,104,019) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL; Vazyme, Cat# GMP4104PC-01). Digested tissue was passed through a 70-μm cell strainer, washed, and counted. Cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BD Biosciences, Cat# 565,388), blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend), and surface-stained at 4 °C for 30 min with antibodies against CD45 (PE; clone 30-F11; Thermo Fisher Scientific/eBioscience, Cat# 12–0451–82), CD3 (PerCP-Cy5.5; BD Biosciences, Cat# 3,138,327), CD4 (PE-Cy7; BD Biosciences, Cat# 116,016), CD8α (FITC; BD Biosciences, Cat# 553,030), and PD-1/CD279 (eFluor 450; clone RMP1–30; Thermo Fisher Scientific/eBioscience, Cat# 48–9981–82). For intracellular cytokine staining, tumor-derived cells were stimulated ex vivo as specified for each experiment in the presence of Brefeldin A (10 μg/mL; BD Biosciences), then fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm (eBioscience) and stained with anti-mouse IFN-γ (APC; clone XMG1.2; BD Biosciences, Cat# 554,413).

Samples were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.0). Debris was excluded by forward- and side-scatter parameters, doublets were removed by singlet discrimination, and live cells were identified by exclusion of FVS780⁺ events. For tumor-derived samples, leukocytes were gated as CD45⁺, followed by identification of CD3⁺ T cells and delineation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets. IFN-γ–producing cells were quantified within the CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T-cell gates after intracellular staining, and PD-1 expression on tumor-infiltrating CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and imaging

Within the same tumor microenvironment study described above, multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on tumors from the blank LNP control and JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg groups. Tumors from the blank LNP control group were harvested on day 15, whereas tumors from the JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg group were harvested on day 30. Tumor tissue allocated for mIF was processed for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sectioning.

mIF staining and whole-slide scanning were performed by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) using a 4-marker, 5-color panel consisting of antibodies against mouse CD3, CD4, CD8, and PD-1, with DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Whole-slide images were acquired using a Pannoramic Digital Slide Scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). Digital slides were reviewed and visualized using CaseViewer software (version 2.4).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2) or Stata (version 19). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Treatment efficacy was evaluated by calculating the tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate on designated days using the formula: TGI(\%)=[1-Vt÷Vc]×100, where Vt and Vc represent mean tumor volumes of treated and control groups, respectively. Group comparisons were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) depending on the experimental design. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used when comparing each treatment group to a single control (e.g., E7-specific immune responses, PD-1 expression), whereas two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was applied for multi-factorial or repeated-measures experiments (e.g., tumor growth kinetics and dose–response comparisons). Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test, and correlations between immune parameters such as T-cell infiltration density, IFN-γ production, and PD-1 expression were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance denoted in figures as ns, P ≥ 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

JJ-saRNA-HPV01 vaccine–induced E7-specific immunity was mediated by CD8⁺ T cells

The saRNA vaccine construct, JJ-saRNA-HPV01, was based on an alphavirus replicon designed to support intracellular RNA self-replication and sustained antigen expression (Fig. 1A). The encoded antigen consisted of a codon-optimized HPV16 E6/E7 fusion protein containing a signal peptide, a furin cleavage site positioned between E6 and E7, and a C-terminal MITD sequence. Two control RNA constructs were generated in parallel: JJ-023, a non-replicating mRNA lacking the replicase sequence, and JJ-0020, an EGFP-expressing negative control.

All RNA constructs were formulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) by microfluidic mixing, yielding particles with a uniform size distribution (~ 90 nm), low polydispersity index (PDI < 0.17), and high encapsulation efficiency (> 94%) (Fig. 1B). Cryo-TEM imaging showed a uniform spherical morphology typical of RNA-LNP complexes (Fig. 1D). This physicochemical characterization indicated that the LNP formulations were comparable, supporting the interpretation that differences in immunogenicity were primarily attributable to the encoded RNA constructs [27]. Western blot analysis further confirmed expression of the HPV16 E6/E7 fusion protein (~ 40 kDa) in cells transfected with both the saRNA and non-replicating mRNA constructs, indicating successful LNP delivery and functional antigen expression (Fig. 1C). Together, these data support the physicochemical consistency and in vitro expression of the JJ-saRNA-HPV01 vaccine construct.

JJ-saRNA-HPV01 induced a robust, dose-dependent cellular immune response predominantly mediated by E7-specific T cells (Fig. 2). Following a single immunization (day 14 terminal cohort; Fig. 2B), E7 peptide stimulation elicited significant IFN-γ secretion in the high-dose groups. The 5 µg and 1 µg doses yielded mean responses of 1,602 ± 415 and 909 ± 212 SFU/10⁶ cells, respectively, which were significantly higher than the JJ-0020 control (5 μg vs. JJ-0020: P < 0.0001; 1 μg vs. JJ-0020: P = 0.0092). In contrast, E6-specific responses remained low across all dose groups (< 100 SFU/10⁶ cells) and were not statistically distinguishable from controls (P ≥ 0.05).

Booster immunization further amplified E7-specific immunity (day 28 terminal cohort; Fig. 2C). The 5 µg dose elicited the highest response (2,438 ± 356 SFU/10⁶ cells), followed by the 1 µg dose (1,061 ± 297 SFU/106 cells) and the 0.5 µg dose (1,210 ± 323 SFU/10⁶ cells), all of which were significantly elevated compared to the JJ-0020 control (5 μg vs. JJ-0020: P < 0.0001; 1 μg vs. JJ-0020: P = 0.0325; 0.5 μg vs. JJ-0020: P = 0.0118). Even the 0.1 µg dose showed a detectable ELISpot signal (700 SFU/10⁶ cells), indicating a successful recall response. No significant difference was observed between the blank LNP and JJ-0020 negative controls (ns), confirming assay specificity.

Flow cytometry of splenocytes from the day-28 post-boost cohort showed an increased frequency of activated CD8⁺CD69⁺ T cells after vaccination (Fig. 2D). Activated CD8⁺CD69⁺ T cells were significantly increased in vaccinated mice compared to JJ-0020 controls (P < 0.0001 across doses). The 5 and 1 µg groups exhibited activation frequencies of 1.92 ± 0.19 and 1.46 ± 0.24%, respectively, compared to 0.02 ± 0.01% in the JJ-0020 group (P < 0.0001). The 0.1 µg dose also induced significant activation (1.54 ± 0.26%) relative to controls (P < 0.0001). Overall, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 increased systemic CD8⁺ T-cell activation (CD69 upregulation) after boosting across doses, complementing the E7-dominant IFN-γ ELISpot responses.

JJ-saRNA-HPV01 vaccination suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival in HPV16⁺ TC-1 mice

Therapeutic efficacy of JJ-saRNA-HPV01 was evaluated in C57BL/6 mice bearing subcutaneous TC-1 tumors expressing HPV16 E6/E7. Thirty mice were inoculated; at the time of randomization (target tumor volume 50–100 mm3), three animals were excluded prior to treatment allocation according to prespecified criteria because tumor volumes fell outside the target window, yielding 27 evaluable mice (blank LNP n = 6, 0.1 µg n = 10, 1 µg n = 11). Both vaccine doses (0.1 and 1 µg) significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with controls (Fig. 3B; P < 0.0001 versus blank control for both doses), demonstrating potent in vivo antitumor activity.

Individual tumor growth kinetics indicated durable tumor control in most mice treated with 1 µg, whereas partial tumor regrowth occurred in a subset of animals receiving 0.1 µg (Fig. 3C). In contrast, control mice displayed rapid, unchecked tumor progression and reached endpoint criteria by approximately day 18. Consistent with these effects, the tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate was 95.7% for the 1 µg group and 93.0% for the 0.1 µg group (Fig. 3B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further demonstrated a significant treatment benefit (Fig. 3D): the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test yielded P = 0.0002, indicating that survival/time-to-endpoint distributions differed significantly across groups, with vaccinated mice surviving longer than controls. By day 30, survival was 10/11 (90.9%) in the 1 µg group and 4/10 (40.0%) in the 0.1 µg group, compared with 0/6 (0%) in controls. Together, these data support selection of 1 µg as the preferred therapeutic dose, providing the most durable tumor control and survival benefit while maintaining dose efficiency within the saRNA platform.

JJ-saRNA-HPV01 vaccination conferred complete prophylactic and long-term protection against HPV16⁺ tumors

To assess preventive efficacy (Fig. 4A, upper panel), naïve C57BL/6 mice were immunized intramuscularly twice and then challenged subcutaneously with TC-1 cells (2.5 × 105) 60 days after the booster immunization. During the 35-day post-challenge observation period, both JJ-saRNA-HPV01 dose groups (0.1 µg and 1 µg) showed complete protection against tumor engraftment, whereas both control groups developed progressive tumors (Fig. 4B; vaccinated vs. controls, P < 0.0001). Importantly, tumor growth did not differ between the two negative controls (blank LNP versus JJ-0020, an irrelevant reporter saRNA-LNP; ns), indicating that the saRNA/LNP platform alone did not confer measurable protection in this prophylactic setting.

To evaluate long-term recall protection, a separate re-challenge study was conducted as outlined in Fig. 4A (lower panel). In this experiment, mice were first inoculated with TC-1 cells and then immunized according to the study design. Mice that achieved tumor-free status (n = 6/group) after the initial challenge phase were re-challenged with TC-1 cells in the contralateral flank on day 45, and tumor growth was monitored for 35 days after the second challenge. Both vaccinated groups resisted tumor re-establishment, whereas blank-control mice developed progressive tumors and reached humane endpoints earlier (Fig. 4C; vaccinated vs. blank control, P < 0.0001).

Consistent with functional protection, IFN-γ ELISpot assays performed at the re-challenge study endpoint demonstrated persistent E7-specific cellular responses (Fig. 4D). Background responses were low following DMSO stimulation (1 µg: 28.5 ± 7.1 SFU/10⁶ cells, n = 5; 0.1 µg: 21.9 ± 2.8 SFU/10⁶ cells) and remained weak upon E6 peptide stimulation (1 µg: 61.0 ± 15.7; 0.1 µg: 21.3 ± 3.6 SFU/10⁶ cells; all ns, P > 0.05 versus control). In contrast, E7 peptide stimulation induced robust IFN-γ responses in both vaccinated groups (1 µg: 1557 ± 862 SFU/10⁶ cells; 0.1 µg: 462 ± 144 SFU/10⁶ cells), significantly exceeding blank controls (1 µg vs blank: P < 0.001; 0.1 µg vs blank: P < 0.05). Moreover, the 1 µg dose elicited a significantly greater E7-specific response than 0.1 µg (P < 0.05), indicating dose-dependent enhancement of E7-directed T-cell memory.

Together, these two independent experiments show complete protection in a tumor-naïve prophylactic setting and resistance to tumor re-challenge after therapeutic cure, consistent with functional recall immunity within the timeframe tested.

JJ-saRNA-HPV01 vaccination enhanced tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes abundance and effector function within the tumor microenvironment

Having established the therapeutic and prophylactic efficacy of JJ-saRNA-HPV01, we next conducted an independent mechanistic tumor microenvironment study to determine whether systemic immune priming translated into local intratumoral immunity. A total of 24 mice were included in this study, with six mice per group across four groups: blank LNP control, JJ-0020 control, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 0.1 μg, and JJ-saRNA-HPV01 1 μg. Tumors were collected at assay-specific time points: day 15 for the blank LNP and JJ-0020 control groups, day 27 for the 0.1 μg group, and day 30 for the 1 μg group. Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on tumors from the blank LNP control and 1 μg groups to assess spatial T-cell infiltration and PD-1 expression (Fig. 5A), whereas flow-cytometric analysis was performed on tumors from the JJ-0020, 0.1 μg, and 1 μg groups to quantify T-cell infiltration, effector cytokine production, and inhibitory checkpoint expression (Fig. 5B–F).

Representative multiplex immunofluorescence images showed increased intratumoral CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T-cell signals in vaccinated tumors relative to controls (Fig. 5A). To quantify tumor-infiltrating leukocytes, dissociated tumors were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 5B). Compared with JJ-0020 (CD45⁺: 1.19 ± 0.41%; CD45⁺CD3⁺: 0.80 ± 0.29%; CD3⁺CD8⁺: 0.42 ± 0.16%; CD3⁺CD4⁺: 0.21 ± 0.07%), the 1 µg JJ-saRNA-HPV01 group exhibited significantly increased overall immune infiltration (CD45⁺: 3.92 ± 1.42%, P = 0.0199; CD45⁺CD3⁺: 3.66 ± 1.62%, P = 0.0149). In contrast, the 0.1 µg group did not differ significantly from JJ-0020 (CD45⁺: 1.81 ± 0.84%; CD45⁺CD3⁺: 1.23 ± 0.72%; P ≥ 0.05). Within the CD3⁺ compartment, CD8⁺ T cells were numerically more abundant than CD4⁺ T cells across groups (Fig. 5B). Although CD3⁺CD8⁺ and CD3⁺CD4⁺ frequencies trended higher in the 1 µg group (CD3⁺CD8⁺: 2.42 ± 1.21%; CD3⁺CD4⁺: 0.64 ± 0.19%), these subset-level comparisons did not reach statistical significance (P ≥ 0.05). Notably, inter-animal variability was substantial (large SEM), particularly for CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells, consistent with heterogeneous intratumoral immune recruitment and providing a plausible explanation for the non-significant subset comparisons despite an apparent upward trend.

To explore coordinated recruitment of cytotoxic and helper T cells, we performed correlation analysis using the per-tumor CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ infiltration values quantified from the multiplex immunofluorescence dataset (1 µg tumors pooled; n = 6 tumors). Intratumoral CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ infiltration was positively correlated across tumors (r = 0.83), consistent with coordinated recruitment of helper and cytotoxic T-cell subsets.

Vaccine-induced T cells also exhibited potent effector activity, evidenced by substantial increases in IFN-γ–producing CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets within the TME (Fig. 5C, D). IFN-γ production correlated modestly with CD8⁺ infiltration (r = 0.372) and moderately with CD4⁺ infiltration (r = 0.444), suggesting that functional activation may contribute to vaccine efficacy in addition to overall T-cell abundance.

PD-1 mean fluorescence intensity was reduced on CD4⁺ T cells in vaccinated groups compared with JJ-0020 controls (Fig. 5E; 1 µg: P = 0.0026; 0.1 µg: P = 0.0322). In the 1 µg JJ-saRNA-HPV01 group, PD-1 mean fluorescence intensity was reduced on both CD4⁺ (~ 55%) and CD8⁺ (~ 33%) T cells relative to control, consistent with reduced expression of an exhaustion-associated marker. PD-1 expression was inversely correlated with intratumoral T-cell infiltration density (CD4⁺ r = − 0.413; CD8⁺ r = − 0.809), such that tumors with higher CD4⁺/CD8⁺ infiltration tended to exhibit lower PD-1 levels, consistent with a tumor microenvironment characterized by greater T-cell influx and reduced inhibitory receptor expression.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the low-dose saRNA vaccine (JJ-saRNA-HPV01) encoding HPV16 E6/E7 elicited potent immunogenicity and robust antitumor activity in the TC-1 model. Mechanistically, vaccine effects extended beyond systemic activation to local reprogramming of the TME through a synergistic triad of immune events (Fig. 5): (i) increased intratumoral infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells, (ii) enhanced IFN-γ–mediated effector activity, and (iii) reduced PD-1 expression on TILs, consistent with the alleviation of T-cell exhaustion [28, 29]. These findings indicated that JJ-saRNA-HPV01 effectively reprogrammed the TME into a pro-inflammatory, immune-active milieu capable of sustaining effector function and long-term tumor surveillance. This outcome aligns with prior preclinical evidence that RNA-based vaccines enhance T-cell priming, infiltration, and effector memory formation in vivo [30, 31], and with human studies demonstrating long immune memory following mRNA vaccination [32].

The concurrent activation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells suggests that antigen processing occurred through both MHC class I (via intracellular E6/E7 expression) and MHC class II (via antigen-presenting cell processing), thereby enabling coordinated cytotoxic and helper-mediated immune responses [18, 33]. While E7 is an intracellular antigen that predominantly elicits MHC I-restricted CD8⁺ cytotoxicity, E7-derived peptides can also be cross-presented on MHC II, stimulating CD4⁺ helper T cells [18, 34, 35]. This interplay is essential, as CD4⁺ T cells provide cytokine support (e.g., IFN-γ and IL-2) and immune-licensing signals that augment antigen presentation, sustain CD8⁺ activity, and counteract tumor-induced immunosuppression within the TME [35–37]. Consistent with this framework, protective immunity in the TC-1 model has been shown to rely primarily on E7-specific CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells [34]. In the context of JJ-saRNA-HPV01, this coordinated CD4⁺/CD8⁺ activation likely underpinned both tumor regression and the establishment of durable protection.

To contextualize translational potential, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 can be viewed alongside other therapeutic HPV vaccine platforms in clinical development. Plasmid DNA vaccines delivered with electroporation (e.g., VGX-3100 and GX-188E) have validated E6/E7 as clinical targets, achieving histopathological regression in 23.7% of patients (vs. 11.3% placebo; P = 0.02) and inducing durable immunity lasting 18 months [12, 13]; however, this required high dosing (6 mg) and electroporation. More recently, the non-replicating mRNA vaccine mHTV-03E2 demonstrated robust intratumoral immunity in mice–characterized by multifunctional IFN-γ⁺GzmB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells and a > sixfold expansion of effector-memory subsets–and was evaluated at microgram-range doses (0.3–12.5 μg per mouse) [11]. In contrast, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 achieved complete tumor regression and prophylactic protection at doses as low as 1 and 0.1 µg, respectively. Because these approaches differ in antigen architecture, delivery route/device requirements, and reported endpoints, direct cross-study comparisons are inherently limited; head-to-head evaluations using matched antigens and standardized assays will be important to define relative potency, durability, and safety across platforms [11–13].

Recent work by da Silva et al. [34] directly compared self-amplifying and non-replicating mRNA vaccines encoding HPV16 E6/E7 in the same TC-1 model. Our findings are consistent with and extend those results, showing that JJ-saRNA-HPV01 achieves comparable tumor control within a lower dose range (0.1–1 µg). The 1 µg dose induced near-complete tumor regression and prolonged survival, whereas the tenfold lower 0.1 µg dose, although less durable in the therapeutic setting, conferred complete prophylactic protection (Figs. 2 and 3). More broadly, recent first-in-human data with the optimized srRNA vaccine RBI-4000 further support the translational feasibility of low-dose replicon RNA platforms, demonstrating strong preclinical bioactivity at very low doses and de novo protective immune responses in most participants after 0.1–10 μg dosing in a phase I study, without serious adverse events [38]. Therapeutic survivors in our study also resisted secondary tumor challenge, providing functional evidence of long-lived cellular memory, consistent with previous mRNA vaccine studies demonstrating durable effector and memory formation in vivo [11, 36, 39]. Together, these findings underscore the dose-sparing advantage of saRNA vaccines and support their scalability, safety, and translational potential [38, 40, 41].

IFN-γ can exert a dual role in tumor immunity by supporting cytotoxic effector function while also promoting adaptive immune resistance via JAK2–STAT1–IRF1–dependent induction of PD-L1 within the tumor microenvironment [42, 43]. In JJ-saRNA-HPV01–treated tumors, the increased frequency of IFN-γ–producing CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ TILs coincided with reduced PD-1 expression on both subsets, consistent with enhanced effector activity and diminished inhibitory receptor engagement. However, PD-L1 expression on tumor and/or myeloid compartments was not directly measured, which limits mechanistic interpretation of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and precludes definitive conclusions regarding reversal of T-cell exhaustion; future studies should quantify PD-L1 (e.g., by flow cytometry and/or IHC) and relate it to IFN-γ signaling and clinical correlates. Importantly, vaccine-induced T-cell priming may increase the susceptibility of tumors to checkpoint blockade by promoting a more T-cell–inflamed microenvironment, a phenotype associated with improved response to PD-1 blockade [44]. Consistent with this rationale, neoantigen vaccination has been shown to generate intratumoral T-cell responses in patients [45], and individualized neoantigen therapy combined with pembrolizumab improved recurrence-free survival versus pembrolizumab monotherapy in resected melanoma [46]. In the HPV setting, Peng et al. [9] further showed that a therapeutic HPV16/18 E6/E7 DNA prime–recombinant vaccinia boost regimen synergized with anti–PD-1 treatment in the TC-1 model, improving tumor control and extending survival, whereas anti–PD-1 monotherapy had minimal activity. Accordingly, combining JJ-saRNA-HPV01 with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade represents a rational future direction to mitigate potential PD-L1–mediated adaptive resistance and further enhance durable antitumor immunity [47].

Despite its strong E7-specific cellular immunogenicity, JJ-saRNA-HPV01 elicited comparatively weaker E6-specific responses, as reflected by lower IFN-γ secretion following E6 peptide stimulation after prime/boost immunization (Fig. 2B, C) and at the re-challenge endpoint (Fig. 4D). This discrepancy likely reflects multiple, non-mutually exclusive factors, including intrinsic differences in immunodominance and epitope availability between E6 and E7, as well as potential conformational constraints imposed by the fusion format (e.g., linker-dependent epitope masking) [7, 10]. Consistent with this interpretation, some preclinical HPV16 vaccine studies have reported stronger or more durable protective immunity with E7 than with E6. For example, Li et al. [48] found that immunization with full-length HPV16 E6 or E7 protein both inhibited TC-1 tumor growth, but the E7-immunized mice remained tumor-free longer and showed a more sustained protective effect. Future vaccine iterations should therefore focus on optimizing E6 antigen design or evaluating alternative construct architectures and may also incorporate additional high-risk HPV subtypes (e.g., HPV18) to broaden protection [9].

Moreover, while the TC-1 model remains the benchmark for HPV-targeted immunotherapies, it differs from human tumors in molecular features, E7 expression, and immune contexture. Validation in orthotopic, genetically engineered, or humanized mouse models expressing human leukocyte antigens will therefore be essential for translational relevance [49, 50]. Parallel optimization of lipid nanoparticle formulations may further improve mRNA delivery efficiency, stability, and tissue selectivity while minimizing reactogenicity [50, 51]. A key limitation of this study is that platform-matched comparator controls were not included uniformly across all in vivo experiments. Blank LNP was used throughout; however, the EGFP-expressing self-amplifying RNA negative control (JJ-0020) was not included in the therapeutic efficacy experiment. Because self-amplifying alphavirus RNA can engage innate sensing pathways [19, 20], inclusion of JJ-0020 in this setting would provide additional confidence in attributing observed effects specifically to the HPV16 E6/E7 antigen cassette rather than to any platform-associated effects. In addition, although the non-replicating mRNA control (JJ-023) was used for in vitro expression validation, JJ-023 was not evaluated in vivo; therefore, this work does not provide a direct in vivo comparison between self-amplifying RNA and conventional mRNA encoding the same antigen cassette. Future studies will incorporate both JJ-0020 and JJ-023 comparator arms across immunogenicity and tumor-efficacy experiments. In addition, a limitation of this study is that group sizes were selected based on field precedent and ethical considerations rather than a formal a priori power calculation; future studies will incorporate prospective power analyses tailored to each endpoint and expected variance. Importantly, while re-challenge supports functional recall immunity, memory T-cell subsets were not phenotypically characterized in this study; future work should incorporate longitudinal profiling of antigen-specific memory populations (TCM, TEM, and TRM) to define the durability and tissue localization of protective immunity and to assess potential cross-protection against non-HPV16 tumor types [40, 49].

Conclusions

In conclusion, the self-amplifying mRNA vaccine JJ-saRNA-HPV01 achieved potent therapeutic and prophylactic efficacy at microgram doses (0.1–1 µg) by inducing E7-mediated CD8⁺ T-cell responses, concurrent CD4⁺ activation, enhanced IFN-γ–driven cytotoxic activity, and reduced PD-1–associated exhaustion within the TME. These findings demonstrate that saRNA vaccines can elicit durable, antigen-specific immunity while maintaining a favorable safety and scalability profile. The immunological reprogramming observed in this study provided a mechanistic rationale for combining JJ-saRNA-HPV01 with immune checkpoint blockade to further enhance efficacy. Continued optimization of antigen design and LNP delivery systems will be crucial to translate this low-dose saRNA platform into clinical benefit for HPV-associated malignancies and other viral-driven cancers.
