A comparative study on quality and flavor of plum wine under different aging techniques
Authors: Yuyang Gao, Xingyun Xie, Milin Li, Jiajia Song, Huayi Suo, Zegang Li, Huazhi Huang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compared the effects of four aging techniques, pulsed electric field, ultra-high pressure, cold–hot, and natural aging, on the quality and flavor profile of plum wine. Ethanol and titratable acid contents declined during aging. Cold–hot and pulsed electric field treatments improved color, while ultra-high pressure showed better clarity. Amino acid levels decreased as aging progressed, accompanied by a reduction in bitter amino acids. All treatments exhibited an overall downward trend in antioxidant capacity, and no significant differences were observed among treatments by the end of aging. Furthermore, the cold–hot treatment exhibited higher ester and alcohol contents, associated with floral, fruity and sweet sensory attributes. Compared with natural aging, the flavor and taste attributes of the wines were largely preserved in the other treatments. These findings offer insights into how different aging techniques affect plum wine quality and support targeted flavor modulation strategies.

Introduction

Fermented fruit wines are high-value-added fruit-processed products that are globally popular for their unique flavors, low alcohol content, and rich nutritional content, including polyphenols, amino acids, and organic acids (Lou et al., 2025b). During fermentation, naturally occurring or artificially inoculated yeast converts fruit sugars into ethanol, thereby completing the fermentation process. Each fruit material imparts distinct flavor and taste characteristics to fruit wines. Grape wine, cider, mulberry wine, and plum wine are some of the common fermented fruit wines. Yinhong is a plum variety that is extensively cultivated in Sichuan, China. This variety is processed into plum wine owing to its susceptibility to postharvest spoilage and short shelf life (Ye et al., 2025). To further harmonize the wine body, improve flavor, and enhance final quality, plum wine must be stored for a period of time, a process known as aging (Ma et al., 2022).

Aging is a critical step in producing premium fermented fruit wines as it enhances fruit aromas and phenolic content and optimizes the mouthfeel (Zhang et al., 2023). Conventional aging in oak barrels usually takes several months to complete, which is time-consuming and restricts the production scale of wineries (Ma et al., 2022). For this reason, a variety of artificial aging treatments have been widely investigated in recent studies. For instance, red wines treated using high hydrostatic pressure (also called ultra-high pressure [UHP]) do not exhibit any differences in colorless phenol compounds (Sun et al., 2016). Pulsed electric field (PEF) treatment effectively reduces the concentration of SO₂ in wine while augmenting its color attributes (González-Arenzana et al., 2019). Microwave treatment can promote the release of oak-derived aroma compounds, with ultrasound assistance contributing to improved sensory perception of aged aromas (Muñoz-García et al., 2023). In addition, micro‑oxygenation and electric field treatment can increase the content of flavor compounds in yellow wine (Shen et al., 2021). These studies provide useful references for the application of physical treatments in fruit wine aging and quality regulation.

Studies concerning artificial physical treatments for plum wine aging remain limited. It is necessary to explore the effects of different treatments on the quality changes of plum wine during aging. To fill this research gap, this study systematically compared the effects of four artificial aging methods — PEF, UHP, temperature cycling, and natural aging—on the physicochemical properties, visual characteristics, bioactive components, antioxidant activity, flavor compound composition, and sensory performance of plum wine. This work aimed to explore the effects of these aging techniques on the quality and flavor characteristics of plum wine. The findings are expected to provide theoretical support and practical guidance for the application of these artificial treatments in plum wine production.

Materials and methods

Preparation of plum wine

Seasonal Yinhong Plum, were harvested in July 2025 from Yibin, Sichuan, China (28°42′N, 103°58′E). Saccharomyces cerevisiae FR (2.3 × 1010 CFU/g) and Oenococcus oeni (2.3 × 109 CFU/g) were purchased from OENOFRANCE® in France. Fully ripe plums (13°Brix) were pitted and crushed and then treated with pectinase (0.2 g/kg) and potassium metabisulfite (0.1 g/kg) in a 45 °C water bath for 2 h. Subsequently, the Brix level was adjusted to 30°Brix using sucrose. The mixture was inoculated with active dry starter FR at 1.5 g/kg, followed by O. oeni (10 mg/kg) after 48 h. The mixture was then fermented at 28 °C for 6 days and centrifuged at 9000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant wine served as the aged raw wine. This wine was then subjected to different aging treatments: (1) Natural aging (CK): No treatment applied; (2) PEF: Processed for 10 min at 15 kV voltage and 10 kHz frequency, pulse width 30 ms, within operating temperature range 15–35 °C; (3) UHP: Raw wine was placed in a PE + PET composite bag and processed for 25 min at 300 MPa; (4) Cold-hot (CH): The treatment consisted of three repeated temperature cycles: each cycle comprised 1 day at 40 °C and 1 subsequent day at 4 °C. After three cycles, the wine was placed under consistent aging conditions. The numbers after grouping signify the aging time. All samples were divided into three sets and stored in amber glass bottles at 10 °C. The samples were collected from all groups at 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 days of aging for subsequent analyses.

Ethanol, titratable acidity, and reducing sugar

The ethanol content was determined using an alcohol meter (0–40 v/v; Chuangji Instrument Factory, Hebei, China) based on the method referenced in Chinese National Standard GB 5009.225–2023. The titratable acidity content was measured using a 0.01 mol/L NaOH standard solution with reference to the Chinese National Standard GB 12456–2021. The reducing sugar content was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (D7800; Solarbio, China).

Color characteristics, turbidity, and clarity

The absorbance values were recorded using a Gen5™ microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX; Agilent, USA). Colorimetric measurements and hue assessments were conducted based on the method described by Chira et al. (2012). Turbidity and clarity of the plum wine were measured at wavelengths of 580 nm and 680 nm, respectively.

Non-volatile flavor compounds

Organic acids

The organic acid content was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (UltiMate 3000 HPLC; Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled with a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent, USA). After centrifugation and filtration, 10 μL of the sample was injected for analysis. The mobile phase was composed of 0.05 mol/L KH₂PO₄ (pH 2.0) and methanol in a 97:3 (v/v) ratio and maintained at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. A 10-μL sample volume was injected onto the column (30 °C). Detection was performed at 210-nm wavelength, and the composition and content of organic acids were quantified by using an external standard method.

Amino acids

The amino acid content was determined by using a previously reported method (Li et al., 2024). The samples were derivatized with phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) and triethylamine and subsequently separated and quantified by using an HPLC system (LC-20 A; Shimadzu, Japan). Chromatographic separation was performed using a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent). Analytical parameters were as follows: flow rate (0.8 mL/min), detection wavelength (254 nm), column temperature (35 °C), and injection volume (20 μL).

Bioactive compounds and antioxidant capacity

Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolic content was determined by the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent method. Briefly, 0.1 mL of the sample was taken, to which 0.1 mL of ultrapure water and 1.0 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent were added. After thorough mixing, the reaction was allowed to proceed at 25 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 3.0 mL of 7.5% Na₂CO3 was immediately added. After mixing, the mixture was allowed to stand in the dark for 30 min. Finally, the absorbance was recorded at 760 nm. The total phenolic content was expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE).

Determination of the total flavonoid content

The total flavonoid content was determined by using a previously reported method (Chen et al., 2023). The sample (0.5 mL) was mixed with 0.5 mL of ultrapure water and 0.3 mL of 5.0% NaNO2 solution and incubated for 6 min. Then, 0.3 mL of 7.5% AlCl3 was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 30 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of 4% NaOH solution was added to terminate the reaction. The absorbance was recorded at 510 nm, and the total flavonoid content was expressed as rutin equivalents (RE).

Determination of anthocyanin content

The determination of anthocyanin content was based on the method reported by Sui et al. (2024a) with some modifications. Briefly, 75 μL of the sample was pipetted into 675 μL of hydrochloric acid–methanol solution (1:99), mixed thoroughly, and allowed to stand at 4 °C in the dark for 20 min. Subsequently, the absorbance of the mixture was measured at 530 nm and 600 nm wavelengths. The anthocyanin content was calculated based on the difference in the absorbance between these two wavelengths.

Determination of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC)

The T-AOC assay kit (G0115W48; Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of the sample. In a 96-well plate, 5 μL of the sample was sequentially added to 25 μL of distilled water and 170 μL of the color development solution. The solution was mixed thoroughly and allowed to stand for 10 min at room temperature. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 590 nm. The T-AOC results were expressed as the equivalent amount of the antioxidant Trolox.

Volatile flavor compounds

The volatile compounds in the samples were extracted and identified using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS). Extraction was performed using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber. Accurately measured 5 mL of sample was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by the addition of 1.0 g NaCl and 10 μL methyl heptanoate (58.6 mg/L) as the internal standard. The vial was sealed and equilibrated at 50 °C for 15 min, followed by extraction under the same conditions for 30 min. After extraction, the sample was desorbed at the GC inlet at 250 °C for 5 min. Gas chromatography separation was performed on a GC–MS instrument (8890-5977B, Agilent) equipped with an HB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm; Agilent). High-purity helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injection mode was splitless. The temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C held for 3 min, followed by a ramp of 4 °C/min to 150 °C held for 2 min, and then a further ramp of 10 °C/min to 250 °C held for 5 min. Mass spectrometry detection conditions included the following: ion source temperature of 250 °C, electron impact energy of 70 eV, and a scan range of 40–500 m/z. Compound identification was performed by measuring the retention indices (RI) using C7–C40 n-alkane standards (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and comparing the results with reference data from the NIST 23 standard library.

Sensory evaluation of fuzzy mathematical methods

A fuzzy mathematical model was established based on the method of Xin et al. (2025) for analysis. The evaluation factors comprise four key attributes: aroma (V1), taste (V2), appearance (V3), and typicality (V4), forming the evaluation factor set V = {V1, V2, V3, V4}. The corresponding evaluation set is defined as K = {Excellent, Good, Poor}, with corresponding scores of (90, 70, and 50). The weighting set is C = {0.3, 0.4, 0.1, 0.2}, with aroma and taste being the key factors that influence sensory performance. The sensory quality of the final plum wine was evaluated by a panel of ten professionals (aged ≥18) from the College of Food Science at Southwest University, all of whom possessed sound theoretical knowledge and rich experience in sensory evaluation. Prior to the evaluation, a dedicated training session was organized to ensure panelists reached a consensus on the application of fuzzy mathematical models and the specific evaluation attributes. All evaluations were carried out in the sensory laboratory under strict blind tasting conditions, in accordance with the wine sensory evaluation criteria described in GB/T 15037–2006. All samples were maintained at (25 ± 1) °C during evaluation. Between samples, the assessors rinsed their mouths with plain water and consumed soda biscuits to minimize the influence of residual flavors. The votes for each grade were tallied, and the fuzzy relationship matrix R was obtained by dividing each vote count by the total votes. The comprehensive sensory quality evaluation result for the sample, X = C × R, was then derived. Finally, the comprehensive sensory evaluation result was multiplied by the corresponding grade score to obtain the specific sensory score for the sample. This study was conducted under the continuous supervision of the Ethics Committee of the College of Food Science, Southwest University, and was ethically approved (Approval No. HF20251003). Each member of the sensory panel signed an informed consent statement before the sensory evaluation begins. In addition, the rights and privacy of all participants were safeguarded throughout this study.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corporation, New York, NY). Significant differences at p < 0.05 were determined through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's post-hoc HSD test. The figures were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), TBtools (Toolbox for Biologists; Version 1.082, China), and Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/heatmap.html). All experiments were conducted in triplicate with independent aging batch, and the data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Ethanol, titratable acidity, and reducing sugar contents

Ethanol content is a key indicator for assessing the style, quality, and sensory characteristics of plum wine. During aging, a series of physicochemical reactions occur in the wine body. These include the oxidation of ethanol, esterification reactions resulting in the formation of flavor compounds, and a slow evaporation of ethanol (Yan et al., 2024). In this study, the ethanol content exhibited an unstable decline across most treatment groups as aging progressed (Table 1). Compared with the beginning of aging (day 0), the content of ethanol in the cold–hot group declined significantly on day 60 (p < 0.05). The heating stage may promote ethanol degradation or accelerate the formation of other substances, such as esters, enriching the flavor of plum wine (Lou et al., 2025a). Although the ethanol levels in the UHP and cold–hot groups on day 60 were lower than those in the natural aging group, the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Titratable acidity is the source of the sour taste of fruit wine, which contributes prominently to the taste and flavor profile of fruit wines (Che et al., 2024). The titratable acidity of the samples ranged from 6.03 g/L to 6.51 g/L, displaying a consistent overall decline during aging. According to previous studies, ester synthesis involving acids during wine aging and the promotion of the malate–lactate pathway by O. oeni are likely the primary factors responsible for this phenomenon (Yang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2022). At the end of aging, the titratable acidity in the cold–hot group decreased significantly (p < 0.05) but did not differ significantly compared with other groups at the same time point (p > 0.05). The reducing sugar content increased gradually across all treatments. Even a minor increase in sugar content can imporve the palatability and organoleptic characteristics of the final aged plum wine. Color characteristics, turbidity, and clarity

The color characteristics and clarification status of plum wine throughout the aging cycle are depicted in Fig. 1. In quantitative color description, chroma is proportional to lightness, with higher chroma values representing higher color intensities perceptible to the human eye (Canas et al., 2022). As illustrated in Fig. 1-A, raw wine had a low color value (0.86). As aging progressed, the color intensities of all plum wine treatments decreased slightly from day 0 to day 60, ultimately ranging between 0.8 and 0.87. The PEF and cold–hot groups exhibited significantly higher chroma values than the other two groups (p < 0.05), indicating the beneficial effects of these treatments on color stability during plum wine aging. The hue value is defined as the ratio between yellow (420 nm) and red (520 nm) (Martínez-Moreno and Bautista-Ortín, 2025). The change in the hue pattern was similar across all treatments and demonstrated an overall decrease (Fig. 1-B). No significant differences were observed after day 60 (p > 0.05). The changes in the turbidity of plum wine were presented in Fig. 1-C. Turbidity increased in all samples between day 0 and day 30, decreasing gradually and stabilizing thereafter. This finding reflects the transformation of the early colloidal system in plum wine from instability to settlement and clarification. Turbidity in the cold–hot group remained at high levels throughout the aging process (day 0 to day 30), differing significantly compared with natural aging and UHP groups (p < 0.05). The instability resulting from temperature fluctuations may aggregate the proteins in the wine matrix, thereby increasing the turbidity (Dumitriu et al., 2018). UHP treatment and natural aging exhibited more stable colloidal evolution. Regarding clarity during aging, the overall trend was opposite to that of turbidity (Fig. 1-D). Clarity in the PEF group declined continuously, whereas that in the cold–hot group decreased initially and then recovered. The UHP group showed excellent clarity throughout the aging process, consistent with the findings of Santos et al. (2013).

Nonvolatile flavor compounds

Organic acid content

Organic acids contribute to the taste and overall flavor profile of fruit wine (Zheng et al., 2025). Therefore, the composition of organic acids and the dynamic changes in it during the aging process of plum wine must be analyzed. The total organic acid content exhibited an overall declining trend (Fig. 2-A), with a transient increase observed on day 10. This temporary acid elevation arose from metabolic activities of residual indigenous microorganisms, which generate organic acids in the early aging period The esterification reaction continues during the aging process, leading to a steady decrease in total organic acid content. During the entire aging period, the concentration of lactic acid remained high, mitigating astringency and harmonizing the body of the wine. Moreover, it imparts distinctive aromatic properties to the liquor, such as flavors reminiscent of butter, cream, and cheese, via specific biochemical reactions (Sun et al., 2024). The malic acid content remained stable during the first 30 days of aging, but decreased in the PEF and UHP groups by day 60. The observed reduction in citric acid concentration could be explained by its involvement in esterification reactions, either promoted by chemical processes or by enzymatic activity during the aging of plum wine (Zagrodzki et al., 2023). The content of succinic acid, which can produce fruity esters via esterification and enhance the sensory quality of the wine body, decreased sharply after day 10 and was undetected after day 20 (Torres-Guardado et al., 2024). The levels of both oxalic and tartaric acid remained relatively stable during aging. Amino acid content

Free amino acids act as key precursors for numerous flavor compounds, exerting both direct and indirect effects on the flavor of fermented foods and playing a crucial role in the overall sensory quality (L. Li et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025). Dynamic alterations in the amino acid composition of plum wine during aging are presented in Table S2 and Fig. 2-B. The total amino acid content of plum wine increased from 556.16 mg/L to 735.76–919.59 mg/L (day 0 to day 10). The total amino acid content peaked on day 20 (highest in the CH20 group at 1364.8 mg/L), declined by day 30, and dropped markedly by the end of aging (day 60), a trend that was prominent in the cold–hot group (70.64 mg/L). Before the completion of the aging process, the primary amino acids in plum wine were arginine, cysteine, glutamine, alanine, and aspartic acid. Based on taste characteristics, the amino acids in plum wine can be categorized into tasteless, bitter, sweet, and umami amino acids (Meng et al., 2024). The dynamic changes in amino acid composition clearly reflected flavor transformation (Fig. 2-C). In the early stage (day 0 to day 30), bitter amino acids were predominant, accounting for 79%–91% of the total amino acid content. However, as aging progressed, their proportion fell considerably to 38%–46%. Tartrate precipitates interact with free amino acids in the wine matrix, enhancing the complexity of flavor and smoothness of texture during the aging (Zhang et al., 2026). This may explain the phenomenon. The proportion of tasteless amino acids increased from 5% to 47%–57%, whereas that of sweet amino acids remained relatively stable. Umami amino acids became undetectable by the end of aging. These shifts, particularly the reduction in bitter amino acids, are directly linked to the balanced overall flavor of the aged plum wine.

Bioactive compounds and antioxidant capacity

Total phenols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins are collectively responsible for the antioxidant activity of fruit wine. The core mechanism behind their health-beneficial effects is the attenuation of cellular oxidative damage via free radical scavenging (Sui et al., 2024b). The total phenol content increased in all groups after 10 days of aging and peaked on day 30, which was the highest in the PEF group at 1.17 mg/mL, and stabilized by day 60 (Fig. 3-A). The initial increase was due to the conversion of bound phenolics to their free forms by polyphenol hydrolases secreted by residual microorganisms, resulting in higher detection values (Lian et al., 2025). The stabilization of phenolic compounds in the later stage of aging implies that a dynamic equilibrium might have been attained in their oxidation and precipitation reactions. The total flavonoid content (Fig. 3-B) exhibited a similar pattern of initial increase and subsequent decrease in all groups, with the content of the PEF group being significantly lower than that of the other groups on day 30 (p < 0.05). By the end of aging, the total flavonoid content had increased overall by 30.19%–32.08% compared with that on day 0. Flavonoids are a class of compounds that exhibit various bioactive functions (Hao et al., 2025), and the aging process appears to provide favorable conditions for their accumulation in plum wine. Despite fluctuations, a clear downward trend in total anthocyanins was observed during aging (Fig. 3-C), with no significant differences among treatments by the end of aging (p < 0.05). Anthocyanins, the primary colorants, are chemically unstable and prone to degradation and polymerization during aging, leading to a net loss in content and color intensity (Wu et al., 2022). These reactions are temperature-sensitive, especially under bottled storage conditions (Gómez-Plaza et al., 2002), potentially explaining why the cold–hot group consistently showed lower anthocyanin levels than the other three groups from day 10 to day 30. The T-AOC of plum wine was evaluated using the Ferric Reducing Ability of Plasma assay, which exhibited an overall decline during aging (Fig. 3-D). This decrease was consistent with the trend in total anthocyanin levels, suggesting that the diminished antioxidant activity was primarily due to anthocyanin degradation. The key contribution of anthocyanins lies in their ability to act as hydrogen donors, effectively quenching free radicals and interrupting chain reactions, thereby alleviating oxidative stress (Wu et al., 2022). Aging led to a decrease in the Trolox value from an initial 4.36 ± 0.24 μmol/mL across all treatments (PEF: 2.52%–30.28%; UHP: 4.82%–33.03%; cold–hot: 10.55%–23.39%; natural aging: 6.42%–26.83%). Notably, after 60 days of aging, the cold–hot group maintained relatively a high Trolox value compared to others. As observed in studies by Lan et al. (2017), antioxidant capacity showed an overall downward trend during the aging process. Aging naturally led to this phenomenon, and the differences in antioxidant activity between the treatments were limited.

Volatile flavor compounds

Quantity and composition of volatile flavor compounds

To elucidate the effects of different aging techniques on the flavor formation of plum wine, volatile flavor compounds in the samples were quantitatively analyzed using GC–MS. As depicted in Fig. 4-A, 34, 53, 52, 40, and 54 volatile flavor compounds were identified in the samples on day 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 of aging, respectively. A total of 23 compounds were consistently detected at all five time points, suggesting their role as the fundamental flavor base of plum wine throughout the aging process (Fig. 4A). During the four stages of aging, seven compounds were collectively detected, including ethyl 9-hexadecenoate, ethyl stearate, benzaldehyde, 1-octanol, (R)-(+)-beta-citronellol, 1-decanol, and linalool. The aging process might have enhanced their formation (Table S3). The species and quantity distribution of volatile flavor compounds at each time point are presented in Fig. 4-B. Esters, alcohols, and aldehydes–ketones were consistently the predominant flavor components. Of these, esters showed the most pronounced increase in diversity, with their total number rising by 88% by the end of aging compared with that on day 0, likely due to enhanced esterification reactions during aging. Plum wines aged using the four techniques (PEF, UHP, cold–hot, and natural aging) contained 64, 63, 63, and 59 flavor compounds, respectively (Table S4–S7). In the initial stage of aging (day 0), the flavor components were dominated by esters. Ethyl esters (such as phenethyl acetate, ethyl caprylate, and ethyl caprate) were particularly prominent. These ethyl esters produce pleasant floral and fruity aromas in oak-aged brandy, suggesting a similar role in shaping the flavor profile of plum wine in this study (Duan et al., 2024). Phenylethyl alcohol was the most abundant flavor component during aging, and this alcohol has been recognized as a key aroma substance in brandy (Coldea et al., 2020). Beta-damascenone, alpha-ionone, and beta-ionone were the primary aldehyde and ketone compounds in aging plum wine. In addition, two acids, three phenols, and three other compounds were detected in the samples, further enriching the overall flavor profile. An obvious declining trend in the total volatile content was observed from day 10 to day 60 in all groups (PEF: from 10,092.83 μg/L to 1060.16 μg/L; UHP: from 9159.82 μg/L to 818.66 μg/L; cold–hot: from 7678.38 μg/L to 1246.63 μg/L; natural aging: from 23,022.40 μg/L to 591.57 μg/L). Particularly, the cold-hot treatment showed relatively higher levels of esters and alcohols by the end of aging (511.40 μg/L) and alcohols (644.28 μg/L). At suitable temperatures, the formation of organic acid esters in aged wine is enhanced (Cejudo-Bastante et al., 2013). The higher ester content observed in cold-heat treatment in this study may be attributed to the heat treatment providing the energy required for esterification reactions, consequently accelerating the reaction rate. Nonetheless, this change is often accompanied by the volatilization and hydrolysis of certain alcohols and unstable aromatic components (Ma et al., 2022). Specifically, the natural aging group and UHP group exhibited high intragroup similarity and clear intergroup separation, establishing the consistent effects of these treatments on the metabolite profiles. In contrast, the PEF group and the cold–hot group presented a more complex clustering pattern. This observation suggests that under these treatments, competition or synergy may exist among multiple metabolic pathways, resulting in a nonlinear dynamic characteristic of substance transformation. Overall, the trends in volatile compound changes in plum wine under different aging techniques were generally consistent. All treatment groups exhibited an obvious decrease in aromatic components as aging progressed. Particularly, the concentrations of esters, alcohols, and aldehydes–ketones remained high throughout the aging process, asserting their crucial role in the recombination and balance of flavor in plum wine. Ethyl esters, predominantly phenethyl acetate, ethyl caprylate, ethyl caprate, and ethyl laurate, were prominent across all samples, mainly due to ongoing esterification reactions during aging. Of the various alcohols, phenethyl alcohol and 1-nonanol were the principal representatives, and their concentrations were high during the entire aging period. Aldehydes–ketones showed a consistent decline from the beginning of aging. At the end of aging, benzaldehyde emerged as the most abundant aldehyde, indicating that it is retained during the aging process. This compound enhances the herbal and almond-like flavor characteristics in plum wine (X. Li et al., 2025). Furthermore, eugenol and 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol were detected during aging. The concentration of 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol was elevated on day 30, increasing almost 4–9 times compared with that at the start of the aging process. Odor activity value (OAV) analysis

The OAV, defined as the ratio of the concentration of a compound to its odor threshold, was used in this study to identify the key aroma compounds (OAV >1) in plum wine (Pu et al., 2023). The OAVs of 20 compounds, including seven esters, five aldehydes, three ketones, three alcohols, one phenol, and one sulfur-containing compound, were maintained at >1 during aging (Table S8). The OAVs of these compounds were not static but strongly influenced by the specific aromatic substance itself and the duration of aging (Fig. 5-E). During aging, the OAVs of ethyl caprylate (grape), beta-damascenone (apple, rose, and honey), beta-ionone (seaweed, violet, flower, and raspberry), and phenylethyl alcohol (honey, spice, rose, and lilac) were consistently >1. Compounds with high OAVs of >100, including beta-damascenone, alpha-ionone (wood and violet), beta-ionone, phenylethyl alcohol, and dodecanal (lily, fat, and citrus), were likely to play an important role in shaping the fundamental aroma profile of plum wine. Meanwhile, compounds with OAVs of >10, such as isoamyl acetate (banana), ethyl caprylate (fruit and fat), phenethyl acetate (rose, honey, and tobacco), ethyl salicylate (wintergreen and mint), and ethyl (E)-cinnamate (flower and honey), may contribute notably to the overall aroma intensity.

Different aging techniques effectively modulated the OAVs of key aroma compounds in plum wine. Specifically, phenethyl acetate maintained an OAV of >1 until day 60 in both PEF and cold–hot groups. Similarly, ethyl salicylate was detected in both the PEF (OAV = 4.17) and cold–hot (OAV = 12.74) groups, suggesting a potential contribution to the preservation of floral and herbal characteristics. Benzeneacetaldehyde showed high OAVs on day 10 in PEF (32.33) and UHP (41.48) groups; and, by day 60, it was only detected OAVs in the UHP and cold–hot groups. Cold–hot treatment resulted in much higher OAV values for beta-damascenone, beta-ionone, and phenylethyl alcohol (reaching 2105, 252.86, and 212.35, respectively) compared with the other groups on day 60. These results suggest that the cold–hot treatment may be particularly effective in maintaining relatively higher OAVs for certain floral, fruity, and sweet-associated compounds, indicating its potential to modulate the aromatic profile of aged plum wine.

Fuzzy mathematical sensory evaluation

Based on the sensory evaluation panel of 10 individuals for four samples (Table S9), in which the data represent the number of evaluators at each rating level, the fuzzy relationship matrix was established as follows:

RCK (natural aging)=0.60.400.60.30.10.90.100.60.20.2, RPEF (pulsed electric field)=0.50.500.60.30.10.80.200.60.40,

RUHP (ultra-high pressure)=0.60.20.20.50.40.10.70.300.50.50, RCH (cold–hot) =0.50.500.40.50.10.80.200.30.60.1.

The comprehensive sensory evaluation was computed by performing matrix composition and fuzzy transformation operations, defined by X = C × R. The resulting overall assessment of sensory quality for all samples is as follows: XCK = (0.63, 0.29, 0.08); XPEF = (0.59, 0.37, 0.04); XUHP = (0.55, 0.35, 0.10); XCH = (0.45, 0.49, 0.06). The overall sensory scores calculated using the formula Y = X × K were YCK = 81; YPEF = 81; YUHP = 79; YCH = 77.8.

The advantage of using fuzzy mathematics for sensory data lies in its ability to mitigate panelist subjectivity (Wang et al., 2025), providing reliable data for the objective comparison and accurate discrimination of sensory attributes among plum wines. This approach improves the scientific validity, rationality, and objectivity of the sensory evaluation. The results indicate that natural aging and PEF treatment showed relatively superior sensory performance, with the CH treatment group exhibiting slightly lower overall acceptability, possibly attributable to the extreme temperature fluctuations. Consistent with the findings of previous research, different artificial aging techniques present varied effects on wine sensory attributes. Several studies have established the efficacy of different artificial aging techniques. The study by Puértolas et al. (2010) confirmed the advantage of PEF in preserving sensory quality as the treated wine did not develop any off-flavors. In contrast, Santos et al. (2019) suggested that while the UHP treatment well preserves color and taste, its potential impact on the overall aroma profile warrants attention. Although artificial treatments alter the chemical composition of the aromatic compounds, the overall sensory scores indicate that the resulting plum wine exhibits no significant advantage in terms of overall aroma and taste profile compared to natural aging.

Conclusion

This study systematically assessed the effects of different aging techniques on the physicochemical properties, appearance, bioactive compounds, antioxidant capacity, and volatile and nonvolatile flavor compounds of plum wine. The findings showed that cold–hot treatment significantly influenced the contents of ethanol and total acid and that cold–hot and PEF treatments contributed to better color stability. During aging, organic acids and amino acids gradually decreased, along with a reduction in bitter amino acids. The contents of total phenols and total flavonoids increased after aging, whereas anthocyanin levels decreased. Antioxidant capacity presented a downward trend in all groups, and the cold–hot group only maintained relatively higher antioxidant activity in the late stage without significant differences among treatments. In terms of flavor substances, total volatile compounds decreased obviously during aging. The cold–hot group possessed higher levels of esters and alcohols. A total of 20 key aroma compounds with OAVs >1 were identified, among which beta-damascenone, beta-ionone, and phenylethyl alcohol showed the highest OAVs. On day 60, these compounds made the greatest contribution to the overall aroma in the cold-hot treatment group. Sensory evaluation indicated that the overall sensory quality of artificial treatments showed no obvious superiority over the natural aging. The findings provide a systematic understanding of how different aging techniques affect plum wine quality during short-term aging. This study provides a reliable reference for the application of artificial treatments in plum wine production and aging regulation.
