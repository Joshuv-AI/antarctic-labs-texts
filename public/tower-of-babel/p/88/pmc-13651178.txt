Harnessing tumor-associated exosomal aminopeptidase N as a dual-function tool for monitoring and tackling chemoresistance in gastric cancer
Authors: Wangyu Cai, Zheru Ma, Wen Xie, Ende Lin, Shengyuan Zheng, Lingyun Lin, Yuanyuan Xie, Zinan Dong, Peiyu Lv, Jia Cheng, Li Yang, Xiaoteng Fu, Fuxing Zhang, Zengpeng Li, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemoresistance remains a major obstacle to effective treatment in gastric cancer (GC), with a notable scarcity of clinically applicable biomarkers and therapeutic targets. In this study, we employed an integrated proteomic approach analyzing paired serum samples from GC patients at disease control and progressive disease stages during chemotherapy, exosomes from chemoresistant and chemosensitive GC cells, and corresponding whole-cell proteomes to identify tumor-associated exosomal drivers of chemoresistance. Cross-dataset integration identified aminopeptidase N (APN/CD13) as selectively enriched under chemoresistant conditions. Functional validation demonstrated that APN depletion via shRNA or CRISPR restored cisplatin sensitivity in refractory cells, whereas APN overexpression conferred chemoprotection. A neutralizing anti-APN monoclonal antibody resensitized resistant cells and showed superior efficacy compared with the broad-spectrum peptidase inhibitor bestatin in vivo. Mechanistically, APN sustained a ferroptosis-suppressive state: its inhibition increased ROS and lipid peroxidation, shifted transcriptomic profiles toward pro-ferroptotic pathways, and downregulated the lipid peroxide detoxifier AKR1C2, which correlated with poor prognosis and APN expression in the TCGA-STAD cohort. Exosomal transfer assays confirmed that APN-enriched vesicles disseminated chemoresistance and ferroptosis evasion to sensitive cells, accelerating tumor progression during chemotherapy, whereas APN-deficient exosomes lacked this capacity. Clinically, serum exosomal APN levels robustly distinguished progressive disease from disease control in a cohort of 105 patients (AUC = 0.85; optimal cutoff 0.936 ng/mL). Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks among the most aggressive human malignancies, featuring prominently within the top five cancers globally in both incidence and mortality [1–3]. Chemoresistance remains a major clinical barrier in GC management, driving disease progression and compromising therapeutic efficacy [4, 5]. Currently, chemoresistance assessment relies primarily on RECIST 1.1 criteria from imaging follow-ups and serum tumor marker detection, often resulting in delayed diagnosis [6, 7]. This diagnostic lag frequently leads to disease progression before patients can transition to alternative therapies. Therefore, the identification of reliable biomarkers for real-time, non-invasive chemoresistance monitoring, coupled with the development of targeted therapeutic strategies, is critical for optimizing precision treatment in GC.

Exosomes are naturally secreted by various cell types and exhibit high stability in bodily fluids, establishing them as key mediators of intercellular communication and disease progression [8–10]. Hence, they represent promising candidates for both biomarkers and therapeutic targets [11–13]. Tumor-associated exosomes, which are enriched with bioactive molecules like RNAs and proteins, have been implicated in acquiring and disseminating drug resistance after multi-cycle therapies [14–17]. For example, Qu et al. demonstrated that exosomal lncARSR mediates the transfer of sunitinib resistance in renal cell carcinoma [18]. Although most previous studies have focused on exosomal RNAs, proteomic components are increasingly recognized for their direct functional roles in modulating tumor microenvironment interactions [19–22], highlighting their potential for exosome-based diagnostics and therapy. Nevertheless, the specific exosomal proteins and mechanisms driving chemoresistance in GC remain poorly understood.

In this study, we employed chemoresistant samples from both cellular and clinical sources to conduct a systematic proteomic analysis, through which we identified tumor-associated exosomal protein profiles linked to chemoresistance in gastric cancer. Our functional investigations revealed that elevated exosomal Aminopeptidase N (APN), secreted by chemoresistant GC cells, facilitates the transfer of chemoresistance to susceptible tumor cells and suppresses ferroptosis during chemotherapy. Furthermore, our translational findings highlight serum exosomal APN as a vital biomarker for non-invasive chemoresistance detection and propose highly specific APN blocking antibodies as a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance in clinical practice.

Results

Integrated proteomic analysis identifies candidate tumor-associated exosomal drivers of gastric cancer chemoresistance

Based on the integrated proteomic workflow outlined in Fig. 1, we identified tumor-associated exosomal proteins associated with chemoresistance in gastric cancer. Our analysis integrated three complementary datasets (Table S1): (1) paired serum exosomes from 10 gastric cancer patients at disease control (DC) and progressive disease (PD) stages during chemotherapy (Table S2); (2) exosomes from cisplatin-resistant SGC-7901/DDP cells versus their sensitive SGC-7901 counterparts (n = 3 biological replicates per group); and (3) whole-cell proteomes of SGC-7901/DDP versus SGC-7901 cells (n = 3 biological replicates per group). Through cross-dataset intersection of consistently upregulated proteins under chemoresistant conditions, we identified four overlapping hits enriched in exosomes from both chemoresistant cells and PD patient sera, including Aminopeptidase N (APN/CD13), CD82, SET-binding factor 1 (SBF1), and vitronectin (VTN), suggesting their potential role as drivers of chemoresistance in gastric cancer.

Multi-model screening identifies serum exosomal APN as a clinical biomarker for monitoring chemoresistance in gastric cancer

To functionally characterize the top candidates, we performed shRNA knockdown of APN, CD82, SBF1, and VTN in SGC-7901/DDP cells and assessed cisplatin sensitivity by CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2A). APN knockdown significantly reduced cell viability compared with the Ctrl shRNA group across all DDP concentrations tested (2–10 µg/ml, all P < 0.0001, two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test). In contrast, knockdown of CD82, SBF1, or VTN did not consistently alter cell viability, with most groups showing no significant difference from the Ctrl shRNA group at the majority of concentrations. These results suggest a specific role for APN in mediating chemoresistance in this experimental setting. Western blot analysis further confirmed consistent APN upregulation in chemoresistant GC cell lines independently established against cisplatin, 5-FU, or paclitaxel, compared with their drug-naïve counterparts (Fig. 2B). Notably, exosomes isolated from the conditioned media of chemoresistant cells were also highly enriched in APN, whereas those from chemosensitive cells showed minimal APN content (Fig. 2C). To validate these findings in vivo, we utilized our previously established cisplatin-resistant xenograft model (MGC80-3 CDX) [23] and observed that both tumor tissues and circulating serum exosomes exhibited progressive APN elevation with successive chemotherapy cycles, while cycle-matched untreated controls maintained consistently low expression levels (Fig. 2D).

Translating these findings to the clinical setting, we evaluated the potential of serum exosomal APN as a chemoresistance biomarker in a cohort of 105 gastric cancer patients undergoing conventional chemotherapy (50 with progressive disease [PD] and 55 with disease control [DC]). Transmission electron microscopy and NanoFCM analysis (Figure S1) confirmed that the isolated serum exosomes exhibited characteristic cup-shaped morphology within the 50–150 nm diameter range and showed typical size distribution profiles. Using ELISA, we quantified serum exosomal APN levels and found significantly higher concentrations in PD patients (1.175 ± 0.471 ng/ml) than in DC patients (0.567 ± 0.290 ng/ml) (Fig. 2E; P < 0.0001, Mann-Whitney U test). ROC analysis established an optimal cutoff value of 0.936 ng/mL as determined by the Youden index, with an area under the curve of 0.8549 (95% CI: 0.778–0.932, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2F). Together, these results support serum exosomal APN as a promising biomarker for monitoring chemoresistance in gastric cancer.

Ferroptosis-associated signaling downstream of APN correlates with chemoresistance and adverse clinical outcomes in gastric cancer

To further validate the functional role of APN in chemoresistance, we modulated its expression in cellular models. Overexpression of APN in chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells significantly enhanced cell survival during cisplatin treatment, as measured by real-time cellular analysis (Fig. 3A). In contrast, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of APN in cisplatin-resistant SGC-7901/DDP cells substantially reversed the resistant phenotype (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, treatment with a neutralizing APN monoclonal antibody significantly restored cisplatin sensitivity in wild-type resistant cells (Fig. 3C, WT + α-APN vs. WT + IgG) but showed no effect in APN-knockout cells (Fig. 3C, APN-KO groups), confirming the essential role of APN in maintaining chemoresistance.

We performed RNA sequencing of chemoresistant cells after APN blockade to identify transcriptomic alterations linked to APN-mediated chemoresistance. Pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes identified ferroptosis among the top 10 enriched pathways following APN inhibition (Fig. 3D, left). Given that APN has been previously implicated in ROS regulation in cancer [24–27] and that ferroptosis is a ROS-driven cell death program with lipid peroxidation as its direct execution mechanism [28], we further examined the transcriptomic data for ferroptosis-related gene signatures. Consistent with the pathway enrichment, APN inhibition produced a distinct shift toward a pro-ferroptotic gene expression profile (Fig. 3D, right). In SGC-7901/DDP cells pretreated with APN-blocking antibody prior to cisplatin exposure, the canonical ferroptosis marker (PTGS2, green) and ferroptosis-promoting genes (red) were significantly upregulated, while key ferroptosis defense genes (blue) were coordinately downregulated in the α-APN group compared with the IgG control (Fig. 3D, right).

To assess the clinical relevance of these findings, we examined associations among APN expression, ferroptosis-related genes, and patient outcomes in public datasets. In the TCGA-STAD gastric cancer cohort, elevated APN expression correlated with shorter overall survival (Fig. 3E, left). Among the ferroptosis-related transcripts identified in our transcriptomic analysis, AKR1C2, an aldo-keto reductase that detoxifies lipid peroxides, was the only gene that showed significant correlations with both poor prognosis (Fig. 3E, right) and APN expression levels (Spearman r = 0.232, P < 0.001; Fig. 3F). Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis of a matched patient tissue pair spatially confirmed concurrent elevation of APN and AKR1C2 in the post-chemotherapy recurrent lesion compared to the primary tumor (Fig. 3G). Together, these results indicate that APN is associated with ferroptosis suppression in chemoresistant gastric cancer cells and link APN-related ferroptosis signaling to adverse clinical outcomes.

Aminopeptidase N shields chemoresistant gastric cancer from chemotherapy-triggered ferroptosis

To further validate the functional involvement of ferroptosis in APN-mediated chemoresistance, we examined the response to RSL3, a specific GPX4 inhibitor and well-established ferroptosis inducer [29]. As shown in Figure S2, RSL3 treatment induced substantial lipid peroxidation in chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells but not in resistant SGC-7901/DDP cells. APN knockout in resistant cells significantly increased RSL3-induced lipid peroxidation, indicating that APN deficiency sensitizes resistant cells to ferroptosis.

We next investigated whether APN regulates the ferroptosis defense gene AKR1C2 and modulates chemotherapy-induced ferroptosis. In chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells, baseline AKR1C2 expression was significantly higher than in chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells. Genetic ablation of APN reduced AKR1C2 expression to levels comparable to those in sensitive cells (Fig. 4A, left). Notably, no significant differences in AKR1C2 expression were observed between PBS and DDP treatment within any cell line, indicating that DDP treatment itself does not directly induce AKR1C2 expression (Fig. 4A, left). Conversely, induction of the canonical ferroptosis marker PTGS2 was suppressed in wild-type resistant cells under DDP treatment but significantly increased in chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells (Fig. 4A, right). Both APN knockout and shAKR1C2 knockdown partially restored DDP-induced PTGS2 expression, whereas AKR1C2 overexpression in APN knockout cells abolished this effect. In PBS-treated control groups, PTGS2 expression remained low across all cell lines (Fig. 4A, right).

To further validate the role of APN, we treated SGC-7901/DDP cells with an APN-blocking antibody. Consistent with the genetic knockout results, APN blockade significantly reduced AKR1C2 expression compared with IgG control under baseline conditions, while DDP treatment did not alter AKR1C2 expression in any group (Fig. 4B, left). Upon DDP exposure, APN-blocked cells showed significantly increased PTGS2 induction compared with IgG-treated controls, whereas no such effect was observed when the antibody was applied to APN-knockout cells (Fig. 4B, right), confirming target specificity.

To visualize functional impacts on lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation, we employed the BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe, where oxidation shifts fluorescence from red to green, and DCFDA staining for intracellular ROS levels. DDP treatment triggered substantial lipid peroxidation and ROS production in chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells (Fig. 4C). In contrast, SGC-7901/DDP resistant cells showed markedly lower levels of lipid peroxidation and ROS under identical conditions (Fig. 4C). Compared with resistant cells, both shAKR1C2 knockdown and APN knockout significantly increased lipid peroxidation and ROS levels following DDP exposure (Fig. 4C). Notably, AKR1C2 overexpression in APN knockout cells reversed these effects, with lipid peroxidation and ROS levels returning to values comparable to those observed in resistant cells (Fig. 4C). Consistently, APN blockade with α-APN antibody also significantly increased lipid peroxidation and ROS levels in WT cells following DDP exposure compared with IgG-treated controls, whereas no such effects were observed in APN-knockout cells (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that APN regulates AKR1C2 expression and that both genetic and pharmacological targeting of APN sensitizes resistant gastric cancer cells to chemotherapy-induced ferroptosis.

Exosomal APN drives acquired chemoresistance and ferroptosis evasion from resistant to sensitive gastric cancer cells

We hypothesized that tumor-associated exosomal APN could serve as a transferable mediator of chemoresistance. Western blot confirmed abundant APN in exosomes from chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells, while drug-sensitive SGC-7901 exosomes showed minimal APN (Fig. 5A). Exosomes from APN-knockout resistant cells lacked APN, confirming its cellular origin. To track exosome uptake, we incubated palmGFP-labeled resistant cell exosomes (green) with tdTomato-expressing sensitive recipient cells. Confocal microscopy revealed efficient internalization of green exosomes within red-labeled cells after 12 h, with distinct puncta indicating successful intercellular transfer (Fig. 5B).

We then evaluated functional consequences of exosomal APN transfer on drug resistance and ferroptosis evasion. Chemosensitive SGC-7901 cells preconditioned with exosomes from chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells showed significantly enhanced colony formation under cisplatin treatment compared to those receiving exosomes from either drug-sensitive SGC-7901 cells or APN-knockout resistant cells (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis of recipient cell lysates confirmed successful APN protein transfer in cells incubated with APN-containing exosomes from chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells (Fig. 5C, middle). Furthermore, sensitive cells receiving exosomes from chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells exhibited substantially reduced levels of both lipid peroxidation and intracellular ROS upon cisplatin challenge compared to cells treated with exosomes from drug-sensitive SGC-7901 cells, whereas this protective effect was substantially diminished when using exosomes from APN-knockout resistant cells (Fig. 5D).

In the in vivo setting, intravenous administration of exosomes from chemoresistant SGC-7901/DDP cells to mice bearing SGC-7901 xenografts resulted in significantly enhanced tumor progression during cisplatin treatment relative to animals receiving exosomes from drug-sensitive cells. By contrast, tumors in mice treated with exosomes derived from APN-knockout resistant cells remained responsive to cisplatin, exhibiting growth kinetics and final tumor weights comparable to the sensitive exosome group (Fig. 5E). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated sustained co-expression of APN and AKR1C2 along with suppressed PTGS2 in tumors receiving chemoresistant exosomes, while tumors treated with either APN-deficient exosomes or drug-sensitive cell-derived exosomes displayed elevated PTGS2 levels with minimal APN and AKR1C2 staining (Fig. 5E, right). Collectively, these findings establish tumor-associated exosomal APN as a key mediator that drives the intercellular propagation of chemoresistance and ferroptosis evasion in gastric cancer.

APN-blocking antibody reverses chemoresistance in orthotopic drug-resistant gastric tumors

Based on APN’s established role in promoting chemoresistance, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of APN inhibition using an orthotopic gastric cancer model. Luciferase-expressing SGC-7901/DDP cells were implanted into the gastric wall of mice to establish chemoresistant tumors that recapitulate clinical disease progression. Animals were randomized into four treatment groups receiving vehicle control, cisplatin monotherapy, cisplatin combined with the broad-spectrum APN inhibitor Bestatin [30], or cisplatin together with an APN-blocking antibody. Tumor progression was monitored by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the inherent chemoresistance of this model, cisplatin monotherapy led to only a modest reduction in tumor growth relative to the vehicle control, indicating a partial therapeutic response (Fig. 6B). In contrast, both combination therapies significantly impeded tumor progression. Notably, cisplatin plus the APN-blocking antibody achieved the greatest antitumor effect, significantly outperforming both cisplatin monotherapy and cisplatin plus Bestatin (Fig. 6B). Histopathological assessment further revealed tumor shrinkage, altered microscopic architecture, and a reduced Ki-67 proliferation index in tumors from the APN antibody combination group, indicative of enhanced chemotherapy-induced damage and growth suppression (Fig. 6D). To assess the tolerability of the combination treatment, we monitored body weight changes throughout the experimental period. As shown in Fig. 6C, no significant differences in body weight ratios (day 20 / day 0 × 100%) were observed among any treatment groups (one-way ANOVA, P > 0.05), suggesting that the addition of APN-blocking antibody to cisplatin was not associated with additional body weight loss in this model. Collectively, these results demonstrate that antibody-mediated APN blockade restores chemosensitivity in resistant gastric tumors, offering a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance.

Discussion

In this study, we establish aminopeptidase N (APN/CD13) as a multifunctional driver of chemoresistance in gastric cancer through integrated proteomic and functional analyses. We demonstrate that APN is selectively enriched in chemoresistant cells and patient-derived exosomes under therapeutic pressure, where it sustains a ferroptosis-suppressive state during chemotherapy through enhanced defense against lipid peroxidation. Collectively, these findings establish exosomal APN as both a dynamic on-treatment biomarker and a tractable therapeutic target in chemoresistant gastric cancer.

The clinical validity of serum exosomal APN as a real-time biomarker for chemoresistance is substantiated by three key lines of evidence. First, APN levels increased dynamically over treatment cycles in resistant xenografts but remained low in cycle-matched controls, supporting its role as a therapy-responsive indicator rather than a static marker of tumor burden. Second, in a clinical cohort of 105 patients, serum exosomal APN robustly discriminated progressive disease from disease control with an AUC of 0.85 at a predefined cutoff, aligning with the specificity and stability requirements for liquid biopsy applications. Third, chemoresistant cells specifically secrete APN-enriched exosomes, providing a biological basis for the circulating signal. While previous gastric cancer investigations have predominantly focused on exosome-derived nucleic acids [31–34] and a limited set of exosomal proteins [35–37] as potential diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers, our proteomics-driven investigation establishes exosomal APN as a distinctive biomarker with specific diagnostic utility for monitoring chemoresistance in gastric cancer.

APN represents a druggable node in gastric cancer chemoresistance, operating through dual functions that encompass both cell-intrinsic control of drug response and intercellular propagation via exosomes. In our gastric cancer models, loss of APN function resensitized refractory cells to cisplatin, whereas its overexpression conferred chemoprotection. Extending beyond gastric cancer, APN has been implicated in poor prognosis and chemoresistance across multiple malignancies [38–43], including hepatocellular, colorectal, and breast cancers, suggesting a potentially broader role for APN in chemoresistance across cancer types. In addition to these cell-autonomous effects, we identify for the first time in cancer that APN serves as a transmissible resistance factor through exosomal transfer. This intercellular transmission is demonstrated by sensitive cells acquiring chemoprotective traits, including enhanced survival and reduced oxidative stress under cisplatin treatment, following uptake of APN-containing exosomes. The pathological relevance of this transfer mechanism is confirmed by accelerated tumor progression following systemic administration of these exosomes during chemotherapy in animal models. Conceptually, these findings reveal an innovative dimension of chemoresistance where APN-dependent resistance can be disseminated through exosomes. Therapeutically, the exceptional efficacy of a specific APN-neutralizing antibody over the clinical-stage broad-spectrum inhibitor bestatin demonstrates that chemoresistance depends on APN-specific functions rather than general peptidase activity. The established clinical safety profile of bestatin, together with our antibody data and the observed APN upregulation in clinical specimens from chemoresistant metastatic lesions, supports the translational potential of APN-targeted therapy. These collective insights establish antibody-mediated APN blockade as a strategic therapeutic approach capable of simultaneously targeting both intrinsic and transmissible forms of chemoresistance in gastric cancer.

Mechanistically, APN has been reported to promote chemoresistance in cancer through various mechanisms, including maintenance of cancer stemness, antioxidant defense, and ferroptosis inhibition [44–48]. Within this complex landscape, we sought to identify key downstream effectors of APN in the context of gastric cancer chemoresistance. Under chemotherapeutic treatment, APN blockade consistently induced a pro-ferroptotic state, characterized by coordinated alterations in ferroptosis-related gene expression as revealed by transcriptomic screening, together with ROS accumulation and elevated lipid peroxidation. While other pathways such as p53 and TNF signaling were also enriched in our transcriptomic analysis, we focused on ferroptosis based on several lines of evidence: prior knowledge linking APN to ROS regulation, the aforementioned functional phenotypes, and subsequent mechanistic validation at ferroptosis-related gene-regulatory levels. Collectively, these findings suggest that ferroptosis may represent a key process in APN-mediated chemoresistance.

To identify the specific molecular mediators underlying this ferroptotic response, we interrogated the transcriptomic data for clinically relevant ferroptosis-related genes. Analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort revealed that among these, AKR1C2 showed statistically significant correlations with both APN expression and poor prognosis, a pattern not observed for other genes in this set. This clinical relevance was further supported by paired analysis of matched primary and post-chemotherapy recurrent lesions, revealing spatial co-localization and concurrent elevation of APN and AKR1C2 in the recurrent metastases. Previous studies have independently established AKR1C2 and related family members as ferroptosis suppressors, while also highlighting ferroptosis as a therapeutically targetable tumor-suppressive cell death pathway [49–51]. Having established the clinical relevance of the APN-AKR1C2 axis, we next confirmed the functional dependency. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of APN significantly reduced AKR1C2 expression, and AKR1C2 knockdown recapitulated the pro-ferroptotic effects of APN inhibition. Conversely, AKR1C2 overexpression in APN-knockout cells restored resistance to chemotherapy-induced ferroptosis. Together, these results identify AKR1C2 as a key downstream effector of APN and provide a rationale for combining APN blockade with ferroptosis-promoting therapeutic strategies.

Our findings support a dual-path clinical translation strategy. First, serum exosomal APN can be deployed as a dynamic biomarker for early detection of chemoresistance, complementing current imaging and serum marker assessments with mechanistically informed, real-time monitoring. Second, antibody-mediated APN blockade represents a promising therapeutic strategy that can be integrated with standard chemotherapy and rationally combined with ferroptosis-promoting agents. The development of standardized assays for exosomal APN detection is clinically feasible, given the ongoing advancement of extracellular vesicle-based diagnostics. While safety considerations regarding APN expression in specific myeloid and endothelial cell populations warrant attention [52], the documented clinical experience with agents like bestatin suggests a manageable risk profile [53], and our data highlight the potential for superior specificity with antibody-based targeting.

Several key aspects of APN-mediated chemoresistance warrant further investigation. Future studies should delineate the transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms underlying chemotherapy-induced APN upregulation. Additionally, the molecular machinery governing APN’s selective loading into exosomes requires systematic exploration, as targeting this process may provide a novel strategy to disrupt resistance transmission. We also acknowledge that exosomes contain diverse bioactive cargoes, including other proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, which may contribute to chemoresistance [9, 20, 54]. While our APN knockout exosome experiments establish an essential role for APN in transferring resistance, other exosomal components could act in concert with or independently of APN to modulate recipient cell sensitivity. Systematic profiling of exosomal cargoes, integrating proteomic, transcriptomic, and lipidomic analyses, combined with functional screening approaches such as CRISPR based knockout or pharmacological inhibition of individual candidate factors, would help further distinguish APN specific contributions from those of other exosomal proteins, nucleic acids, or lipids in mediating chemoresistance. Such integrative strategies could also identify cooperative or synergistic interactions among exosomal components, and ultimately facilitate the development of combination biomarker panels or multitargeted therapeutic strategies for gastric cancer. The precise pathway through which APN regulates AKR1C2-mediated ferroptosis also warrants clarification, particularly its integration with established ferroptosis regulatory networks. To advance therapeutic targeting, developing and validating APN-specific nanobodies represents a crucial direction, with the aim of identifying epitopes that selectively block pro-resistance functions while preserving physiological roles.

In conclusion, our study establishes APN as a master regulator of chemoresistance development and dissemination in gastric cancer, supporting an integrated diagnostic-therapeutic framework in which exosomal APN detection enables early resistance identification and antibody-based APN blockade provides a mechanism-based strategy to restore drug sensitivity, paving the way for precision oncology in this disease.

Methods

Human subjects and serum collection

Patients with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma receiving standard-of-care chemotherapy (TP or FOLFOX regimens) were prospectively enrolled after obtaining written informed consent. Blood samples were collected prior to treatment initiation and serially every 10–12 days throughout the treatment course. Serum tumor markers were measured at each blood draw, whereas imaging assessments (computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging) were performed according to standard clinical practice at the discretion of the treating physician. Disease control (DC) and progressive disease (PD) were determined based on RECIST 1.1 criteria, integrating imaging findings, serum tumor marker dynamics, and overall clinical evaluation. For each patient, the blood sample corresponding to the time point of confirmed DC or PD status was selected for exosomal analysis.

For the discovery proteomics phase, paired peripheral blood samples were collected from 10 patients at both DC and PD stages during the same chemotherapy protocol (clinical characteristics detailed in Table S2). For the biomarker validation cohort, serum samples were obtained from an independent set of 105 patients (55 DC, 50 PD). All blood samples were processed within 2 h of venipuncture, centrifuged at 1,600 × g for 10 min, and the resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until exosome isolation.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The SGC-7901 human gastric cancer cell line was obtained from the Institute of Cell Biology (Shanghai, China, http://www.cellbank.org.cn). The parental SGC-7901 and its chemoresistant derivatives, comprising SGC-7901/DDP (cisplatin-resistant), SGC-7901/5FU (5-fluorouracil-resistant), and SGC-7901/PTX (paclitaxel-resistant), were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and routinely tested mycoplasma-negative. These resistant variants were established through a 6-month stepwise selection process with increasing concentrations of the respective chemotherapeutic agents: cisplatin (0.1–2.0 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, C2210000), 5-fluorouracil (0.5–10 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, F6627), and paclitaxel (0.5–10 nM; Sigma-Aldrich, T7402). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Unless otherwise stated, chemoresistant cells were maintained in drug-free medium for at least two passages before experimental assays. Ferroptosis was chemically induced by treatment with cisplatin or RSL3 (MedChemExpress, HY-100218 A). For in vitro functional assays, recipient cells were co-cultured with 5 µg/mL exosomes isolated from producer cells and APN blockade was achieved through incubation with an APN-neutralizing antibody (5 µg/mL; Abcam, ab7417 [WM15]). For orthotopic imaging, SGC-7901/DDP cells stably expressing firefly luciferase were utilized.

Animal studies

Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were used in all experiments. Subcutaneous xenograft models were established by implanting 2 × 106 SGC-7901 cells in 50 µL serum-free RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) into the right flank. Mice bearing subcutaneous tumors received cisplatin (1.5 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, C2210000) via intraperitoneal injection every 4 days for a total of 5 doses. In parallel exosome transfer experiments, 100 µg exosomes in 100 µL PBS were administered via tail vein injection every 4 days for a total of 5 doses. Tumor volume was routinely measured every 4 days using a caliper and calculated as Volume = (Length × Width²) / 2.

Orthotopic gastric cancer models were established by surgically implanting 2 × 106 luciferase-expressing SGC-7901/DDP cells suspended in 50 µL of a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Sigma-Aldrich, CLS356234) and serum-free RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) into the gastric wall under anesthesia. Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescent imaging using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer) following intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, L6882). Mice with orthotopic tumors were treated with cisplatin (3 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, C2210000) via intraperitoneal injection, APN-neutralizing antibody (5 mg/kg; Abcam, ab7417 [WM15]) via tail vein injection, or bestatin (10 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, B8385) via tail vein injection every 4 days for 5 cycles.

All treatments were initiated following confirmed tumor establishment. Mice were euthanized 4 days after the final treatment administration. Throughout the study, animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups at the start of treatment, and investigators responsible for tumor measurements, imaging analysis, and histological evaluations remained blinded to group assignments. Sample sizes for each experiment are indicated in the respective figure legends.

Exosome isolation and characterization

For exosome collection from cell culture, cells plated in 15-cm dishes were maintained in 30 ml of RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% Exo-FBS™ Exosome-depleted FBS (System Biosciences, EXO-FBS-250 A-1) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen) for 48 h. For serum-derived exosomes, blood samples were centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then diluted 1:10 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for subsequent processing.

Both cell culture medium (20 ml) and diluted serum (20 ml) were pre-cleared through 0.22-µm membrane filters (Millipore) and concentrated by centrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C using a Hitachi CP100MX ultracentrifuge. The resulting vesicle pellets were resuspended in 100 µl PBS and further purified using a discontinuous iodixanol gradient (Sigma-Aldrich, D1556). Gradients were prepared by sequential layering of 1.2 ml each of 40%, 20%, 10%, and 5% iodixanol solutions, followed by centrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. Twelve 400-µl fractions were collected from the top, and exosome-enriched fractions 6–9 (diluted 1:10 in PBS) were pooled and pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C.

Purified exosomes were resuspended in PBS, quantified for protein concentration using a Thermo Varioskan LUX microplate reader, and stored at − 80 °C. For all functional assays, exosome preparations from matched culture volumes were normalized by total protein content and particle counts. Particle morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi HT-7700), and size distribution was analyzed by nano-flow cytometry (NanoFCM N30E), confirming the presence of cup-shaped vesicles with modal diameters of 50–150 nm (Figure S1).

Label-free proteomic analysis

Serum exosomes were lysed in 2% SDS/7 M urea buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and proteins were extracted followed by ultrasonication and acetone precipitation. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. For each sample, 100 µg of protein was reduced with 20 mM DTT (57 °C, 1 h), alkylated with 90 mM iodoacetamide (room temperature, 40 min, in the dark), and digested overnight with trypsin using filter-aided sample preparation (FASP). Resulting peptides were desalted using ZipTip C18 pipette tips, dried under vacuum, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an EASY-nLC 1000 system coupled to a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were separated using a 120-min binary gradient and analyzed in data-dependent acquisition mode. Comparative analysis of proteomic profiles from 10 paired patient serum exosome samples (progressive disease [PD] versus disease control [DC]) identified 23 proteins significantly upregulated in PD (fold-change [PD/DC] ≥ 1.2; P < 0.05, two-tailed Student’s t-test; Table S1).

iTRAQ proteomic analysis

Proteins were extracted from conditioned medium-derived exosomes and whole-cell lysates using lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% SDS) containing protease inhibitors, followed by ultrasonication and centrifugation. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay, and integrity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE. For iTRAQ labeling, 100 µg of protein per sample was reduced with 10 mM TCEP (37 °C, 1 h), alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide (room temperature, 40 min, in the dark), and digested with trypsin (1:50 ratio) overnight. Peptides were labeled with iTRAQ 8-plex reagents (AB Sciex, 4390812) per manufacturer’s instructions. Fractionation was performed using high-pH reverse-phase chromatography on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system with a BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm), collecting 20 fractions consolidated into 10 pools. LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on an EASY-nLC 1200 system coupled to a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific), with peptides separated over a 120-min gradient and analyzed using a top-20 DDA method. Comparative analysis identified proteins significantly upregulated in chemoresistant cells across two comparisons (Table S1): exosome proteomes (SGC-7901/DDP vs. SGC-7901, n = 3) using a fold-change threshold of ≥ 1.5, and whole-cell proteomes (SGC-7901/DDP vs. SGC-7901, n = 3) using a more stringent fold-change threshold of ≥ 2.0, both with P < 0.05 (two-tailed Student’s t-test).

Exosomal APN quantification by ELISA

Serum exosomal APN concentrations were measured using the Human Aminopeptidase N/ANPEP ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, EH25RB) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Standards and diluted samples were incubated for 2.5 h at room temperature, followed by sequential incubations with biotin-conjugated detection antibody and streptavidin-HRP. After TMB substrate development, the reaction was stopped and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. APN concentrations were determined from the standard curve, with each sample measured in technical duplicates. The averaged values were used for subsequent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Lentivirus-mediated gene perturbations

For all lentivirus-based genetic manipulations, viral particles were produced by co-transfecting 293T cells with respective transfer plasmids and the helper plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h after transfection and used to infect target cells at 60–70% confluence with 10 µg/ml Polybrene. Following 24 h of infection, stable cell pools were selected using puromycin-containing medium.

For gene knockdown experiments, the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector was used to express short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting APN, CD82, SBF1, VTN or AKR1C2, with a non-targeting sequence (GTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTT) serving as the control. The knockdown efficiency of all shRNA constructs was assessed by qPCR analysis, indicating over 70% reduction in expression for their respective target genes. The specific target sequences for each gene were as follows:

APN: sh1, GCAGGACTACTGGCTGATAGA; sh2, GGTGAAGGACAGCCAGTATGA

APN: sh1, GCAGGACTACTGGCTGATAGA; sh2, GGTGAAGGACAGCCAGTATGA

CD82: sh1, GGAAGAGGACAACAGCCTTTC; sh2, GCATCGTGACTGAGCTCATTC

CD82: sh1, GGAAGAGGACAACAGCCTTTC; sh2, GCATCGTGACTGAGCTCATTC

SBF1: sh1, GGACGCCTGTGTTCCACAATT; sh2, GCCTGTCATCCGCTTCCATAA

SBF1: sh1, GGACGCCTGTGTTCCACAATT; sh2, GCCTGTCATCCGCTTCCATAA

VTN: sh1, GCTGACCAAGAGTCATGCAAG; sh2, GGTTGCTCTGGCTGACCAAGA

VTN: sh1, GCTGACCAAGAGTCATGCAAG; sh2, GGTTGCTCTGGCTGACCAAGA

AKR1C2: sh1, GAAGCCGGGTTCCACCATATT; sh2, GAGGCCATGGAGAAGTGTAAA

AKR1C2: sh1, GAAGCCGGGTTCCACCATATT; sh2, GAGGCCATGGAGAAGTGTAAA

For gene knockout, the lentiCRISPRv2 vector was used to deliver Cas9 and sgRNAs targeting ANPEP. The specific guide sequences were CCTTGGACCAAAGTAAAGCG (KO1) and GGTACGGTCTCAGCGTCACC (KO2). Single-cell-derived APN-knockout clones were established and validated by Sanger sequencing and immunoblotting.

For gene overexpression, the pLV-CS2.0 vector was utilized to deliver APN and AKR1C2 cDNA, with GFP serving as the transduction control.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using Tripure Isolation Reagent (Roche). cDNA was prepared from the total RNA using a HiFi-MMLV cDNA Kit (Beijing CoWin Biotech, CW0744). Quantitative PCR was performed using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) with the UltraSYBR Mixture (Beijing CoWin Biotech, CW0956). A eukaryotic 18 S rRNA endogenous control was used as an internal standard, and the results were calculated using the △△CT (where CT is threshold cycle) method. The following primers were used: PTGS2 (F: CGGTGAAACTCTGGCTAGACAG, R: GCAAACCGTAGATGCTCAGGGA), AKR1C2 (F: GGGTTCCACCATATTGATTCT, R: CACTGCCATCTGCAATCT), and 18 S (F: GTCTGTGATGCCCTTAGATG, R: AGCTTATGACCCGCACTTAC).

H&E staining

For histological analysis, tissue samples were fixed overnight in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices using a microtome (Leica RM2235, Leica Microsystems). After dewaxing and rehydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat. No. D006) for 3 min, rinsed in running tap water, and briefly differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 30 s. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with eosin (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat. No. D006) for 30 s. Whole-slide images were captured under consistent conditions using an automated slide scanning system (Evident VS200, Olympus).

Immunoblotting and immunohistochemistry

Protein lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies specific for APN (Proteintech, 14553-1-AP). For detection of exosomal markers, TSG101 (Abcam, ab125011) was used as a positive marker, while Calnexin (Abcam, ab22595) served as a negative control to evaluate exosomal purity. β-Actin (Sigma, A1978) was used as a loading control for whole-cell proteins. All antibodies were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Chemiluminescent signals were captured within the linear dynamic range.

For immunohistochemical analysis, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, peroxidase quenching, and incubation with validated antibodies against APN (1:500; Proteintech, 14553-1-AP), AKR1C2 (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology, 13035), PTGS2 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology, 12282), and Ki-67 (1:250; Abcam, ab16667), followed by DAB development and hematoxylin counterstaining.

Multiplex immunofluorescence on human tissues

Matched primary gastric carcinoma and post-chemotherapy recurrent liver metastatic lesions from a patient treated with the FOLFOX regimen were analyzed. The patient was a 59-year-old male diagnosed in March 2021 with primary gastric adenocarcinoma (stage T3N1M0), which progressed to liver metastasis (M1) by 2022 following treatment. Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections using the Opal Polaris 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, NEL871001KT). The staining panel consisted of antibodies against APN (1:500; Proteintech, 14553-1-AP), AKR1C2 (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology, 13035), and pan-Cytokeratin (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, 4545), with DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Following spectral unmixing, representative images were captured to visualize protein expression and co-localization patterns within cytokeratin-positive epithelial cells.

Cell viability

Initial drug resistance screening of cells with multiple shRNA-mediated gene knockdowns was performed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MedChemExpress, HY-K0301) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For dynamic monitoring of treatment response, real-time cell analysis was carried out on the xCELLigence RTCA DP16 system (Agilent), in which cellular impedance was continuously recorded and expressed as Cell Index over time.

Colony formation

SGC-7901 recipient cells were seeded in 3.5 cm dishes at a density of 2,000 cells per dish. Cells were pre-treated with exosomes followed by DDP treatment as indicated. After 10 days of culture, colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged. Colonies consisting of more than 50 cells were counted in a blinded manner.

Lipid peroxidation and ROS assays

Cells treated with DDP or RSL3 were analyzed for lipid peroxidation by staining with BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D3861). The oxidation-induced spectral shift from red to green fluorescence was captured on a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, TCS SP8) and quantified by calculating the ratio of green to red fluorescence intensity per cell using ImageJ software. For intracellular ROS detection, cells were incubated with H2DCFDA (MedChemExpress, HY-D0940) and imaged under standardized acquisition settings. Total fluorescence intensity was quantified and normalized to the number of DAPI-positive nuclei. All analyses were performed on raw, non-saturated images with consistent thresholds across all conditions.

RNA sequencing and pathway analysis

Total RNA was extracted from SGC-7901/DDP cells pretreated with α-APN or IgG and subsequently exposed to DDP (n = 3 per group). Poly(A)-selected libraries were constructed and subjected to paired-end sequencing. Following quality control and alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome, gene-level counts were obtained (Table S3) and analyzed using DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes were defined as those with an adjusted p < 0.01 (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure) and |log2FC| > 1. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on significant gene sets using a hypergeometric test with FDR correction. Ferroptosis-related markers, promoters, and defense genes were pre-defined based on curated literature and pathway databases, and their expression patterns were highlighted in subsequent visualizations.
