Mapping facility-based tuberculosis preventive treatment provision in Ethiopia: a geospatial analysis
Authors: Alemneh Mekuriaw Liyew, Beth Gilmour, Archie C A Clements, Haileab Fekadu Wolde, Kefyalew Addis Alene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge in Ethiopia, despite being a preventable disease. TB preventive treatment (TPT) is a critical intervention to prevent the progression from latent TB infection to active disease, particularly among household contacts of TB patients and people living with HIV due to weakened immunity. However, the initiation and completion rates of TPT at subnational and local levels have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aims to map facility-based TPT initiation and completion rates among household contacts of TB across Ethiopia.MethodsWe used national TB data from the Health Management Information System (HMIS) of the Ethiopian Ministry of Health. We included TPT initiation and completion data reported between July 2022 and June 2023. Demographic and geographic covariates were obtained from publicly available sources. In total, we used 1,132 geolocated data points for TPT initiation and 838 data points for TPT completion. Using Bayesian geostatistical analysis, we estimated TPT initiation and completion rates at a 5 × 5 km spatial resolution across Ethiopia. These high-resolution estimates were then aggregated to generate district-level estimates.ResultsA total of 208,508 contacts of index TB cases were screened for TB during the reporting period, of whom 169,890 were found negative for active TB. Among these, 31.50% were initiated on TPT. Additionally, 39,760 individuals had started TPT in the 12 months prior to the reporting period, and 87.0% of them successfully completed treatment. Both TPT initiation and treatment completion showed substantial local and subnational variations across Ethiopia. For instance, district-level TPT initiation ranged from 0.01% in Bora to 99.0% in Tiro Afeta districts of Oromia region. Distance to health facilities had a negative association with both TPT initiation (β = -0.06; 95% CrI: -0.10, -0.01) and TPT completion rate (β = -0.57; 95% CrI: -0.73, -0.42). Higher population density was negatively associated with TPT completion rate (β = -0.34; 95% CrI: -0.58, -0.10), while HIV/AIDS prevalence showed a positive association with TPT initiation (β = 0.24; 95% CrI: 0.18,0.30).ConclusionOur findings revealed marked geographic variations in TPT provision at local and district levels. This suggests that districts with low performing health facilities, may require targeted interventions to optimize TPT implementation and help reduce TB burden in Ethiopia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14283-x.Keywords: Tuberculosis preventive treatment, Treatment initiation, Treatment completion, Geospatial analysis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains the leading cause of death from an infectious disease globally [1]. In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million people globally developed active TB, resulting in 1.5 million deaths [2]. Yet, the majority of individuals infected with Mtb do not develop active disease and are classified as having latent TB infection (LTBI), in which the bacteria remain dormant without clinical symptoms [3]. It is estimated that nearly a quarter of the global population, around 2 billion people, are infected with latent TB [4]. Without preventive treatment, about 5–10% of individuals with LTBI will develop active TB during their lifetime [5], with the risk being significantly higher among those with weakened immunity, such as people living with HIV (PLHIV), individuals receiving immunosuppressive treatments, and young children [6]. For instance, PLHIV are 15 to 22 times more likely to develop active TB compared to those without HIV [3, 7].

Preventing the progression from latent infection to active TB is a critical component of the global End TB Strategy, which aims to drastically reduce TB incidence and mortality by 2035 [8]. TPT not only prevents the onset of active TB but also contributes to breaking the chain of transmission, thereby reducing the overall burden of the disease in the communities [9]. In 2023, a new global target was introduced to achieve 90% coverage of TPT among high risk individuals by 2027 [10]. Although approximately 4.7 million people received TPT globally in 2023, more than half of whom were household contacts of TB patients [11], this remains below the established target [11].

Ethiopia remains among the 30 high-burden countries for TB [11] with an estimated TB incidence of 146 per 100,000 population in 2023 [12] underscoring the urgent need to accelerate preventive efforts including TPT scale-up to reduce transmission, morbidity and mortality. TB prevention has already been integrated into the country’s essential healthcare package [13]. Ethiopia began implementing isoniazid preventive therapy (IPT) for PLHIV in 2007 and has recently adopted the WHO’s updated TPT guidelines, which recommend safer and shorter regimens [14]. Currently, Ethiopia uses isoniazid for PLHIV, isoniazid plus rifapentine for HIV-negative children and adolescents and isoniazid plus rifampicin for HIV-negative children under two years of age [15]. These policy advances have made Ethiopia one of the few countries in sub-Saharan Africa to demonstrate considerable progress in scaling up TPT implementation [5, 16].

However, programmatic data show wide variation in TPT uptake across the country. Reported coverage among PLHIV ranged spatially from 13% to 64.3% over recent years [5], with 2020 data showing only 47% coverage, well below the national target of 90% [5]. Although coverage increased to 64% in 2021 [17], it still did not meet the country’s target. Treatment completion rates remain largely unknown or alarmingly low in many settings, with some sites reporting completion as low as 12% [18].

Despite TPT being a proven and cost-effective intervention for TB prevention [19], its delivery remains suboptimal, constrained by operational, community and health system level barriers [20]. For instance, initiation is largely driven by health system-related factors, such as medication stock out, health worker training and capacity [21], whereas completion is often influenced both by facility-level support such as adherence monitoring, follow-up, treatment durations and by individual-level factors including patient health seeking behaviours, side effects of the therapy, and stigma about TB [22].

To ensure efficient use of limited national resources and strengthen programme effectiveness for optimal TPT delivery, it is essential to identify geographic areas with low TPT implementation. Although a number of studies have explored spatial patterns in various aspects of TB epidemiology in Ethiopia, such as TB prevalence [23], TB-HIV coinfection [24], TB treatment outcome [25] and drug resistance [26], there remains a substantial gap when it comes to granular estimates of TPT initiation and treatment completion. Subnational and local level insights on TPT provision are crucial for identifying areas with underperforming facilities and designing targeted interventions. Therefore, this study aims to produce subnational and local level TPT initiation and completion estimates in Ethiopia by applying Bayesian geospatial models to HMIS TPT data integrated with spatial covariates.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Ethiopia, Africa’s second-most populous country, with a population of approximately 132 million people [27]. The country operates under a multi-level administrative structure which is divided into regions (first level), zones (second level), districts (third level) (Figure S1), and neighbourhoods or kebeles (lowest level). TB data, including all information about TPT, are compiled and reported to the national authorities from various health facilities within districts.

Ethiopia’s Health Care System is structured into three tiers: primary, secondary, and tertiary care. At the primary level, health posts, health centres and district hospitals provide basic health services, particularly in rural and semi urban areas, aiming to deliver essential healthcare close to communities. Secondary level healthcare consists of general hospitals offering more specialized services, while the tertiary level includes specialized hospitals that provide advanced medical care. Health posts, health centres and primary hospitals which are the first point of contact for most people, play a crucial role in delivering TB services, including screening, diagnosis, treatment, and preventive care [28].

Data source

This study utilized nationwide TB data obtained from the Health Management Information System (HMIS) of the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, covering the period from July 2022 to June 2023. The dataset included health facility level information on the initiation and completion of TPT across the country. Data from all regions were included, with the exception of the Tigray region, where no data were available during the study period due to conflict-related disruptions.

The TPT initiation and completion rates were the two primary outcomes investigated in this study. As part of the routine TB notification process, individuals who were in contact with both drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) and drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) cases were systematically screened at health facilities. Contacts who tested negative for active TB were subsequently initiated on TPT. The initiation and progress of TPT treatment were monitored and reported on a routine basis. Additionally, individuals who commenced TPT within the previous 12 months were evaluated to ensure completion of the full treatment regimen.

Therefore, health facility based TPT initiation rate is defined as the number of individuals initiated on TPT out of those who have been screened negative for TB during health facility’s reporting period. Completion of TPT is defined as finishing the entire course of treatment within the designated timeframe. Thus, TPT completion rate refers to the number of individuals that completed TPT among the cohort who started treatment, 12 months prior to the health facility’s reporting period. The 12-month duration is standard period used in the HMIS guide to allow sufficient time for people who started TPT to finish their treatment course.

Data for both TPT initiation and completion were available at health facility and district levels. Health facilities and districts with missing or incomplete data on key TPT indicators specifically TPT eligibility, initiation or treatment completion were excluded. Health facilities, including hospitals, health centres, health posts, and clinics, were geolocated to specific geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) using tools such as Google Maps, Mapcarta, and Google Earth. In instances where data were aggregated and reported at the district level rather than by individual facility, the population-weighted centroid of each district was calculated using the 2022 Ethiopian population estimate raster as the weighting surface (Figure S2). This approach provides a more accurate spatial representation of where individuals were likely to access TPT which could serve as proxy for health facility concentration.

Spatial covariates were extracted at district centroids for district-level analyses and at health facility coordinates for facility-level analyses using R software. To reduce potential spatial misalignment between the aggregated outcomes and their corresponding covariates, we generated 5 km circular buffers around each health facility and extracted covariate values within these buffer areas, rather than relying on point locations alone. Then a summary statistic was taken for each covariate by averaging all raster cells falling within each 5 km buffer to provide a more representative approximation of the environmental and contextual characteristics of the populations served. Finally, the georeferenced TPT data were linked to extracted spatial covariates and explored in Geographic Information System (GIS) software, ArcGIS (developed by ESRI, based in Redlands, California, USA) for visualization.

Data on potential covariates for multivariable analysis were obtained from publicly available sources. Data on distance to health care were obtained from the Malaria Atlas Project (MAP) [29]. Grid-based population density and relative deprivation index estimates were obtained from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Centre (SEDAC) [30]. HIV/AIDS prevalence data were obtained from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) [31]. Polygon shapefiles for the administrative boundaries of Ethiopia were obtained from DIVA-GIS [32]. The pixel level maps of the covariates including the data sources, are provided in supplementary information (Figure S3 and Table S1).

Parameter estimation was performed using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) method in R (R-INLA package). Multiple iterations were run to ensure an adequate characterization of the posterior distributions of all parameters. Multicollinearity was checked by using variance inflation factor (VIF) and variables with VIF < 5 was retained. Model selection was conducted using the widely applicable Watanabe Information Criteria (WAIC). The covariate combinations were selected using a stepwise approach guided by prior evidence and theoretical relevance. Starting with a set of candidate covariates, we iteratively fitted models including an increasing number of covariates and calculated the WAIC for each combination. The combination with the lowest WAIC, indicating the best predictive performance while avoiding overfitting, was selected as the final model (Table S2-3). The final model, selected based on the WAIC, was subsequently used to predict TPT initiation estimates. The probability integral transform (PIT) statistic, which was obtained through a leave-one-out cross-validation procedure in INLA, was employed for model validation (Figure S5).

To account for prediction uncertainty, 10,000 samples of the posterior parameters were drawn. Each sample was used to estimate TPT initiation rate at a 5 × 5 km pixel resolution. The resulting pixel-level predictions were summarised to create median predictive maps. We then aggregated the pixel-level estimates to the district level using the median given the skewness of the posterior distributions. The same procedure was applied to estimate and map TPT treatment completion.

Results

A total of 211,211 contacts to TB cases were identified from 2022 to 2023. Of these, 119,902 contacts were associated with DR-TB index cases, and 199,309 were linked to DS-TB index cases. Out of 211,211 contacts, a total of 208,508 individuals were screened for TB, and 169,890 were found to be negative for TB. Following screening, 53,498 (31.50%) individuals were initiated on TPT (Fig. 1 and Table S4). In addition, 39,760 individuals had started TPT in the 12 months prior to the reporting period, and of these, 34,580 (86.97%) successfully completed treatment (Table S4).

TB preventive treatment initiation rate

The national TPT initiation was 31.50%. However, there was substantial variation in TPT initiation rate across different localities in Ethiopia. Most local areas in eastern Ethiopia had low TPT initiation rate estimates well below the national rate. Specifically, the lowest initiation rates were estimated in most areas of Somalia and Afar regions, the eastern and western parts of the Oromia region, and the western part of the South Nation Nationalities and Peoples Region (SNNPR). In contrast, the highest TPT initiation estimates were found in central and western part of Ethiopia (Fig. 3A).

Median TPT initiation rate at 5 × 5 km spatial resolution (A) and district level (B) in Ethiopia, 2022–2023. Red indicates initiation rate, while blue represents areas with high initiation rate. Areas with no data are marked in white. Administrative units represent region (A) and districts (B)

Furthermore, TPT initiation rate varied significantly between districts across the country, ranging from 0.01% in Bora district, East Shewa Zone to 99.0% Tiro Afeta, Jima zone with the majority of districts in eastern Ethiopia exhibiting initiation rate (Fig. 3B). For instance, in the Somali region, all districts except Fafan (36.0%) had coverage estimates below 30.0%. Similarly, in the Afar region, except for the districts of Gulina (65.0%) and Ewa (50.0%), initiation rates were below 50.0%. The highest initiation rates were observed in districts from central and western part of Ethiopia. Notably, Tiro Afeta (99.0%), Kersa (99.0%), and Shala (93.0%) from Oromia region, as well as Kindo Dida (92.0%), Ofa (86.0%), and Boloso Bombe (83.0%) from the South Nation Nationalities and Peoples Region, were among the six districts with the highest estimated TPT initiation rate. Exceedance probability of districts achieving at least 90% TPT initiation rate was also provided as supplementary information (Figure S6).

TB preventive treatment completion rate

Figure 4 illustrates the variation in TPT completion rates across different local areas and districts in Ethiopia. Despite high national TPT completion (86.9%), the subnational findings reveal considerable disparities in completion rates both between regions and within regions of the country. Oromia, SNNPR, and Amhara regions exhibited the most pronounced variations, with some local areas showing very low completion rates, while others achieved significantly higher rates. In contrast, urban areas such as Addis Ababa (the capital city) and Harari regions displayed relatively higher estimated completion rates with minimal intra-regional variation (Fig. 4A).

At the district level, the variation in TPT completion rates was even more marked. Districts such as Bora (0.02%), Kercha (0.03%), and Chora (0.04%) had the lowest estimated completion rates, while districts like Tiro Afeta (97.0%), Kersa (92.0%), and Shalla (90.0%) had the highest rates, all located within the Oromia region (Fig. 4B). Additionally, districts located in the central part of Oromia generally exhibited higher completion rates, whereas remote districts in the region had considerably lower rates, reflecting a significant disparity within the region. Many districts in the regions of Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambela, Somalia, and Afar had low TPT completion rates. In contrast, relatively high completion rates were observed in districts located in Addis Ababa, Harari, Dire Dawa, and the northern and northwestern parts of SNNPR.

Some districts exhibited discordance between TPT initiation and completion rates. For example, Gulina district in Afar and Jikawo, Itang, and Lare districts in Gambela showed high treatment initiation rates but low treatment completion. Conversely, districts such as Gerbo and Hamero in the Somali region demonstrated high treatment completion rates despite low treatment initiation. A spatial overlap between low TPT coverage and low completion rates was seen in districts in Somalia, Afar, Northeastern part of Amhara, and southern part of Oromia region. However, the standard deviation of the estimates was high for most parts of the Afar and Somali regions likely indicating sparsity of data (Figure S7). The estimated district level TPT initiation and completion rates were provided as supplementary information (Appendix 2).

Model validation

The probability integral transform (PIT) distributions indicate an overall good model fit, although some deviation from uniformity suggests that minor under- or over-estimation may persist in specific locations in both models adjusted for TPT initiation rate and TPT completion rate. The plot line departures at the lower and upper tails of the PIT distribution indicate that the models may modestly under-predict in some areas (left tail) and over-predict in others (right tail) (Figure S5).

Discussion

This is the first study to provide local and district-level estimates of health facility based TPT initiation and treatment completion rates across Ethiopia. The findings revealed considerable variation in TPT provision across the country, with factors such as proximity to healthcare facilities, HIV/AIDS prevalence, and population density significantly associated with these disparities.

TPT initiation and treatment completion were substantially varied across subnational areas in Ethiopia indicating the substantial variation in the performance of local and district health facilities in TPT implementation. This might be partly due to suboptimal performance of TB programs that oversee TPT implementation, stemming from various barriers. In resource-limited settings such as Ethiopia, lack of funding and trained healthcare personnel is a key health facility level challenge, particularly during the initial phase of TPT implementation, which requires healthcare workers to be trained in specific TPT monitoring and evaluation practices for optimal reach and treatment adherence [35].

The findings in this study revealed that the majority of districts in remote areas of Ethiopia, including the Somali and Afar regions, had low health facility based TPT initiation and low treatment completion rates. Although health facility based TPT implementation in these high TB-burden regions [36, 37] might be expected to be higher due to increased awareness and service demand, our analysis indicates that TPT service provision in these areas remains limited. These patterns likely reflect long-standing health-system limitations that disproportionately affect these regions. For instance, Somali and Afar regions face chronic shortages of trained healthcare workers, high staff turnover, and limited availability of personnel trained in TB services, and clinical monitoring [38–40]. Such human resource constraints reduce the capacity of facilities to provide consistent TPT initiation, follow-up, and adverse event monitoring. Weak supportive supervision and limited program oversight at the district level may further contribute to inconsistent adherence to national TPT guidelines and poor-quality documentation [41], both of which compromise the delivery and monitoring of TPT services.

The geographical contexts in remote areas of the country might also contribute to poor TPT performance. For instance, Afar, Somali, Benshangul Gumuz and Gambela regions, areas where low TPT implementation is noted are characterized by low health facility density, dispersed settlements, and long travel distances to health centres, which limit physical access to routine TB services [42]. This is further compounded by limited transportation infrastructure and poor road conditions [43] further hindering timely follow-up and referral, reducing both TPT uptake and treatment completion. Because many communities in these regions are pastoralist and highly mobile, patients frequently move across districts or regions during seasonal migration, making it difficult for health facilities to maintain continuity of care and resulting in missed appointments and high rates of TPT treatment interruption [43]. In addition to health-system and geographic barriers, supply chain instability may also play important role in the low performance observed in remote areas in the country [44]. Health facilities in Somali and Afar regions are more prone to stockouts of TB drugs due to logistical challenges in delivering medicines to remote districts [41, 45]. Stock shortages not only delay initiation but also contribute to treatment discontinuation among those already enrolled.

Conversely, districts in urban regions such as Hareri, Dire Dawa, Addis Ababa (capital city), and parts of Oromia region demonstrated relatively higher health facility based TPT initiation and better treatment completion rates. This might be partly attributed to relatively higher health facility density, better infrastructure, and a more stable health workforce in these districts compared to the peripheral ones [42]. Urban and semi-urban districts, in particular, have stronger clinical staffing levels, more consistent mentorship and supervision, and better availability of TB medications [46]. These system-level strengths likely support more reliable implementation of TPT guidelines, enabling timely identification of eligible individuals, prompt TPT initiation, and improved follow-up for treatment adherence. The higher TPT initiation and better treatment completion rates in Central part of Ethiopia might also be attributed to more effective integration of TB and HIV services within health facilities in these areas. For example, in the Oromia region, the coordinated implementation of TB and HIV control activities has been associated with enhanced service delivery, facilitating the identification of eligible individuals and the provision of TPT [47]. Integrated service delivery models are known to streamline patient care, reduce missed opportunities for TPT initiation, and improve treatment adherence, thereby contributing to better program outcomes [48].

In the current study, HIV/AIDS prevalence was positively associated with TPT initiation, likely supporting the notion that integrated service delivery could enhance TPT implementation. Regions with elevated HIV prevalence often have more established HIV care and treatment platforms [3], where routine TB screening and TPT provision are integrated into comprehensive HIV services [49]. This close programmatic relationship between HIV and TB has likely fostered the development of integrated health services that jointly address both conditions, ultimately contributing to improved TPT uptake [50].

Longer distance to healthcare facilities was an important factor negatively associated with both TPT initiation rate and completion rate, which is supported by the finding in the previous study in Ethiopia [51]. In areas where health facilities are scarce, distant, or difficult to reach, people may be unable to visit health facilities regularly for medication, monitoring, or follow-up care. This is more prominent in pastoral regions. For instance, 40.4% of districts in Somali region have at least 80% of the population living more than 5 km from the nearest health facility [52]. Similarly, over 90% of rural districts in pastoralist regions such as Afar, Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambela, and Somali do not have adequate access to TB services [53]. These geographic and infrastructural constraints likely contribute to higher rates of loss to follow-up among enrolled patients, ultimately undermining TPT adherence and reducing treatment completion.

Additionally, population density was negatively associated with TPT completion rates. In densely populated areas, healthcare systems may become overstretched, leading to challenges in providing adequate patient follow-up and comprehensive care. This strain on the healthcare system can result in suboptimal management of treatment adherence, side effects, and TB counselling, all of which are critical components for successful TPT completion [54]. While studies examining the impact of population density on TPT completion are limited, our findings highlight the need for targeted interventions in crowded settings, such as strengthened patient follow-up and facility-level capacity building, to improve TPT completion rates. However, this association might be influenced by the fact that some TB data were geolocated at the district centroid, which was spatially weighted by population which might have potentially emphasised areas with lower treatment completion rates.

This study provides valuable, policy-relevant insights by mapping health facility level TPT initiation and treatment completion rates across Ethiopia. Specifically, these spatially resolved maps can support policymakers by identifying where TPT initiation need to be improved and where targeted adherence and follow-up interventions are required for better treatment completion, thereby informing programmatic actions to support underperforming facilities and improve service quality. Our modelling framework is transferable to other high-TB-burden countries in Africa and beyond with comparable facility-level data systems, enabling subnational mapping of TPT initiation and treatment completion rates while accounting for local contextual differences. However, several limitations should be considered. Although we have applied spatial buffers, the extracted covariates might not fully capture facility catchment areas, which may affect the validity of the findings. In addition, facility-based data are prone to incompleteness, missing reports, and non-reporting facilities, and they do not cover TPT initiation outside the formal health system, potentially limiting the accuracy and generalizability of the results. Urban facilities generally have higher diagnostic capacity, reporting and completeness than rural facilities, which may bias the analysis toward urban areas. The reliance of TB contact data on the detection of index TB cases also means that missed or undiagnosed cases could lead to underestimation of TPT-eligible contacts. Additionally, limited data from eastern regions, particularly Afar and Somali, may have affected the robustness of the findings, while the absence of data from northern Ethiopia, specifically the Tigray region, constrained comprehensive national representation. Future work integrating community survey data with catchment area-based covariates could allow evaluation of community TPT coverage, which would complement the health facility focused insights presented in this study. Future studies could also leverage the correlation between TPT coverage and completion by applying joint Bayesian spatial models and areal geospatial models ideally informed by population-based health data to produce community-based TPT coverage estimates.

Conclusion

Despite overall progress in TPT coverage and treatment completion in Ethiopia, substantial variations persist across districts. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions in underperforming areas, including strengthened program management and improved healthcare access, to optimize TPT delivery. By identifying districts with low facility-based TPT initiation and completion rates, policymakers and program implementers can design focused strategies to enhance TPT uptake and adherence, ultimately improving TB prevention outcomes nationwide.
