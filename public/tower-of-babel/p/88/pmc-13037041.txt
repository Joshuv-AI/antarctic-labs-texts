Clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a low-income country: Ethiopia
Authors: Zeki Abubeker, Tebarek Jemal Hassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaparoscopic cholecystectomy remains the gold standard and minimally invasive surgery for management of gallbladder disease, offering safe outcome. However, significant disparities exist across low -middle income countries. This study presents surgical outcomes of laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed at a high-volume general hospital with a hepatobiliary surgeon in Ethiopia.MethodsThe institutional based cross-sectional medical record review conducted was carried out at Addis Hiwot General Hospital, Addis Ababa. Based on previously published literature, a structured questionnaire was created for this study with the goal of collecting thorough data. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, and the χ² test was used to compare groups. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate the distribution of continuous variables. Means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for data that was normally distributed; medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used for data that was not normally distributed.ResultsA retrospective analysis of 424 patients who had laparoscopic cholecystectomy revealed that the female-to-male ratio was roughly 5:1, and the mean age was 42·2 years (SD 12·7). 91% of cases involved elective procedures. In 62·0% of patients, symptomatic cholelithiasis (biliary colic) was the most common indication, followed by chronic calculous cholecystitis (22·9%). The conversion rate to open cholecystectomy was 3·5% overall. The overall mean hospital stay was 21 h (SD 9.7), with a shorter duration observed in elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Within 30 days, 2.8% of patients experienced postoperative complications; no deaths were reported.ConclusionThe study demonstrated that laparoscopic cholecystectomy remains to be a safe procedure with low morbidity, conversion rate and no mortality. Even at general hospital in a low-income country. These findings underscore the urgent need for investment in surgical infrastructure.

Background

Benign gallbladder disease—including cholelithiasis and choledocholithiasis—represents a growing global health burden, contributing substantially to morbidity, disability, and economic loss [1]. These conditions affect millions worldwide and place considerable demands on healthcare systems [2, 3]. Gallstone disease represents a significant global health burden, with prevalence patterns influenced by demographic, genetic, and lifestyle factors. A comprehensive meta-analysis encompassing 115 studies and a pooled sample of 32,610,568 individuals estimated the global prevalence of gallstones at 6.1% (95% CI 5.6–6.5) [2]. Notably, the condition was more prevalent among females than males, with rates of 7.6% and 5.4%, respectively. This sex-based disparity may reflect hormonal influences, particularly the role of estrogen in cholesterol supersaturation and gallstone formation, as well as differences in health-seeking behavior and diagnostic access [2]. In 2021, the global age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR) for benign gallbladder and biliary tract diseases was estimated at 870 per 100,000 population, with marked variation by sex and national development status [1]. Globally, an estimated 115 cholecystectomies are performed annually per 100,000 population [3], underscoring the widespread need for operative management of symptomatic gallstone disease and its complications. In England alone, nearly 70,000 cholecystectomies are performed each year, reflecting the scale of demand on surgical services [3, 4]. In the United States, gallstone disease affects an estimated 20 million individuals, with between 300,000 and 750,000 cholecystectomies performed annually [5], costing nearly 6.5 billion dollars each year [2]. In Europe, the annual volume of cholecystectomy procedures exceeds 900,000, reflecting the substantial burden of gallbladder disease and the widespread adoption of LC as the standard operative approach [5, 6]. This burden necessitates efficient, equitable, and context-sensitive strategies for diagnosis and treatment. As health systems worldwide contend with rising case volumes, particularly in resource-constrained settings, optimizing care pathways and surgical access remains a critical priority.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the preferred surgical technique for the removal of a diseased gallbladder and has become the standard approach for cholecystectomy since its introduction in the early 1990s [7]. Compared with open surgery, the laparoscopic method offers several advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery [8]. These benefits have led to its widespread adoption in high-resource settings and increasing uptake globally. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is indicated in a range of benign gallbladder conditions, including acute and chronic cholecystitis, symptomatic cholelithiasis, biliary dyskinesia (both hypomotility and hypermotility disorders), acalculous cholecystitis, gallstone pancreatitis, and gallbladder polyps or masses [7, 9]. Patient selection is critical to ensure safety and efficacy. Contraindications to laparoscopic cholecystectomy include inability to tolerate general anesthesia or pneumoperitoneum, uncorrectable coagulopathy, and the presence of known metastatic disease [7]. Common pathological conditions contributing to surgical difficulty include acute calculous cholecystitis, empyema, gangrene, perforation, and Mirizzi syndrome. Some authors have proposed criteria for identifying a “bad gallbladder,” including necrosis, gangrene, perforation, Mirizzi syndrome, dense adhesions obscuring anatomical landmarks, prior tube cholecystostomy, operative duration exceeding 120 min, or the need for conversion to open surgery [10]. Reported rates of difficult LC vary, with estimates reaching up to 26% in large series [5]. Reported conversion rates from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy range from 2·6% to 7·7%, primarily attributed to intraoperative difficulty associated with severe inflammation, distorted anatomy, or other complicating factors in developed countries [11–13].

Evidence from systematic reviews conducted in sub-Saharan African countries indicates that the conversion rate from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy ranges from 0.7% to 6.3%, which is comparable to rates reported in developed countries [14].

Surgical outcomes following laparoscopic cholecystectomy are influenced by multiple factors, including timing of surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, operative duration, and the number of prior emergency admissions for gallbladder disease. Patients typically undergo laparoscopic cholecystectomy via one of three clinical pathways: emergency cholecystectomy during an index admission for acute gallbladder pathology; elective cholecystectomy without previous emergency presentation, usually for symptomatic cholelithiasis; or delayed cholecystectomy following one or more prior emergency admissions, often after initial conservative management. A population-based prospective cohort study conducted across 167 hospitals in the UK included 8,909 patients undergoing cholecystectomy. The distribution of surgical timing was as follows: 16·3% underwent emergency cholecystectomy, 37·0% delayed cholecystectomy, and 46·8% elective cholecystectomy [3]. Analysis from this large, prospective cohort study revealed that the 30-day readmission rate was 7·1%, and the overall complication rate within 30 days was 10·8% [3].

There is a scarcity of studies evaluating the outcomes of laparoscopic surgery in Ethiopia. This study presents surgical outcomes from a high-volume hepatobiliary center in Ethiopia, contributing valuable data to this underrepresented field.

Methodology

Study area and period

This study was conducted at the Department of Surgery, Addis Hiwot General Hospital, a general hospital located in Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. The hospital has 75 patient beds, three major operating rooms. It has Two full time general surgeons and part time working urologist, colorectal, and hepatobiliary surgeons. All the patients in the study were operated by the hepatobiliary surgeon. The study was conducted over six months from February 1/2021 to 139 January 30/2025.

No specific sampling technique was employed; instead, all consecutive patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery on each clinic day were included until the required sample size was reached.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed from previously published research, designed to obtain detailed information in three domains: sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex), clinical profiles (ASA classification, history of cholecystitis), and laboratory and imaging findings. Before full-scale data collection began, the instrument was pretested on 5% of the intended sample population to guarantee internal consistency, clarity, and relevance. The phrasing and structure of the questions were improved based on input from the pilot phase. Secondary data was taken from electronic medical records in addition to primary data collected via the questionnaire. These records complemented and validated the self-reported data gathered during the study by offering comprehensive clinical and biochemical information about patients who had laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Data analysis

Data analysis use performed using SPSS version 27. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the χ² test. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, means and standard deviations (SD) were reported; for non-normally distributed data, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used.

Discussion

This study represents one of the largest investigations into the outcomes of laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) in Ethiopia, providing valuable insights into patient demographics and surgical trends within a major referral center. The median age of patients undergoing LC was in the fourth decade of life, consistent with the known epidemiology of gallstone disease, which typically manifests in middle age due to cumulative metabolic and hormonal factors [18, 19].

A notable finding was the predominance of female patients, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 5:1. Previous studies from Kenya reported a female-to-male ratio of 4:1, previous Ethiopia study 5.1: 1, while a study from South Africa reported a ratio of approximately 5.6:1 [20]. These findings suggest that the sex distribution of indications for laparoscopic surgery remains consistent, irrespective of geographical and sociodemographic differences. The age and sex distribution observed in this study was also consistent with findings from previous research conducted in Ethiopia [21]. However, this sex distribution exceeds that reported in high-income settings. For example, a large multicenter study conducted in the UK involving 8,909 patients across 167 hospitals documented a female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1 [3]. The observed discrepancy may be attributed to a combination of geographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural factors. Additionally, hormonal influences—particularly the role of estrogen in cholesterol metabolism—may further explain the higher prevalence of gallstone disease among women. Further research is warranted to explore the underlying drivers of these demographic differences and to assess whether similar trends are observed across other surgical conditions in low-resource settings.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) can be categorized into three clinical pathways: elective cholecystectomy performed without prior emergency admission, emergency cholecystectomy conducted during an initial acute presentation, and delayed cholecystectomy following one or more previous emergency admissions related to gallbladder pathology [3].

In our study, the majority of patients (91·7%) underwent elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), whereas only 8·3% received surgery during an emergency admission. This pattern differs from reports in South Africa, where of 1,000 LCs performed, 70·7% were elective and 29·3% were undertaken during the same admission [20]. The distribution in our cohort also contrasts with findings from a large multicentre study in the UK, which reported that 46·8% of cholecystectomies were elective, 37·0% were delayed following prior emergency admissions, and 16·3% were performed as emergency procedures [3]. Similarly, a large global prospective study involving 21,706 surgical patients across 57 countries found that cholecystectomies were performed in elective (49·9%), emergency (19·7%), and delayed (30·5%) settings [22].

The lower proportion of emergency and delayed procedures in our cohort may reflect differences in the adoption of global recommendations, availability of surgical expertise, scheduling capacity, and thresholds for operative intervention in resource-limited settings. The predominance of elective procedures could indicate a more conservative approach to acute gallbladder disease, potentially driven by limited access to emergency theatre time, anaesthetic support, or surgical expertise during off-hours. Conversely, the relatively low rate of delayed cholecystectomy may suggest under-recognition or under-documentation of prior emergency presentations, or barriers to timely follow-up after initial non-operative management. Together, these findings highlight important health-system constraints that shape surgical decision-making in low-resource environments and underscore the need for strategies to improve timely access to emergency and delayed cholecystectomy.

Another key quality indicator in laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is the rate of conversion to open cholecystectomy. Conversion is generally necessitated by intraoperative challenges such as dense adhesions, unclear biliary anatomy, or complications that compromise the safety of continuing laparoscopically. Although these factors are often patient-related, the likelihood of conversion is also influenced by surgical expertise and institutional experience.

In our study, the conversion rate was 3·5%, which aligns closely with international benchmarks. A large systematic review from sub-Saharan Africa reported conversion rates ranging from 0·7% to 6·3% [14], while a multicentre study from the UK documented a rate of 3·3% [3]. These findings suggest that, despite resource limitations, surgical outcomes in our setting are comparable to those achieved in more developed health systems, particularly when procedures are performed in high-volume tertiary centres.

The conversion rate observed in our cohort may reflect both the complexity of cases referred to large private referral hospitals and the evolving proficiency of surgical teams in Ethiopia. Continued investment in laparoscopic training, structured mentorship, and surgical infrastructure is essential to further reduce conversion rates and to strengthen the safety and efficiency of minimally invasive surgery in low-resource settings.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is widely recognized as the standard surgical approach for gallbladder pathology, offering substantial advantages over open techniques, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and improved quality of life. Nevertheless, despite its minimally invasive nature, LC is not without risk, and postoperative complications remain an important measure of surgical quality and patient safety.

In our study, the overall postoperative complication rate was 2·8%, with most events classified as Clavien–Dindo grade I, indicating minor complications requiring minimal intervention. This rate is notably lower than those reported in comparable studies from other regions. For example, a study from South Africa documented complication rates ranging between 5·1%, with bile duct injuries and bile leaks occurring in 0·4–1·2% and 1·4–1·8% of cases, respectively [20]. Similarly, study in Montenegro (2005–2014) reported a complication rate of 13·1% among 740 patients, alongside a conversion rate of 3·9% and a bile duct injury rate of 1·9%, with no mortality observed [23]. A large multicentre study in the UK further highlighted the burden of postoperative morbidity, reporting 30-day readmission and complication rates of 7·1% and 10·8%, respectively [3]. Evidence from broader analyses reinforces these findings. A meta-analysis of 151 studies encompassing more than 505,000 patients confirmed that LC is a safe procedure, associated with low morbidity (pooled prevalence 1·6–5·3%) and mortality (0·08–0·14%) [24]. Conversion rates to open cholecystectomy ranged between 4·2% and 6·2%, underscoring the reliability of LC across diverse clinical settings. Additionally, a large global prospective study involving 21,706 surgical patients across 57 countries reported a 30-day postoperative complication rate of 8·0%, with mortality at 0·4%. Bile leaks (Strasberg grade A) occurred in 1·3% of patients, while severe bile duct injuries (Strasberg grades B–E) were reported in 0·2% [22].

The comparatively lower complication rate observed in our cohort, and in other studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), may reflect the predominance of younger patients, lower ASA risk profiles, and the high proportion of elective procedures. Nonetheless, the occurrence of even minor complications highlights the need for continued vigilance, structured surgical training, and robust postoperative monitoring to ensure optimal outcomes. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of LC in resource-limited settings, while reinforcing the importance of context-specific data to guide surgical practice and inform policy development.

Length of hospital stay is an important indicator of the quality and safety of laparoscopic surgery. In our study, the mean hospital stay was less than 24 h, which is slightly shorter than that reported in a South African study (mean 1·5 days) [20]. This finding is consistent with reports from high-income countries, where short hospital stays following laparoscopic cholecystectomy are well established. The ability to achieve comparable outcomes in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) highlights the safety of laparoscopic cholecystectomy and underscores its potential to deliver efficient care when supported by adequate infrastructure and surgical expertise.

The principal strength of this study is the generation of high-level evidence on minimally invasive cholecystectomy, specifically regarding safety, conversion rates, and complications, based on a large dataset. However, the study has important limitations. All procedures were performed in a private hospital by the most senior surgeon in the country, which may limit generalizability. In addition, the retrospective nature of the data introduces inherent biases. Future research should include multicentre studies across both governmental and private settings, with surgeons of varying levels of experience, to enhance external validity.

In conclusion, despite persistent inequities in access to laparoscopic surgery in Ethiopia, the procedure remains safe, with low morbidity, a low conversion rate to open surgery, and no associated mortality.
