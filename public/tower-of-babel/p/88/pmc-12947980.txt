The Impact of Two Data‐Generating Processes for Competing Risk Data on the Discrimination and Calibration of Two Types of Competing Risk Regression Models
Authors: Peter C Austin, Hein Putter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMonte Carlo simulations are an important tool in modern statistical research. The data‐generating process is foundational to any simulation. In survival analysis, a competing risk is an event whose occurrence precludes the occurrence of the primary event of interest. Two data‐generating processes have been described for simulating competing risk data: one based on all the cause‐specific hazard functions for the different types of events, and one based on a subdistribution hazard model for the primary event of interest. There is a paucity of research on the impact of the choice of data‐generating process. We used a series of Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the impact of the choice of data‐generating process on the performance of prediction models when assessing discrimination using the time‐dependent AUC and accuracy using the time‐dependent Brier score. We also assessed the impact of the choice of competing risk regression used for computing smoothed event probabilities for use when computing the calibration metrics ICI (integrated calibration index), E50, and E90. The impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on both the time‐dependent AUC and the time‐dependent Brier score was minimal. When computing the ICI, E50, and E90, we recommend that researchers use a model for computing smoothed event probabilities that is concordant with the type of model whose calibration is being assessed.Keywords: competing risks, data‐generating process, model calibration, model discrimination, Monte Carlo simulations

Introduction

In survival analysis, a competing risk is an event whose occurrence precludes the occurrence of the primary outcome of interest [1]. In a study in which the primary outcome of interest is time to death due to cardiovascular causes, death due to noncardiovascular causes is a competing risk. An individual who dies of cancer is no longer at risk of death due to cardiovascular causes.

There are two primary sets of regression models for the analysis of competing risk data. The Fine‐Gray subdistribution model directly models the association of covariates with the cumulative incidence function (CIF) for the outcome of interest [2]. An advantage to the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model is that the direction of the estimated regression coefficient indicates the direction of the effect of the associated covariate on the CIF. Thus, increasing values of a covariate for which the regression coefficient is positive are associated with an increase in the CIF, while increasing values of a covariate for which the regression coefficient is negative are associated with a decrease in the CIF. However, one cannot easily determine the magnitude of the increase or decrease in the CIF [3]. An alternative to the use of the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model is to fit cause‐specific hazard models for each of the competing risks (e.g., fit one cause‐specific hazard model for cardiovascular death and one cause‐specific hazard model for noncardiovascular death). One can combine the resultant cause‐specific hazard models to estimate the CIF for each individual and for each of the types of events [4]. An advantage to the use of the cause‐specific hazard models is that, at every value of time, the sum of the cause‐specific CIFs is constrained to be at most 1 (100%), whereas this is not necessarily true for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model, for which the sum of the individual cause‐specific CIFs can exceed 1 (100%) [5]. A drawback to the use of all the cause‐specific hazard models is that it can be difficult to determine the direction (and the magnitude) of the effect of a given covariate on the cause‐specific CIF. While many authors appear to use the term “competing risk regression model” as a synonym for “Fine‐Gray subdistribution regression model,” both the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and the use of all cause‐specific hazard models are valid methods for estimating cause‐specific CIFs for specific covariate patterns.

Monte Carlo simulations are an important tool in modern statistical research. The foundation of any Monte Carlo simulation is the data‐generating process. Beyersmann and colleagues described two different data‐generating processes for simulating competing risks data. The first data‐generating process is based on the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model [6]. The second data‐generating process is based on the set of all cause‐specific hazard models [7]. Bonneville and colleagues reviewed a variety of data‐generating processes for competing risks data such that the proportional subdistribution hazards assumption holds for at least one of the failure causes [8]. They argued that it is preferable to use methods based on the set of all cause‐specific hazard models.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has compared the impact of the choice of data‐generating process for competing risks. In particular, the impact of the concordance or discordance between the type of competing risk regression model that was fit (i.e., Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model vs. combining all the cause‐specific hazard models) and the data‐generating process that was used to simulate competing risk data has not been examined. Specifically, the impact of estimating cause‐specific CIFs by combining all the cause‐specific hazard models when the data were generated using a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazards model has not been assessed. Similarly, the impact of estimating a cause‐specific CIF using a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model when the data were generated using all cause‐specific hazard models has not been assessed.

Clinical prediction models are increasingly being used to inform clinical decision making. In the setting of time‐to‐event outcomes, clinical prediction models allow one to estimate an individual's risk of experiencing the outcome of interest within a specified duration of time. An important aspect of assessing the accuracy of clinical prediction models is the assessment of discrimination and calibration [9, 10]. Discrimination refers to the ability of a model to discriminate between those who do and do not experience the event of interest, while calibration refers to the concordance between predicted and observed risk.

A method was recently described for assessing the calibration of competing risk regression models [11]. The authors proposed three calibration metrics: the integrated calibration index (ICI), E50, and E90. The ICI is the mean absolute difference between smoothed observed cumulative incidence probabilities and predicted cumulative incidence probabilities derived from the model whose calibration is being assessed. E50 and E90 are the median and 90th percentile of the absolute difference between these two quantities. In the initial paper proposing these metrics, the authors suggested that a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model be used to compute the smoothed observed probabilities. It is not known whether assessment of calibration depends on the competing risk regression model that is used to obtain smoothed observed probabilities.

Accurate evaluation of competing risk prediction models is essential for valid estimation of event probabilities in clinical and epidemiological research. Monte Carlo simulations are frequently used to examine the performance of such models; however, the influence of the underlying data‐generating process—whether based on cause‐specific or subdistribution hazard functions—has not been systematically studied. Understanding how this choice influences measures of discrimination and calibration is important to ensure methodological rigor and the validity of simulation studies.

The objective of the current paper was two‐fold. First, to assess the impact of discordance between the type of competing risk regression model used to simulate data and the type of competing risk regression model fit to the simulated data on the performance of the fitted competing risk regression model. Second, to assess the impact of the choice of competing risk regression model used to compute smoothed observed probabilities under different data‐generating processes. The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we briefly describe two data‐generating processes for competing risks data. In Section 3, we describe a series of Monte Carlo simulations that were designed to address the study objectives. In Section 4, we report the results of these simulations. Finally, in Section 5, we summarize our findings and place them in the context of the existing literature.

Data‐Generating Processes for Competing Risks Data

We briefly describe two data‐generating processes for competing risks data that were developed by Beyersmann and colleagues. The first is based on an underlying Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazards model for the primary event of interest, while the second is based on specifying the cause‐specific hazards models for each of the events of interest.

Data‐Generating Process Based on a Fine‐Gray Subdistribution Hazards Model for the Primary Event of Interest (FG‐DGP)

Beyersmann and colleagues described a data‐generating process based on a method of indirect simulation [6] (section 5.3.6 of the cited work), which in turn is based on an approach described by Fine and Gray [2]. This approach ensures that the sum of the two CIFs equals exactly 1 as time approaches infinity. In doing so, one needs only to specify the underlying subdistribution hazard function for the primary event of interest and the hazard function for the conditional distribution of the event time given failure from a competing event. One is not required to specify the cause‐specific hazard functions for the different event types.

Assume that one has simulated a vector X of baseline covariates for each individual in the synthetic population (e.g., from a multivariate normal distribution or from a different multivariate distribution). Furthermore, assume that there are two competing events: the primary event of interest (e.g., cardiovascular death) and a single competing event (e.g., noncardiovascular death). The data‐generating process for competing risks outcomes requires specifying three parameters: p, β, and α. The first parameter, p, denotes the proportion of subjects with covariates equal to zero who experience the event of interest as t→∞ (where t denotes time [this parameter quantifies the relative frequency with which the two types of events occur]). The second and third parameters denote vectors of regression coefficients for the two competing risks. The first, β, denotes the regression coefficients for the underlying subdistribution hazard model for the primary event, while the second, α, denote the regression coefficients for the association between the covariates and hazard function of the competing events, conditional on a competing event occurring.

The first step is to generate a binary variable denoting the type of event that occurred: ZT=1 denoting that a primary event occurred and ZT=2 denoting that a competing event occurred. This data generating process, originally described by Beyersmann and colleagues (and which in turn was based on an approach described by Fine and Gray [2]), has subsequently been used in several studies that used Monte Carlo simulations to examine the performance of statistical methods in settings with competing risks [5, 12, 13].

Data‐Generating Process Based on Models for All the Cause‐Specific Hazard Functions (CSH‐DGP)

Beyersmann and colleagues described a data‐generating process for simulating competing risks data that requires specifying the cause‐specific hazard functions for each of the event types (see section 3.2 of the cited work) [7]. Assuming two types of events, one specifies the baseline cause‐specific hazard functions for the two types of events: α01(t) and α02(t). One also specifies two vectors of regression coefficients for the two cause‐specific hazard models: β1 and β2. Then, for a subject with simulated covariate vector X, the two cause‐specific hazard functions are: h1(t)=α01(t)eβ1X and h2(t)=α02(t)eβ2X, respectively. The all‐cause hazard function is h1(t)+h2(t). One can then use the approach described by Bender and colleagues to simulate an event time with the specified all‐cause hazard function [14]. Applying Bender's approach for simulating event times requires inverting the cumulative all‐cause hazard function. In many instances the inverse of this function may not be analytically tractable and numerical methods may be required to compute the inverse of the cumulative all‐cause hazard function. Finally, once an event time T has been simulated, one simulates the event type using a Bernoulli distribution such that the probability of a primary (type 1) event is h1(T)h1(T)+h2(T).

For both data‐generating processes that we have summarized, one can also induce censoring by specifying a censoring distribution and then simulating a censoring time for each individual. The observed survival time is the minimum of the simulated event time and the simulated censoring time. Subjects are classified as censored if the simulated censoring time is less than the simulated event time.

Monte Carlo Simulation Methods

In reporting the design of these simulations, we used the ADEMP (Aims, Data‐generating mechanisms, Methods, Estimands, Performance measures) framework proposed by Morris and colleagues [15].

Aims

We conducted a series of Monte Carlo simulations to address two questions: first, the effect of discordance (vs. concordance) between the data‐generating process for competing risks data and the competing risks regression model that was fit to the simulated data on discrimination and accuracy of predictions made at specified prediction horizons. Second, to examine the impact of the type of competing risk regression model that was used to compute smooth observed event probabilities when computing the calibration metrics ICI, E50, and E90.

Data‐Generating Processes

We used the two data‐generating processes described in Section 2, one based on the set of all cause‐specific hazard models (CSH‐DGP) and one based on a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model for the primary outcome (FG‐DGP).

Fine‐Gray Subdistribution Hazard Data‐Generating Process (FG‐DGP)

For each subject in a super‐population of size 1 000 000, we generated a single continuous baseline covariate from a standard normal distribution: Xi˜N(0,1). We used the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard data‐generating process described in Section 2.1. The parameter α, which denotes the regression coefficient for the association between the covariates and conditional hazard function of the competing event was fixed at log(1.5). The parameter β, which denotes the regression coefficients for the underlying subdistribution hazard model for the primary event was allowed to take on four values: log(1), log(1.5), log(2), and log(3). These denote the subdistribution hazard ratios for continuous covariate and the primary event of interest. The parameter p (which denotes the proportion of those subjects whose covariate value was zero who experienced the event of interest as time approaches infinity) was allowed to take on five values: 0.25, 0.33, 0.50, 0.67, and 0.75. We used a complete factorial design and thus considered 20 = 4 × 5 scenarios. In each of the 20 super‐populations we computed the quintiles of event times (i.e., the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles of event times). These will be used as the prediction time horizons at which predictions will be made in the analyses described below.

We induced censoring by assuming an exponential distribution for censoring times. We used a bisection procedure to determine the rate parameter for the censoring distribution so that 20% of the individuals in the super‐population were subject to censoring [16]. We thus simulated 20 super‐populations, each consisting of 1 000 000 individuals. We use the acronym FG‐DGP to refer to this data‐generating process for generating competing risk outcomes.

Cause‐Specific Hazard Model Data‐Generating Process (CSH‐DGP)

For each subject in a super‐population of size 1 000 000, we generated a single continuous baseline covariate from a standard normal distribution: Xi˜N(0,1). We used the cause‐specific hazard data‐generating process described in Section 2.2 to simulate competing risk outcomes for each subject in the super‐population. We assumed that the two baseline cause‐specific hazard functions were Weibull distributions. We used the Weibull parametrization of Moriña and Navarro, where the survival function is parametrized as exp−λjtϕj and the density function is parameterized as λjϕjtϕj−1exp−λjtϕj, where λj=exp−ϕjβ0,j+Xβ1,j [17], where j denotes the cause of failure (j = 1 denotes the primary event of interest; j = 2 denotes the competing event). Note that Moriña and Navarro used the parameter p, while we have replaced this with the parameter ϕ, since our data‐generating process based on a Fine‐Gray subdistribution model also uses a parameter p and we wanted to avoid confusion by using the same parameter to refer to two different quantities. We fixed ϕj = 1 for both event types (i.e., we assumed an exponential distribution). We fixed β0,1=2 for the primary event type (i.e., type 1 events). We also fixed β1,2=−log(1.25) for the competing event (i.e., type 2 events) so that the cause‐specific hazard ratio for the competing events was equal to 1.25 (based on the parametrization proposed by Moriña and Navarro) [17]. We allowed two factors to vary in the design of these simulations: the cause‐specific hazard ratio for the primary event and the ratio between the cause‐specific hazard function for the primary event and the cause‐specific hazard for the competing events. This latter allows one to control the relative frequency of the two types of events. The first factor took on four values: 1, 1.25, 1.5, and 2 (i.e., β1,1= −log(1), −log(1.25), −log(1.5), and −log (2), respectively, when using the parametrization of Moriña and Navarro). The second factor took on nine values: 1, 1.25, 0.8, 1.5, 2/3, 2, 0.5, 3, and 1/3. We used a complete factorial design and thus created 36 = 4 × 9 super‐populations.

We induced censoring by assuming an exponential distribution for censoring times. We used a bisection procedure to determine the rate parameter for the censoring distribution so that 20% of the individuals in the super‐population were subject to censoring [16]. We thus simulated 36 super‐populations, each consisting of 1 000 000 individuals. In each of the 36 super‐populations we computed the quintiles of event times (i.e., the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles of event times). These will be used as the prediction time horizons at which predictions will be made in the analyses described below.

The methods described in the previous two paragraphs simulated data in which event times had an exponential distribution. We repeated the above process assuming a Weibull distribution for event times. To do so, we changed β0,1=1.5 and chose ϕj so that the cumulative cause‐specific hazard function for the primary event over the interval [0,365] was equal to the cumulative cause‐specific hazard function under the exponential distribution above: ϕj=log(365)−2log(365)−β0,1≈0.886. The value of β0,2 was chosen as above. Apart from that, the simulations were identical to those described above. We use the acronym CSH‐DGP to refer to this data‐generating process for generating competing risk outcomes.

Statistical Analyses in the Simulated Datasets (Methods)

From each super‐population (20 super‐population when using the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard data‐generating process and 36 super‐populations when using the cause‐specific hazard data‐generating process), we drew 1000 pairs of derivation and validation samples, each of size 1000. Each random sample was selected using simple random sampling without replacement such that, in each pair of derivation and validation samples, no subject was contained in both samples.

In each derivation sample we used two methods to estimate the incidence of the primary outcome at each of the four prediction windows described above. First, we fit a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model in each derivation sample, in which the subdistribution hazard of the primary outcome was regressed on the single continuous predictor variable. We use the acronym FG‐Model to denote analyses in which a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model was fit to the simulated data, regardless of the nature of the data‐generating process that was used to generate the competing risk outcomes. Second, we fit the two cause‐specific hazard models for the two types of events. We then combined the two cause‐specific hazard functions. We use the acronym CSH‐Model to denote analyses in which both cause‐specific hazard models were fit to the simulated data, regardless of the nature of the data‐generating process that was used to generate the competing risk outcomes. Using the regression coefficients and baseline hazard (subdistribution hazard) functions estimated in the derivation, we obtained estimates of the risk of the outcome at each of the four prediction windows for each individual in the paired validation sample.

(Quasi) Estimands

The study does not focus on estimation of estimands, as there are no underlying parameters that are being estimated. Instead, the focus was on model performance metrics used for assessing the accuracy of estimated risk at specified prediction horizons.

The accuracy of the predictions obtained in each validation sample was assessed using five metrics: (i) the time‐dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (henceforth referred to as the AUC) at each of the four prediction windows [10, 18]; (ii) the time‐dependent Brier score at each of the four prediction windows [10, 18]; (iii) the ICI at each of the four prediction windows [11]; (iv) E50 at each of the four prediction windows [11]; (v) E90 at each of the four prediction windows [11]. For the first metric, increased values of the AUC denote improved accuracy, while for the other four metrics, decreased values of the metrics denote improved accuracy.

Calculation of the last three performance metrics (ICI, E50, and E90) requires the computation of smoothed observed event probabilities [11]. In the original paper describing these metrics, the authors proposed using a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model to regress the CIF of the primary event on the predicted probabilities of the primary event, with restricted cubic splines used to allow for a flexible nonlinear relationship between predicted probabilities and the CIF. To assess the impact of the choice of competing risk regression model used for computing smoothed observed event probabilities on the metrics, we computed each metric twice. First, we used the methods suggested by the authors who proposed these three metrics. Second, we used both cause‐specific hazard functions to compute the smoothed observed probabilities by regressing each of the two cause‐specific hazard functions on the predicted probabilities of the primary event, using restricted cubic splines to allow for a flexible nonlinear relationship between the predicted probabilities and the cause‐specific hazard functions. We use the acronyms Smooth‐FG and Smooth‐CSH to denote when the smoothed observed event probabilities were obtained using a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and the set of both cause‐specific hazard models, respectively.

Performance Measures

For the time‐varying AUC and the time‐varying Brier score, we compared the mean performance metric across the 1000 simulation replicates when a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model was used and when the set of two cause‐specific hazard models was used. We then computed the difference in the mean performance metrics for the two different modeling approaches. When comparing FG‐Model with CSH‐Model, the model with the lowest mean performance metric had the superior performance.

ICI, E50, and E90 take strictly nonnegative values, with a value of zero denoting perfect calibration, and with increasing values denoting increasing lack of calibration. When using FGM‐DGP, then FG‐Model would be the correctly specified model. Similarly, when using CSH‐DGP, then using CSH‐Model would be the correctly specified model. A correctly specified model should have better calibration than an incorrectly specified model. Thus, ICI, E50, and E90 should be closer to zero for a correctly specified model than for an incorrectly specified model. We computed the mean performance metric (ICI, E50, or E90) across the 1000 simulation replicates for each of the four methods: FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG, FG‐Model/Smooth‐CSH, CSH‐Model/Smooth‐FG, and CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH.

Note that we are computing the mean of performance metrics (e.g., time‐varying AUC) and not estimating parameters. Thus, we do not estimate standard errors or compute the empirical coverage rates of confidence intervals.

Software

The simulations were conducted using the R statistical programming language (version 3.6.3) [19]. When using the CSH‐DGP, competing risk outcomes were simulated using the crisk.sim function from the survsim package (version 1.1.8). The Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model was fit using the FGR function from the riskRegression package (version 2020.12.08), while the cause‐specific hazard models were fit using the CSC function from the riskRegression package. The time‐dependent AUC and the time‐dependent Brier score were computed using the Score function from the riskRegression package. Simulation results were summarized using the rsimsum package (version 0.13.0).

Monte Carlo Simulation Results

We report our results separately for each of the two data‐generating processes that were used to simulate competing risk outcomes.

Fine‐Gray Subdistribution Hazard Data‐Generating Process for the Primary Event (FG‐DGP)

We considered 20 scenarios when using the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard data‐generating process. Combined with examining model performance at four prediction time horizons, this resulted in 80 comparisons.

The values of the mean time‐dependent AUC across the 80 settings are reported graphically in the left panel of Figure S1 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent AUC for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model fit to the validation sample across the 80 settings ranged from 0.499 to 0.836, with a median of 0.679 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.587 and 0.762). Across the 80 comparisons, the difference between the mean time‐dependent AUC for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model fit to the validation sample and the mean time‐dependent AUC for the cause‐specific hazard model fit to the validation sample ranged from −0.003 to 0.002, with the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles all equal to 0. Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model, there was essentially no impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model discrimination, as assessed using the time‐dependent AUC.

The values of the mean time‐dependent Brier scores across the 80 settings are reported graphically in the right panel of Figure S1 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent Brier score for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model fit to the validation sample across the 80 settings ranged from 0.046 to 0.249, with a median of 0.160 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.116 and 0.195). Across the 80 comparisons, the difference in the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the cause‐specific hazard model (cause‐specific hazard model—Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model) ranged from −0.00002 to 0.00090, with a median of 0.00009 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.00003 and 0.00019). Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model, the impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model accuracy, as assessed using the time‐dependent Brier score, was negligible (however, the direction tended to be in favor of the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model).

The values of the mean ICI across the 80 settings are reported graphically in Figure 1, which uses nested loop plots to report the mean ICI for each of the four combinations of prediction methods and smoothing method in each of the 80 settings [20]. For each of the 80 settings, four values of ICI are reported: (i) using Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard for prediction and Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard for computing smoothed observed probabilities (FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG); (ii) using Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard for prediction and cause‐specific hazard models for computing smoothed observed probabilities (FG‐Model/Smooth‐CSH); (iii) using cause‐specific hazard models for prediction and Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard for computing smoothed observed probabilities (CSH‐Model/Smooth‐FG); (iv) using cause‐specific hazard models for prediction and cause‐specific hazard models for computing smoothed observed probabilities (CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH). While the use of FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG tended to result in values of ICI that were closer to zero compared to the other three methods., differences between FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG and CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH were tended to be minimal. The difference between FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG and CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH (CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH—FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG) ranged from −0.00020 to 0.01640 with a median of 0.00050 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.00010 and 0.00150). Qualitatively similar results were obtained for E50 and E90 (results not shown). To summarize these observations, when the data were generated using a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model, when fitting models whose linear predictor was correctly specified, the computed calibration metrics were closer to zero when the fitted model used for predictions was concordant with the model used for computing smoothed observed probabilities.

CSH‐DGP for the Primary Event (Exponential Distribution)

We considered 36 scenarios when using the cause‐specific hazard data‐generating process when event times followed an exponential distribution. Combined with examining model performance at four prediction time horizons, this resulted in 144 comparisons.

The values of the mean time‐dependent AUC across the 144 settings are reported graphically in the left panel of Figure S2 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent AUC for the cause‐specific hazard model fit to the validation sample across the 108 comparisons ranged from 0.499 to 0.712, with a median of 0.579 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.532 and 0.639). In examining the left panel of Figure S2, one observes that the mean time‐dependent AUC increased as the magnitude of the true cause‐specific hazard ratio increased. Across the 144 comparisons, the difference between the mean time‐dependent AUC for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model fit to the validation sample and the mean time‐dependent AUC for the cause‐specific hazard model fit to the validation sample (cause‐specific hazard model—Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model) ranged from −0.00658 to 0.02415, with a median of 0 (25th and 75th percentiles: −0.00039 and 0.00003). Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on cause‐specific hazard models, there was frequently no impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model discrimination, as assessed using the time‐dependent AUC. However, there were a small number of settings in which the use of a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model resulted in predictions with modestly lower AUC (i.e., worse discrimination) compared to the use of cause‐specific hazard models (with the caveat that there is currently no consensus as to what constitutes a meaningful difference in AUC).

The values of the mean time‐dependent Brier scores across the 144 settings are reported graphically in the right panel of Figure S2 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent Brier score for the cause‐specific hazard models fit to the validation sample across the 144 settings ranged from 0.039 to 0.250, with a median of 0.154 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.099 and 0.205). In examining the right panel of Figure S2, one observes that the mean Brier score increased with the time prediction horizon. Across the 144 comparisons, the difference in the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the cause‐specific hazard model (Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model—cause‐specific hazard model) ranged from 0.00002 to 0.00121, with a median of 0.00024 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.00013 and 0.00045). Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on cause‐specific hazard models, the impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model accuracy, as assessed using the time‐dependent Brier score, was negligible.

The values of the mean ICI across the 144 settings are reported graphically in Figure 2, which has a structure similar to that of Figure 1. Recall that in this set of simulations, the competing risk outcomes were generated using the set of both cause‐specific hazard models. Furthermore, a correctly specified model should have an ICI closer to zero than that of an incorrectly specified model. Across the 144 comparisons, the difference in the mean ICI values (FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG—CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH) ranged from −0.0003 to 0.0016, with a median of 0.0001 (25th and 75th percentiles: −0.0001 and 0.0003, respectively). In contrast to this, using either CSH‐Model/Smooth‐FG or FG‐Model/Smooth‐CSH often resulted in ICI values that were discernibly further from zero than those obtained using CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH. Thus, the primary observation is that using the CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH tended to result in values of ICI that were closer to zero than the other three methods. However, the FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG method resulted in values of ICI that were often very similar to those obtained using CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH. Qualitatively similar results were obtained for E50 and E90 (results not shown). To summarize these observations, when the data were generated using a cause‐specific hazard model, fitting a correctly specified model and using the same type of model for computing the smoothed observed probabilities resulted in values of ICI that were closer to zero (the ICI value that one would anticipate for a correctly specified model). Thus, it was important that the same type of competing risks regression model be used for computing smoothed observed probabilities as was used for estimating risk.

CSH‐DGP for the Primary Event (Weibull Distribution)

We considered 36 scenarios when using the cause‐specific hazard data‐generating process when event times followed a Weibull distribution. Combined with examining model performance at four prediction time horizons, this resulted in 144 comparisons.

The values of the mean time‐dependent AUC across the 144 settings are reported graphically in the left panel of Figure S3 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent AUC for the cause‐specific hazard model fit to the validation sample across the 144 comparisons ranged from 0.499 to 0.760, with a median of 0.600 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.541 and 0.676). Across the 144 comparisons, the difference between the mean time‐dependent AUC for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model fit to the validation sample and the mean time‐dependent AUC for the cause‐specific hazard model fit to the validation sample (cause‐specific hazard model—Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model) ranged from −0.00967 to 0.02598, with a median of 0 (25th and 75th percentiles: −0.00019 and 0.00004). Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on cause‐specific hazard models with a Weibull distribution for event times, there was essentially no impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model discrimination, as assessed using the time‐dependent AUC (with the caveat that there is currently no consensus as to what constitutes a meaningful difference in AUC).

The values of the mean time‐dependent Brier scores across the 144 settings are reported graphically in the right panel of Figure S3 in the Supporting Information. The mean time‐dependent Brier score for the cause‐specific hazard models fit to the validation sample across the 144 settings ranged from 0.029 to 0.250, with a median of 0.151 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.091 and 0.196). Across the 144 comparisons, the difference in the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and the mean time‐dependent Brier score for the cause‐specific hazard model (Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model—cause‐specific hazard model) ranged from 0.00003 to 0.00174, with a median of 0.00039 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0.00018 and 0.00076). Thus, when the data‐generating process was based on cause‐specific hazard models with a Weibull distribution of event times, the impact of discordance between the fitted model and the data‐generating process on model accuracy, as assessed using the time‐dependent Brier score, was negligible.

The values of the mean ICI across the 144 settings are reported graphically in Figure 3, which has a similar structure to Figure 2 (exponential distribution of event times). When interpreting the results, recall that lower values of ICI denote better calibration and that a correctly specified model should have an ICI closer to zero compared to an incorrectly specified model. Across the 144 scenarios, the mean ICI was 0.018, 0.018, 0.024, and 0.024 for the CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH method, the FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG method, CSH‐Model/Smooth‐FG method, and the FG‐model/Smooth‐FG method, respectively. The CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH tended to result in the lowest ICI across the 144 settings. However, the ICI values obtained using the FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG method were very similar. The differences between these two methods (FG‐Model/Smooth‐FG—CSH‐Model/Smooth‐CSH) ranged from −0.00140 to 0.00200, with a median of 0.00010 (25th and 75th percentiles: 0 and 0.00040). Similar results were observed for E50 and E90 (results not shown). Thus, discordance between the fitted model and the model used to obtained smoothed observed probabilities resulted in an ICI further from zero compared to when there was concordance between the two models.

Discussion

There are two standard data‐generating processes for competing risks data: one based on the Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model and one based on the set of all the cause‐specific hazard models [6, 7]. However, the consequences of choosing one data‐generating process over the other have not been previously examined. We examined the impact of choice of data‐generating process on the calibration and discrimination of competing risk regression models.

There are primary conclusions that we draw from the current study. First, discordance between the fitted model (FG‐Model or CSH‐Model) and the nature of the data‐generating process (FG‐DGP or CSH‐DGP) did not have an impact on the discrimination as assessed using the time‐varying AUC or on overall accuracy as assessed using the time‐varying Brier score. Essentially identical estimates of model performance were obtained regardless of whether there was concordance or discordance between the nature of the fitted model for estimating risk and the nature of the data‐generating process. Second, when computing calibration metrics (ICI, E50, and E90) it is important that the same type of competing risks regression model be used for computing smoothed observed probabilities as was used for estimating risk. Discordance between these two models will tend to result in the computed concordance metric being further from zero compared to when there is concordance between the two models. These results provide guidance for the design and interpretation of simulation studies involving competing risks, clarifying when the choice of data‐generating process and smoothing model affects study conclusions. Future work could extend these findings to alternative data‐generating mechanisms and to settings with more complex model misspecification or multiple competing events.

Computation of ICI, E50, and E90, which are quantitative calibration metrics, requires the computation of smoothed observed event probabilities. In the original publication which described these metrics in the context of competing risks, the authors suggested that a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model be used to compute the smoothed observed event probabilities [11]. Based on the results of the current study, we suggest a modification to this approach. We suggest that the method used to compute the smoothed observed event probabilities be concordant with the nature of the model whose calibration is being assessed.

The primary limitation of the current study relates to its use of Monte Carlo simulations. Due to the computational complexity of the simulations, we were restricted to examining a limited number of scenarios. However, these scenarios included a range in terms of the relative frequency of the two types of events and of the magnitude of the subdistribution hazard ratio/cause‐specific hazard ratio relating the baseline predictor variable to the hazard of the primary outcome.

The current study focused on two data‐generating processes for competing risk outcomes. A third data‐generating process, the latent failure time approach, has also been described [7]. With the latent failure time approach, an event time is simulated for each of the different event types (e.g., using Bender's approach with each of the cause‐specific hazard functions). For a given individual, the simulated event time is the minimum of the different simulated cause‐specific event times, and the event type is the event type that had the shortest cause‐specific event time. Beyersmann and colleagues criticized this approach both for its lack of plausibility in biomedical settings and for nonidentifiability problems [7]. Consequently, our focus was on the other two data‐generating processes. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the only study that has examined the consequences of the choice of data‐generating process. Due to space constraints, we have restricted our examination to that of discrimination and calibration of prediction models.

In the current study we focused on one type of model misspecification: misspecification of the model for competing events. However, regression models can be subject to other types of misspecification: (i) specifying an incorrect functional form for a continuous variable (e.g., specifying that a continuous variable has a linear relation with the outcome, whereas the true relation is nonlinear); (ii) omitting an interaction; (iii) omitting an important predictor variable. Each of these forms of model misspecification could affect the accuracy of predictions derived from the fitted model. While the magnitude of the impact would likely vary across the types of misspecifications, one could assume that, in general, each of these types of misspecifications would decrease the accuracy of the resultant predictions.

In empirical analyses, the analyst does not know whether the true data‐generating process was based on a Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model or on the set of all the cause‐specific hazard models (indeed, the data could have arisen from a different data‐generating process). Regardless of which of the two methods were used to generate the competing risks outcomes, the choice of type of prediction model had at most a minimal impact on discrimination (as assessed using the time‐dependent AUC) and accuracy (as assessed using the time‐dependent Brier score). Our primary recommendation is that when computing calibration metrics that require smoothed event probabilities (i.e., ICI, E50, and E90) the best strategy is to have concordance between the type of model whose calibration is being assessed and the type of model using for computing smoothed observed probabilities.
