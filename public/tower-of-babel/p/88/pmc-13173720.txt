Adherence, safety, bioavailability, and acceptability of amaranth grain flatbread during pregnancy: evidence from a cluster randomized trial in Sidama region, Ethiopia
Authors: Lakew Mussie, Mehretu Belayneh, Francisco Guillen-Grima, Amanuel Yoseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMaternal anemia is a major public health concern in Ethiopia. Nutrient-dense local foods like amaranth may improve iron status during pregnancy. This study evaluated adherence, safety, bioavailability, and acceptability of amaranth grain flatbread during pregnancy in Sidama region, Ethiopia.MethodsBetween September 2024 and February 2025, a two-arm parallel cluster randomized controlled trial was carried out in the Hawela Lida district, Ethiopia. A total of 306 pregnant women (< 12 weeks gestation) were randomized to amaranth (n = 153) and maize flatbread (n = 153) for six months. Primary outcomes: haemoglobin concentration and iron deficiency anemia (IDA). Secondary outcomes: adherence, safety, bioavailability biomarkers, and acceptability. Blood samples were analyzed using HemoCue and ELISA. Mixed-effects models accounted for clustering and baseline covariates.ResultsOf 306 participants, 293 (95.8%) completed the study. The amaranth group had higher mean haemoglobin concentration (adjusted β = + 2.67, 95% CI: 2.41–2.91) and lower IDA (ARR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.26–0.84) than controls. Adherence was higher in amaranth arm (AOR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.08–1.44) and no serious adverse events occurred. Acceptability was greater in the intervention arm (AOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.21–2.46). Qualitative results supported quantitative findings, with participants reporting enthusiasm and readiness to continue consuming.ConclusionDaily amaranth flatbread is safe, highly acceptable, and improves maternal iron status, supporting its integration into maternal nutrition programs in low-resource settings.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06536153, registered on July 30, 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01315-2.

Introduction

Women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are disproportionately affected by maternal undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, which continue to be serious global health challenges. The most prevalent of them is iron deficiency anemia (IDA), which has a major impact on maternal morbidity, poor pregnancy outcomes, and intergenerational cycles of poor health [1]. According to recent estimates, anemia affects around 37% of pregnant women globally, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing the highest of this burden [2]. Depending on the location and population segment, the prevalence of anemia during pregnancy in Ethiopia varies from 23 to 41%, which is still unacceptable [3]. Effectiveness is restricted by obstacles including uneven supply chains, low adherence, and a lack of nutritional diversity, even in the face of continuous governmental initiatives like iron-folic acid supplements and prenatal counseling [4]. To support supplementation programs, it is imperative to investigate food-based, culturally acceptable, and sustainable alternatives.

A prospective neglected crop with a high nutritional density is amaranth (Amaranthus spp.). Compared to widely consumed cereals like maize and teff, its grain has significantly higher levels of iron, protein, and critical amino acids [5, 6]. Furthermore, when amaranth is cooked using conventional techniques that lower the phytate level and improve iron absorption, it exhibits excellent bioavailability of micronutrients [7]. Although earlier research has emphasized amaranth's nutritional makeup and ability to treat IDA [8], there is still a dearth of studies looking at how to incorporate it into maternal diets. Notably, our earlier study showed that, as compared to maize bread, amaranth-based flatbread decreased IDA and increased hemoglobin concentration in pregnant women [9]. Beyond biochemical effectiveness, though, the long-term viability of such dietary interventions depends on several other variables, such as safety, adherence, and acceptance in certain cultural and economic situations.

In maternal nutrition research, adherence to dietary treatments is frequently overlooked, even though it dictates their practical efficacy. Taste preferences, gastrointestinal side effects, household food dynamics, or perceived cultural inappropriateness can all cause pregnant women to stop or consume interventions inconsistently [10]. Safety is similarly important; although amaranth has been used for millennia, there is still a lack of rigorous research on any potential negative effects during pregnancy [11]. Additionally, there hasn't been much thorough research done on the economics and cultural acceptability of incorporating amaranth grain into regular diets, especially in rural Ethiopian populations. To ensure that treatments are both culturally appropriate and nutritionally effective, these aspects are crucial for directing programmatic adoption and scale-up.

Due to financial limitations, eating patterns in the Sidama region of southern Ethiopia continue to be dominated by cereals, with little variation and decreasing availability of meals derived from animals [12]. Despite being a common crop in this region, amaranth is frequently disregarded as a "wild" crop rather than a staple grain. Therefore, it is essential to comprehend how pregnant women and their households view and consume foods based on amaranth to turn this locally accessible crop into a sustainable maternal nutrition approach. Furthermore, assessing safety and adherence in a randomized controlled trial offers solid proof that connects effectiveness with practical viability. This study fills a significant research vacuum in maternal nutrition interventions by combining qualitative insights on cultural acceptance with quantitative evaluations of adherence and bioavailability.

Thus, this study’s objective was to use data from a cluster randomized controlled trial to investigate the acceptability, safety, bioavailability, and adherence of eating amaranth grain flatbread during pregnancy in the Sidama region of Ethiopia. The study aims to address these aspects to offer thorough insights that can guide the development, application, and expansion of food-based, culturally relevant methods to address maternal anemia and undernutrition in environments with limited resources.

Methods

Reporting and ethical considerations

The cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) followed the CONSORT 2010 extension for cluster trials (Supplementary File 1). The protocol was prospectively filed with the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (Registration ID: NCT06536153). The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Hawassa University provided ethical approval (IRB reference: IRB/027/16). Administrative approval was obtained from the Sidama Regional Health Bureau, the Hawela Lida District Health Office, and kebele (lowest administrative unit in Ethiopia) leaders. All participants provided written informed consent following community consent and sensitization initiatives.

Study setting

The trial was conducted in Hawela Lida district, Sidama Region, southern Ethiopia. The district located 289 km south of Addis Abeba and consists of 13 kebeles, 10 of which were included in the study. The district's population was estimated to be 131,848 in 2024, with women of reproductive age accounting for 24.3%. The local economy is dominated by subsistence farming of Enset (false banana), maize, coffee, and pulses, with health services provided by a network of health posts and health centres. The study area was chosen for its high burden of maternal undernutrition and anemia, as well as feasibility for supervising household-level nutrition interventions.

Study design and participants

We conducted a two-arm parallel cluster RCT from September 2024 to February 2025. Kebeles were employed as the unit of randomization to reduce contamination across trial arms. Pregnant women were eligible if they were: (i) under 12 weeks gestational age, (ii) had a singleton pregnancy, (iii) lived in the kebele for at least 6 months, and (iv) agreed to participate. Women with severe anemia (Hb < 7 g/dL), multiple pregnancies, chronic illnesses (e.g., HIV/AIDS, diabetes, cancer), or a history of blood transfusions within 6 months were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcomes of the trial (IDA). Using the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey, the prevalence of IDA in the control group was estimated at 41%, and a 10% absolute reduction in the intervention group was considered clinically meaningful.

For an individually randomized trial, 102 participants per arm were required to achieve 80% power with a 95% confidence level. Adjusting for clustering (intraclass correlation coefficient) = 0.05 and the design effect, the final sample size was set at 306 participants (153 per arm across 10 kebeles). This sample provides sufficient power to detect meaningful differences in the primary haematological outcomes.

Randomization and allocation

Ten kebeles were chosen at random from among thirteen eligible kebeles. An independent statistician used a computer-generated lottery method to stratify them by rural/urban status and randomly assigned them to the intervention (amaranth flatbread) and control (maize flatbread) arms. A house-to-house census was conducted to identify eligible pregnant women in each kebele, led by Health Extension Workers (HEWs) in collaboration with Women Development Team (WDT) leaders. To ensure a balanced distribution, 31 eligible pregnant women were randomly selected from each kebele using a simple random sampling method.

Masking

To maintain blinding, all participants, bread distributors, and outcome assessors were unaware of study group assignments and bread content. Separate distributors were assigned to each arm, with 12 distributors covering the 10 kebeles, and daily deliveries were supervised to ensure compliance. Both amaranth and maize breads were prepared to be as similar as possible in appearance, texture, and packaging, and delivered in identical boxes labeled only with participants’ names. Any unopened bread was returned and recorded by the coordinator.

Although the amaranth bread has a slightly nutty flavor, every effort was made to minimize perceptible differences, preserving the integrity of blinding throughout the trial.

Intervention procedure

Women in the intervention group consumed 200 g of amaranth-based flatbread (70% amaranth grain, 30% chickpea flour) daily for 6 months. Controls received 200 g of maize flatbread every day, made according to conventional household recipes (Supplementary File 2). Amaranth grain was soaked, germinated, roasted, and fermented to improve nutritional bioavailability and reduce phytate levels. For the control arm, a maize-based flatbread was specifically prepared for the trial to match the intervention arm; it was not an existing commercial product. The WDT leaders distributed the supplies daily, with HEWs monitoring them bimonthly. All women continued to receive standard antenatal care and iron-folic acid supplements in accordance with national guidelines. Adherence to supplementation was assessed through weekly self-reports and biweekly home visits, and no significant differences were observed between study arms.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were haematological indicators of iron status measured at baseline and six months: (i) haemoglobin concentration and (ii) IDA, defined using haemoglobin and C-reactive protein (CRP) adjusted serum ferritin thresholds in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) guidance.

Secondary outcomes included adherence to the intervention, safety (incidence of adverse events), serum ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor concentrations as biomarkers of iron status (bioavailability), and acceptability assessed through hedonic scoring and qualitative exploration.

Adherence

Adherence to the flatbread intervention was defined as consuming ≥ 80% of the distributed servings during the six-month trial. Daily distribution records, weekly participant self-reports, and cross-checks of household leftovers were used to monitor adherence consistently across both intervention and control groups.

Safety

Adverse events such as gastrointestinal complaints, allergic responses, and pregnancy difficulties were all monitored for safety purposes. Adverse events were assessed during biweekly home visits and reported on WHO-standardized case report forms. A trial safety monitoring committee assessed serious adverse occurrences and referred them to appropriate health services.

Bioavailability

Bioavailability (change in iron status) was determined utilizing iron status biomarkers such as hemoglobin, serum ferritin, and transferrin receptor levels. Venous blood samples were taken at baseline and after 6 months. Hemoglobin was tested with HemoCue 301 analyzers, while ferritin and transferrin receptor were examined using ELISA methods at the Hawassa University Nutrition Laboratory. The CRP was used to modify ferritin levels for inflammation.

Acceptability

Acceptability was measured at the midline and endline using a standardized 5-point hedonic scale that included sensory qualities (taste, scent, texture, and appearance). Additional variables, such as perceived health advantages, cultural compatibility, and willingness to continue use, were investigated through structured surveys and focus group discussions.

Data collection and quality assurance

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from previously published studies [3, 6–9, 11] and modified to suit the local context. The English version of the adapted questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 3. Data were collected electronically using the Open Data Kit (ODK) on tablets by trained enumerators who spoke Sidaamu Afoo. All enumerators received comprehensive training in laboratory method, dietary recall, and interviewing techniques. The data collectors and laboratory personnel were not aware of the allocation groups. To assure accuracy, biochemical measurements were collected twice, and 10% of individuals were randomly re-measured by supervisors.

Missing data

All participants with complete outcome data were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. Only participants whose outcomes could not be measured—7 in the intervention arm and 6 in the control arm were excluded from analysis due to residence change or early pregnancy loss (abortion). No additional data were missing, and therefore no imputation was required. This approach ensured that the analysis reflected the full and robust dataset while maintaining adherence to the ITT principle (Supplementary File 4).

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics. Adherence rates were compared between arms using chi-square testing and mixed-effects logistic regression. Safety outcomes were described using incidence proportions. Mixed-effects linear regression was used to assess mean differences in hemoglobin, ferritin, and transferrin receptor levels between groups while controlling for baseline values, inflammation, and clustering. Acceptability scores were examined using mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression. For haematological outcomes (haemoglobin and IDA), mixed-effects linear and modified Poisson regression models were used, adjusting for baseline values, clustering, and potential confounders, including routine iron-folic acid supplementation, mass media use, wealth quintile, age at first marriage/pregnancy, malaria infection, iron-rich food consumption, household food insecurity, dietary diversity, undernutrition, and place of residence. Serum ferritin values were corrected for CRP to account for inflammation. All analyses used the ITT principle, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Qualitative methods

To gain an in-depth understanding of participants’ experiences, perceptions, and acceptability of the dietary intervention, qualitative data were collected using focus group discussions (FGDs). A total of four FGDs were conducted, each including nine purposively selected pregnant women. Participants were chosen to capture diversity in age, parity, and place of residence, ensuring a range of perspectives.

FGDs were conducted in a quiet, private setting at local health posts, using a semi-structured discussion guide developed by the research team. Discussions focused on adherence to the intervention, perceived benefits, sensory preferences, and any challenges encountered. All sessions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim to preserve the richness of the responses.

Thematic content analysis was conducted using MAXQDA 2022 software. Two trained researchers independently coded the transcripts, identifying initial codes and emerging patterns. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus to ensure consistency and reliability. Themes were then organized to reflect participants’ perceptions of adherence, acceptability, safety, and overall experience with the dietary intervention.

This rigorous approach provided a comprehensive understanding of the contextual and experiential factors influencing intervention outcomes and complemented the quantitative findings, enhancing the overall validity of the study.

Results

Trial profile

From August 1 to 30, 2024, WDT leaders and HEWs examined 528 pregnant women for eligibility. 306 women from 10 kebeles matched the inclusion criteria and were randomly assigned to one of two arms: intervention (amaranth; n = 153) or control (maize; n = 153). Thirteen women were lost to follow-up, leaving 293 participants for the primary analysis (146 intervention and 147 control), with an overall response rate of 95.8% (Fig. 1). Intervention delivery and adherence

Delivery completion: During the intervention period, 145 of 153 women in the amaranth arm and 144 of 153 in the maize arm received all scheduled flatbread servings; distribution fidelity was good and comparable across arms.

Among the 293 women included in the endline analysis, adherence ≥ 80% was achieved by 132/146 (90.4%) in the amaranth group and 112/147 (76.2%) in the maize group. (χ2 test, p = 0.004). In mixed-effects logistic regression, accounting for kebele clustering and controlling for baseline wealth index and dietary diversification, amaranth consumption was still linked with greater probabilities of adherence (adjusted odds ratio = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.44; p = 0.003).

Safety and adverse events

There were no significant adverse effects related to the intervention. Four pregnancy losses occurred during follow-up (two in each arm); all were evaluated and determined by the study's clinical supervisor to be unconnected to the intervention (external trauma). Minor, temporary gastrointestinal symptoms were noted by 12 people (7.8%) in the amaranth arm and 9 participants (5.9%) in the maize arm, but all resolved without medical intervention. There were no allergic reactions or safety-related withdrawals (see Table 2).

Bioavailability: hemoglobin and iron biomarkers

At the endline, the amaranth group had significantly higher mean hemoglobin (12.39 ± 1.34 g/dL) than the maize group (10.17 ± 1.36 g/dL). In the adjusted mixed-effects linear model, amaranth intake was related with an adjusted β = + 2.67 g/dL (95% CI: 2.41–2.91; p < 0.001) compared to maize (Table 2). Changes in iron status, as reflected by hemoglobin, CRP-adjusted ferritin, and soluble transferrin receptor, suggest improved iron status consistent with enhanced bioavailability. Following multivariable mixed effects modified Poisson regression, the risk of IDA at endline was significantly reduced in the amaranth arm (ARR = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.26–0.84; p = 0.010) (Table 2).

Acceptability—quantitative and qualitative findings

Quantitative result

The amaranth group had greater hedonic acceptance scores (5-point scale) than the maize group (mean 4.9 ± 0.6 vs. 4.1 ± 0.7). Mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression, adjusted for clustering and baseline covariates, revealed a higher likelihood of good acceptability in the amaranth arm (adjusted odds ratio = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.21–2.46; p = 0.003). More than 80% of amaranth users expressed a willingness to continue using the product after the trial (Table 2).

Qualitative (focus group discussions) result

Thematic analysis revealed three prominent themes: sensory appeal, perceived health benefits, and cultural compatibility, all of which correlated with quantitative acceptability scores. Illustrative quotations (with participant age and parity):

Sensory appeal

Participants repeatedly commented on the amaranth flatbread's great taste, aroma, and texture. While the darker color was first unusual, it gradually became connected with good health and power. According to one 30-year-old multipara, "The bread has a good smell and is softer than our usual maize bread." A 24-year-old primipara stated, "At first, I thought the color was strange, but now I see it means it is a strong food." A 24-year-old primipara remarked, "At first I was unsure, but now I feel strong after eating it daily," and a 30-year-old multipara said, "The bread has a pleasant taste and soft texture, much better than I expected."

Perceived health benefits

Women typically associated amaranth consumption to increased energy and maternal health, which encouraged adherence throughout pregnancy. A 28-year-old multipara said, "Since eating this bread, I feel less tired and stronger in my body." A 22-year-old primipara stated, "I believe it helps my blood and will make my baby grow well."

Cultural compatibility

The flatbread was regarded as familiar and simple to incorporate into household diets, with no cultural taboos identified. Some reported occasional pressure to share portions, indicating communal meal behaviors. A 35-year-old multipara said, "This bread is like injera; everyone in the house can enjoy it." A 25-year-old primipara stated, "There is no restriction to eat it in pregnancy because it comes from grains we already use."

Acceptability grew during the trial's brief run, and participants praised the product's palatability and cultural fit; infrequent intra-household sharing was noted, but it had no significant impact on adherence.

Qualitative findings on adherence

Qualitative research confirms this finding. One 30-year-old multipara said, “It was easy for me to eat the bread regularly because I got it every day,” and a 24-year-old primipara said, “I never missed a serving because the WDT leader reminded me every day." This emphasizes how crucial community participation is to nutritional programs.

Random effect parameters

The trial included ten kebeles, with an average of 31 participants per kebele. Moderate variability was observed between kebeles for key outcomes. The intra-class correlation coefficients were 0.126 for haemoglobin, 0.267 for IDA, 0.098 for adherence, and 0.109 for acceptability scores. These results indicate moderate clustering for haematological outcomes and low-to-moderate clustering for adherence and acceptability, highlighting the importance of accounting for cluster effects in the analysis.

Discussion

Adherence to amaranth flatbread intervention

Comparable to the maize control group, 145 out of 153 participants in the amaranth arm completed the whole intervention, demonstrating high adherence during the experiment. This suggests that regular monitoring, participant engagement tactics, and organized community-based distribution were successful in maintaining compliance. These results are in line with earlier research that shown that integrating interventions within local community structures promotes adherence [13, 14]. High adherence guarantees that the observed biochemical benefits are due to the intervention and not to differential exposure, which enhances internal validity.

Safety of the intervention

No serious adverse events were reported, confirming the safety of daily consumption of amaranth flatbread during pregnancy. Minor gastrointestinal symptoms, such as transient bloating or abdominal discomfort, were mild, self-limiting, and did not lead to discontinuation. These results align with existing literature suggesting that plant-based, naturally processed grains have a favorable safety profile for pregnant women [15, 16].

Bioavailability and hematological outcomes

Higher hemoglobin values and a lower prevalence of IDA among the amaranth group were indicative of the intervention's notable increases in improved iron status consistent with enhanced bioavailability. The processing methods—soaking, germination, roasting, and fermentation that lower phytate levels and improve iron absorption are responsible for these results [17, 18].

Kebele-level clustering was responsible for 12.6% of hemoglobin and 26.7% of IDA variability, according to multilevel mixed-effects models, highlighting the need of taking community-level factors into consideration in nutritional studies [19]. The intervention effect's physiological efficacy in enhancing maternal iron status was further supported by adjusted models.

Acceptability

Amaranth flatbread was shown to be highly acceptable in both qualitative focus group talks and quantitative hedonic ratings. Positive reactions were influenced by perceived health advantages and sensory qualities (taste, texture, scent, and appearance). As participants adjusted to the novel food product and its cultural integration, acceptance grew over time. Cultural acceptance is crucial, particularly in areas where dietary customs are strongly ingrained, indicating that iron-rich plant-based foods can be effectively incorporated into the diets of mothers.

This trial demonstrated that daily consumption of amaranth grain flatbread among pregnant women led to significant improvements in haemoglobin levels and reduced incidence of IDA. High adherence, good safety profile, and strong acceptability indicate that the intervention is both feasible and culturally appropriate.

Compared to the control group, the improvements in iron status are consistent with the expected nutritional benefits of amaranth, particularly when processed to reduce phytates and enhance iron absorption. The findings also underline the importance of community-based distribution and monitoring in sustaining adherence and ensuring program effectiveness.

While the study was conducted in Hawela Lida district in southern Ethiopia, the observed effects may extend to other rural areas in Ethiopia and similar low-resource settings where dietary diversity is limited, and maternal undernutrition is prevalent. Nevertheless, context-specific factors, such as local food habits and cultural acceptance, should be considered when adapting the intervention to new populations [20, 21].

Policy and public health implications

These results offer strong support for the inclusion of amaranth-based products in maternal nutrition initiatives in low-resource environments such as Ethiopia. Strong acceptability, enhanced bioavailability, high adherence, and superior safety show that this strategy is both practical and successful for scaling up [22, 23]. Additionally, promoting locally farmed amaranth can enhance community nutrition security and support sustainable food systems [24, 25].

Strengths and limitations

The rigorous cluster-randomized design, strong statistical modeling that takes variables and clustering into account, and the combination of quantitative and qualitative methodologies are some of the study's strong points. A six-month follow-up time and a geographically confined study sample are two limitations that could hinder generalizability.

Future research directions

Future research should look at the scalability, cost-effectiveness, and long-term maternal and newborn effects of amaranth-based therapies. National dietary guidelines for pregnant women will be informed by research conducted in a variety of situations, which will assist assess broader applicability.

Conclusion

Pregnant women in Hawela Lida district, Sidama region, Ethiopia, who consumed amaranth grain flatbread daily reported great adherence, safety, and acceptance. Iron deficiency anemia was less common, and maternal hemoglobin levels and iron status were greatly increased. These results support the wider adoption of nutrient-dense local foods and offer compelling evidence for its incorporation into maternal nutrition programs.
