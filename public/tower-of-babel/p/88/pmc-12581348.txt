Childhood trauma exposure, working memory updating, and exercise experience in adolescents with major depressive disorder
Authors: Zhe Li, Junfeng Qin, Xianyun Zhu, Yinglin Wang, Wenxin Zhang, Donghui Shen, Xiaolei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing evidence indicates that childhood trauma (CT) experiences and impaired working memory updating (WMU) are critical factors in the development and maintenance of depression. Conversely, exercise activities serve as protective mechanisms against depression. However, it remains unclear about 1) whether adolescents with major depressive disorder (MDD) exhibit impairments in updating non-social and social information, and 2) the interplay among CT, WMU, exercise experience (EE) and depression in adolescents.MethodsTo address these questions, this study recruited 72 first-episode, treatment-naïve adolescents with MDD and 50 healthy controls (HC), matched for age, gender and education. The WMU abilities were assessed using a classic n-back task including both non-social digits and social emotional faces. The CT experience, EE and depressive symptoms were evaluated by the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), Subjective Exercise Experience Scale (SEES) and Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), respectively. Additionally, the mediating roles of WMU and EE in the association between CT and depression were analyzed.ResultsOur results showed that compared to HC group, MDD patients exhibited decreased accuracy and increased reaction time to both non-social digits and social emotional faces in the n-back task. Furthermore, the behavioral accuracy in the WMU, as well as fatigue and positive-wellbeing experiences during exercises, significantly mediated the association between CT and depression across the entire sample.ConclusionsFindings from this study revealed that adolescents with MDD exhibit generalized impairments in WMU, and the pathway from CT to depression is partially mediated by both WMU and EE. These findings highlight WMU training and EE improvement as clinical intervention targets for CT-related depression.Keywords: Childhood trauma, Working memory, Exercise experience, Depression, Adolescent

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD), with a prevalence rate of 3%, has become one of the most prevalent mental disorders in Chinese children and adolescents [25]. Longitudinal studies have consistently demonstrated that adolescent depression is linked to elevated risks of adult suicidality and enduring social dysfunction [10, 50]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the onset and persistence of depression in youth is essential for developing effective prevention strategies and therapeutic interventions.

Childhood trauma (CT) is identified as one of the most significant risk factors for depression [17, 22, 47]. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), as the most widely used measurement for assessing CT experience, identifies five specific childhood trauma including emotional abuse, physical abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect and sexual abuse [2]. Several meta-analysis studies have demonstrated that all five types of CT are highly associated with depression [26, 34]. Additionally, converging evidence indicates that physical exercise serves as a protective factor against depression and can also mitigate the link between adverse childhood experiences and depression in children, adolescents and adults [42], Biddle et al., 2011; [11, 38]. Structural physical exercise, when used as an adjunctive therapy for adolescents with depression and anxiety, has shown positive effects on reducing depressive symptoms [40]. This may because physical exercise generated positive emotions and further reduced depressive levels [7]. However, most previous studies have primarily focused on the effects of exercise categories, duration, and frequency, leaving the role of subjective exercise experience (EE) in the association between CT and depression unexplored.

Working memory updating (WMU) refers to the process of continuously monitoring and dynamically replacing the outdated, task-irrelevant information with new, task-relevant information in working memory [33]. Specifically, updating non-social information such as numerical digits and spatial locations is known as cognitive WMU, whereas updating social information such as emotional faces and beliefs is known as social WMU [32, 49]. The n-back paradigm has been widely used to investigate WMU in both clinical and experimental research [36, 55]. This task entails the administration of a continuous stream of stimuli, where participants are required to identify if the current stimulus matches the one presented n trials prior [20, 36]. By manipulating the stimuli involved in the task and the n (1-, 2-, 3-, this task allows to examine both cognitive and social WMU with different loads in children, adolescents and adults (Neta & Whalen, 2011,Xin & Lei, 2015. Specifically, the 0-back is typically employed to examine the maintenance component of the working memory system while when n > 0, both maintenance and updating processes are engaged as every stimulus needs to be maintained and further acts as an updated target in the subsequent trial [23, 41]. Increasing evidence has consistently demonstrated that n-back performance was correlated with childhood trauma experience [39, 46]. Therefore, in the present study, we employed the n-back paradigm to assess WMU in adolescents. Given our previous experience that depressed adults have difficulties completing emotional n-back task with high cognitive loads (n > 1, [54]), the 1-back task was employed for adolescents in the present study.

Increasing studies have investigated the updating function in adult patients with MDD. For example, two studies found that depressed patients exhibited impaired performance when updating digits and letters, indicating substantial deficits in cognitive WMU [13, 35]. Despite numerous studies have also identified deficits in social WMU, findings regarding emotional-specific updating biases remain inconsistent. Some studies revealed that depressed patients had difficulties in updating negative information, while others indicated that they were only lack of the priority in updating positive information [18, 19, 23, 24, 56, 57]. In addition, our prior functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study showed that MDD patients did not display impairment on the behavioral level but exhibited abnormal functional connectivity between the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and insula, potentially reflecting a compensatory mechanism at the neural level during the updating of emotional faces [54].

In line with the aforementioned evidence from studies on depressed adults, findings of the social WMU in depressed adolescents are also mixed. One study reported impaired WMU for neutral rather than positive or negative information, whereas another found impaired WMU specifically for negative information in MDD patients [21, 48, 52]. Additionally, in a preclinical sample, compared to healthy adolescents who exhibited an updating bias toward positive materials, those with depressive symptoms showed no emotional biases [43]. This contrasts with findings in preclinical adults with depressive symptoms showing deficits in updating positive information [58]. Regarding cognitive WMU, it remains unclear whether it is impaired in depressed adolescents. Although one fMRI study using machine learning algorithm identified a subtype of depression in youth characterized by impairment in a fractal n-back task, another subtype exhibited better performance than healthy adolescents [1].

Although the results of the emotion-specific updating bias in depression remain controversial, it is well established that impaired WMU may be a key cognitive mechanism contributing to the development and maintenance of MDD [15]. Previous studies have revealed robust associations between childhood trauma and impaired cognitive functions at both behavioral and neural levels [30]. Recent studies have attempted to disentangle the relationships among childhood experience, cognitive function and MDD and found that cognitive function may serve as a mediating factor in the relationship between CT and MDD [27],Manshadi et al., 2024). However, as the core component of the central cognitive system, the specific role of WMU in the relationship between CT and depression, particularly in adolescents, remains unclear. In addition, while exercise is widely recognized as a protective factor against depression, little is known about whether CT influences the experience of exercise and subsequently affects depressive symptoms.

Against this background, the present study aims to investigate the WMU in adolescents with MDD by employing a classic n-back task that includes both digits and social emotional faces thereby allowing to directly compare the impairment of cognitive and social WMU simultaneously [53]. To minimize the confounding effects of symptom severity, disease duration, episode frequency, and intervention variability on WMU performance [44], this study exclusively included first-episode, treatment-naïve patients. Additionally, we examined the mediating roles of WMU and EE on the pathway from CT to depression. Given that previous studies have demonstrated impairments in both cognitive and social WMU among depressed adults, as well as strong correlations between CT, cognitive functions, exercise and depression, we hypothesized that: 1 depressed adolescents would exhibit overall impaired WMU,2 WMU mediates the correlation between CT and depression; and 3 EE mediates the correlation between CT and depression.

Methods

Participants

72 first-episode treatment-naïve adolescents with MDD diagnosed by an experienced psychiatrist according to ICD-10 criteria were recruited from the Qingdao Municipal Hospital and 50 healthy controls (HC) were recruited by social media and advertisements. The inclusion criteria were: 1) 12–18 years; 2) right-handed; 3) first-episode treatment-naïve patients; 4) receiving normal education. The exclusion criteria for patients included: 1) Severe suicidal tendencies; (2) Related clinical conditions or neurobiological disorders such as dementia, stroke; (3) Mental illness history or bipolar disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder; (4) A history of using benzodiazepine drugs; (5) Current or past 5-year history of alcohol or substance abuse that meets DSM-V diagnostic criteria; (6) Any axis II personality disorder. All participated adolescents and their parents were fully informed about the details of the study, allowed to withdraw at any time without negative consequences and provided written informed consent before the experiment. This study was fully approved by the local Ethics Committee of Shandong Normal University and Qingdao Municipal Hospital. All experimental procedures were in accordance with the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaires assessments

Depression and anxiety symptoms, CT experience and EE were assessed by the Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS, [60], Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS, [59]), Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ, [6]) and Subjective Exercise Experience Scale (SEES, McAuley et al., 1994), respectively.

The n-back task

A valid 1-back paradigm including two non-social blocks with 0–9 digit as stimuli and six social blocks with happy, neutral and sad emotional facial expressions (two blocks per emotion) as stimuli was employed in this study [54]. For digital blocks, participants were required to identify whether the current digit matched the one presented in the previous trial and for facial blocks they were required to indicate whether the identity of the current face matched the one presented in the previous trial. Each block began with an instruction indicating the upcoming block was digital or facial block. The instruction disappeared after participants pressed the button and a white fixation was presented on the center of the screen for 500 ms followed by a digit or face stimulus where participants were required to respond within 2000 ms (Fig. 1). A total of 248 trials were included in this task with each block contained 31 trials and the first trial was unnecessary to respond. This task lasted approximately 12 min, with short breaks between blocks to minimize the potential effects of fatigue. Upon completion of the task, all participants were asked to report their current emotional state, and none indicated experiencing fatigue.

Trial procedure of the digital and facial emotional 1-back task. This task contains both non-social digital and social emotional blocks. In the digital blocks, digital numbers were presented and in the social blocks the facial emotional expressions were presented. Participants were required to identify whether the current digit or identity of the face matched the one presented in the previous trial

Statistical analysis

The gender difference between MDD and HC group was analyzed using Chi-square test and group differences in age, education and questionnaire scores were analyzed using the two-sample t-test. The 2 (group: MDD, HC) × 4 (stimuli: digit/happy/neutral/sad) mixed ANOVA was conducted on behavioral performance (accuracy and reaction time) in the n-back task with group as between-subject factor and stimuli as within-subject factor. Significant main and interaction effects were further analyzed using pairwise comparisons with post-hoc Bonferroni corrections. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to examine the associations between behavioral performance and the questionnaire scores. The mediating effect was analyzed using SPSS PROCESS macro by Hayes (v4.1, model 4, [14]) with Bootstrap resampling (n = 5000) including sex, age and education as covariates.

Results

Demography and questionnaire scores

Eleven participants were excluded according to the exclusion criteria and data quality check leading to a final sample of 111 (MDD = 63, HC = 48, details see Fig. 2). The Cronbach's α of the questionnaires in the present study were SDS = 0.937, SAS = 0.934, CTQ = 0.905, SEES-psychological distress = 0.910, SEES-fatigue = 0.914, SEES-positive-wellbeing = 0.850.

Number of participants in the MDD and HC groups

There were no significant differences in gender, age and education between the two groups (ps > 0.29). The depression and anxiety symptoms, CT experience, fatigue and psychological distress during excise activities in MDD group were significantly higher than that in HC group while the positive-wellbeing during excise in MDD groups was significantly lower than that in HC group (details see Table 1).

Behavioral results

Accuracy

The 2 (group: MDD, HC) × 4 (stimuli: digit, happy, neutral, sad) mixed ANOVA revealed significant main effects of group (F1,109 = 12.31, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.10) and stimuli (F3,302 = 16.97, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.14). The accuracy in MDD group (82.80 ± 1.15%) was significantly lower than that in HC group (88.94 ± 1.32%, p = 0.001, Fig. 3a). The accuracy for digit (89.32 ± 0.87%) was significantly higher than that for sad (85.84 ± 1.01%, p < 0.001), happy (84.57 ± 1.07%, p < 0.001) and neutral faces (83.76 ± 1.10%, p < 0.001) and the accuracy for sad was also significantly higher than that for neutral faces (p = 0.02). The group × stimuli interaction was not significant (F3,327 = 1.52, p = 0.21, ηp2 = 0.01).

Reaction time

The 2 (group: MDD, HC) × 4 (stimuli: digit, happy, neutral, sad) mixed ANOVA reveled significant main effect of group (F1,109 = 24.00, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.18) with shorter reaction time in HC group (579.36 ± 24.32 ms) than that in MDD group (737.52 ± 21.23 ms, p < 0.001, Fig. 3b). there were no other significant main and interaction effects (ps > 0.09).

Associations and mediating results

Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between behavioral accuracy and SDS, CTQ and SEES scores. While the reaction time was also significantly associated with SDS, CTQ, and SEES – positive-wellbeing/SEES – fatigue, the correlation between reaction time and SEES – psychological distress was not significant (details of the correlations please see Fig. 4).

Given the correlations between psychological distress and reaction time was not significant, the psychological distress was no longer considered as mediator. Therefore, the subsequent mediation analysis included CTQ as independent variable, SDS as dependent variable, behavioral performance (accuracy and reaction time) and SEES (fatigue and positive-wellbeing) as mediators. Results showed a significant direct effect for the pathway from CTQ to SDS (β = 0.23, p = 0.005), the significant indirect effects of CTQ on SDS via working memory accuracy (β = −0.27, p < 0.001), fatigue (β = 0.31, p < 0.001) and positive-wellbeing (β = −0.25, p = 0.002). However, the mediating effect of reaction time on the relationships between CTQ and SDS was not significant (β = 0.12, p = 0.08, Fig. 5).

Discussion

The present study employed a classic n-back task including both non-social and social content to investigate the WMU in adolescents with MDD as well as the mediating effects of WMU and subjective exercise experience on the association between CT and depression. Our results showed that compared to heathy controls, adolescents with MDD exhibited overall impairment in both non-social and social WMU as reflected by lower accuracy and longer reaction times. Furthermore, the WMU performance was significantly associated with the CT experience and depressive symptoms. Specifically, the accuracy of WMU, rather than reaction time, and subjective exercise experience both mediated the pathway from CT to depression.

In line with previous studies demonstrating that adults with MDD exhibit impaired cognitive WMU, our results found that adolescents with MDD also display deficits in updating non-social digits [13, 35]. In addition, adolescents exhibited superior cognitive WMU compared to social WMU, as evidenced by higher accuracy for digits than for emotional faces. This discrepancy may be attributed to the rapid development of cognitive working memory efficacy after the age of seven, which approaches adult levels during adolescence, while social working memory remains susceptible to emotional influences due to the underdeveloped cognitive and affective control [5, 12, 16, 28, 37, 45].

In contrast to the findings of three prior studies that reported either negative/positive or neutral specific biases in updating social information in MDD adolescents compared to healthy controls, results from this study revealed general deficits in social WMU in MDD patients irrespective of emotional valence [21, 48, 52]. Two factors may account for these discrepancies. First, the present study exclusively recruited first-episode treatment-naïve MDD patients and our sample size was larger (MDD, n = 63) compared to previous studies with smaller samples (MDD, n < 30). Second, in the present study we used a facial emotional 1-back task whereas previous studies employed a high-load 2-back task. Given the moderate difficulty of the 1-back task used in our study (accuracy > 80%), it is less likely to be influenced by the emotional valence of stimuli compared to the more challenging 2-back task (accuracy < 70% in two studies) which may have led to specific emotional updating biases. Consistent with our current finding of no emotional updating bias in MDD adolescents, our previous study using the same facial emotional 1-back task in first-episode, treatment-naïve MDD adults also failed to detect any emotional updating bias [54].

We found that CT experience not only directly predicts depressive symptoms but also indirectly predicts them via the accuracy of WMU, fatigue and positive-wellbeing experiences during exercise in adolescents. The link between CT and depression has been well established [17, 22, 47]. Our findings further demonstrate that this link is mediated by WMU, particularly the behavioral accuracy, and subjective exercise experiences. Both animal and human studies have showed robust associations between CT and impaired cognitive functions at both behavioral and neural levels [29, 30]. As a core component of cognitive system, WMU may be affected by CT, which in turn contributes to depressive symptoms. In addition, while an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that exercise can prevent depression, few have explored the effects of subjective exercise experience (Ekkekakis P, 2017; [4]). Our results indicate that, compared to healthy adolescents, MDD patients experience more negative and fewer positive feelings during exercise. Additionally, CT significantly increases fatigue and decrease positive-wellbeing feelings during exercise leading to higher depressive symptoms. This may suggest that subjective exercise experience should be considered when examining the impact of exercise on emotional disorders.

There are two limitations in the present study. First, due to the relatively small sample size, we were unable to explore potential gender differences although recent study indicating that the CT-depression association is stronger in female than male children [50]. Second, our investigation was restricted to subjective exercise experiences, however, exercise-related factors such as type, frequency, duration and intensity may also contribute to the interplay between CT, WMU and depression. Therefore, future studies should incorporate more detailed exercise-related information to address this gap.

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that adolescents with MDD exhibit overall impairments in WMU. Furthermore, both WMU accuracy and EE – with particular emphasis on fatigue and positive well-being – mediate the pathway from CT to depression in adolescents. These results suggest that for adolescents with a history of CT, enhancing WMU capabilities and implementing exercise interventions that cultivate positive exercise experiences may serve as critical strategies for preventing depression.
