ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 methylation governs palatal development by regulating WNT5B
Authors: Zhichen Ling, Xueqin Zhu, Xinyi Tan, Zhangke Ma, Furong Xie, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrofacial clefts, particularly cleft palate, are among the most common congenital anomalies, imposing substantial functional impairment and psychosocial burden on affected individuals. Emerging evidence implicates epigenetic modifications, especially histone lysine methylation, as critical regulators of embryonic development, yet their precise roles in palatogenesis remain poorly defined. The H3K4 methyltransferase complexes, which require the core subunit ASH2L for full enzymatic activity, govern transcriptional activation during development; however, whether ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 deposition contributes to palatal morphogenesis is unknown.ResultsHere, we show that conditional deletion of Ash2l in Six2-positive palatal mesenchymal cells results in complete cleft palate in mice. Multi-omics analyses (RNA-seq and CUT&Tag-seq) reveal that Ash2l loss causes genome-wide reduction of H3K4me3 enrichment at key developmental genes, most notably Wnt5b, leading to their transcriptional downregulation. Functionally, Ash2l deficiency impairs proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of palatal mesenchymal cells, resulting in stunted palatal shelf growth and disrupted osteogenesis. Pharmacological activation of WNT signaling downstream of Wnt5b partially rescues these cellular defects, confirming the functional relevance of this axis.ConclusionThese findings establish ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 as a critical epigenetic regulator of palatal development through WNT5B modulation, and identify potential therapeutic targets for cleft palate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13072-026-00690-1.

Introduction

Orofacial clefts (OFCs) are among the most common congenital anomalies, affecting approximately 1 in 700 live births worldwide. OFCs are clinically classified into cleft lip with or without cleft palate (CL/P) and cleft palate only (CPO). Although CPO represents the minority subtype (approximately one-third of all OFCs) [1, 2], it imposes the most severe functional impairments on critical orofacial functions, including speech articulation, mastication, and deglutition [3]. These deficits profoundly impact the quality of life of affected children and their families, imposing substantial physical, psychological, and economic burdens on individuals, families, and healthcare systems [1, 3–6].

The etiology of cleft palate is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition and environmental exposures such as maternal smoking, alcohol consumption, and nutritional deficiencies [2, 6–8]. Accumulating evidence further implicates epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant histone modifications, as a significant contributing factor. Pathogenic variants in histone modification enzymes, including lysine demethylase 6A (KDM6A), lysine methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) and PHD Finger Protein 8 (PHF8) impair epigenetic patterning critical for craniofacial morphogenesis. Patients with these gene mutations suffer from maxillofacial dysplasia and are very likely to have cleft palate [9–11].

For instance, KMT2D mutations cause Kabuki syndrome, a disorder characterized by multiple developmental abnormalities, including cleft palate [9]. As a member of the KMT2 histone methyltransferase family, KMT2D encodes an enzyme responsible for H3K4 methylation in mammals [12, 13]. KMT2 family enzymes require assembly with the WRAD complex (WDR5, RbBP5, ASH2L, and DPY30) to attain functional H3K4 methyltransferase activity, with Absent, Small, or Homeotic 2-Like (ASH2L) serving as an essential core subunit [14–16]. Existing studies have shown that ASH2L regulates embryonic stem cell maintenance and differentiation, with established roles in embryonic development [17]. Therefore, ASH2L knockout can prevent WRAD-dependent activation of KMT2 complexes, impairing H3K4 methylation establishment.

Given the ubiquitous expression of ASH2L, investigating its specific functions in palatal mesenchyme necessitates a cell-type-specific targeting approach. Sine Oculis Homeobox Homolog 2 (SIX2) mutations cause Frontonasal Dysplasia 1 (FND1) with severe midfacial hypoplasia [18, 19]. Mouse models with Six2-conditional knockout recapitulate human FND1 phenotypes [20–24], demonstrating its essential role in craniofacial development. Additionally, studies indicate that SIX2-expression cells localize to frontonasal mesenchyme (nasal, maxillary, and cranial base tissues, including the palate) during embryogenesis, with minimal distribution in mandibular mesenchyme and nasal epithelium [21], explaining region-specific defects. Crucially, as SIX2 labels palatal mesenchymal [25], conditional knockout of Ash2l in Six2-expressing cells enable precise dissection of Histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3)-mediated palatogenesis regulation.

In this study, we demonstrate that ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 deposition is critical for mammalian palatal development. Conditional Ash2l knockout depletes H3K4me3 at key palatogenesis genes, thereby downregulating their expression and propagating effects through signaling cascades such as the WNT pathway. This epigenetic silencing impairs palatal mesenchymal cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately disrupting palatal morphogenesis and leading to cleft palate.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee at Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The studies performed in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences (SIBCB-S353-2401-07). The Ash2lflox/flox mice were kind gifts from Professor Minghan Tong (Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) [26], and the Six2-creGFP mice were kind gifts from Professor Fujun Lin (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China) [27]. The Ash2lflox/flox, Six2-creGFP mice were the experimental group (KO group), and the Ash2lflox/flox or Ash2lflox/+, Six2-creGFP mice from the same litter were the control group (CTRL group). The estimated ages of the embryos were determined based on the identification of the vaginal plug, with embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5) being defined as noon on the day the plug was observed. All mice were euthanized with an overdose of CO2.

Micro computed tomography (microCT)

The heads of mice at P0 were collected, fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, and completely wrapped with paraffin film. Then the head samples were scanned by micro-CT(SKYSCAN1272, Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen, Germany). (Scaled Image Pixel Size (μm) = 7.999916, Filter = Al 0.25 mm) The source files were reconstructed by DataViewer software, and analyzed by CT-Voxel software.

Skeletal staining

P0 mouse heads were skinned, fixed in 100% ethanol for 3 days, and immersed in acetone for 1 day. Samples were stained with Alcian Blue 8GX (A5268, Sigma-Aldrich; 15 mg in 10 mL glacial acetic acid, brought to 50 mL with 100% ethanol) for 1–2 days to label cartilage, washed in 100% ethanol for 1 day, and cleared in 1% KOH for 3–5 days. Bones were counterstained with Alizarin Red S (A5533, Sigma-Aldrich; 1.5 mg in 50 mL of 1% KOH) for 1 day. Specimens were then cleared through graded glycerol–KOH series (20%, 40%, 80%, and 100% glycerol) and stored in 100% glycerol. Images were captured under a stereomicroscope.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The heads of mice at P0 were collected, fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, and then washed, dehydrated and dried. After the samples were completely dry, they were bonded to the sample stage with conductive adhesive, covered with conductive coating, and scanned and photographed by SEM.

Histology and immunofluorescence staining (H&E and IF)

For each embryonic stage (E13.5, E14.5, and E15.5) and genotype, heads from 4 animals were collected under the stereomicroscope. Then the head samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 24–48 h, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and serially sectioned in the coronal plane at 4 μm. Comparable coronal sections were selected based on tongue morphology, with the tongue-mandible connection decreasing from posterior to anterior palate. The selected sections were dewaxed and rehydrated for the following staining.

For hematoxylin and eosin staining, the sections were incubated in hematoxylin for 3 min, then rinsed with water and incubated in fresh hydrochloric acid—alcohol solution for 2–3 s. Finally, the sections were incubated in eosin for 15 s, rinsed with water and mounted with balsam solution.

For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 98 °C for 20 min and then blocked in blocking buffer (P0260, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for at least 10 min. Primary antibodies were diluted in antibody diluent (WB100D, NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) and incubated with the sections overnight at 4 °C. They were then incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to AlexaFluor 488 or 594 (1:300, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, PA, USA). Nuclei were counterstained by DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

All images were captured and analyzed by microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). All antibodies were provided in Table S4.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays

PBS containing 2 mg/ml EdU (ST067, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was intraperitoneally injected into pregnant mice at a dose of 50 mg EdU per kilogram of body weight 1 h before they were sacrificed. Then the head samples were processed into the palate sections accordance with the above methods. The sections were detected by the BeyoClick EdU-488 Cell Proliferation Kit (C0071S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All images were e captured and analyzed by microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Flow cytometry

The palate tissues were carefully separated from mice at E14.5, cut into small pieces, and digested with 1 U/mL dispersing enzyme II for 30 min at 37 °C. After centrifugation to remove the supernatant, the palate tissues were digested with trypsin (0.25%) for 5 min at 37 °C. Enzymatic digestion was stopped by adding DMEM/F-12 (DF042-B, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) containing 10% FBS (04001-1A, Biological Industries, CT, USA). After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in DMEM/F-12 containing 10% FBS and sequentially filtered twice through a 40-μm cell strainer(352340, Biosharp, Corning, NY, USA). The single-cell suspension was transferred to a polypropylene tube (352063, Corning, NY, USA) and kept on ice. Then GFP-expressing palatal mesenchymal cells were isolated using a cell sorter (MA900, Sony, Tokyo, Japan) and collected in DMEM/F-12 containing 10% FBS.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Three biological replicate pairs of FACS-sorted GFP-expressing cells from CTRL and KO groups were processed for RNA isolation using RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) per manufacturer's protocol (Each biological replicate originated from an individual embryo). Then, RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix Kit (RR036A, TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). Moreover, the mRNA expression levels were quantified by RT-qPCR with Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11201ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Data were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. Detailed protocols, reaction conditions, and primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3(Actb was used as the housekeeping gene). The 2–ΔΔCt method was used for normalization of RT-qPCR data.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was isolated from three biological replicate pairs of FACS-sorted GFP-expressing cells from CTRL and KO groups as described (Each biological replicate originated from an individual embryo). RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced by OE Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Raw FASTQ reads were quality-trimmed and adapter-filtered using fastp software, generating high-quality clean reads. Resulting in clean reads suitable for subsequent data analysis. These reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using hisat2 (v2.2.1.0), and raw counts were quantified with htseq-count (v0.9.1). In addition, differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs).were defined as those with |log2 fold change|≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation sequencing (CUT&Tag-seq)

Two biological replicate pairs of FACS-sorted GFP-expressing cells from CTRL and KO groups were processed for CUT&Tag library preparation using the NovoNGS® High-Sensitivity CUT&Tag 3.0 Kit (N259-YH01, NovoProtein, Shanghai, China) (Each biological replicate originated from an individual embryo). Washed cells were incubated with ConA beads in Binding Buffer for 10 min, incubated with anti-H3K4me3 antibody(diluted 1:50 in antibody buffer; AB2615077, Active Motif, CA, USA) for 2 h and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (diluted 1:50 in antibody buffer; catalog no. 31210, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature with end-over-end rotation. It is worth noting that after each cell incubation, the cells were washed twice using wash buffer. Then cells were incubated with pAG-Tn5 transposon for 1 h at room temperature with end-over-end rotation and washed twice using pAG-Tn5 buffer. Tagmentation was activated with tagmentation buffer (37 °C, 1 h) and terminated with stop buffer (55 °C, 10 min). DNA was purified using Tagment DNA Extraction Beads, washed twice with 80% ethanol, briefly air-dried and eluted in 37 μL EB buffer. Purified DNA fragments were amplified in 50 μL PCR reactions comprising: 35 μL DNA template, 2.5 μL i5 primer (10 μM), 2.5 μL i7 primer (10 μM), 10 μL 5 × Amplification buffer. After 20 cycles (98 °C 15 s; 60 °C 10 s; 72 °C 8 s), products were purified using DNA Clean Beads, washed with 80% ethanol, briefly air-dried and eluted in 22 μL EB buffer.

CUT&Tag library sequencing and data analysis were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). In brief, quality-controlled libraries underwent 150-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were preprocessed with fastp (v0.23.4) to remove adapters and filter low-quality reads, generating high-quality clean reads. Protein-DNA binding sites were identified by aligning reads to GRCm39 using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) and calling peaks via SEACR (v1.3). Consensus peaks were annotated with ChIPseeker (v1.34.1).

P2 cells (passaged once from P1 at 80% confluency using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA) were utilized for all downstream functional assays.

Results

Ash2lflox/flox, Six2-creGFP mice exhibit cleft palate

Ash2lflox/flox, Six2-creGFP mice exhibited cleft palate and died shortly after birth. In contrast, Ash2lflox/+, Six2-creGFP mice survived normally and showed no phenotypic differences compared to wild-type littermates. Further analysis confirmed the absence of both cleft palate and conditional Ash2l knockout in Ash2lflox/+, Six2-creGFP mice (based on gross morphology, micro-CT, RT-qPCR, and IF). Since littermate controls comprising Ash2lflox/flox and Ash2lflox/+, Six2-creGFP mice displayed identical phenotypes, they were collectively designated as the Control group (CTRL). Ash2lflox/flox, Six2-creGFP mice were designated as the Knockout group (KO). Importantly, no cleft palate or other craniofacial abnormalities were observed in any other genotypes examined.

At postnatal day 0 (P0), KO group mice exhibited marked hypoplasia of the cranial base and maxilla, although body and limb development appeared normal. Birth weights of KO mice showed no significant difference compared to CTRL littermates (Fig. S1C). All KO mice displayed neonatal lethality, evidenced by a cyanotic (dark red) body surface, contrasting with the viable CTRL pups exhibiting a normal (bright red) coloration (Fig. 1B). Dissection and gross examination of P0 palates revealed abnormal development exclusively in the KO group. Bilateral palatal shelves failed to fuse at the midline totally, resulting in cleft palate of varying severity: 65.4% exhibited complete cleft palate with no midline contact, 26.9% showed partial cleft palate with residual clefting, and 7.7% displayed submucous cleft palate with intact epithelium but underlying defects (Table 1). In contrast, CTRL littermates exhibited fully fused and intact palates (Fig. 1C, S2A). To characterize the palatal morphology in greater detail, SEM was performed on P0 palates. SEM analysis confirmed the presence of prominent midline clefts in all KO mice examined. Furthermore, high-magnification imaging revealed distinct surface abnormalities in KO palates. While CTRL palatal surfaces were smooth and intact, displaying only the expected palatal rugae, KO palatal surfaces exhibited widespread irregularities. To determine the onset and potential mechanisms of palatal dysmorphogenesis in conditional mesenchymal Ash2l knockout mice, we histologically analyzed palatal shelves at critical developmental stages (E13.5, E14.5, and E15.5). Immunohistochemistry confirmed abundant Ash2l expression throughout palatal mesenchyme in CTRL mice at all stages, while KO mice exhibited near-complete loss of Ash2l in palatal mesenchyme (Fig. 1F, G, S1D, E, S2A–C). This was corroborated by RT-qPCR of E14.5 palatal mesenchyme (Fig. 1H), verifying efficient mesenchymal Ash2l deletion in KO mice. At E13.5 and E14.5, palatal shelf orientation was comparable between KO and CTRL mice: shelves grew vertically alongside the tongue, with initial elevation observable at E14.5 in both groups. However, at E15.5, there was a significant difference in the palatal morphology between the CTRL and KO mice. CTRL shelves had contacted and fused at the midline. In contrast, KO shelves failed to contact totally. Where limited contact occurred, a persistent epithelial seam separated the mesenchyme, preventing fusion (Fig. 1E, S3C). These results demonstrate that mesenchymal Ash2l loss disrupts palatal shelf growth, leading to failed contact and fusion at the midline.

Impaired proliferation and osteogenic differentiation abilities of MEPM cells in Six2-specific Ash2l knockout mice

Given the dependence of palatal shelf growth on mesenchymal cell proliferation and differentiation (notably osteogenesis), we assessed these processes in KO mice.

First, we evaluated the cell proliferation ability by EdU assays. Temporal analysis revealed a progressive decline in proliferating cells within palatal mesenchyme during normal development (E13.5 → E15.5), consistent with developmental maturation. Crucially, KO mice exhibited significantly reduced EdU⁺ cell proportions versus CTRL littermates at all stages examined (Fig. 2A, B S4A). In contrast, TUNEL staining revealed no significant difference in apoptosis between CTRL and KO palatal tissues at the corresponding stages (Fig. S4B), indicating that the reduced cellularity in KO palatal shelves primarily resulted from impaired proliferation rather than increased cell death. Second, we evaluated the osteogenic differentiation capacity of cells using tissue immunofluorescence staining and microCT. Given that significant bone formation in fetal mice initiates from E15.5, we performed immunofluorescence staining for osteoblast differentiation markers in palatal tissues of CTRL and KO mice at this stage. Results revealed markedly reduced distribution of key markers, including tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (Alpl), dentin matrix acidic phosphoprotein 1 (Dmp1), Ocn, Opn, and Osx in KO mice compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 2D–H). Differences were particularly pronounced for the terminal differentiation markers Ocn and Opn. Consistent with this, RT-qPCR analysis of MEPM cells isolated at E14.5 revealed significant downregulation of osteogenic differentiation related genes (Ibsp, Spp1, Bglap, Bmp8a, Sp7, Alpl, Col1A1, Col1A2, and Runx2) in KO mice compared to CTRL mice (Fig. 2I). These findings indicate that Ash2l deficiency during palatal development impairs osteogenic differentiation in MEPM cells.

Moreover, microCT analysis of P0 mouse skulls demonstrated robust bone formation throughout the palate in CTRL mice, notably at the periphery and midline, with dense, well-structured trabeculae. In contrast, KO mice exhibited near-complete absence of palatal bone, significantly reduced maxillary dimensions surrounding the palate, and disorganized trabecular architecture (Fig. 2C). Consistent with these findings, skeletal staining of P0 mice showed comparable results, further confirming maxillary hypoplasia in KO mice, while mandibular development remained largely unaffected (Fig. S3D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate a selective defect in palatal bone formation in Six2-specific Ash2l knockout mice, whereas mandibular development was essentially preserved.

In summary, Six2-Cre-mediated Ash2l ablation impairs proliferation and osteogenic differentiation in murine palatal mesenchymal cells, disrupting palatal bone formation and shelf morphogenesis. These cell-autonomous defects drive cleft palate pathogenesis through failed midfacial development.

This epigenetic perturbation is expected to dysregulate the transcription of essential developmental genes, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed palatal dysmorphogenesis.

To functionally rescue WNT pathway inhibition, we treated MEPM cells from E14.5 KO mice with either SKL2001 (a canonical WNT/β-catenin agonist) or calcium chloride (CaCl2; activating the WNT/Ca2+ branch).

Firstly, we detected the proliferation ability of MEPM cells through the CCK-8 assays. Results demonstrated significantly attenuated growth in KO versus CTRL at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 5D). Pharmacological activation of WNT signaling partially rescued this deficit: both SKL2001 and CaCl2 significantly increased proliferation in KO cells at both time points (Fig. 5D). Notably, neither agonist affected the proliferation of CTRL cells, indicating that the rescue effects were specific to the Ash2l-deficient state. Despite these improvements, neither treatment achieved CTRL-level proliferation. These results further prove that Ash2l is essential for MEPM cell proliferation, and also establish that targeted WNT pathway activation (SKL2001 and CaCl2) partially reverses proliferation defects caused by Ash2l deficiency.

In addition, we also detected the osteogenic differentiation ability of MEPM cells in different groups after osteogenic induction through ALP staining. It revealed severely impaired differentiation in KO, exhibiting minimal ALP staining area. Both WNT pathway agonists partially rescued this deficit; however, neither treatment fully restored it to the CTRL level (Fig. 5E, F). These data confirm that Ash2l is essential for osteogenic commitment in palatal mesenchyme, and pharmacological WNT activation via distinct mechanisms partially reverses differentiation impairments caused by epigenetic dysregulation.

In conclusion, pharmacological activation of WNT signaling via SKL2001 (targeting β-catenin) and CaCl2 (activating WNT/Ca2+) partially rescues both proliferative and osteogenic impairments in Ash2l KO MEPM cells. This functional recovery demonstrates that suppressed WNT signaling constitutes a principal mechanism downstream of ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 dysregulation. Collectively, our epigenetic, transcriptomic, and functional evidence establishes that Ash2l orchestrates palatogenesis through H3K4me3-dependent activation of WNT signaling, whose disruption drives cleft palate pathogenesis.

Discussion

We generated a mouse model with conditional knockout of Ash2l specifically in Six2-expressing cells, showing Ash2l loss in palatal mesenchyme depletes H3K4me3, downregulating palatogenesis genes and inhibiting WNT signaling. This reduces mesenchymal proliferation and osteogenesis, causing palatal shelf hypoplasia with failed fusion and cleft palate.

Histone modification plays a crucial role in embryonic development, with dynamic changes in modification landscapes directing morphogenetic processes [30–32]. H3K4me3 serves as a key transcriptional activator, enriched at promoter regions to license gene expression programs [30]. Mounting evidence has demonstrated that ablation of key epigenetic regulators—including lysine demethylase 6B (KDM6B) [33], PR/SET domain 16 (PRDM16) [34], enhancer of zeste 2 (EZH2) [35], and protein arginine methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) [36]—severely impairs palatogenesis in murine models, yielding cleft palate phenotypes. In summary, these studies confirm histone modification machinery as a critical governor of palatal morphogenesis. Among these modifications, histone methylation demonstrates the strongest clinical and experimental association with orofacial cleft etiology. Immunofluorescence revealed pervasive H3K4me3 occupancy across palatal tissues throughout development. Conditional Ash2l KO in Six2+ cells reduced H3K4me3 modification level of genes, especially key loci in palatal mesenchyme, culminating in cleft palate. This ASH2L-dependent H3K4me3 deficiency impaired MEPM cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, functionally validating H3K4me3 as an essential epigenetic regulator of palatogenesis.

Our findings indicate that following Ash2l ablation, the levels of H3K4me3 modification on genes such as Shh, Bmp4, and Dlx5 were markedly reduced in MEPM cells. Genetic evidence establishes Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling's role in facial morphogenesis: Epithelial-mesenchymal SHH signaling coordinates palatal outgrowth through Smo-mediated proliferation. Mesenchymal Smo deletion abrogates mitogenic responses to epithelial SHH, establishing this paracrine axis as fundamental for shelf morphogenesis [37, 38]. Furthermore, SHH and FGF signaling cooperate through reciprocal positive feedback to orchestrate proliferation in palatal epithelium and mesenchyme [38, 39]. And SHH signaling can upregulate the expression of Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 (Bmp2) and thereby promote cell proliferation within the palatal mesenchyme [40]. Beyond its role in proliferation, BMP signaling is critical for osteogenic differentiation of palatal mesenchymal cells, and its dysregulation may contribute to cleft palate [29]. Crucially, Bmp4 functionally bypasses Muscle Segment Homeobox 1 (Msx1) dependency and operates upstream to regulate Shh and Bmp2, thereby coordinating palatal morphogenesis [40]. The transcription factor Distal-less Homeobox 5 (DLX5) critically regulates palatal morphogenesis. Dlx5-deficient mice exhibit complete palatal shelf elevation and fusion, but develop maxillary arch hypertrophy with soft palate deformities. Mechanistically, DLX5 controls SHH spatial patterning in the palate through Fibroblast Growth Factor 7 (FGF7)-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk [41]. Collectively, this epigenetic dysregulation impaired MEPM cell proliferation and osteogenic capacity, mechanistically linking H3K4me3 deficiency to functional deficits via transcriptional silencing of key morphogenetic regulators.

In addition, we observed significant H3K4me3 depletion at WNT pathway loci (Wnt2 and Wnt5b), with concomitant transcriptional downregulation of these genes and downstream effector such as Camk2a, Camk2d, and Nfatc2. Neural crest-derived craniofacial mesenchyme receives instructive signals from WNT families during facial and palatal morphogenesis [8, 42]. Among these, the Canonical and non-canonical WNT pathways critically regulates embryonic development [43]. During craniofacial morphogenesis, it orchestrates palatal shelf patterning, osteogenic differentiation, and fusion [44–49]. Murine models with perturbations in core pathway components—including Wnt6 [50], Wnt9b [51, 52], Ctnnb1(β-catenin) [53], and Wnt5a [54]—constitutively exhibit cleft palate. These findings establish WNT signaling as a central coordinator of palatogenesis, integrating upstream morphogenetic cues with downstream transcriptional programs. These findings establish the WNT pathway as a critical mediator of palatal morphogenesis, whose epigenetic dysregulation drives cleft palate in Ash2l-deficient models.

WNT5B primarily activates non-canonical signaling pathways, where it partners with WNT5A in the WNT/Ca2⁺ cascade while independently engaging the WNT/PCP (planar cell polarity) pathway [55, 56]. Additionally, context-dependent modulation of canonical signaling by WNT5B has been reported [57–60]. Zebrafish Wnt5b mutants exhibit convergence-extension (CE) defects characteristic of impaired planar cell polarity, along with a concomitant reduction in palatal complex length [61, 62]. Moreover, WNT5B regulates hematopoietic stem cell maintenance [63], pacemaker cardiomyocyte differentiation [60], and chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation [64]. Although previously understudied in palatal development, WNT5B is now recognized as critical for this process. Causative WNT5B mutations were identified in a case of bilateral cleft lip/palate [65], and population-level evidence from multinational studies (US [66], Poland [67], China [68]) supports its role as a susceptibility gene for non-syndromic orofacial clefts. Notably, our study demonstrated that pharmacological activation of WNT signaling downstream of Wnt5b using SKL2001 (canonical) or CaCl2 (WNT/Ca2⁺) partially rescued the impaired proliferation and osteogenic differentiation capacity in Ash2l-deficient MEPM cells. Our findings provide the first functional evidence that WNT5B is involved in mammalian palatal development, identifying it as a novel downstream effector of the ASH2L-H3K4me3 axis. WNT5B contributes to embryonic morphogenesis—particularly palatogenesis—by regulating WNT-mediated cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation. Collectively, these data position the WNT5B-mediated WNT pathway as a relevant downstream effector linking ASH2L deficiency to cleft palate pathogenesis. We acknowledge, however, that conditional ablation of Wnt5b in palatal mesenchyme will be required to definitively establish its essential role in this process.

Importantly, our findings from the mouse model also offer a potential explanation for the apparent absence of ASH2L-associated cleft palate cases in humans. In our study, heterozygous mice (Ash2lflox/+, Six2-creGFP) were viable and phenotypically normal, with Ash2l expression and Ash2l protein levels comparable to wild-type controls. In contrast, homozygous knockouts (Ash2lflox/flox, Six2-creGFP) exhibited complete cleft palate and neonatal lethality. Extrapolating to humans, we propose that heterozygous ASH2L mutations may not produce haploinsufficiency and thus may not result in clinically recognizable phenotypes; homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations, if they occur, may be embryonically lethal and therefore escape clinical detection. This scenario would explain why ASH2L variants have not been identified in existing cleft palate cohorts, despite the critical role of ASH2L in palatogenesis demonstrated here.

Conclusion

Our study elucidates the mechanistic role of ASH2L-mediated H3K4me3 in palatogenesis. Ash2l controls H3K4me3 deposition at key palatal developmental genes-notably Wnt5b-modulating their expression to orchestrate both canonical Wnt/β-catenin and non-canonical Wnt/Ca2⁺ signaling. This epigenetic regulation coordinates downstream transcriptional networks that govern MEPM cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately driving palatal morphogenesis (Fig. 5G). These results define a molecular nexus linking H3K4me3 modifications to palate development and identify novel etiological mechanisms of cleft palate, offering new insights into its etiology. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate the essential role of Wnt5b in palatal development and provide an important direction for future research in this field.
