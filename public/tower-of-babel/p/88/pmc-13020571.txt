Veno‐Venous Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation for High‐Risk Airway Interventions: A Safety‐Net Hospital Case Series
Authors: Stephanie Rothweiler, Christian Tagle, Anilkumar Mehra, Rodrigo Garcia Tome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐risk airway interventions in patients with critical airway obstruction carry a substantial risk of respiratory failure, and conventional ventilation strategies may be inadequate to ensure procedural safety. Consequently, some patients are denied potentially life‐saving or palliative interventions. Veno‐venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) has emerged as an adjunctive strategy to provide temporary respiratory support during complex airway procedures, though evidence remains limited. We present a case series of three patients with near‐complete airway obstruction who underwent planned VV‐ECMO–assisted therapeutic airway interventions at a safety‐net hospital. Indications included severe benign tracheal stenosis and malignant central airway obstruction. In all cases, VV‐ECMO enabled rigid bronchoscopy and multimodal airway intervention without intraprocedural respiratory decompensation or reliance on conventional ventilation. All patients experienced rapid symptomatic improvement, were successfully decannulated, and had favourable outcomes. These cases demonstrate that planned VV‐ECMO can safely facilitate high‐risk airway interventions and highlight its feasibility in a safety‐net setting, with implications for expanding equitable access to advanced airway rescue strategies.

Introduction

Interventional Pulmonology is a rapidly evolving subspecialty dedicated to the diagnosis and management of complex airway and pleural diseases. Many therapeutic airway interventions are performed in patients with critical airway pathology, in whom even minor procedural complications can precipitate acute airway compromise and respiratory failure [1, 2, 3].

Conventional respiratory support strategies, including mechanical ventilation, jet ventilation and other adjunctive oxygenation techniques, may be insufficient to ensure adequate gas exchange during these high‐risk procedures [1]. As a result, the anticipated risk of respiratory decompensation may be prohibitive and can preclude patients from receiving potentially life‐saving or life‐prolonging interventions [1].

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has emerged as a strategy to enhance procedural safety during select airway interventions [4, 5]. However, ECMO initiation is resource intensive and carries inherent risks, including bleeding, thromboembolic events, vascular injury and neurological complications [1]. Consequently, its use has traditionally been reserved for patients with refractory respiratory or cardiac failure. In carefully selected cases, planned pre‐procedural ECMO initiation may prevent respiratory emergencies and expand access to therapeutic bronchoscopy for patients otherwise deemed too high risk for intervention [1].

More recently, ECMO has been increasingly applied in nontraditional settings [6], including short‐term use to provide oxygenation and carbon dioxide removal during interventions for critical airway obstruction. In this context, ECMO allows airway procedures to be performed without reliance on conventional ventilation strategies, reducing concerns related to airway security, hypoxemia, hypercapnia and airway fire during thermal therapies.

Despite these advances, substantial disparities persist in access to ECMO. Female patients, individuals insured by Medicaid, and those residing in low‐income neighbourhoods are less likely to receive ECMO support [7]. These inequities may reflect limited access, restrictive transfer policies, patient preferences and implicit provider bias. Recognising that inequity in care threatens outcomes for all patients, major medical societies have emphasised the need to address disparities across the continuum of critical care [7, 8].

Here, we present a series of successful VV‐ECMO–assisted therapeutic airway interventions performed in a safety‐net hospital in Los Angeles, United States, demonstrating that with a committed multidisciplinary team, advanced airway rescue strategies can be safely implemented and disparities in access to care can be meaningfully addressed in vulnerable populations.

Case 1

A 22‐year‐old woman with psychogenic nonepileptic seizures and a history of more than 10 emergent endotracheal intubations developed severe subglottic tracheal stenosis. She was managed as an outpatient by otolaryngology with serial balloon dilations, most recently 6 weeks prior to presentation, with plans for definitive tracheal resection.

She presented to the emergency department with acute worsening dyspnea and increased work of breathing. She was tachycardic and hypertensive, with oxygen saturation > 95% on room air. Examination revealed biphasic stridor and tracheal tugging. Computed tomography of the neck and chest demonstrated marked progression of subglottic stenosis with a residual lumen of approximately 3–4 mm.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and treated with nebulised bronchodilators, racemic epinephrine and intravenous corticosteroids. Despite therapy, her respiratory status rapidly deteriorated and was refractory to heliox (80% helium/20% oxygen). Bedside nasolaryngoscopy and tracheoscopy under topical anaesthesia revealed a critical subglottic stenosis measuring 3–4 mm in diameter, partially obstructed by secretions and located approximately 3.5 cm below the vocal cords.

Given the critically narrowed airway and inability to safely intubate, a multidisciplinary discussion involving intensive care, interventional pulmonology, otolaryngology and interventional cardiology concluded that veno‐venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) support was the safest approach to facilitate therapeutic rigid tracheoscopy.

The patient was taken emergently to the operating room, where bilateral femoral venous cannulation was performed and VV‐ECMO initiated. Rigid tracheoscopy with radial electrocautery incisions followed by balloon dilation to 10 mm was performed. The patient remained fully supported on ECMO without endotracheal oxygenation, reducing the risk of airway fire during thermal therapy.

Within 12 h, she was extubated and decannulated from ECMO, with complete resolution of dyspnea and stridor. Forty‐eight hours later, she underwent successful en bloc resection of a 3‐cm tracheal segment and was discharged home in excellent condition 5 days postoperatively. Case 2

A 44‐year‐old woman with metastatic endometrial sarcoma, refractory to multiple lines of chemotherapy, presented with progressive malignant central airway obstruction due to both intrinsic tumour growth and extrinsic compression from metastatic disease originating in the right lung parenchyma with invasion of the right‐sided airways. She presented to the emergency department with tachycardia, severe dyspnea, biphasic stridor, and hypoxemia to 85% on room air.

Computed tomography of the chest demonstrated dramatic interval tumour progression, with near‐complete (> 95%) obstruction of the distal trachea. Although her malignancy was deemed incurable, a multidisciplinary discussion and goals‐of‐care conversation were held, and a decision was made to pursue palliative rigid bronchoscopy with tumour debulking for symptom relief.

Given the extensive intraluminal tumour burden and the high procedural risk associated with near‐total airway obstruction, veno‐venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) support was utilised. Multimodal debulking was performed, including rigid coring mechanical debulking, cryotherapy, argon plasma coagulation and electrocautery. This resulted in complete removal of tumour obstructing the distal trachea, restoration of a fully patent left‐sided airway, and successful recanalization of the right upper lobe bronchus.

In light of the palliative intent and the risks associated with further debulking of chronically infiltrative tumour originating from the proximal bronchus intermedius, the procedure was concluded at that point. The patient was decannulated immediately in the procedure suite and returned to the intensive care unit breathing comfortably on room air. She was discharged the following day without complications. Case 3

A 70‐year‐old woman with a history of rheumatoid arthritis and acquired tracheal stenosis secondary to repeated endotracheal intubations during episodes of severe gastrointestinal bleeding presented to the emergency department with acute shortness of breath.

On examination, she was noted to have biphasic stridor. In the setting of known tracheal stenosis, she was treated with nebulised bronchodilators, inhaled racemic epinephrine and intravenous corticosteroids. Bedside laryngotracheoscopy performed by otolaryngology revealed approximately 90% tracheal stenosis located 4 cm below the vocal cords.

Within several hours of admission, the patient developed rapidly progressive dyspnea and was unable to tolerate the supine position despite inhaled heliox therapy. Given the critical endoscopic findings and ongoing clinical deterioration, the decision was made to proceed with therapeutic rigid bronchoscopy under veno‐venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV‐ECMO) support.

Following cannulation and initiation of VV‐ECMO, radial electrocautery incisions and cryotherapy were applied at the level of stenosis, followed by balloon dilation to 12 mm. The patient required no mechanical ventilation during the procedure, as ECMO provided adequate oxygenation and carbon dioxide removal.

Immediately following completion of the intervention, the patient was decannulated and returned to the intensive care unit breathing comfortably on room air. She subsequently underwent definitive surgical tracheal resection within 6 months of this admission. Discussion

Practice patterns for the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during high‐risk airway interventions remain highly variable, and consensus recommendations are lacking. Available evidence is limited largely to case reports and small case series, reflecting both the rarity and complexity of these clinical scenarios. Despite its inherent risks, ECMO has the potential to prevent or mitigate catastrophic respiratory complications during airway interventions that would otherwise carry prohibitive risk.

The successful use of veno‐venous ECMO (VV‐ECMO) in this series highlights its role as a proactive adjunct rather than a last‐resort rescue therapy. Our limited experience demonstrates that planned initiation of VV‐ECMO can safely facilitate high‐risk airway interventions across a range of indications without intraprocedural respiratory decompensation in patients deemed unsuitable for conventional ventilatory strategies. In carefully selected individuals with near‐complete airway compromise or anticipated high‐risk airway manipulation, pre‐procedural VV‐ECMO may expand therapeutic options and enable definitive interventions that might otherwise be deferred or deemed unfeasible.

Initiating ECMO prior to airway intervention may reduce adverse events related to hypoxemia, hypercapnia and airway loss; however, this strategy must be balanced against the risks inherent to ECMO itself, including bleeding, thromboembolic events, vascular injury and neurological complications, as well as increased procedural cost and resource utilisation. These considerations underscore the importance of careful patient selection, multidisciplinary planning and institutional expertise when adopting ECMO‐supported airway interventions.

Beyond procedural considerations, ECMO‐supported airway intervention raises important questions regarding health equity and access to advanced care. Population‐level data in the United States demonstrate substantial disparities in ECMO utilisation, with patients insured by Medicaid, individuals residing in low‐income neighbourhoods, and female patients significantly less likely to receive ECMO support compared with privately insured or higher‐income populations [7]. These disparities likely reflect a combination of factors, including differences in referral patterns, restrictive transfer policies, implicit provider bias and the uneven geographic distribution of ECMO‐capable centres. Many established ECMO programmes remain concentrated in large tertiary referral centres, while safety‐net and community hospitals—often serving the most vulnerable populations—lack the infrastructure to provide this level of care.

In this context, our experience from a safety‐net hospital is particularly relevant. These cases demonstrate that with robust multidisciplinary collaboration, standardised protocols and appropriate resource allocation, VV‐ECMO–assisted airway interventions can be safely and effectively performed in settings that care predominantly for underserved or underinsured patients. By reporting these cases, we aim not only to contribute to the growing body of literature supporting ECMO‐assisted airway management but also to highlight the feasibility of extending advanced interventional pulmonology services beyond traditionally well‐resourced institutions.
