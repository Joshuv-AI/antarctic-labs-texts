Predictive value of stress hyperglycaemia ratio for 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fracture: a retrospective single-centre study in Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China
Authors: Xue Zhan, Hao Wang, Ying Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the predictive value of stress hyperglycaemia ratio (SHR) for 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures.DesignA retrospective study.SettingBeijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University.ParticipantsThis study included elderly patients with hip fractures treated at our hospital between January and April 2023.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and Youden index were employed to ascertain the optimal predictive threshold for SHR and to evaluate its predictive accuracy for 28-day all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 443 elderly patients with hip fractures was analysed, with a mean age of 78.11±7.83 years in the survival group and 88±5.48 years in the non-survival group. Deceased patients exhibited higher rates of pulmonary infection, acute coronary syndrome, acute kidney injury, cardiac insufficiency and renal insufficiency than survivors. They also had older age, lower body weight, hypoalbuminaemia, elevated admission glucose, increased N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide and higher SHR. The cohort comprised 121 men (27.3%), and all-cause mortality was observed in 17 patients (3.84%). Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that SHR (p<0.001, OR=6.944, 95% CI 2.584 to 18.659) was an independent risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality. The SHR demonstrated an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.70 for predicting 28-day mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures, with a sensitivity of 64.71% and specificity of 85.68%.ConclusionsThe SHR is associated with 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures, suggesting its potential as a predictive marker for this outcome.Keywords: DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY; Fractures, Bone; Observational Study

Introduction

Hip fractures are a significant health concern among the elderly, often precipitating a cascade of complications that can lead to all-cause mortality.1 2 These fractures not only result in substantial mortality but also impose a considerable burden on healthcare systems due to the extended recovery periods and increased need for long-term care.3 Vigano et al have consistently shown that the 1 year mortality rate following a hip fracture can exceed 20%, with the majority of deaths occurring within the first few months post-injury.4 The high mortality rate is multifactorial, encompassing factors such as advanced age, comorbidities, pre-fracture functional status and post-fracture complications like infections, venous thromboembolism and prolonged immobility.4 5 In light of these findings, it is crucial to further explore the intricate relationship between hip fractures and all-cause mortality in the elderly. This exploration is essential for informing the development of more effective preventative measures, refined treatment protocols and enhanced post-fracture care.

Despite considerable research, the clinical significance of hyperglycaemia in hospitalised patients, particularly stress hyperglycaemia in non-diabetic individuals, is not fully elucidated. Hyperglycaemia is associated with increased morbidity and mortality in various conditions such as myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), stroke and critical illness, yet the specific impact of stress hyperglycaemia often goes unaddressed in clinical practice.6,8 This is a significant gap, as new-onset stress hyperglycaemia potentially poses a greater mortality risk than pre-existing diabetes.9 10 Stress hyperglycaemia, resulting from inflammatory and neurohormonal responses to severe illness, can worsen clinical outcomes through mechanisms like endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress.11 While glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) provides a measure of blood glucose control over the preceding 8–12 weeks, its predictive value for stress hyperglycaemia outcomes has been underexplored.12 The stress hyperglycaemia ratio (SHR), proposed by Roberts et al, is calculated as the admission glucose level divided by the estimated average glucose from HbA1c (eAG=HbA1 c×1.59–2.59).13 This metric has been associated with adverse outcomes, with cohort studies confirming SHR’s independent prediction of increased mortality and complications risk.14 15 However, the clinical efficacy of SHR is not definitively established, and there is a scarcity of data on its prognostic value in elderly patients with hip fractures. Therefore, this study aims to determine whether the SHR predicts 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of elderly patients who sustained hip fractures and were treated at our hospital from January to April 2023. Inclusion criteria: (1) age of 65 years or older and (2) hip fractures that conformed to the 2022 ‘Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Hip Fractures in the Elderly,’16 as issued by the General Office of the National Health Commission. This included fractures of the femoral neck, intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric regions.

Exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete medical records, specifically those lacking initial blood glucose levels or HbA1c data and (2) a history of hormone therapy within the preceding 28 days.

Data collection and definitions

Patient demographics and medical histories were meticulously documented, including age, gender, height, body mass index and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, previous myocardial infarction and smoking status.

Unmeasured confounders: Pre-fracture mobility (eg, ability to walk independently outdoors), frailty status (Clinical Frailty Scale) and Charlson Comorbidity Index were inconsistently documented and therefore not included in the multivariable model.

On admission, venous blood glucose levels (admission blood glucose, aBG) were recorded for elderly patients with hip fractures to evaluate stress-induced hyperglycaemia. Subsequently, HbA1c was assessed from morning blood samples to establish a baseline for long-term glycaemic control. The 28-day all-cause mortality in patients was determined via a 28-day post-discharge follow-up, combining outpatient visits and telephone interviews.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of 443 elderly patients with hip fractures included in this study was determined based on the consecutive eligible cases admitted to our hospital from January to April 2023. During this study period, all patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria (age ≥65 years, hip fractures conforming to the 2022 ‘Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Hip Fractures in the Elderly’) and excluded those with incomplete medical records (lacking initial blood glucose levels or HbA1c data) and a history of hormone therapy within the preceding 28 days were enrolled. Although no a priori power calculation was performed, the sample size is sufficient to reflect the clinical characteristics and outcomes of elderly patients with hip fractures in our centre during this specific period. Additionally, considering the low number of events (17 cases of 28-day all-cause mortality), we applied Firth penalised maximum-likelihood regression and 1000 bootstrap resamples for internal validation to mitigate the potential impact of small event numbers on the model, ensuring the reliability of the study results as much as possible.

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables exhibiting normal distribution, descriptive statistics were presented as the mean±SD. Group comparisons for these data were conducted using the independent samples t-test. In cases where data were markedly skewed, medians with IQRs were reported, and group comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test, also known as the rank-sum test. Categorical data were expressed as rates (%), and group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to assess the relationship between stress hyperglycaemia and 28-day mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures. The predictive performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at p<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 443 patients were included in this study, 121 (27.3%) were men. Significant differences were observed between patients who died within 28 days (non-survival group) and those who did not (survival group) in general situation (table 1). Notably, compared with the survival group, the non-survival group had significantly higher rates of pulmonary infection (64.71% vs 28.9%, p=0.002), acute coronary syndrome (ACS) (11.76% vs 1.2%, p=0.026), acute kidney injury (AKI) (29.41% vs 4.0%, p<0.001), cardiac insufficiency (35.29% vs 5.2%, p<0.001) and renal insufficiency (17.6% vs 4.0%, p=0.036). In addition, the non-survival group was older (p<0.001), had lower body weight (p=0.013), lower albumin (p=0.037), higher aBG (p=0.007), higher N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (p=0.007) and higher SHR (p=0.02). To explore the potential impact of surgical procedure type on patient outcomes such as pulmonary infection rates, we included the type of surgery (internal fixation, femoral head replacement, total hip arthroplasty) as a variable in the statistical analysis. However, the results showed that there was no significant difference in the distribution of surgical types between the survival group and the non-survival group (p=0.87). Specifically, in the survival group, 221 cases (51.9%) underwent internal fixation, 165 cases (38.7%) underwent femoral head replacement and 40 cases (9.4%) underwent total hip arthroplasty; in the non-survival group, 8 cases (47.06%) underwent internal fixation, 8 cases (47.06%) underwent femoral head replacement and 1 case (5.88%) underwent total hip arthroplasty. This suggests that in our study cohort, the type of surgical procedure did not have a significant effect on the difference in pulmonary infection rates between the two groups.

Unfortunately, due to the retrospective nature of the study and incomplete documentation in some medical records, detailed information on the causes of death in the non-survival group (17 cases) could not be fully collected. This is a limitation of the current study, as understanding the specific causes of death would help to further clarify the mechanisms by which SHR and other factors affect mortality. Future studies should pay more attention to collecting detailed data on causes of death to provide more comprehensive insights into patient outcomes.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

The multivariate logistic regression analysis (table 2) revealed that age (p=0.001, OR=1.19, 95% CI 1.079 to 1.314), body weight (p=0.017, OR=0.92, 95% CI 0.859 to 0.985), NT-proBNP levels (p=0.024, OR=1.001, 95% CI 1.000 to 1.001), SHR (p<0.001, OR=6.944, 95% CI 2.584 to 18.659) and the presence of cardiac insufficiency (p=0.015, OR=6.299, 95% CI 1.424 to 27.862) and renal insufficiency (p=0.001, OR=33.442, 95% CI 4.621 to 242.018) were significantly associated with 28-day all-cause mortality.

Predictive effectiveness

The predictive value of stress hyperglycaemia, as measured by the SHR, was further evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (figure 1). The AUC for SHR was 0.7 (95% CI 0.655 to 0.742), indicating a good discriminatory ability. The optimal cut-off value for SHR was determined to be 1.476, with a sensitivity of 64.71% and a specificity of 85.68% (table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the SHR is a significant predictor of 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures. The SHR’s prognostic value suggests its utility as a clinical tool for risk assessment, underscoring the importance of stress hyperglycaemia monitoring in optimising treatment and patient care.

Hip fractures, a common affliction among the elderly, are anticipated to affect 6.3 million individuals globally by 2050, with a majority in Asia.17 The postoperative mortality rate for these patients is alarmingly high, ranging from 15% to 36% within the first year.18 Therefore, refining clinical management strategies for elderly patients with hip fractures is a critical challenge in medical practice and hospital administration. In addition to the association between SHR and 28-day all-cause mortality, our results also showed significant differences between the survival and non-survival groups in several other aspects. The non-survival group had a significantly higher incidence of pulmonary infection (64.71% vs 28.9%, p=0.002), which may be related to the compromised immune function of elderly patients after hip fracture. Hip fracture often leads to prolonged bed rest, reduced respiratory function and increased risk of respiratory tract infections. For the non-survival group, the higher incidence of ACS (11.76% vs 1.2%, p=0.026) may be due to the stress response caused by hip fracture and surgery, which can induce or exacerbate myocardial ischaemia. This study’s results, in concordance with existing literature, highlight the significant association between higher SHR quartiles and the risk of ischaemic stroke progressing to haemorrhagic stroke,19 as well as SHR’s independent correlation with the severity of cerebral oedema following acute ischaemic stroke, poor functional outcomes and increased mortality.20 A multicentre prospective study has also linked high SHR to increased long-term mortality.21 Stress hyperglycaemia has been implicated in adverse outcomes among patients without diabetes and with stroke and acute myocardial infarction.22 23 These findings underscore the imperative to address stress hyperglycaemia in elderly patients with hip fractures and underscore the necessity for interventions aimed at reducing mortality and adverse event risks.

AKI (29.41% vs 4.0%, p<0.001) and cardiac insufficiency (35.29% vs 5.2%, p<0.001) were also more common in the non-survival group. The stress of fracture and surgery, combined with potential pre-existing renal and cardiac dysfunction, may contribute to the development of these complications. Older age (p<0.001) in the non-survival group is consistent with previous studies, as advanced age is a well-known risk factor for poor outcomes in patients with hip fractures, associated with decreased organ function and increased susceptibility to complications. Lower body weight (p=0.013) and hypoalbuminaemia (p=0.037) in the non-survival group suggest poor nutritional status, which can impair the body’s ability to repair tissues, resist infections and recover from surgery, thereby increasing mortality risk. Renal insufficiency (17.6% vs 4.0%, p=0.036) in the non-survival group may further compromise the body’s metabolic and excretory functions, making it more difficult to cope with the stress of illness and treatment. These factors, together with the elevated SHR, may interact to increase the 28-day all-cause mortality risk in elderly patients with hip fractures. Clinically, attention should be paid not only to monitoring and regulating SHR but also to preventing and treating these complications, improving nutritional status and managing pre-existing comorbidities to reduce mortality. Although the low event-per-variable ratio raises the possibility of overfitting, the Firth correction and bootstrap validation yielded consistent effect sizes, supporting the robustness of our findings.

In elderly patients with hip fractures, higher SHR levels are linked to an increased risk of all-cause mortality, likely due to inflammatory responses to glucose fluctuations. The elevated SHR indicates a complex interaction of stress hormones including catecholamines, glucocorticoids and cytokines, which rise following fracture and surgery.24 25 This stress-induced hyperglycaemia enhances mitochondrial reactive oxygen species in endothelial cells, leading to endothelial dysfunction.26 Experimental data show that transient hyperglycaemia can significantly impair immune function, provoke cytokine-mediated inflammation and promote oxidative stress in organs such as the liver.27 28 Hyperglycaemia also activates monocytes, increasing the production of proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6).29 High IL-6 levels worsen insulin resistance and stimulate hepatic glucose output, perpetuating hyperglycaemia.30 31 IL-6, involved in inflammation and tissue damage, is associated with adverse outcomes.32 33 Tumour necrosis factor-α, a major proinflammatory factor in sepsis, is linked to poor outcomes and insulin resistance.34 35 The profound pro-inflammatory effects of hyperglycaemia suggest that even minor reductions in blood glucose levels can have anti-inflammatory effects, highlighting the importance of glycaemic control in elderly patients with hip fractures.

Recent studies have also explored other preoperative predictors of outcomes in elderly patients with hip fractures. For example, Çiçek et al in their single-centre study found that preoperative cardiac risk factors are associated with in-hospital mortality in elderly patients without heart failure undergoing hip fracture surgery.36 Orhan et al reported that atrial fibrillation as a preoperative risk factor predicts long-term mortality in elderly patients without heart failure and undergoing hip fracture surgery.37 These studies highlight the importance of comprehensive assessment of preoperative factors in predicting outcomes of elderly patients with hip fractures. In addition to traditional clinical indicators, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) based systems or algorithms have shown potential in predicting outcomes of various diseases. For instance, a study developed a new risk prediction model using relevant data to assess myocardial injury in elderly patients undergoing non-elective surgery,38 and another study used deep learning to predict short-term mortality in patients with acute pulmonary embolism.39 These advanced technologies may provide more accurate and efficient prediction tools for clinical practice in the future. However, the application of AI/ML in predicting outcomes of elderly patients with hip fractures, especially in combination with indicators like SHR, remains to be further explored.

Although we performed multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for potential confounding factors, there may still be residual confounding. Important variables such as pre-fracture mobility (eg, ability to walk independently outdoors), frailty status (Clinical Frailty Scale) and Charlson Comorbidity Index, which are known to be associated with postoperative mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures, were not included in the multivariable model due to inconsistent documentation in medical records. These unmeasured factors may have some impact on the observed association between SHR and 28-day all-cause mortality. Future studies with more complete data collection on these variables are needed to further adjust for these confounders and validate the relationship between SHR and mortality.

This study is a single-centre retrospective study, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. Different medical practices, patient populations and hospital resources across different centres may lead to variations in patient outcomes and the predictive value of SHR. Additionally, the study lacks external validation. External validation using data from other independent centres is essential to confirm the reliability and generalisability of the predictive value of SHR for 28-day all-cause mortality in elderly patients with hip fractures. Future multicentre prospective studies are needed to address these limitations.

In conclusion, this study confirms that elevated SHR levels are an independent predictor of 28-day all-cause mortality and are significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse events in elderly patients with hip fractures, underscoring SHR’s potential as a valuable prognostic tool in this demographic. Future multicentre, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and further explore the clinical utility of SHR in predicting outcomes following hip fractures.
