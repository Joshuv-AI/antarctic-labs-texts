Sex-specific associations between body mass index trajectories and risk of hypertension
Authors: Jiayu Wang, Jialing Zhang, Yiting Gui, Yu Huo, Da Xu, Fan Bai, Li Cao, Yonghao Gui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough it is well established that obesity is a risk factor for hypertension, the effect of distinct long-term patterns of body mass index (BMI) on blood pressure (BP) in later life is poorly understood.MethodsBased on the China Health and Nutrition Survey, we analyzed 2920 participants aged 3–17 years with initial normal BP at baseline (1991–2011), who were followed up for the development of hypertension (1993–2015). The group-based trajectory model was applied to identify BMI trajectories, and Cox regression was used to assess their associations with hypertension risk. Stratified analyses were conducted to explore differences across subgroups.ResultsDuring an average follow-up time of 11.20 (7.69) years for males and 7.20 (5.21) years for females, 339 males and 212 females were identified with hypertension, respectively. Three BMI trajectories were identified: low-increasing (60.58% of males and 73.03% of females), moderate-increasing (33.08% of males and 24.22% of females), and high-increasing (6.34% of males and 2.76% of females). Our study found a significant positive association between a higher BMI trajectory and hypertension risk in males (all P for trend < 0.05). Specifically, males in the high-increasing BMI group had a higher risk of hypertension compared with those in the low-increasing group (HR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.04–2.97). Stratified analyses revealed stronger associations among smokers, drinkers, and inactive individuals.ConclusionOur findings suggest that maintaining a normal BMI and healthy lifestyle from childhood may lower subsequent risk of hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05151-w.Keywords: Body mass index, Childhood obesity, Hypertension, Blood pressure, Trajectory analysis

Background

Hypertension is a leading cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD), accounting for approximately 10.8 million deaths globally each year [1, 2]. A 6-round nationwide survey conducted between 2004 and 2018 revealed a significant increase in the prevalence of hypertension among Chinese adults, rising from 20.8% in 2004 to 24.7% in 2018 [3]. Notably, hypertension in adulthood can be traced back to childhood [4, 5]. Based on the Chinese National Survey on Students’ Constitution and Health from 2010 to 2019, the prevalence of hypertension at a single time point in school-age children aged 7–17 years was 13.0% [6].

It is well established that childhood overweight/obesity, indicated by body mass index (BMI), is associated with subsequent risk of hypertension [7]. However, most prior studies rely on a single BMI measurement, neglecting its dynamic change over time. Recently, epidemiologic research has used advanced methods, such as the latent class growth mixture model (LCGMM) and group-based trajectory model (GBTM), to investigate the life-course trajectories of BMI and their effects on BP/hypertension [8–10]. Trajectory analysis can illustrate the changing patterns of certain exposures over time, identifying critical time points and specific groups for targeted intervention [11]. However, most of the past studies were limited by the absence of sex-specific analyses [12], the inclusion of patients with various diseases at baseline [13], short follow-up periods [14], and/or inadequate control for potential confounders [15]. A meta-analysis pooling data from 6 studies found that participants with worsening or persistent obesity were more likely to develop hypertension in adulthood than those who had a stable, normal weight trajectory [16]. However, the observation of considerable between-study heterogeneity has limited the reliability of overall estimates. Furthermore, little information regarding lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, drinking, and physical activity) is available, and these may play a crucial role in modifying the association between obesity and hypertension.

Therefore, by leveraging data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), we aimed to assess the association between BMI trajectories and the subsequent development of hypertension. Additionally, we sought to explore this association within lifestyle subgroups, offering insights regarding the interplay between BMI and lifestyle factors.

Methods

Study population

CHNS, a population-based longitudinal survey conducted in China, examined health risk factors, nutritional status, sociologic and demographic transformation, and health-related outcomes in the Chinese population [17]. The open database, study materials, and acknowledgment are available at the organization’s website (https://www.cpc.unc.edu/projects/china). Briefly, a multistage random cluster method was applied to select a sample of approximately 7200 households with over 30,000 individuals in 15 provinces [18]. The first survey wave started in 1989 and subsequent surveys were conducted at 2- to 4-year intervals until 2015 (comprising 10 waves overall).

In the present study, data from the 1989 survey were not used because only children younger than 7 years of age and adults aged 20–45 years of age were surveyed in that year [19]. For the present study, 1204 participants with abnormal data on BMI (< 10 or > 45 kg/m2) were excluded. A total of 24,670 participants with ≥ 2 BMI measurement between 1991 and 2015 were included. We further excluded 18,813 participants who were either below age 3 or older than age 17 at baseline (1991–2011). Additionally, we excluded 1507 participants without BP records and 378 individuals who exhibited hypertension at baseline (1991–2011). Furthermore, we excluded 445 individuals without BP data at the end of the follow-up. Consequently, a total of 2920 participants remained in the final analysis (1578 males and 1342 females) (Fig. 1).

Exposure

BMI was computed from anthropometric measurements of height and weight (BMI = weight [kg]/square of height [m2]). Height was measured without shoes to the nearest 0.1 cm using a portable stadiometer and weight was measured without shoes and in light clothing on a calibrated floor scale to the nearest 0.1 kg. Childhood overweight/obesity was determined according to the weight criteria for Chinese school-age children and adolescents [20].

Outcome

At each assessment, after the subjects had 10 min of seated rest, trained physicians used standard mercury sphygmomanometers to measure the blood pressure 3 times on the right arm. The first and fifth Korotkoff sounds were recorded as systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respectively [12, 14]. We used the average of the BP values in our analysis. Pediatric hypertension was defined according to the latest Chinese guidelines [21]. Specifically, it was characterized by measured SBP and/or DBP values that exceeded or were equal to the 95th percentile for the corresponding age, sex, and height-specific BP references. These references were established based on a comprehensive national sample of Chinese children [22]. In adulthood, hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg [21]. Additionally, participants with self-reported hypertension, or those using antihypertension medications were defined as hypertension.

Covariates

Each subject’s residential area was categorized as urban (cities and towns) or rural (outlying areas). The geographic region was classified into south (Shanghai, Jiangsu, Hubei, Hunan, Guangxi, Guizhou, and Chongqing) and north (Beijing, Liaoning, Heilongjiang, Shandong, and Henan). Smoking and drinking status were divided into former (coded as 1) and never (coded as 0) according to the interview responses of participants. Physical activity was measured using a detailed weekly activity recall, including occupational, domestic, travel and active leisure activity [23]. Based on this assessment, individuals were classified into active (participating in at least one physical activity, coded as 1) and inactive (coded as 0). Abdominal obesity was defined as follows: [1] For children aged 7–18 years: WC ≥ 90th percentile for age- and sex-specific references [2]. For adults: WC ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women. In the CHNS study, subjects with missing values on any covariates were coded as 9 in order to preserve power.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the mean (standard deviation [SD]). Categorical variables were presented as numbers (proportion). BMI trajectories were identified using GBTM [24], a data-derived statistical approach that does not rely on predefined subgroups. This method uses maximum likelihood to identify latent subgroups of individuals with similar trajectories for a variable. The optimal number of trajectory groups and the degree of polynomials in each trajectory group were selected on the basis of a combination of rigorous criteria, including the Bayesian Information Criterion and average posterior probability [25]. All trajectory models consisting of 2–5 trajectory groups were fitted until the best model was obtained. We conducted GBTM separately for both sexes because BMI trajectory could have sex disparities [26].

We used Cox regression models to examine the association of BMI trajectory groups with hypertension. Years of follow-up were calculated from baseline to the end of follow-up (the survey date of hypertension diagnosis, death, or the date of the last survey, whichever came first). P for trend was calculated by modeling the ordered BMI trajectories as a 1-degree-of-freedom linear term. Model 1 adjusted for baseline age. Model 2 additionally adjusted for race (Han or other), residential area (urban or rural), and geographic region (north or south). Model 3 additionally adjusted for baseline BMI, SBP and DBP. Model 4 additionally adjusted for current smoking status, alcohol drinking status, and physical activity. Model 5 additionally adjusted for current abdominal obesity. Stratified analyses were performed to examine whether the associations differed across subgroups according to current smoking status (ever, never), alcohol drinking status (ever, never), and physical activity (inactive, active). To investigate potential interaction effects, we compared the models with and without a cross-product term between the BMI trajectory and stratified variables by likelihood ratio tests in multivariable Cox regression models. In the sensitivity analyses, we excluded participants within 2 years of follow-up time.

Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with GBTM conducted using the PROC TRAJ plug-in. All statistical tests were 2-sided with a 5% significance level.

Results

Trajectory of body mass index and baseline characteristics

Figure 2 illustrates 3 distinct BMI trajectory groups in each sex, categorized as follows: low-increasing (60.58% of males and 73.03% of females), moderate-increasing (33.08% of males and 24.22% of females), and high-increasing (6.34% of males and 2.76% of females). The red trajectory represents the low-increasing group, characterized by a low initial BMI (16.39 kg/m2 in males and 16.30 kg/m2 in females) and a slight increase during follow-up. The blue trajectory represents the moderate-increasing group, characterized by an initial above-average BMI (17.95 kg/m2 in males and 19.40 kg/m2 in females) and a moderate increase thereafter. The green trajectory represents the high-increasing group, characterized by a high initial BMI (22.07 kg/m2 in males and 21.35 kg/m2 in females) and a moderate increase thereafter. The performance of the optimal model was good, with average posterior probabilities of patients classified in each group greater than 0.80 (Table S1).

Associations of body mass index trajectories with the risk of hypertension

Table 2 shows the results of the association between BMI trajectories and hypertension in males and females. The mean (SD) follow-up time was 11.20 (7.69) and 7.20 (5.21) years in males and females, respectively. During this time, 339 males and 212 females were identified with hypertension. All trend P values from lower to higher trajectory groups in males were less than 0.05. Compared with the low-increasing group in males after adjusting for all the covariates, the high-increasing group was associated with a higher risk of hypertension (HR = 1.76 [95% CI: 1.04–2.97]). A similar result was found in females albeit without reaching statistical significance; in comparing the high-increasing group with the low-increasing group, the HR (95% CI) of hypertension was 1.83 (0.94–3.57). After excluding cases of hypertension that occurred within the first 2 years of follow-up, the associations persisted, with adjusted HRs (95% CIs) of 1.98 (1.13–3.47) in males and 2.08 (1.01–4.27) in females, respectively (Table S2).

Discussion

This study investigated the sex-specific association between BMI trajectories and subsequent risk of hypertension. Three distinct BMI trajectory groups of low-, moderate-, and high-increasing were identified. Males in the high-increasing trajectory group demonstrated a higher risk of developing hypertension than those in the low-increasing trajectory group, with similar findings among females. Further stratified analysis revealed that this association was particularly pronounced among specific subgroups, including smokers, drinkers, and physically inactive individuals.

Previous research in the United Kingdom [27], Germany [9], Spain [28], Denmark [29], Finland [30], and several regions of China [8, 15, 31] demonstrated BMI trajectories during certain periods of the life course in different populations. These studies identified BMI trajectories by age and uncovered diverse patterns and group numbers (range: 3–6). Previous research utilizing different statistical methods, such as LCGMM or GBTM, to classify children based on their BMI growth patterns showed similar results [15, 28, 32]. The most representative multicenter cohort study is the International Childhood Cardiovascular Cohort (i3C) Consortium-including participants from Australia, Europe, and the United States- which delineated 5 distinct BMI trajectories among 9830 participants from childhood to middle adulthood (from ages 6 to 50) [32]. Notably, approximately half of the participants maintained a low BMI, and a substantial proportion exhibited a progression to moderate/high/very high BMI. Only a minority (< 5%) demonstrated an improvement from high BMI after about age 30. Our study’s follow-up time ended around this age, but trends before the age of 30 were similar to the results observed in the i3C study.

Three related studies have been conducted using the CHNS data; they focused solely on childhood BMI trajectories and did not continue to track them into adulthood. The outcome of the study by Teng et al. focused on hypertension in adulthood but failed to exclude individuals with hypertension at baseline, which could have led to reverse causality [13]. The limited sample size (n < 2,000) also prevented the researchers from conducting a sex-specific analysis. The latest study examined BMI trends separately for boys and girls, revealing 4 trajectory groups with similar growth rates [31]. The primary outcome was childhood hypertension, which was diagnosed by focusing on 3 separate occasions, with an incidence rate of 3.34% [31]. However, we were unable to diagnose hypertension requiring the presence of BP values on at least 3 separate occasions and instead used a single time point of value as a surrogate measure. Our findings revealed that 21.48% of males and 15.80% of females developed newly hypertension, which aligns closely with the findings reported by Fan et al. [14] and Wang et al. [15]. Furthermore, the peak age for the development of hypertension was identified as 14 years old in both males and females, aligning with the findings of a global meta-analysis [33]. Given these findings, it is essential to conduct timely screening and intervention during this critical period.

Our study found that participants who had high baseline BMIs with continued growth were more likely to develop hypertension in the future than those who had low/moderate baseline BMIs with continued growth. This result is consistent with findings reported in previous research conducted in Iran [9], Spain [28] and China [8, 15, 31]. In general, these studies included a range of variables and factors, but few of them collected data on the distribution of CVD risk factors in childhood, particularly smoking and drinking. In our study, we adjusted for smoking status, alcohol drinking status, and physical activity at the end of follow-up as potential confounders, and found that the association between a higher BMI trajectory and hypertension remained significant after adjusting for these lifestyle factors. Given the low prevalence of smoking and drinking among females, we conducted stratified analyses for smoking and drinking only in males. We found that the association was significantly stronger in smokers and drinkers than in nonsmokers and nondrinkers. Although the results of the interaction analysis were not significant, this may be due to our limited sample size. Our study suggests that unhealthy lifestyles can aggravate the association between BMI and the incidence of hypertension, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a healthy lifestyle.

Fan et al. identified a sharp BMI growth trajectory, with BMI increasing rapidly from a low level to the obesity level, which was accompanied by a markedly higher future incidence of hypertension as compared with the situation in those with stable BMIs (HR = 12.33) [8]. This suggests that a higher rate of change in BMI is important for the development of hypertension. However, our study did not capture the impact of rate, and the 3 curves we depicted exhibited similar slopes. In our study, high BMI levels in childhood were found to be a risk factor for hypertension. It is important to note that the age range of participants in the study by Fan et al. was 20–40 years, whereas our study began in childhood, which may account for the difference. Previous studies have identified potential mechanisms linking obesity to hypertension, including sympathetic nervous system overactivity, proinflammatory adipokines secreted in visceral adipose tissues, insulin resistance, increased activity of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and the gut microbiota [34]. An understanding of these mechanisms is crucial for developing effective strategies to prevent and manage hypertension in individuals with obesity.

Our study has several strengths. First, we present sex-specific results, which are crucial in understanding the effects of BMI trajectories on BP risk among males and females. Second, we adjusted for lifestyle factors-such as smoking, drinking, and physical activity-which are known to influence BP levels. These adjustments provide a more accurate picture of the association between BMI trajectory and the development of hypertension. However, our study also has several limitations. First, the relatively short follow-up time may have limited our ability to capture long-term changes in BMI and BP, especially in females. Although the proportion of high-increasing group in females was small (2.76%), the result still indicated that this group was more likely to develop hypertension than the low-increasing trajectory group. To more accurately assess the impact of BMI trajectories on the development of hypertension, longer-term studies are necessary. Second, although we adjusted for several lifestyle factors, other potential confounders, such as dietary habits and genetic factors, may have influenced our results. Additionally, we adjusted for physical activity; however, owing to data constraints (primarily in childhood), we categorized participants as either physically active or not. Future studies can perhaps further analyze physical activity by categorizing it into low, moderate, and high-intensity types based on metabolic equivalents. Furthermore, it should be noted that family history of hypertension is a recognized risk factor for hypertension. However, the CHNS dataset did not include this data. Future studies should consider incorporating this variable to further refine the result. Overall, a more comprehensive set of confounders should be considered to enhance the reliability of the findings.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that there is a positive association between a higher BMI trajectory and the development of hypertension in both sexes. We also identified high-risk groups with unhealthy habits. It is recommended that further studies should explore the complex relationship between BMI, lifestyle, and BP. Given the importance of preventing hypertension, the regular monitoring and management of BMI and development of healthy lifestyle from childhood is crucial.
