Evaluation of plasma kynurenine 3-monooxygenase and Ki-67 as screening biomarkers for neoplasia in dogs
Authors: Ka-Mei Sio, Chiao-Hsu Ke, Chen-Si Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of tumors might prolong the survival of dogs. Kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO) and Ki-67 are tumor biomarkers, but few studies have described the clinical value of KMO and Ki-67 in veterinary medicine.Hypothesis/ObjectivesThis study aims to compare the activity of KMO and the concentration of Ki-67 between healthy dogs and dogs with neoplasms.AnimalsA total of 162 dogs with different types of tumors were included. Forty-six tumor-free individuals were included as the control group.Methods This is a case–control study. Dogs with neoplasms, confirmed through medical record review, were recruited. Introduction

Cancer is a major cause of illness and death in dogs, with early and accurate diagnosis remaining challenging in veterinary medicine.1,2 Traditional methods, such as histopathology and imaging, are invasive, expensive, and time-consuming, restricting their use for routine screening. In human medicine, blood biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein,3 carcinoembryonic antigen,4 prostate-specific antigen,5,6 and cancer antigens (CA-125, CA19-9) are widely used for cancer detection and monitoring.7,8 In veterinary medicine, research on blood biomarkers is increasing, and biomarkers such as serum amyloid A,9,10 lactate dehydrogenase (LDH),11 and Ki-67 show promise for tumor detection and prognosis.12,13 However, single markers often lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity. Combining multiple biomarkers might improve diagnostic accuracy.14,15 Identifying reliable biomarkers that simultaneously reflect diverse aspects of tumor biology could enhance the early detection and management of cancer in dogs. These strategies have been developed in human oncology and also offer new tools for veterinary medicine.2

Numerous tumor biomarkers are reported. One such biomarker is kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO), an enzyme which is involved in the kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism and plays a critical role in modulating immune responses and tumor progression.16,17 In human oncology, elevated KMO expression is associated with tumor growth, immune evasion, and poor prognosis.17,18 Although research on KMO in veterinary oncology is still limited, KMO expression is significantly higher in mammary gland tumors and melanoma in dogs.19 These results suggested that KMO has potential to be a biomarker reflecting tumor aggressiveness and progression. Ki-67, another tumor biomarker, is extensively used in both human and veterinary oncology to assess tumor aggressiveness and predict clinical outcomes.20,21 Ki-67 is a nuclear protein expressed during all active phases of the cell cycle and is a marker of cellular proliferation.22–24 In dogs, elevated Ki-67 is documented in various malignant tumors, including mammary gland tumors, lymphoma, and perianal gland tumors.12,25 Higher Ki-67 concentration generally correlates with increased tumor malignancy and poorer prognosis.26 However, Ki-67 alone might not reliably distinguish between malignant and benign lesions in all cases, as some benign or inflammatory conditions also exhibit increased cellular proliferation.

Studies report the single diagnostic values of KMO and Ki-67.12,19 However, implementing multiple marker strategies has the potential to improve tumor detection and assist in monitoring therapeutic responses, thereby ultimately enhancing the management and outcomes of dogs with cancer.27,28 Converging evidence indicates that KMO activity is associated with tumor progression, while Ki-67 reflects the proliferative status.

We hypothesized that combining KMO and Ki-67 as dual biomarkers could improve the early detection of cancer in dogs. Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to evaluate whether the clinical application of measuring both KMO and Ki-67 could provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of tumors in dogs.

Materials and methods

Case recruitment and sample processing

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan (protocol code: IACUC No. NTU110-EL-00096). All blood samples used in this study were primarily collected from the National Taiwan University Veterinary Hospital (NTUVH). The study included a total of 208 dogs divided into two groups: a control group of 46 clinically healthy dogs with a median age of 5.78 years (19 females and 27 males; 35 purebred and 11 mixed breed), and a neoplastic group of 162 tumor-bearing dogs with a median age of 9.69 years (85 females and 77 males; 95 purebred and 61 mixed breed). Blood samples were obtained via venipuncture, after institutional ethical guidelines and owner consent, with clinical data, including age, sex, and breed, recorded for each subject. Samples were processed and stored under standardized conditions for subsequent biomarker analysis.

Determination of KMO and Ki-67

The activity of KMO and concentration of Ki-67 in plasma were determined using commercially available double-antibody sandwich ELISA kits (KMO: MBS8803176; Ki-67: MBS089640) from MyBioSource (Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada).12,29 Samples and standards were tested in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

A combination of parametric and non-parametric tests was employed to evaluate continuous and categorical variables based on data distribution (Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Fisher’s exact test). Multiple linear regression was utilized to account for potential confounders such as age, while the diagnostic efficacy of the biomarkers was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and DeLong’s test. All analyses and visualizations were conducted using R software.

Detailed inclusion criteria, protocols for sample processing, ELISA specifications, and extended statistical methodologies are provided in the Appendix I.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 162 dogs with tumors diagnosed by histopathology or cytology were enrolled in the study. The most common tumor types included lymphoma (n = 60), mast cell tumor (n = 31), melanoma (skin and oral, n = 14), and mammary gland tumor (n = 6). Other less frequent tumor types comprised hepatocellular carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, chondrosarcoma, histiocytic sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, leukemia, lipoma, meningioma, osteosarcoma, perianal gland tumor, plasmacytoma, sarcoma, seminoma, squamous cell carcinoma, thyroid gland tumor, transitional cell carcinoma, and trichoblastoma. The study sample included various breeds, with mixed breeds being the most prevalent (n = 61), followed by Schnauzers (n = 17), Miniature Poodles (n = 14), Golden Retrievers (n = 12), Dachshunds and Huskies (both n = 7), Shiba Inu (n = 6), French Bulldogs (n = 5), Beagles and Border Collies (both n = 4), Maltese (n = 4), and several other breeds represented by fewer than 5 individuals each. Breed information was unavailable for 6 dogs (Table S1).

A total of 46 healthy control dogs were included in the study. The most common breeds were Miniature Poodles (n = 7), Mixed breeds (n = 11), Beagles (n = 5), Mountain Curs (n = 5), and Miniature Schnauzers (n = 3), with other breeds such as Border Collies, French Bulldogs, Golden Retrievers, Irish Setters, Maltese, Pembroke Welsh Corgis, Shiba Inu, and Shih Tzu also represented. The ages of the control dogs ranged from 1.5 to 11 years. The sex distribution included intact males, castrated males, intact females, and spayed females, reflecting a balanced representation of the sexes (Table S2).

The mean age of dogs in the neoplastic group (9.69 years; range, 1-17 years) was significantly higher than that in the control group (5.78 years; range, 1.5-11 years) (P < .0001). Regarding sex distribution, there was no statistically significant difference between the control and tumor-bearing groups (P = .242). The groups were comprised of females at 41% (19/46) of controls and 52.5% (85/162) of tumor-bearing groups, and males at 59% (27/46) and 47.5% (77/162), respectively. Breed composition also did not differ significantly between groups (P = .079), although a higher proportion of mixed-breed dogs was observed in the tumor-bearing group (39.1%; 61/156) compared to the control group (24%; 11/46). Purebred dogs accounted for 76% (35/46) of controls and 60.9% (95/156) of the tumor-bearing group (Table 1).

Increased KMO and Ki67 concentrations in dogs with neoplasms

To validate whether KMO and Ki67 can be ideal tumor biomarkers, we compared these two variables from dogs with neoplasms and controls. As shown in Figure 1, the median KMO concentration in the control group was 0.69 (0.23-1.18) ng/mL, whereas in dogs with neoplasms, it was markedly higher at 2.02 (1.03-3.65) ng/mL (P < .0001). Similarly, the median concentration of Ki-67 in controls was 3.80 (3.31-4.43) ng/mL, and 4.28 (3.75-4.84) ng/mL in dogs with neoplasms (P < .01). Detailed descriptive statistics are summarized in Table S3. The detailed results of the multiple linear regression models are summarized in Table 2, confirming that age is not a significant confounding variable in our study. These findings demonstrate that both KMO and Ki67 are significantly higher in dogs with tumors, supporting their potential as blood-based biomarkers for the detection of cancer in dogs.

Determination of cut-off values for KMO and Ki-67 in the diagnosis of dogs with tumors

Additional diagnostic test variables were calculated for KMO. The diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of KMO varied depending on the selected cut-off values. With cut-offs ranging from 0.9309 to 1.023 ng/mL, sensitivity ranged from 75.3% to 78.4%, while specificity ranged from 60.9% to 67.4% (Table 3). Based on the maximum Youden’s index, an optimal cut-off value of approximately 0.9676 ng/mL was selected, yielding a sensitivity of 77.8% (95% CI, 70.6-83.9) and specificity of 67.4% (95% CI, 52.0-80.5). These results suggest that plasma KMO has moderate diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing dogs with tumors from healthy controls. Similarly, diagnostic performance variables were analyzed for Ki-67 at various cut-offs between 4.038 and 4.136 μg/mL. Sensitivity ranged from 57.4% to 61.7%, and specificity ranged from 56.5% to 60.9% (Table 4). The optimal cut-off identified was approximately 4.097 μg/mL, with a sensitivity of 60.5% (95% CI, 52.5-68.1) and specificity of 60.9% (95% CI, 45.4-74.9). These findings indicate that Ki-67 alone has limited sensitivity and specificity for tumor diagnosis in dogs when measured in plasma.

Low correlation between KMO and Ki-67 concentrations

The aforementioned findings suggested higher KMO and Ki67 concentrations in individuals with neoplasms. We hypothesized that these two tumor-related markers would exhibit a high correlation because they have been separately reported as tumor biomarkers. There was no significant correlation between plasma KMO and Ki67 concentrations in dogs with neoplasms (Figure 2, R2 = 0.003, P > .05). High KMO concentration in an individual does not necessarily imply a corresponding higher Ki67 concentration. This result suggests that these 2 markers might possess independent diagnostic values.

Linear regression analysis showing no relationship between KMO and Ki67 concentrations for dogs with neoplasms (R2 = 0.0031, P = .48).

Combination determination of KMO and Ki67 potentiating diagnostic power

The diagnostic performance of KMO and Ki-67 in differentiating dogs with tumors from healthy controls was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for KMO alone was 0.837, indicating good diagnostic accuracy, while Ki-67 alone showed a lower AUC of 0.644, suggesting limited discriminative ability. Although the combined KMO and Ki-67 model yielded the highest AUC of 0.863, DeLong's test revealed no significant difference when compared to the KMO-alone model (AUC = 0.837; P = .117).

The agreement between plasma KMO and Ki-67 levels for tumor diagnosis was assessed using Kappa statistics. At the selected cut-off values (KMO ≥ 0.9676 ng/mL and Ki-67 ≥ 4.097 μg/mL), the overall agreement between the 2 biomarkers was low, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.146 (P = .0534). Among the dogs, 43.8% (n = 71) tested positive for both KMO and Ki-67, while 5.5% (n = 9) were negative for both markers. However, 16.7% (n = 27) were positive for KMO but negative for Ki-67, and 34.0% (n = 55) were positive for Ki-67 but negative for KMO. These findings indicate a slight agreement between KMO and Ki-67, suggesting that the 2 biomarkers might reflect different and complementary biological aspects of tumor presence and progression in dogs (Table 5).

Discussion

This study evaluated plasma KMO and Ki-67 as non-invasive, preliminary screening biomarkers for the early detection of cancer in dogs. We found significantly increased concentrations of both KMO and Ki-67 in dogs with neoplasms compared to healthy controls (Figure 1), suggesting that tumorigenesis drives both metabolic dysregulation (via KMO) and systemic cellular proliferation (via Ki-67). Notably, KMO emerged as a robust standalone screening biomarker, demonstrating an AUC of 0.837 (Figure 3). Conversely, while Ki-67 levels were elevated in the neoplastic cohort, their standalone diagnostic utility was limited (AUC = 0.644). Furthermore, although combining both markers yielded the highest AUC of 0.863, statistical comparison (DeLong’s test) revealed that this combination did not significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy compared to using KMO alone. Therefore, we conclude that KMO provides robust standalone utility for the preliminary detection of cancers in dogs, whereas Ki-67 serves primarily as an exploratory adjunctive marker reflecting underlying systemic tumor biology rather than offering synergistic diagnostic value.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves comparing the diagnostic performance of KMO and Ki-67. The curves demonstrate the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for differentiating dogs with tumors from healthy controls. The evaluated models include the combination of KMO and Ki-67 (AUC = 0.863), KMO alone (AUC = 0.837), and Ki-67 alone (AUC = 0.644). Pairwise comparisons using DeLong's test revealed that while KMO alone significantly outperformed Ki-67 alone (P < .001), the addition of Ki-67 to KMO in the combined model did not yield a statistically significant improvement in diagnostic performance compared to KMO alone (P = .117).

Elevated activities of KMO in tumor-bearing dogs likely reflect its role in the kynurenine pathway, which is frequently hijacked by tumors to promote immune evasion and metabolic adaptation.19 In human cancers, KMO overexpression correlates with a poor prognosis and resistance to therapy, as it facilitates the production of immunosuppressive metabolites, such as quinolinic acid.18 The current study extends these findings to veterinary medicine, showing that KMO’s systemic release into circulation makes it a practical biomarker for non-invasive detection. Notably, Ki-67, a well-studied tumor biomarker, exhibited limited diagnostic utility as a standalone plasma biomarker (AUC = 0.644). We speculated that this discrepancy might result from its nuclear localization, which restricts its release into circulation in the absence of marked tumor cell death.23 While Ki-67 remains a valuable prognostic tool in histopathology, its low sensitivity and specificity restrict its translation utility in veterinary medicine. Similar limitations are reported in human studies, where circulating Ki-67 levels often fail to correlate with tumor burden.23,25

In human medicine, KMO and Ki-67 are well-known tumor biomarkers. Therefore, we anticipated that these two tumor-related markers would exhibit a high correlation. However, a low linear regression was found between these two markers (Figure 2). We hypothesized that these findings might result from their independent aspects of tumor biology. KMO reflects metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation, whereas Ki-67 indicates proliferative activities. Given these distinct pathways, we hypothesized that combining KMO and Ki-67 might synergistically enhance diagnostic ability. However, as demonstrated in our ROC and DeLong’s test analyses, while the combined model yielded a marginally higher AUC (0.863), the addition of Ki-67 did not provide a statistically significant improvement over KMO alone (AUC = 0.837) for initial detection. KMO independently demonstrated robust sensitivity and specificity across various cut-off values. In human medicine, integrating metabolic and proliferative markers, such as LDH and circulating tumor DNA, has facilitated the detection of malignancies, including colorectal and breast cancers.25,30,31 Similarly, in veterinary applications, while the dual-marker approach might not mitigate false negatives or boost primary diagnostic accuracy as initially expected, it still provides a broader biological context. Tumors with prominent metabolic dysregulation detectable via KMO might exhibit highly variable proliferation rates due to tumor heterogeneity. Therefore, KMO should be utilized as the primary standalone screening tool, while Ki-67 could be reserved as an adjunctive marker to further characterize the proliferative behavior of the detected neoplasia.

This study highlights plasma KMO as a highly potent standalone biomarker for veterinary clinical screening, while Ki-67 offers complementary insights into tumor biology. The primary advantage of utilizing KMO lies in its robust diagnostic performance, which facilitates the preliminary identification of tumor-bearing dogs at an early stage while minimizing the misclassification of healthy individuals. For clinical practice, KMO serves as a reliable, non-invasive screening tool that supports earlier intervention and improved clinical management for dogs with cancer. The clinical relevance of evaluating these biomarkers lies in their practicality. Both KMO and Ki-67 can be quantified using widely available ELISA kits requiring only small blood volumes, making them highly feasible for routine use. For example, a dog presenting with non-specific signs, such as weight loss or lethargy, could undergo rapid blood testing, enabling earlier referral for advanced imaging or biopsy if markers are elevated. Furthermore, for long-term medical follow-up, simultaneously monitoring KMO and Ki-67 levels during treatment could provide multifaceted insights into therapeutic efficacy.

The current study had several limitations. First, the retrospective study design and limited sample size restrict the generalizability of the findings. It would be prudent to validate our findings using a large-scale prospective study. Second, the current study excluded dogs with non-neoplastic diseases, such as inflammatory conditions, which could have overestimated the specificity of KMO and Ki-67, as these markers could also be elevated in non-cancerous diseases. Last, while the inclusion of a broad spectrum of tumor types demonstrates the potential versatility of KMO and Ki-67, it could also obscure important differences in biomarker expression among specific neoplasms. For example, melanomas and mammary gland tumors might differently regulate KMO, Ki-67, or both, thereby potentially affecting diagnostic performance. Subgroup analyses by tumor type could help refine cut-off values and enhance diagnostic precision. Despite this retrospective study restricting our further explorations, we highlight the value of dual detection of KMO and Ki-67 and propose possible suggestions for future studies.

In conclusion, this study utilizes the combined blood-based detection of KMO and Ki-67 in veterinary medicine and positions KMO as a potentially robust standalone biomarker for the non-invasive preliminary screening of cancer in dogs. While integrating metabolic (KMO) and proliferative (Ki-67) signals did not statistically surpass the primary diagnostic accuracy of KMO alone, this approach aligns with advancements in human liquid biopsy by providing a broader biological context. The primary value of Ki-67 in this context lies not in standalone initial diagnosis, but rather in its potential to reflect underlying systemic tumor biology and complement the high sensitivity of KMO. While further large-scale validation focusing on specific tumor subtypes is warranted, these findings underscore the potential of blood-based biomarkers to advance veterinary oncology. In response to the clinical need for effective liquid biopsies, this study provides a promising, minimally invasive screening strategy to facilitate the early detection and management of canine cancers.

Appendix

The detailed inclusion criteria and the protocols for blood sampling

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows. Dogs that had received non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or steroids within 14 days before blood collection were excluded. Food was withheld from all dogs for at least 8 hours before blood sampling. A minimum of 0.5 mL of whole blood was collected into an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube (BD Vacutainer, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and centrifuged at 450 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C within 60 minutes of collection. The supernatant was then collected and centrifuged again at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The plasma samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Detailed detection methods, detection limits, and intra-assay coefficients of variation for KMO and Ki-67

For KMO detection, plasma samples diluted 1:20 were added to microtiter plates coated with biotinylated antibodies, incubated at 37°C for 50 minutes, washed, and then reacted with tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate for 20 minutes. A microplate reader (SpectraMax® M5 Microplate Reader, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to determine the absorbance at 450 nm. For Ki-67 detection, undiluted plasma samples were added to microtiter plates coated with biotinylated antibodies, incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate at 37°C for 60 minutes, washed, and then incubated with TMB for 15 minutes before measurement. The optical density was finally measured at 450 nm. The concentrations were calculated based on the standard curves, and the detection ranges of KMO and Ki-67 were 0.79-50 ng/mL and 0.625-20 ng/mL, respectively. For KMO ELISA, the limit of detection (LOD) was 0.3 ng/mL. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was < 8%, and the inter-assay CV was < 10%. For Ki-67 ELISA, the LOD was 0.1 ng/mL. The intra-assay CV was < 15%, and the inter-assay CV was < 15%. This method can sensitively and specifically quantify the two biomarkers in canine plasma for the diagnostic evaluation of tumors.

Detailed statistical analysis

Before statistical analysis, all continuous variables were rigorously checked for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data (such as age), we applied the Student’s t-test and presented the results as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Conversely, for non-normally distributed data, specifically the concentrations of KMO and Ki-67, we utilized the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test and reported the values as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentiles) to more accurately reflect the central tendency of skewed data. Categorical variables, including reproductive status and breeds, were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test.

To address potential confounding factors, multiple linear regression models were performed to evaluate the independent effect of disease status on biomarker levels while adjusting for age. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the diagnostic performance of the biomarkers, and DeLong’s test was utilized to statistically compare the differences in the area under the curves (AUCs). All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A P-value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.
