Inbred strains of Xenopus tropicalis show morphological and genetic variation
Authors: Takeshi Igawa, Makoto Suzuki, Momoka Shimamoto, Nanoka Suzuki, Akihiko Kashiwagi, Keiko Kashiwagi, Hajime Ogino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of individuality and morphological divergence within a population is crucial for evolutionary potential. This study investigated genetic and phenotypic variation among four inbred strains of Xenopus tropicalis, a promising model amphibian species. We examined four inbred lineages derived from source populations in Nigeria and Côte d’Ivoire, focusing specifically on phenotypic diversity in the absence of environmental influences. Through comprehensive morphological assessments—including body size measurements, mating calls, and coloration—we identified significant differences between sexes and among strains, with pronounced variations in body size, coloration, and claw morphology. In particular, acoustic traits differed between Nigerian-derived strains and the Côte d’Ivoire-derived strain. These findings indicate that inbred strains of X. tropicalis retain measurable phenotypic divergence under controlled rearing conditions. This study underscores the rich genetic and morphological variation retained among inbred strains, highlighting their value for studying phenotypic divergence and evolutionary potential. This strain panel therefore provides a useful resource for future studies of the genetic and developmental mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation in amphibians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56343-6.Keywords: Xenopus tropicalis, Inbred strain, Microsatellite, Residual heterozygosity, Sexual size dimorphism, Geometric morphometrics, Body coloration, Advertisement call, Phenotypic divergence, Strain differentiationSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Zoology

Introduction

The biological species exist as populations rather than singular entities. In most cases except clonal populations or parthenogenetic individuals, individuals in a population possess phenotypic variation, which is brought about by genetic variation and environmental factors. These variation within a population underpins the evolutionary potential that allows the population to adapt to environmental changes or new environments when the population expand their range1,2. Of these, morphological variation is the most prominent and common factor for the adaptation3–5. In recent genomic approaches, it is important to know how and what the genomic variation contribute to morphological variation. Nevertheless, given that morphological variation is significantly affected by environmental influences throughout the developmental trajectory6, it becomes challenging to discern the factors that are exclusively attributable to genetic variation. This intricacy highlights the necessity of comprehending the interplay between environmental determinants and genetic predispositions in generating phenotypic diversity that is observed. In addition, interactions of those factors may pose considerable difficulties in differentiating the genetic and environmental contributions to morphological characteristics7,8. Therefore, it is almost impossible to elucidate phenotypic variation that reflects the individuality of genomes within wild population. Especially diploid individuals in a population under random mating also exhibit intermediate traits between two genomes, making it challenging to observe the extent of phenotypic differences produced by the genome. On the other hand, in inbred model organisms, although the genetic and environmental background is clear and fixed, it is generally impossible to reproduce the diversity in natural populations. This limitation highlights the challenge of studying evolutionary processes in controlled settings, where the complexity of natural selection and genetic variation is often oversimplified or overlooked.

One of the approaches to overcome these difficulties should be comprehensive observation of emerging model organisms with multiple strains created from a single founder population. The genetic diversity of natural populations and the individuality of their genomes can be manifested through stabilizing multiple strains through inbreeding. Xenopus tropicalis, which has been introduced since the 1990s, and the strains with several lineages were made by researchers8–10. Unlike its larger relative X. laevis, X. tropicalis is diploid rather than tetraploid, making genetic analysis and manipulation significantly more straightforward11,12. The species exhibits rapid development with a relatively short generation time, and its embryos are large and easily accessible for experimental manipulation with well documented developmental phenomena13. X. tropicalis has become increasingly valuable for genetic modification studies. CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing achieves high efficiency (> 90% mutation rates), enabling rapid phenotyping and heritable targeted modifications14,15. The species benefits from comprehensive genomic resources, including complete genome sequences, extensive transcriptomic datasets across developmental stages, and integrated databases like Xenbase16–18.

Inbreeding of the four strains has been conducted, stabilizing their genetic background as inbred strains, which makes it possible to observe the differences in traits10. Three of the four strains were derived from Nigerian source populations, whereas the IC strain was derived from Côte d’Ivoire. Therefore, this strain panel allows two levels of comparison: phenotypic divergence among Nigerian-derived strains and broader differentiation between Nigerian- and Côte d’Ivoire-derived strains. Together with the experimental advantages of X. tropicalis, these strains provide a useful system for describing phenotypic divergence under controlled laboratory conditions.

In this study, we examined genetic and phenotypic variation among four inbred strains of X. tropicalis. We measured body size and shape, claw morphology, body coloration, and male advertisement calls, and compared these traits among sexes and strains. Because three strains were derived from Nigerian populations and one from Côte d’Ivoire, we distinguished variation among Nigerian-derived strains from broader differences between Nigerian- and Côte d’Ivoire-derived strains. Our aim was to describe strain-level phenotypic divergence under controlled laboratory conditions and to provide a basis for future studies of the genetic and developmental mechanisms underlying these traits.

We measured the sizes of each body part, investigated the mating calls, and measured the body color in detail for over 100 males and females, finding significant differences between the lineages. The most obvious difference between lineages was sizes of body parts represented by body size. Furthermore, mating calls, a reproductive trait, evolved rapidly between lineages from different origins. The degree of claw formation was significantly different even among homoeologous populations. These results show that measurable phenotypic differences can be maintained among inbred strains and highlight the value of these strains for future genetic and developmental analyses.

Geometric morphometrics

In the geometric morphometrics (GMM) data, PCA for all individuals also showed obvious clusters of males and females (Fig. 6B) and graphical representation of PC1 showed a more rounded cranial shape in females and a narrower cranial shape and thicker thigh in males (Fig. 6A). Because PCA for female and male datasets did not show strain-specific clusters, we thus conducted CVA for female and male datasets. The CVA plot of each sex showed clusters of each strain in both datasets, and the male was clearer than the female (Fig. S1). Graphical representation of reciprocal comparison of CV1 also showed clear differences among strains in the male dataset (Fig. 7). Interestingly, in comparison between NAH and NBH, CV1 showed opposite shape trend in females and males of these strains: NBH showed more elongated cranial shape and narrower abdomen than NAH in females, and those were opposite in males.

Differences in body color

The body color of X. tropicalis is basically dark on the dorsal side and bright on the ventral side. Our comparison of color variables showed that there are significant differences between the strains (Fig. 8). In L*, NBH and NH were higher values (brighter) than NAH and IC in both dorsal and ventral sides. In a*, differences on absolute values were less, but NAH and IC were higher values (more reddish) than NBH and NH in both dorsal and ventral sides. In b*, NH and NBH were higher values (more yellowish) than NAH and IC in dorsal side, but this was opposite in ventral side. In summary, our measurements showed that the body color was divided into a brighter, yellowish group consisting of NBH and NH, and a darker group consisting of NAH and IC (Fig. 8). However, each lineage appears to have its own unique body color, as statistically significant differences were observed in most comparison combinations. No significant differences between males and females were observed within each strain.

Acoustic analysis of male advertisement call

Our acoustic analysis of male advertisement call showed clear difference between IC and the other Nigerian strains (NAH, NH, NBH) in pulse intervals which constitute a call (interpulse intervals: IPIs), showing significantly longer IPIs in IC (mean IPI: NAH = 0.033 s, NH = 0.030 s, NBH = 0.031 s, IC = 0.051 s, Fig. 9A-C). Within Nigerian strains, distribution of pulse frequency of NAH was shifted lower. The mode of the pulse frequency of NAH was approx.0.5 kHz, while the others were approx. 0.75 kHz (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

The consistent heterozygosity reduction observed across all strains (Fig. 2) is consistent with the expected effect of continued brother-sister mating. The high relatedness values (~ 0.95, Table 2) indicate that the sampled individuals within most strains are genetically highly similar. However, the persistent deviation from theoretical predictions suggests that the actual breeding histories, initial founder heterozygosity, and marker-specific patterns may differ from the assumptions of the simple inbreeding model. The contrasting fixation index values among strains also suggest that residual heterozygosity may not be distributed uniformly across loci. However, because our genetic analysis was based on a limited set of SSLP markers and individuals from single families, these results should be interpreted as marker-based estimates of residual heterozygosity rather than direct measures of genome-wide diversity. Moreover, interpretations based on presumed neutral markers are model-dependent. Thus, the present results should not be taken as direct evidence for neutrality, selection, purging, or any specific evolutionary mechanism. Future whole-genome sequencing could identify specific genomic regions maintaining heterozygosity and test whether they harbor genes related to viability or reproduction.

Our morphological analysis revealed distinct sexual size dimorphism (SSD) in X. tropicalis. Similar to the traditional introduction of X. tropicalis19, our results showed that females were 1.25 times larger than males in terms of body size (SVL) overall (Fig. 3B). The female/male SVL ratio was slightly different in each strain, especially between Nigerian and Ivory Coast strains (NAH: 1.22, NH: 1.27, NBH: 1.27, IC: 1.14). In contrast, males had longer fingers and toes compared to SVL (Fig. 3C). Particularly, the male SVL ratio of 4FL was 1.25 times longer than that of females overall and slightly different between strains (NAH: 1.19, NH: 1.22, NBH: 1.37, IC: 1.20). The body shapes were also different, with males having rectangular shapes and females having rounded shapes. Such SSD has been widely accepted as evolutionary outcome due to the different reproductive roles of females and males20. SSD is commonly considered to be caused by sexual selection and fecundity selection21–23.

Beyond sexual dimorphism, clear phenotypic differences were observed among strains. The most notable differences between lineages are observed among the three Nigerian lineages (NAH, NH, NBH) and IC. These differences are likely influenced by differences in genetic background and founder populations from Nigeria and Adiopodoume, Ivory Coast. IC is smaller in body size and has longer finger lengths relative to SVL in females compared to the Nigerian lineages. A more significant difference between IC and NAH is also observed in the mating calls of males, where the pulse interval of the advertisement calls in IC is longer than that in the Nigerian lineages. Reproductive traits, including mating calls, are known to evolve rapidly among geographically distant populations24, and this is also evident in the phenotypic differences between NAH and IC. Although there is no post-mating isolation between the two (inter-strain hybrids have constantly been produced and provided in NBRP), whether these acoustic differences affect mate recognition or reproductive behavior remains to be tested experimentally. Recent studies on advertisement calls suggest that the pulse rate was controlled by a nucleus, DTAM in hindbrain which also linked to respiration25. Environmental adaptation may have contributed to acoustic divergence, but this also requires further testing.

Furthermore, it is interesting to note morphological differences among the three Nigerian lineages, which originate from the same founding population. Similar to sexual dimorphism, morphological differences between lineages could be explained by SVL. As in many vertebrates, including human, body size (SVL, total length, mass, height) is a trait likely influenced by multiple genetic and developmental factors26–28. The pronounced SVL differences among Nigerian strains suggest that body size is a useful phenotype for future genetic and developmental studies in X. tropicalis.

Further, the SVL differences are particularly pronounced in females with the size order being NH > NAH > NBH. NAH also has longer finger and toe lengths relative to SVL compared to the other lineages (Fig. 4D) and exhibits the darkest body coloration (Fig. 8). In contrast, NBH is the smallest in body size and has the lightest body coloration among the lineages. Additionally, NBH shows weak claw formation, with a tendency for claws to fall off. These traits indicate that NBH may be useful for future studies of strain-specific variation in epidermal and limb-associated structures. Because claws in Xenopus express hair keratins under the control of Hoxc13-related pathways29, genes involved in keratinized appendage development may represent candidates for future investigation. It is also interesting that the loss of claws is more in females, indicating sexual dimorphism; males likely exhibit more developed claws than females. Males also have keratinized nuptial pads on the ventral side of forelimbs that function during amplexus for mating30, providing another example of male-biased development of keratinized epidermal structures.

In summary, the four inbred strains of X. tropicalis showed clear differences in body size, body shape, coloration, claw formation, and male advertisement calls. Comparisons among the Nigerian strains revealed phenotypic divergence even among closely related laboratory lineages, whereas comparisons with the IC strain highlighted broader differences associated with distinct geographic origins. Because the present study was based on phenotypic measurements and a limited set of SSLP markers, it does not identify the causal genetic variants underlying these traits. Nevertheless, these strains provide a useful resource for future genomic, developmental, and behavioral studies of phenotypic divergence in amphibians.

Materials and methods

Strains used in this study

We used four inbred strains which have been maintained in Amphibian Research Center, Hiroshima University: Nigerian A (Xtr.NigerianAhuarc, RRID: HUARC_1001, NAH), Nigerian H (Xtr.NigerianHHuarc, RRID: HUARC_1002, NH), Nigerian BH (Xtr.NigerianBHHuarc, RRID: HUARC_1003, NBH, formerly called as Golden) and Ivory Coast (Xtr.IvoryCoastHuarc, RRID: HUARC_1004, IC). There are genetic divergences among these colonies, reflecting differences in the source populations located in Nigeria and Côte d’Ivoire, as well as additional diversification through successive transfers between laboratories10. The colonies and number of individuals used for each experiment were summarized in Table 1.

All experimental protocols involving live animals were approved by the Hiroshima University Animal Research Committee (Approval No. G17-9). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of Hiroshima University and with applicable institutional animal care and use guidelines.

Genotyping and inference of kinship

Forty individuals consisting of ten offspring from each single mating of four strains were used for DNA extraction and genotyping DNA (Table 1). DNA was extracted using DNA suisui-F (Rizo Tsukuba, Japan) following the standard protocol described in the instructions. We genotyped 60 SSLP markers previously used for genetic characterization of X. tropicalis strains10. Marker names, primer sequences, genomic positions, and expected product sizes are provided in Supplementary Table S3. PCR amplification and allele scoring were performed following Igawa et al.10. For each marker, alleles were scored based on fragment size, and ambiguous genotypes were excluded from downstream analyses. Observed heterozygosity was calculated as the proportion of heterozygous individuals at each locus and averaged across loci. Expected heterozygosity was calculated from allele frequencies. Fixation indices were calculated as F = 1 − Ho/He. Pairwise relatedness was estimated using Coancestry version 1.0.1.231.

Morphological measurement

For morphological analyses, we used 156 males and females from two colonies of each of the four strains (approximately 20 individuals in each colony) (Table 1). We employed two different approaches of morphological analyses: distance measurement method (DMM)32 and landmarks-based geometric morphometrics method (GMM)33. In both methods, we conducted all measurements and identification of landmarks after taking digital images. For each individual, we took four digital images: the lateral and ventral sides of whole body and the ventral side of left forelimb and right toe. Those images were taken from right above their body after anesthesia using a digital camera (D810, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a telephoto macro lens (SP AF180mmF / 3.5 Di LD [IF] MACRO1:1, TAMRON, Saitama, Japan). Individuals were anesthetized by immersion in a 0.2% MS-222 solution (buffered to neutral pH) for five minutes, in accordance with the protocols approved by the Hiroshima University Animal Research Committee (Approval No. G17-9). All individuals recovered fully and appeared normal after the measurements.

In DMM, 20 morphometric characters (Fig. 1A): (1) snout-vent length (SVL), length between the tip of the snout to the cloaca; (2) mouth width (MW), length between the left and right corners of the mouth; (3) mouth-snout length (MSL), perpendicular distance from snout to the line connecting the left and right corners of the mouth; (4) snout length (SL), perpendicular distance from snout to the line connecting anterior tip of left and right eyes; (5) internarial distance (IN), length between left and right nostrils; (6) interorbital distance (IOD), distance between edge of left and right eyes; (7) eye-nostril distance (EN) distance between an eye edge and an nostril; (8) eye diameter (ED); (9) nostril-snout distance (NS), perpendicular distance from snout to the line connecting the left and right nostrils; (10) ankle width (AW), width of the constricted part of hind limb at the base of the toe; (11) first toe length (1TL); (12) second toe length (2TL); (13) third toe length (3TL); (14) fourth toe length (4TL); (15) fifth toe length (5TL); (16) wrist width (WW), width of the constricted part of forelimb at the base of the fingers; (17) first finger length (1FL); (18) second finger length (2FL); (19) third finger length (3FL); (20) fourth finger length (4FL). Measurement of these distances were conducted using Fiji34 after conversion to actual size using a ruler placed in every image. The presence or absence of claws was also visually confirmed and the number of toes with claw in right hind limb were counted in each strain.

In GMM, ten homologous landmarks on contour of the body were used (Fig. 1B): (1) snout tip; (2) contour point closest to right eye; (3) upper base of right arm; (4) lower base of right arm; (5) upper base of right leg; (6) vent; (7) upper base of left leg; (8) lower base of left arm; (9) upper base of left arm; (10) contour point closest to left eye. Identification of homologous landmarks of each individual was conducted using tpsDig235.

Statistical analyses

In DMM, we prepared three datasets including all individuals, females, and males and conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) for DMM data (raw measurement data in milli meter) using the ‘prcomp’ function in R with scaling and centering option. PCA was used as an exploratory multivariate analysis to summarize major patterns of morphometric variation. Squared principal component loading of each variable in the principal component (PC) 1 in PCA was also calculated, and 10 major variables contributing to PC1 were selected. These univariate comparisons were used to interpret the variables contributing to the PCA patterns, rather than as independent evidence for multivariate separation. Statistical significance was tested using unpaired Student’s t test with p values adjusted by Bonferroni correction. To account for overall body size, we also analyzed size-adjusted variables by dividing each linear measurement by SVL, except for SVL itself. These SVL-adjusted variables were used to evaluate proportional differences in limb and cranial measurements among sexes and strains.

In GMM, all statistical analyses were conducted using MorphoJ version 1.06d36. We prepared three datasets same as GMM and performed Procrustes fitting for each dataset. PCA analyses for all individuals and canonical variate analysis (CVA) for male and female datasets were then conducted after generation of covariance matrix.

Measurement of body color

We measured dorsal and ventral body colors in Commission internationale de l’éclairage L* a* b* (CIELab) color space, because CIELab is standard which developed to match human vision closely37. Ten females and ten males of each strain were measured using Nix Mini 3 (Nix Sensor Ltd., Hamilton) (Table 1). Measurements were taken at fixed positions on the dorsal and ventral body surfaces of animals. The sensor was placed perpendicular to the body surface, and care was taken to avoid shadows, wet reflections, and areas with obvious injury or irregular pigmentation. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before measurement. Because color measurements were obtained from 10 males and 10 females per strain, the results were interpreted as descriptive comparisons of the sampled individuals.

For the dorsal and ventral body colors, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effect of strain on variables L*, a*, and b*. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test to identify significant differences between groups.

Acoustic analysis of male advertisement call

To detect vocalization difference among strains, we recorded and analyzed male advertisement calls. We have confirmed that males started advertisement call after injection of 150 U of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, Asuka Pharmaceutical Co., Tokyo, Japan). Thus, recording was conducted ~ 3 h after injection of 150 U hCG into a pair of male and female after placing them in a plastic container containing 3 L of water in 26 ℃, using TASCAM DR-07MKII digital recorder (TEAC Co., Tokyo, Japan) set above the container. Recorded vocalization data were denoised and visualized as sonogram using Raven Lite 2.0.1 (Cornel Laboratory of Ornithology). Individual advertisement calls were sampled in each file and analyzed using ‘sound’38, ‘seewave’39 and ‘tuneR’40 packages for R.

For acoustic analyses, multiple advertisement calls recorded from the same male were first measured separately and then averaged to obtain one representative value per individual. Statistical comparisons among strains were performed using individual males, rather than individual calls, as the unit of replication. When only one call was available from an individual, that value was used as the individual-level value. Sample sizes indicate the number of males analyzed after averaging multiple calls per individual.
