Simultaneous removal of fluoride and arsenic from water in a fixed-bed column using Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite: optimization by response surface methodology
Authors: Ephrem Alemu, Ashootosh Mandpe, Shubham More, Dejene Beyene, Esayas Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluoride and arsenic contamination of groundwater remains a major concern for drinking water safety, particularly in regions relying on untreated groundwater sources. Although metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have demonstrated effective adsorption for contaminant removal, their application under continuous-flow conditions remains underexplored, limiting their practical deployment in water treatment systems. In this study, a structured bimetallic Fe-Al metal-organic framework was grown on carbon felt to form a composite (Fe-Al-MOF/CF), which was evaluated for simultaneous fluoride and arsenic removal from water in a fixed-bed column. The composite adsorbent was synthesized through electrochemical deposition, enabling uniform MOF growth on the conductive substrate and improving structural stability during continuous operation. SEM, XRD, and FTIR analyses confirmed that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF structure remained intact after adsorption and regeneration, suggesting that fluoride and arsenic removal is primarily governed by surface complexation at Fe3+ and Al3+ sites. Response Surface Methodology coupled with a Central Composite Design (CCD) was used to optimize the operating conditions, yielding statistically significant quadratic models (R2 = 0.91 for fluoride and 0.94 for arsenic) and achieving fluoride removal above 97% and arsenic removal exceeding 98%. Breakthrough curve analysis indicated favorable column performance, with maximum adsorption capacities of 29.38 mg g− 1 for fluoride and 14.01 mg g− 1 for arsenic, as estimated using the Thomas model, while the Yoon-Nelson model predicted 50% breakthrough times of approximately 3,958 min for fluoride and 3,846 min for arsenic. Regeneration experiments demonstrated good reusability, with adsorption capacity retention exceeding 83% for fluoride and 93.7% for arsenic after the fifth adsorption-desorption cycle. Structural and spectroscopic analyses further confirmed the framework’s stability after repeated adsorption and regeneration cycles, without compromising its integrity. These results demonstrate that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite is an effective structured adsorbent for continuous removal of fluoride and arsenic, highlighting the potential of MOF-based composites for continuous-flow water treatment applications.

Introduction

The scarcity of drinking water is a global issue, as more than 2 billion people worldwide depend on groundwater as their primary source1,2. Increasing reliance on groundwater for domestic, agricultural, and industrial activities has altered its chemical quality in many regions3. Contaminants originating from natural processes and human activities can migrate into aquifers, contributing to the deterioration of drinking water quality2,4. Among these contaminants, the World Health Organization (WHO) has identified fluoride and arsenic as major threats to public health due to their toxicity at elevated concentrations5. The guideline limits for drinking water are 10 µg L− 1 for arsenic and 1.5 mg L− 1 for fluoride, as established by WHO and adopted in recent studies and regulatory frameworks6,7. Long-term exposure to excess fluoride is known to cause dental and skeletal fluorosis, while arsenic contamination has been linked to several forms of cancer and other chronic health effects8,9. It is estimated that about 220 million people worldwide are exposed to unsafe levels of arsenic in drinking water, and nearly 180 million people face health risks associated with elevated fluoride concentrations in groundwater10,11. Therefore, these concerns underscore the need for reliable, sustainable methods that simultaneously remove fluoride and arsenic from contaminated water sources.

Common methods used for fluoride and arsenic removal include coagulation-flocculation, electrocoagulation, ion exchange, membrane-based methods, chemical precipitation, and electrochemical methods12–14. While these approaches can be effective under certain conditions, each method has practical limitations. For instance, both coagulation and electrocoagulation are relatively simple and suitable for bulk contaminant removal; however, they generate large amounts of sludge, and their efficiency often varies with changes in water chemistry15. Ion-exchange methods offer good selectivity for fluoride and arsenic ions. However, their large-scale application is limited by high costs, frequent brine regeneration, secondary waste generation, and sensitivity to competing ions16–18. Membrane technologies achieve high removal efficiencies for both contaminants, yet they are associated with high energy demand, membrane fouling, significant capital investment, and challenges related to concentrate disposal13. Although advanced electrochemical methods allow simultaneous removal of multiple contaminants, they typically require high energy input and complex operation, thereby increasing overall treatment costs19.

Adsorption methods are widely regarded as a promising alternative because they are simple to operate, cost-effective, can be prepared from locally available materials, and exhibit strong performance for fluoride and arsenic removal20,21. Moreover, adsorption systems are relatively easy to regenerate or replace, making them particularly suitable for resource-limited regions22,23. Adsorbent materials can be produced from low-cost, locally available materials, and avoid large sludge volumes typical of coagulation methods21,22. Numerous previous studies have reported strong removal of fluoride and arsenic using a range of adsorbents, including engineered materials that exhibit high adsorption capacities, rapid uptake, and the ability to maintain performance across multiple regeneration cycles24,25. These characteristics make adsorption a practical and cost-effective treatment approach for resource-limited locations where simple operation and low lifecycle costs are important.

Adsorption has been extensively investigated for the removal of fluoride and arsenic using various adsorbent materials, including activated carbons, biochars, metal oxides and hydroxides, ion-exchange materials, and emerging engineered sorbents such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)20,22,26. Recent studies have reported high removal efficiencies and rapid adsorption kinetics for engineered MOFs, which exhibit promising adsorption capacities27,28. MOFs have attracted considerable research interest due to their exceptionally high surface areas, tunable pore structures, and adjustable functional sites, which can improve the adsorption of fluoride and arsenic anions29,30. However, most MOF studies focus on batch experiments, and a major gap remains in continuous fixed-bed column studies25,31. This limitation mainly arises from the poor mechanical robustness of MOFs, which often necessitates the use of supporting substrates to ensure stability during continuous-flow operation29,32.

Compared with existing MOF-based fixed-bed studies, which often rely on powder MOFs mixed with supporting materials, this work addresses key limitations in mechanical stability and applicability under continuous-flow conditions33,34. In this study, Fe-Al-MOF was directly grown on conductive carbon felt to form a Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite, enabling continuous-flow operation without diluting the active material or using packing additives. Furthermore, this study demonstrates simultaneous removal of fluoride and arsenic, a feature that remains limited in MOF-based column systems. The integration of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with fixed-bed column experiments further provides a systematic and quantitative optimization framework. Therefore, the novelty of this study lies in addressing the limited application of MOFs under continuous-flow conditions through the integration of (i) a stable bimetallic MOF composite, (ii) fixed-bed column operation, and (iii) simultaneous multi-contaminant removal with statistical optimization. This approach advances the applicability of MOF-based adsorption systems for potential real-world groundwater treatment.

This study aimed to optimize the simultaneous removal of fluoride and arsenic from synthetic groundwater in a fixed-bed column packed using a bimetallic Fe-Al metal-organic framework grown on conductive carbon felt (Fe-Al-MOF/CF). Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was applied to determine optimal operating conditions and evaluate the effects of key process parameters. The objectives of this study were to: (i) study the structural stability, surface properties, and adsorption mechanism of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite under continuous-flow conditions; (ii) evaluate the individual and combined effects of influent concentration, pH, and flow rate on fluoride and arsenic removal; (iii) study column performance using breakthrough models to estimate adsorption capacity (q0) and breakthrough time (tb); and (iv) investigate the reusability and operational stability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite through repeated adsorption-desorption cycles. The results indicate that Fe-Al-MOF/CF enables efficient and stable removal of fluoride and arsenic under continuous fixed-bed operation, indicating its potential for practical application in MOF-based adsorption systems.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were sourced from SISCO CHEM (Mumbai, India). Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (Al (NO3)3·9H2O), iron (III) chloride anhydrous (FeCl3), potassium nitrate (KNO3), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), nitric acid (HNO3), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, methanol, and deionized water (DI water) were used in this study. The organic linker 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (Trimesic acid, H3BTC) was also obtained from SISCO CHEM. Sodium arsenate heptahydrate (Na2HAsO4·7H2O), and sodium fluoride (NaF) were used for preparing stock solutions. In addition, SPADNS dye powder (sodium 2-(p-sulfophenylazo)-1,8-dihydroxy-3,6-naphthalenedisulfonate) and zirconyl chloride octahydrate (ZrOCl2·8H2O) were also acquired from SISCO CHEM. The pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH.

Adsorbent material preparation

In this study, a bimetallic aluminum/iron-based metal-organic framework (Fe-Al-MOF) was cathodically electrodeposited onto conductive carbon felt (CF) to serve as a structured adsorbent material. The synthesis approach was adapted from previously reported strategies involving in-situ growth of MOFs on conductive carbon substrates and electrochemical deposition techniques35–37. In this work, the method was adapted to electrochemically grow a bimetallic Fe-Al-MOF directly on carbon felt, forming a structured Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite. The CF was first pretreated with 5 M HNO3, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h to improve hydrophilicity and surface functionality. The MOF phase was electrochemically synthesized directly on CF to form the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite using a two-electrode configuration with a graphite rod as the anode and the pretreated CF as the cathode, applying 5 V DC to drive a current density of 2.5 mA cm− 2 for 30 min at room temperature. The electrolyte consisted of 200 mL of an aqueous 0.1 M KNO3 solution containing aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (3.76 g), iron (III) chloride (2.70 g, anhydrous basis), and trimesic acid (1.38 g), with pH adjusted to 3.8–4.2. During deposition, nitrate reduction at the cathode generated hydroxide ions, producing a localized high‑pH zone that deprotonated the trimesic acid linkers and promoted their coordination with migrating Al3+ and Fe3+ cations, leading to direct Fe-Al-MOF nucleation on the CF. The presence of the organic linker and controlled electrochemical conditions promoted rapid coordination between deprotonated trimesic acid and Fe3+/Al3+ ions, favoring the formation of Fe-Al-MOF/CF over bulk metal hydroxide precipitation, as reported for electrochemically assisted MOF growth on conductive substrates and for coordination-driven framework assembly38. This is supported by the absence of characteristic hydroxide peaks in XRD and the FTIR evidence of metal-ligand coordination, confirming that MOF formation dominates under the applied conditions. After deposition, the composite was gently rinsed in a 1:1 water/ethanol mixture, followed by sequential rinsing with DMF and ethanol. It was then dried at 80 °C for 1 h, activated by immersion in methanol for 24 h, and finally vacuum-dried at 100 °C. The freestanding Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite was cut into discs or cylinders, ready for use in fixed‑bed column adsorption experiments.

Stock solution preparation

Sodium arsenate heptahydrate (Na2HAsO4·7H2O), and sodium fluoride (NaF) were used for preparing stock solutions. A 1000 mg L−1F− stock solution was prepared by dissolving 2.210 g of NaF in DI water and diluting to a final volume of 1 L. A 100 mg L− 1 As(V) stock solution was prepared by dissolving 0.4165 g of Na2HAsO4–7H2O in DI water and diluting to 1 L. Working solutions for adsorption experiments were prepared by diluting the respective stock solutions with DI water to the desired concentrations. The pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH.

Experimental setup

The fixed-bed column setup used a programmable peristaltic pump (DBK Instruments, Mumbai, India) to deliver the influent at a controlled flow rate. The influent was introduced through an inlet tube with an internal diameter of 4 mm. The cylindrical column with a height of 30 cm and an internal diameter of 3 cm was packed with a 20 cm adsorbent bed positioned centrally, as shown in Fig. 1. A predetermined mass of 100 g of adsorbent was carefully packed into the column. Glass wool layers (5 cm thick) were placed at the inlet and outlet to retain the adsorbent and ensure uniform flow. All column experiments were conducted at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The system operated in a downward-flow mode, with the influent at the top and the effluent at the bottom for collection and analysis. The fixed-bed column had a bed volume of 141.4 mL (inner diameter of 3 cm and bed height of 20 cm). For the response surface methodology (RSM) experiments, the influent flow rate was varied from 3 to 7 mL min− 1, with EBCT values ranging from 20.2 to 47.1 min. Based on RSM optimization, the optimal flow rate was determined to be 3.65 mL min-1, corresponding to an EBCT of 38.7 min. This EBCT was then used for breakthrough curve analysis, determination of the mass transfer zone (MTZ), and regeneration studies.

Sample analysis

Fluoride ion concentrations were measured using the SPADNS colorimetric method in accordance with standard analytical protocols. Measurements were performed using a SYSTRONICS PC-based double-beam spectrophotometer, Model 2202. The method is based on the reaction between fluoride ions and the zirconium-SPADNS dye complex, leading to proportional bleaching of the colored complex. The decrease in absorbance was measured at 570 nm, and fluoride concentrations were determined from calibration standards. Additionally, fluoride measurements were performed under controlled pH and solution conditions to minimize matrix interferences, particularly those arising from competing complexation reactions, and to ensure accurate fluoride measurement using the SPADNS method. Concentrations of arsenic were determined using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The analysis was conducted in accordance with standard operating procedures, with arsenic quantified using calibration standards to ensure accurate and reliable measurements.

Characterization of the adsorbent material

All characterization of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite was carried out at the SIC, IIT Indore. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine the surface morphology and particle structure, while energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the elemental composition. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was used to confirm the crystalline nature of the MOF phase in the composite. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) identified functional groups and confirmed metal-linker coordination. Additionally, the specific surface area, pore volume, and pore-size distribution were determined from nitrogen (N2) adsorption-desorption isotherms using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the dried Fe-Al-MOF/CF adsorbent was determined using the potentiometric mass titration (pH drift) method.

RSM-based experimental design and analysis

This study employed Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to systematically design and optimize the experimental conditions. Experimental design and data analysis were conducted using Design-Expert software version 13 (DX13) with a Central Composite Design (CCD) in RSM. This CCD approach in RSM efficiently evaluated the interactive effects of the independent variables on the responses. It also enabled the identification of optimal operating conditions with fewer experimental runs.

In this study, the CCD included four independent variables: initial fluoride concentration, initial arsenic concentration, pH, and flow rate. A face-centered CCD (α = 1) was employed to avoid operating conditions beyond the practical limits of the fixed-bed column system, while still allowing estimation of quadratic effects. In addition, fluoride and arsenic removal efficiencies were included as response variables. The levels of the variables are presented in Table 1, and the corresponding response parameters are listed in Table 2. The CCD in RSM was used for process optimization, which involves four main steps39. First, an experimental design was developed based on the selected independent variables, and experiments were conducted accordingly. Second, a mathematical model was developed using the experimental response data, and an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the model. Third, the model’s suitability was examined using diagnostic plots. Finally, the model was used to analyze the responses, determine the optimum conditions, and validate the results through a confirmatory experiment40.

Breakthrough curve analysis

Breakthrough curve models

The performance of fixed-bed column adsorption was efficiently explained using the breakthrough curve concept. In this study, the Thomas model and Yoon–Nelson model were applied to the column experimental data for describing the dynamic behavior of fluoride and arsenic adsorption using Fe-Al-MOF/CF in a fixed-bed column. The Adams-Bohart model was not applied because it is limited to the initial breakthrough region and does not adequately describe the full column behavior.

Thomas breakthrough curve model

The Thomas model is one of the most widely used kinetic models for predicting the performance of fixed-bed columns. Regeneration and reusability study

Regeneration and reusability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF were evaluated through consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles in a fixed-bed column operated under the optimized conditions obtained from RSM. After breakthrough of fluoride and arsenic, the influent was stopped, and the column was flushed with deionized water. Regeneration was carried out by pumping 0.05 M NaOH solution through the column at the optimum flow rate for 5 bed volumes to desorb fluoride, followed by elution with 0.1 M Na2HPO4 to desorb arsenic. The column was repeatedly rinsed with DI water until a neutral pH was attained before starting the next adsorption cycle. The adsorption-regeneration cycle was repeated, and breakthrough behavior and adsorption capacity for fluoride and arsenic were evaluated to assess reusability. Results and discussion

Adsorbent material characterization

Point of zero charge (pHPZC)

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the adsorbent was determined by potentiometric mass titration (pH drift) and was found to be 7.7. The Fe-Al-MOF/CF exhibited a neutral to slightly basic pHPZC, primarily governed by the dominance of metal-centered surface sites (Fe-OH/Al-OH), which control the surface charge behavior42. In BTC-based frameworks, the coordination of trimesic acid linkers to metal clusters reduces the availability of free acidic functional groups, whereas linker deprotonation contributes to surface charge evolution rather than intrinsic acidity28. Consequently, the metal-rich composition outweighs linker-induced acidity, leading to an elevated pHPZC and a less acidic surface character43.

Surface morphology and particle structure

The high-magnification SEM image of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite shows a compact, uniformly distributed granular MOF layer tightly anchored to the carbon felt (CF) surface. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the MOF phase appears as densely packed, irregular micro-crystallites forming a continuous coating that effectively covers the underlying CF substrate. In particular, surface asperities are observed throughout the coating, which improve pore accessibility and facilitate the mass transfer of adsorbates44. The absence of cracks, peeling, or particle detachment indicates strong interfacial adhesion and good structural integrity of the MOF layer within the composite. This well-developed morphology provides abundant exposed active sites, thereby improving the adsorption performance of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite.

SEM images of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite (a) before adsorption and (b) after adsorption, showing preserved surface morphology and structural stability.

The SEM image in Fig. 2b, recorded after adsorption, indicates that the MOF layer within the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite remains largely intact on the carbon felt surface, confirming the composite’s structural stability under adsorption conditions. Compared with the pristine adsorbent, the surface appears partially smoothed, with reduced visibility of sharp MOF features and inter-particle voids. This morphological change is attributed to surface coverage by adsorbed fluoride and arsenic species, which is commonly observed after adsorption45. Moreover, no significant cracking or detachment of the MOF layer is observed, indicating that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite maintains its mechanical integrity and is suitable for continuous fixed-bed column operation.

The EDS spectrum of the pristine Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite in Fig. 3a confirms the successful formation of the bimetallic MOF phase within the composite. The peaks of C and O originate from the carbon felt substrate and the organic linker, while the presence of Fe and Al verifies the incorporation of both metal centers in the MOF phase. The well-defined Fe and Al signals indicate homogeneous distribution within the composite. The EDS spectrum after adsorption (Fig. 3b) shows the presence of C, O, Fe, Al, F, and as elements. In addition to the characteristic peaks of the composite, distinct signals corresponding to F Kα (~ 0.68 keV) and As Kα (~ 10.4 keV) are observed, confirming the successful adsorption of fluoride and arsenic onto the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite. The relatively lower intensity of the as peak compared to Fe and Al is attributed to its lower concentration on the adsorbent surface. The coexistence of Fe, Al, F, and as signals is consistent with adsorption occurring at metal-centered active sites, where arsenic species form coordination complexes and fluoride interacts with metal-hydroxyl groups through complexation and ion exchange, as widely reported in MOF and metal-based adsorbent systems46. Overall, the EDS results confirm the uptake of fluoride and arsenic while preserving the composition of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The XRD pattern presented in Fig. 4(a) indicates that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite exhibits distinct diffraction peaks at 28.60, 29.40, 33.140, 36.060, 39.420, 47.50, and 56.440. These peaks are attributed to the crystalline Fe-Al-MOF phase formed through the coordination of Fe and Al metal centers with the organic linker. The sharp, well-defined reflections indicate good crystallinity of the MOF phase in the composite, consistent with previously reported XRD patterns of Fe-based MOFs47. In addition, a broad amorphous feature observed in the 22–230 region corresponds to the CF support, suggesting its presence in the composite structure48. Furthermore, the absence of additional diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline iron or aluminum oxide phases suggests that no separate crystalline impurities are present, and that the metal species are predominantly incorporated within the MOF structure or exist in a highly dispersed, XRD-amorphous form42. Therefore, the coexistence of crystalline MOF peaks and the amorphous CF background suggests the successful formation of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite.

The XRD pattern of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite after fluoride and arsenic adsorption (Fig. 4(b)) retains the characteristic diffraction peaks of the MOF phase at approximately 28.5–29.40, 33.10, 47.50, and 56.40, indicating preservation of the crystalline structure after adsorption. This comparison with reported diffraction patterns confirms the formation of a BTC-based bimetallic MOF phase within the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite49,50. However, it should be emphasized that the analyzed material is a composite (Fe-Al-MOF/CF), and therefore, the XRD pattern reflects the combined contributions of the MOF phase and the amorphous CF support. Fe-Al-MOF/CF cannot be assigned to a single well-defined crystallographic structure due to its bimetallic and composite nature. The material is treated as a composite system with MOF-like active sites rather than a single crystalline MOF phase.

After adsorption, no significant peak shifts are observed, confirming structural stability of the composite51. Minor reductions in peak intensity and slight peak broadening are evident, which can be attributed to surface coverage and partial pore occupation by adsorbed fluoride and arsenic species52. An increase in the amorphous background in the 20–250 regions further supports interaction between the adsorbate and the composite surface53. These observations indicate that adsorption primarily occurs through surface complexation without compromising the structural integrity of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite54.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectral analysis

Figure 5a and b) show the FTIR spectra of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite before and after adsorption of fluoride and arsenic, respectively. In Fig. 5a, the characteristic bands in the range of 550–900 cm− 1 are attributed to metal-oxygen vibrations (Fe-O and Al-O) associated with the MOF phase within the composite55. Strong absorption peaks at approximately 1120 cm⁻¹ and 1580 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O stretching and asymmetric carboxylate (–COO⁻) vibrations of the organic linker, respectively, with additional contributions from oxygen-containing functional groups in the carbon felt support56.

After adsorption (Fig. 5(b)), new and intensified bands appear near 585 cm⁻¹ and 860 cm⁻¹, which are assigned to metal-fluoride interactions and arsenic-related surface complexes, respectively57,58. These changes indicate the interaction of fluoride and arsenic species with Fe/Al active sites in the composite. In addition, the broad hydroxyl band around 3100 cm− 1 decreases in intensity, suggesting the involvement of surface –OH groups in the adsorption process59. The observable shifts and intensity variations in the carboxylate region (1400–1650 cm− 1) further support the occurrence of chemical interactions between the adsorbates and the composite surface60. Generally, the spectral changes suggest that adsorption occurs through surface complexation mechanisms while maintaining the structure of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite.

Thermo gravimetric and differential thermal analysis

The thermo-gravimetric (TGA) and differential thermal (DTA) analyses of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite (Fig. 6) reveal its thermal stability and decomposition behavior over the temperature range of 34–900 °C. The TGA curve shows a minor mass loss below 150 °C, attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and volatile species. A relatively stable region is observed up to approximately 550 °C, indicating good structural stability of the composite. A significant mass loss occurs between 600 and 800 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of the organic 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (BTC) linker in the MOF phase and partial degradation of the carbon felt support61, resulting in the formation of thermally stable metal oxide residues (≈ 55–60 wt%). The DTA profile supports these observations, showing a weak endothermic peak at low temperature due to moisture removal and a strong exothermic peak in the range of 650–750 °C, associated with decomposition of the organic framework and formation of metal oxide phases. Compared with reported Fe-based MOFs61,62, the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite exhibits improved thermal stability, which can be attributed to the carbon felt support and strong interfacial interactions within the composite. Overall, the TGA and DTA results confirm that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite possesses sufficient thermal stability for adsorption applications.

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analysis

Figure 7 shows the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite. The isotherm exhibits a Type IV profile with a distinct hysteresis loop, indicating a predominantly mesoporous structure63. At low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.3), the gradual nitrogen uptake corresponds to monolayer-multilayer adsorption, supporting the applicability of the BET model for surface area determination. The increase in adsorption at intermediate relative pressures is attributed to capillary condensation within mesopores associated with the MOF phase supported on the carbon felt substrate64. The hysteresis loop further suggests mesoporosity and pore connectivity within the composite structure. At high relative pressures, the adsorption plateau indicates pore saturation. Overall, these results demonstrate that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite possesses a mesoporous architecture with accessible surface sites suitable for adsorption applications.

Interaction effects of operating parameters on the removal efficiency

Three-dimensional response surface plots from the developed models were used to analyze interactions between operating parameters and determine the optimal fluoride and arsenic removal efficiencies. The interaction effect of the operating variables on fluoride removal efficiency is presented in Fig. 12a and the corresponding contour plot, which shows the combined influence of initial fluoride concentration and flow rate while the other variables are maintained at their central levels. The surface plot in Fig. 12a shows that the fluoride removal efficiency increases with increasing initial fluoride concentration (A) up to an optimum range. The increase in removal efficiency at higher concentrations is attributed to a greater driving force for mass transfer66 and to adsorption at the adsorbent’s active sites. However, the effect of initial fluoride concentration is influenced by the flow rate (D). At lower flow rates, higher fluoride removal efficiencies are recorded due to longer contact time between the adsorbent and the fluoride ion. As the flow rate increases, the removal efficiency decreases, mainly at lower fluoride concentrations, suggesting that reduced residence time limits adsorption kinetics. The curvature of the response surface and the elliptical contour lines confirm a significant interaction between these two operating variables. Generally, Fig. 12a demonstrates that the effect of initial fluoride concentration on removal efficiency cannot be considered independently of flow rate, underscoring the importance of simultaneously optimizing operating variables.

The response surface plot in Fig. 12b shows the interaction between influent pH (C) and flow rate (D) on fluoride removal efficiency. The results indicate that fluoride removal efficiency is higher at lower to moderate pH values, indicating that acidic to near-neutral conditions favor fluoride adsorption due to improved electrostatic attraction between fluoride ions and the positively charged adsorbent surface. As the pH increases toward alkaline conditions, a gradual decrease in removal efficiency is observed, suggesting reduced adsorption affinity caused by increased competition with hydroxyl ions.

The surface plot in Fig. 12c indicates that the initial arsenic concentration has a negligible effect on fluoride removal efficiency, as evidenced by the nearly flat response along this axis. In contrast, fluoride removal decreases with increasing flow rate. As shown in Fig. 12d, fluoride removal efficiency decreases with increasing pH, indicating that acidic to near-neutral conditions favor adsorption. In contrast, increasing the initial fluoride concentration results in a slight increase in removal efficiency over the studied range. The response surface and contour plots reveal a clear interaction between pH and initial fluoride concentration, with maximum fluoride removal achieved at lower pH and higher initial concentrations.

The observed decrease in arsenic removal efficiency with increasing fluoride concentration (Fig. 13a) indicates competitive adsorption between fluoride and arsenic ions. Arsenic exhibits a stronger affinity for Fe-based active sites via inner-sphere complexation67,68, whereas fluoride is mainly adsorbed via ligand exchange and electrostatic interactions69,70. As a result, arsenate preferentially occupies high-affinity sites, while fluoride adsorption is more affected by competition and changes in surface charge68. In addition, pH plays a significant role in modulating this interaction69,71. Under acidic to near-neutral conditions, the positively charged adsorbent surface enhances the adsorption of both anions, whereas at higher pH values, increased hydroxide competition reduces adsorption efficiency, particularly for fluoride70,71. The elliptical contour lines and surface curvature in Fig. 13b demonstrate that the initial arsenic concentration and pH interact strongly. At an intermediate level (Fig. 13b), arsenic removal efficiency increases with initial arsenic concentration, then declines, indicating saturation of adsorption sites. Influent pH also significantly affects adsorption performance, with lower efficiency at higher pH and higher removal efficiency at acidic to near-neutral pH.

Figure 13c shows the interaction effect of initial arsenic concentration and flow rate on arsenic removal efficiency. Arsenic removal efficiency increases with initial arsenic concentration up to an intermediate level and then declines. Increasing flow rate consistently reduces arsenic removal efficiency. The curved response surface and elliptical contour lines confirm a significant interaction between initial arsenic concentration and flow rate, with optimal arsenic removal achieved at moderate arsenic concentration and lower flow rates. In addition, Fig. 13d shows that arsenic removal efficiency decreases with increasing pH, indicating more favorable adsorption under acidic to near-neutral conditions. Similarly, higher flow rates lead to reduced removal efficiency due to reduced contact time, as widely reported in fixed-bed adsorption systems66. The smooth curvature of the surface and contour lines confirms the interaction between pH and flow rate, with maximum arsenic removal achieved at lower pH values and lower flow rates.

Fixed-bed column performance analysis

Breakthrough behavior of fluoride and arsenic

The optimized operating conditions were obtained using RSM based on a CCD. The analysis identified the optimum combination of process variables that maximized fluoride and arsenic removal efficiencies while minimizing experimental error. These optimized conditions were then selected and applied in the fixed-bed column experiments to validate the predictive reliability of the RSM-CCD model under continuous-flow operation. The breakthrough curves were developed using the optimized operating parameters obtained from the RSM-CCD analysis and presented in Fig. 14.

The breakthrough curves under optimized operating conditions are presented in Fig. 14a for fluoride removal and in Fig. 14b for arsenic removal, using experimental column data. The experiments were conducted at an initial fluoride concentration of 12.9 mg L− 1, an initial arsenic concentration of 0.35 mg L− 1, a flow rate of 3.65 mL min− 1, and an influent pH of 3.045. Both fluoride and arsenic curves in Fig. 14 showed a typical sigmoidal (S-shaped) breakthrough curve characteristic of fixed-bed adsorption systems. At the initial stage, the effluent concentrations of fluoride and arsenic remained very low, indicating effective utilization of available adsorption sites. As the operation time increased, the effluent concentration rose sharply as the mass transfer zone progressed along the column. At prolonged operation, Ct/C0 approached unity, indicating near exhaustion of the adsorbent bed and reduced removal efficiency. The smooth breakthrough curves in Fig. 14 confirm stable column operation and effective mass transfer under optimized conditions72. Overall, the experimental breakthrough behavior in Fig. 14 demonstrated the suitability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF adsorbent material for continuous fluoride and arsenic removal.

Thomas breakthrough curve model fitting

For fluoride removal, the experimental breakthrough data as shown in Fig. 14 were fitted using the nonlinear Thomas model of Eq. (1). The Thomas model for fluoride removal showed excellent agreement with the experimental data, with a Thomas rate constant (KT) of 9.145 × 10− 5 L mg− 1 min− 1 and a maximum adsorption capacity (q0) of 29.38 mg g− 1. The low reduced chi-square value (0.00112) indicates a good fit, confirming the suitability of the Thomas model for fluoride removal. In addition, the high coefficient of determination value (R2 = 0.9906) confirmed the adequacy of the model in describing the fixed-bed adsorption behavior for Fe-Al-MOF/CF for fluoride removal61.

The experimental breakthrough data and simulated curve for arsenic removal are presented in Fig. 14b, developed using the Thomas model. Figure 14b shows that there is an excellent agreement between the predicted and observed Ct/C0 values. The fitted Thomas rate constant (KT = 2.47 × 10− 4 mL mg− 1 min− 1) and maximum adsorption capacity (qe) of 14.01 mg g− 1 indicate favorable adsorption kinetics and a high affinity of the adsorbent for arsenic. The very low chi-square value of 0.00138, together with the high coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.99016, confirms the reliability of the Thomas model45. Generally, these results confirm the applicability of the Thomas model for describing the adsorption behavior of fluoride and arsenic on Fe-Al-MOF/CF in a continuous-flow system.

The breakthrough curve presented in Fig. 14 indicates that the adsorption column provides a long service time for both fluoride and arsenic removal before adsorbent exhaustion. The extended low Ct/C0 region indicates that the effluent fluoride concentration remains within permissible limits for a significant period of operation. Yoon-Nelson breakthrough curve model fitting

The experimental and simulated breakthrough curves presented in Fig. 15a and Fig. 15b demonstrated that the Yoon-Nelson model effectively describes the dynamic adsorption behavior of fluoride and arsenic, respectively, in the column system. As shown in Fig. 15a, the close agreement between the experimental and simulated Ct/C0 data confirms the applicability of the Yoon-Nelson model to describe the time-dependent breakthrough behavior of fluoride during column adsorption using Fe-Al-MOF/CF, with a fitted rate constant (KYN) of 0.00118 min− 1 and a 50% breakthrough time (τ) of 3958 min, indicating delayed breakthrough for efficient bed utilization. Also, Fig. 15(b) demonstrates that the Yoon-Nelson model provides an excellent fit for arsenic removal, as evidenced by the strong correspondence between the experimental and predicted curves, yielding a rate constant of 0.00123 min⁻¹ and a 50% breakthrough time of approximately 3846 min. The high coefficients of determination obtained for both fluoride (R2 = 0.9909; adjusted R2 = 0.9906) and arsenic (R2 = 0.9902; adjusted R2 = 0.9898) further confirm the reliability and applicability of the Yoon–Nelson model for predicting the breakthrough behavior of both contaminants28.

The mass transfer zone was determined using Eq. 3. The MTZ was evaluated from the experimental breakthrough data by determining the times (min) corresponding to Ct/C0 values of 0.1 and 0.9 via linear interpolation of the Ct/C0-time relationship in Origin Pro. Using a bed height of 20 cm and the corresponding breakthrough times, the MTZ was calculated to be 7.18 cm, indicating that a significant portion of the bed participated in mass transfer during fluoride adsorption. The MTZ accounted for approximately 36% of the bed height, indicating a relatively narrow mass transfer zone and good dynamic performance of the fixed-bed column, with efficient bed utilization during fluoride adsorption.

Regeneration and reusability testing

The regeneration performance of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF was further evaluated using the breakthrough curves obtained after the fifth adsorption-regeneration cycle for both fluoride and arsenic. As shown in Fig. 18(a), the regenerated column exhibited a fluoride breakthrough profile similar to that of the fresh adsorbent shown in Fig. 14a, indicating that the fluoride adsorption behavior was preserved after repeated use. Thomas model fitting of the fifth cycle fluoride breakthrough data yielded a maximum adsorption capacity (q0) of 24.51 mg g− 1, compared to 29.38 mg g− 1 for the fresh column, corresponding to a capacity retention of 83.42%. Although slight reductions in breakthrough time and adsorption capacity were observed after five cycles, the high coefficient of determination (R2 > 0.99) indicates that the Thomas model adequately describes the fluoride breakthrough behavior of the regenerated adsorbent. The slight performance loss is attributed to partial blockage of active sites and incomplete desorption, while the results confirm good structural stability and reusability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF for fluoride removal.

Metal leaching and chemical stability during regeneration

Monitoring metal leaching is an important criterion for evaluating the environmental safety of MOF-based adsorbents, as framework degradation may release metal ions and organic ligands into treated water. In addition to adsorption performance, the chemical stability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF during repeated regeneration was evaluated by considering potential metal leaching. According to the WHO guidelines for drinking-water quality, Fe and Al do not have health-based guideline values; however, operational guideline limits of 0.3 mg L− 1 for Fe and 0.1 to 0.2 mg L− 1 for Al are recommended to prevent aesthetic and operational issues. The measured concentrations of Fe and Al in the treated effluent after the fifth regeneration cycle were 0.02 mg L⁻¹and 0.01 mg L⁻¹, respectively, at Ct/C0 = 0.9. These concentrations are within the recommended limits, indicating that the regeneration process did not induce significant metal leaching. Consistent with the study conducted by Wang et al.74, these results confirm the good chemical stability and safe reusability of Fe-Al-MOF/CF during repeated fixed-bed operation. The low metal release combined with high-capacity retention further demonstrates the long-term operational reliability of Fe-Al-MOF/CF for drinking water treatment74.

Comparison with previously reported studies

MOF-based adsorbents have demonstrated promising performance for fluoride and arsenic removal, particularly under fixed-bed column operation72,73. For arsenic, Fe3O4-ZIF-8@eggshell membrane matrices72 and UiO-66/chitosan composite beads45 achieved high removal efficiencies, with up to 90% simultaneous arsenic and nitrate removal and treatment of approximately 1600 bed volumes, respectively as presented in Table 12. In addition, regeneration efficiency and metal leaching were rarely quantified. Fe-BTC MOF columns66 showed a higher adsorption capacity with > 75% arsenite removal and low Fe leaching (0.23 mg L− 1). In contrast, most MOF-based fluoride removal studies, including MIL-88 A (Fe)73, have been conducted primarily in batch systems, which limit direct comparison with continuous fixed-bed column performance. Compared to these studies, the Fe-Al-MOF/CF fixed-bed column demonstrated a high adsorption capacity of 29.38 mg g-1, a removal rate of greater than 95%, good capacity retention over five cycles, and negligible metal leaching. These results indicate that Fe-Al-MOF/CF offers strong potential for continuous-flow water treatment applications.

Although numerous studies on MOF adsorbents report high fluoride adsorption capacities under batch conditions, explicit fixed-bed column parameters, such as the Thomas model adsorption capacity (q0) and quantitative metal-leaching values, are rarely reported. However, the present study provides comprehensive fixed-bed performance metrics, including breakthrough behavior, dynamic adsorption capacity, regeneration efficiency, and metal leaching.

Conclusions

This study optimized the simultaneous removal of fluoride and arsenic from synthetic groundwater in a fixed-bed column using a bimetallic Fe-Al metal-organic framework grown on conductive carbon felt, resulting in the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite. Material characterization confirmed the formation of a stable, well-adhered mesoporous MOF layer on the conductive substrate. SEM, XRD, and FTIR analyses verified that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite maintained its structural integrity after repeated adsorption and regeneration cycles, suggesting that adsorption primarily occurs through interactions with Fe3+ and Al3+ active sites. Process optimization using Response Surface Methodology generated statistically significant quadratic models for both contaminants (R2 = 0.91 for fluoride and 0.94 for arsenic), with ANOVA identifying influent pH and flow rate as the dominant factors influencing removal efficiency. Under optimized conditions, removal efficiencies exceeded 97% for fluoride and 98% for arsenic during continuous operation. Breakthrough experiments demonstrated stable column performance, with maximum adsorption capacities of 29.38 mg g− 1 for fluoride and 14.01 mg g− 1 for arsenic, as determined by the Thomas model. The Yoon–Nelson model adequately described the column behavior, predicting breakthrough times of approximately 3,958 min for fluoride and 3,846 min for arsenic, corresponding to 50% breakthrough. Regeneration studies further confirmed the strong reusability of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite, with fluoride and arsenic adsorption capacities remaining above 83% and 93.7%, respectively, after five adsorption-desorption cycles. Overall, these results demonstrate that the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite is an efficient and structurally stable adsorbent for simultaneous fluoride and arsenic removal in continuous fixed-bed systems, offering a promising approach for advancing MOF-based composite adsorption technologies for potential real-world applications. Future studies should evaluate the performance of the Fe-Al-MOF/CF composite using real groundwater to assess the effects of competing ions, natural organic matter, and background water chemistry. In addition, long-term continuous operation, extended regeneration cycles, improved modeling approaches, pilot-scale column performance, economic feasibility of the electrochemical synthesis method, and potential metal leaching should be further investigated to support practical implementation.
