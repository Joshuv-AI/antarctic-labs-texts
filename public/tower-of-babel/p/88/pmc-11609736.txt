Proline‐rich transmembrane protein 2 regulates the magnitude and frequency of dopamine release by repetitive neuronal stimuli in the striatum of L‐dopa‐treated mice
Authors: Daisuke Hatta, Shiho Makiya, Kaito Kanamoto, Kaori Watanabe, Yuki Fuchigami, Shigeru Kawakami, Akira Kinoshita, Koh‐Ichiro Yoshiura, Naohiro Kurotaki, Keiro Shirotani, Nobuhisa Iwata
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMutations in proline‐rich transmembrane protein 2 (PRRT2) cause paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD). Recently, we reported that a Prrt2 mutation exacerbated L‐dopa‐induced motor deficits in mice, suggesting that the basal ganglia might contribute to PKD pathology. Here, we demonstrated that the Prrt2 mutation enhanced depolarization stimuli‐induced extracellular dopamine levels in the mouse striatum, which were attenuated by repeated stimulation. L‐dopa administration maintained high dopamine levels in Prrt2‐KI mice even during repetitive stimuli but did not affect dopamine levels in wild‐type mice. Thus, the enhanced and prolonged responsiveness of dopamine release in nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons to sequential excitation may be partially implicated in Prrt2‐related dyskinesia.

INTRODUCTION

Mutations in proline‐rich transmembrane protein 2 (PRRT2) cause various neurological disorders such as paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD) 1 with attacks of involuntary movements triggered by abrupt motion. 2 PKD is thought to develop in a loss‐of‐function manner because the Prrt2 mutant protein was not detected in the brain of Prrt2 knock‐in (KI) mice harboring the c.672dupG mutation corresponding to PKD‐related human mutation c.649dupC. 3 Although many cases of PKD are treatable with carbamazepine, a blocker of voltage‐gated sodium channels, the pathogenic mechanism of PKD, including PRRT2 functions and its responsible brain region, remains unclear. It has been reported that PRRT2 is involved in various neural functions, such as regulation of sodium channels 4 and synaptic vesicle release machinery. 5 The cerebellum has been regarded as a key region for PKD pathogenesis 6 ; however, we found that Prrt2‐KI mice developed more severe L‐dopa‐induced motor deficits than wild‐type mice during rotarod tests, 3 suggesting that the basal ganglia could also be implicated in PKD.

Prrt2‐KI mice exhibit PKD‐like involuntary movements. However, the movements are very infrequent under non‐stressed conditions unlike PKD patients' attacks, which might be attributed to differences in brain network stability or expression levels of molecules that functionally compensate for the loss of Prrt2 between the species. Due to the infrequency of attacks, we needed to give any stress to the KI mice to access the motor phenotypes. Actually, similar Prrt2 mutant mice, which were reported from other laboratories, 6 also showed infrequent PKD‐like movements, and excitatory stress by pentylenetetrazole induced more frequent dyskinesia, which was alleviated by the anticonvulsant drug carbamazepine used in PKD treatment, 3 confirming predictive validity. Similarly, we gave L‐dopa‐induced stress to dopaminergic system in the present and previous 3 studies. Because PKD is characterized by running‐triggered attacks, the rotarod test has been commonly used for evaluation of PKD motor phenotypes in PKD research field. 6 Therefore, we consider that L‐dopa‐induced motor impairment in rotarod test may be a valid model of PKD.

The basal ganglia form complex brain structures, characterized by dense dopaminergic projections from the substantia nigra pars compacta to the striatum. The basal ganglia constitute a neural circuit together with the cerebral cortex and thalamus, namely the cortico‐basal ganglia loop, which regulates voluntary movement. Dysfunction of the dopaminergic neurons, which control the motor loop, results in various motor disorders. 7 Therefore, if PKD has a pathological mechanism that partially overlaps with other types of dyskinesia, such as L‐dopa‐induced dyskinesia and tardive dyskinesia, it is plausible that the basal ganglia might be related to PKD.

In this study, we hypothesized that the Prrt2 mutation‐related dyskinetic phenotype after L‐dopa administration 3 might be caused by excessive dopaminergic transmission and analyzed alterations in extracellular dopamine levels in the striatum of wild‐type and Prrt2‐KI mice after L‐dopa administration. Given that PKD symptoms arise in a movement‐dependent manner, 2 Prrt2 may play an important role, especially in neuronal excitation. Therefore, to identify activity‐dependent neurochemical phenotypes of the Prrt2 mutation, we administered potassium chloride (KCl) to the striatum of mice after L‐dopa administration.

METHODS

Materials and methods are described in the Supplementary Methods (Data S1). All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagasaki University and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experimentation of Nagasaki University. Extracellular dopamine levels in the mouse striatum were analyzed using microdialysis followed by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)‐electrochemical detection (ECD) (Figure 1A), according to previously reported methods. 8 The time schedule of drug administration is presented in Figure 1B.

In vivo measurement of extracellular dopamine concentration in the mouse striatum and the experimental design. (A) Schematic of in vivo microdialysis coupled with a high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)‐electrochemical detector (ECD). A dialysis probe (cut‐off 50 kDa) was implanted into the dorsal striatum of anesthetized mice, which were perfused with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) with or without 60 mM potassium chloride (KCl). Dopamine concentrations in the dialysates were determined using HPLC‐ECD. (B) Time schedule of drug administration. Wild‐type and Prrt2‐KI mice were randomly divided into two groups (control and L‐dopa). Mice in the L‐dopa groups were pretreated with carbidopa (25 mg/kg body weight, i.p.) 30 min before the administration of L‐dopa (100 mg/kg body weight, i.p.). Mice in the control groups were injected with vehicle A (saline) and vehicle B (saline including 0.04 N HCl) in place of carbidopa and L‐dopa, respectively. The time point for L‐dopa administration was set to zero min. After equilibration, the microdialysate was collected at 10‐min interval. Administration of 60 mM KCl for 10 min via a dialysis probe (namely, “reverse microdialysis”) was started 30 min after L‐dopa administration and repeated four times, as shown in (B).

RESULTS

Since Prrt2‐KI mice show more severe L‐dopa‐induced motor impairment than wild‐type mice, 3 we determined striatal extracellular dopamine levels during L‐dopa exposure (Figure 2, Tables S1 and S2) to clarify the relationship between the Prrt2‐related dyskinetic phenotype and changes in dopaminergic neurotransmission. Extracellular dopamine levels before drug administration were similar between the wild‐type and Prrt2‐KI mice (Figure 2A, −50 to −30 min). In the control groups, the first KCl stimulation increased extracellular dopamine levels in Prrt2‐KI mice compared to those in wild‐type mice (Figure 2A,B, KCl #1, cyan vs. blue columns, wild‐type control vs. Prrt2‐KI control, p = 0.049). When KCl stimulation was repeated, extracellular dopamine levels in Prrt2‐KI mice decreased to levels equivalent to those in wild‐type mice (Figure 2B, cyan vs. blue columns, wild‐type control vs. Prrt2‐KI control; KCl #2, p = 0.052; KCl #3, p = 0.895; KCl #4, p = 0.380).

Effect of Prrt2 mutation on extracellular dopamine levels after L‐dopa administration. Extracellular dopamine concentrations of fractions collected at 10‐min interval were quantified as described in Materials and Methods. Individual data plots of each fraction (A) are overlaid on each column with error bar presented as the mean ± standard error of six male mice. Data for each KCl fraction (at time points [#1] 30–40, [#2] 60–70, [#3] 90–100 and [#4] 120–130 min) are re‐plotted from (A) to compare repetitive stimuli‐induced changes in extracellular dopamine levels (B). Repeated‐measures three‐way ANOVA showed significant main effects of genotype (F (1,20) = 17.480, p < 0.001) and L‐dopa (F (1,20) = 5.145, p = 0.035) on the extracellular dopamine levels. Other factors or interactions were not statistically significant: KCl stimuli, F (2.248,44.966) = 1.151, p = 0.330; genotype × L‐dopa, F (1,20) = 0.569, p = 0.460; KCl stimuli × genotype, F (2.248,44.966) = 1.476, p = 0.239; KCl stimuli × L‐dopa, F (2.248,44.966) = 0.248, p = 0.807; KCl stimuli × genotype × L‐dopa, F (2.248,44.966) = 0.927, p = 0.413). A Bonferroni post‐hoc test showed significant differences (KCl #1, wt control vs. Prrt2‐KI control, p = 0.049, wt L‐dopa vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa, p = 0.011; KCl #3, Prrt2‐KI control vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa, p = 0.015; KCl #4, wt L‐dopa vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa, p = 0.021). *p < 0.05.

Without KCl stimulation, L‐dopa administration resulted in only a slight or no peak in extracellular dopamine levels over 0–120 min, as well as in the control groups, regardless of Prrt2 genotype (data not shown). In the L‐dopa and control groups, the first KCl stimulation increased the extracellular dopamine levels of Prrt2‐KI mice more than those of the wild‐type mice (Figure 2A,B, KCl #1, yellow vs. orange columns, wild‐type L‐dopa vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa, p = 0.011) whereas no effect of L‐dopa was detected (Figure 2B, KCl #1, control vs. L‐dopa, p = 0.287). Interestingly, when the KCl stimulation was repeated (Figure 2B), significant main effects of L‐dopa (p = 0.035) and Prrt2 mutation (p < 0.001) on extracellular dopamine levels were observed (repeated measures three‐way ANOVA): L‐dopa administration allowed Prrt2‐KI mice to maintain higher extracellular dopamine levels during repetitive stimuli as compared with vehicle administration to Prrt2‐KI mice (Figure 2B, blue vs. orange columns, Prrt2‐KI control vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa, KCl #3, p = 0.015, KCl #4, p = 0.067) or L‐dopa administration to wild‐type mice (Figure 2B, yellow vs. orange columns, wild‐type L‐dopa vs. Prrt2‐KI L‐dopa; KCl #3, p = 0.091; KCl #4, p = 0.021).

DISCUSSION

Prrt2 mutation elevated extracellular dopamine levels during the activated state induced by KCl (Figure 2A, KCl #1, cyan vs. blue columns, control groups) but not during static states (Figure 2A, −50 to −30 min, cyan vs. blue columns, control groups), suggesting an activity‐dependent role of Prrt2, which might be related to functional change in Prrt2 due to activity‐dependent cleavage of Prrt2 by calpain. 9 Moreover, the activity‐induced manifestation of the dopaminergic phenotype is consistent with the clinical features of PKD characterized by movement‐induced attacks, generally, because voluntary movements accompany activation of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons. 10 We speculate that the mechanism of the Prrt2 mutation‐induced extracellular dopamine surge depends on the enhancement of dopamine release rather than the suppression of dopamine uptake, because the extracellular levels of dopamine in the static state should be elevated if dopamine uptake is inhibited 11 ; however, in this study, there was no difference between wild‐type and Prrt2‐KI mice.

L‐dopa administration exerted little influence on the baseline level of extracellular dopamine as reported previously 12 , 13 ; however, the latter report showed that it could be markedly increased by pharmacological inhibition of monoamine oxidase B, 13 indicating that there may be various homeostatic regulations, such as the catabolism of dopamine, to minimize the elevation of extracellular dopamine levels.

In contrast, L‐dopa elevated extracellular dopamine levels during repetitive KCl stimuli especially during the third stimulation in Prrt2‐KI mice (Figure 2B, KCl #3, 90–100 min, orange column, Figure 3D), which is consistent with a report that L‐dopa administration was involved in an increase in activity‐dependent dopamine release rather than a rise of baseline levels of extracellular dopamine. 14 Furthermore, the latency until the effect of L‐dopa administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) arose (90–100 min) was consistent with the result from a radiotracer study that [18F]‐Fdopa reached at the highest level in the mouse striatum 95.4 ± 14.6 min after i.p. injection. 15 Extracellular dopamine levels in the vehicle‐treated Prrt2‐KI mice were declined over the second and the third stimulation (Figure 2B, blue columns). Considering that this decline was rescued by supplementation with a precursor of dopamine (L‐dopa), and exogenous dopamine converted from exogenous L‐dopa in the brain should be transported into synaptic vesicles in the same manner as endogenous dopamine, we anticipate that the use‐dependent decline in Prrt2 mutation‐enhanced dopamine release was attributed to the depletion of dopamine (Figure 3B), and that L‐dopa prevented dopamine depletion (Figure 3D). In addition, replenishment of dopamine into synaptic vesicles is considered a rate‐limiting factor in determining extracellular dopamine levels during the second or subsequent stimulation in Prrt2‐KI mice. This might be a reason why L‐dopa administration successfully sustained extracellular dopamine levels after 90–100 min that it takes for L‐dopa to reach enough into the brain after administration. 15

Schematic diagram showing the effects of Prrt2 mutation and L‐dopa injection on stimuli‐induced dopamine release. After administration of vehicle, a single stimulation with KCl elevated striatal extracellular dopamine levels in homozygous Prrt2 knock‐in (KI) mice compared to wild‐type mice (A), whereas repetitive stimulation with KCl attenuated the dopamine levels of Prrt2‐KI mice to levels equivalent to those of wild‐type mice (B). Preadministration of L‐dopa had little effect on the magnitude of dopamine surge by a single stimulation (C), but effectively suppressed repetitive stimuli‐induced decline in the dopamine surge in Prrt2‐KI mice (D). (E) Prolonged nigrostriatal dopaminergic activity during neuronal excitation may be implicated in Prrt2 mutation‐caused aggravation of L‐dopa‐induced motor deficits observed in our previous study (Hatta et al. 3 ).

Meanwhile, the first KCl stimulation, corresponding to a single stimulation, led to a Prrt2 mutation‐dependent increase in the extracellular dopamine levels (Figure 2B, KCl #1, blue column, Figure 3A), which was unaffected by L‐dopa administration (Figure 2B, KCl #1, orange column, Figure 3A,C). This is a plausible result considering the penetration time of L‐dopa into the brain. 15 The second KCl stimulation after L‐dopa administration made no significant difference in extracellular dopamine levels between two genotypes (Figure 2B, KCl #2, yellow vs. orange columns) because L‐dopa administration slightly elevated extracellular dopamine levels with a flat peak from 60–70 min (KCl #2) to 90–100 min (KCl #3) only in the wild‐type mouse striatum (Figure 2B, the yellow columns).

Our previous study showed that Prrt2‐KI mice have poorer rotarod performance than wild‐type mice 60 or 90 min after administration of 100–200 mg/kg L‐dopa (Figure 3E), 3 which overlapped with the time (90–100 min) until the effect of 100 mg/kg L‐dopa administration on striatal extracellular dopamine levels was observed in this study, suggesting a causal relationship between dopaminergic and dyskinetic phenotypes of the Prrt2 mutation. Thus, the basal ganglia and the cortico‐basal ganglia loop controlled by nigrostriatal dopaminergic system might be implicated in a motor symptom of PKD, although the cerebellum is still an important region for PKD pathology as previously reported. 6 In addition, we cannot exclude the effects of other neurotransmitters or other brain regions on Prrt2 mutation‐related dysregulation and their contribution to the motor phenotype; therefore, further studies are required.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that Prrt2 mutation induces striatal evoked dopamine release and exacerbates L‐dopa‐induced motor deficits via retention of repetitive activity‐dependent dopamine release in the mouse striatum. Considering that this mutation causes loss of Prrt2 expression, 3 Prrt2 may regulate the magnitude and frequency of activity‐dependent dopaminergic transmission to suppress excessive excitability of the motor loop and involuntary movements. Further studies are necessary to clarify the mechanism of Prrt2‐mediated regulation of extracellular dopamine levels and an action site of Prrt2 in motor circuits.
