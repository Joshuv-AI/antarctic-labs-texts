Ten years of European International Intensive Infectious Disease Courses (I3DC/IDEAL): long-term impact for undergraduate medical students
Authors: Federica I Wolf, Valentina Trapani, Katleen De Gaetano Donati, Erika Vlieghe, Ingólfur Johannessen, Claire Le Jeunne, Roberto Cauda, Caroline Charlier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAfter a decade of successful cooperation through a series of Erasmus+ projects, we sought to evaluate the long-term impact of undergraduate medical students’ participation in the Infectious Disease International I3DC/IDEAL course organized in 4 different European Institutions: Catholic University, Rome-Italy; Paris-Descartes, currently Université de Paris Cité-France, University of Edinburgh-UK, and Antwerp University-Belgium.MethodsWe invited 323 I3DC/IDEAL former students to complete an online questionnaire focused on how participation in these courses influenced: (a) study approach, (b) international experience, (c) European awareness, (d) choice of career path, (e) interest in Infectious Diseases.ResultsFormer I3DC/IDEAL students reported a positive impact of course participation across all surveyed domains. Overall satisfaction was high, with 90% rating the course as very good or excellent. The international dimension was particularly valued, with 78% rating exposure to international faculty and 70% rating European perspectives on disease management with the highest score, indicating strong effects on international experience and European awareness. Active-learning approaches were well received, with over 50% assigning the highest score to active student involvement and key methodologies (case-based, team-based, interdisciplinary learning, knowledge application, and clinical reasoning). As a result, 61% reported a very good to excellent impact on study approach, and 70% considered infectious disease knowledge highly relevant to future learning and professional activities. While only 20% reported a direct influence on career choice, 67% indicated increased motivation to pursue international educational opportunities.ConclusionsThe implementation of an international intensive infectious disease course for undergraduate medical students yields substantial long-term benefits. By integrating cutting-edge cultural content and expanding students’ awareness of a European dimension, this format is a highly valuable investment in the future of European medical education, contributing to its modernization and harmonization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08882-9.Keywords: global health, infectious diseases, internationalization, teacher exchange

Background

In the present work we sought to sum up the outcome of more than a decade of structured collaboration among four European academic institutions—Catholic University of Rome (Italy), Université de Paris Cité (formerly Paris-Descartes, France), the University of Edinburgh (UK), and the University of Antwerp (Belgium). Before presenting the results, it is important to frame them within the context of the educational pathway that generated them. Such long-term cooperation was designed to address the growing need for innovative, internationally oriented undergraduate medical education. Understanding this shared history and educational vision is essential to appropriately interpret the long-term outcomes reported below.

A group of European Institutions that have been Erasmus partners for many years decided to collaborate on enhancing the harmonization of medical studies across Europe. The CHarME project (Challenges in Harmonizing Medical Education in Europe, 2008-142388-LLP-1-DE-Erasmus-EMHE) started in 2009 and marked the beginning of a long series of successful projects which we continue to pursue. The subsequent project, MED-MOTION (2011–2014 517702-LLP-1-2011-1-BE-ERASMUS-ESMO), included several work packages, one of which focused on organizing international intensive courses; unlike traditional student mobility programs, these courses innovatively emphasized faculty mobility.

At that time, a survey conducted among Erasmus students guided the choice of the topic for the international courses. Infectious Diseases (ID) and Emergency surfaced as the most popular subjects. We were particularly interested in infectious diseases for several reasons [1, 2]. This discipline is delivered in European medical curricula in several formats: either as a single thematic course (e.g., in Italy or France), or fragmented across the different clinical disciplines (e.g., in Edinburgh), or distributed between undergraduate and postgraduate medical studies (e.g., in Antwerp). Following the students’ suggestions, we agreed that there was an urgent need to update and harmonise European ID medical curricula, and proposed integrating the basic concepts of ID into clinical scenarios, adopting a global health perspective [1, 3]. From 2014 onwards, we organized an annual Intensive Infectious Diseases Course (I3DC) for undergraduate medical students at each partner institution: Catholic University, Rome-Italy, Paris-Descartes, Paris-France and University of Edinburgh-UK and, at a later time, University of Antwerp-Belgium. Each institution contributed volunteer specialists who travelled across Europe to teach at partnering institutions. The course content (a 30-hour, one-week curriculum) was agreed upon by the international ID teaching team as the core knowledge required for all European medical students. This curriculum was updated annually to address emerging ID issues [1, 2]. Examples of course curricula can be found in Supplementary Material 1. Minor adjustments to the agreed program were necessary to accommodate local student curricula and levels (3rd year in Rome and Paris; 4th year in Antwerp and from the 2nd to the 5th year in Edinburgh). From 2016 to 2019, as a part of the first strategic partnership funding IDEAL (Infectious Disease European - African Learning”, 2016-1-FR01-KA203-024171), we included two institutions from Uganda; some teachers from these institutions participated in our courses and shared their experiences with major infectious disease epidemics.

Since the beginning of these projects, we aimed to provide students with a modern teaching approach. Leveraging the small group size (approximately 30 voluntary students per group), we organized interactive classes that incorporated case-based learning and role-play simulations [4].

From 2016 to 2018 students could additionally participate to a summer school that was organized at the ILUMENS simulation centre in Paris and focused on ID emergencies [5, 6]. Furthermore, students attending the I3DC/IDEAL course could apply for a two-week internship in ID wards at any partner’s local hospital (Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli in Rome, Hȏpital Cochin-Port Royal in Paris, Royal Infirmary in Edinburgh, and Universitair Ziekenhuis Antwerpen (UZA) in Antwerp).

During the third dedicated Erasmus+ project, we upgraded our teaching methodology by involving specialists from the Department of Medical Education of Utrecht Medical School - NL [7, 8] and Aston University – UK [4], who organized Teacher Training Courses during the IDEAL+ project (2019-1-FR01-KA203-063058). We also welcomed new partners: the Kapodistrian University of Athens - Greece, and Hamburg University – Germany, who continue to be part of the current programme (the third strategic partnership Erasmus programme, IDEALIter, 2023–2025, project 2022-1-FR01-KA220-HED-000086662).

We here present the results of a survey that was conducted among I3DC/IDEAL former students from different course editions to evaluate the long-term impact of their participation in the programme, in particular how a more integrated view of infectious diseases, beyond the standard curricula, helped them shaping their professional development.

Methods

Study population

Traceable students who participated in the programme between 2015 and 2022 were contacted, representing approximately one third of all course participants over the years. Students from Athens and Hamburg were excluded, as they attended the course exclusively online during the COVID-19 pandemic. Given the strong emphasis of the I3DC/IDEAL programme on in-person active-learning methodologies, small-group interaction, and international peer exchange, online participation was considered not fully comparable to on-site attendance and potentially associated with a different educational experience [9].

Students from Rome and Paris were homogenous in terms of year of study (3rd ), background and motivation. Indeed, students received academic credits upon course completion, as the I3DC/IDEAL course was considered a surrogate for the ID curricular course. In contrast, in Edinburgh, the course was optional, and included students from the 2nd to the 5th year of study. The Antwerp group began three years later, and integrated the course into their master’s degree in Medicine (4th year) as an optional course granting academic credits.

Survey

A survey was developed collaboratively by all partners. Former students were contacted via their institutional email and, when available, through personal email accounts to reduce the likelihood of undelivered invitations due to outdated contacts. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and the questionnaire was administered in English, consistent with the language of instruction of the course, through a dedicated online platform (www.Jotform.com). The survey was initially open for two weeks; two reminder emails were sent during this period, after which the survey window was extended for an additional two weeks to maximize response rate.

The complete questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Material 2. The questions were designed to explore the perceived long-term impact of course participation and focused on:

Responses to questions 1 to 4 were provided on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor; 2 = fair; 3 = good; 4 = very good; 5 = excellent). Questions 5 to 7 required a yes/no answer, while question 8 was open-ended.

Ethics

Student surveys were a crucial component of the assessment process for the intensive ID courses organized during the 4 Erasmus+ projects (2011–2014 517702-LLP-1-2011-1-BE-ERASMUS-ESMO, 2016-1-FR01-KA203-024171, 2019-1-FR01-KA203-063058, 2022-1-FR01-KA220-HED-000086662). Voluntary student participants were informed and consented to anonymous surveys evaluating each individual lecture and the overall course experience. This long-term survey is an integral part of the officially approved activities of the aforementioned Erasmus+ projects.

Results

Demographics

Based on the available records, all 323 traceable former students who had attended the course in person were contacted, while those who participated exclusively online were excluded. Eighty-nine answers were collected (89/323, 28%). Among the respondents, we had 60% (50) female and 40% (33) male students. As to the academic position, 22% (18) were still undergraduate, 24% (20) graduate and 54% (45) in post-graduate/specialization training. The majority of respondents attended the course in 2020, with a gradual decrease for the previous courses dating back to 2015. Most of the respondents were Italian (50) and French (33), followed by a smaller number of Belgian students [6]. Additionally, there were a few Erasmus students from other EU countries (Poland and Greece) and one student from Edinburgh.

Course evaluation

Figure 1 reports the overall appreciation of the course (question 1): 48% (40) participants attributed the maximal score to the course, while 42% (35) assigned a score of 4. Thus, collectively, 90% (75) of respondents expressed very good/excellent satisfaction with this teaching format.

Answers to question 1: How useful was the I3DC/IDEAL course? Results are colour-coded according to a 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor; 2 = fair; 3 = good; 4 = very good; 5 = excellent)

Next, we asked feedback on specific aspects of the course (Question 1, a-j), as reported in Fig. 2. The most valued aspects of the course were the exposure to international teachers and diverse European perspectives on disease management and treatment protocols, as indicated by 78% (65) and 70% (58) of respondents, respectively, who rated these aspects with a score of 5 (Fig. 2h and j).

Appreciation of specific aspects of the I3DC/IDEAL course (Question 1a-j, see Supplementary Material 2). Results are colour-coded according to a 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor; 2 = fair; 3 = good; 4 = very good; 5 = excellent)

Active student involvement in the learning process also aligned with student preferences, with 63% (52) of students assigning a grade of 5 (Fig. 2c). In this context, various teaching approaches, including case-based learning, team-based learning, interdisciplinary learning, knowledge application, and clinical reasoning, were well-received, with over 50% of respondents rating them with a score of 5 (Fig. 2f, e, j, b, and g, respectively). Regarding knowledge acquisition in infectious diseases, this was the only domain in which the majority of respondents assigned a score of 4 rather than 5: 43% (36) rated it 4, compared with 38% (32) who assigned the highest score (Fig. 2a). This finding likely reflects the intentionally condensed format of the I3DC/IDEAL course (a 30-hour, one-week intensive course), which prioritizes depth of understanding, clinical reasoning, and perspective over the sheer quantity of information typically delivered in longer curricular formats (e.g. ≥ 60 h in Rome). Nevertheless, overall satisfaction with knowledge acquisition remained high, with 81% rating it 4 or 5. Importantly, as shown in Fig. 2d, most students reported an increased awareness of the relevance of ID, with 57% (47) assigning a score of 5 and 28% (23) a score of 4.

Medical training

As depicted in Figs. 3a and 25% (21) of respondents rated the course’s impact on their subsequent study approach as a 5, 36% (30) as a 4, and 28% (23) as a 3 (question 2). Conversely, Fig. 3b reveals that 70% (58) of respondents rated the relevance of infectious disease knowledge to their future learning and professional activities (question 4) as a 4 or 5, indicating its significant perceived value several years after course completion.

Answers to (a) Question 2: Did the course impact on your subsequent study approach? and (b) Question 4: How relevant are infectious diseases in your current medical activity? Results are colour-coded according to a 5-point Likert scale (1 = poor; 2 = fair; 3 = good; 4 = very good; 5 = excellent)

Communication skills

69% (57) respondents declared that the course enhanced their communication skills at various levels (with students, colleagues and patients) (Fig. 4a, question 5). Nearly half of the respondents maintained contact with their I3DC/IDEAL student group, engaging in professional and cultural exchange (Fig. 4b, question 7).

Answers to (a) Question 5: Did this experience improve your communication skills? and (b) Question 7: Did you keep contacts with I3DC/IDEAL participants?

Career path

Only 20% (17) of students affirmed that the course had a significant impact (score 4 or 5) on the choice of their medical career (Fig. 5, question 3). However, 67% (56) of respondents indicated that the course encouraged them to pursue further international opportunities, broadening their perspective on European medical education (question 6, not shown).

Additional information

The final question in the survey (question 8) invited students to provide additional comments and suggestions through an open-ended response. These answers were not subjected to formal qualitative analysis; rather, we report here the most recurrent and salient themes of appreciation. All responses are freely available in the Zenodo repository [10.5281/zenodo.14732878].

Overall, students expressed strong appreciation for the commitment and dedication of the organizers and teaching staff. They highlighted their enthusiasm for the topics addressed and the engagement fostered by active-learning approaches. Participants who were involved in complementary activities, such as summer schools and internships, particularly valued the opportunity to interact with peers from different countries. The international dimension of the I3DC/IDEAL course was frequently highlighted as a unique opportunity to experience prestigious European medical institutions, and to gain insight into diverse different curricula and teaching approaches.

Discussion

Based on our survey, the international Intensive Infectious Diseases Course I3DC/IDEAL was highly appreciated by undergraduate students from three European Universities: Catholic University Rome, Paris Descartes/Cité, and Antwerp University. The majority of respondents were Italian and French students. Without performing any formal comparison between institutions, we note that in both Italy and France the course was integrated at a similar stage of the medical curriculum (third year) and was officially recognized as a curricular activity. This relative homogeneity in academic level and curricular framework may have facilitated student engagement and could partly explain the higher response rate observed in these groups.

The most successful aspect was internationalization, driven by the participation of European teachers, and the students’ exposure to innovative active-learning approaches. Students also valued the opportunity to “apply knowledge” through team work and interaction in an international environment. This course broadened their perspective on medical practice across Europe, inspiring them to seek further international exploration.

In contrast to previous surveys conducted immediately after each individual course, this study was specifically aimed to assess student feedback on long-term outcomes (at a later stage of their medical training). While the delay between course implementation and collection of the students’ feedback may have affected response rates, it allowed us to gauge the enduring impact of the unique I3DC/IDEAL course format compared to more traditional teaching approaches.

The course provided students with a broader and more integrated approach to ID, by including topics such as emerging infections, vaccinology, travel medicine and antimicrobial stewardship [10]. Such strategy heightened their awareness of global health issues [11]. By focussing on simulations of novel ID epidemics management, we anticipated the challenges that would arise during the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic amply underscored the need for uniform preparedness to deal with ID emergencies, which requires a homogeneous core curriculum in different European countries. In our understanding, the I3DC/IDEAL course, as designed and implemented by our team over the past decade, directly addresses these aspects. This is a significant and enduring aspect of the course, reflected in the overall positive feedback that confirmed the effectiveness of our innovative and timely approach. To our great gratification, the I3DC/IDEAL experience inspired some students to pursue specialization in Infectious Diseases.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the proportion of respondents represents only a small percentage of the total number of former I3DC/IDEAL students. As participation was voluntary, respondents are likely to represent a subgroup that was already more motivated, open-minded, and positively inclined toward international and innovative educational experiences, introducing a potential self-selection bias. Second, the survey relied on self-reported outcomes and did not include objective metrics to quantify the actual long-term impact of course participation on academic performance, career trajectories, or professional behaviors. While this limits the ability to draw causal inferences, self-reported data still provide valuable insight into perceived impact, attitudes, and reflections over time, particularly when assessing educational experiences whose effects are often qualitative and multifactorial. Such perceptions are especially relevant in evaluating outcomes related to awareness, motivation, and professional orientation, which are not easily captured through quantitative measures. Finally, we acknowledge that a major limitation of this course is its sustainability. Involving a group of at least five international teachers per course session is financially demanding and, in our case, teachers received just travel reimbursement. Nevertheless, we firmly believe that engaging international teachers is a crucial step that every European institution should undertake.

Conclusions

Overall, the results of our long-term survey demonstrate that the implementation of an international intensive infectious disease course for undergraduate medical students is highly beneficial, both for its cutting-edge cultural contents and for widening student’s awareness of a European medical dimension. In addition, the flexible design of the proposed course enables it to be readily adapted to a wide range of disciplines, contributing to modernize and harmonize European medical education in different contexts.
