Cinematic rendering of penetrating cardiac injury
Authors: Elias Lugo-Fagundo, Taha M Ahmed, Connor W Smith, Elliot K Fishman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough rare, penetrating cardiac injury (PCI) is highly lethal, with survival rates reported to be as low as 20%. Mechanism of injury, anatomic involvement, and hemodynamic stability are all key determinants in patient outcomes. While unstable patients require immediate surgical intervention, computed tomography has emerged as the preferred diagnostic modality in hemodynamically stable individuals, given its ability to efficiently detect foreign bodies, hemopericardium, and other associated complications. Furthermore, a recently developed photorealistic post-processing technique, cinematic rendering, has shown promise due to its enhanced visualization of myocardial involvement and guidance of management and preoperative planning. In this article, we report the case of a 29-year-old male who presented to the emergency department due to a self-inflicted stabbing PCI. We focus on the critical role of computed tomography in evaluating PCIs and introduce cinematic rendering as a potential valuable adjunct to preoperative planning.Keywords: Cardiac, Cinematic rendering, Computed tomography, Penetrating, Trauma

Introduction

Penetrating cardiac injury (PCI), although infrequent, carries substantial mortality, as reported survival rates fall below 20% [1,2]. Stab wounds and gunshot wounds (GSW) represent the most common mechanism of trauma, with most injuries occurring within the “cardiac box,” an anatomical region at high risk for cardiac and great vessel involvement [3]. Clinical presentation guides initial management: hemodynamically unstable patients require immediate surgical intervention, while hemodynamically stable patients undergo imaging to facilitate proper visualization and guide further intervention. Focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) and CT, more specifically electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated cardiac CT, have become standard modalities for the prompt diagnosis of cardiac injury in PCIs [1]. CT is recommended as the initial imaging modality for delineating foreign bodies, detecting hemopericardium, and identifying complications such as cardiac tamponade [1]. CR, a novel photorealistic post-processing technique [4], may be particularly valuable in cases of PCI, where detailed assessment of the trajectory and myocardial involvement is critical for operative strategy and risk assessment. Given the poor prognosis and high mortality rates associated with PCIs, prompt and adequate management, whether surgical intervention or diagnostic imaging, is crucial to patient outcomes. In this article, we present the case of a 29-year-old male who presented to the emergency department (ED) following a self-inflicted PCI and discuss the imaging findings.

Case report

A 29-year-old man with a history of major depressive disorder (MDD) presented following a self-inflicted PCI with a sewing needle to the chest. On arrival, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed a puncture wound on the anterior left chest wall, and a FAST exam was negative. Cardiac CT angiography with prospective ECG-gating demonstrated a linear foreign body coursing in the myocardium of the left ventricle (Fig. 1). Subsequent 3-dimensional (3D) CR reconstruction performed for preoperative evaluation of the needle’s orientation offered enhanced visual detail, demonstrating the needle extending through the left ventricle and into the chest wall. A small mediastinal hematoma and a left hemothorax with no active bleed were also appreciated. The patient underwent median sternotomy with foreign body removal and left ventricle repair. Intraoperative findings were consistent with preoperative CT imaging and CR revealing the penetrating needle in the anterolateral aspect of the left ventricle. Mediastinal bruising and hematoma were also appreciated with no evidence of arterial damage or active extravasation, corroborating preoperative imaging findings. The patient’s postoperative course was unremarkable, and he was subsequently discharged to the psychiatry unit for ongoing management of MDD with psychotic features.

A 29-year-old man with left chest wall PCI. Intravenous contrast-enhanced axial arterial phase CT image (A) demonstrates a 3 cm linear metallic foreign body (solid arrow) traversing from the left anterior chest wall, penetrating the intercostal musculature, and extending into the anterolateral wall of the left ventricular myocardium. The proximal tip of the foreign body is located within the subcutaneous fat of the chest wall, while the distal tip projects into the pericardial space. There is a small adjacent hemopericardium (dashed arrow), without evidence of active extravasation or cardiac tamponade. Axial (B), coronal (C) and sagittal oblique (D) cinematic rendered IV contrast-enhanced arterial phase images demonstrate the linear foreign body (arrow) coursing through the left ventricle and abutting the ventricular cavity. Cinematic rendering provided enhanced definition and delineation of the foreign body's relationship to myocardial structures compared with standard CT imaging.

Discussion

Here, we review a case of a self-inflicted PCI. With an overall survival rate as low as 19%, most deaths related to PCI occur at the scene of injury, and as many as 50%-90% of patients succumb to their injuries before reaching a hospital [2,5]. Unfortunately, despite recent advances in trauma care, of those who manage to arrive at a trauma center alive, only 40% survive [2].

Patient outcomes are driven by the mechanism of injury, anatomic involvement, and, most importantly, hemodynamic stability. Young males are the most common victims of PCIs, with stab wounds and gunshot wounds being the most common sources of injury [2]. When considering the wounding instrument, guns are associated with mortality rates as high as 90% compared to stabbings, which range between 16%-67% [2]. Furthermore, most PCIs occur within the “cardiac box,” referring to the area bounded superiorly by the clavicles, laterally by the midclavicular lines, and inferiorly by the costal margins [3]. Trauma to this area increases the probability of penetrating injuries to the pericardium or cardiac chambers, as seen in the presented case. More specifically, although both ventricles are equally susceptible to injury, the right ventricle is most frequently the site of entry due to its anterior location, accounting for 62% of cases compared with 38% involving the left ventricle [1]. However, as demonstrated in a study by Sugg et al. [6], penetrating injuries originating in the left ventricle are far more lethal, with only 2% of patients reaching the hospital alive.

Ultimately, an individual's hemodynamic status after suffering a PCI is crucial to their prognosis. Hemodynamically unstable patients present due to either active bleeding or cardiac tamponade, prompting immediate surgical intervention. Conversely, if a patient is hemodynamically stable, imaging allows for further evaluation and more informed subsequent intervention. As illustrated by this case, stable patients with a PCI and a negative FAST are best evaluated with CT, which provides a superior spatial, contrast, and temporal resolution, enabling rapid and accurate evaluation of the entire chest—including the heart, pericardium, and great vessels [1,6]. In practice, ECG-gated cardiac CT is preferred when evaluating suspected PCIs given its capability to minimize motion artifact and optimize temporal and spatial resolution, thereby enhancing the characterization of myocardial, valvular, and coronary structures [1]. This technique also facilitates the visualization and detailed assessment of posttraumatic complications, including cardiac pseudoaneurysms and ventricular septal defects, by providing precise anatomic detail and, subsequently, assisting operative planning. CT is also highly sensitive for detecting cardiac herniation, pericardial and myocardial laceration, pleural and pericardial effusions, pneumopericardium, and pneumothorax [1,6,7]. Additionally, it can delineate the wound path in penetrating trauma, aiding therapeutic decision-making and revealing retained foreign bodies [7].

As illustrated in the image findings above, cinematic rendering is a useful adjunct in the diagnosis, management, and preoperative planning of PCI. CR, a relatively novel post-processing technique that enables photorealistic visualization of anatomic structures by simulating complex light interactions, can be particularly valuable in this setting [4]. Beyond reproducing lifelike visualizations, CR’s ability to depict large volumetric datasets can be advantageous in PCI, where the course of the piercing object in relation to coronary arteries, cardiac chambers, and great vessels may otherwise be underappreciated. Furthermore, these reconstructions enhance the detection of complex cardiac anatomy including coronary anomalies, coronary aneurysms, and vessel irregularity caused by traumatic vascular injuries [4]. As also depicted in this case, CR enables detailed visualizations of the injury and any surrounding structures even in the context of extensive artifact from metallic foreign bodies due to stabbings or GSWs [8]. Consequently, CR’s ability to enhance depth perception, improve tissue differentiation, and more clearly depict extracardiac structures holds promise for assessing wound trajectory and definitively assessing myocardial involvement—elements critical for operative strategy and risk stratification.

Conclusion

Penetrating cardiac injuries, while uncommon, demand timely and accurate assessment to potentiate lifesaving management. This case illustrates that in hemodynamically stable PCI patients, ECG-gated CT provides critical anatomic detail by delineating foreign body trajectory, hemopericardium, and associated injuries. Cinematic rendering offered enhanced, photorealistic visualization of the needle’s relationship to the left ventricular myocardium and surrounding structures, augmenting CT findings and guiding surgical strategy. Integration of CR as an adjunct to conventional CT may improve preoperative risk assessment and communication among surgical teams, particularly in complex or ambiguous cases. However, further study is required to quantify the added diagnostic and clinical value of CR in penetrating cardiac trauma and to standardize protocol for its routine use.
