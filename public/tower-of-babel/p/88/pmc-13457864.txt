Co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 promotes clear cell renal cell carcinoma progression through LIMK1-mediated cytoskeletal reorganization
Authors: Shotaro Sakka, Satoshi Nitta, Shuya Kandori, Reo Takahashi, Shuhei Suzuki, Kazuki Hamada, Kozaburo Tanuma, Masanobu Shiga, Yoshiyuki Nagumo, Hiromitsu Negoro, Bryan J. Mathis, Farhana Ferdousi, Hiroko Isoda, Takashi Matsuzaka, Hitoshi Shimano, Hiroyuki Nishiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) frequently exhibits dysregulated lipid metabolism yet the contribution of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) elongation to malignant phenotypes remains incompletely defined. Because PUFA-elongation enzymes ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 are highly expressed in ccRCC, we investigated the clinical and functional significance of their co-overexpression. Using TCGA-KIRC data and clinical ccRCC specimens, we assessed ELOVL2/ELOVL5 expression and associations with clinicopathological features and survival. Functional studies using siRNA-mediated knockdown in renal cancer cell lines demonstrated that dual knockdown markedly suppressed proliferation, invasion, and invadopodia formation. Transcriptomic profiling and pathway analyses indicated that dual knockdown downregulated actin filament–related processes and identified LIMK1 as a candidate mediator. LIMK1 knockdown phenocopied the effects on proliferation, invasion, and invadopodia. These findings link PUFA-elongation programs to LIMK1-associated cytoskeletal remodeling in ccRCC and suggest that the ELOVL2/ELOVL5–LIMK1 axis may represent a therapeutic vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55807-z.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common type of kidney cancer, accounting for more than 90% of all kidney malignancies, and is most commonly diagnosed as clear cell RCC (ccRCC), followed by papillary RCC (pRCC) and chromophobe RCC (chRCC)1. Current treatment options include surgery, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and possible radiation therapy1,2. The current gold standard for localized RCC is surgical resection but advanced and metastatic cases often require a combination of systemic therapies. While targeted therapies (such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors [TKIs], mammalian target of rapamycin [mTOR] inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [ICIs]) have improved objective response and survival rates, complete remission remains rare1,3.

Lipid metabolism has recently gained attention4 since, in addition to their structural role in cell membranes, lipid moieties may function as secondary messengers and energy sources5, rendering lipid metabolism a promising target for cancer therapy4. Fatty acids (FAs) serve as the primary lipid backbone, which is then modified in diverse ways to increase functionality, including with triglycerides, sphingolipids, and phospholipids6. FAs stabilize cellular and organelle membranes, regulate signaling pathways, and remodel the tumor microenvironment. Alterations in FA metabolism, especially the generation of immune-interactive and polyunsaturated lipid derivatives, are critical for the growth, division and survival of cancer cells7. FA length is an important functional indicator and the rate-limiting step in de novo long-chain fatty acid (LC-FA) synthesis is regulated by a family of conserved elongation of very long–chain fatty acid enzymes (ELOVLs), of which seven are currently recognized6. Among ELOVLs, ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 are involved in elongation of the polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) known to be abundant in RCC8.

We have previously shown that ELOVL2 inhibits apoptosis and promotes cellular proliferation by regulating lipid metabolism in RCC9. Moreover, ELOVL5 has also been shown to promote cell proliferation and invasion by regulating lipid metabolism in ccRCC10. Because ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 participate in sequential and partially overlapping steps of PUFA elongation (and because both are highly expressed in ccRCC), we hypothesized that their coordinated upregulation may cooperatively promote malignant phenotypes. However, the significance of potential ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 co-upregulation of expression levels in ccRCC remains unclear.

In this study, we investigated the role of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 synergy in ccRCC progression and found that high co-expression results in poorer prognoses. We also found that the ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 in renal cancer cell lines reduces cellular proliferation and invasion. Furthermore, a bioinformatic analysis revealed that such co-ablation affects actin filament-based process through the downregulation of LIMK1. These data suggest that the coordination of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 may contribute to cancer progression via cytoskeletal modification.

Results

Co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 is associated with poor prognoses in clear cell renal cell carcinoma

We first evaluated the association between ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 mRNA expression in the TCGA database (KIRC cohort), finding weakly positive correlation in ccRCC tissues (Spearman r = 0.267, P < 0.0001;Fig. 1A). Next, co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 at the protein level were observed in 55% of cases in our cohort (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S1). In addition, mRNA expression levels of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 were significantly elevated in RCC cell lines compared to 293T cells (Fig. 1C).

We next evaluated clinical significance of the co-overexpression tumors using the TCGA database (KIRC cohort). Co-overexpression was associated with higher T and pathological stages (Table 1 and Table S1). As shown in Fig. 1D, overall survival (OS) was significantly shorter in ccRCC patients with co-overexpression tumors among four groups (ELOVL2-High/ELOVL5-High vs. ELOVL2-Low/ELOVL5-Low; P = 0.000348). Although Kaplan-Meier analysis in our institutional cohort did not reach statistical significance, patients with high ELOVL2/ELOVL5 expression tended to have poorer prognoses (Fig. S2), supporting the same direction of association observed in the TCGA cohort. These results suggest that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 is associated with ccRCC progression.

The ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 alters fatty acid composition in renal cancer cell lines

To investigate whether ELOVL2 and/or ELOVL5 regulates proliferative and invasive potential, we first performed knockdown experiments using siRNAs specific for renal cancer cell lines and found that the mRNA expression levels of ELOVL2 and/or ELOVL5 were significantly decreased in both A498 and 786-O cells (Fig. 2A, B). To further investigate changes in lipid metabolism following these siRNA knockdowns, fatty acid levels in phospholipids were compared among A498 siControl cells, A498 siELOVL2 cells, A498 siELOVL5 cells, and A498 siELOVL2 + ELOVL5 cells using GC/MS analysis. In A498 siELOVL2 + ELOVL5 cells, the levels of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, C20:5 n-3) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6 n-3) were decreased compared to A498 siControl and A498 siELOVL2 cells, but no differences were observed compared to A498 siELOVL5 cells (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the level of arachidonic acid (AA, C20:4 n-6) did not differ from A498 siControl and A498 siELOVL2 cells but increased compared to A498 siELOVL5 cells (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the levels of vaccenic acid (18:1 n-7) in A498 siELOVL2 + ELOVL5 cells were significantly decreased compared to A498 siControl and A498 siELOVL2 cells (Fig. 2E). Docosapentaenoic acid (DPA, C22:5 n-3, n-6) in A498 cells was not detected (Fig. 2C, D). These results suggest that the co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 does not simply elongate omega-3 and omega-6 PUFAs but affects complex and diverse aspects of FA metabolism in renal cancer cells.

The ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 more effectively inhibits cellular proliferation and invasion in renal cancer cell lines

Next, we used WST-8 and Matrigel invasion assays to investigate the effects of ELOVL2 and/or ELOVL5 knockdown in renal cancer cells. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, knockdown of ELOVL2 and/or ELOVL5 significantly inhibited cellular proliferation in vitro compared to controls. Furthermore, the knockdowns significantly inhibited cellular invasion (Fig. 3C, D). Interestingly, the ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 more significantly inhibited cellular proliferation and invasion in A498 and 786-O renal cancer cell lines. These results support that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 promotes ccRCC progression.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 is associated with the regulation of actin filament in renal cancer cells

To examine the mechanism of co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 in promoting cellular proliferation and invasion of ccRCC, we compared the gene expression profile of A498/siControl, A498/siELOVL2, A498/siELOVL5, and A498/siELOVL2 + ELOVL5 cells using RNA sequencing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as follows: 410 DEGs between A498/siControl and A498/siELOVL2 cells, 365 DEGs between A498/siControl and A498/siELOVL5 cells, and 484 DEGs between A498/siControl and A498/siELOVL2 + ELOVL5 cells. Among these, 272 genes were uniquely altered in siELOVL2 + ELOVL5, 208 in siELOVL2, and 261 in siELOVL5, while 34 DEGs were commonly shared across all three conditions (Fig. 4A). Each set of DEGs was divided into upregulated and downregulated genes; their overlap is shown in Fig. 4B.

GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed distinct biological processes associated with the knockdown of ELOVL2, ELOVL5, or both in A498 cells. The combined ablation of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 (ELOVL2 + 5) markedly enriched pathways related to cytoskeletal regulation, including regulation of actin filament–based processes, actin cytoskeleton organization, and actin filament organization, as well as processes linked to angiogenesis and vasculature development. ELOVL2 knockdown alone also showed significant enrichment of pathways involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell migration, motility, and protein phosphorylation, whereas ELOVL5 knockdown displayed fewer and less significant enrichments. These findings suggest that ELOVL2, particularly when combined with ELOVL5, exerts a stronger influence on cytoskeleton-dependent processes than ELOVL2 knockdown alone, which may contribute to enhanced cell motility, invasion, and vascular development in ccRCC.

Consistent with these results, KEGG pathway analysis revealed downregulation of Ras and Rap1 signaling pathways following the ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 (Fig. 4D).

Since actin filaments play an important role in cell shape, motility, interaction with the extracellular matrix, and proliferation/invasion, these results indicate that dynamic reorganization and maintenance of the cytoskeletal filament network relies on ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 co-expression, at least in part through the Ras/Rap1 pathway11–18.

LIMK1 is a candidate gene that promotes cellular proliferation and invasion by co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 in clear cell renal cell carcinoma

Based on the above results, we focused on upstream regulation of actin filaments in the GOBP terms, including 12 total actin filament regulation genes (Fig. 5A), and screened candidate genes as shown in Fig. 5B. First, we examined the association between the gene expression level and prognosis using the TCGA dataset (KIRC cohort). Higher LIMK1, IL1A, and SIGLEC15 gene expression levels were significantly associated with poor prognosis in ccRCC patients (Fig. 5B and C).

Next, we examined alteration effects by ELOVL2 and/or ELOVL5 knockdown of these 3 genes. PCR analysis identified LIMK1 as the gene whose expression was more significantly downregulated by the ablation of both ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S1). Protein levels were also decreased by co-ablation in two renal cancer cell lines, A498 and 786-O (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 regulate actin filaments through the regulation of LIMK1 at the transcript level to modulate ccRCC progression.

LIMK1 is associated with clear cell renal cell carcinoma proliferation and invasion

To evaluate the roles of LIMK1 in renal cancer cells, we performed siRNA-mediated LIMK1 knockdown experiments using A498 and 786-O cell lines, confirming that LIMK1 mRNA expression levels were significantly decreased in both cell lines (Fig. 6A). While WST-8 assays showed a decrease in cellular proliferation after LIMK1 knockdown (Fig. 6B, C), Matrigel invasion assays using these siRNA-treated cells also showed significant inhibition of cell invasion in both cell lines (Fig. 6D, E). Interestingly, invadopodia formation was significantly suppressed in LIMK1-knockdown cells (Fig. 6F) and, furthermore, we observed a trend toward suppression of invadopodia formation by knockdown of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 in A498 cells (Fig. 6G).

We next analyzed the association between LIMK1 gene expression levels and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stages to clarify the clinical significance of LIMK1 in ccRCC by using the TCGA KIRC cohort. Importantly, LIMK1 expression levels were higher in cases with locally advanced disease, lymph node metastasis, metastatic disease, and higher clinical stage (Fig. 6H). Taken together, these results suggest that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 promotes cellular invasion and proliferation through the regulation of LIMK1 in ccRCC.

Discussion

The present study has demonstrated that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 contributes to ccRCC progression via cellular proliferation and invasion, at least in part through regulating LIMK1. Our findings provide novel and important insights into therapeutic intervention for ccRCC, focusing on lipid metabolism.

ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 are key enzymes in the elongation of PUFAs6. ELOVL2 elongates eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and arachidonic acid (AA) to docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and omega-6 docosapentaenoic acid, respectively, while ELOVL5 elongates linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid to AA and EPA6. These membrane-bound enzymes are predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum, where they mediate PUFA elongation6. We previously have shown that ELOVL2 promotes cellular proliferation through the regulation of apoptosis by mediating lipid metabolism in RCC9 and also demonstrated that ELOVL5 promotes both cellular proliferation and invasion through the regulation of CCL2 by AKT phosphorylation in RCC10. However, mechanisms of coordination between ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 remained unclear until the current study.

Here, we reveal that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 promotes cellular invasion and proliferation through the regulation of LIMK1 mRNA expression in renal cancer cells. LIMK1 (LIM domain kinase 1) is a protein kinase member of the Rho/ROCK/LIMK/cofilin signaling pathway that directly regulates actin cytoskeletal dynamics by inactivating cofilin through phosphorylation to promote cellular migration and invasion via actin filament stabilization and stress fiber formation19,20. Moreover, LIMK1 is also involved in the regulation of cellular proliferation, as evidenced by its effects on the cell cycle21, inhibition of cell apoptosis22, and regulation of microtubule dynamics23. Importantly, LIMK1 is upregulated in a variety of cancers, including breast cancer24, gastric cancer25, prostate cancer21, colon cancer26, lung cancer27, oral carcinoma28 and malignant melanoma29, suggesting global contributions to tumor cell invasion, growth, and metastasis. Similarly, the present study has shown that LIMK1 contributes to cellular invasion and proliferation in ccRCC. However, because these phenotypic findings were derived from in vitro experiments using renal cancer cell lines, the extent to which the ELOVL2/ELOVL5-LIMK1 axis contributes to tumor progression in vivo remains unclear. Therefore, further in vivo validation will be needed to determine the biological significance of this axis in the context of tumor growth and metastasis.

Further supporting the role of PUFAs in cytoskeletal regulation, several reports have highlighted their effects on actin dynamics30,31. For example, Yamada et al. reported that EPA modulates actin polymerization by decreasing the levels of F-actin, phosphorylated cofilin (p-cofilin), and Rab27a in melanoma cells30. According to Witt et al., in valvular interstitial cells (VICs) isolated from porcine aortic valves, DHA increases the G/F-actin ratio through the reduction of active RhoA and p-cofilin levels, thereby affecting cytoskeletal dynamics31. Additionally, cofilin phosphorylation has been shown to play a crucial role in the formation and maturation of invadopodia, key structures involved in cancer cell invasion. Bravo-Cordero et al. demonstrated that RhoC activation regulates cofilin phosphorylation, leading to dynamic actin remodeling within invadopodia, a process that facilitates actin filament reorganization, promoting the formation of invasive protrusions that enable cancer cells to degrade extracellular matrix via matrix metalloproteases (MMP)32,33. These findings support our hypothesis that the elongation reaction of n-3 PUFAs promotes cytoskeletal reorganization and influences cellular invasion capacity through the formation of MMP-enriched invadopodia. However, the mechanistic link between PUFA alterations and LIMK1-associated cytoskeletal remodeling remains speculative and will require further experimental validation.

EPA and DHA levels in the ELOVL2/5 double-knockdown group were not significantly different from those in the ELOVL5 single-knockdown group, despite stronger suppression of proliferation and invasion in the double-knockdown condition in the present study. This discrepancy raises the possibility that these ELOVLs may contribute to ccRCC progression not only through the increase of intracellular PUFAs but also through remodeling of the fatty-acyl composition of membrane phospholipids. Gimple et al. demonstrated that ELOVL2 depletion altered the composition of membrane phospholipids, disrupted membrane structural integrity, and diminished EGFR signaling pathways by regulating fatty acid elongation in glioblastoma34. Consistent with this possibility, our results showed reduced receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-related signaling pathways (RAS signaling, Rap1 signaling, ErbB signaling, and MAPK signaling) in the ELOVL2/5 double-knockdown condition (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that the role of these ELOVLs in cancer may not be explained solely by changes in intracellular PUFA levels.

The clinical relevance of our findings is supported by the strong association between high ELOVL2/ELOVL5 expression and poor survival outcomes in RCC patients. The significant reduction in RCC cell invasion upon LIMK1 inhibition further suggests that targeting the ELOVL2/ELOVL5-LIMK1 axis may be a promising therapeutic strategy for RCC. In fact, multiple small-molecule approaches targeting LIM kinases have been developed and reviewed for cancer therapy35. Given that current treatments for unresectable or metastatic RCC have limited efficacy, our study provides a rationale for the development of novel therapies targeting lipid metabolism pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals that co-overexpression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 play a critical role in ccRCC progression, influencing cytoskeletal dynamics through regulating lipid metabolism. Moreover, we identified LIMK1 as the key downstream effector that controls cytoskeletal reorganization and thereby promotes proliferation and invasion in renal cancer cells. Targeting the ELOVL2/ELOVL5-LIMK1 pathway may lead to the development of novel therapeutic strategies in the future.

Materials and methods

Patients and RCC samples

Tumor specimens were collected from 40 patients who underwent either radical nephrectomy or partial nephrectomy at the University of Tsukuba Hospital. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval numbers: H22-511, R04-261). Written, informed consent was obtained from all patients. Pathological stages were determined according to the Union for International Cancer Control TNM staging system36.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described37. Briefly, formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded specimens were cut into 4-µm sections, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using a microwave in citric acid buffer for 21 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the immunohistochemical reaction was visualized using Histofine Simple Stain MAX PO secondary antibody (Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) and diaminobenzidine as chromogen. Immunohistochemical expression of ELOVL2 and ELOVL5 was semi-quantitatively classified as high or low according to staining intensity relative to the corresponding normal renal tissue, which served as an internal control. Cases showing stronger staining intensity than the corresponding normal tissue were classified as high expression, whereas the others were classified as low expression. Primary antibodies are listed in Table S2.

Cell lines and cell cultures

A498, 786-O, ACHN and Caki-1 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) while 293T (RCB2202) cell lines were purchased from RIKEN BioResource Center. A498, 786-O, ACHN and Caki-1 cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37℃ in a 5% humidified CO2 atmosphere. The 293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% humidified CO2 atmosphere.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously9,38. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S2. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse, anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) were used at 1:10,000. Western blots were visualized with ImmunoStar Zeta (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) using a Fujifilm LAS-4000 imager (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). For western blot analysis after siRNA transfection, protein lysates were collected 72 h after transfection unless otherwise stated. β-actin was used as an internal control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, normalized to β-actin, and expressed relative to the control group.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from renal cancer cells using TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and, after purification, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (cat.# 4368814; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Gene expression levels were measured using a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Fast SYBR-Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR cycling conditions were as follows: an initial hold at 95℃ for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95℃ for 3 s and 60℃ for 30 s, with a final melting curve analysis from 95℃ for 15 s to 60℃ for 1 min. Hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT1) was used as an internal control. All primer sequences are shown in Table S3. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method. Cells were harvested for RT-qPCR analysis 48 h after siRNA transfection unless otherwise stated.

RNA interference

Cells were transfected with 25 nmol/L of ON-TARGETplus Human ELOVL2 SMARTpool siRNA (L-009531-01-0005, Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA), 25 nmol/L of ON-TARGETplus Human ELOVL5 SMARTpool siRNA (L-009260-01-0005, Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA), 25 nmol/L of ON-TARGETplus Human LIMK1 SMARTpool siRNA (L-007730-00-0005, Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA), or 25 nmol/L of ON-TARGETplus non-targeting pool siRNA (D-001810-10-05, Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA) with Lipofectamine RNAiMax Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell lines affected by siRNA are presented with “si-” appended as a prefix. Cells were generally harvested 48 h after siRNA transfection for RT-qPCR and 72 h after transfection for western blotting and functional assays unless otherwise stated.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Mashiki, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 48 h after siRNA transfection, 1 × 10³ cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates and 10 µL of WST-8 was added at measurement. Optical density at 450 nm wavelength was measured after 1 h incubation.

Cell invasion assay

Cell invasion assays were performed using BioCoat Matrigel invasion chambers (#354480, Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 2 × 105 cells were seeded in each upper chamber 48 h after siRNA transfection. After 22 h, cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were removed by scraping, followed by fixation and staining. The number of invasive cells was counted under an Olympus BX50 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The assays were performed in triplicate and 5 fields were counted per transwell filter.

RNA sequencing

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted from renal cancer cells using TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Library preparation was performed using NucleoSpin® RNA Clean-up (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Düren, Germany) and library size distributions and concentrations were validated by a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The validated libraries were then sequenced at Tsukuba i-Laboratory LLP (Tsukuba, Japan) on a NextSeq500 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the 36-base paired-end read option.

RNA sequencing data were filtered and normalized using count sum plots showing the sum of read counts for each sample, box plots filtered for genes with low relative abundance, and low variance before normalization by Log2-CPM transformation and PCA plotting to reveal similarities and differences between samples before and after filtering and normalization.

CLC Genomics Workbench version 10.1.1 (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) was used to map, quantify, and normalize sequencing reads to the human reference genome using the quantile method. Differentially expressed gene analyses were performed using the Empirical Analysis of DGE tool, with the statistical threshold for DGEs set at a P value < 0.05 and an absolute fold-change > 2. Biological functions were evaluated by enrichment analyses of Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the online analysis platform Metascape39,40.

Bioinformatics analysis of gene expression

Clinical and RNA sequencing data for 530 primary tumors from RCC patients (KIRC) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) data portal41. The mRNA expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM). For survival analysis, the 530 patients were divided into four groups (low and high expression groups) based on the expression cutoff that maximized the survival difference between groups as determined by the lowest log-rank P value.

In the analysis of the correlation between the expression patterns of ELOVL2 plus ELOVL5 or LIMK1 and the clinicopathological characteristics of RCC, N stage data were not available for 274 patients, M stage data were not available for 32 patients, and pathological stage data were not available for 3 patients. Therefore, these patients were excluded from this analysis.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis of FA metabolites

Cells were collected 72 h after siRNA transfection for GC-MS analysis of fatty acid metabolites. Total lipids were extracted from the cells, and free fatty acids, neutral lipids, and phospholipids were fractionated using a solid phase extraction column. The fatty acid composition of the phospholipid fraction was then analyzed. Cells were washed with PBS and scraped into 300 µL of methanol containing 1% acetic acid, followed by sonication on ice. A mixture of chloroform: methanol: ethanol (1:2:2, v/v/v; 2 mL) and chloroform: methanol: ethanol: acetic acid (2:1:1:1, v/v/v/v; 100 µL) was added. After vortexing at room temperature, samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min. The organic layer was collected and phospholipids were separated using a PSA Bonded Silica solid-phase extraction column (Supelco). The phospholipid fraction was dried under reduced pressure. Extracted phospholipids were methylated with 2.5% sulfuric acid in methanol to form fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs), which were extracted with n-hexane and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) using a Clarus SQ 8 GC/MS system (PerkinElmer Japan, Kanagawa, Japan).

Invadopodia assay

QCM™ Gelatin Invadopodia Assays were performed and quantified according to manufacturer instructions (ECM670; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). In each experiment, 2 × 104 A498 cells were plated per well of an 8-well chamber slide 48 h after siRNA transfection. The number of invadopodia formed after 24 h was then quantified from three independent experiments (20 cells per experiment = 60 total cells per condition). F-actin co-localized with degradation was defined as invadopodia and only cells with at least one invadopodium were analyzed. Images of the stained cells were acquired with a fluorescence microscope BZ-X800L (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). DAPI staining was used only for nuclear visualization/cell identification and not for quantitative analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data from in vitro experiments are presented as mean ± SD. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected frequencies were small. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for comparisons among three or more groups. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test; Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons when appropriate. Correlations between two variables were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), JMP 14 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and R (version 4.2.3) as appropriate. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
