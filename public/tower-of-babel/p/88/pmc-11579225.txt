Pupillometry as a biomarker of postural control: Deep‐learning models reveal side‐specific pupillary responses to increased intensity of balance tasks
Authors: Jernej Rosker, Tinka Leskovec, Katja Tomazin, Ziva Majcen Rosker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPupillometry has been used in the studies of postural control to assess cognitive load during dual tasks, but its response to increased balance task intensity has not been investigated. Furthermore, it is unknown whether side‐specific changes in pupil diameter occur with more demanding balance tasks providing additional insights into postural control. The two aims of this study were to analyze differences in steady‐state pupil diameter between balance tasks with increased intensity and to determine whether there are side‐specific changes. Forty‐eight healthy subjects performed parallel and left and right one‐legged stances on a force plate with and without foam with right and left pupil diameters measured with a mobile infrared eye‐tracker. Differences between balance tasks in parameters (average pupil diameter of each eye, average of both pupil diameters and the difference between the left and right pupil diameter) were analyzed using a two‐way repeated measures analysis of variance, and deep learning neural network models were used to investigate how pupillometry predicted each balance task. The pupil diameter of the left eye, the average pupil diameter of both eyes and the difference in pupil diameters increased statistically significantly from simpler to more demanding balance tasks, with this being more pronounced for the left eye. The deep learning neural network models revealed side‐specific changes in pupil diameter with more demanding balance tasks. This study confirms pupillary responses to increased intensity of balance task and indicates side‐specific pupil responses that could be related to task‐specific involvement of higher levels of postural control.Keywords: balance task intensity, cognition, postural control, Pupillometry, tonic alertness

INTRODUCTION

Postural control is an important component affecting daily activities across different population groups. Factors such as properties of sensory drive (Maurer et al., 2006; Peterka & Loughlin, 2004) support surface characteristics (Büchel et al., 2021; Sozzi, Do, et al., 2022), biomechanical constraints of the human body (Sarabon et al., 2013; Wojciechowska‐Maszkowska & Borzucka, 2020), emotional states (Hall et al., 2023) and cognitive involvement (Mitra et al., 2022) have been identified to importantly influence postural balance. Cognition is of particular interest as daily balance tasks frequently involve cognitive resources, such as selective or divided attention during visual perception (Bonnet et al., 2022; Bonnet & Baudry, 2016), increased working memory load (Zhang et al., 2023) or inhibitory processes during the perception of conflicting stimuli (Wollesen et al., 2016).

In recent years, measurements of pupillary cross‐section oscillations (i.e., dilatations) have been applied to study involvement of cognitive processes during different locomotor and balance tasks (Hayashi et al., 2010; Kahya et al., 2018, 2022). More specifically, pupil dilatations have been related to increased activity of the locus coeruleus as well as superior colliculus and thus to the ascending norepinephrine excitatory system of the central nervous system, which drives higher‐level cognitive processes (Joshi & Gold, 2020; Peinkhofer et al., 2019).

To date studies investigating postural balance have primarily focused on studying dual‐task paradigms in healthy young adults (Kahya et al., 2018, 2022) and patients with Parkinson's disease (Kahya et al., 2021) for evaluation of cognitive involvement. These studies showed increased cognitive load solely as a consequence of covering one's eyes in young adults and even more so in older adults. However, they did not investigate pupillary responses to changes in the compliance of the support surface or foot position. This would be of interest as the latter two factors are frequently manipulated in research and clinical practice and lead to adaptations in cognitive processes involved in balance control (Bolton, 2015).

Studies investigating balance control using pupillometry analyzed the involvement of cognitive resources via rapid pupillary oscillations described by the index of cognitive activity (ICA) (Kahya et al., 2021, 2022). ICA has been associated with increased involvement of executive functions (Strauch et al., 2022) and phasic alertness (O'Bryan & Scolari, 2021), i.e., the ability to focus on a specific information (O'Bryan & Scolari, 2021). On the contrary, tonic alertness (i.e., changes in steady‐state pupil diameter) has been associated with the ability to attend to multiple information simultaneously (O'Bryan & Scolari, 2021). Moreover, it has been suggested that it relates to exploratory behavior, such as disengagement from inadequate sensory resources and the search for new, more appropriate information (Strauch et al., 2022). This is of particular interest to balance diagnostics as previous research has shown, that manipulating the compliance of the support surface or foot position during balance tasks leads to a reweighting of centrally processed sensory information and changes in the preparation of postural motor responses in the cerebral cortex, i.e., increased cognitive involvement (Büchel et al., 2021), which leads to increased body sway. Based on the other related studies confirming increased pupillary dilatation as a result of more involved cognitive functions during increased level of exerted force (Zénon et al., 2014), aerobic exertion (Hayashi et al., 2010) or a change in head's vertical posture (Pompeiano et al., 1991) it could be speculated, that changes in steady‐state pupil diameter respond to increased balance task intensity; however, this has not yet been investigated.

An important methodological discrepancy between studies investigating pupillometry during locomotor and postural tasks is the decision of which eye to use for further analysis. Some studies used the right eye (Kahya et al., 2018), whereas others used the average pupillometric response of both eyes (Saeedpour‐Parizi et al., 2020; White & French, 2017). This issue is of importance, as Serrien et al. (2006) reported on side‐specific cerebral hemispheres function during unilateral hand movement and stability control, possibly resulting in side‐specific locus coeruleus and consequently pupillometric responses (Cagiltay & Menekse Dalveren, 2019; Kim et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2018). Although there is still no direct evidence for the relationship between the side‐specific function of the cerebral hemispheres and pupillary responses, Nobukawa et al. (2021) reported side‐specific pupillary responses related to the degree of cognitive load. Moreover, Liu et al. (2017) described bilateral, ipsilateral and contralateral neural circuitry connecting the locus coeruleus, the Edinger Westphal nucleus and the pupillary muscle responsible that are responsible for pupillary constriction, These neural circuits showed asymmetric pupillary responses when activated by unilateral tonic activation of the locus coeruleus (Liu et al., 2017), which is related to the control of average pupil diameter (Strauch et al., 2022). Therefore, it could be expected that the left and right steady‐state pupillometric diameters would respond differently to changes in the intensity of the balance task and standing leg, but this has not yet been investigated.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze, whether the steady‐state pupillometric diameter relates to increased intensity of balance tasks based on the change in compliance of the support surface and the stance type. In addition, the response of each pupil was investigated separately to analyze whether there are side‐specific steady‐state pupillometric responses to changes in balance task intensity (e.g., stance type, standing leg and support surface compliance).

METHODS

Participants

For this study, 48 healthy subjects (31 female and 17 males; mean age 25.59 ± 2.71; average height 1.73 ± 0.87; mean weight 69.24 ± 11.84) were recruited who met the following inclusion criteria: age between 18 and 35 years and regular physical activity (i.e., low to moderate intensity exercise 2 times per week). Exclusion criteria were: use of glasses or contact lenses for vision correction, vestibular or neurological disorders, cardiorespiratory disorders, headaches, dizziness, musculoskeletal disorders or injuries in the last 3 years and the use of alcohol, sedatives, opioids and banned psychoactive substances in the last 48 h, as these substances could affect pupil responses (Laeng & Alnaes, 2019). The subjects were recruited among university staff, students and their friends.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later additions and approved by the institutional ethics committee (no. 13: 2020/2046). In addition, each participant gave written informed consent before participating in the study and could withdraw at any time.

Experimental design

All measurements were taken in a room with constant light measured with a lux meter (SDL400, Extech Instruments, Knoxville, USA) to ensure the accuracy of the pupil size measurements. The participants took part in the measurements individually, once and in random order. Participants performed six balance tasks in a random order. The tasks included (i) unilateral stances on both dominant and non‐dominant foot and (ii) parallel stance with feet positioned close one to another. All three stances were performed with their hands positioned on the hips while standing on two types of flat surface; firm surface and a 48 × 40 cm foam surface (Balance‐pad Elite Airex AG, 1956, Sins, Switzerland). Leg dominance was determined by asking each participant with which leg they would prefer to kick the ball. Each balance task was performed once for 60 s with a 2‐min break in between. During the balance tasks, subjects directed their gaze to a white dot located at an eye level and 2.20 m distance from the force plate. The dot was on a black wall to minimize the influence of luminance and surrounding events on pupillometric measures (Zhang et al., 2022).

An eye tracker (Tobii ProGlasses 2, Tobii, Danderyd, Sweden), with data acquisition frequency of 50 Hz was used to record changes in pupil size. The eye tracker was calibrated by having participants seat on a chair 1.5 m away from the calibration point at which they directed their gaze. The pupil size data was processed using Tobii Pro Lab software (Tobii Pro lab 1.145, Tobii, Danderyd, Sweden). The width of the pupil was averaged during each balance task using Matlab (R2021a, MathWorks Inc., Massachusetts, USA). Balance tasks were performed on a force plate (9260AA, Kistler Instruments AG, Winterthur, Switzerland). Data were recorded at 24 bits per channel by data acquisition system (5695B, Kistler Instruments AG, Winterthur, Switzerland) and analyzed with the software Kistler MARS (MARS 5.0, Kistler Instruments AG, Winterthur, Switzerland).

Data analysis

Pupil size was expressed as average pupil diameter during each balance trial, where instances of eyeblinks were interpolated using an average of three samples before the onset of a blink and average of three samples after blink cassation. Average pupil diameter was calculated for the left (D L), right (D R) and average of both eyes (D AVG). To additionally analyze differences between pupillary responses, difference between D L and D R was calculated (D DIFF).

Following parameters were calculated for each balance task: center‐of‐pressure (CoP) movement velocity (V tot), CoP movement velocity in medial‐lateral (V ml) and anterior–posterior (V ap) direction, average amplitude of CoP movement in medial‐lateral (A ml) and anterior–posterior (A ap) direction, average frequency of CoP movement in medial‐lateral (F ml) and anterior–posterior (F ap) direction.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Software (SPSS 21.0 Software, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables and expressed as mean and SDs. Normality of data distribution was analyzed using skewness and kurtosis as well as Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables that had a non‐normal distribution were examined and, if necessary, outliers were excluded to achieve normality of data distribution. Differences in individual pupil size and balance parameter in three different stances and two support surfaces were analyzed with a two‐way repeated measures analysis of variance. The effect size was expressed with a partial eta squared (ƞ 2). The ƞ 2 value was considered as; .01–.059 a small effect, .06–.14 a medium effect and more than .14 a large effect (Field, 2005). Dependent sample t test was used for individual pair‐wise comparisons. Cohen's d (d) was used to calculate the effect size for pairwise comparisons treated as; d ≥ 0.2 as small, d ≥ 0.5 as medium and d ≥ 0.8 as large (Lakens, 2013). Spearman correlation coefficient (r) was used to test for correlations between individual parameters of pupil diameter and CoP movement. The r was treated as no correlation for r < .3, small correlation for .3 < r < .5, medium correlation for .5 < r < .7 and high correlation for r > .7 (Field, 2023). To compensate for multiple comparisons in post‐hoc tests and correlation analysis, the p‐values were corrected using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance was accepted at 5% alpha error.

To study whether intensity of specific balance tasks could be predicted based on D L and D R or their secondary parameters D AVG and D DIFF, neural network based deep learning approach (Serghiou & Rough, 2023) was used (Orange 3.36, Ljubljana, Slovenia). Sample consisted of 252 datapoints (42 participants for each of 6 balance task). One hundred hidden lawyers, ReLu activation function, Adam solver and regularization α of .003 were used to calibrate the learning procedure. In addition, the classification procedures were limited to 200 iterations and the learning process was performed on five folds. The described setting of the neural networks provided with the highest average classification accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC) both expressed as average scores for all balance tasks. AUC, classification accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were calculated for performing classification of each individual stance separately. Moreover, contribution of D L and D R or D AVG and D DIFF towards classifying each balance task was studied using SHAP values (Shapley Additive Explanations; Goodwin et al., 2022). SHAP values for individual patients were plotted for individual pair of predictors (D L and D R or D AVG and D DIFF) to study their contribution to final classifications for each individual balance task. Order of pupil diameter plots was based on their contribution to the prediction importance, with the most important pupil diameter listed first.

DISCUSSION

The primary aim of the study was to analyze whether the steady‐state pupil diameter increases as a result of changes from parallel to single‐leg stance or decreased surface stiffness. This would potentially indicate increased involvement of cognitive processes during balance tasks. The results of this study confirm that D L and consequently D AVG increase in more demanding single leg postural stances as well as during maintaining balance on a foam surface for all stances. However, D R increased only during maintaining balance on a foam surface. This is in line with the results from the deep learning analysis, where more demanding tasks (i.e., single leg stances) were better predicted by increased D L, however less demanding tasks (i.e., parallel stance) were represented by lower values of D L and D R. Although, D L and D AVG increased with balance task intensity, pupil diameter did not correlate with parameters describing CoP movement for individual balance tasks. These results suggest, that pupil diameters respond to changes in balance tasks intensity, but do not relate to biomechanical markers of CoP movement when performing balance tasks of specific intensity.

The second aim of this study was to analyze whether side‐specific pupillometric responses could be observed in single leg stances performed with the dominant and non‐dominant leg. Our results suggest, that there are no differences in pupil diameter of the left and right eye when comparing single leg balance tasks performed on the dominant and non‐dominant leg. However, more important insight was provided by the deep learning analysis, revealing that the D L and D R presented with a different trend of pupil constriction or dilatation when comparing balance tasks of different intensity. In the parallel stance, low pupil diameters of both eyes regardless of the surface compliance type were observed, however, the single leg stance on firm surface was accompanied by slight increase in D L and persistence of low D R. Finally, the most difficult single leg stance performed on foam was accompanied by increase in D L and slight increase in D R. This observation suggests, that asymmetry in pupillary responses could provide additional information on changes in postural controlling mechanisms and are in line with reports on asymmetric and dynamically changing function of cerebral hemispheres that results from task and performer specific characteristics (Serrien et al., 2006).

Pupillometric responses to increased balance task intensity

Results of our study are in line with previous research (Kahya et al., 2018, 2021, 2022) confirming an increase in pupillometric dilatation as a response to increased balance task intensity. However, our results add an important insight into understanding balance controlling mechanisms via measures of steady‐state pupil diameter. More specifically, steady‐state pupillometric diameter has been linked to tonic alertness, related primarily to the dispersed attention (O'Bryan & Scolari, 2021), enabling exploration and integration of novel sources of information during the sensorimotor processing (Strauch et al., 2022). As suggested by Peterka (Peterka & Loughlin, 2004), integration of different sensory information (i.e., proprioceptive, vestibular and visual) provides important feedback for preparing corrective torques, especially during changes in balance task characteristics such as support surface and stance type. Changes from bilateral to single‐leg support as well as from standing on firm to soft support surface, lead to engagement of additional degrees of freedom that need to be controlled during balance tasks and consequently integration of additional proprioceptive information (Kilby et al., 2015; Winter et al., 2003). The increased demand on processes of sensory integration during standing on a soft surface are further supported by observations, that plantar and ankle proprioception become less reliable sources of information (Peterka & Loughlin, 2004). This leads to increased dependence on vestibular information which is known to provide less reliable feedback on body sway (Peterka & Loughlin, 2004). Such findings are further supported by neuro‐physiological studies reporting on increased activity of the frontal cortex during more demanding stances suggesting additional involvement of cognitive resources (Büchel et al., 2021). Based on all presented evidence and the results of our study it can be concluded, that the steady‐state pupil diameters are related to changes in increased central sensory‐motor processing caused by more demanding stances and softer support surfaces.

When compared with to‐date research (Kahya et al., 2018, 2021, 2022), our study importantly upgrades the current knowledge by analyzing each pupil separately. The left pupil proved to be more responsive, whereas the right pupil proved to be less responsive to changes in balance task intensity, while related research (Kahya et al., 2018, 2021) reported primarily on increased oscillations of the right pupil. However, these studies used ICA, which is supposed to report on phasic attention and involvement of higher cognitive resources, and therefore relates at least partially to different cognitive processes (Strauch et al., 2022). No studies reported on side‐specific steady‐state pupil diameter and ICA responses during balance task simultaneously. Therefore, it is impossible to interpret this discrepancy between the two pupils and the two different pupillometric readouts. Future studies should therefore compare the two pupillometric parameters during different balance tasks to provide additional insight into how these two parameters differ and whether they display differences between the left and right pupil.

Another important finding of our study was the non‐existent correlations between CoP movement and pupillometry in none of the individual balance tasks which is partially in line with results of Kahya (Kahya et al., 2022). Their study reported of small correlation between ICA and V ap however only during eye‐covered balance tasks while performing an additional cognitive task. Integration of their and our results suggest, that pupillometry could represent a specific readout of balance controlling mechanisms, that responds primarily to changes in balance task intensity but not CoP movement characteristics during individual balance task. This could be related to organization of balance controlling mechanisms. More specifically, inherent mechanical variability of the human body (Latash et al., 2007), sensory error (Peterka & Loughlin, 2004) and specific muscle synergy control (Nardon et al., 2022; Yamagata et al., 2021) could indicate certain variability in CoP movement which is not directly controlled by posture controlling mechanisms and does therefore not influence pupillometry which is related by cognitive involvement. It remains unclear whether such correlations become apparent in more body sway restricted balance tasks. Such insight could be provided by the power‐spectral frequency analysis of CoP movement performed in previous studies (Sozzi, Ghai, et al., 2022; Zatsiorsky & Duarte, 2000) which provides information on central and peripheral components of balance control which were also proven to respond to changes in balance task intensity as a result of somatosensory modulations (Gerber et al., 2022). This limitation of our study should be addressed by future research and provide further understanding of how pupillometry relates to central and peripheral components of body sway control.

Side‐specific pupillometric responses

A new and important finding of this study was the indication of asymmetric and side‐specific pupillometric response to increase in balance task intensity as revealed by deep‐learning neural networks approach, which is in line with the reports from Nobukawa et al. (2021) and Liu et al. (2017). Asymmetric pupillometric responses have been previously reported during monocular viewing tasks (Wang et al., 2018) and were related to hemisphere specific control of autonomic nervous system. More specifically, the right hemisphere has been related to increased activity of the sympathetic nervous system resulting in increased pupillometric response of the left eye via retinocollicular pathway that projects to the contralateral superior colliculus which further projects to ipsilateral mesencephalic reticular formation incorporating locus coeruleus (Liu et al., 2017). On the contrary, the left hemisphere has been related to increased activity of the parasympathetic nervous system and consequently related to pupillometric responses of the right eye. Based on these reports it could be speculated, that increased sympathetic activity and consequently increased D L, as observed in our study, could result from increased balance task demand while standing single‐legged on a foam surface. These findings further confirm the speculations that steady‐state pupillometric diameter is related to tonic attention which is associated with processing of new sensory information (e.g., sensory reweighing) as mentioned in the introduction (O'Bryan & Scolari, 2021; Strauch et al., 2022).

Another possible explanation of side‐specific pupillometric responses observed in our study is the specialization of the right cerebral hemisphere for controlling limb position and therefore possibly postural stability (Sainburg, 2024). This rationale is in line with our results, where DL proved to change as a result of increased balance task intensity regardless of the standing leg. However, this interpretation has to be considered with caution as these underlying studies analyzed hand dexterity and therefore considered hand but not leg preference for performing stabilization and movement control tasks (Kim et al., 2023; Sainburg, 2024).

In addition to the above explanations, the asymmetry of average pupil size responses has been shown to follow an inverted U‐shaped relationship when plotted against average pupil size (Nobukawa et al., 2021). This relationship suggests that asymmetry is greatest at lower and higher average pupil diameters, which has been associated with low and high cognitive load (sympathetic activity), respectively. The increased pupillary asymmetry was more pronounced when the average pupil diameter (baseline pupil diameter—tonic activation) increased above 3.9 mm. In our study, the average pupil diameter during single‐leg stances while standing on foam exceeded these values, supporting our observation of a possible increase in sympathetic activity associated with asymmetry in pupillary responses. As previous studies indicate a high variability of the inverted U ratio (Nobukawa et al., 2021) which is in line with low accuracy (AUC) of neural network classification models, its importance as a pupillometric marker for balance control needs to be further investigated in future studies.

Final explanation of asymmetric pupillometric responses could be provided by the fact that left hemisphere is associated with higher order closed‐loop planning of skilled movements, whereas the right hemisphere is associated with more basic movement control of the contralateral side of the body (Serrien et al., 2006). However, this alternative explanation was not in line by the results of our study as the opposite pupillometric responses were observed. Therefore, above mentioned explanations based on increased sympathetic activity and increased postural control resulting from increased activity of the right cerebral cortex probably provide a more valid explanation for side‐specific pupillometric responses to changes in balance task intensity.

It would be expected that the asymmetry of the average diameter of the pupils would be better indicated by the D diff, however this parameter failed to indicate changes in the intensity of the balance task. This observation is consistent this reports from the literature (Schreiber & Schmitz, 1996; Theiler et al., 1992) in which the simple measurement of the difference in pupil diameter failed to capture this phenomenon. Future research should therefore use other computational methods that are better able to capture the deterministic processes that determine asymmetry of pupil responses (Nobukawa et al., 2021).

An important limitation of our study was the use of mobile eye trackers, which have been shown to provide less accurate pupillometric readings (Coyne et al., 2019) with the main concern being the reduced accuracy of pupil diameter measured during increased amplitude of eye movements (Choe et al., 2016; Petersch & Dierkes, 2022). However, the measurement protocol in our study used a central and stationary gaze fixation, which has been shown to provide small amplitude eye movements with the least measurement artifacts (Petersch & Dierkes, 2022). In addition, averaging the pupil diameter over a period of 60s may further reduce measurement error of the pupil diameter. Therefore, it can be assumed that the results of this study provide a measurement error that is smaller than the observed effects.

The present study can be upgraded in the future by investigating steady‐state pupillometric responses in relation to variety of different balance tasks on elderly population or patient populations suffering from postural balance impairments. More specifically, side‐specific pupillometric responses could be observed at less demanding postural tasks. In addition, more prominent correlation between pupillometric responses and CoP movement in individual tasks could be expected, especially in patients suffering from centrally induced balance deficits.

CONCLUSION

The results of this study support the use of steady‐state pupillometry in studying postural balance control. More specifically, the increase in balance task demands (changes in stance and support surface type) are accompanied by increase in average diameter of the left pupil. Such increase could be related to increased activity of the sympathetic nervous system as a result of more demanding sensorimotor processing during increased postural instability. In addition, our results indicate the presence of side‐specific pupillometric responses, which could provide an interesting parameter of balance control mechanisms engagement in providing postural stability. However, our observations need to be further explored in future research to provide better understanding of how changes in steady‐state pupillometry can be interpreted and possibly applied in clinical practice as a diagnostic tool.
