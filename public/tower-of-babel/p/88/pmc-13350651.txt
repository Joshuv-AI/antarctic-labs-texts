Extending the Gift
Authors: Christiane Haeffele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Heart transplant remains a gift of scarcity and an invaluable chance to extend life. Safeguarding this gift requires considerable effort to prevent rejection, survey for cardiac allograft vasculopathy, optimize immunosuppression, and manage the myriad complications of long-term transplantation. Yet, one of the most common valvular complications after orthotopic heart transplantation—tricuspid regurgitation (TR)—has historically received comparatively little attention.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Ozdemir et al1 describe successful transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with the EVOQUE system in a patient who developed severe symptomatic TR 2 decades after heart transplantation.1 The report demonstrates procedural feasibility and highlights early favorable changes in ventricular volumes following elimination of severe regurgitation. Although the technical success is noteworthy, the broader significance of this case may extend beyond the tricuspid valve itself. The report invites us to reconsider the role of TR in the transplanted heart and to ask whether treatment of TR may ultimately become a strategy for preserving graft function and extending graft longevity and freedom from heart failure.

TR after heart transplantation is common, but it is not a single disease. Its causes evolve throughout the life of the graft.2 Early after transplantation, TR may arise from primary graft dysfunction, perioperative right ventricular injury, elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, donor-recipient size mismatch, or surgical factors related to atrial geometry. In many patients, these abnormalities improve as the transplanted heart adapts to its new physiologic environment.

In contrast, late TR frequently reflects cumulative structural injury. Repeated endomyocardial biopsies may result in leaflet damage, chordal disruption, or flail segments, particularly involving the septal leaflet. Chronic rejection, pulmonary vascular disease, progressive right ventricular dysfunction, and annular remodeling may further contribute to valve incompetence.2 Up to one-fourth of transplant patients require a pacemaker long-term, which may cause leaflet impingement.2

For many patients who successfully dodge other transplant complications over the years, the mechanisms for severe TR are often multiple and may magnify one another. The patient described by Ozdemir et al1 exemplifies this complexity, with a history of both repeated biopsies and prior rejection episodes contributing to severe, late TR. Although the pathways differ, the physiologic endpoint is remarkably consistent: chronic right ventricular volume overload.

Chronic volume overload has consequences. Progressive right ventricular dilation, declining forward cardiac output, renal dysfunction, hepatic congestion, exercise intolerance, and recurrent heart failure admissions are familiar manifestations of advanced TR.2,3 Prior studies have demonstrated an association between significant post-transplant TR and reduced long-term survival, suggesting that TR may represent more than a marker of graft dysfunction—it may contribute to it.3,4 Viewed through this lens, TR becomes more than a valvular lesion—it becomes a potentially modifiable threat to graft health.

This concept is familiar throughout congenital and valvular heart disease. We no longer wait for irreversible ventricular dysfunction before intervening on chronic valve lesions. Instead, intervention is timed to preserve myocardial function and improve long-term outcomes. The same principle may ultimately apply to transplant recipients with severe TR.

Historically, intervention thresholds in transplant patients were high. The only available treatment option was repeat surgery, often in medically complex patients with prior sternotomies and significant comorbidities. Today, transcatheter tricuspid therapies offer less-invasive alternatives for selected patients. Both repair and replacement strategies continue to evolve, and early experience suggests that these therapies can be performed successfully in transplant recipients who previously had few therapeutic options.1,5

The present case also raises another important question: When is the optimal time to intervene? The reduction in right ventricular volumes observed after TTVR is consistent with growing evidence demonstrating reverse remodeling following elimination of severe TR.1,6 Although short-term changes in ventricular volumes should be interpreted cautiously, they raise the possibility that eliminating severe TR may alter the trajectory of adverse ventricular remodeling. Whether this translates into improved survival, reduced heart failure hospitalizations, or prolonged graft longevity remains unknown. These are the outcomes that matter most to patients and clinicians alike.

The case presented by Ozdemir et al1 highlights a potential evolution in the care of transplant recipients. The significance of this report lies not only in demonstrating that TTVR can be performed successfully after transplantation, but also in prompting a broader reconsideration of the role of TR in long-term graft health. As transcatheter tricuspid therapies continue to mature, severe TR should no longer be viewed simply as a late complication of transplantation. Instead, it may represent a therapeutic opportunity—one that could allow clinicians not only to relieve symptoms, but also to extend the life of the transplanted heart itself.
