Supramolecular nanofiber stabilization of microdroplets enables ultra-sensitive virus infection analysis
Authors: Noriyuki Uchida, Atsuya Yaguchi, Shiori Kondo, Kazuyoshi Muranishi, Haruka Kawabata, Haruka Umezawa, Naito Ishimoto, Michiko Tajiri, Satoko Akashi, Takuya Seki, Go Watanabe, Ayano Yoshida, Genki Higuchi, Jakuei Fu, Takanobu Takenouchi, Daisuke Yoshino, Hirotsugu Hiramatsu, Masaki Okumura, Tomohide Saio, Hiroyuki Noji, Young-Ho Lee, Sam-Yong Park, Takahiro Muraoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aqueous two-phase system (ATPS) droplets in cells act as fluidic microreactors by concentrating biomacromolecules. Inspired by this phenomenon, dextran-rich microdroplets formed in an ATPS with polyethylene glycol have been explored as artificial microreactors for sensitive detection and spatiotemporal control of biochemical reactions. However, the rapid fusion of the microdroplets into bulk phase separation has limited practical applications of this approach. Here, we report the stabilization of dextran-rich microdroplets using supramolecular nanofibers of an azobenzene-appending self-assembling peptide (AzSAP). Physicochemical characterization and structural analyses elucidate the mechanism of nanofiber formation and its role in droplet stabilization. The nanofiber network prevents droplet coalescence while maintaining macroscopic fluidity, thereby enabling highly sensitive quantitative virus detection via microfluidic analysis by confining infecting viruses within droplets. Furthermore, the photo-responsive properties of the AzSAP allow dynamic control over droplet size and intra-droplet virus activity, highlighting its exceptional potential as a platform for programmable artificial microreactors.

Introduction

Cells construct aqueous two-phase system (ATPS) droplets as fluidic microreactors. Due to their interfacial permeability, these droplets can import solutes, including reactants, and condense biomacromolecules such as nucleic acids and proteins, thereby enabling various biological reactions to proceed efficiently and maintain homeostasis in response to external stressors1–8. Inspired by such biological droplets, artificial ATPS microdroplets9–26—typically formed by mixing two immiscible water-soluble polymers such as dextran and polyethylene glycol (PEG)—have attracted considerable attention as controllable microreactors in segregated spaces. Leveraging the capacity to accumulate biomolecules and maintain macroscopic fluidity, dextran-rich ATPS microdroplets (Dex-droplets) have been integrated into two-dimensional microchamber devices27, in which fluidity allows the direct filling of microchambers. The property of accumulation is also useful for green chemistry28,29, constructing artificial cells30,31, and culturing cells9.

Extending this concept to a three-dimensional (3D) Dex-droplet microreactor system would markedly enhance reaction productivity, and the macroscopic fluidity of these systems is compatible with flow-based detectors (such as fluorescence-activated cell sorters) for highly sensitive and quantitative analyses. However, the inherently low stability and short lifetime of conventional Dex-droplets, owing to rapid fusion leading to bulk phase separation, critically limit their applicability in 3D systems. Inspired by intracellular systems, in which F-actin stabilizes ribonucleoprotein droplets32, synthetic supramolecular polymers have been utilized to construct and stabilize ATPS droplets. Short peptides such as carboxybenzyl-protected diphenylalanine form ATPS droplets in the presence of metal cations, but these droplets deform into fibrillar morphologies after several hours of incubation33. A synthetic amphiphile UPy-Gly appending ureidopyrimidinone and glycine units self-assembles in water to produce ATPS droplets with a characteristic tactoid morphology and high rigidity after extended incubation over several days13. The supramolecular polymers of UPy-Gly are densely generated within Dex-droplets in the dextran and PEG system for stabilization and deformation. The use of Pickering emulsions with liposomes10 and platelets11, and coating of droplets with triblock copolymers are also reported to be effective for droplet stabilization12. However, these conventional approaches compromise key microreactor properties of the droplets, such as interfacial permeability and fluidity. In addition, stimuli-responsive control of droplet fusion has remained a challenging task.

Here, we report effective stabilization of Dex-droplets in a 3D medium by suppressing fusion using nanofibers formed by an azobenzene-appending self-assembling peptide (AzSAP, Fig. 1a–c), without loss of macroscopic fluidity. Physicochemical characterization revealed that AzSAP nanofibers retain their structural integrity within the ATPS, thereby remaining permeable to small molecules, and they do not interfere with droplet mobility. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structural analyses further showed that the nanofibers consist of two symmetrical protofilaments connected via cross-β hydrogen bonds and stabilized by azobenzene π-stacking. The nanofibers provide a robust supramolecular scaffold that suppresses droplet coalescence. Functionally, stabilized Dex-droplets efficiently concentrated M13 bacteriophages (M13 phage)34–41 and Escherichia coli (E. coli) cells, while preventing secondary infections outside the droplets (Fig. 1d, e). The preserved macroscopic fluidity enabled ultra-sensitive, quantitative detection of infection events within droplets using flow cytometry (Fig. 1e). In the previously reported Dex-droplets, no such application for analyzing virus infection has been achieved. Design of peptides that form supramolecular nanofibers

To suppress the fusion of microdroplets while maintaining macroscopic fluidity, we focused on mixing Dex-droplets with supramolecular nanofibers that form 3D network structures in aqueous media and exhibit the dynamic property of collapsing and re-forming due to non-covalent interactions. We chose peptides as the supramolecular nanofiber-forming component because of their high structural designability, based on established synthesis principles that can generate a variety of molecular and self-assembling structures. We recently developed self-assembling peptides with an iterative sequence between hydrophilic and hydrophobic side groups containing an azobenzene group42,43. These peptides form supramolecular nanofibers via the construction of β-sheet motifs through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds. By coupling with optical controls, the potential applications of the photoresponsive functionality of the azobenzene group can be expanded. Based on our previous study, herein we designed azobenzene-containing amphiphilic peptides, AzSAP (Ac-RAzRADMR-NH2, where Az indicates an amino acid residue with an azobenzene group appended at the side chain) and rev-AzSAP (Ac-RMDARAzR-NH2) consisting of 7 amino acid residues with opposite sequences (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1).

AzSAP and rev-AzSAP dissolved in water at 1.0 wt% to provide transparent solutions, suggesting good dispersibility. After incubation at 25 °C for 6 h, AzSAP remained in solution, whereas the fluidity of rev-AzSAP in water decreased (Supplementary Fig. 2). Rheological analysis of rev-AzSAP in water (1.0 wt%) after incubation indicated G’ and G” values of 12.4 Pa and 2.1 Pa, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). Even though rev-AzSAP exhibited rather weak viscoelasticity, the G’ > G” relationship suggested the formation of a hydrogel. Interestingly, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that AzSAP and rev-AzSAP formed well-defined nanofibers with high rectilinearity after incubation (Fig. 2a, c). Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopic measurements of AzSAP (1.0 wt%) in water showed negative Cotton signals at 219 and 332 nm, indicative of β-sheet formation and stacking at the azobenzene unit in a chiral fashion, respectively (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3). Although rev-AzSAP also showed a negative Cotton effect at 219 nm immediately after dissolution, a continuous increase in CD signals was observed during incubation, with a stronger negative signal at 219 nm than AzSAP and a positive signal at 326 nm (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 3). The opposite signals of AzSAP and rev-AzSAP at around 330 nm suggest different packing structures of the azobenzene moiety within the fibers44. The stronger 219-nm negative CD signal was suggestive of a larger population of β-sheet structures of rev-AzSAP in water compared with AzSAP, which was likely the reason for the different macroscopic states between the two peptides. We observed that AzSAP fibers maintained a fibrous structure against external environmental changes such as saline conditions and upon heating, while rev-AzSAP fibers were less stable and disassembled upon heating (Supplementary Fig. 4). To investigate the structural mechanism by which different macroscopic states of AzSAP and rev-AzSAP are formed in water, we further conducted structural analyses by cryogenic electron microscopic (cryo-EM) observation. Cryo-EM imaging of AzSAP nanofibers visualized the packing of two symmetric protofilaments with a crossover distance of 72.5 nm (Fig. 2e, f, Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Structural optimization at a resolution of 0.19 nm revealed a C2-symmetric butterfly-shaped cross-sectional structure composed of 14 molecules of AzSAP, and the horizontal and vertical sizes of the cross-section were 9.0 nm and 7.5 nm, respectively (Fig. 2g–i, Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). Interestingly, the butterfly-type cross-section is constructed by dimerization of two clusters of AzSAP formed by the assembly of three (a/b/c-cluster; AzSAP-a/AzSAP-b/AzSAP-c) and four (d/e/f/g-cluster; AzSAP-d/AzSAP-e/AzSAP-f/AzSAP-g) molecules of AzSAP. In the AzSAP nanofiber, two filaments were assembled by electrostatic interactions between two AzSAP-g molecules and hydrophobic interactions between AzSAP-a and AzSAP-g. The two clusters between a/b/c-cluster and d/e/f/g-cluster were assembled by interactions between AzSAP-c and AzSAP-d via guanidine groups at the terminal arginine residues, where water molecules were arranged to fill the space between the clusters (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11). A similar butterfly-shaped cross-sectional structure was observed with rev-AzSAP nanofibers. In both the AzSAP and rev-AzSAP fibers, each cluster contained aggregations of the azobenzene groups and hydrophobic side chains of Met and Ala in the interior region, and these azobenzene groups formed π-stacks both within the clusters and between the layers along the filaments (Fig. 2g, h, i, l, m, n). The crossover distance of the rev-AzSAP fibers was 94.3 nm (Fig. 2j, k, Supplementary Figs. 5, 9–11). The horizontal and vertical dimensions of the cross-section of rev-AzSAP fibers were 8.5 nm and 6.5 nm, respectively (Fig. 2l). A characteristic difference in the packing structures was observed at the core of the cross-section. Namely, observation of the cross-section of the AzSAP assembly revealed a cavity at the central core. By contrast, the core of the rev-AzSAP assembly is filled by residues R1, D3, and R5 in rev-AzSAP-g fibers, in which rev-AzSAP-g and rev-AzSAP-g’ interact via contact at these residues. This filled-core architecture results in a denser, mechanically more rigid packing that likely enhances nanofiber stability at the cost of dynamic flexibility, correlating with the formation of a self-standing hydrogel and reduced macroscopic fluidity. Due to the difference in the packing structure at the core, rev-AzSAP fibers have a smaller cross-sectional area than AzSAP fibers (i.e., rev-AzSAP fibers are more densely packed than AzSAP fibers). In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation showed that the interactions between AzSAP clusters mediated by hydrophilic peptide residues are not robust (Supplementary Fig. 12). Cryo-EM analyses thus directly reveal how subtle rearrangements in peptide packing determine the balance between stability and fluidity in the resulting supramolecular networks, an essential structure–function relationship that explains why the densely packed rev-AzSAP nanofibers exhibit greater stability and more readily form hydrogels, whereas the more loosely packed AzSAP fibers maintain macroscopic fluidity.

Stabilization of ATPS microdroplets by supramolecular peptide nanofibers

Although vortexing of a mixture of dextran and PEG in water (5.0 wt% dextran; Mw = 450,000–650,000, 5.0 wt% PEG; Mw = 35,000) afforded a cloudy suspension due to the formation of Dex-droplets, the solution returned to a transparent state due to macroscopic phase separation within a 15 min incubation (Fig. 3a). Phase-contrast microscopic observation of the mixture in a 200 μm-thick glass chamber consistently showed rapid fusion of the dextran droplets during the initial 10 min, resulting in the formation of enlarged droplets with a diameter of >100 μm (Fig. 3f). Critically, such rapid fusion limits application of the conventional dextran droplets formed in aqueous PEG solutions to microreactors. Interestingly, the aqueous mixture of dextran and PEG remained turbid even after 24 h of incubation after vortexing in the presence of AzSAP (Dex-droplets/AzSAP, Fig. 3b) and rev-AzSAP (Dex-droplets/rev-AzSAP, Fig. 3c) fibers. The size of the dextran droplets ( < 30 µm) changed only minimally during the incubation, clearly indicating suppression of droplet fusion (Fig. 3g, h). Also, the turbidity of the dispersions due to the formation of Dex-droplets changed very little in the presence of AzSAP and rev-AzSAP, in contrast to the Dex-droplets without peptides, which showed a rapid drop in turbidity due to fusion (Supplementary Fig. 13). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) observation of Cy5-labeled peptide nanofibers and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis indicated that AzSAP and rev-AzSAP fibers were distributed in both the dextran-rich and PEG-rich phases (Supplementary Fig. 14). TEM observations showed formation of AzSAP and rev-AzSAP nanofibers in an aqueous medium containing dextran and PEG, with similar morphologies observed in water (Supplementary Fig. 15). In addition, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis showed that molecular diffusivity in the system was maintained even after the stabilization of Dex-droplets by AzSAP fibers (Supplementary Fig. 16). To investigate the structural influences of the peptide self-assemblies on droplet stabilization efficiency, we synthesized several AzSAP derivatives, AzSAP-K (Ac-KAzRADMK-NH2) and AzSAP-D (Ac-DAzRADMD-NH2) possessing lysine and aspartic acid residues at the respective termini, respectively, JigSAP (Ac-RIDARMRADAIR-NH2) having a jigsaw-shaped hydrophobic region without an azobenzene unit42, and RADA16 (Ac-RADARADARADARADA-NH2) having a planar hydrophobic region composed solely of alanine residues45. Importantly, AzSAP-K, AzSAP-D, and RADA16 formed particulate, plate-like, and flexible fibrous assemblies in water, respectively, as observed by TEM, CD measurement, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analyses (Supplementary Figs. 17, 18), and their stabilization effect on Dex-droplets was limited (Fig. 3d, e, i, j, Supplementary Fig. 19). Although JigSAP self-assembled into continuous nanofibers stabilizing Dex-droplets, the dispersion formed a hydrogel with low fluidity (Supplementary Fig. 20). These findings, in light of the results of cryo-EM structural analyses, suggest that the nanofibers with a long persistence length, consisting of AzSAP, rev-AzSAP, and JigSAP fibers that are amenable to the construction of homogeneous networks, are critical for arresting droplet fusion. Fluidic systems are advantageous in terms of compatibility with microfluidic devices. We therefore investigated the fluidity of Dex-droplet systems containing the fiber-forming peptides. A vortexed mixture of dextran and PEG in water showed a viscosity of 10.5 ± 0.2 mPa•s (1 mPa•s = 1 cP) at 20 °C (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG, 1.0 wt%). Interestingly, the viscosity of an aqueous dispersion of dextran and PEG containing AzSAP (1.0 wt%) was 11.1 ± 1.8 mPa•s, retaining high fluidity (Fig. 3k). However, the viscosity of Dex-droplet systems containing rev-AzSAP (1.0 wt%) was approximately 3-fold higher (31.9 ± 0.1 vs 11.1 ± 1.8 mPa•s, p < 0.01, Fig. 3k). This result suggests that the viscosity of the Dex-droplet system containing the fiber-forming peptides is influenced by the structure of the hydrophobic moiety of the peptides, and rev-AzSAP fibers may possess a longer persistence length than AzSAP fibers, leading to the assembly of a more robust network, thereby increasing macroscopic viscosity46. Frequency-dependent rheological analyses (shear strain = 0.5%) of Dex-droplet systems containing AzSAP and rev-AzSAP showed a common profile with a slope of 0.4 at a frequency of 0.10–1.0%, indicating that both systems were essentially in a fluidic solution state (Fig. 3l)47.

Spatiotemporal control of droplet fusion using light irradiation

Thanks to the photo-responsive functionality of the azobenzene unit of AzSAP, size tuning of the Dex-droplets48,49 and optical control of infection events can be demonstrated (Fig. 4a). Upon UV light irradiation (λ = 350 nm) of an aqueous solution of AzSAP fibers (1.0 wt%) for 3 min, absorption spectroscopy showed a decrease in the absorption band intensity at 330 nm and an increase in the band intensity at 430 nm due to trans-to-cis isomerization of the azobenzene group (Fig. 4b)50. In CD spectroscopy, signals assigned to the β-sheet structure and azobenzene stacking decreased upon UV irradiation (Fig. 4e). In conjunction with these spectroscopic changes, TEM observation revealed a transition of the self-assembly structure of AzSAP from nanofibers (Fig. 4c) to nanoparticles (Fig. 4d) upon UV irradiation. This structural transition of AzSAP fibers was reversible, as AzSAP nanofibers re-formed following irradiation with 450-nm light to prompt the backward cis-to-trans isomerization after exposure to UV light (Supplementary Fig. 21). We also confirmed that the localization of AzSAP fibers in the system composed of a mixture of dextran and PEG changed only minimally after photo-isomerization (Supplementary Fig. 22). Interestingly, the depolymerization of AzSAP nanofibers destabilized the Dex-droplets, thereby facilitating fusion. Exposure of the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG, 1.0 wt% AzSAP) to 350 nm light for 15 min resulted in a rapid change in the turbid mixture through macroscopic phase separation (Fig. 4f, g), indicating that droplet fusion proceeded. Indeed, phase-contrast microscopy observation revealed an increase in the size of Dex-droplets in the UV-irradiated area during droplet fusion (Fig. 4h–j). It is reasonable that droplet fusion commenced immediately after UV irradiation (Fig. 4i), since the concentration threshold of AzSAP for the stabilization of Dex-droplets ( > 0.9 wt%, Supplementary Fig. 23) was close to the experimental conditions. In addition, the destabilized Dex-droplets were again stabilized by reconstruction of AzSAP fibers upon irradiation with 450-nm visible light (Supplementary Fig. 24), and this photo-reversible process was repeatable for at least 20 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 25). When we varied the duration of irradiation with UV light before the visible light-triggered suppression of droplet fusion (Fig. 4k), phase-contrast microscopy observation clearly showed that droplet size increased with increasing duration of UV-light irradiation (Fig. 4l–o). The diameter of Dex-droplets was 22.2 ± 3.8 µm (before light irradiation), 26.9 ± 3.9 µm (after 2 min UV irradiation), 36.0 ± 11.5 µm (after 4 min UV irradiation), and 58.0 ± 25.1 µm (after 5 min UV irradiation) (Fig. 4p), demonstrating the potential for irradiation time-dependent control of Dex-droplet size by employing the photo-responsiveness of AzSAP fibers. Furthermore, phage infection was suppressed when the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system containing M13 phages was mixed with E. coli cells. Subsequent light irradiation to trigger fusion of Dex-droplets/AzSAP rapidly facilitated M13 phage infection (Supplementary Fig. 26), while Dex-droplets/rev-AzSAP did not suppress infection even before the light irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 27). To evaluate applications to microfluidic analyses, Dex-droplets/AzSAP containing Cy5-labeled dextran (5.0 wt% dextran, 4 × 10–4 wt% Cy5-dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG, 1.0 wt% AzSAP, 1.5 wt% lactoferrin) in LB medium was injected into a ⌀400-μm microfluidic channel51. Due to macroscopic fluidity, the Dex-droplets/AzSAP flowed into the channel with negligible leakage to the external solution, as visualized using fluorescence microscopy (λex = 650 nm, Fig. 5g, h), suggesting the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system is applicable to microfluidic analyses. On the other hand, a water-in-oil droplet52 caused disordered flow in the flow cytometry device (Supplementary Fig. 30). Analysis of Dex-droplets/AzSAP containing Cy5-dextran using flow cytometry (core size = 40 μm) revealed spots corresponding to the fluorescence of Cy5-labeled dextran in the forward scatter (FSC) profile of 48 K–200 K and the side scatter (SSC) profile of 10 K–65 K (Fig. 5i, j). Notably, flow cytometry analysis of aqueous samples containing only dextran, PEG, or AzSAP showed few spots in this region (Supplementary Fig. 31), indicating that the profile displayed in Fig. 5i was derived from Dex-droplets/AzSAP; thus, each droplet can be analyzed by flow cytometry.

Next, we investigated the restraining effects of the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system as a bioreactor on secondary infection in the amplification of M13 phages. M13 phages (4 × 105 or 4 × 106 virions ml–1) and E. coli cells were cultured in LB medium, and the incubation time-dependent M13 phage concentration was determined using real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis (Fig. 5k). In Dex-droplets without AzSAP (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG), the number of M13 phages increased exponentially over time and reached saturation after incubation for 24 h (Fig. 5l), indicating that repetitive amplification of M13 phages proceeded via multiple unrestrained infection events during droplet fusion. By contrast, the increase in the number of M13 phages over time proceeded more slowly in the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system than in Dex-droplets alone (Fig. 5l, Supplementary Figs. 32, 33). We also found that Dex-droplets prepared by continuous vortexing without AzSAP showed a lower suppression effect on infection than the Dex-droplets/AzSAP (Supplementary Fig. 34). Importantly, in the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system, the number of M13 phages at a given time point after addition was essentially proportional to the initial number. These results indicate that secondary infection by M13 phages was minimized in the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system; that is, the Dex-droplets effectively functioned as a bioreactor, providing a confined reaction space for M13 phage infection of E. coli cells. Notably, the proportional relationship between the amplified and initial number of M13 phages enables evaluation of the initial number by quantifying droplets containing infecting M13 phages. This approach would be useful for highly sensitive quantitative detection of viruses using flow cytometry.

To demonstrate highly sensitive quantitative detection of M13 phages, Dex-droplets/AzSAP were mixed with 9H-(1,3-dichloro-9,9-dimethylacridin-2-one-7-yl) β-D-galactopyranoside (DDAO galactoside), which emits red fluorescence after degradation, generating DDAO dye through reaction with β-galactosidase produced in M13 phage–infected E. coli cells53. After incubation of M13 phages at a concentration of 2 × 102–2 × 103 virions ml–1 with E. coli cells, CLSM observation revealed an increase in the number of Dex-droplets exhibiting DDAO dye fluorescence correlated with the initial concentration of M13 phages (Fig. 5m–q, Supplementary Fig. 35). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis showed that the number of droplets exhibiting DDAO dye fluorescence increased depending on the initial M13 phage concentration (Fig. 5r). In sharp contrast to those results, incubation of E. coli cells and M13 phages in LB medium without Dex-droplets/AzSAP resulted in a saturation of the number of fluorescent droplets, regardless of the initial phage concentration (Supplementary Fig. 36). It should be noted that the concentration that could be detected using flow cytometry was >10 times higher than the detection sensitivity of conventional plaque assays performed on a two-dimensional plate (Supplementary Fig. 37). In addition, not only M13 phages but also lambda phages54,55 can be used for the detection with comparable sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 38), highlighting the compatibility and potential application of the 3D Dex-droplet/AzSAP system with flow cytometry analysis for highly sensitive detection of viruses.

Discussion

Here, we report a macroscopically fluid ATPS-based material stabilized by a network of supramolecular peptide nanofibers. AzSAP is composed of an iterative hydrophilic and hydrophobic amphiphilic sequence with an azobenzene unit. AzSAP affords a fluidic solution in water at 1.0 wt%, in which supramolecular nanofibers with high rectilinearity are constructed through β-sheet formation. Cryo-EM structural analyses revealed that AzSAP constructs two types of hetero clusters (3-mers and 4-mers) with azobenzene-based hydrophobic cores, which dimerize into a C2-symmetric butterfly-shaped cross-section of 14 molecules (Fig. 2). The cross-section of AzSAP features a central cavity and loose packing, whereas rev-AzSAP, which has the reverse amino acid sequence, exhibits a denser packing in which the core is filled by the R1, D3, and R5 residues. This loose packing of AzSAP nanofibers enhances macroscopic fluidity and thermal stability compared with rev-AzSAP nanofibers, which is advantageous for microfluidic analysis and bioreactor applications. Such molecular-level understanding of the chemical and physical properties of AzSAP self-assembly, demonstrated by the comparison with rev-AzSAP, is highly valuable for optimizing molecules and materials with more sophisticated functionalities. The dextran-rich droplets formed by mixing with PEG in aqueous medium are inherently unstable and show prompt fusion within 1 h, which critically limits their application as microreactors. Addition of AzSAP nanofibers to a mixture of dextran and PEG afforded Dex-droplets/AzSAP, in which droplet fusion was effectively suppressed (Fig. 3). Suppression of droplet fusion was also observed following the addition of rev-AzSAP, which affords a self-standing hydrogel at 1.0 wt% in water. These functional differences directly mirror the nanofiber architectures revealed by cryo-EM analyses: AzSAP fibers, with their less dense packing, form a dynamic inter-droplet network that halts fusion yet preserves mobility, whereas the more compact rev-AzSAP fibers yield stronger, more viscous networks with reduced flow. Unlike Dex-droplets stabilized by liposome10 and triblock polymer12, Dex-droplets/AzSAP are stable even in saline conditions and allow molecular transportation. Also, as compared to reported oil- or protein-based droplets56–58, Dex-droplets and PEG minimize interactions with biomacromolecules, indicating that the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system is desirable for developing a variety of bioreactors.

A notable advantage of the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system is its photo-controllability, which allows for reversible triggering and suppression of droplet fusion for size control and photo-triggered initiation of M13 phage infection (Fig. 4). At the molecular level, this photo-responsiveness originates from the ordered π–π stacking of azobenzene units within the nanofiber cross-section, which enables structural rearrangements and polarity changes upon trans–cis isomerization that carry into macroscopic droplet behaviors. As observed in conventional Dex-droplets, M13 phages and E. coli cells localized in the droplets in the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system. Importantly, M13 phages in Dex-droplets infected E. coli cells and replicated, but secondary infection outside the droplets was effectively suppressed (Fig. 5). Nevertheless, the macroscopic fluidity, high stability of the microdroplets, and the restraining effect on secondary virus infection make it possible to use flow cytometry to quantitatively detect M13 phages in droplets of the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system. As a result, we achieved highly sensitive detection of viral titer, a complementary quantitative indicator to viral counts measurable by q-PCR59 and microfluidic chip detection27,60, with sensitivity >10 times higher than that of conventional plaque assays that use two-dimensional plates (Supplementary Table 1). By optimizing fluorescent probes for virus-infected cells61, the sensitivity and range of applicable target viruses for the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system could be further improved. In addition, the Dex-droplet/AzSAP system can be applicable for analyzing a variety of targets since dextran and PEG are known as biocompatible polymers, and the surface of AzSAP can be coated with blocking agents such as lactoferrin to effectively suppress undesired nonspecific interactions with proteins of interest. Detection costs could be improved in the future by optimizing the peptide structure to consist only of natural amino acids.

Thus, the present study established a direct structure–function relationship: the nanometer-scale packing geometry (visualized by cryo-EM) dictates micrometer-scale droplet stability, fluidity, and responsiveness. This correlation opens the way for the rational design of artificial droplet systems that function as high-performance, tunable microreactors for applications such as light-triggered initiation of bioreactions and ultra-sensitive virus detection.

Synthesis of peptides

Ac-RAzDARMR-NH2 (AzSAP), Ac-RMDARAzR-NH2 (rev-AzSAP), DAzDARMD-NH2 (AzSAP-D), Ac-KAzDARMK-NH2 (AzSAP-K), Ac-RIDARMRADAIR-NH2 (JigSAP)42, and Ac-RADARADARADARADA-NH2 (RADA16)45, and corresponding peptides conjugated with fluorescent Cy5 [Cy5-GRAzDARMR-NH2 (Cy5-AzSAP), Cy5-GRMDARAzR-NH2 (Cy5-rev-AzSAP), Cy5-GDAzRADMD-NH2 (Cy5-AzSAP-D), Cy5-GKAzRADMK-NH2 (Cy5-AzSAP-K), Cy5-GRIDARMRADAIR-NH2 (Cy5-JigSAP), and Cy5-GRADARADARADARADA-NH2 (Cy5-RADA16)] were prepared using 9-fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl solid-phase peptide synthesis42,43. Typically, a condensation-reagent cocktail consisting of HBTU (3.1 g, 8.0 mmol) and HOBt∙H2O (1.3 g, 8.2 mmol) in DMF (16 ml), a mixture of DIEA (2.8 ml) and NMP (14.3 ml), and a cleavage cocktail consisting of TIS (62.5 μl), TFA (2.4 ml), and water (62.5 μl) were prepared just prior to peptide synthesis. Fmoc-NH-SAL resin (0.1 mmol) in a polypropylene tube was soaked in DMF (2 ml) for 3 h at 25 °C. After removal of DMF, piperidine in DMF (20%, 2 ml) was added and mixed by vortexing for 1 min. After the reaction solution was removed, piperidine in DMF (20%, 2 ml) was added, and the reaction tube was shaken for 10 min at 25 °C. After removal of the reaction solution, the resin was washed with DMF (2 ml, 5 times), CH2Cl2 (2 ml, 3 times), and DMF (2 ml, 3 times). Next, Fmoc-protected amino acids (0.3 mmol) dissolved in condensation-reagents cocktail (700 μl) and a mixture of DIEA and NMP (700 μl) were added to the resin. After shaking for 15 min at 25 °C, the reaction solution was removed, and the resin was washed with DMF (2 ml, 5 times), CH2Cl2 (2 ml, 3 times), and DMF (2 ml, 3 times). The Fmoc deprotection reactions with piperidine and coupling reactions of Fmoc-protected amino acids were repeated following the designed sequence. After the final Fmoc deprotection reaction and washing step, acetic anhydride in CH2Cl2 (25%, 2 ml) was added to the resin, and the reaction tube was shaken for 5 min at 25 °C. After removal of the reaction solution, the resin was washed with DMF (2 ml, 5 times), CH2Cl2 (2 ml, 3 times), and DMF (2 ml, 3 times). Cleavage cocktail (2.5 ml) was then added to the resin, and the reaction tube was left to stand for 90 min at 25 °C, with gentle shaking every 30 min. The solution was collected into a polypropylene centrifuge tube by filtration. The reaction tube was rinsed with TFA (500 μl, 3 times), which was also collected by filtration. Et2O (40 ml) was then added to the centrifuge tube and mixed by vortexing for 1 min, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C (3500 × g, 5 min) and removal of the supernatant. This process was repeated 3 times, and the resulting peptide was dried under vacuum for 2 h at 25 °C, dispersed in water, and lyophilized. Peptide samples were purified by HPLC using a system consisting of a PU-4180 RHPLC pump, a UV-4075 UV/Vis detector, and LC-Net II/APC (JASCO) equipped with a Triart C18 column (⌀4.6 × 250 mm, YMC, Kyoto, Japan) or Actus Triart C18 column (⌀20 × 250 mm, YMC). Purified peptides were characterized using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) on an Autoflex Speed spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) in positive reflector mode with 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid as the matrix.

Preparation of peptide self-assemblies

Typically, lyophilized powder of AzSAP (10 mg) was dissolved in 0.5 ml of an aqueous dispersion of PEG (10 wt%). After gentle shaking and sonication for 20 min at 25 °C, the sample was left to stand overnight at 20 °C. The sample was then mixed with 0.5 ml of an aqueous dispersion of dextran (10 wt%), and the resulting sample was vortexed for 10 s. Dex-droplets prepared using AzSAP and rev-AzSAP and the corresponding Cy5-labeled assemblies of AzSAP (10 mg AzSAP, 0.03 mg Cy5-AzSAP), rev-AzSAP (10 mg rev-AzSAP, 0.03 mg Cy5-rev-AzSAP), JigSAP (10 mg JigSAP, 0.03 mg Cy5-JigSAP), and RADA16 (10 mg RADA, 0.03 mg Cy5-RADA) were prepared according to the same method. AzSAP and rev-AzSAP fibers in aqueous dispersions were prepared using water instead of the mixture of PEG (5 wt%) and dextran (5 wt%). To prepare Dex-droplets encapsulating M13 phages and E. coli cells, a dispersion consisting of 10 wt% dextran, 40% LB medium, M13 phages, and 4% E. coli cells prepared by incubation in LB medium at 37 °C overnight was used instead of the aqueous dispersion of dextran (10 wt%).

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

AzSAP fiber samples were diluted with water, and 3 µl of the diluted solution was applied to a glow-discharged holey carbon grid (Quantifoil R1.2/1.3, Cu, 300 mesh). After blotting for 3 s with a blotting force of –10 at 4 °C under 100% humidity using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the grids were rapidly plunge-frozen in liquid ethane and stored in liquid nitrogen. Cryo-EM data were acquired on a Titan Krios G4 transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV and equipped with a Selectris X energy filter (10-eV slit) and Falcon-4i direct electron detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at Okayama University (Okayama, Japan). A total of 10,020 movies were collected automatically using EPU software at a nominal magnification of 165,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.727 Å, with an exposure time of 4.6 s and a total electron dose of 50 e/Å².

Cryo-EM data processing

Collected movies were gain-corrected, aligned, dose-weighted, and summed using RELION’s implementation of MotionCor262,63. Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using CTFFIND464. All subsequent image-processing steps were performed using the helical reconstruction procedure in RELION-5.065–67. Filaments were initially picked manually, followed by reference-free 2D classification. The resulting segments were used to train Topaz, and filaments were then picked automatically using a modified version of Topaz68,69. From the AzSAP datasets, 7,687,512 and 3,699,956 filament segments were extracted, respectively.

Subsequent reference-free 2D classification and dendrogram-based filament sorting identified 106,867 and 550,492 homogeneous filament segments, respectively, for further processing. An initial 3D reference model was reconstructed from 2D class averages using relion_helix_inimodel2d70 with an estimated C2 symmetry and cross-over distance of 72.5 nm. 3D auto-refinement was performed without imposing high symmetry, revealing pseudo-21 helical symmetry from map features. Iterative CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing64,71 were then applied, followed by 3D auto-refinement with pseudo-21 helical symmetry. High-resolution helical segments were further filtered to 92,358 using CryoSieve72.

The final 3D reconstructions were post-processed using standard RELION procedures, and the overall resolution was determined from Fourier shell correlations at 0.14373. Map handedness was validated by examining density for main-chain carbonyl oxygens and confirmed using Ramachandran plots69. Detailed cryo-EM data processing workflows are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Atomic modeling

The initial atomic model was manually built in Coot74, with restraint files for modified residues generated using aceDRG75. The asymmetric unit model was refined under helical symmetry constraints using Servalcat76, and the sharpened, weighted FoFc map from Servalcat was used for iterative model building in Coot and for visualization. The final model was validated using MolProbity77,78. For structural visualization, the model was expanded to a multi-filament assembly using helical symmetry, and ChimeraX79 was employed to generate structural representations, including electrostatic potential and hydrophobicity surface maps. Detailed cryo-EM data collection, reconstruction, and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

FTIR analysis of peptide self-assemblies

FTIR spectra of self-assemblies of AzSAP, rev-AzSAP, AzSAP-K, and AzSAP-D were measured in the hydrated state using BioCell CaF2 windows, in which the path length was fixed (BioTools, West Palm Beach, FL, USA). Band decomposition analysis of the spectra was carried out using six Gaussian bands42.

Handling of phages

M13 phages were amplified using E. coli cells (ER2738) in LB medium containing tetracycline (45 µM) and purified by two cycles of precipitation and re-dispersion using a solution containing PEG (14 wt%) and NaCl (290 mM)80,81. The concentration of M13 phages was determined by plaque assay on X-gal plates82. For FITC labeling, M13 phages (1.5 × 1013 virions ml–1) were incubated with FITC isomer (FITC-I, 250 µg ml–1) in Tris-HCl buffer (0.5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) for 3 h at 25 °C; unreacted FITC-I was removed by dialysis. Lambda phages were amplified using E. coli cells (ME) in LB medium. The concentration of lambda phages was calculated based on the assumption that its plaque-forming unit (PFU) in lysate is estimated to be 1 × 108 virions ml–183, and particle/PFU ratio is estimated to be 384.

Flow cytometry analysis

Typically, flow cytometry analysis was performed on a BD AccuriTM C6 flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) with gating of FSC-H on 15,000 and SSC-H on 1000 at a flow rate of 100 μl min–1 using a flow channel with a core size of 40 μm; alternatively, a flow cytometry device with a channel width of 400 μm was used51. To prepare the device, the flow pattern was machined into a bulk sheet of polyacetal resin using computer numerical control machining followed by pouring of silicone resin. After incubation for 8 h at 60 °C for curing, the flow pattern was transferred to the resin (Supplementary Fig. 39). Fluorescent microscopy of the flow cytometry device was performed on a THUNDER Imager Model Organism (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) through an excitation filter (λ = 620 ± 60 nm) and fluorescence filter (λ = 700 ± 75 nm).

DNA extraction and qPCR

In the typical procedure for quantifying M13 phages by qPCR, a mixture of Dex-droplets/AzSAP (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG, 1.0 wt% AzSAP) containing lactoferrin (1.5 wt%), M13 phages (4 × 105 or 4 × 106 virions ml–1), and E. coli cells (2% of confluent sample) in 20% LB medium was incubated for 0 to 24 h. The samples were diluted 10-fold with sterile water and purified using NucleoSpin® Virus (Takara Bio). Fluorescence quantification of the purified products was performed on a CFX Opus Real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The total reaction volume was 20 μl and consisted of 10 μl of THUNDERBIRD® Next SYBR™ qPCR Mix, 300 nM forward primer (AGAATAATAGGTTCCGAAATAGGCAGG), 300 nM reverse primer (GTACTGGTAATAAGTTTTAACGGGGTC), 2 μl of purified product, and sterile water. The PCR procedure was set as follows: first 95 °C pre-denaturation for 30 s; second 95 °C denaturation for 5 s, 60 °C annealing for 30 s, 40 cycles. The concentration of M13 phages was estimated from the qPCR results. qPCR analyses of samples of Dex-droplets without AzSAP (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG) were performed using the same method.

Photo-control of self-assemblies of AzSAP

In the typical procedure for inducing depolymerization of AzSAP, an aqueous dispersion of a mixture of dextran and PEG (5.0 wt% dextran, 5.0 wt% PEG) in the presence of AzSAP (1.0 wt%) was irradiated with 365-nm light to induce trans-to-cis photoisomerization of the Az group using an Olympus IX-73 microscope equipped with a U-HGLGPS (130 W) and U-FUNA filter (λ = 365 ± 5 nm) (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) under phase-contrast microscopic observation. Similarly, samples were irradiated with 420-nm light to induce cis-to-trans photoisomerization under phase-contrast microscopy using a U-FBVW filter (λ = 420 ± 20 nm). Light irradiation at 350 nm and 450 nm to induce trans-to-cis and cis-to-trans photoisomerization for macroscopic observations was performed using a MAX-303 300-W Xe-lamp (Asahi Spectra, Tokyo, Japan) through filters (λ = 350 ± 5 nm and 450 ± 5 nm, respectively).

Statistics and reproducibility

Experiments, including preparation of peptide fibers, stabilization of ATPS droplets, rheological measurements, and investigations of light-responsive behaviors of the peptide fibers, were performed at least three times to ensure reproducibility. To evaluate the size of Dex-droplets from microscopic images, the diameters of 100 Dex-droplets were measured.
