Inhibition of WNK Kinases in NK Cells Disrupts Cellular Osmoregulation and Control of Tumor Metastasis
Authors: Ji Sung Kim, John H. Kehrl
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe serine/threonine with-no-lysine (WNK) kinase family function in blood pressure control, electrolyte homeostasis, and cellular osmoregulation. These kinases and their downstream effectors are considered promising therapeutic targets in hypertension and stroke. However, the role of WNK kinases in immune cells remains poorly understood.MethodsUsing the small-molecule WNK kinase inhibitors WNK463 and WNK-IN-11, we investigated how WNK kinase inhibition affects natural killer (NK) cell physiology.ResultsWNK kinase inhibition with WNK463 or WNK-IN-11 significantly decreased IL-2-activated NK cell volume, motility, and cytolytic activity. Treatment of NK cells with these inhibitors induced autophagy by activating AMPK and inhibiting mTOR signaling. Moreover, WNK kinase inhibition increased phosphorylation of Akt and c-Myc by misaligning activity of activating kinases and inhibitory phosphatases. Treatment of tumor-bearing mice with WNK463 impaired tumor metastasis control by adoptively transferred NK cells.ConclusionThe catalytic activity of WNK kinases has a critical role of multiple aspects of NK cell physiology and their pharmacologic inhibition negatively impacts NK cell function.

Introduction

Osmoregulation is a vital process that regulates the osmotic pressure of fluids and electrolytic balances in organisms by the control of water and salt concentrations. The with-no-lysine (WNK) serine/threonine kinases respond to osmotic/hypertonic stress and play an important role in maintaining intracellular ion and cell volume homeostasis [1]. There are four WNK kinase family members (WNK1–4) in mammals [2]. WNK kinases activate their downstream target kinases, oxidative stress responsive 1 (OSR1) and SPS/STE20-related proline-alanine-rich kinase (SPAK) [3]. Subsequently, activated OSR1 and SPAK phosphorylate solute carrier family 12 (SLC12) ion cotransporters activating the Na+-coupled Cl− importers (NCC, NKCC1, and NKCC2) and inhibiting the K+-Cl− cotransporters (KCC1-4) [3]. Physiologically, WNK kinases help maintain normal blood pressure and salt homeostasis [4, 5]. In the kidney, WNK-SPAK/OSR1 pathway activates NCC and NKCC2, which mediate renal sodium reabsorption [1]. Thiazides and furosemide are FDA-approved antihypertensive drugs that have been in use for decades targeting NCC and NKCC2, respectively.

Genetic studies in humans have shown that mutations in WNK1 and WNK4 cause familial hyperkalemic hypertension (also known as pseudohypoaldosteronism type II or Gordon’s syndrome). A large deletion in the first WNK1 intron leads to WNK1 overexpression [6], while missense mutations in WNK4 disrupt its interaction with the E3 ligase complex, resulting in increases in WNK4 protein expression [7]. Genetically engineered mouse models to analyze the function of the WNK kinases in vivo exist. Wnk1+/− mice have low blood pressure [8], while loss of both alleles causes embryonic lethality possibly from cardiovascular defects [8]. In contrast, mice with a first intron Wnk1 deletion or that express a transgene for the familial hyperkalemic hypertension-causing mutation Q562E WNK4 have elevated blood pressure [4, 5]. These findings make the WNK-SPAK/OSR1 signaling pathway a promising candidate and target for development of a new class of antihypertensive drugs and prompted the development of several small-molecule inhibitors. WNK kinase inhibitors include WNK463 [9] and WNK kinase inhibitor 11 (WNK-IN-11) [10]. Identified SPAK inhibitors include STOCK1S-14279 [11], closantel, rafoxanide [12], verteporfin [13], 20I [14], and ZT-1a [15]. STOCK1S-50699 and STOCK2S-26016 inhibit SPAK/OSR1 activation by blocking interaction between SPAK/OSR1 and WNK kinases [16]. Besides blood pressure regulation, the WNK kinase pathway affects neuronal Cl− homeostasis [17], autophagy [18], cancer [19], and cell division [20]. Neuropathic pain [21], stroke [22], cancer [19], and hypertension may benefit from therapeutic targeting WNK kinases and their downstream effectors.

Important functional roles for WNK kinases in immune system cells have emerged. WNK1 is required for optimal T-cell migration and homing into lymphoid organs [23]. Moreover, WNK1-mediated ion homeostasis is required for early thymocyte development [24]. WNK1-SPAK/OSR1 pathway is crucial for pre-TCR-induced upregulation of MYC, which regulates the expression of cell-cycle genes [24]. In macrophages, WNK1, but not WNK4, negatively regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation by balancing intracellular chloride concentration [25]. WNK kinase inhibition by WNK463 and WNK-IN-11 as well as SPAK/OSR1 inhibition by closantel and rafoxanide increase caspase-1 p20 and mature IL-1β release [25]. WNK1 also assists B-cell migration, proliferation, and antibody responses [26].

Natural killer (NK) cells are a type of cytotoxic lymphocytes of the innate immune system that mediate antitumor and antiviral responses. Here we investigate whether the WNK kinase inhibition impacts NK cells. We show that small-molecule WNK kinase inhibition reduces NK cell size, migratory capacity, and cytolytic activity. Notably, WNK kinase inhibition impairs adoptively transferred NK cells control of B16 tumor metastases to the lung. WNK kinase inhibition induces autophagy by increased AMPK signaling and increases phosphorylation of c-Myc at S62 residue and Akt at S473 residue by misbalancing kinase/phosphatase activity.

Materials and Methods

Mice and Cells

C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. LifeAct-GFP mice [27] were backcrossed 12 times on to a C57BL/6 background. All mice used in this study were 8–14 weeks of age. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Splenic NK cells ware isolated by negative selection using NK isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Purified NK cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1,000 U/mL recombinant human IL-2 (NCI Fredrick), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 2 mml-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 50 μm 2-mercaptoethanol. NK cell purity was greater than 92%. IL-2-activated NK cells were used on day 6–8 of culture. B16-F10 cells were a kind gift from Dr. Germain (NIAID/NIH) and cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. Yac-1 and RMA-S cells were kind gifts from Dr. Yewdell (NIAID/NIH). YTS and 721.221 cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS.

Flow Cytometry

Single cells were resuspended in complete medium at the presence or absence of various concentration of inhibitors or sodium chloride for indicated time at the 37°C, which are listed in the resource and reagent tables in the supplement. Data acquisition of forward scatter of the NK cells was done on FACS Canto II (BD) flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo 10.8.x software (Treestar). We based our assumption of the osmolarity of RPMI 1640 medium on the manufacturer’s datasheet, which indicates a value of 300 mOsm/kg. Osmolarity was determined through the measurement of the volume of additional water containing 3 μm WNK463.

Cytotoxicity Assay

NK cells were incubated with B16-F10 cells in the presence or absence of 3 μM WNK463 in 96-well plates at various effector-to-target cell ratios. After 4 h incubation, the plates were centrifuged, and 100 μL of the supernatants was transferred to new 96-well plates. B16-F10 cell death was determined using LDH release assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Takara). The percentage of specific lysis was calculated from LDH content as follows: (experimental release−target spontaneous release−effector spontaneous release)/(target maximum release−target spontaneous release) × 100%. To measure NK cell cytotoxicity against suspended target cells, 721.221, Yac-1 or RMA-S cells were stained with 0.5 μM CFSE dye for 15 min in 37°C water bath. Then, cells were washed and incubated for 30 min. After that, 721.221, Yac-1, or RMA-S cells were cocultured with NK cells in the presence or absence of 3 μM WNK463 or 10 μM WNK-IN-11 into round-bottom 96-well plates. 721.221 cells were cocultured with YTS cells in the indicated concentration of WNK463. After 2 h incubation, cells were harvested and washed with 1% BSA-contained PBS. Cells were stained with propidium iodide for 15 min and analyzed by flow cytometry. Target cell death was determined by % of CFSE+PI+ cells in total CFSE+ cells.

RNA Interference

siRNAs to target human WNK1 (Gene ID: 655125) and control siRNA were purchased (Santa Cruz). 721.221 cells were transfected with siRNAs (1 μM) using Neon™ transfection system (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and used for cytotoxicity assay. Gene knockdown efficiency was checked by immunoblotting with WNK1 antibody.

RT-PCR

Total RNA from NK cells was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596026) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the Omniscript RT kit (Qiagen, 205113). RT-PCR was performed with the synthesized cDNA using Thunderbird SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo, QPS-201) and primers for genes of interest on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences are listed in supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000540744). The results were normalized to GAPDH. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunoblotting

NK cells were lysed in a buffer of 20 mm HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mm β-Glycerophosphate, 1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100, and 10% (vol/vol) glycerol with cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail and PhosStop™ phosphatase inhibitor tablets (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 4°C. The lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes by the iBLOT gel transfer system (Thermo Fisher). The membrane was incubated with 5% BSA (w/v) in TBS buffer (25 mm Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mm NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h, and then reacted with the primary Ab in TBS buffer with 5% BSA (w/v) overnight by shaking at 4°C. The appropriate second Abs conjugated to HRP was used to detect the protein of interest via enhanced chemiluminescence. All the primary and secondary antibodies used, and their source are included in the resource and reagent tables in the supplement. To determine autophagic flux, 50 nM Bafilomycin A1 (Calbiochem) was used to inhibit autophagosome-lysosome fusion.

Chemotaxis Assay

Chemotaxis assays were performed using a transwell chamber (Costar) as previously described [28]. NK cells were added to the upper wells of a 24-well transwell plate containing a 5-μm insert. The lower wells contained 10 ng/mL CCL5 in 600 μL of media. The numbers of cells that migrated to the lower well after 1-h incubation were counted for 60 s using a FACS Canto II flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). The percent migration was calculated by the numbers of cells that migrated into the bottom chamber divided by the total number of cells in the starting cell suspension and multiplying the results by 100. For these experiments using WNK463 (MedChemExpress), WNK-IN-11, NaCl, or bumetanide (Cayman), the inhibitor was added during a 1-h preincubation phase and present in both chambers of the chemotaxis chamber. DMSO alone served as the control.

Phosphatase Assay

Phosphatase activity was assayed using a Ser/Thr phosphatase assay kit to detect phosphatase activity by dephosphorylation of the phosphopeptide (Upstate). For cellular assay, NK cells were treated with WNK kinase inhibitors or okadaic acid as a positive control for an hour. Then cells were washed in HBSS, then lysed on ice in phosphatase assay buffer (20 mm imidazole-HCl, 2 mm EDTA, 2 mm EGTA, 0.1% NP-40, pH 7.0, with freshly added cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail). For cell-free assay, cell lysates were treated with each inhibitor for 30 min. Protein concentration was measured, and the same amount of protein was used for this assay. PP2A activity was assayed by incubating the cell lysates with the synthetic phosphopeptide (K-R-pT-I-R-R) at 37°C for 10 min prior to detection with malachite green phosphate detection solution, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Upstate).

Time-Lapse Imaging

The day before the assay, glass-bottom culture dishes (MatTek) were coated with Matrigel (Corning) in RPMI1640 media at 4°C. On the following day, the dishes were washed 3 times with complete media. Subsequently, LifeAct-GFP NK cells were seeded onto the dish and allowed to incubate for 30 min to facilitate attachment. Images were recorded every 15 s for a total of 30 min. At this point, the image recording was paused, and the media was carefully replaced with media containing WNK463 at a concentration of 3 μM, or extra NaCl (90 mM). The imaging was then restarted for another 30 min. After that, the image recording was paused, and the media was carefully washed out twice with fresh complete media. Finally, the imaging was restarted to continue observation. Images were acquired on a PerkinElmer UltraVIEW spinning disc confocal system (PerkinElmer Life Science), with a Zeiss Axiovert 200 inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss Microimaging) equipped with a ×40 oil-immersion objective (NA, 1.3). Images were transformed into three-dimensional videos using Imaris software v.9.8.0 64x (Oxford Instruments plc), and the track analysis was used for semiautomated tracking of cell motility in two dimensions by using the following parameters: autoregressive motion algorithm, estimated diameter 10 μm, background subtraction true, maximum distance 20 μm, and maximum gap size 3. Some tracks were manually examined and verified. To analyze the polarization index, AR (aspect ratio) values (cell length/cell width) were automatically measured and calculated using ImageJ software (NIH). The average GFP gray value and integrated GFP density per LifeAct-expressing cell were measured by ImageJ.

In vivo Experiment

B16-F10 cells (4 × 105 cells/mouse) were injected by tail vein into C57BL/6 mice on day 0. NK cells (I.V. 4 × 106 cells/mouse) and/or WNK463 (I.P. 1 mg/kg) were injected on day 2. Then WNK463 was injected daily up to day 7. Lungs were collected on day 14, and the metastatic nodules were counted.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using Graph-pad Prism 9.x (La Jolla, CA, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was conducted to analyze the distribution of the data. If the data followed a normal distribution, a parametric ANOVA test was performed. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Statistical significance was assessed by p < 0.05 to be considered statistically significant. p values for each comparison are indicated in the figure legends.

Results

Small-Molecule WNK Kinase Inhibitors Decreased NK Cell Volume Mimicking Hypertonic Stress and Blocked Normal Regulatory Volume Increase (RVI)

WNK1 deletion in HEK293 cells impairs SPAK/OSR1 activity and the RVI response following hypertonic stress [29, 30]. RNA sequence data indicate that both human and murine NK cells predominately express WNK1 with very low amounts of WNK2, WNK3, and WNK4 mRNA (GEO accession number: GSE227743 and GSE122597) [31]. To assess the role of WNK kinases in NK cell RVI, we first exposed murine IL-2-activated NK cells to hypertonic stress by adding increasing amounts of NaCl or sucrose to the culture media. As expected, this caused a concentration dependent decrease in NK cell volumes (Fig. 1a). Next, we tested whether the WNK kinase inhibitors affected NK cell volume. Both WNK kinase inhibitors, pan-family member inhibitor WNK463 and the WNK1-specific inhibitor WNK-IN-11, dose dependently decreased NK cell volume without any change in external osmolytes (Fig. 1a). These results suggested that constitutive WNK1 activity in the activated NK cells maintained cellular osmotic homeostasis. To determine whether hypotonicity could restore the WNK463 shrunken cell volumes, we employed hypotonic medium by dilution with water. This increased the volume of the shrunken cells arguing that WNK1 inhibition had affected activity of the Na+/K+/Cl− transporters, leading to net ion egress, water loss, and cell shrinkage (Fig. 1b). Next, we monitored the cell volumes as a function of time following hypertonic stress or following WNK kinase inhibitor treatment. As previously, added NaCl and the WNK kinase inhibitors decreased NK cell volume. However, the NaCl-treated cell partially recovered their cell volume over time, while the WNK463- or WNK-IN-11-treated cells did not, i.e., no RVI occurred (Fig. 1c). Thus, WNK1 maintains NK cell volume homeostasis and is needed for RVI following hypertonic stress.

Hypertonic stress leads to WNK1 phase separation and phosphorylation on multiple residues [30]. Active WNK1 phosphorylates T185 and T243 in the catalytic domains of the murine STE20 kinases OSR1 and SPAK, respectively. In lymphocytes, OSR1 phosphorylation also occurs downstream of chemokine or antigen receptor triggered PI3K/Akt signaling [23]. Activated Akt phosphorylates WNK1 at T58 (T60 for human) [23]. To assess the status of the WNK1/SPAK-OSR1 signaling pathway in activated NK cells, we exposed the cells to the CCR5 ligand CCL5 for 1 min, or not. One hour prior to CCL5, we added DMSO, WNK463, WNK-IN-11, or NaCl to the cell culture. We collected cell lysates and immunoblotted them with antibodies directed at WNK1, pT58 WNK1, OSR1, or pT185 OSR1. We focused on OSR1 because of its higher expression level in NK cells (GEO accession number: GSE122597) [31]. The different cell lysates had similar amounts of total WNK1 as well as OSR1. The CCL5 treatment increased pT58 WNK1 phosphorylation, and the WNK463 pretreatment enhanced CCL5-induced pT58 WNK1 phosphorylation. WNK-IN-11 had little effect while NaCl inhibited the induction by CCL5 (Fig. 1d). WNK1 is phosphorylated by Akt at Thr-58, which contributes to the serum- and glucocorticoid-induced protein kinase SGK1 activation. However, this site has little effect on WNK1 kinase activity by osmotic stress [32]. The basal level of pT185 OSR1 increased following CCL5 exposure. Preincubation with both the WNK kinase inhibitors decreased pT185 OSR1 and blocked the CCL5-induced increase. Hypertonic stimulation by added NaCl strongly increased pT185 OSR1.

Hypertonic stress activates the MAPK p38 cascade and the transcription factor nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5), which increases the expression of genes that limit the adverse effects of hypertonicity [33]. We examined the status of p38 phosphorylation (T180/Y182) and NFAT5 gene expression in NK cells treated with WNK kinase inhibitors, added NaCl, or DMSO for 1 or 6 h, respectively. Both WNK kinase inhibition and NaCl-induced hypertonic stress in NK cells significantly increased p38 phosphorylation and NFAT5 mRNA expression (Fig. 1e, f). While the inhibitors similarly increased NFAT mRNA expression, WNK463 enhanced p38 phosphorylation more than WNK-IN-11. Besides increasing NFAT5 expression, the WNK kinase inhibitors and NaCl also increased WNK1 gene expression. These results confirm that WNK kinase inhibition had a hypertonic stress-like impact on the NK cells. We also confirmed that hyper- and hypoosmotic stress induced by NaCl, sucrose, and hypotonic medium increased the phosphorylation of OSR1 and p38 (Fig. 1g).

WNK Kinase Inhibition in Activated NK Cells Increases Autophagy and Suppresses mTORC1 Signaling

Hypertonic stress induces autophagy in murine embryonic fibroblasts independent of the unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) complex while autophagy following WNK1 depletion depends upon ULK1 [18, 34]. To test whether WNK kinase inhibition induced autophagy in activated NK cells, we prepared cell lysates from NK cells treated with WNK463, WNK-IN-11, or NaCl for an hour in the presence or absence of bafilomycin A1 (BafA1), an inhibitor of autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Autophagic flux is calculated as the ratio of LC3-II levels in the presence or absence of BafA1 [35, 36]. Immunoblotting p62, LC3-I, and LC3-II levels in the NK cell lysates led to the conclusion that WNK kinase inhibition and hypertonic stress increased autophagic flux and reduced p62 levels (Fig. 2a). To understand the mechanism, we immunoblotted DMSO control, WNK463, WNK-IN-11, or NaCl-treated cell lysates with antibodies directed against phosphorylated liver kinase B1 (LKB1), AMP-activated protein kinase alpha subunit (AMPKα), or tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2) on T180/Y182, S428, T172, or S1387, respectively. Hypertonic stress decreases mTOR signaling [37] and increases LKB1/AMPK signaling [38], which in-turn phosphorylates TSC2 increasing its activity [39]. A GTPase-activating protein TSC2 reduces Rheb GTP levels inhibiting mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) activation [37]. In NK cells, inhibition of WNK kinase activity increased LKB1, AMPKα, and TSC2 phosphorylation at the tested residues (Fig. 2b). The NaCl-treated cell lysates showed a similar pattern of phosphorylation with exception of pAMPKα (T172).

Next, we examined mTOR and its downstream targets: S6 ribosomal protein (S235/236) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1, T37/46 and S65). Phosphorylation of mTOR downstream targets was decreased by WNK kinase inhibition (Fig. 2c). mTOR phosphorylates ULK1 at S757, and its dephosphorylation promotes the interaction between ULK1 and AMPK promoting autophagy. There are multiple bands in total 4E-BP1 immunoblotting. 4E-BP1 has multiple phosphorylation sites, which include T37, T46, T70, and S65 [40, 41]. mTORC1 phosphorylates 4E-BP1 priming sites (T37/46), which still allows a 4E-BP1/eIF4E interaction. However, when S65 on 4E-BP1 is phosphorylated, it dissociates from eIF4E [40]. These data imply that WNK kinase inhibition suppresses the mTORC1 signaling of NK cells and promotes autophagy. Immunoblots of the NaCl-treated NK cell lysates resembled those from the WNK kinase inhibitor-treated cells except for the unaltered AMPKα phosphorylation at the time point examined. To determine if hypertonic stress had affected AMPKα phosphorylation in the NK cells, we assessed pT172 AMPK levels at earlier time points. We found a 1-min exposure to hypertonic stress partially decreased pAMPKα, which gradually recovered to basal levels at 60 min (online suppl. Fig. S1a). In activated NK cells, acute inhibition of WNK kinases disrupts the mTORC1 signaling and promotes autophagy largely phenocopying their response to hypertonic stress.

WNK Kinases Limit Akt Signaling, Maintain c-Myc Stability, and Preserve Phosphatase Activity in Activated NK Cells

The reduced phosphorylated mTOR at S2448, a major Akt target residue, suggested that WNK1 inhibition or the subsequent hypertonic stress had impaired Akt activation. Yet a previous study using murine endometrial cells had shown that reducing WNK1 expression augments Akt activity [42]. To assess the status of Akt in the WNK kinase inhibited NK cells and to compare them to hypertonic stressed cells, we examined the status of pS473 Akt and two Akt targets: pT246 proline-rich Akt substrate of 40 kDa (PRAS40) and pS256 Forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1). PRAS40 is an mTORC1 component that promotes cell growth and survival, while FoxO1 is a transcription factor that is a major target of insulin signaling. We also checked whether the chemokine CCL5 appropriately activated Akt and the Akt substrates in the WNK kinase-inhibited cells and NaCl-treated cells. In line with the WNK1-deficient uterine cells, WNK463 treatment raised the basal level of pS473 Akt and increased the phosphorylation of the two Akt targets; however, WNK-IN-11 had less, and NaCl had no significant effect (Fig. 3a). As expected, CCL5 exposure increased pS473 Akt, pT246 PRAS40, and pS256 FoxO1, and both WNK kinase inhibitors augmented these responses, while NaCl exposure again had no impact. We compared increasing concentrations of the two WNK kinase inhibitors on the CCL5-induced pS473 Akt. At each dose tested, WNK463 raised the pAkt response more than did the addition of WNK-IN-11. In contrast, WNK463 dose dependently decrease the CCL5-triggered pERK response, while WNK-IN-11 had no effect (online suppl. Fig. S1b). Despite the increased Akt activity following WNK kinase inhibition, pS2448 mTOR remained low as did its downstream targets T389 70-kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K), and S757 ULK1 (Fig. 3b). Unexpectedly, pS2448 mTOR in control NK cells was not enhanced upon CCL5 treatment for 1 min. We further examined an additional time point of CCL5 stimulation in NK cells. However, no change in pS2448 mTOR was observed even after NK cells were stimulated with CCL5 for up to 30 min (online suppl. Fig. S1c). We hypothesized that p-mTOR might already be fully activated in IL-2-activated NK cells.

Protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) negatively regulates Akt activity [43, 44]. Furthermore, in the WNK1-deficient uterine cells, the increase in Akt activity depends on PP2A loss [42]. To initially assess PP2A phosphatase activity in activated NK cells subjected to WNK kinase inhibition, we focused on the phosphorylation status of c-Myc at S62 and T58, as well as c-Myc stability. Activated proline-directed kinases phosphorylate c-Myc at S62, which stabilizes and enhance its activity [45], while glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) phosphorylates pS62 c-Myc at T58. This leads to a PP2A-dependent dephosphorylation of the stabilizing pS62 residue. Singly phosphorylated T58 c-Myc undergoes ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent degradation [45]. Consistent with constitutive PP2A activity in the activated NK cells, treatment with PP2A inhibitor okadaic acid or LB-100 raised pS62 c-Myc and pT172 AMPKα levels (online suppl. Fig. S1d) [45, 46]. The WNK kinase inhibitors also dose dependently increased pS62 c-Myc, but they concomitantly decreased total c-Myc expression. Conversely, pERK1/2 and pS6 underwent dose dependent dephosphorylation on the tested residues with stable protein levels (Fig. 4a). The short half-life of c-Myc [47, 48] complicates the interpretation of these results. The reduced c-Myc protein 1 h after adding WNK463 could be accounted for by enhanced PP2A activity, counter to our expectation, or reduced protein synthesis secondary to hypertonic stress. Therefore, we examined c-Myc phosphorylation and expression level at earlier time points. A 15-min exposure to WNK463 increased pS62 c-Myc without changing c-Myc protein levels. Subsequently, c-Myc protein gradually declined yet the pS62 c-Myc/c-Myc ratio progressively rose (Fig. 4b). At the 15-min time point, the basal pS235/236 S6 ribosomal protein and pT203/Y205 ERK1/2 levels had already declined, while pT183/Y185 JNK levels had increased. Active Akt limits the constitutively activity of GSK-3β by phosphorylating S9, yet the addition of WNK463 did not affect the basal levels of pS9 GSK-3β nor that of pT58 c-Myc. Neither the ERK inhibitor SCH772984 nor the GSK-3β inhibitor CHIR99021 affected the short-term WNK463 induced increases in pS62 c-Myc. CHIR99021 eliminated and SCH7772984 reduced pT58 c-Myc, respectively, both in the presence and absence of WNK463. CHIR99021 increased c-Myc protein while the presence of WNK463 reduced this increase (Fig. 4c). Together these results suggested that loss of WNK kinase signaling in the activated NK cells had caused a misbalance between serine/threonine kinase and phosphatase activity targeting both Akt and c-Myc.

To more directly test whether WNK kinase inhibition had affected PP2A phosphatase activity, we measured activated NK cell lysate phosphatase activity directed at the short peptide K-R-pT-I-R-R, a PP2A target sequence. Pretreatment with WNK kinase inhibitors or okadaic acid decreased the phosphatase activity in cell lysates compared to controls (Fig. 4d). However, when we added the WNK kinase inhibitors directly to activated NK cell lysates, they no longer affected PP2A activity, whereas the addition of okadaic acid completely repressed it. These data indicate that a short inhibition of WNK kinase catalytic activity reduces the activity of PP2A phosphatases.

WNK Kinase Inhibition Arrests NK Cell Motility

Little is known about the impact of hypertonic stress or the functional role of WNK1 signaling in NK cell motility. Hypertonic stress causes HL-60 cells and neutrophils to shrink and results in the accumulation of F-actin at the plasma membrane. This newly generated F-actin forms a continuous ring under the membrane [49]. To follow the impact of inhibiting WNK kinases on the localization of F-actin and NK cell polarity, we used NK cells isolated from LifeAct-GFP mice. Live-cell imaging of NK cells migrating on Matrigel-coated plates (Fig. 5a, online suppl. video 1) showed that prior to drug addition, the NK cells showed normal front-rear polarity with asymmetric distribution of F-actin accumulating at the leading edges of the cells. Following addition of WNK463, the cells rapidly lost their polarity and exhibited a symmetric, submembranous distribution of F-actin. Drug washout rapidly restored the NK cells to their predrug treatment state. Addition of WNK463 decreased the polarization index calculated by the relationship between cell width and length and the cell surface contact area (Fig. 5b). The WNK463 treatment increased the average GFP gray value/cell without changing the integrated GFP density indicating that WNK463 induced NK cell shrinkage (Fig. 5b). We further investigated the impact of hypertonic stress on NK cell motility. Hyperosmotic stress induced by additional NaCl exhibited a similar phenomenon to the inhibition of WNK kinases by WNK463 (online suppl. Fig. S2).

Next, we analyzed NK cell motility. After adding WNK463, the instantaneous speed of the NK cells gradually declined over 20 min eventually reaching a complete standstill (instantaneous speed less than 2 μm/min, defined by estimated the diffusion coefficient) [50, 51]. Cell motility was rapidly restored after drug washout (Fig. 5c). The trajectory and mean speed of NK cells also indicated that cell motility was confined by WNK463 treatment compared to either pretreatment or following washout (Fig. 5d, e). Track straightness can be used to compare directionally cell movement. The track straightness decreased following WNK463 treatment (Fig. 5e). Finally, we plotted the mean-squared displacement using a log-log plot (Fig. 5f). The slope α of these plots is a measure of the diffusion behavior of particles and can be applied to evaluate cell migration patterns. Log-log curves with slope α < 1, α = 1, or 1< α < 2 indicate subdiffusion (confined motion), diffusion (Brownian motion), or superdiffusion, respectively [52–55]. Before drug treatment or after washout, NK cells showed superdiffusive movement with α ≈ 1.21 and 1.45, respectively. However, NK cells treated with drug moved with subdiffusive motion of α ≈ 0.48. Overall, live cell imaging data showed that WNK kinase inhibition decreased NK cell size and severely reduced their motility.

WNK Kinase Inhibition Impairs NK Cell Migration, Cytotoxicity, and Control of Tumor Metastasis

Next, we investigated whether WNK kinase inhibition affects NK cells encounter with tumor cells. WNK kinase inhibitors impaired NK cell migration to the chemokine CCL5 (Fig. 6a). While previous research has shown that the NKCC1/2 inhibitor bumetanide inhibits CCL21-induced migration of CD4+ T cells [23], it did not affect the CCL5-induced migration of IL-2-activated NK cells. WNK kinase inhibition also severely decreased NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against several tumor target cell lines including B16-F10 melanoma, Yac-1 lymphoma, and RMA-S lymphoma cells (Fig. 6b). Hypertonic stress induced with added sodium chloride (90 mm) also decreased NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against B16-F10 cells (Fig. 6c). We also utilized the YTS human NK cell line and its target 721.221 human HLA-negative B-cell lymphoblastoid cell line. WNK463 treatment dose dependently decreased YTS cell cytotoxicity (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, reducing the WNK1 protein level in YTS cells using siRNA reduced their cytotoxicity against 721.221 cells (Fig. 6e).

Discussion

The present study reveals that constitutive WNK1 signaling maintains intracellular isotonicity and cellular homeostasis in activated NK cells. Acute inhibition of WNK kinase activity, either by the ATP competitive pan-WNK kinase inhibitor WNK463, which targets the catalytic site of the four mammalian WNK kinases, or by the allosteric WNK1 inhibitor WNK-IN-11, largely mimics the effects of placing the cells into a hypertonic environment. Such an environment causes activated NK cells to shrink, but they partially recover their cell volume by boosting the activity of their WNK/OSR1/transporter cascade. Enhanced WNK1 signaling increases Na+/K+/Cl− influx while decreasing K+/Cl− efflux. The net result is the flow of water into the cell [29, 30, 56]. The WNK kinase inhibited NK cells likely shrink due to a net ion efflux, and since they cannot activate the WNK/OSR1/transporter cascade, they fail to recover their cell volume.

Several lines of evidence indicate that acute inhibition of WNK1 in the activated NK cells caused hypertonic stress. First, as stated above, inhibition of WNK1 caused cell shrinkage much like increasing the extracellular NaCl concentration. Second, exposure to hypertonic stress or WNK kinase inhibition rapidly activated p38 and within several hours had increased NFAT5 mRNA expression. NFAT5 helps restore cellular homeostasis by activating a genetic program of osmoadaptive responses, where intracellular electrolytes are gradually replaced by uncharged small organic osmolytes [57]. Third, both hypertonic stress and WNK kinase inhibition induced autophagy. Autophagosomes accumulate shortly after a hypertonic challenge due to an increase in autophagic flux. Autophagy provides an osmoprotective response helping cells clear damaged and aggregated proteins caused by intracellular crowding. Fourth, hypertonic stress and WNK kinase inhibition rapidly decreased pERK1/2 in the activated NK cells. Similarly, the addition of 100 mm NaCl to cultured murine embryonic fibroblasts rapidly decreases their pERK1/2 levels [39]. Fifth, WNK kinase inhibition caused a profound rearrangement of the cytoskeleton, loss of cell polarity, reduced chemotaxis, and poor motility. Finally, both exposure to hypertonic stress and WNK kinase inhibition impaired NK cell cytotoxicity in vitro. Furthermore, prolonged disruption of WNK1 in vivo led to poor NK cell killing of an adoptively transferred tumor.

In addition to regulating ion transport, WNK kinases affect PP2A, Akt, TGF-β, and Wnt signaling pathways [42, 58, 59]. In activated NK cells with a constitutively active WNK1/OSR1/transporter signaling pathway, the hypertonic stress response dominates the signaling landscape following its acute interruption. In contrast, a more prolonged inhibition or a genetic deficiency of WNK1 protein will unmask defects in other WNK kinase signaling programs and likely trigger compensatory pathways. For example, a cursory assessment of HEK293 cells following CRISPR/Cas9 deletion of WNK1 revealed a decrease in WNK4 and WNK2, an increase in WNK3, and an increase in E3 ubiquitin ligase CUL3 and the adapter protein KLH3. WNK1 and WNK4 are substrates of the KLH3-CUL3 E3 ubiquitin ligase [29]. A careful assessment of the compensations that occur in specific cell types lacking WNK1 should help unravel the direct versus indirect effects of its loss.

Both hypertonic stress and WNK1 mRNA knockdowns have been reported to induce autophagy. The kinetics of autophagy induction in the NK cells and the similar biochemical profile of NK cells exposed to increased extracellular NaCl or WNK kinase inhibitors favors a more prominent role for hypertonic stress versus the acute loss of WNK1 signaling. A two- or three-day knockdown of WNK1 mRNA in adherent cells enhances AMPK signaling and autophagic flux without affecting mTOR signaling [18, 60]. In the activated NK cells, WNK kinase inhibition not only enhanced AMPK signaling but also decreased mTOR signaling. Unexpectedly, inhibition of WNK kinase enhanced pAkt signaling, a well-known upstream kinase of mTORC1. However, this effect did not affect mTOR signaling in NK cells. A knockdown of STK39, but not OSR1, in HEK293 cells also causes an increase in autophagy. Whether disrupted ion transporter activity contributed to the enhanced autophagic flux noted following the WNK1 knockdowns is unknown.

Both the loss of WNK1 and hypertonic stress altered the organization of the cytoskeleton and decreased cell motility. WNK1-deficient T and B cells responded poorly to chemokines in vitro, and following their adoptive transfer into recipient mice, they displayed reduced homing and impaired migration within lymph nodes [23, 26]. In cultured podocytes, activated OSR1/SPAK increases lamellipodia, accelerates cell migration, and promotes traction force, while depletion of WNK1 inhibits these responses [61]. The addition of WNK463 to mouse B cells migrating on integrin/chemokine-coated plates causes the cells to depolarize and arrest their motility much like the activated NK cells [62]. While the induction of hypertonic stress likely accounts for these acute effects, the motility defects in the WNK1-deficient cells support additional roles for WNK1 in cell adhesion and motility. The reduction in NK cell cytotoxicity by adding the WNK kinase inhibitors mirrored that of hypertonicity, yet that mechanism likely does not explain how WNK463 inhibited NK cell-mediated tumor reduction. WNK kinase inhibition may have decreased contact-dependent elimination of lung B16-F10 tumor cells or interfered with the extravasation of NK cells into the lung tissue. Verifying that the WNK463 treatment did not affect NK cell tumor site localization would eliminate the second possibility. Previous studies suggest that WNK463 has antitumor activity. WNK kinase inhibition decreases tumor-induced ectopic vessel formation, tumor cell proliferation, and metastases [63–65]. Furthermore, elevated WNK1 expression predicts a poor outcome in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer [66, 67]. In this study, WNK463 in the absence of adoptively transferred NK cells did not affect the number of B16-F10 lung metastases.

While the loss of normal ion transporter activity can explain the NK cell shrinkage and subsequent the decline in pERK, increase in p38 activity, induction of NFAT5, increase in autophagy, loss of cell polarity, decline in cell motility, and poor cytotoxic activity, it does not explain the increase in pAkt and certain Akt substrates, nor the increase in pS62 c-Myc and decline in cell phosphatase activity. Hypertonicity had no effect on pAkt levels in the activated NK cells, suggesting that pAkt did not affect WNK1 activity. As mentioned previously, in murine endometrial cells, WNK1 represses Akt phosphorylation by interacting with PPP2R1A, stabilizing it and other PP2A subunits [42]. However, no change in PPP2R1A levels had occurred to explain the increased pAkt levels in the NK cells. Nevertheless, increased phosphorylation of several PP2A-regulated sites supports a role for WNK kinase activity in maintaining PP2A activity. Following WNK kinase inhibition, the phosphorylation at S473 Akt, S428 LKB1, T172 AMPKα, and S62 c-Myc increased. In addition, when we measured intracellular phosphatase activity using a PP2A substrate following inhibitor treatment, it had declined. The rise in pS62 c-Myc occurred within 15 min of adding WNK463. c-Myc is phosphorylated at S62 by one of several proline-directed kinases, which transiently increases c-Myc stability and transcriptional activity. Since pERK1/2 levels simultaneously fell, the increased S62 c-Myc phosphorylation reflects either falling PP2A activity, increased JNK activity, or some combination. Inhibition of WNK kinases also destabilized c-Myc protein. By 1 h after WNK463, c-Myc proteins levels had fallen 80%. The reduction in c-Myc protein likely reflects both ongoing proteasomal degradation as well reduced c-Myc protein synthesis due to hypertonic stress.

Overall, our results indicate that WNK kinases help balance several downstream signaling pathways to maintain cellular homeostasis and to respond changing environmental conditions. In contrast to sessile cells, lymphocytes are subjected to multiple extracellular environments as they circulate from the blood into lymphoid organs, transit the lymphatics, and enter tissues and sites of inflammation. Lymphoid organs are hyperosmolar compared to serum and an inability to adapt to tonic/hypertonic stresses causes lymphoid hypocellularity and impaired antibody responses [68, 69]. Complex tumor microenvironments include tonicity challenges [70–72] that NK cells must overcome to successfully eradicate tumor cells. Constitutive and dynamic control of WNK1 expression and activity help NK cells negotiate these changing extracellular milieus. Indiscriminate targeting of WNK kinases for therapeutic purposes may impair NK cell function and that of other circulating immune cells.
