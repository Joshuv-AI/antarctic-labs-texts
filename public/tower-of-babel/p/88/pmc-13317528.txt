Spinal solitary fibrous tumor metastatic to the fourth ventricle: illustrative case
Authors: Ryan Wang, Shervin Pejhan, Claire I Coire, Lee Cyn Ang, Joseph F Megyesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDCNS solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare mesenchymal neoplasms that arise from the meninges and are characterized by a NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion and abundant STAT6 nuclear expression on immunohistochemistry. On imaging, SFTs can mimic meningiomas, but they differ in their clinical behavior. CNS SFTs have a propensity to recur locally and metastases, although rare, have been described.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 76-year-old male with a history of a lower cervical spine SFT treated with surgery and radiation therapy. He presented with progressively worsening gait and balance, and headaches. Imaging showed a fourth ventricular mass that was resected via a midline suboccipital craniectomy. Pathological analysis showed a WHO grade 2 epithelioid SFT, confirmed by STAT6 expression and a rare NAB2ex5-STAT6ex17 fusion variant. The histology slides from 22 years prior were compared and found to be similar, suggesting metastasis of the original tumor.LESSONSTo the authors’ knowledge, this is the first reported case of a spinal SFT with metastasis to the fourth ventricle and the first epithelioid SFT presenting within the fourth ventricle. Solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare and highly vascular mesenchymal neoplasms that are characterized by a genomic inversion at the 12q13 locus, NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion, and abundant STAT6 nuclear expression on immunohistochemistry.1 The 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors merges SFTs and hemangiopericytomas into a single entity (SFT) given their similar molecular features.1 In the CNS, SFTs predominantly arise from the meninges and can mimic meningiomas on imaging; however, their clinical behavior is drastically different. CNS SFTs are rare and comprise less than 1% of all intracranial tumors, with spinal SFTs being even rarer.1–3 Approximately 10% of CNS SFTs arise in the spine, predominantly in the thoracic region, and can have intradural and/or extradural involvement.2–4 Given the rarity of CNS SFTs, there are limited data about their clinical behavior and outcome. We describe the case of a patient with a previous cervical spine SFT that had been resected and locally irradiated who presented 22 years later with a fourth ventricular mass that was suspected to be a metastasis of the original tumor. This is also the first epithelioid SFT described in the fourth ventricle.

Illustrative Case

A 76-year-old male presented with a > 1-year history of progressively worsening gait and balance, mild headaches, and nausea and vomiting. The patient’s past medical history was significant for a posterior cervical laminectomy in 2002 for resection of a C5–7 cervical lesion. The pathology at that time was reported as a hemangiopericytoma. The patient then underwent focal radiation therapy to the lower cervical/upper thoracic spine with a dose of 50 Gy/25 daily fractions. There was no recurrence of the spinal lesion on serial imaging, and the last MRI study of the brain and cervical spine in 2016 did not show any new lesions.

On physical examination, he had evidence of truncal ataxia and wide-based gait. CT (Fig. 1A and B) showed a hyperdense fourth ventricular mass measuring 1.6 × 2.4 × 2.4 cm and no obstructive hydrocephalus. MRI (Fig. 1C and D) showed a heterogeneously enhancing 2.7 × 2.6 × 2.1–cm fourth ventricular mass with solid and cystic components. MRI of the complete spine was not done. CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis did not show metastatic disease. He was referred to the neurosurgery team for surgical management.

Surgical Management

The patient underwent a midline suboccipital craniectomy and transvermian approach for resection of the fourth ventricular mass. Transection of the inferior vermis was minimized and the fourth ventricle was opened, revealing a hypervascular mass within it. The tumor was not infiltrative and significant care was taken to avoid manipulation of the dorsal brainstem during the resection. Gross-total resection (GTR) and hemostasis were attained. An external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed intraoperatively.

Postoperative Course

The patient was admitted to the ICU postoperatively for delayed extubation. Postoperative MRI (Fig. 2A and B) confirmed GTR of the mass and a thin rim of diffusion restriction along the margins of the surgical cavity. Over the ensuing days, the patient was extubated and the EVD was weaned and removed after follow-up imaging did not demonstrate hydrocephalus. The patient had a challenging postoperative course complicated by pneumonia, immobility, and persistent speech and swallowing difficulties. He remained bedbound and did not show improvement. Repeat neuroimaging did not show new infarcts, hemorrhage, or hydrocephalus. Following discussions with the family, the patient was transferred to hospice for palliative care, where he died a short time later.

Postoperative axial (A) and sagittal (B) MR images of head with gadolinium contrast demonstrating GTR of the fourth ventricular mass.

Pathological Findings

Tissue samples revealed a hypercellular tumor composed of epithelioid cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 3A and B). Nuclei were moderately pleomorphic and contained finely dispersed chromatin and prominent eosinophilic nucleoli in some. Tumor cells were arranged in nests and sheets separated by thin-walled branching to thick-walled hyalinized vessels with frequent areas of hemorrhage (Fig. 3B). There was no necrosis. Tumor cells showed brisk mitotic activity (about 8 mitoses/mm2). On immunohistochemical evaluation, tumor cells were strongly positive for STAT6 and CD34 (Fig. 3C and D) and also positive for CD99, CD34, BCL-2, P53, and CK8/18. Tumor cells were negative for EMA, NeuN, Olig-2, S100, and GFAP. INI-1 expression was retained. The Ki-67 index was approximately 10%–15%. DNA mismatch repair testing did not reveal any abnormalities. Neurotrophic-tropomyosin kinase receptor (NTRK) gene fusion testing identified a NAB2ex5-STAT6ex17 fusion variant. The final diagnosis was epithelioid SFT, CNS WHO grade 2.

Thin-walled branching vessels are present among tumor cells. There was brisk mitotic activity (8 mitoses/mm2). Tumor cells show intense nuclear expression of STAT6 (C) and are diffusely positive for CD34 (D). Scale bar 50 μm (A and B), 80 μm (C), and 70 μm (D).

The patient’s initial pathology slides and report from 2002 were reviewed. Sections showed a cellular spindle cell tumor with cells arranged in featureless sheets punctated by numerous capillaries and occasional irregularly dilated vascular channels (Fig. 4A). The nuclei were mildly atypical. Frequent mitoses at up to 10 mitoses/10 high-power fields (HPF) (approximately 4.2 mitoses/mm2) were seen. There was no necrosis. Tumor cells were positive for vimentin and negative for GFAP, EMA, S100, actin, and desmin. Additional stains (not available in 2002) for STAT6 and CD99 were positive, supporting a diagnosis of SFT (Fig. 4B and C).

The nuclei are mildly atypical. Frequent mitotic figures are present without necrosis. Additional stains for STAT6 (B) and CD99 (C) were positive, further confirming the initial diagnosis of SFT. Scale bar 80 μm (A) and 50 μm (B and C).

Since the introduction of the 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors, SFT and hemangiopericytoma have been grouped under one entity: SFT.1 SFTs of the CNS and elsewhere in the body display a similar histological spectrum and are unified by common genetic features consisting of genomic inversion at the 12q13 locus and NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion. STAT6 is a dependable surrogate immunostain for detection of the fusion gene, making it a key diagnostic feature.1 CNS SFTs are divided into three histological grades: grade 1 tumors have < 2.5 mitoses/mm2, grade 2 tumors have ≥ 2.5 mitoses/mm2 without necrosis, and grade 3 tumors have ≥ 2.5 mitoses/mm2 with necrosis.1 The tumor in this report had approximately 8 mitoses/mm2 and no obvious necrosis, suggesting at least a WHO grade 2 tumor. The brisk mitotic rate may indicate a more aggressive tumor. Histologically, SFTs have varied phenotypes, but they classically appear as highly cellular tumors with spindled cells with hyalinized, dilated, branching blood vessels and a collagen matrix.1 Recently, there have been reports of an epithelioid SFT, with only two known CNS presentations in the literature. One, reported by Fu et al., was a recurrent intracranial SFT presenting 68 months after initial resection.8 The second case, by Zhao et al., was in the right occipital horn of the lateral ventricle.9 A case described by Huang et al. was an epithelioid SFT in the occipital subcutaneous tissue adherent to the skull.10 The clinical behavior of the epithelioid SFT variant is not defined. Our case would be the first of this rare subgroup of SFT presenting in the fourth ventricle. The lack of epithelioid cells in the initial tumor in our case is of unknown significance. However, given the suboptimal quality of the tissue slides from the original tumor, likely due to the significant sample age, a phenotypic change cannot be reliably confirmed. Interestingly, the case described by Fu et al. reported a phenotypic change from spindled morphology to epithelioid morphology.8 There are descriptions of “dedifferentiated” SFTs with epithelioid morphology characterized by loss of CD34 and a lack of morphological features of typical SFTs, among others.11 However, in both our case and the one of Fu et al., these findings are not present.

Specific NAB2-STAT6 fusion variants have been characterized with next-generation sequencing.12 Certain fusion variants may be correlated with different histopathological phenotypes and clinical factors including age, location, and propensity for recurrence or metastasis.13–15 Grouped together, NAB2ex5-7–STAT6ex16/17 variants are more common in meningeal SFTs and also appear to be associated with higher-grade phenotypes, increased mitoses, and higher risk of metastasis.13,16,17 Currently, there is no clear association between fusion subtype and overall survival (OS).15–17 The NAB2ex5-STAT6ex17 variant identified in this case is rare and has not been described in meningeal or CNS SFTs; only two soft tissue SFT cases harboring this fusion variant are reported in the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) (cancer.sanger.ac.uk). Although there are no published reports describing the clinical significance of the NAB2ex5-STAT6ex17 variant, its inclusion within the broader group of NAB2ex5-7–STAT6ex16/17 variants in Eschbacher et al. suggests that it may be associated with a more aggressive clinical course.17 Further characterization of NAB2-STAT6 fusion variants is required to better understand their relationships to the pathogenesis and clinical behavior of SFTs. Methylation classification of SFTs may also have an emerging role in the molecular and clinical characterization of these rare tumors.17 In our case, methylation profiling was considered; however, it was not possible due to the insufficient amounts and poor quality of the 2002 tissue.

Overall 5- and 10-year OS rates for intracranial SFT in the recent literature range from 78.3% to 98% and 60.2% to 80%, respectively.2,18,19 Survival data for spinal SFT are not well characterized given its rarity, but 5-year and 10-year survival have been reported to be around 76%.3,20 Given their extreme rarity, survival data for intraventricular SFTs, particularly fourth ventricular tumors, are lacking.

Resection remains the preferred upfront treatment for SFTs. These tumors can be locally aggressive and have a propensity to recur.2,21 For both intracranial and spinal SFTs, risk factors for recurrence identified in case series and meta-analyses include subtotal resection (STR), higher tumor grade, lack of postoperative radiation therapy, and, for spinal SFTs, intramedullary involvement.3,4,20,22 Several studies have shown a clear survival benefit of GTR over STR in addition to improved local control.21,23–25 In this case, although GTR was achieved, the patient had a poor outcome. The transvermian approach has been reported to have a higher risk of postoperative complications compared to the telovelar approach, although recent meta-analyses suggest that outcomes are comparable.26–28 The patient’s poor outcome was likely multifactorial, with the tumor’s high-risk location, the patient’s advanced age, and postoperative pneumonia being possible contributors. Therefore, an individualized approach accounting for the patient’s clinical status and tumor features and location should be taken. If safe, GTR remains the surgical goal.

The role of radiation therapy remains undefined. However, there are several studies showing improved progression-free survival (PFS) with radiation therapy following STR.18,21,23 Radiation therapy post-GTR has more variable outcomes, but it may still confer a benefit in PFS.18 Additionally, postoperative radiation therapy improves local tumor control, which likely contributes to increased PFS.18 Postoperative radiation therapy does not appear to influence OS.18,21,24,29,30 Indeed, there are reports indicating that radiation therapy does not influence rates of distant metastases, which is a cause of mortality in these patients.3 Kinslow et al. developed a risk stratification model for SFT utilizing a national database and characterized three risk categories, low, intermediate, and high, based on WHO grade and extent of resection.31 They found that radiation therapy provides an OS benefit in patients who have intermediate-risk (WHO grade 2 and STR) and high-risk (WHO grade 3 and any extent of resection) tumors.31 Overall, there is growing consideration for postoperative radiation therapy in patients with residual disease or higher-grade tumors.

CNS SFTs can exhibit extracranial and extraspinal spread to other organs including the lung, liver, abdomen, and long bones.30,32 There have been rare reports documenting metastasis from an intracranial SFT to the spine.33–36 However, to our knowledge, there are no reports of spinal to intracranial metastasis, let alone a metastasis to the fourth ventricle. Metastases usually occur via hematogenous spread, but CSF dissemination has been reported.34 The time interval to metastasis varies significantly, with Wu et al. reporting a range of 35–199 months after initial surgery and a meta-analysis by Giordan et al. reporting a mean time to metastasis of 7.4 years for intracranial SFT and 4.3 years for spinal SFT.3,21 Additionally, Giordan et al. found that the risk of metastasis was lower after the first 5 years following surgery.3 The rate of metastasis also varies, with rates reported as low as 3.1% to as high as 55%.2,3,21 A meta-analysis by El-Abtah et al. suggested that male sex may be a risk factor for metastasis.37 Other reported risk factors include higher WHO tumor grade, increased mitotic count, and a history of recurrent tumor; however, metastasis has been seen with all tumor grades.3,30,32 Unfortunately, once patients present with metastatic extracranial disease, their prognosis is poor.22,33 Chemotherapy agents have been explored for metastatic disease but their effectiveness is limited. Anti-angiogenic and targeted therapies have also been trialed, but more data are required to evaluate their efficacy.38 In our case, the patient’s initial tumor exhibited 10 mitoses/10 HPF but no necrosis, indicating at least a WHO grade 2 neoplasm. This could have contributed to the risk of metastasis. Despite our patient not having local recurrence following resection and focal radiation therapy, metastasis still occurred 22 years later. Although SFTs are known to have late recurrence and metastasis, the significant length of time from the initial diagnosis and absence of spinal imaging after 2016 could introduce the possibility of the fourth ventricular tumor being a metachronous primary lesion. The histopathological similarities between the tumors and the known natural history of SFTs would make this scenario less plausible. DNA methylation and NAB2-STAT6 fusion testing of the spinal tumor could have provided further insight, but sample limitations prevented us from pursuing this.

There are currently no established recommendations for postoperative follow-up of CNS SFTs. Given the risk of recurrence and delayed metastasis, some have recommended long-term or even indefinite follow-up of patients with CNS SFTs with interval craniospinal and systemic (CT body or positron emission tomography) imaging.24,39 Swaminathan et al. suggested craniospinal MRI every 3 months postoperatively and gradually extending to annual scans for an indefinite period.39 They also suggested annual CT body imaging; however, a “symptom-driven” approach could also be considered to reduce cumulative radiation exposure.39 Thus, a reasonable strategy would be to obtain craniospinal MRI every 3 months and gradually extend to annual scans, if stable. This can be supplemented with systemic imaging if concerning symptoms arise. Given the risk of future recurrence and metastasis, long-term or indefinite surveillance imaging is warranted.

Lessons

This report describes the first known case of metastasis of a spinal SFT to the fourth ventricle, 22 years after initial treatment. Additionally, this is the first reported case of an epithelioid SFT in the fourth ventricle. The management of SFT includes resection and consideration of adjuvant radiation therapy in higher-risk patients. Given the risk of local recurrence and delayed metastasis, it is recommended to consider long-term or even indefinite follow-up of patients with CNS SFTs with interval surveillance imaging of the craniospinal axis. Further studies are needed to better characterize the clinical behavior and long-term outcomes of this rare tumor.
