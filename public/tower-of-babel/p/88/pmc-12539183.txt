Revitalizing equine metabolism: how SHBG improves mitochondrial function and reduces inflammation
Authors: Nabila Bourebaba, Justyna Domagała, Lynda Bourebaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEquine metabolic syndrome (EMS) is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, disruptions in mitochondrial dynamics and function, and an increased risk of developing laminitis. Recent research has highlighted that reduced levels of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) in the bloodstream are linked to higher susceptibility to obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes, potentially contributing to broader metabolic imbalances. This study aimed to evaluate whether exogenously administered SHBG could protect adipose-derived stem cells from horses affected by EMS (EqASCEMS) against mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory activation.ResultsFollowing treatment with 50 nM of SHBG, analyses were conducted to assess cell viability, mitochondrial metabolism and dynamics, as well as the expression of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers. Results showed that SHBG significantly enhanced mitochondrial flexibility, as indicated by the upregulation of MFN-1, PARKIN, and PINK genes. SHBG also positively influenced the expression of genes related to mitoribosomes and the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system. In addition, SHBG treatment markedly reduced EMS-induced inflammation by decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β as well as IL-6 and boosting anti-inflammatory cytokines gene expression (IL-10 and IL-13).ConclusionOur findings suggest that SHBG exerts beneficial effects on the metabolic function of stem cells affected by EMS and holds promise as a potential therapeutic agent in the development of effective treatment strategies for this condition.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05033-y.

Background

Equine metabolic syndrome (EMS) is an endocrine disorder observed in obese horses, primarily characterized by insulin resistance (IR), laminitis, and abnormal fat distribution. While the key clinical features of EMS include increased body fat, IR, and laminitis, the condition likely encompasses a wider spectrum of physiological disturbances. These may involve altered energy metabolism, impaired adipocyte function, increased risk of thrombosis, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells [1, 2]. The central feature of EMS is insulin dysregulation, leading to disrupting the balance of plasma insulin, glucose, and lipids. IR arises from deficient insulin action in target tissues, leading to hyperinsulinemia and metabolic disorders such as hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, and endothelial dysfunction [3, 4]. Factors like aging, obesity, inactivity, and stress contribute to IR. In type 2 diabetes mellitus, fasting hyperglycaemia results from increased hepatic gluconeogenesis and impaired lipolysis, linked to the dysregulation of transcription factors like FKHR-Forkhead in rhabdomyosarcoma (FOXO1) in the liver [5].

On the other hand, mitochondrial dysfunction is closely linked to IR, as impaired mitochondrial activity disrupts energy metabolism and increases oxidative stress [6, 7]. In fact, at the cellular level; the activity, function, and oxidative stress of mitochondria directly correlate with hyperglycaemia. The mitochondria is the main unit of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [8]; hyperglycaemia increases ROS, disrupting cellular homeostasis and causing damage [9, 10]. Excessive O2• − production impairs glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) translocation to the plasma membrane, reducing insulin activity and exacerbating hyperglycaemia [11–13]. This cycle of ROS overproduction leads to mitochondrial changes, activation of the polyol pathway, glucose autoxidation, and accumulation of advanced glycation end products in diabetic patients. Furthermore, changes in the oxidative phosphorylation process (OXPHOS), decreased activity of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) + hydrogen (H) (NADH), citrate synthase, and oxidoreductase lead to IR [14]. Also, fatty acid catabolism (beta-oxidation) occurs in the mitochondria, but when it is reduced, it causes an accumulation of diacylglycerol and lipids, leading to an overproduction of ROS and the activation of kinase C; this subsequently leads to deteriorations in the mitochondrial functioning mechanism affecting ATP synthesis, subsequently causing excess lipids and the development of IR. Thus, oxidative stress exacerbates and perpetuates inflammation, stimulates lipid peroxidation, and triggers alterations in the insulin signalling system. IR induced by hyperlipidaemia significantly disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, leading to alterations in both mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and key regulatory proteins. Specifically, IR affects the expression and integrity of mtDNA-encoded genes such as 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA), ATP synthase F0 subunit 6 (ATP6), and cytochrome c oxidase subunits 1, 2, and 3 (COX1, COX2, and COX3), which are essential for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and energy production. Moreover, it impairs the expression of proteins critical for mitochondrial dynamics and quality control, including Mitofusin 1 (MFN-1), which regulates mitochondrial fusion; Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (PARKIN), a key player in mitophagy; and PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), which works in concert with PARKIN to identify and eliminate damaged mitochondria. These disruptions contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and impaired cellular metabolism commonly observed in metabolic disorders [5, 11, 15].

On the other hand, adipocytes subjected to oxidative stress express distress factors which are subsequently identified by the cluster of differentiation 8 (CD8), lymphocytes as well as natural killer cells (NK) which will synthesize interferon-γ (IFN-γ) inducing the transition of adipose tissue macrophages from the anti-inflammatory (M2) state to the pro-inflammatory (M1) state triggering inflammation [16]. In fact, chronic low-grade inflammation is strongly associated with laminitis and EMS [17]; in fact, it plays a critical role in both systemic and peripheral tissue inflammation and triggers high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) or interleukin-1/6 (Il-1/6) that have been indicted as the main cytokines secreted by macrophages and adipose tissue [18], creating a not-favorable microenvironment that impairs adipose tissue stem cells’ (ASC) multilineage differentiation potential [19], viability, and apoptosis, as well as autophagy and mitophagy [20] as well as oxidative stress that impairs ASC immunomodulatory potential [21]. Adipose tissue functions as an endocrine organ, regulating energy balance and influencing metabolic and cardiovascular complications through adipokine secretion. In obesity, hypertrophied adipocytes disrupt this process, increasing pro-inflammatory adipokines like leptin and resistin while reducing anti-inflammatory adiponectin, which enhances insulin sensitivity. This imbalance exacerbates local and systemic inflammation [16, 22]. Furthermore, gradual storage of lipid levels in adipocytes induces hypertrophy and hypertrophy of adipose tissue, resulting in adipocyte stress and local tissue hypoxia, leading to a tendency to adipocyte necrosis and macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue [16]. Our recent study demonstrates the crosstalk between impaired mitochondrial metabolism and dynamics, oxidative stress, inflammation, and ASC malfunction, which suggests that pharmacological modulation of mitochondrial activity might benefit ASC immunomodulatory potential [23].

Sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) is a 90–95 kDa protein that is mainly synthesized in the liver and secreted into the bloodstream. The main role of SHBG includes transporting sex hormones and regulating hormone dynamics [24]. Beyond its classical role as a hormone transport protein, SHBG has been increasingly recognized as an active regulator of metabolic homeostasis [25]. SHBG levels show a strong inverse association with IR, obesity, and related metabolic disorders across both genders; indeed, Low SHBG is linked to impaired glucose homeostasis, altered lipid profiles, reduced testosterone levels, and increased risk of metabolic syndrome, T2DM, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and cardiometabolic complications often independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. In fact, SHBG influences insulin signaling, glucose transport, and lipid metabolism, making it a potential biomarker and mechanistic contributor to metabolic dysfunction [26–28]. Decreased circulating SHBG levels have been consistently reported in individuals with metabolic and inflammatory disorders [24]. Multiple studies have demonstrated an inverse correlation between SHBG and inflammatory markers, including the C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and TNF-α, suggesting that chronic low-grade inflammation may suppress hepatic SHBG synthesis. This effect is thought to occur through cytokine-mediated downregulation of SHBG gene transcription in hepatocytes, thereby contributing to a sustained pro-inflammatory state. Moreover, low SHBG levels are closely linked to insulin resistance, which further exacerbates inflammation, creating a vicious cycle that promotes the progression of metabolic dysfunction [29–31]. The role of SHBG in the modulation of inflammatory reaction and insulin sensitivity has been finally demonstrated in db/db rodents with overexpressed human SHBG that showed resistance to the development of obesity and hepatosteatosis [32]. Moreover, a single nucleotide polymorphism related to an elevated plasma SHBG concentration has been found to correlate with a reduced risk of diabetes [33]. As a matter of fact, our previous study on the effect of SHBG on EMS-affected ACSs has confirmed that SHBG enhances stem cells' viability and metabolic activity and protects against apoptosis and oxidative stress; it also mitigates ER stress and modulates the insulin signaling cascades [34]. Above mentioned studies led us to speculate that SHBG might modulate ASCs immunomodulatory activity by modulating mitochondrial dynamics. In this study, using insulin-resistant ASC, we investigated in vitro effect of SHBG on inflammation as well as mitochondrial dynamics, since mitochondrial deterioration in IR ASC has been proposed to be a critical player in adipose tissue IR development. Herein, we present evidence of the favorable of SHBG ASC.

Materials and methods

EqASCs cell culture and propagation

Equine adipose-derived stem cells (EqASCs) used in this study were obtained from the established cell bank of the Department of Experimental Biology, University of Environmental and Life Sciences, Wrocław, Poland. The original collection of adipose tissue samples and establishment of the cell bank was approved by the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments in Wrocław, PAN Ludwik Hirszfeld Institute of Immunology and Experimental Therapy in Wrocław (Instytut Immunologii i Terapii Doświadczalnej im. Ludwika Hirszfelda PAN we Wrocławiu), approval no. [058/2021/P1, on 23 September 2021], with informed consent obtained from the horse owners.

Adipose tissue samples were obtained from the tail base region of adult healthy and EMS-affected horses under local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine (Polfa S.A., Warsaw, Poland). The horses enrolled in the study were between 10 and 14 years of age and were clinically evaluated by experienced veterinarians based on their medical history and clinical profiles. The inclusion criteria, established according to previously described procedures, encompassed body weight (BW), body condition score (BCS), cresty neck score (CNS), OGTT, Leptin, Adiponectin, the history of Laminitis and fasting insulin concentrations [20, 35]. Clinical assessment data of the qualified healthy horses (n = 6) and EMS horses (n = 6) are provided in Table 1. Following collection, adipose tissue explants were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (P4474, Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (P4333, Sigma- Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) to prevent microbial contamination and then transferred into transport medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium–low glucose (DMEM-LG containing 1000 mg/L of glucose) (D9443, Sigma- Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) supplemented with 1% P/S. The tissue was mechanically minced with sterile scissors and enzymatically digested in collagenase type I solution (0.1 mg/mL) (SCR103, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) for 40 min in a CO₂ incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2 and 95% humidity) (BINDER GmbH, CBF170, Tuttlingen, Germany). After centrifugation (1200 × g, 10 min), the supernatant was discarded, and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in culture medium supplemented with 5% of (FBS) (F4135, Sigma- Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) and seeded into cell culture flasks. The mesenchymal phenotype of equine ASCs was confirmed by flow cytometry (FACS) using fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies specific for CD44, CD45, CD90, and CD105 (Table 2) [36]. EqASCs SHBG treatment

SHBG protein (sex hormone binding globulin native protein, Fitzgerald, 30R-AS012) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. It was reconstituted in sterile PBS to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. In addition, a 4% sterile solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A9418, Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland) was prepared in distilled water. Once the cells reached approximately 80% confluency, they were rinsed with PBS, and fresh DMEM-LG medium supplemented with 5% of the 4% BSA solution was added to the cultures. This medium did not contain fetal bovine serum (FBS) or penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Subsequently, 50 nM of SHBG was added to the experimental group, and the cells were incubated for 24 h.

Cytotoxicity assessment

The biocompatibility of the SHBG treatment was assessed with a resazurin-based test alamarBlue™ Cell Viability Reagent (DAL1100, Invitrogen™, Massachusetts, USA). The experimental cell groups (i.e. EqASCHE, EqASCEMS, EqASCEMS + 50 nM SHBG) were seeded onto a 96-well plate at the density of 10 × 103 cells/per well and then treated as described above. After treatment, the post-cultured medium was replaced with a 10% resazurin solution prepared in a DMEM-LG culture medium, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h [37, 38]. Then the spectrophotometric measurement was performed at 570 nm and 600 nm as the reference wavelength (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The results were analyzed with GraphPad Prism9 Software.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity assessment

In order to determine the cell damage and death, the loss of intracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and its release into the culture medium was investigated using the Pierce™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (88953, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Accordingly to manufacture’s instructions, the assay was performed as following: after incubating the ASCs with the SHBG, culture media was collected and centrifuged in order to remove remaining debris; then, 50 µL of the latter from each experimental group was transferred to a new 96-well plate. Additional wells were used for: LDH background (serum-free medium), maximum LDH activity control (10 × lysis buffer) and spontaneous LDH activity control (water). The plate were then incubated at 37 °C, with 5% of CO2 and 95% of humidity during 45 min, after which 50 µL of reaction mixture was added to each well and incubated for additional 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 50 µL of Stop Solution was added to each well and gently mixed. The plate was measured spectrophotometrically at 490 nm and 680 nm wavelength (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The absorbance was measured using the formula A (480–680) = LDH 480 nm-LDH 680 nm. Click-iT™ plus TUNEL assay

To perform TUNEL Alexa Fluor Staining, the commercial Click-iT™ TUNEL Alexa Fluor™ Imaging Assay (C10247, Invitrogen ™, Warsaw, Poland) was used in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer. First, cells were seeded onto coverslips at a density of 20 × 104 cells. 24 h later, once the cells had attached, SHBG was applied at a concentration of 50 nM. The post-cultured medium was replaced with PBS to wash the cells once; the cells were then fixed in a 4% PFA solution for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized right after in 0.25% Triton™ X-100 in PBS during 20 min at room temperature. After permeabilization, the cells had been washed twice with PBS. Then, the samples were incubated with 100 μL of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) reaction buffer for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction buffer has been replaced with the TdT reaction cocktail, and coverslips were incubated for 60 min at 37 °C; after what, samples were washed twice with 3% BSA in PBS for 2 min each. Meanwhile, the Click-iT™ reaction cocktail was prepared, and 100 μL of it was added on coverslips. After 30 min of incubation at room temperature in the dark, the solution was removed, and cells were washed with 3% BSA in PBS for 5 min and the nuclei were stained with Hoechst3332. Cells were visualized using the confocal microscopy (Observer Z1 Confocal Spinning Disc V.2 Zeiss) and the obtained data was analyzed using Image J Software.

Mitochondrial Transmembrane Potential (MMP) determination

Mitochondrial Transmembrane Potential (MMP) was evaluated with the Muse® MitoPotential Kit (MCH100110, Luminex Corporation, Poznań, Poland), in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Briefly, treated and untreated cells (i.e. EqASCHE, EqASCEMS, EqASCEMS + 50 nM SHBG) were incubated with the Muse® MitoPotential working solution (1:1000 in 1 × Assay Buffer) for 20 min at 37℃. Then, Muse® 7-AAD reagent was added. After 5 min of incubation in room temperature, mitochondrial potential was investigated with Muse ™ Cell Analyzer (0500–3115, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany).

Glucose consumption measurement

Cellular glucose consumption was assessed in the cell culture media through an enzymatic method which employs the Glucose Colorimetric Assay Kit (10,009,582, Cayman Chemicals, Tallin, Estonia). After, treating the cells with the 50 nM SHBG and conditioned at 37 °C for 24 h; the culture medium was subsequently replaced with DMEM-LG supplemented with additional 25 mM glucose (1.08337.1000, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated for 24 h. To measure glucose consumption kinetics, 100 µl aliquots were collected at 0, 30, 60, 120 min, and 24 h. The glucose concentrations in the collected media were determined following the manufacturer's instructions for the glucose assay kit. Absorbance readings were then taken at 520 nm using a spectrometer (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The amount of glucose utilized by the cells was calculated by subtracting the readings from the wells containing cells from those without cells.

Oxygen consumption/glycolysis measurement

The Oxygen consumption rate and glycolysis of ASC cells has been measured using the Oxygen Consumption/Glycolysis Dual Assay Kit (601,060, Cayman Chemical, Tallin, Estonia) following manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured with a 96-well microplate reader (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany).

Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) quantification assay

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using the ATP Assay Kit (Colorimetric/Fluorometric, ab 83,355, Abcam, Gdańsk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Untreated control and SHBG-treated cells were first rinsed two times with PBS and lysed using the kit-provided lysis buffer under cold conditions to preserve nucleotide integrity. Following cell lysis, samples were deproteinized using the perchloric acid and ice-cold potassium hydroxide to remove enzymatic contaminants. The clarified lysates were then incubated with the ATP reaction mixture, which included ATP converter, developer, and detection probe. Reactions were carried out at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. ATP levels were determined by measuring fluorescence at Ex/Em = 535/587 nm, using a multi-well plate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek® Instruments, Inc., Warsaw, Poland). A standard curve prepared with the supplied ATP standard was used for quantification and ATP concentrations were normalized to total cellular protein content (µmol ATP/mg protein).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA from cells was isolated with the EXTRAzol reagent (EM30, Blirt, Gdańsk, Poland) and subjected to Real-Time PCR following manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA quality, purity and concentrations were established using a nano spectrophotometer (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was evaluated using the commercially available Takara PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit with genomic DNA (gDNA) Eraser (RR047A, pTAKARA Bio, Gdańsk, Poland). The expression level of genes (Table 2) was analyzed by Real-Time PCR using the SensiFast SYBR & Fluorescein Kit (BIO-96020, MERIDIANE BIOSCIENCE, Ohio, USA) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The forward and reverse primers of specific genes are listed in Table 3. The Real-Time PCR was performed with a CFX ConnectTM Real-Time PCR Detection System (BIO-RAD, Warsaw, Poland) and following cycling conditions: 95° C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95° C for 15 s, annealing for 15 s, and elongation at 72° C for 15 s. Discussion

Obesity related to equine metabolic syndrome is characterized by excessive accumulation of fatty tissue causing damage to the metabolic function of other tissues and organs. However, during abnormal calories intake, the adipose tissue expands, either by increasing the number of adipocytes (hyperplasia), or by increasing the size of the adipocytes (hypertrophy). Thus, during obesity, adipose tissue is distinguished by increased fibrosis, macrophage infiltration, a pro-inflammatory phenotype, leading to the appearance of insulin resistance through the release of free fatty acids, pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines. In addition, adipose tissue may express molecular dysfunctions characteristic of increased ROS that induces endoplasmic reticulum stress, deterioration of the mitochondrial function, as well as the onset of senescence and an apoptotic profile [47, 48]. Moreover, due to its endocrine activity, adipose tissue has been the most studied organ in recent decades; indeed, the latter releases adipocytokines, cytokines, transcriptional and growth factors, which all together form a secretum. From now on, adipose tissue is no longer viewed solely as a storage site for energy, a thermal insulator, or a cushioning agent; in fact, recent research has revealed that it is intricately involved in a complex interplay with the endocrine, nervous, and cardiovascular systems [49]. This tissue arises from the mesoderm and is composed of adipocytes, as well as stromal cells including vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, monocytes, macrophages, pre-adipocyte, lymphocytes, and adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs). ASCs have the potential to differentiate into mesodermal or trans-mesodermal lineages, and can give rise to cells originating from the ectoderm [50]. Hence, the search for novel therapeutic strategies to address the dysfunction of ASCs is gaining increasing attention, with a focus on identifying ways to restore their normal functions.

This study characterized the mitochondrial dynamics, mitochondrion, and inflammation in EqASCEMS treated with the exogenous SHBG glycoprotein. We have uncovered several key insights that strongly suggest SHBG plays a crucial regulatory role not only in mitochondrial dynamics and metabolism, but also in the modulation of inflammatory processes. These findings highlight the multifaceted influence of SHBG in cellular function, particularly in maintaining mitochondrial health and controlling inflammation at a systemic level. Hence, we firstly established the equine metabolic syndrome diagnosis in the EqASCEMS by measuring the glucose consumption and the protein expression of the GLUT-4 to found out that they were both downregulated in EMS cell group when compared to the healthy control group; being both two main indicators of insulin resistance in cells; it allowed us to confirm that the EMS cells used during this investigation are subjected to the metabolic syndrome.

The proliferation of progenitor cells including ASCs, stability of DNA, and morphological integrity are fundamental factors that impact ASC’s stemness, pro-regenerative ability, and finally clinical usefulness [51–53]. That is why it is important to demonstrate that the SHBG is not cytotoxic; in this regard, we analysed the proliferative and viability rate via the Alamar Blue test, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity as well as DNA fragmentation in apoptotic cells, which showed us that SHBG increased the proliferative potential of EqASCEMS, and protect DNA against its fragmentation. In fact, The elevated LDH activity observed in EqASCEMS confirms the presence of cellular damage and compromised membrane integrity, which are characteristic features of chronic metabolic stress in EMS. This finding is consistent with previous reports linking metabolic dysfunction to increased cytotoxicity and reduced cell viability. Importantly, the significant reduction in LDH activity following SHBG treatment suggests that SHBG exerts a protective effect by stabilizing the plasma membrane and limiting cell damage. These results support the cytocompatibility of SHBG and highlight its potential role in preserving the functional integrity of metabolically impaired ASCs [54, 55]. At the same time, we noticed, that SHBG does not affect the morphological architecture of EqASCEMS growth pattern which correlates with our previous findings in which we demonstrated that the exogenous SHBG treatment enhanced the proliferation, cell cycle progression, and survival of ASCs, while concurrently decreasing the expression of p53 and p21, which are known as pro-apoptotic mediators [34]. On the other hand, we have found that EqASCEMS treated with SHBG are characterized by lower polarization of mitochondrial membrane which justified the enhanced proliferative ability of EqASCEMS when treated with SHBG. The observed molecular phenomenon stands in good agreement with our and other researchers previous findings indicating the pro-survival role of SHBG on the ASC population isolated from EMS-affected patients [23].

Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of many disorders including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, obesity, and insulin resistance [56]; defective mitochondria are characterized by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and secretion which has been proposed as an EMS-initiating factor [57]. Enhanced ROS production in turn promotes protein carbonylation, impairs electron transport chain proteins, and substantially reduces mitochondrial respiration in insulin resistant and/or obese patients [58]. Although obesity is not always an accompanied factor for EMS, adipose tissue dysfunction, defective lipid storage, impaired biochemical profile, and fatty acid oxidation are all critical components that affects those residing in adipose tissue ASC. Insulin resistance or dysregulation in individuals with EMS carries profound molecular implications, particularly for mitochondrial dysfunction in adipose-derived stromal/stem cells (ASC). Our and other earlier extensive studies showed, that EMS-derived ASC are characterized by defective mitochondrial metabolism and dynamics which promotes insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress [23]. Mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, and mitochondrial morphological patterns significantly modulate ASC’ fate and their stemness in the course of insulin resistance/dysregulation in EMS-affected patients. We have found that SHBG promotes the expression and activity of MFN-1, PINK on both gene and protein expression levels while it is decreasing the genre expression of PARKIN, MIEF and Wnt-3 related mitophagy and modulators of the mitochondrial fission and fusion. Moreover, we have found that EqASCEMS treated with the 50 nM exogenous SHBG have presented dense, small globules and twisting-like mitochondrial morphotypes that additionally confirm enhanced mitophagy and improved bioenergetic status. It was earlier shown, that PINK, PRKIN, MFN-1, MIEF and UCP-1 mediates mitophagy maintaining intracellular homeostasis and becoming a key point in mitochondrial quality control which was found to correlate with insulin resistance [59]. Moreover, previous studies have shown, that defective PINK expression and impaired mitochondrial dynamics favor reduced sensitivity for insulin and result in defective adipogenic differentiation potential of ASC. Additionally, it was demonstrated, that failure PINK/PARKIN axis expression favors the accumulation of defective mitochondria that are not removed from the cells due to the impaired lysosomal system which in consequence promotes cellular damage, generates oxidative stress, and impairs sensitivity for insulin [60]. Moreover, we found that SHBG promotes the activity of mitochondrial complex II, which has been shown to be a novel mediator of insulin sensitivity associated with visceral adiposity [61]. The enhanced activity of complex mitochondrial complex II together with the regulation of the expression of MTEFF-4, OX1AL, COX4L1 and COX4L2 at the gene level axis followed by application of SHBG in EqASCEMS. Moreover, increased defective mitochondrial clearance promoted by SHBG strongly correlates with our findings in this study; indeed, The marked reduction in ATP generation observed in EqASCEMS reflects mitochondrial dysfunction, a well-recognized hallmark of equine metabolic syndrome and a key contributor to cellular energy imbalance [62]. This decline in mitochondrial activity is consistent with the metabolic inflexibility and oxidative stress commonly reported in EMS conditions. Notably, the significant increase in ATP production following SHBG treatment suggests that SHBG supports mitochondrial recovery, at least partially restoring bioenergetic capacity in compromised cells. These findings further emphasize the beneficial role of SHBG in improving mitochondrial performance and overall cellular metabolism in EMS-affected ASCs [63]. On the other hand, the glycoprotein treatment has enhanced EqASCEMS bioenergetics and increased their oxygen consumption as well as their glycolysis rate -all fundamental the EqASC progenitor cells stemness and metabolism. Obtained results shed promising light on SHBG as a mito-regulatory protein that might navigate mitochondrial dynamics and bioenergetics and anti-oxidative defense.

Previous results might explain what the anti-inflammatory activity of the SHBG glycoprotein; we have found that SHBG reduces the expression of master pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β on both gene and protein level as well as IL-6 gene expression, which have been shown to play a critically important role in initiating “cytokine storm” in adipose tissue of EMS-affected horses [64]. These findings clearly showed that ASC are losing their anti-inflammatory activity under EMS conditions [65–67]. Obtained data showed, that application of SHBG not only reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines but also promotes the activity of anti-inflammatory ones including IL-4, IL-10 and IL-13. Previous reports have demonstrated that pro-inflammatory cytokines are the critical inducers of insulin resistance leading to T2D or syndrome X development [68–70]. However, as described in the results section, the expression of TNF-α in the EqASCEMS group is highly downregulated when compared to the EqASCHE group; and after applying the SHBG we can spot the elevated levels of TNF-α gene; this phenomenon can be explained by the fact that during chronic low grade inflammation; the EMS ASCs are facing the feedback inhibition mechanisms during which, when the organism is facing chronic inflammation, negative feedback loops are activated to prevent excessive tissue damage caused by prolonged TNF-α production; because, it is leading to cellular desensitization, where receptors like TNF becomes less responsive, reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α. on the other hand, anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 can suppress TNF-α transcription or translation; whichever, correlates with our finding; in fact, the levels of IL-10 gens were restored in EqASCEMS + 50 nM SHBG. Thus, the anti-inflammatory activity of SHBG might be critically important for the modulation of inflammation in EMS-affected horses [71–74].

Conclusion

The present study characterized the impact of mitochondrial metabolism, dynamics, bioenergetics, and inflammation in EqASCEMS. Our results indicate that SHBG may promote mitophagy, OXPHOS, mitochondrial dynamics, and metabolism, while also reducing the expression of selected pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhancing the expression of certain anti-inflammatory markers. Additionally, EqASCEMS treated with SHBG exhibited improved anti-oxidative defense, which could be relevant for the modulation of insulin sensitivity in ASCs. These findings suggest that SHBG warrants further investigation to better understand its potential role as a candidate for the treatment of EMS.
