Viral destruction of the olfactory mucosa elicits immune residence, bone metaplasia, and long-term smell loss
Authors: Skye Y. Tracey, Alexa C. Blanchard, Sebastian A. Wellford, William M. Harbour, Alan R. Tang, Dorian B. McGavern, E. Ashley Moseman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The upper airway is comprised of two distinct mucosal surface tissues, the respiratory mucosa (RM) and the olfactory mucosa (OM), both of which are constantly being exposed to airborne pathogens. The olfactory mucosa is responsible for detecting and conveying odorant information into the central nervous system, and infection poses serious neuroinvasion risk. Using vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) as a model of olfactotropic viral infection,1 we identify long-term cellular and functional changes within the olfactory mucosa following infection. VSV infection rapidly destroys the neuroepithelium causing extensive tissue damage to the olfactory mucosa. Infection drives recruitment of numerous immune cell types to the olfactory mucosa where many continue to reside after viral resolution. Specifically, antigen-specific resident T cells are recruited and remain in the olfactory mucosa where they can produce inflammatory cytokines upon rechallenge. We also find following viral clearance, viral mediated damage results in ectopic bone growth within the nasal airway, significant olfactory tissue loss, and neurogenic failure leading to metaplastic conversion of olfactory mucosa into respiratory mucosa. This mucosal damage in previously infected animals results in functional olfaction deficits. Despite damage to the tissue and loss of olfactory surface area, an infection-based fate mapping approach demonstrated that previously infected precursor cells can survive infection, retain neuro-regenerative potential, and generate mature olfactory sensory neurons that synapse onto the olfactory bulb. Recent studies from SARS-CoV2 infected individuals indicate that long-term immune activity within the olfactory tissues can drive functional changes that impair olfaction. Our data provide an animal model for viral infection of the olfactory mucosa, one that leads to long-term immune residence and tissue architectural changes that suggest underlying commonalities with human studies linking immune activity following infection to permanent smell loss.

Introduction

The upper airway is comprised of two distinct tissues, the respiratory mucosa (RM) that is primarily responsible for warming and conducting air to the lung, and the olfactory mucosa (OM) that mediates the sense of smell.1 Normal respiration draws environmental air across both RM and OM bringing them both into contact with inhaled airborne pathogens. To mediate scent detection, the OM contains bipolar olfactory sensory neurons (OSN) with dendrites that reach into the airway to detect airborne compounds (odorants) and axons that project through the cribriform plate to synapse directly onto the olfactory bulb in the brain.2 This anatomical arrangement creates a tissue specific vulnerability that neurotropic pathogens can exploit. By infecting OSNs, pathogens can move intracellularly directly into the brain. This allows pathogens to bypass physical barriers like the skull, blood brain barrier (BBB), and blood cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB). Thus, the immune defenses within this barrier are essential to prevent neural invasion.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of defining host immune responses to upper respiratory infections. SARS-CoV-2 infection can infect sustentacular cells of the OM,3–5 which can in turn can lead to acute smell loss, potential systemic viral infection, and continued pathogen transmission.3,6–7 Several other viruses,3 including other coronaviruses,3,8–10 influenza,11–12 parainfluenza,13 Japanese encephalitis virus,14 and herpes simplex virus15 can also infect the OM. Compared with seasonal influenza strains, highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 has been shown to robustly infect OSNs, replicate within the olfactory tissue,16–18 and is strongly neuroinvasive in animal models, using the olfactory route to access the CNS.17,19 Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) infection is a robust model for intranasal olfactory infection in which viral replication is almost exclusively in the OM and not the RM, and rapidly enters the CNS.20–22 Virus is instilled into the nasal airway, where it then infects epithelial cells including OSNs and spreads across the cribriform plate of the skull and into the olfactory bulb of the CNS. In immunocompetent animals, viral replication is typically halted within the olfactory bulb, and our previous work has demonstrated key roles for T cells and microglia in coordinating this defense.20 In this current study, we sought to identify alterations in the long-term local immune response and tissue repair within the olfactory mucosa following cytopathic olfactory infection.

Unlike central nervous tissues, olfactory neurons have a life-long capacity for regeneration from local neural stem cells. Adult neurogenesis is likely needed at this anatomic location because the continuous contact with environmental air leaves the olfactory neuroepithelium highly susceptible to damage by infection or airborne toxicants.23–27 At steady state, OSNs are thought to turnover every 30–60 days.26 Following a severe injury or inflammatory insult, dormant basal stem cells become active and proliferate25,28–29 and can repopulate OSNs. Despite this regenerative potential, one of the most common causes of smell loss, or anosmia, is post-viral olfactory dysfunction.30–31 Indeed, one of the most common initial symptoms during the COVID-19 pandemic was anosmia, and for a subset of individuals, smell loss lasts long after recovery from the acute infection.6,32–35 Several respiratory viruses are linked to changes in olfaction that may last following resolution of infectious virus.30 Persistent inflammation and OSN loss have been implicated in human patients with long-term smell loss post-COVID,6 and the mechanisms that underly different smell loss outcomes following olfactory infection remain unclear. In fact, as humans age, sensory area is lost, likely from a lifetime of infections and aberrant regeneration. The result is presbyosmia, or aging-related olfactory loss, which is also associated with immune signaling.36 However, unlike many tissues, emerging literature suggests that balancing immune defense and neuroepithelial repair following infection are key to preserving normal chemosensory function in the upper airway.

We undertook this study to understand how olfactory tissue is changed by infection with olfactotropic and cytopathic viruses like VSV, which infects neurons. We have found long-term changes within the local immune compartment as well as impacts on the ability of animals to smell following infection. We also describe significant olfactory tissue remodeling following infection that leads to loss of sensory ability. Nevertheless, some infected cells are able to survive and contribute to olfactory regeneration.

Results

Vesicular stomatitis virus infects olfactory sensory neurons and drives long-term immunological changes in the olfactory mucosa

To investigate effects of VSV on the olfactory turbinates, we infected mice intranasally with VSV expressing DsRed (VSV-DsRed). Confocal imaging of coronal sections of olfactory turbinates identifies a large population of infected olfactory sensory neurons 48 h after infection (Fig. 1 A–E). The normal neuroepithelial architecture is disrupted by viral induced cell death, leading to “ruffling” of the neuroepithelial surface, as shown by the scalloped apical epithelium facing the airway (Fig. 1 B) and neuronal sloughing where the neuroepithelium is lost and the lamina propria is bare (arrowheads, Fig. 1 C). ICAM1 is highly expressed by horizontal basal cells (HBCs) that separate the neuroepithelium and lamina propria, however during acute inflammation ICAM1 is expressed more broadly. Using flow cytometry from the nasal turbinates of infected OMP-GFP mice in which mature OSNs express GFP, we confirmed that at least 80% of virally infected cells were mature OSNs (Fig. 1D, E). This viral replication is not restricted to neuronal cell types, but does predominate in OSNs. Olfactory viral titers spike rapidly following infection but are contained within 8 days (Fig. 1F). We have previously shown infiltration and tissue damage to the airways by day 6 after intranasal infection.20 This widespread VSV-induced tissue damage coincides with infiltration of innate hematopoietic cells as early as day 2 and increases over time until virus is contained (Fig. 1F, G). The early response to olfactory viral infection was dominated by neutrophils during the first 4 days, was followed by Ly6Chi monocyte infiltration. By day 8, no viral titer was detected, yet immune infiltration remained high (Fig. 1 F, G). Short-lived inflammatory cells dominate the response against VSV infection (Fig. 1 F, G), yet VSV also drives an adaptive immune response20 and we speculated that these cells may remain in residence upon resolution. Indeed, the number of CD45 + cells was significantly increased within olfactory tissue 40 days post-VSV infection (Fig. 1 H–J).

These data are consistent with our previously described single cell RNA sequencing data (Fig. 1K) comparing uninfected and day 40 VSV infected olfactory tissue.22 Large scale changes in immune cell composition emerge after VSV is cleared. More specifically, we observed increases in T cells and plasma cells21–22 and relative decreases in steady state macrophage populations. However, the olfactory mucosa is stratified into at least two distinct tissue layers. An avascular neuroepithelium is filled with OSN cell bodies that detect odorants. Beneath a basal lamina lies a lamina propria containing OSN axon tracts and the blood vasculature. The naïve neuroepithelium generally contains very few lymphocytes (Fig. 1K, H) and we wondered if this is because in homeostasis lymphocytes are actively excluded, or simply have yet to be recruited. We sought to characterize how immune cell populations changed across the different olfactory mucosal compartments following infection. Because flow cytometric analyses are devoid of this sub-anatomical information, we utilized immunohistochemistry to evaluate immune cell changes within three functional zones of the olfactory mucosa: the neuroepithelium (NE), the lamina propria (LP), and the basal lamina (BL), with the latter denoting cells that were in contact with either the apical or basal side of ICAM1hi horizontal basal cells (HBCs) separating lamina propria and neuroepithelium (Fig. 1L). Corroborating the single cell data (Fig. 1K), we found that all three zones had marked increases in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells at day 40 post-infection (Fig. 1M, N).

While the overall prevalence of CD11b+ expressing cells was not changed, the frequency of MHC Class II expressing cells was increased, suggesting that resident macrophage populations have shifted toward antigen presenting functions (Fig. 1O, P), consistent with our prior work.22 The lamina propria always contained the most immune cells across all analyzed cell types, however infection did result in significant immune cell enrichment within the neuroepithelium, suggesting that lymphocytes are able to reside within this tissue (Fig. 1M–P, Supplementary Fig. 1). Indeed, the neuroepithelium saw the largest relative increase in resident immune cells between naïve and infected compartments, indicating that while lymphocytes are quite rare within the naïve neuroepithelium, the inflammatory cues associated with olfactory tissue regeneration creates niches that support a variety of adaptive immune cells.

Viral infection establishes resident memory T cell populations within the olfactory mucosa

Prior work has indicated that chronic inflammation and T cell functions can impair olfactory function,6,37–41 but resident T cell activation may also provide protection during reinfection. Our observed increase in immune cells, particularly T cell populations (Fig. 1K, M, N), within olfactory tissues long after viral resolution led us to specifically characterize the tissue residence of antigen specific memory T cells following infection. We had previously generated a recombinant VSV expressing Cre-recombinase and ovalbumin (VSV-OVA-Cre)20 that allowed for fate mapping infected areas of the olfactory mucosa while also expanding ovalbumin specific T cells. Prior to VSV-OVA-Cre infection of floxed Stop TdTomato reporter (Stopfl/fl TdTomato), animals received adoptively transferred T cells from TCR-transgenic mice with CD4+ cells specific for a VSV glycoprotein epitope presented on I-Ab (L7)42 and OT-I TCR transgenic mice with OVA specific CD8+ T cells (H2Kb restricted OVA257–264).43 This system allowed us to track the surviving progeny of infected olfactory cells (TdTomato+) and antigen-specific CD8+ (OT-I) and CD4+ (L7) cells. Both VSV-specific T cell populations were found in the olfactory mucosa adjacent to TdTomato + fate mapped cells at ~30 days post infection (Fig. 2A) and FACS analysis revealed that both antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells entered the tissues early after infection and expressed CD103+, indicating that many of these cells likely represented antigen-specific tissue resident T cells. (Fig. 2B).44,45 These resident T cells were able to produce cytokines in response to ex-vivo peptide re-stimulation, with the majority of CD8+ resident memory T cells (Trm) producing IFN-ɣ and some producing both IFN-ɣ and TNF-α (Fig. 2C–E). Neutralizing antibody prevents olfactory re-infection with the same serotype (VSV-Indiana, VSV-IND),20 however we were able to evaluate T cell functions after reinfection by challenging mice with the heterologous VSV-New Jersey (VSV-NJ) serotype that shares T cell epitopes, but not neutralizing antibody epitopes. Using a VSV-IND expressing DsRed, we rechallenged previously infected GREAT mice in which YFP reports IFN-ɣ production.46 After this rechallenge, we noted that resident immune cells rapidly upregulate IFN-ɣ (within 24hrs) in regions where heterologous VSV reinfection has occurred (Fig. 2F), while uninfected areas have undetectable IFN-ɣ reporter activity (Fig. 2G). OSNs are the primary target of VSV infection but these cells express nearly undetectable levels of MHC Class I (Fig. 2H–I), suggesting that direct engagement by CD8 T cells is unlikely. However, T cells may be activated through bystander MHC Class I antigen presentation as reported for microglia during VSV encephalitis,20 or by olfactory macrophages.22 Peripheral T cell depletion does not deplete TRM47 and when peripheral CD8+ and CD4+ T cells were depleted prior to rechallenge, there was no impact on viral control in the olfactory mucosa (Fig. 2J) or in the anatomically linked olfactory bulb in the brain (Fig. 2K). Interestingly, we also found while T cells could not significantly alter viral replication in the olfactory mucosa, the presence of resident T cells did prevent CNS infection (Fig. 2J and K). It is unclear whether this CNS protection is due to mucosal resident T cells that impede viral dissemination into the CNS or resident T cell activity within the CNS. Nevertheless, intranasal VSV infection leads to Trm recruitment to olfactory turbinates, and these cells are poised to respond following rechallenge.

Olfactory viral infection leads to structural changes within the nasal turbinates

The OM must contact environmental air to detect odorants and for this reason, it carries a substantial risk of barrier damage. This anatomically imposed risk likely underlies the remarkable lifelong ability to regenerate the neuronal epithelium.29,48–52 In the case of VSV infection, the virally mediated epithelial damage could be regenerated as is observed in chemical depletion models,50–51,53 but surprisingly, we observed widespread alterations in turbinate macrostructure, as well as aneuronal regions of formerly olfactory mucosa following regeneration.

Following chemical ablation, the olfactory surface is typically fully regenerated by 30 days post lesion.51 However, even more than 45 days after an intranasal VSV infection, we observed notable alterations in the typical structure of the olfactory turbinates. This was most evident in the superior ethmoid turbinates, where overgrowth of bone and extracellular matrix collagen altered the normal airway architecture (Fig. 3A–E, Suppl Movie1). These changes appeared permanent and remained months after initial infection. Ossification was also associated with focal OSN loss and deterioration of the chemosensory surface area. Even in regions that did not display overt airway ossification, olfactory sensory neuronal coverage of the epithelial surface was often lost. Infected turbinates displayed epithelial thinning and aneuronal regions suggestive of a metaplastic transformation in which olfactory surfaces become covered by respiratory epithelium (Fig. 3C).

By three weeks post intranasal VSV infection, variable amounts of ethmoid airway space was replaced by marrow filled bone, suggesting rapid generation and migration of osteogenic precursors in response to viral damage. These precursors form new bone that leads to permanent airway ossification. To interrogate the development of this new bone, we probed factors important for mesenchymal differentiation into osteogenic precursors at early time points after viral clearance. Histological staining for Osterix (OSX), one of the main transcription factors driving osteoblast differentiation, revealed increased numbers of OSX-expressing cells in the superior olfactory turbinates throughout the second week post infection (Fig. 3F, G). We also found significant upregulation of numerous transcripts associated with osteogenic processes, including Sp7, the gene name for OSX, as well several bone and extracellular matrix genes and growth factors (Bglap, Bmp5, Col1a1, Fn1, Tgfb3) (Fig. 3H). The mechanisms underlying bony overgrowth within the airspace remain unknown, but we postulate that widespread immune response to sever viral induced damage, contributes to long-term OSN surface area loss in infected animals.

Post-viral smell loss and peripheral olfactory surface loss

Because the central function of the olfactory mucosa is to detect and relay odor information from the outside environment to the brain, and given the increased ossification and respiratory conversion following infection, we sought to determine how VSV infection may impact olfaction using a buried food test (BFT)54 to quantify volatile odor detection (smelling) within previously infected animals. Mice were trained for a week on the “food” (Cap’n Crunch™ Cereal, see Methods for details), fasted overnight, then evaluated for total time mice needed to locate the buried food “pellet”. Mice were assayed at least 45 days post infection.

Naïve mice with normal olfaction found the pellet within a minute (Fig. 4A), sometimes within just seconds. However, previously infected mice required significantly more time, and some mice failed to find the pellet within a ten-minute window (Fig. 4A), suggesting that VSV infection left some mice anosmic. We histologically analyzed the olfactory turbinates of BFT tested mice to determine the total OMP+ OSN sensory surface area. As our observations of airway ossification suggested, previously infected mice had a significant reduction in OMP+ surface area (Fig. 4B). The loss of sensory area was variable, and when mice were binned into minimal, intermediate, and severe OSN loss groups by the amount of remaining OMP surface area (Fig. 4C), OSN loss correlated with impaired BFT performance (Fig. 4D). Additionally, there was a positive correlation between CD45+ cells in the OM and latency to find the buried pellet (Fig. 4E). Together, these data demonstrate that olfactory impairment is associated with loss of normal olfactory sensory surface area and increased long-term immune invasion of the olfactory turbinates. VSV infection damages olfactory tissue leading to post viral olfactory dysfunction and anosmia in some animals.

Olfactory stem cells survive infection to rebuild olfactory epithelial cell lineages including OSNs

Despite the profound tissue damage in previously infected areas of olfactory turbinates, the olfactory neuroepithelium appears to still retain neuroregenerative potential. While VSV infection generally leads to cytopathic death of infected cells, we showed previously in VSV-OVA-Cre infected Stopfl/fl TdTomato mice that noncytopathic clearance and survival of previously infected cells occurs within the brain.20 Similarly, Tomato + cells were found within the olfactory turbinates at late time points following infection (Fig. 2A), suggesting that olfactory cells can survive infection and remain within the chemosensory surface.

Using this same paradigm, using a similar approach with Stopfl/fl TdTomato x OMP-GFP dual reporter mice (OMPxTomato) we sought to understand the role of viral infection on regenerative potential. At early time points post-infection, virtually no TdTomato positive cells co-expressed OMP-GFP (mature OSN) and indeed, most OMP expressing mature OSNs are lost (consistent with Fig. 1) (Fig. 5 A–C). However, at later time points post infection, we histologically observe TdTomato + OMP expressing mature OSNs (Fig. 5D). Because these OMP+ cells were virally ablated and needed to be regenerated, this suggested that some neuronal stem cells within the airway can clear VSV infection and survive to differentiate into mature OSNs, along with other olfactory cell lineages. Indeed, we found infected (TdTomato+) ICAM1hi horizontal basal cells55 (HBC, olfactory stem cells) following infection (Fig. 5E). To ascertain the prevalence of olfactory stem cell (HBC and GBC) survival and contribution to olfactory regeneration, we analyzed virally fate-mapped animals at least 100 days post infection, a timepoint when regeneration following infection should be complete.51,56 At these late timepoints, approximately 10 % of the mature OSN population expressed the TdTomato reporter, suggesting they had arisen from a previously infected stem cell (Fig. 5F–H). Furthermore, VSV-Cre infection of Confetti mice, in which Cre drives the stochastic expression of a combination of fluorescent proteins57 revealed “clonal” clusters of stem cell offspring where single infected stem cells marked by different fluorescent proteins gave rise to numerous surrounding cell lineages including OSNs (Fig. 5I, J), further suggesting olfactory mucosal tissues remain resilient even in the face of intense viral damage.

OSN patterning guides OSN axons of the same receptor specificity to synapse within the same glomeruli on the olfactory bulb exterior surface.58 We sought to track the fate of an individual OSN specificity in a mouse strain in which OSNs express GFP from the M72 olfactory receptor promoter59 (M72-GFP). Following unilateral VSV-OVA-Cre infection of M72-GFP x Stopfl/fl TdTomato reporter mice, we observed gross atrophy of the olfactory bulb on the infected (ipsilateral) side (right, Fig. 5L, M), which associated with a loss of M72-GFP + glomeruli (right, Fig. 5L). These data suggested that for certain olfactory specificities, viral infection can alter sensory function as suggested by complete ablation of M72 receptor. Despite the M72 loss, we observed reinnervation of TdTomato-positive neurons into the olfactory bulb at 60 dpi (right, Fig. 5M), indicating that while the M72 population was lost following infection, stem cell precursors for other OSN specificities were able to survive viral infection and generate mature OSNs that formed connections within the olfactory bulb.

Discussion

Here, we use vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) as a murine model of olfactotropic viral infection1 to identify cellular and functional long-term changes to the olfactory mucosa following infection. Intranasal VSV infection rapidly infects OSNs and causes extensive tissue damage to the olfactory mucosa. This damage later results in ectopic bone growth within the nasal airway, significant olfactory tissue loss, and neurogenic failure leading to metaplasia from olfactory to respiratory epithelium. Viral infection also recruits numerous immune cells to the olfactory mucosa where many reside after viral resolution. For example, antigen-specific resident T cells are recruited and remain in the olfactory turbinates where they can produce cytokines upon rechallenge (Fig. 2). Interestingly, previously infected animals demonstrated functional deficits in olfaction that correlated with increases in inflammatory cells residing in olfactory tissue (Fig. 4F). Despite damage to the tissue and loss of olfactory epithelium, some previously infected precursor cells survive infection, retain neurogenerative potential, and generate mature olfactory sensory neurons that innervate the olfactory bulb.

Our data indicate that viral olfactory infection can acutely lead to upper airway damage and subsequent immune recruitment that is maintained long-term in the airways.60,61 T cell recruitment into olfactory tissues led to establishment of CD8+ and CD4+ resident memory T cells within the olfactory mucosa, including the neuroepithelium where these cells are poised to produce IFN-ɣ upon viral rechallenge (Fig. 2). Previous studies have demonstrated a role for nasal Trm in providing increased viral control following influenza rechallenge,61 which supports the presumption that tissue resident T cell functions are beneficial. However, in humans, post-COVID-19 patients with smell loss were found to have enhanced olfactory infiltration of IFN- ɣ producing T cells.6 Another study showed patients with long COVID have elevated interferon and T cell associated gene signatures in olfactory tissue,62 both of which correspond to correlated increased T cell activation, interferon response, and smell loss observed in a hamster SARS-CoV-2 infection model.62 Our work contributes to a growing body of literature indicating both protective and potentially detrimental roles for Trm in olfactory tissue health.6 Classically, type-2 inflammation is thought to contribute to smell loss in chronic rhinosinusitis,41 yet others have shown previously that non-type-2 chronic inflammation can increase T cell cytokine (IFN-ɣ and TNF-α) production correlating with smell loss37,39,40,63–65 in mouse models. Of note, our data indicate that peripheral T cell depletion does not impact OM viral titer control after rechallenge. In failing to control viral titer, Trm likely are also unable to prevent tissue damage associated with rechallenge. However, prior olfactory infection may permanently reduce OSN numbers, reducing the cells that VSV can replicate in. This makes it difficult to conclude if Trm contribute to secondary viral resistance or perhaps virus simply has fewer cells in which to replicate. We have observed the new bone formation following secondary infection, which suggests that extensive OM damage still occurs. However, Trm presence does prevent virus from entering the brain. Brain Trm have previously been reported after intranasal VSV infection,60,66 but their role in controlling viral replication after intranasal infection was not tested. Prior studies have suggested roles for brain Trm in controlling viral titer,67,68 however, these studies did not address protection during viral neuroinvasion via the olfactory mucosa. Our data are consistent with this prior work, however, we cannot distinguish between Trm activity in the OM or OB Additional work is needed to determine the contribution of these resident T cells to post-viral olfactory dysfunction.

The dramatic mucosal remodeling of the olfactory turbinates after infection leads to reduced OSN coverage and predictably correlates with functional deficits in odor detection (Fig. 4). Although the relative contribution of olfactory mucosal damage and central olfactory processing deficits cannot be distinguished here, the correlation between smell loss and loss of olfactory neuron coverage on the nasal turbinates suggests these two are linked. A marked reduction in olfactory sensory neurons should lead to deficiencies in odor detection. Tissue remodeling may confound the interpretation of smell loss, as it is difficult to ascertain if loss is due to aberrant sensory function or ablation of sensory area. Some OSN coverage loss can be attributed to areas of abnormal bone overgrowth, or hyperostosis. This hyperostosis is reminiscent of the osteoneogenesis and bone thickening that commonly occurs in chronic rhinosinusitis patients.69–71 Hyperostosis is more common in severe and refractory cases, however the etiology of the osteoneogenesis and its contribution to disease relapse remain controversial.72 After VSV infection, we observed increased osteogenesis that led to heterotopic ossification, which is further supported by the gene expression data following infection (Fig. 3). The most common benign bony hypertrophy of the nasal cavities is the paranasal osteoma.73 Proposed etiologies of osteoma include trauma, genetic predisposition, and infection/inflammation. However, the bone overgrowth seen after VSV infection is not well circumscribed and round, as is the common presentation of osteomas, and there is no evidence that viral infection in humans triggers paranasal osteoma formation. Other rare causes of benign turbinate bone hypertrophy of nasal turbinates include ossifying fibroma and fibrous dysplasia,74 which also lack evidence for viral etiology in humans. Nevertheless, our data clearly show that viral infection can result in significant nasal ossification.

Nasal bone is formed through intramembranous ossification from mesenchymal precursors, whereas the ethmoid bone is formed through endochondral ossification in development.75 During mucosal infection, VSV induced cytopathology results in copious cellular debris accumulation within the airway. This debris, likely containing OSN cell bodies and infiltrating immune cells, continues to accumulate within the nasal turbinates for up to a week post infection, and it is possible that this cellular debris provides a scaffold for mesenchymal precursor seeding of new trabecular bone. Infection induced bone growth appears to move outward from existing turbinates (Fig. 3F), suggesting a misguided healing response, but the mechanisms underlying initiation of bone growth and expansion of the osteoblastic field remain unclear. The osteoneogenesis observed in our study often results in marrow rich bone formation, indicating proper revascularization.75 Unlike long bones, which are fully vascularized in neonatal development,76 skull bones increase in vascularity and bone marrow content into adult life.77 It is possible that post-viral osteogenesis amplifies this natural process.

In addition to hyperostosis of olfactory turbinates, VSV intranasal infection causes a metaplastic conversion of olfactory to respiratory tissue. In humans with post-viral olfactory dysfunction, histological analyses have demonstrated degeneration and reduced OSNs in the epithelium, suggesting respiratory conversion.78 In other animal models of olfactory damage, including bulbectomy29,79 and chemical models like methimazole, the neuroregenerative potential of the tissue is retained.50,53 Our data suggest that VSV intranasal infection may serve as a model of the clinical respiratory conversion observed following human infection. Future studies will be important to address the mechanisms by which the neuroregenerative potential is halted in infected areas such that respiratory metaplasia occurs, as this has clinical relevance for post-viral olfactory dysfunction. Virally induced respiratory metaplasia is also a potential mechanism underlying age-related anosmia or presbyosmia, as respiratory metaplasia is commonly found in aged samples.36,80–84 Most patients over the age of 65 and 80 % of patients over the age of 80 have olfaction impairments.85,86 In human samples of presbyosmia, single cell RNA-sequencing data showed an increased inflammatory signature in stem cells, suggesting derangement in neuronal differentiation.36 While immune activity is likely involved in human presbyosmic and post viral olfactory loss, the exact mechanisms that lead to these changes remain unknown. The VSV infection model described herein provides a defined system to explore at least one environmental trigger of olfactory mucosal deterioration, respiratory metaplasia, and subsequent smell loss.

Our prior work has shown that VSV infection can be non-cytolytically cleared from cells within the olfactory bulb of the brain20 and that influenza B infected OSNs can also non-cytolytically clear virus and survive with an increased interferon response.11 Indeed, during influenza B infection, viral Cre expression labels infected OSNs within 3 days, and these neurons survive long-term.11 Unlike influenza infection, we demonstrate here that VSV causes rapid OSN death and effectively ablates the mature OMP expressing OSN population from the olfactory mucosa (Figs. 1, 3, 5). While infected OSNs themselves are lost following infection, a population of virally fate-mapped OSNs, arise at later timepoints, indicating that some infected and fate mapped olfactory stem cells survive and give rise to clonal clusters of OSNs and other olfactory mucosal populations, including sustentacular cells (Fig. 5D, F–H). This is especially clear when observing clonal clusters of neuroepithelial cells that have arisen from single infected olfactory stem cells (Fig. 5I, J). M72-GFP reporter animals illuminate OSNs of a single specificity that target axonal projections to a specific glomeruli on the dorsal surface of the mouse olfactory bulb.58 Following unilateral VSV-Cre infection and olfactory regeneration in M72-GFP x Stop fl/fl TdTomato reporter animals, we observed permanent ablation of the M72 specificity; however, these same animals displayed previously infected (Tomato+) OSNs projecting to different glomeruli (Fig. 5 M). These Tomato+ projections represent OSNs regenerated from surviving infected stem cells, likely retaining original olfactory receptor usage. Both globose basal cell and horizontal basal cell populations can repopulate the olfactory neuroepithelium, but horizontal basal cells only differentiate following severe epithelial damage.29,87 Individual OSN specificities are restricted to distinct zones within the olfactory turbinates,88,89 and it is possible that horizontal basal cells populating these different zones are differentially equipped to survive infection, intrinsically or through enhanced capacity to recognize immune signals. It is also possible that viral damage is simply more intense in some region, or there could be damage associated changes to recently described “cellular neighborhoods”.90 Regardless, our data are consistent with recent work suggesting that there are regional differences in OSN ability to properly reinnervate the OB after a significant chemical olfactory lesion.91 Whether OSNs arising from previously infected stem cells possess lasting intrinsic differences such as enhanced innate immune capacity, whether at baseline or in response to local innate immune signals, remains the subject of future study.

Our study is not without limitations. Although respiratory metaplasia is seen in humans, the exact viral mechanisms driving this change are still understudied. VSV directly infects olfactory sensory neurons, providing an excellent model of a virus that not only infects neurons, but that efficiently invades the CNS via this route. However, other respiratory pathogens like SARS-Cov2 cause olfactory pathology but do not directly infect olfactory sensory neurons. Future work will be needed to determine how other upper respiratory tract infections, particularly those that can infect neurons with or without CNS invasion such as avian influenza impact olfactory tissues. Nevertheless, our system demonstrates how olfactory infection can lead to long term immune residence and tissue changes corresponding to smell loss.

In conclusion, we propose that a delicate balance exists in olfactory mucosal tissue between the maintenance of regenerative capacity and the ability to mount an effective immune defense against primary and secondary infections. In this study, the host response to infection leads to immune invasion and tissue regeneration that differs from baseline behaviors. Despite these aberrancies, establishment of tissue resident memory T cells within olfactory tissue after infection likely protects it from secondary challenge by eliciting a rapid cytokine response. The evident damage to olfactory function is mitigated to some degree by OSN repopulation from previously infected neuronal precursors, but we postulate that the olfactory system may trade a level of sensory function in favor of installing a long-term antiviral immune defense system. For example, our previous work has illustrated that olfactory mucosal macrophage populations shift following infection from a P2RY12-expressing “neuronal support” macrophage to MHCII-expressing “pathogen defense” macrophage, suggesting that the system rebalances toward strengthening pathogen defense over tissue maintenance.22 In general, the VSV model of infection provides an excellent paradigm to explore many outstanding questions relating to how viruses affect different immune, bone, and neuronal cell populations in the olfactory turbinates.

Viruses and plaque assays

VSV-OVA serotype Indiana (provided by Leo Lefrancois, formerly at the University of Connecticut95), VSV-DsRed, VSV-NJ, VSV-OVA-Cre20 were propagated on BHK21 or green monkey kidney (Vero) cells. Infectivity of VSV preparations was quantified by plaque assay on Vero cells. VSV titers from olfactory mucosa of infected mice were determined similarly. Briefly, animals were perfused with saline, olfactory turbinates were dissected and immersed in 1ml of 2% FBS RPMI containing zirconia silica beads and homogenized with a FastPrep 24-5G (MP Biomedical). Homogenates were spun for 3min at 15,000g, and supernatants were serially diluted for quantification on Vero cells. One-hundred microliters of diluted homogenate was added to Vero monolayers in flat bottom 24-well plates and incubated for ~3h. One-hundred microliters of 0.75% methylcellulose in DMEM (containing 10% FBS, 1% glutamine, and 1% Pen/Strep) were added to each well and incubated for 44–48 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were subsequently fixed with 4% neutral buffered formalin and stained with crystal violet, and plaques were enumerated.

Infections

Eight- to 10-week-old mice were infected intranasally by pipetting 10μL of viral diluent in each nostril under isoflurane anesthesia. For titering, immunohistochemical, flow cytometric, and qPCR experiments, mice were infected with 1 × 105 PFU of VSV per nostril.

Flow cytometry staining

Tissue digestion for Flow cytometry: Mice were perfused intracardially with saline solution prior to harvesting organs and mincing with scissors. Tissues were digested in 0.5mL Hibernate A with DNase I (Roche, 0.5mg/mL) and Collagenase D (Roche, 0.5mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min with constant shaking. Cells were washed, spun down at 500g for 5 min, and resuspended in PBS. Cell populations were stained as follows: CD45+ cells: neutrophils (CD45+, TCRβ− Ly6Cint, Ly6Ghi, CD11b+) and monocytes (CD45+,TCRβ− Ly6Chi, CD11bhi), T cells (CD45+,TCRβ+ CD4+ of CD8a+). Antibodies used were from Biolegend. CD45 (PE and BV650; 30-F11), anti-CD4 (BV785; RM4-5), anti-CD8 (BV421 and APC-Cy7; 53–6.7), anti-TCR-beta (Alexa-647; H57-597), anti-Ly6C (APC-Cy7; HK1.4), anti-Ly6G (BV510; 1A8), anti-CD11b (PE-Cy7; M1/70), anti-CD103 (PE-Cy7; 2E7), anti-H-2Kb/H-2Db (Alexa-647). Samples were acquired on an LSR II digital flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data was analyzed using the FlowJo software (Tree Star).

For ex vivo peptide stimulation and intracellular cytokine staining

Single cell suspensions prepared as above for flow cytometry were plated in 96 well round bottom plates in RPMI complete media (RPMI, 10 % FBS, 200 μM glutamine, 55 μM 2-mercaptoethanol) with 5ug/ml of Brefeldin A (Sigma) at 37°C for five hours with or without 2ug/ml SIINFEKL peptide. To detect intracellular IFNɣ production, single cell suspensions were surface stained as described above, treated with cytofix/cytoperm (BD), and then stained intracellularly with anti-IFNɣ (Biolegend, XMG1.2) and anti-TNFα (BioLegend, MP6-XT22). Samples were acquired on an LSR II digital flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data was analyzed using the FlowJo software (Tree Star).

In vivo depletion studies

Antibodies used for cell-specific depletions were purchased from BioXCell. C57BL6/J mice were injected with 300µg of anti-CD8α (YTS169), 600µg of anti-CD4 (GK1.5) or 600µg of isotype Rat IgG2b control antibodies. All antibodies were administered intravenously one day prior to infection.

Adoptive lymphocyte transfer

For imaging and flow cytometric studies, blood was isolated from OT-I, OT-II, or L7 mice, checked for transgene positivity, and counted to determine cell numbers for transfer. Three thousand mCerulean+ or mTomato+ CD8+ OT-I T cells, 40,000 CD45.1 OT-II, and 20,000 YFP+ CD4+ L7 cells were transferred into naïve WT recipients one day prior to infection.

Quantitative PCR

Mice were anesthetized and subsequently euthanized via intracardiac saline perfusion at the indicated days post VSV intranasal infection. Olfactory turbinates were removed and placed into screw cap 2ml tubes containing sterile zirconia/silica beads (Biospec) and Trizol (ThermoFisher). Tubes were homogenized and chloroform was added to the mixture and spun down (12,000g at 4°C) and the supernatant was isolated. RNA was extracted using a PureLink RNA Mini kit (ThermoFisher). RNA concentrations were quantified using a Nanodrop, and gDNA was digested using a DNAse I kit (ThermoFisher). cDNA was generated using an iScript cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). 96-well premade PrimePCR plates (Bio-Rad) were used for qPCR experiments. 10-20ng of cDNA and SYBR green reagent (Bio-Rad) were added to lyophilized primers in each well and the plate was read on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time with C1000 Thermal Cycler system. On each PrimePCR plate there were control wells for RNA quality and gDNA contamination. qPCR data was analyzed using Bio-Rad Gene Study software, with expression values relative to those of the control genes Gapdh and Tbp. Values from 4 naïve animals were averaged and compared to 3 experimental animals per timepoint. Normalization was done by average naïve values and then dividing individual timepoint values by the average naïve value. Selected values with p values of 0.05 or less.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were perfused intracardially with 2.5% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and skin around the head and nose was removed before fixing whole heads in 2.5% NBF overnight at 4°C. Heads were then submerged in a 0.5M EDTA solution for 5–7 days at 4°C to decalcify bone around the olfactory regions. Once decalcified, heads were embedded in tissue freezing medium (Triangle Biomedical Sciences) for coronal sections. Cryosections were cut using a Leica CM1850 cryostat at a thickness of 30μm onto SuperFrost glass slides (Fisher) after which sections were washed and stained in PBS. Primary antibodies were incubated on cryosections overnight at 4°C. Antibodies used to label tissue sections were from BioLegend unless otherwise noted: CD45 (Alexa-647; PE; 30-F11), CD54 (PE and APC; YN1/1.7.4), anti-CD4 (Alexa-647; RM4-5), anti-CD8a (Alexa-647; 53–6.7), anti-IA/IE (MHC-II) (Alexa-647; M5/114.15.2), anti-CD11b (Alexa-647; M1/70). To stain for Osterix (OSX, or Sp7), tissues were stained with rabbit anti-Osterix (ab22552, AbCam) overnight at 4°C, followed by 2–4hr staining with anti-rabbit Alexa-647 (111-605-003, Jackson Immunoresearch). Rabbit anti-GFP antibody was used to identify Cerulean and YFP expressing cells (Invitrogen, A21311). After staining, sections were washed 2 times with PBS, and 2–3 drops 2–3 drops of FluorSave Reagent (Calbiochem) were added to each section before addition of a coverslip. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV1200 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped 4 detectors, 6 laser lines (405, 458, 488, 515, 559, and 635 nm) and 5 objectives (4×/0.16NA, 10×/0.4NA, 20×/0.75NA, 40×/0.95NA, and chromatic aberration corrected 60×/1.4NA). Paraffin embedding, sectioning, and Masson trichrome staining were performed by Histoserv, Inc.

Immune cell quantification within olfactory mucosa layers

Coronal sections of previously infected OMP-GFP+ mice were stained for cell populations and imaged as above. Imaris software (Bit-plane) was used to measure OMP+ segments of olfactory turbinates and cells were manually counted in subregions (Neuroepithelium (cells entirely within OMP + OSN cell bodies, Basal lamina (cells that were in contact with the apical or basal side of the basement membrane, identified by ICAM1) or lamina propria (cells that were with elsewhere within the lamina propria). Subregion cell counts were subsequently divided by individual segment lengths. Measured turbinate segments were only quantified if they were fully lined with OMP+ cells, metaplastic regions were excluded.

Quantification of OSX spots for total surface area

Coronal tissue sections from C57BL/6 mice days 10 and 15 post intranasal VSV infection were stained and imaged for Osterix as noted above. Total turbinate surfaces areas were generated using Imaris software, followed by total OSX spots quantified using Imaris software. The number of spots per square micron were then calculated for each different timepoint (average of two sections per animals).

Buried food test

VSV infected animals were allowed to recover for at least 40 days following infection. A buried food test was performed on adult mice to evaluate their sense of smell.54 Animals were trained for 3–4 consecutive days by adding treats (Captain Crunch cereal, (1 pellet/mouse/day) to the cage. The night prior to testing, regular chow was removed from the home cage, and animals fasted for 18–24h. On the day of the test, each mouse was placed in a clean cage with 3 cm of clean bedding, allowed to acclimate for 5 min, removed from the cage briefly so a food pellet could be buried, and then then animal returned to the cage to search for the pellet (Captain Crunch cereal) hidden beneath the surface of the bedding. Mice were given a maximum of 10 min (600s) to find the buried food. The food was considered “found” when the animal began eating the pellet, latency to pellet measured by time returned to the cage until mouse uncovered and began to eat the treat. All tests were performed in the morning (9am – 12am).

Single Cell RNA seq

As described in Wellford, 202422 scRNAseq data was generated using the Seq-Well platform. Four separate samples from OMP-GFP+ mice were concatenated to comprehensively survey olfactory mucosa immune cells while minimizing contaminate from turbinate bone marrow. One contained OMP-GFP− CD45+ cells, another contained OMP-GFP− CD45+ Ly6g− CD19− cells, one contained OMP-GFP− CD45+ Ly6g− CD19− cells from intranasally VSV infected mice (45 days post infection), and one contained bone marrow. Data analysis and processing was performed in RStudio using the Seurat v_4.1.0 analysis package and standard pre-processing workflow. Nearest-neighbor clustering was used to identify CD45+ cell populations. Clusters with a higher proportion of bone marrow sample cells than olfactory mucosa sample cells were then removed from the data to yield true “olfactory mucosa” immune cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined using Student’s t test (two groups, parametric data), ranked sum test (two groups, non-parametric data), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; more than two groups with parametric data), or a one-way ANOVA on ranks (more than two groups with non-parametric data). Statistical differences between survival curves were determined used log-rank test. The correlation coefficient and p value shown in Fig. 4F were calculated using a Pearson’s product-moment correlation test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 or SigmaPlot 11.0.
