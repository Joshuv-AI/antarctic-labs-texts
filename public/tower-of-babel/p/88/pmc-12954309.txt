Outcomes of relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia after menin inhibition failure∗
Authors: Kuo-Kai Chin, Brian J Ball, Yasmin Abaza, Jessica K Altman, Rahul K Thakur, Moiez Ali, Andriy Derkach, Mark B Geyer, Aaron D Goldberg, Tamanna Z Haque, Meghan C Thompson, Jae H Park, Martin S Tallman, Sheng F Cai, Eunice S Wang, Ibrahim T Aldoss, Eytan M Stein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Among patients with R/R AML after MENINi treatment, outcomes are poor.•Responses with further therapy are primarily seen with VEN-based therapies.

Introduction

Menin is a scaffold protein essential for leukemogenesis in KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2Ar) and nucleophosmin 1-mutated (NPM1c) acute myeloid leukemia (AML), as well as other leukemias associated with HOX gene overexpression.1, 2, 3 Menin inhibitors (MENINi) are a novel class of targeted therapies that have shown promise in relapsed or refractory (R/R) KMT2Ar or NPM1c acute leukemias.4,5 Revumenib (SNDX-5613, Syndax) is a first-in-class MENINi that entered clinical studies in 2020. In the AUGMENT-101 study, revumenib treatment led to complete remission (CR) or CR with partial hematologic recovery in 22.8% of patients with KMT2Ar and 23.4% of patients with NPM1c, with an overall response rate (ORR) of 63.2% and 46.9%, respectively.6,7 Based on these results, revumenib was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for R/R KMT2Ar acute leukemias and is under review for R/R NPM1c acute leukemias.8

Several additional MENINi are also under development. For instance, ziftomenib (KO-539, Kura) and bleximenib (JNJ-75276617, Janssen) are being studied in early phase clinical trials, and ziftomenib is under priority review for R/R NPM1c acute leukemias.5,9, 10, 11, 12 MENINi are also being studied in other lines of therapy including in de novo AML, in combination with intensive chemotherapy or a hypomethylating agent (HMA) and venetoclax (VEN), and in other acute leukemias with HOX gene overexpression.13, 14, 15, 16, 17

Despite encouraging responses seen with MENINi, resistance occurs rapidly. For instance, the median duration of CR/CR with partial hematologic recovery with revumenib in patients with KMT2Ar and NPM1c acute leukemia were 6.4 and 4.7 months, respectively, whereas the median duration of composite complete remission was 5.6 months for ziftomenib in the published phase 1 data.5, 6, 7 One mechanism of resistance is through the development of MEN1 mutations, which were first reported with revumenib therapy.18 As described by Perner et al, MEN1 mutations disrupt revumenib binding to restore HOX gene overexpression and leukemogenesis. It is unclear to what extent MEN1 mutations occur with other MENINi, and what alternative mechanisms of resistance may be present, because not all patients who experience disease relapse or refractory disease have MEN1 mutations. The broader mutational landscape and outcomes after MENINi inhibitor failure are largely unknown, and the full complement of resistance mutations and mechanisms need to be better elucidated. In addition, the question of how to most effectively treat patients after MENINi failure remains almost entirely unanswered.

Given the rapid adoption of MENINi in AML, it is critical to understand resistance patterns and to establish effective therapies after MENINi failure. We hypothesized that patients who have R/R AML after MENINi will have poor outcomes, a variety of mutational profiles, and that response to specific salvage regimens can be predicted based on molecular and cytogenetic features. To test this, we conducted a multicenter retrospective analysis of patients with R/R AML after MENINi failure to describe genomic evolution at MENINi failure and assess efficacy of subsequent therapies.

Methods

Patients

We performed a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with R/R AML after MENINi monotherapy across 4 US academic centers between January 2020 and March 2025. All patients had R/R AML before MENINi initiation. MENINi failure was defined as refractory disease or response followed by relapse with MENINi therapy. Treatment discontinuation for reasons other than disease progression, such as planned allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) or, rarely, toxicity, was not classified as MENINi failure. All MENINi agents used at the participating centers were included in the analysis.

Definitions

Relapsed AML was defined as ≥5% marrow blasts after CR, CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi), or morphologic leukemia-free state (MLFS). Refractory AML was defined as lack of response after at least 1 cycle of MENINi, or progression of disease despite initiating and receiving at least 14 days of MENINi. Overall survival (OS) was defined from the date of treatment initiation to the date of death. Demographics, disease characteristics, treatment histories, and outcomes were identified from the medical record. AML diagnosis, risk classification, and response were defined based on European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 criteria.19 The combination of CR, CRi, and MLFS was considered an overall response (OR). When defining lines of therapy, consolidative treatments and allo-SCT were considered a single line of therapy. Measurable residual disease was determined based on multiparameter flow cytometry at a cutoff of 10−4 according to leukemia-associated immunophenotype and/or different from normal aberrant immunophenotype approaches.20 Next-generation sequencing was performed per institutional standard of care.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are reported descriptively. Fisher exact tests and Student t tests were used for significance testing of categorical and continuous data, respectively. Kaplan-Meier methodology was used for survival statistics and comparisons between groups were performed using the log-rank test. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2.

Ethical considerations

This project was approved by the institutional review board of all participating institutions. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Characteristics of patients who experience MENINi failure

Our cohort included 84 adult patients (median age, 55 years; 43% male [n = 36]; Table 1) who had R/R AML after receiving MENINi monotherapy. This cohort included 53 patients with KMT2Ar (63%), 19 patients with NPM1c (23%), and 12 patients with other mutations such as NUP98r (14%). At diagnosis, patients were typically intermediate (n = 34, 40%) or adverse risk (n = 42, 50%) by ELN 2022 criteria, and 25% of patients had therapy-related AML. Before MENINi treatment, 86% (n = 72) of patients had received intensive chemotherapy (IC) and 77% (n = 65) had received VEN-based therapy, including 65% (n = 55) who had received both. A total of 32 (38%) patients had previously received allo-SCT for AML before initiating MENINi.

Revumenib was the most commonly received MENINi in this cohort (n = 61 [73%]; Table 2), followed by bleximenib (n = 12 [14%]) and ziftomenib (n = 9 [11%]). Patients most commonly received MENINi after 4 previous treatment regimens (n = 45 [54%]) and received a median of 3 cycles of MENINi (range, 1-22). Choice of salvage therapy after MENINi failure

After MENINi failure, 40% of patients (n = 34) did not receive any further antileukemia therapies (Table 3). Of those who did receive antileukemia therapies, HMA/VEN (n = 13 [26%]), clinical trial (n = 13 [26%]), and gilteritinib or gilteritinib in combination (GILT; n = 9 [18%]) were the most common therapies. One patient received GILT without repeat confirmation of FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) at time of MENINi failure. Of note, 5 patients who experienced disease relapse after responding to, and discontinuing, the MENINi, such as after allo-SCT, were rechallenged with their previous MENINi. Two patients who were refractory to 1 MENINi were subsequently treated with a different MENINi. Sixty-two percent of patients receiving HMA/VEN (n = 8) had previously received VEN, and 50% of the patients receiving IC + VEN (n = 3) had previously received VEN (supplemental Table 1A). Nearly all patients receiving subsequent therapy had previously received IC (n = 35 [92%]), and all patients who were IC naïve received HMA/VEN (n = 3; supplemental Table 1B).

Post-MENINi therapies

Response to subsequent therapy after MENINi failure

Due to the heterogeneity of clinical trial therapies as well as the uncertain availability of such treatments, our analysis is focused on patients who received subsequent standard-of-care therapy (n = 37). However, of 13 patients who received investigational therapies after MENINi failure, the CR/CRi rate was 7.6% (n = 1) and the ORR was 15% (n = 2). Noninvestigational post-MENINi therapy resulted in a CR/CRi rate of 19% (n = 7) and an ORR of 32% (n = 12; Table 4). The regimens that were able to achieve CR/CRi and OR were HMA/VEN (CR/CRi, 15% [n = 2]; ORR, 38% [n = 5]), IC + VEN (CR/CRi, 67% [n = 4]; ORR, 83% [n = 5]), and switching of MENINi (CR/CRi, 50% [n = 1]; ORR, 100% [n = 2]). No patient responded when rechallenged with their previous MENINi. The IC + VEN regimens were primarily FLAG-IDA-VEN (fludarabine, high-dose cytarabine, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, idarubicin, and VEN) and FCE + VEN (fludarabine, cytarabine, etoposide, and VEN).

Two patients with refractory KMT2Ar AML after bleximenib were switched to revumenib, with 1 achieving CR and 1 achieving MLFS. The patients had been treated with bleximenib at doses below the recommended phase 2 dose of 100 mg twice daily (50 mg daily and 60 mg twice daily, respectively). Clinical predictors of response to therapy after MENINi failure

To identify potential predictors of CR/CRi and OR to therapy after MENINi failure, we conducted a series of comparisons (Tables 5 and 6). Examining underlying patient and leukemia characteristics, as well as treatment histories, there was a statistically significant difference in the likelihood of response based on the subsequent therapy selected (CR/CRi, P = .016; ORR, P = .029), with VEN-based therapies and MENINi switching being potentially promising, albeit with limited sample size. There was association with response in patients without previous VEN exposure (20% ORR with previous VEN, 58% ORR without; P = .029).

Molecular and cytogenetics features at time of MENINi failure and response

We examined the molecular and cytogenetic features at time of MENINi failure and their correlation with response. The most common mutations at time of MENINi failure were FLT3-ITD (n = 12 [32%]), WT1 (n = 8 [21%]), and MEN1 (n = 8 [21%]; Figure 1; supplemental Table 2). The FLT3-ITD and WT1 mutations were all preexisting mutations noted before MENINi, whereas the MEN1 mutations were all newly emergent. Of patients with known cytogenetics at time of MENINi failure, 22 (67%) had ELN adverse cytogenetics, including 13 (39%) with complex karyotype. Using the Fisher exact test to examine the association of individual mutations, the presence of FLT3-ITD, which was often associated with use of GILT-based therapy (8 of 12 [67%]), was associated with not achieving CR/CRi (CR/CRi, 0% vs 32%; P = .036; supplemental Table 3A). No other individual mutations or cytogenetic features were statistically significant predictors of response, albeit with limited sample size. One patient with WT1-mutant AML achieved CR/CRi with subsequent MENINi switch (WT1 mutation, bleximenib to revumenib). One patient with MEN1 mutation after revumenib therapy achieved CR/CRi with IC + VEN. All documented MEN1 mutations were seen in patients treated with revumenib, although MEN1 testing was not available for all patients. Patients who did not have FLT3-ITD, WT1, or MEN1 mutations (n = 15) had CR/CRi of 33% (n = 5) and ORR of 47% (n = 7). Patients with KMT2Ar (n = 24) had a CR/CRi rate of 21% (n = 5) and ORR of 38% (n = 9), whereas patients with NPM1c (n = 5), all of whom had a FLT3-ITD mutation, had a CR/CRi rate of 0% (n = 0) and an ORR of 20% (n = 1). Patients with complex karyotype (n = 13) had a CR/CRi rate of 23% (n = 3) and an ORR of 31% (n = 4).

Survival with therapy after MENINi failure

We then analyzed survival outcomes in our cohort. At a median follow-up of 19.4 months, the median OS (mOS) of patients treated with noninvestigational subsequent therapies after MENINi failure was 4.4 months relative to the start of subsequent therapy (Figure 2A). We stratified the survival analysis based on several factors, including the response group (refractory to MENINi vs relapsed after response to MENINi), previous exposure to VEN, and VEN-based subsequent therapy (Figure 2B-D). Survival outcomes were not significantly different in these comparisons, although there was a trend toward improved survival in patients without previous VEN exposure (mOS, 8.5 vs 4.0 months; P = .075). Of 5 patients who achieved an OR with IC + VEN, 2 died from infectious complications at 4 months, and 1 experienced relapse at 2 months. Of 5 responders to HMA/VEN, 1 experienced relapse at 2 months, possibly limiting the OS benefit of VEN-based therapies.

We examined the relationship between response and survival outcomes. To avoid immortal time bias, we incorporated a 1-month landmark. Achieving CR/CRi and an OR were both significantly associated with improved survival (Figure 2E-F). Patients who achieved CR/CRi had mOS of 15.4 months vs 3.4 months after the landmark in those who did not (P = .048) and patients who achieved OR also had mOS of 15.4 vs 3.4 months (P = .0055). Of 6 responders who had proceeded to allo-SCT, the mOS was not reached at median follow-up of 18.2 months, with 5 patients remaining alive and 4 remaining in remission at last follow-up (supplemental Figure 1; 1 patient who received allo-SCT excluded by 2-month landmark). The mOS of responders who did not proceed to allo-SCT was 5.3 months (3.3 months after 2-month landmark) and significantly lower (P = .013).

Discussion

As the role of MENINi expands in the treatment of AML, understanding patterns of resistance and responses to subsequent therapy is key. In this retrospective study, we detailed the molecular and cytogenetic landscape in patients with R/R AML after MENINi as well as outcomes with subsequent treatment. Although this is a small retrospective study, it is a detailed examination of a novel and growing cohort, and we share several intriguing findings.

First, R/R AML with KMT2Ar, NPM1c, and other mutations associated with HOX overexpression were known to be associated with poor outcomes before the development of MENINi.21, 22, 23 Logically, patients who are refractory to MENINi or who experience disease relapse after response to MENINi also have poor outcomes, because effective subsequent therapies are limited, although responses with chemotherapy and VEN have been reported.24 In our study, 34 of 84 (40%) patients who experienced MENINi failure did not receive a subsequent therapy and transitioned to supportive care. However, when subsequent therapy was pursued with noninvestigational therapies (n = 37), patients were able to achieve CR/CRi 19% of the time (n = 7) and CR/CRi/MLFS 32% of the time (n = 12). Although the choice of subsequent therapy was informed by exposure to previous therapies, fitness, and the characteristics of the patient’s leukemia at time of failure, and these factors may be confounding, all responders either received a VEN-based regimen (HMA/VEN or IC + VEN) or a switch of MENINi. Almost all responders had previous exposure to IC, but more than half of the responders did not have previous VEN exposure, suggesting that prioritizing VEN-based therapy, either IC-based if patient is deemed fit or HMA/VEN otherwise, may be prudent after MENINi failure. This appears especially promising if the patient is fit for subsequent allo-SCT.

Another consideration in therapy after MENINi failure is the possibility of MENINi switching. Data are tremendously limited about this approach. Here, we report 2 responses for patients with KMT2Ar AML who were refractory to bleximenib at non–recommended phase 2 dose dosing yet achieved clinical responses to recommended doses of revumenib monotherapy (1 CR and 1 MLFS). None of these patients had MEN1 mutations and indeed none of those receiving bleximenib were reported to have MEN1 mutations despite becoming refractory to it. Differences in the biochemical properties of different MENINi are yet to be fully understood, with some arguing that bleximenib may be able to overcome MEN1 mutations that can emerge with revumenib and possibly other MENINi.9,25 Our experience is suggestive of patients potentially deriving benefit in the inverse direction (refractory to bleximenib but responding to revumenib), and raises the complex, but exciting, question of how different individual MENINi can be sequenced and how can we most effectively sequence, or even, combine them.

The mutation profile present at time of MENINi failure is also another finding of interest. The most common mutations present at time of R/R disease were FLT3-ITD, WT1, and MEN1, and response rates in these contexts were all disappointingly low (OR in 1 of 13, 1 of 8, and 1 of 8 patients, respectively). Although our understanding of MEN1 as a resistance mutation is emerging, FLT3-ITD and WT1 mutations are well-known predictors of adverse outcomes in AML.26, 27, 28 Of note, 15 patients did not have FLT3-ITD, WT1, or MEN1 mutations and the ORR in those patients was 47% (n = 7), suggesting their potential significance.

Delving into each mutation, most patients with documented FLT3-ITD at the time of MENINi failure were treated with GILT or GILT-based combinations, and none achieved an OR (1 patient achieved MLFS, but with HMA/VEN). All such patients had previously documented FLT3-ITDs. Given the common co-occurrence of FLT3 mutations with NPM1 mutations, this finding possibly supports earlier use of FLT3 inhibition as well as their use in combination, given previous reports of potential synergy between FLT3 and menin inhibition.29 WT1 is a transcription factor that has been described for its role in chemotherapy, FLT3-inhibitor, and VEN resistance in patients with AML.30, 31, 32 Recent data have also linked specific WT1 isoforms to HOX gene overexpression.33 How it may mediate resistance to MENINi and whether it is a main driver of resistance are also worthy of investigation. Finally, the mechanism of MEN1-driven MENINi resistance has been well described, and we present a single case in which IC + VEN resulted in CRi, and 7 cases in which there was no response with subsequent therapy.18 Clearly, preventing and overcoming MEN1 mutations must remain a priority for investigators, and studies with strong preclinical rationale, such as those combining menin inhibition with the Ikaros degrader mezigdomide or other novel agents, should be prioritized.34, 35, 36, 37 These efforts, in conjunction with combination frontline approaches that can lead to deeper and more durable responses, will be crucial.15, 16, 17

Finally, we reviewed survival outcomes associated with therapy after MENINi failure, and as expected, the results were sobering, with mOS of only 4.4 months. However, there is hope for this cohort, because patients who achieve a response to subsequent therapy are associated with having significantly longer survival, with mOS of 15.4 months compared with 3.4 months, and those that respond and then proceed to allo-SCT had an mOS that was not reached in our cohort after a median of 18.2 months of follow-up. Attempting further therapy after MENINi failure in patients who are fit enough to receive it is clearly worthwhile.

Limitations of this study include the fact that it is a retrospective cohort study and that the choice of therapies was therefore nonrandomized and influenced by a multitude of factors that are difficult to fully capture. In addition, our data represent patients who were treated at academic centers with advanced tertiary care capabilities and acute leukemia expertise, that may not be available to all patients. However, we have attempted to capture a multitude of MENINi as well as a range of subsequent therapies across 4 distinct centers to provide a broad and balanced perspective. Also, given the rapid and ongoing transformation of the field with MENINi, with US Food and Drug Administration approval of revumenib in KMT2Ar acute leukemias and possibly other approvals to follow, it is important to present some of the evidence and signals available to guide future studies.8,14

In summary, we present the characteristics and outcomes of patients with R/R AML after treatment with MENINi. In these patients who were either refractory to MENINi or experienced disease relapse after response to MENINi, we demonstrated that although outcomes were poor overall, achieving a response is possible and survival can be improved if patients respond to therapy. In terms of choice of therapy, our data suggest that VEN-based approaches, especially in patients who are naïve to VEN, may be promising. In addition, we show the potential promise of MENINi switching as effective therapy, with 2 responses with switching from bleximenib to revumenib. Finally, through detailed next-generation sequencing profiling, we highlighted the most common mutations present (FLT3-ITD, WT1, and MEN1) and relatively poor outcomes associated with each. Overall, it is clear that MENINi hold much promise. As they gain greater adoption, studies focused on sequencing and development of therapies to prevent and overcome MENINi resistance, are crucial to improving outcomes for this high-risk patient population.
