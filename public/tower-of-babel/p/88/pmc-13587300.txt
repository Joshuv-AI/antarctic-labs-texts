Inefficacy of a novel osmotic associative learning assay in C. elegans
Authors: Jin Hua Li, Kaiden H. Price, Michael N. Nitabach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn this study, we developed and tested a novel osmotic associative assay for facilitating the study of associative learning circuits with constrained sensory inputs in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans).ResultsHyperosmolarity, the unconditioned stimulus, was paired with benzaldehyde, an attractive odorant here used as a conditioned stimulus, in order to train an association between hyperosmolarity and odor. It was hypothesized that animals conditioned in hyperosmotic conditions for 90 min would show reduced chemotaxis to benzaldehyde compared to control animals. Results suggest that, following exposure to a hyperosmolar 500mM fructose solution, C. elegans chemotaxis to benzaldehyde was impaired. At more moderate hyperosmolarity, worms failed to learn to associate hyperosmolarity with benzaldehyde – i.e., there was no difference between chemotaxis index values of conditioned and naive worms in any of the fructose concentrations. Thus, our results both suggest that 90-minute associative learning assays in C. elegans may not succeed with hyperosmolarity as an unconditioned stimulus, and provide a cautionary note that researchers using hyperosmotic solutions in worm behavior assays should be cognizant of potential effects on naïve chemotaxis behavior.

Introduction

In C.elegans, a model organism with a simple nervous system [1], a variety of assays have been used to study learning and memory. These include sensory adaptation, habituation, and associative learning [2]. Research protocols that were initially thought to test for simple sensory adaptation, wherein C. elegans was presented with an odor stimulus in the absence of food [3], were later shown to be tests of associative learning [4]. While sensory adaptation examines the reduced response to a single stimulus following prolonged exposure to that stimulus [2], associative learning involves pairing a conditioned stimulus and an unconditioned stimulus, resulting in a conditioned response (a change in how the animal responds to the conditioned stimulus [4, 5]). In C. elegans, food deprivation is typically used as the unconditioned stimulus in associative learning assays [6–8]. Pairing food deprivation with a conditioned stimulus that is otherwise attractive will reduce the worm’s chemotaxis to the conditioned stimulus [2].

Examining the neuronal circuit changes that occur following associative learning assays has resulted in numerous contributions to our understanding of learning [9–11]. The associative learning circuit begins with the neurons that sense the conditioned and unconditioned stimuli. We used benzaldehyde, an attractive odorant sometimes used in C. elegans associative learning assays [3, 4, 12], as our conditioned stimulus. Benzaldehyde is primarily sensed by the AWC neuron pair [13]. Additionally, the AWA, ASE, and AWB neurons play a contributing role in benzaldehyde sensation [14].

However, due to the system-wide nature of metabolic information [15–17], using food deprivation as an unconditioned stimulus makes it difficult to isolate the source of sensory input. To aid in the identification of a circuit structure involved in associative learning, it could be useful to have an unconditioned stimulus that is detected by a select number of neurons rather than entire systems simultaneously. This paper explores environmental hyperosmolarity as a potential unconditioned stimulus, with a goal of developing an assay that can be used to isolate a relatively constrained circuit for associative learning.

Hyperosmolarity has two potential modes of sensory input: overall osmolarity, and the detection of the specific solute used to create a hyperosmolar solution. We chose to use fructose as our solute. While the neurons that may sense fructose are currently unknown, glucose, a monosaccharide similar in structure to fructose, activates the ASEL neuron [18]. High levels of osmolarity are detected in the worm almost exclusively by the ASH neuron pair [19–21]. While the ASH neuron pair does not respond to low levels of hyperosmolarity [21], more moderate levels of hyperosmolarity can be detected by a trio of sensory neurons, AQR, PQR, and URX [22]. High levels of osmolarity can have negative systemic impacts on the worm, including death [23, 24]. However, an osmotic associative learning assay using low to moderate upshifts in osmolarity as the unconditioned stimulus might be effective for associative learning while reducing the sensory input to a smaller network of neurons. This smaller set of sensory inputs, especially when compared to the systemic effects of food deprivation [15–17], could provide an advantage to further studies working to understand the molecular and circuit mechanisms of associative learning.

Materials and methods

The wild-type Bristol N2 strain was used. Worms were grown on Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli (E. coli) OP50 at 20 °C and maintained using standard methods [25]. All experiments were run on at least two separate days.

Food deprivation associative learning assay

Food deprivation assays were adapted from Colbert and Bargmann [3] and L’Etoile and Bargmann [26], using a liquid conditioning medium [6, 26, 27] to reduce worms lost to climbing the walls of a petri dish. All assays used well-fed, one-day old adults. Prior to conditioning, naive adult worms were washed from NGM plates with S basal buffer solution (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) and 100 mM NaCl) into 7 ml glass vials. The worms were washed three additional times in S basal buffer. After allowing worms to settle at the bottom of the vial, excess S basal was removed with a plastic transfer pipette. Then, 1 ml of S basal buffer was added to the vial, with or without 0.5 mM benzaldehyde. Glass vials were laid horizontally to maintain aeration during odor conditioning. Worms were conditioned for 90 min and then tested on chemotaxis plates.

Hyperosmolarity associative learning assay

To condition worms to reduce their chemotaxis to benzaldehyde due to hyperosmolarity, we added varying concentrations of fructose (Alfa Aesar) with or without 0.5 mM benzaldehyde to the S basal during conditioning. The osmolarity added by the fructose can be calculated as approximately equal to the concentration of the fructose solution (as fructose has a one to one calculation for osmolarity, e.g. 40 mM fructose is calculated to be 40 mOsm). We did not directly measure osmolarity, but expect that the calculated upshift in osmolarity due to adding fructose is likely similar to the actual upshift in osmolarity [22]. To prevent food deprivation (in the “+ E. coli” group in Fig. 1, and all hyperosmolarity groups), E. coli in S. basal buffer was prepared by resuspending a concentrated pellet of E. coli (spun down from 7.5 ml of a confluent culture of OP50 E. coli in B broth) in 500 µl S basal buffer. The “+ E. coli” group in Fig. 1 and all hyperosmolarity groups received a combination of 500 µl of E. coli in S basal buffer plus 500 µl of S basal buffer with or without fructose and/or benzaldehyde (as indicated in figures). S basal buffer added alongside E. coli was prepared as twice the final concentration; e.g., for a final concentration of 250 mM fructose with benzaldehyde, the solution was made as 500 mM fructose and 1 mM benzaldehyde in S. Basal buffer.

Chemotaxis assay

Chemotaxis assays were adapted from Colbert and Bargmann [3]. Chemotaxis agar was prepared as 12 ml of 2.5% agar, 5 mM potassium phosphate (pH 6.0), 1 mM magnesium sulfate, and 1 mM calcium chloride in round petri dishes (100 × 15 mm). After conditioning, worms were washed three times with S basal buffer solution. The worms were pipetted onto the center of the plate, and excess liquid was removed with Kimwipes. 1 µl of 1 M sodium azide in dH2O was pipetted onto two marked spots, on opposite sides of and equidistant from the center of the plate. 1 µl of attractant (1 : 200 benzaldehyde : ethanol) was pipetted onto one spot of sodium azide, and 1 µl of diluent (ethanol) was pipetted onto the other spot. The worms moved freely on the plates for 90 min at room temperature. A chemotaxis index value (CI) was calculated at the end of the 90 min as (number of worms at the attractant - number of worms at the diluent) / total number of worms on the plate. All assays used 50–200 worms. Experimenters were not blinded to the experimental condition.

Statistical analysis

All data are represented as scatter dot plots, using median ± confidence interval. Statistical analysis used one-way or two-way ANOVA (analysis of variance). For multiple comparisons tests, we used Dunnett’s test in Figs. 1 and 2 (comparing each group vs. control), Sidak’s test for pre-selected pairs of groups in Fig. 3, and Bonferroni’s test for post hoc comparisons in Fig. 3. All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 8.

Results

Aversive conditioning of worms with benzaldehyde and food deprivation

We initially tested our conditioning protocol against a previously-validated associative learning assay, the food deprivation associative learning assay pioneered by the Bargmann lab [3, 26]. This assay uses benzaldehyde as the conditioned stimulus and food deprivation as the unconditioned stimulus. Benzaldehyde is an attractive olfactory cue for C. elegans, and previous research has demonstrated that when C. elegans were exposed to benzaldehyde while deprived of food, their attraction to this odor was reduced [4]. Consistent with previous studies [4], worms conditioned with benzaldehyde while food deprived demonstrated a reduced CI relative to naïve worms (Fig. 1, one-way ANOVA F(2, 40) = 40.05, p < 0.0001; Dunnett’s multiple comparison test, p < 0.0001). Next, we tested whether we could prevent this association by repeating this assay in the presence of E. coli. CI values of these worms were indistinguishable from those of naive worms (Fig. 1, Dunnett’s multiple comparison test, p = 0.96), suggesting that removing food deprivation with E. coli prevents associative learning.

Optimizing fructose concentrations

To determine the range of osmolarity (here, increased by adding fructose to the conditioning medium) that would not inhibit naïve chemotaxis, we examined the effect of fructose concentration on chemotaxis (Fig. 2, one-way ANOVA F(4,50) = 42.63, p < 0.0001). As a control, we tested worms that were fed with E. coli in conditioning medium, but had no added fructose. We found that the benzaldehyde CI of animals exposed to 40 mM, 90 mM, or 250 mM fructose in the presence of food were not significantly different from those of the control worms (Fig. 2, Dunnett’s post hoc test, respectively, p = 0.99, 0.99, 0.059). In contrast, however, worms conditioned with 500 mM fructose displayed a steep reduction in CI to benzaldehyde (Fig. 2, Dunnett’s post hoc test, p < 0.0001). Thus, the data show that 500 mM fructose added to conditioning medium negatively impacts worm chemotaxis after conditioning.

Conditioning of worms with benzaldehyde and hyperosmolarity

Having found that 40 mM, 90 mM, and 250 mM fructose concentrations did not compromise worm chemotaxis, these concentrations were used for conditioning. Well-fed adult worms were trained for 90 min in a solution containing both benzaldehyde and fructose in the presence of E. coli to create an association between benzaldehyde and hyperosmolarity (an aversive condition). The CI of these groups were compared to those of animals that were exposed to the same concentration of fructose without benzaldehyde. Importantly, animals conditioned with fructose and benzaldehyde did not differ significantly in CI from animals within the same fructose concentration that were not exposed to benzaldehyde (Fig. 3, two-way ANOVA, effect of interaction F(2,61) = 0.050, p = 0.95, effect of benzaldehyde, F(1,61) = 0.037, p = 0.85; Sidak’s multiple comparisons between benzaldehyde vs. no benzaldehyde at the same fructose concentration, p = 0.99, p = 0.99, p = 0.98, respectively). These results suggest that the animals in this particular assay failed to learn an association between hyperosmolarity in their environment and benzaldehyde.

Interestingly, in this dataset, we did find a difference due to fructose concentration (Fig. 3, two-way ANOVA, F(2,61) = 5.19, p = 0.0083). Post hoc comparisons using the conservative Bonferroni’s correction indicated this was due to a lower CI in the 250 mM fructose group than the 90 mM fructose group (Fig. 3, p = 0.0071). Notably, the difference between 250 mM fructose and control in Fig. 2 approached the bounds of statistical significance (p = 0.059). We suggest that 250 mM fructose added to S basal buffer is likely close to or at the threshold where hyperosmolarity begins to impair subsequent chemotaxis; further experimentation would be required to clarify the effects of 250 mM fructose.

Discussion

C. elegans failed to learn to associate hyperosmolarity with odor in this associative learning assay. Several lines of reasoning could account for this negative result. First, at low concentrations of fructose, it is plausible that the worm is not sensing hyperosmolarity - the concentrations may be below the sensory threshold. Worms have demonstrated avoidance behaviors and neuronal responses to an upshift of 200 mOsm [22]. However, in regards to our lower concentrations of osmolarity (equivalent to about 40 mOsm and 90 mOsm), neither behavioral responses nor neuronal responses have been shown in the literature. One possibility is that the worm is unable to sense osmolar upshifts at that level. Alternatively, it is also possible that worms may have been able to sense the osmolarity of the 40 mM and 90 mM fructose solutions, but they may not have been sufficiently aversive to alter the behavior of the worm. Microbehavioral tests at lower concentrations, similarly to those performed previously to test osmolar upshifts [22], may be useful to test whether worms demonstrate avoidant behaviors to mild osmolar upshifts.

The interpretation of the potential aversiveness of hyperosmolar fructose solutions is complicated by the specific effects of fructose. Naïve C. elegans avoid fructose at a 100 mM concentration [18] and fructose may have systemic effects, especially if ingested [28, 29]. It is possible that there may be something specific about the properties of fructose which could disrupt the ability of the worm to make an association that would reduce its attraction to benzaldehyde. Future experiments using an array of osmotic stressors (such as glycerol and NaCl) could validate whether the inefficacy of our protocol is due to properties of osmolarity in general or of fructose in particular. Additionally, direct measures of osmolarity using an osmometer may be helpful for future studies using complex solutions involving E. coli, as it is possible that our calculated osmolarity levels are somewhat different in actual osmolarity from the osmolarity used in other studies.

At the highest level we tested (500 mM fructose or about 500 mOsm), chemotaxis is impaired after the worms have been removed from the hyperosmotic environment. This is likely due to injury or damage the worms may sustain in this strongly hyperosmolar solution; e.g., control worms can show some deaths after 24 h in a 500 mOsm NaCl solution, and show a reduction in overall body size after just a few minutes in a 400 mOsm NaCl solution [24]. Because the impairment of chemotaxis in Fig. 2 is seen solely from exposure to the fructose solution, it is not feasible to use chemotaxis assays to test associative learning after conditioning for 90 min in 500 mM fructose in S basal buffer.

At lower concentrations of fructose, the failure to demonstrate reduced chemotaxis to benzaldehyde could also be explained by an excessive positive association with the presence of food. C. elegans can be trained to increase their attraction to a conditioned stimulus following exposure to the conditioned stimulus and food [7, 27, 30, 31]. Assays using food to increase chemotaxis to a conditioned stimulus generally must use more involved training protocols; e.g., they may condition the worms for longer periods, such as 4–12 h [18, 30] or food deprive the worms before the presentation of the conditioned stimulus with food [27, 31]. It is plausible that the presence of food may have mitigated the formation of an association between benzaldehyde and the hyperosmolar fructose solution. Future experiments could minimize the potential for a positive food association to confound the results by testing to confirm that their concentration of E. coli does not produce an increase in attraction to the odorant, and potentially using a lower concentration of E. coli in the conditioning solution.

Future experimentation could include modulating the assay to use spaced trials (shorter but repeated conditioning periods), which have been shown to enhance long term memory [31]. Additionally, C. elegans can demonstrate state dependent learning when exposed to ethanol during food deprivation associative learning, such that exposure to ethanol during recall is required in order for the worm to change its response to the odorant [32]. Future experiments could test the worm’s associative memory while it is being exposed to a hyperosmolar fructose solution. Alternatively, it is possible that worms have formed an association between benzaldehyde and hyperosmolarity, but are too injured to properly express it. Giving the worms a recovery period between conditioning and testing could allow for expression of a latent memory. With all these possibilities, the reduced chemotaxis we found in worms that had been exposed to 500 mM Fructose (Fig. 2) is a cautionary note for future experiments: due to the potentially injurious nature of hyperosmolarity, careful controls must be used to ensure any changes in behavior are not due to hyperosmolar injury alone.

Finally, it is possible that worms sense and will avoid hyperosmolarity at low concentrations of fructose (e.g., 90 mM or 250 mM fructose), but are still unable to form an aversive association between hyperosmolarity and benzaldehyde. An intriguing possibility is that worms simply lack the plasticity that would be specifically necessary to create a behavioral change to benzaldehyde due to association with hyperosmolarity. Notably, some research indicates that the aversive association between butanone (another attractive odorant) and food deprivation may occur via EGL-4 nuclear localization in the AWC olfactory neuron [33, 34] which requires insulin signals that are triggered by food deprivation [35]. It is possible that the mechanism for aversive food deprivation learning is specific to the aversive stimulus – e.g., a metabolic signal conveyed to AWC – and that no parallel mechanism exists for hyperosmolar associative memory. Further exploration of hyperosmolarity as an unconditioned stimulus will be required to determine whether or not C. elegans is capable of forming hyperosmolar associative memories.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results suggest that C. elegans is unable to learn to associate hyperosmolarity with benzaldehyde via a 90-minute associative learning assay. This study additionally provides a cautionary note that there may be potential confounds in worm behavior studies involving hyperosmotic solutions.

Limitations

While worms were unable to learn to reduce their chemotaxis to benzaldehyde via association with hyperosmolarity in our assay, it is possible that another experimental design (especially one with shorter conditioning times) could allow the worms to learn this association. Moreover, our results are specific to one-day-old adult hermaphrodites of C. elegans strain Bristol N2; other strains, ages, or sexes may behave differently under these conditions. Future experiments could also examine a variety of wild-caught C. elegans strains [36] or different developmental stages [17, 37] to examine potential variability in learning capacity. Future experiments could also test with other odorants, such as butanone or diacetyl [2]. Our study only examined one simple behavioral output (chemotaxis); examining additional behavioral outputs, such as avoidant microbehaviors [22], could be a useful addition to future studies.
