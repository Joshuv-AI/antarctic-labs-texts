Optimization of protein UFMylation modification method and its application in substrate identification in human cells
Authors: Yaoyao Fang, Xue Wang, Juexi Dong, Xingling Yi, Zhaopeng Lv, Chen Bu, Xianliang Ma, Yu-Sheng Cong, Qian Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUFMylation plays an essential role in regulating intracellular physiological and pathological processes. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that dysregulation of UFMylation is closely associated with the progression of various diseases, including cancers and developmental disorders. However, efficient and specific methods for detecting UFMylated substrate proteins remain challenging. In this study, we generated the UFM1–specific protease 1/2 knockout (UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO) human embryonic kidney 293T cell line and validated it as the most suitable cellular model for the screening, identification, and confirmation of UFMylated substrate proteins. Furthermore, exogenous expression of the E3 ligase components, UFM1–specific ligase 1 (UFL1) and DDRGK domain–containing protein 1 (DDRGK1), significantly enhanced protein UFMylation levels, enabling the large-scale enrichment of UFMylated substrates. Collectively, we have established a more efficient, specific, and reliable method for UFMylation substrate research, facilitating in-depth investigation into its biological functions and regulatory mechanisms.Keywords: UFMylation, UFL1, DDRGK1, UFSP, substrate

Post-translational modifications mediated by ubiquitin and ubiquitin-like (UBL) proteins play a pivotal role in regulating most intracellular processes, including protein folding, degradation, subcellular localization, and signal transduction (1). Among the UBL family, UFMylation is an evolutionarily conserved modification system centered on ubiquitin-fold modifier 1 (UFM1) and has emerged as a critical regulator of cellular homeostasis (2). Analogous to ubiquitination, UFMylation operates through a sequential E1–E2–E3 enzymatic cascade: UFM1-activating enzyme 5 (UBA5), the sole E1-activating enzyme, initiates UFM1 activation; UFM1-conjugating enzyme 1 (UFC1), the unique E2-conjugating enzyme, facilitates the transfer of activated UFM1; and the UFL1–DDRGK1–CDK5RAP3 (CDK5 regulatory subunit-associated protein 3) ternary complex serves as the core E3 ligase responsible for the covalent conjugation of UFM1 to lysine (Lys, K) residues on substrate proteins (3, 4). Both the maturation of UFM1 from its precursor form (pro-UFM1) and the removal of UFM1 conjugates (de-UFMylation) are catalyzed by UFM1-specific proteases (UFSPs), a family of cysteine proteases (5, 6, 7).

Dysregulation of the UFMylation process has been associated with various diseases, including cancers and developmental disorders (e.g., hematopoietic defects, skeletal dysplasia, and early-onset encephalopathy) (8, 9, 10). Despite increasing recognition of UFMylation's involvement in critical biological processes, such as ribosomal quality control (11), the endoplasmic reticulum stress response (12), and tumor suppression (13), progress in elucidating its functional mechanisms has been significantly hindered by a major bottleneck, that is, the limited number of identified UFMylated substrates (4, 14). To date, fewer than 30 UFMylation substrates have been reported (15), a number substantially lower than those characterized in other well-established UBL modification systems, such as ubiquitination or SUMOylation. This scarcity arises from two primary technical challenges. First, the ribosomal protein L26 (RPL26) is the predominant UFMylation substrate in cells (16), and RPL26–UFM1 conjugates account for the majority of intracellular UFM1, thereby obscuring the detection of low-abundance substrates when anti-UFM1 antibodies are employed. Second, conventional methods for detecting UFMylation, which often require cotransfection of multiple UFMylation components (17), are cumbersome, prone to variability, and lack specificity in enriching UFMylated peptides. As a result, the biological functions of UFMylation and its underlying regulatory networks remain poorly understood, and no computational tools currently exist for predicting UFMylation sites, largely because of the insufficient availability of experimentally verified modification sites.

To address these limitations, we developed a systematic strategy to improve UFMylation detection and enable large-scale identification of substrates. In this study, we generated UFSP2 single-KO (UFSP2KO) and UFSP1/UFSP2 double-KO (UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO, DKO) human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cell lines, capitalizing on the role of UFSPs in UFM1 recycling to increase substrate modification levels. We subsequently optimized the transfection system through single-factor experiments, which revealed that exogenous expression of the E3 ligase components, UFL1 and DDRGK1, rather than the E1 enzyme UBA5 or the E2 enzyme UFC1, is critical for maximizing UFMylation efficiency. Most importantly, by integrating this optimized cellular system with a newly developed antibody specifically recognizing the UFM1 remnant motif (K-ε-Gly-Val [GV]), we achieved robust enrichment of UFMylated peptides and identified more than 600 UFMylation substrates using LC–MS/MS. This represents a substantial advancement over previously reported substrate numbers (18). Furthermore, we validated novel substrates, analyzed amino acid preferences surrounding UFMylation sites, and performed subcellular localization and functional annotation of the identified proteins.

This work not only establishes a reliable and highly efficient platform for UFMylation research but also provides a foundational dataset for investigating the physiological functions of this modification. By overcoming technical barriers in substrate identification, our study facilitates the elucidation of UFMylation-mediated regulatory networks and their implications in human health and disease.

Results

Construction of UFM1-specific protease KO cell lines

Early studies proposed that UFSP2 was the sole functional protease responsible for both pro-UFM1 maturation and de-UFMylation in human cells (19). However, we and other groups independently demonstrated that UFSP1 also possesses robust protease activity and participates in both pro-UFM1 processing and the removal of UFM1 from conjugated substrates (6, 7). The initiation of protein UFMylation necessitates the maturation of pro-UFM1 by UFM1-specific proteases, UFSP1 and UFSP2. Therefore, we sought to generate a cell line deficient in UFM1-specific proteases, thereby totally abrogating UFMylation and de-UFMylation. When the mature form of UFM1 with an exposed carboxy-terminal glycine (Gly83) residue (UFM1ΔC2) was introduced exogenously, the UFMylation process was restored, but the de-UFMylation process remained defective. We reason that this strategy could potentially establish a more effective system for investigating the UFMylation of substrate proteins in cells. We initially disrupted the UFSP2 gene using the CRISPR–Cas9-mediated gene editing in HEK293T cells. Using the Western blotting assay, our results revealed a marked increase in protein UFMylation levels in the UFSP2KO cell line, with dominant bands of UFM1-conjugated RPL26 detected (Fig. 1A), which is consistent with previous research (16, 20). The UFSP2KO HEK293T cell line was subsequently used for UFSP1 gene KO to produce the UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cell line. Experimental results showed that the UFMylation of substrate proteins was entirely abolished, with the accumulation of unconjugated UFM1 (Fig. 1A). Moreover, we confirmed the presence of frameshift mutations in both UFSP1 and UFSP2 genes by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1B). In addition, no significant cellular stress was observed in UFSP2KO and UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells (Fig. S1).

Improved detection of protein UFMylation in the UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO DKO cell line

Using the KO cell lines, we sought to explore the UFMylation status of substrate proteins with our strategy. We adapted previously reported protein modification methods and established an optimized UFMylation assay protocol for substrate proteins in human cells (Fig. 2A). Three reported UFMylation substrates, ASC1 (activating signal cointegrator 1), RPL26, and the tumor suppressor protein p53, were selected for further experimental verification (11, 13, 21). Using this protocol, we detected the UFMylation of ASC1, RPL26, and p53 in UFSP2KO, UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO, and WT HEK293T cells, respectively (Fig. 2, B–D). Because UFSP2 null leads to a drastic accumulation of UFMylated proteins, we observed higher UFMylation levels of the three exogenously transfected substrates in UFSP2KO cells than in WT cells (Fig. 2, B–D, lane 6). Notably, there were even better signals of UFMylation status of these substrates in UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells, including stronger UFM1-conjugated bands and more observable poly-UFM1 conjugates, compared with the results in UFSP2KO cells (Fig. 2, B–D, lane 9). In addition, we observed that the protein level of exogenously expressed FLAG-tagged substrates in UFSP2KO cells was lower than those in the other two groups (Fig. 2, B–D, lanes 5 and 6). We attribute this reduction to the chronic high-level UFMylation state in UFSP2KO cells. In contrast, there was no significant difference in exogenously FLAG-tagged substrate expression between UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO and WT cells (Fig. 2, B–D, lanes 2 and 3, and 8 and 9). These results confirmed that our strategy is feasible, and UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells are more suitable for analyzing protein UFMylation. Higher UFMylation levels can be achieved by cotransfecting UFM1ΔC2 and substrate constructs into UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells deficient in UFM1-specific proteases.

Exogenous expression of E3 ligase complex components, UFL1 and DDRGK1, facilitates protein UFMylation

Previously reported protein modification methods for UFMylation tend to involve cotransfecting a substrate construct with multiple UFMylation components (17). To identify the optimal combination of UFMylation components required for substrate UFMylation assays in UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells, we employed single-factor experiments (Fig. 3, A and B). In the DKO cells, endogenous UFMylation was completely blocked when only the empty vector was transfected (Fig. 3, A and B, lane 1), whereas it was rescued upon transfection with mature UFM1ΔC2 (Fig. 3, A and B, lane 2). Based on this observation, we established a cotransfection group comprising all key UFMylation components (UBA5, UFC1, UFL1, and DDRGK1) as a positive control (Fig. 3, A and B, lane 3). Our results demonstrate that the presence or absence of UBA5 or UFC1 in the transfection mixture exerts almost no effect on intracellular UFMylation levels (Fig. 3, A and B, lanes 4 and 5). In contrast, exogenous expression of UFL1 and DDRGK1 plays a significant role in regulating intracellular UFMylation levels (Fig. 3, A and B, lanes 6 and 7).

To further elucidate the roles of E3 ligase complex components, UFL1 and DDRGK1, in substrate modification in UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells, we performed UFMylation assays on ASC1 using single-factor experiments and immunoprecipitation (Fig. 3, C and D). The results demonstrated that the absence of UFL1 or DDRGK1 led to a marked decrease in the UFMylation level of ASC1 (Fig. 3C, lanes 7 and 8), whereas the presence of UFL1 and DDRGK1 was sufficient to support considerable levels of UFM1-conjugated ASC1 (Fig. 3D, lane 9). Meanwhile, we utilized RPL26, the major intracellular UFMylation substrate, to replicate these single-factor experiments and obtained consistent conclusions (Fig. S2, A and B). Collectively, our experiments demonstrate that E3 ligase components, UFL1 and DDRGK1, are essential for enhancing intracellular UFMylation levels, and their exogenous coexpression further promotes substrate UFMylation in UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells.

Identification and validation of UFMylation substrates in HEK293T cells

To date, only a limited number of UFMylated substrates have been identified, with fewer than 30 reported. This scarcity has constrained our understanding of UFMylation’s biological function. Moreover, we found that exogenously expressed UFL1, DDRGK1, and UFM1ΔC2 exhibited markedly elevated UFMylation in UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells (DKO-ΔC2) (Fig. 4A). To achieve large-scale identification of intracellular UFMylation substrates, we developed a high-efficiency approach that utilizes a K-ε-GV antibody to efficiently enrich UFM1-modified peptides (Figs. 4B and S3) and ultimately employed LC–MS/MS to specifically identify UFMylated sites in the DKO-ΔC2 sample. UFMylation induces a specific mass shift of +156.09 Da at the target Lys (K) residue, resulting from the covalent attachment of a GV dipeptide remnant derived from the proteolytic cleavage of the C terminus of UFM1. Surprisingly, we identified more than 600 substrates (Dataset S1), a number far greater than those previously reported. The overlapping substrates included core UFMylation components and several previously reported ones (Fig. 4C). Moreover, the conjugated sites were consistent with previous findings, further validating the robustness of our data (11, 16, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25). Subsequently, we selected five representative proteins (heat shock protein 90-alpha [HSP90α], YEATS2 [YEATS domain–containing protein 2], ANXA5 [annexin A5], ANXA6 [annexin A6], and PRMT5 [protein arginine N-methyltransferase 5]) from the list for further validation and found that all candidates could be conjugated by UFM1 (Fig. 4, D–H), and their modification was deconjugated by UFSP2 (Fig. 4, D–H, lane 4). Moreover, results from the endogenous modification assay further confirmed that these five candidates are indeed UFMylation substrates (Fig. S4). In brief, these results demonstrate the successful development of a reliable strategy for large-scale identification of intracellular UFMylated substrates.

Currently, site prediction tools are available for post-translational modifications, such as ubiquitination and SUMOylation (26, 27), but no such tool is available for UFMylation sites. This limitation is attributed to the small number of known UFMylation substrates. Using our LC–MS/MS data, we sought to analyze the modification motif of specific lysine residues by examining peptides containing the UFM1-remnant K-ε-GV sequence with a motif-based sequence analysis tool (28). Our analysis revealed that glycine and tyrosine (Tyr, Y) residues at positions immediately adjacent (one residue upstream or downstream) to the lysine residue targeted for UFM1 conjugation are more highly conserved than lysine and arginine (Arg, R) residues (Fig. 4I and Dataset S2). In addition, we investigated the subcellular localization of these substrates, and the results revealed that UFMylation substrates are predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and plasma membrane (Fig. S5A and Dataset S3). Functional annotation analyses indicated that UFMylation substrates are primarily involved in multiple cellular pathways, including translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis, intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport (Fig. S5B and Dataset S4). Collectively, these data establish a foundation for the development of UFMylation site prediction tools. Furthermore, they provide critical insights for future studies investigating the biological functions of UFMylation.

Discussion

As a UBL mediated by the UFM1-specific E1–E2–E3 enzymatic cascade, UFMylation plays a crucial role in regulating protein function by modulating protein localization, stability, and interactions with other proteins, and this regulatory effect further supports the precise regulation of multiple cellular processes (4, 14, 19). However, current reports indicate that far fewer UFMylated substrate proteins have been identified compared with those of other prevalent UBLs. The fundamental bottleneck stems from limitations in substrate identification techniques, as existing technologies struggle to efficiently and specifically bind to and identify UFMylated substrates, thereby impeding the discovery of novel UFMylation substrates. Developing highly efficient methods for UFMylation substrate identification has thus become the key to overcoming this research bottleneck. This study aims to develop a high-efficiency modification detection strategy and optimize the associated experimental processes, and by using the K-ε-GV peptide-specific antibody in the optimization process, this approach ultimately provides the optimal solution to date.

Based on the distinct properties of the two UFM1-specific proteases (6, 7), we established two cell lines, UFSP2KO and UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO in HEK293T, which exhibit the two extremes of intracellular UFMylation levels. We further confirmed that the UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cell line exhibits an optimal UFMylation status. This DKO cell line did not exhibit the reduction in substrate expression observed in UFSP2KO cells after exogenous transfection; moreover, it not only significantly enhanced UFMylation levels but also exhibited more abundant poly-UFMylation–modified substrates. Subsequently, we optimized the transfected construct mixture to further improve the overall UFMylation level. Previously reported protein UFMylation methods mainly involve cotransfecting the substrate construct with multiple modification components, including UBA5, UFC1, UFL1, DDRGK1, and UFM1. These approaches are operationally cumbersome and involve several variables, leading to unstable experimental outcomes. In our study, the omission of UBA5 or UFC1 from the transfection mixture did not significantly affect intracellular UFMylation levels, with no evidence indicating a requirement for their exogenous supplementation. In contrast, the absence of UFL1 or DDRGK1 resulted in a marked reduction in substrate UFMylation, demonstrating their dependence on exogenous expression to achieve robust modification. These findings underscore that UFL1 and DDRGK1 serve as critical regulatory components in the intracellular UFMylation cascade; under UFSP-deficient conditions, exogenous transfection of these two factors is indispensable for efficiently driving UFMylation. Based on the above findings, the optimized experimental conditions, which further enhances the efficiency of substrate modification and the stability of experimental results, provide a more reliable experimental approach for subsequent mechanistic studies and functional validation of UFMylation.

Taking advantage of the K-ε-GV peptide–specific antibody, we successfully enriched a substantial number of UFMylated substrates. When combined with LC–MS/MS, this approach further enabled large-scale enrichment and identification of UFMylated substrates. Our identified substrate list included nearly all previously reported UFMylated substrates (11, 16, 21, 22, 23). Furthermore, we selected five unreported substrates from the list for UFMylation and de-UFMylation assays. These results collectively confirm that our UFMylation substrate list is reliable and that the K-ε-GV antibody is highly specific. Meanwhile, leveraging the identified substrate list, we performed bioinformatic analyses to infer potential UFM1-modified motifs and regulatory signaling pathways. Through motif analysis of sequences adjacent to UFMylated sites, we identified amino acid preferences around UFMylated lysine residues, providing sequence-level information for predicting potential UFMylated sites. In addition, through subcellular localization and functional enrichment analyses of UFMylated substrates, we clarified that these substrates are predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and plasma membrane and are involved in biological processes (e.g., translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis, intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport), laying a theoretical basis for elucidating the physiological functions of UFMylation. Integrating the regulatory effects of UFMylation on its substrates with their roles in cellular pathways expands the scope of UFMylation functional studies and refines the understanding of its functional regulatory networks.

Our study provides valuable insights into the methodology for identifying UFMylation substrates. Future expansion of this method’s application in specific research areas will facilitate the screening of UFMylation regulatory factors, thereby offering robust technical support for in-depth investigations into the physiological functions of UFMylation and its molecular mechanisms in disease pathogenesis.

Limitations of the study

While a large-scale enrichment and identification of UFMylated substrate assay performance has been done, several limitations remain. First, the UFMylated substrates identified in this study only reflect the protein expression profile and UFMylation status unique to the HEK293T cell line and thus may not fully capture the UFMylation landscape in organisms. Notably, our established methodology can be extended to cell models that are more representative of specific biological or pathological contexts, and this would facilitate the identification of UFMylated substrate candidates with greater relevance to targeted biological processes or disease conditions. Second, the LC–MS/MS-based identification strategy shows relatively low sensitivity to low-abundance UFMylated substrates; this may have resulted in the omission of some functionally critical substrates with weak modification signals. Third, our substrate identification relies on overexpressing UFMylation components in the DKO background to enrich modification signals; this is a necessary approach to overcome low endogenous-modified substrate abundance but raises theoretical concerns about altered UFMylation component stoichiometry. While we addressed this via complementary endogenous validation for key hits and validating pathway-dependent specificity, future studies using more physiological strategies will further confirm the identified substrates’ physiological relevance.

Experimental procedures

Constructs, single-guide RNAs, antibodies, and chemicals

Human UBA5 (E1), UFC1 (E2), UFL1 (E3), DDRGK1, UFSP2, and UFM1ΔC2 complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were subcloned into the pSG5-HA vector. UFM1ΔC2 refers to a mature form of UFM1 with an exposed glycine 83 (Gly83) residue after removal of its carboxy (C)-terminal two amino acids. Human ASC1, p53, RPL26, HSP90α, YEATS2, ANXA5, and ANXA6 cDNAs were subcloned into the pcDNA3-FLAG vector. All expression constructs were generated using Q5 High-Fidelity 2 × Master Mix (New England Biolabs). The MYC-PRMT5 construct was kindly provided by Zhongwei Li (Wannan Medical College). UFSP1 and UFSP2 fragments from UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells, used for Sanger sequencing, were amplified via high-fidelity PCR and ligated into a blunt-end vector using the pEASY-Blunt Cloning Kit (CB111; TransGen Biotech).

Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the human UFSP1 and UFSP2 genes were synthesized (Tsingke Biotechnology) and subcloned into the BsmBI restriction sites of the lentiCRISPR vector (Addgene).

Primary antibodies against UFM1 (Abcam), UFSP2 (Abcam), UFSP1 (Sigma–Aldrich), DDRGK1 (Sigma–Aldrich), HSP90α (ABclonal), YEATS2 (ABclonal), ANXA5 (ABclonal), ANXA6 (ABclonal), PRMT5 (ABclonal), FLAG tag (Sigma–Aldrich), HA tag (Cell Signaling Technology), MYC tag (Proteintech), and GAPDH (ABclonal) were used. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies (Sigma–Aldrich) were used for detection.

Puromycin, MG132, and polybrene were purchased from Selleck Chemicals. Detailed information on constructs, PCR primers, sgRNA oligos, antibodies, enzymes, chemicals, and kits is provided in the supporting Table S1.

Cell culture and transfection

Human HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (01-055-1A; Biological Industries), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FSP500; ExCell Bio) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140-122; Gibco). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfection of constructs was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Generation of CRISPR–Cas9-mediated KO cell lines

Lentiviral particles were produced by cotransfecting the lentiCRISPR-sgRNA plasmid with packaging vectors (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) into HEK293T cells. After transfection, cells were cultured for 48 to 72 h; the supernatant was then collected, filtered through a sterile 0.45 μm filter, and concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit (UFC910024; Millipore).

UFSP2 KO (UFSP2KO) HEK293T cells (a single-gene UFSP2 KO cell line) were generated via lentiviral infection. For infection, concentrated UFSP2-sgRNA lentivirus was mixed with fresh medium and polybrene (final concentration: 8 μg/ml) to prepare the infection mixture, which was then added to target HEK293T cells. After infection, cells were subjected to continuous selection with puromycin (1 μg/ml) for 1 week. Resistant clones were isolated, and UFSP2 KO efficiency was verified via Western blotting.

To generate DKO UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells, the previously validated UFSP2KO cell line was subjected to a second round of lentiviral infection with the UFSP1-sgRNA construct. At 48 h post-infection, cells were trypsinized and seeded as single cells into 96-well plates to obtain single-cell–derived clones. These clones were screened via Western blotting for UFSP1 protein expression to confirm efficient UFSP1 KO.

Cell lysis and Western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.1% SDS) supplemented with freshly added 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). Total protein concentration was quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After adding 5× SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (P0015; Beyotime Biotechnology), the samples were boiled at 100 °C for 8 min to denature proteins.

Protein lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (ISEQ00010; Millipore), blocked in 5% skim milk prepared in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), probed with primary antibodies (diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin/TBST, according to antibody instructions) overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted in 5% skim milk/TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Western blotting signals were visualized using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (WBKLS0500; Millipore) with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

For membrane stripping and reprobing to detect other proteins on the same membrane, the used membrane was incubated in stripping buffer (PS107S; Epizyme Biotech) to remove bound primary and secondary antibodies. After stripping, the membrane was washed with TBST and reprocessed through the blocking, antibody incubation, and visualization steps described above.

Immunoprecipitation for UFMylation and de-UFMylation assay in cells

Cells used for substrate UFMylation and de-UFMylation assays were seeded in 10 cm dishes and transfected with the indicated constructs. Following transfection, cells were cultured for 36 h and then treated with MG132 for 6 h prior to harvest to inhibit proteasomal degradation.

Subsequently, cells in each dish were washed three times with prechilled PBS on ice and lysed in 400 μl of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.4], 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1% SDS, 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail), followed by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min to denature proteins. Lysates were then sonicated using a noncontact ultrasonic processor (Scientz Biotechnology) for 10 min at 80% maximum power. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000g at room temperature for 10 min. After centrifugation, 10% of the supernatant was collected as input, mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer, and boiled for 8 min for later analysis. The remaining 90% of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube and subjected to immunoprecipitation. For the UFMylation/de-UFMylation assay of exogenously expressed substrates, we used Anti-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma–Aldrich) or Anti-MYC Affinity Gel (Beyotime Biotechnology) with rotation at 4 °C overnight. For the UFMylation assay of endogenous substrates, we used the indicated antibodies plus protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) with rotation at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the affinity gel was washed three times with buffer A (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 2 mM N-ethylmaleimide, and 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail), with each wash involving rotation at 4 °C for 8 min. After discarding the final wash supernatant, 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added to the gel, and samples were boiled at 100 °C for 8 min. Immunoprecipitates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting.

Large-scale identification of intracellular UFMylation substrates

Protein extraction and digestion

UFM1ΔC2-transfected UFSP1KO/UFSP2KO cells were suspended in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 1% NP40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 5 mM DTT, 2 mM EDTA, 30 mM nicotinamide, 3 μM trichostatin A, and 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) for protein extraction (DKO-ΔC2 sample). Following sonication on ice, unbroken cells and debris were removed by centrifugation at 20,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit. A small aliquot of protein was taken from the sample and subjected to Western blotting for detection.

Subsequently, cell lysate samples were reduced with 5 mM DTT at 37 °C for 45 min, alkylated with 30 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature in the dark for 45 min, and then precipitated with prechilled acetone. The pellets were washed three times with acetone, resuspended in 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate, and digested with 1/25 trypsin (Promega) at 37 °C for 12 h. Digestion was terminated with 0.1% TFA. The resulting peptides were cleaned with a C18 SPE column (8B-S100-AAK; Phenomenex) and dried in a vacuum concentrator (SPD111V-230; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Affinity enrichment

To enrich modified peptides, tryptic peptides were dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) and subsequently incubated with the UFMylation Remnant Motif (K-ε-GV) Kit (WM302; Micrometer Biotech) for 4 h with gentle shaking at 4 °C. The beads were washed four times with NETN buffer and twice with ddH2O. Bound peptides were eluted from the beads using 0.1% TFA, followed by vacuum evaporation.

LC–MS/MS analysis

For LC–MS/MS analysis, the resulting peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips (ZTC18S096; Merck Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After adding indexed retention time peptides, samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS on an Orbitrap Astral Mass Spectrometer coupled to a nanoflow Vanquish Neo HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition mode. The analysis was performed in positive ion mode, with a full mass spectrometry 1 (MS1) scan range of 380 to 980 m/z. MS1 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 240,000 (at 200 m/z) with a normalized automatic gain control target of 500% and a maximum injection time of 5 ms. For data-independent acquisition of MS2 spectra, a method with 300 variable windows and an isolation width of 2 m/z was employed. MS2 scans were conducted using higher-energy collisional dissociation with a collision energy of 25 eV, a normalized automatic gain control target of 300%, and a maximum injection time of 3 ms. LC–MS/MS experiments were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co, Ltd. A list of candidate protein peptides is provided in the supporting information.

Database search

For data processing, the resulting LC–MS/MS data were analyzed with Spectronaut software, and tandem mass spectra were searched against the UniProt-Human database. The software parameters were set as follows: enzyme was set to trypsin, maximum missed cleavages were set to 2, fixed modification was Carbamidomethyl (C), dynamic modifications included Oxidation (M), Acetyl (Protein N-term), and UFMylation (K). The false discovery rate was set to <1%.

Bioinformatics analysis

The visualization analyses in this study, including the generation of heatmaps and subcellular localization statistics, were performed using the ggplot2 package in R. Functional annotations of proteins, including EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), were retrieved from the UniProt database.

Data reproducibility

The ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad) was used to collect the Western blot images, and Image Lab 6.0 software (Bio-Rad) and PowerPoint (Microsoft) were used for image processing. All experiments were independently repeated three times with consistent results.
