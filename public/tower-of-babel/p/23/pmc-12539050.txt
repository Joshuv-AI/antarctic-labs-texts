Enhancing academic engagement through online team-based learning: a longitudinal study in nursing education
Authors: Eun Jung Kim, Hae Ran Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid advancement of technology and expansion of online learning environments require innovative educational methods tailored to digital contexts. Team-based learning (TBL) has shown promise in promoting engagement and collaborative problem solving; however, its long-term effectiveness in relation to online platforms, especially in emergency nursing education, remains underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of online TBL on academic self-efficacy, learning immersion, and learning interest among nursing students.MethodsA total of 91 nursing students were randomly assigned to either an experimental group (n = 46), which participated in TBL via an online collaboration platform, or a control group (n = 45), which used traditional paper-based TBL. Data were collected at three time points—baseline (pre-test), four weeks, and eight weeks post-intervention—using validated structured questionnaires. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze changes over time.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between the groups at baseline and after four weeks. However, at eight weeks, the experimental group showed significantly higher scores for academic self-efficacy (t = 2.09, p = 0.039), learning immersion (t = 2.30, p = 0.024), and learning interest (t = 2.85, p = 0.006) than the control group. These findings indicate that the benefits of digital TBL emerge over time.ConclusionsOnline TBL effectively enhances the academic self-efficacy, engagement, and motivation of nursing students. The use of digital platforms enables real-time collaboration regardless of geographic location. Future strategies should incorporate peer interaction and scaffolded tasks to promote active learning in healthcare education.Keywords: Online, Team-based learning, Self-efficacy, Learning immersion, Learning interest

Introduction

In the era of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, the development of various technologies has expanded the learning environment. Since 2020, the spread of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) has made face-to-face education impossible. Consequently, the transition to online classes that utilize real-time video technology and learning management systems for remote learning has accelerated. However, several issues have arisen due to insufficient understanding and lack of preparation. In particular, instructors have faced challenges in adapting to online educational platforms, operating digital devices, and communicating with learners. Many instructors have struggled to apply the teaching methods used in face-to-face classes to online instruction. Students have also faced challenges in adapting to the sudden and unplanned shift to online learning [1]. Although university students born in the 2000s were familiar with digital devices and Internet surfing, they reported difficulties in maintaining focus and managing increased stress during online learning, which led to challenges in active self-directed learning. Consequently, their learning performance and class satisfaction decreased [2]. This indicates the need for teaching methods that can enhance student engagement and concentration in online classes [3].

Team-based learning (TBL) is a student-centered instructional method that emphasizes collaborative group work and peer interaction [4]. TBL is effective in increasing student autonomy, class engagement, and learning outcomes. Effective collaboration among diverse experts is essential in today’s complex and interdisciplinary professional landscape [5, 6]. Reflecting this need, medical and nursing education is increasingly incorporating TBL to foster teamwork and communication skills [7]. The interactive structure of TBL not only promotes collaborative learning but also strengthens academic self-efficacy—students’ belief in their ability to succeed in academic tasks [8, 9]. Higher academic self-efficacy leads to the adoption of more effective learning strategies and improves academic achievement. Among the psychological constructs associated with learning performance, learning engagement plays a critical role because it reflects a learner’s emotional and behavioral involvement in academic tasks [10, 11]. Research has indicated that technology-supported learning activities, such as those involving smart devices or augmented reality, can positively influence learning immersion and interest [11, 12]. These immersive, interactive environments help students engage more deeply in the learning process. Consequently, active learning methods that increase student participation, such as TBL, can significantly improve engagement, interest, and academic self-efficacy [13].

In particular, the adaptability of TBL to digital platforms has made it a valuable strategy for fostering collaboration and engagement, even in online and hybrid learning environments. This highlights the importance of assessing outcomes such as self-efficacy, engagement, and interest when evaluating the effectiveness of online TBL.

However, the abrupt transition from in-person to online TBL during the COVID-19 pandemic introduced several challenges. While online TBL offers benefits such as monitoring individual student engagement and delivering differentiated learning content and assessments, it also has significant limitations. Connectivity issues stemming from students’ unequal access to a stable Internet environment disrupted participation. Additionally, some students lacked active involvement in discussions and team assignments [14]. Online learning often lacks the sense of community that is naturally present in face-to-face settings [15]. This can lead to issues such as free riding, with certain team members contributing less, which diminishes motivation and disrupts group learning dynamics. Moreover, dividing students into isolated teams without intergroup interaction may hinder collaborative learning. Unlike in in-person TBL, instructors are unable to simultaneously monitor all teams online, which limits opportunities for immediate feedback and intervention [16]. To preserve the collaborative nature of TBL in online environments, it is essential to build learning communities that are supported by cooperative social networks. Facilitating diverse forms of student interaction and communication must be an intentional part of instructional design [17].

Therefore, the success of online TBL depends on having adequate Internet infrastructure, platforms that support collaboration tools, and systems that allow for real-time student monitoring and feedback. Tools that enable instructors to provide simultaneous feedback to multiple groups can enhance student motivation and engagement. TBL conducted via online platforms has the potential to overcome these limitations by enabling real-time communication and seamless collaboration [18].

However, empirical evidence regarding the effectiveness of TBL using such online collaborative platforms is still lacking. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the educational effects of TBL implemented through an online collaboration system applicable to both face-to-face and remote learning environments.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a quasi-experimental design and was conducted between August and December 2022. A convenience sample of nursing students at a South Korean university was selected. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) students who used digital devices that could support online platforms, (2) students who had no difficulties engaging in team learning, and (3) students who voluntarily signed up to participate in the study. Students who had mild hearing impairments or exhibited observable difficulties in communication or in responding in a timely manner during class were excluded, as such challenges may hinder effective participation in team-based learning.

Participants were recruited in August 2022. The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1. The sample size for repeated-measures ANOVA was calculated using the G*Power program. A medium correlation effect size of 0.1 was used for the correlation among the time points. With an effect size (f) of 0.25 (medium), a significance level (α) of 0.05, power (1-β) of 0.80, two groups, and three measurement time points, and assuming a correlation of 0.3 between repeated measures, the required sample size was calculated to be 38 participants per group. Ninety participants were initially targeted, with a potential dropout rate of 20%. Ultimately, 94 participants were recruited, 91 of whom completed the study and were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Academic self-efficacy

The Academic Self-Efficacy Scale developed and validated by Kim and Park [8] and revised and validated by Cho and Im [9] was used to measure academic self-efficacy. It consists of three subscales: confidence (reflects belief or assurance in one’s learning abilities [eight items]), task difficulty preference (measures the extent to which learners choose specific and challenging goals in academic situations [10 items]), and self-regulation efficacy (assesses individuals’ judgment regarding how well they can perform self-regulatory mechanisms such as self-observation, self-judgment, and self-reaction [10 items]). The scale consists of 28 items. In Cho and Im’s study, the reliability of the instrument was reported with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89; in the present study, it was 0.92.

Learning immersion

The flow state scale developed and validated by Bae [10] was used to measure learning engagement. It consists of 10 items rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Higher scores indicate a higher level of learning engagement among students. In Bae’s study, the reliability of the instrument was reported with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79; in the present study, it was 0.81.

Learning interest

Learning interest was measured using the nine-item SMILES academic interest scale developed by the Brain Motivation Research Institute at Korea University [19]. It assesses the psychological state, including the cognitive and affective aspects, that learners experience when engaging in specific tasks or subjects within an academic context. Moreover, it focuses on the interest generated in a specific learning situation rather than measuring general interest levels. This scale comprises two subscales: situational and personal interest. It consists of nine items, with items 1–4 assessing situational interest and items 5–9 assessing personal interest. The items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Higher scores indicate a higher level of interest in learning among students. In the study by the Brain Motivation Research Institute at Korea University, the reliability of the instrument was reported with a Cronbach’s alpha of. 94; in the present study, it was 0.95.

Research procedure

This study was conducted over a period of eight weeks, during which fourth-year nursing students enrolled in an emergency nursing course participated in a structured TBL intervention. Prior to each class session, lecture materials and video content were uploaded to an online learning platform, allowing students to complete self-directed pre-class learning. This preparatory step aimed to ensure that the students came to class ready to engage in deeper application-based learning activities. At the beginning of each class session, students completed a five-minute Individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT) to assess their personal understanding of the pre-assigned materials. This was immediately followed by a five-minute Group Readiness Assurance Test (gRAT), during which team members collaborated to answer the same questions, promoting discussion and peer teaching. Following the readiness assurance phase, the instructor provided a 20-minute minilecture that reinforced key learning points, clarified difficult concepts, and aligned the students’ focus with the session’s learning objectives. After the lecture, students were presented with a team-based application task related to the emergency nursing content covered that week. Each team worked collaboratively for 40 min to analyze the scenario, solve the presented problems, and reach a consensus through discussion and critical thinking.

TBL using an online collaboration platform: experimental group

In the experimental group, team interactions were facilitated using the online collaborative platform ALLO (https://allo.io/ko). ALLO can be used on PCs, tablet PCs, and mobile devices connected to the Internet. Team members can access the platform simultaneously and share individual team-learning task content in real time. This enables the collaborative content to be downloaded and shared in PDF format. Moreover, it supports real-time chat and video conferencing, making the exchange of opinions convenient. Professors can monitor the collaboration status of learners in team-learning tasks in real time, and learners’ recorded and modified project content facilitates the monitoring of individual team members’ activities. In this study, after a mini-lecture, team assignments were presented, and team members collaborated by uploading team learning outcomes using ALLO.

TBL using paper task: control group

Paper collaboration was used to facilitate team learning in the control group. Similar to the experimental group, team tasks were presented, and team members collaborated by collectively writing and documenting their results on paper using various writing tools.

Data collection

Baseline data (pretest) were collected in August 2022. The TBL, using an online collaboration platform intervention, was conducted from September to December 2022. Post-test 1 was administered to students in October 2022, one month after the pretest. Post-test 2 was conducted in November 2022, two months after the intervention.

Data analysis

Data collected through Google Forms were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 24. All data were collected using an online survey platform to minimize manual input errors. Two independent researchers crosschecked the data entries to ensure accuracy across the three time points. Discrepancies were carefully reviewed and resolved by referring to the original digital records. This process helped maintain consistency and reliability throughout the data management procedure. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions, ranges, means, standard deviations, and percentages, were calculated to summarize the data.

A repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted at three time points (pretest, four weeks after the intervention, and eight weeks after the intervention) to evaluate the effects of the intervention on academic self-efficacy, learning engagement, and learning interest. Post hoc analyses, such as independent t-tests, were performed for variables that showed significant interaction effects, considering the measurement time points.

Repeated-measures ANOVA allows for the examination of within-subject changes over time, whereas post hoc analyses help identify specific differences between time points. These statistical analyses can provide insight into the effects of digital team-based learning interventions on the variables measured in our study.

Ethical issues

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of H University, South Korea (approval number: 1041223-202210-HR-20). The participants provided informed consent by completing a pre-consent form in the first section of the online survey. The consent form included information on the research purpose, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw with no prior justification. By providing this information, the participants were made aware of their rights and made informed decisions regarding participating in the study. Additionally, the use of anonymous responses further ensured the confidentiality and privacy of participants’ information.

However, a notable change occurred eight weeks after the intervention. The experimental group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in academic self-efficacy (t = 2.09, p = 0.039), learning engagement (t = 2.30, p = 0.024), and learning interest (t = 2.85, p = 0.006) compared to the control group. Additionally, repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant change in academic self-efficacy over time in the control group (F(2, 178) = 5.06, p = 0.009).

However, the interaction effect between group and time was not statistically significant (F(2, 178) = 2.23, p = 0.115). In terms of learning engagement in the control group, a statistically significant change across time was observed (F(2, 178) = 24.26, p < 0.001), and the interaction between group and time was statistically significant (F(2, 178) = 7.29, p = 0.008). Additionally, in terms of learning interest in the control group, a statistically significant change across time was observed (F(2, 178) = 43.44, p < 0.001), and the interaction between group and time was statistically significant (F(2, 178) = 7.71, p = 0.007).

Discussion

This study explored the effects of TBL on academic self-efficacy, learning engagement, and learning interest using an online collaboration platform that allows real-time interaction and compared it with traditional paper task-format TBL. The findings revealed that before the intervention, the academic self-efficacy scores were 3.23 for the experimental group and 3.17 for the control group, closely aligning with the national average (3.19) for nursing students in South Korea during the pandemic [20]. Although a modest increase in academic self-efficacy scores was observed in both groups after four weeks, these changes were not statistically significant. However, a statistically significant improvement in academic self-efficacy was observed in the experimental group at eight weeks.

Previous studies have shown mixed results regarding improvements in academic self-efficacy. One study found that academic self-efficacy did not improve after 16 weeks of four flipped learning sessions [21], whereas another study of a group with low self-directed learning abilities observed that academic self-efficacy improved after 16 weeks of six Padlet (web application) sessions in reflective video self-assessment journals [22]. Academic self-efficacy refers to a learner’s ability to organize and perform the behaviors required to complete tasks and is influenced by the duration and frequency of education. This suggests that more than five educational sessions might be necessary for substantial improvements in self-efficacy, underscoring the need for prolonged and repeated educational interventions [23, 24]. In this study, the experimental group showed significant improvements in academic self-efficacy, learning engagement, and interest in learning after eight weeks, whereas such changes were not prominent in the control group. These results highlight that the online collaboration platform may have enhanced sustained student engagement and contributed to greater self-directed learning through repeated interactions and collaborative activities [21, 22]. A particularly notable finding was that statistically significant effects emerged after the eighth week of intervention, suggesting that a certain adaptation period is necessary before the benefits of online platforms fully manifest themselves. This may be attributed to the time required for learners to develop self-regulated learning abilities and familiarize themselves with new digital tools and learning environments [7]. To accelerate adaptation to online learning, it is recommended that learners be provided with advanced orientation sessions or user guides on how to use digital platforms. Additionally, beginning low-complexity tasks during the early stages of the intervention can help reduce cognitive load and improve accessibility through the gradual adjustment of task difficulty [25]. The combination of real-time collaboration and team-based activities over an extended period seemed to have played a key role in deepening student engagement, supporting the conclusion that the educational effectiveness of online TBL surpasses that of traditional methods in the long run [3]. This blended approach provides the unique advantage of combining the immediacy of face-to-face feedback with the flexibility of online learning. Through real-time interaction, students receive immediate feedback, which strengthens self-directed learning [26]. The flexibility of online learning allows students to engage more deeply with material at their own pace, fostering greater immersion and sustained learning engagement [27]. This dual approach not only supports individual learning preferences but also promotes a more personalized learning experience, further enhancing overall educational effectiveness [28]. Therefore, this study suggests that TBL through an online collaboration platform is more effective in the long term than in the short term [29]. In particular, it demonstrates that gradual effects emerge as students adapt to online learning environments and engage in repeated learning activities [30]. This implies that online learning contributes more to long-term engagement and interest improvement than to short-term outcomes.

Peer-assisted learning was conducted through team activities using an online collaboration platform that allowed for real-time interaction. Students engaged in collaborative learning, formed relationships with each other, actively participated in learning, and enhanced their collaboration skills. Their self-directed learning abilities were enhanced through the process of planning and learning, regulating with peers, and defining each other’s roles, leading to improved academic self-efficacy [27].

Notably, learning engagement exhibited a gradual increase throughout the study, with the experimental group showing a significant increase after eight weeks. This form of engagement, which drives curiosity and desire to learn, is particularly effective in motivating students to perform challenging tasks and achieve success [31]. The utilization of TBL with online platforms proved particularly appealing to Generation Z students, who are accustomed to digital environments and prefer interactive, technology-driven educational experiences [32]. Having grown up with technology, Generation Z excels in learning environments that incorporate digital tools. Through online collaboration platforms, they can work together in real-time, receive immediate feedback, and flexibly access learning materials anytime and anywhere [29]. This matches Generation Z’s learning preferences, allowing them to control their own learning pace, thereby boosting engagement and optimizing learning outcomes. Furthermore, their comfort in digital environments enhances their performance and contributes to higher academic achievement. This demonstrates that technology-driven learning environments are highly effective at meeting the needs of modern learners. Moreover, the blended TBL approach fosters the development of key skills essential for future success, such as creativity, problem-solving, and collaboration [28]. By encouraging students to work together in both online and face-to-face settings, this method enhances their ability to approach complex problems creatively and collaborate effectively in diverse environments [33]. These skills are not only crucial for academic achievement but also vital for equipping students to excel in the evolving professional landscape, in which such competencies are increasingly in demand.

Furthermore, as the students adapted to the digital learning environment and engaged in collaborative activities, their learning engagement continued to increase. This increase in engagement was particularly evident as task complexity increased, further emphasizing the importance of setting clear learning objectives that promote active participation. By carefully adjusting the difficulty level of tasks, educators can ensure that students remain challenged yet motivated, which helps them maintain their interest in and drive for success [34].

Throughout the intervention period, both groups demonstrated a steady increase in learning interest, with the experimental group showing a marked increase after eight weeks. This aligns with the findings from previous studies that prolonged exposure to educational content can significantly increase learning interest [35]. Initially, the experimental group displayed lower interest, possibly because of their unfamiliarity with the new teaching methods and perceived task difficulty [29]. However, as the students became more accustomed to digital tools and methods, their engagement and interest levels improved.

Online collaboration platforms have the potential to revolutionize both in-person and virtual learning environments by fostering real-time interaction and active participation among students. Research has consistently shown that when students are actively involved in the learning process and communicate freely regarding their learning experiences, their satisfaction levels increase [36]. Therefore, educators should employ immersive, learner-centered approaches that activate and sustain motivation, thereby enhancing learning outcomes [37, 38].

In conclusion, this study underscores the importance of adapting teaching methods to meet the evolving needs of new generations and utilizing advanced information technologies to facilitate effective team activities that are crucial for improving learning outcomes. Future research should explore and refine these methods to fully exploit their potential in diverse educational settings.

Conclusions

TBL using an online collaboration platform that enables real-time interaction was found to be more effective in enhancing academic self-efficacy, learning engagement, and learning interest than traditional paper-format TBL. While the initial improvements at four weeks post-intervention were not statistically significant, a clear and significant increase in all three variables was observed at eight weeks, indicating the long-term benefits of digital learning environments, particularly for digital-native learners.

These findings underscore the fact that online-based educational strategies are well aligned with the learning preferences of contemporary students. When implementing TBL through online platforms, the time required for students to adapt to digital tools and the complexity of the learning tasks must be considered. Furthermore, the study suggests that a minimum of five repetitions of a learning activity is necessary to achieve meaningful outcomes, providing valuable insight for instructional design in digital settings.

Future educational efforts should integrate a wider range of digital media and learning approaches and conduct rigorous longitudinal evaluations to confirm the sustainability of these educational effects. Additionally, the incorporation of intelligent information technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) to deliver personalized learning experiences and evaluate their impact presents a promising direction for future research and practice.

Strengths

The novelty of this study lies in its introduction of an innovative blended TBL approach that integrates face-to-face and online interactions. Although numerous studies have focused on either face-to-face or online methods, this study presents a hybrid learning model that combines both approaches to create a new educational framework. This model responds to changes in learning environments caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and offers a hybrid learning environment that differentiates itself from traditional methods with the potential for continued application in the future.

The value of this blended approach goes beyond simply combining learning methods; it provides a highly adaptable and student-centered learning experience. Students can tailor their learning pace and style through the immediacy of face-to-face feedback and flexibility of online learning [39]. This helps strengthen self-directed learning skills and enables deeper student engagement in the learning process. Moreover, in the face of unexpected disruptions such as pandemics or natural disasters, this model allows for a seamless transition to online learning without delays, ensuring continuity and adaptability in educational delivery.

Unlike previous studies, this study is one of the first to experimentally analyze the sustained impact on learning engagement, academic self-efficacy, and learning interest. This analysis provides important insight into how students immerse themselves in learning and develop self-directed learning abilities in a digital environment [40]. Furthermore, this study emphasizes that Generation Z students can achieve better outcomes through interactive, digitally enhanced learning ecosystems. This study makes a unique contribution by presenting a learning model suited to the new digital age that moves beyond traditional learning methods.

This study proposes a forward-looking educational strategy that combines the immediacy of face-to-face learning with the flexibility of online platforms to help students achieve deeper engagement and higher academic performance. This approach fosters a collaborative, dynamic, and interactive learning experience by accommodating diverse learning styles and leveraging the latest technologies. It plays a crucial role in developing essential skills for future education, such as creativity, problem solving, and collaboration. This blended approach is a particularly promising model in modern educational environments and can serve as a key strategy for student-centered learning in the future.

Study limitations and future work

This study successfully demonstrated the benefits of using an online collaboration platform for TBL, particularly in terms of enhancing academic self-efficacy, learning engagement, and learning interest. However, this study had several limitations.

First, the focus on fourth-year nursing students from a single university limits the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of nursing students or other academic disciplines. While the sample size was calculated using G*Power and deemed sufficient to produce statistically significant results, future studies should aim to include a more diverse cohort encompassing various academic years, disciplines, and cultural backgrounds to enhance the applicability and relevance of this research.

Second, the non-randomized design may have introduced confounding variables, limiting the ability to establish clear causal relationships [41]. More rigorous experimental designs, such as random assignments, are required in future studies to better isolate the effects of the intervention.

Third, the narrow scope of participants from a single institution further limits the generalizability of the findings. Expanding this study to include multiple universities and educational levels strengthens the validity and applicability of the results.

Fourth, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires raises concerns regarding common method bias (CMB), which may affect data integrity. Despite efforts to mitigate this bias through anonymized responses and data collection at multiple time points, the limitations of self-reported data remain [42]. Future research should incorporate more objective measures such as observational data or third-party assessments to enhance the validity of the findings.

Finally, this study evaluated the effects of TBL using only one online collaboration platform (ALLO). Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other platforms and digital tools. Future studies should explore the use of diverse platforms and devices to examine the scalability and flexibility of online TBL environments.

By addressing these limitations, future research can build on the insight from this study and explore innovative methodologies and diverse participant bases to further substantiate the effectiveness of TBL.
