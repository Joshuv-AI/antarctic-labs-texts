Proven infectious endocarditis (IE) or suspicious tissue masses adherent to a native mitral valve or bioprosthesis reflect a life-threatening condition when antibiotic therapy and anticoagulation does not resolve the problem.1 The risk of embolism increases by 38% with vegetations larger than 10 mm.2 Furthermore, vegetations or thrombotic masses might increase the transmitral gradient, causing congestion and heart failure symptoms.3 Therapeutic options are limited in patients rejected for cardiac surgery. Although catheter-based debulking of right-sided intracardiac masses is increasingly reported,4 interventional strategies for left-sided mitral valve–adherent infective or thrombotic masses are not established, with only a few cases describing off-label use of the AngioVac system (Aimecs Medical Solutions).5

In this clinical case series, we report 7 high-risk patients treated with a novel bailout approach using the INARI system (Flow-Triever System, Inari Medical) and, in 1 early case, the TriClip guide (Abbott) to debulk mitral valve–adherent structures.

Case Series

This case series includes 7 high-risk patients who were assessed at the Department of Cardiology, University Hospital Heart Centre Brandenburg (Bernau, Germany) between September 2021 and April 2025. Patients presented with either symptoms of congestion, heart failure, or infection. All patients shared the presence of thrombotic or infective masses adherent to native or bioprosthetic mitral valves, combined with high surgical risk as determined by the interdisciplinary heart team. Initial evaluation consisted of transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (TOE), which confirmed the presence of mobile masses ranging from 7 to 29 mm in size (Figure 1). In 3 patients, significantly elevated transmitral gradients were found, causing hemodynamic compromise and heart failure symptoms. Blood cultures (BC) were obtained in all cases and yielded positive results in 3 patients. In the remaining 4 patients, vegetations appeared morphologically suspicious for IE on TOE, yet BC remained negative, consistent with either successfully pretreated IE or sterile thrombotic masses.

All 7 patients underwent transseptal aspiration using a large-bore catheter system (6 with the INARI FlowTriever system and 1 with a TriClip guide in the earliest case). All procedures were technically successful, without stroke or valve injury. Severe mitral stenosis was relieved in 3 patients, and embolic risk was reduced in all others.

Technical and Procedural Aspects

All patients underwent transseptal access under TOE guidance with prior deployment of the SENTINEL cerebral protection device. Cerebral protection was considered essential given the inherent risk of embolization during manipulation of friable or infected masses on the left side of the heart. Intraprocedural anticoagulation was achieved with unfractionated heparin, targeting an activated clotting time between 250 and 300 seconds.

After transseptal puncture, a Supracore wire was placed in the left upper pulmonary vein, and the interatrial septum was predilated with a 6-mm balloon to facilitate introduction of the large-bore aspiration system.

The aspiration system consisted of a Triever20 inner catheter (17-F/5.6 mm, 225° curve, 105 cm working length) introduced through a straight Triever24 outer sheath (21-F/6.9 mm, 90 cm usable length) (Figure 2). Telescoping allowed variable uniplane angulations and permitted fine adjustments as the system was maneuvered toward the mitral valve. However, achieving a stable and truly perpendicular approach to the mass was occasionally challenging. Puncture height, left atrial anatomy, and leaflet mobility influenced the achievable angle, and even with full deflection of the inner catheter, lateral drift could occur. Once TOE confirmed adequate and predominantly perpendicular tip alignment, controlled vacuum suction (648-711 mm Hg) was applied (Videos 1 and 2). Aspiration was performed in short, repeated cycles, with real-time echocardiographic assessment of mass reduction, transmitral flow, and leaflet motion. Manual reinfusion of aspirated, filtered blood allowed repeated vacuum attempts without blood loss. The maneuver was repeated until sufficient debulking and gradient reduction were achieved.

Lessons Learned From Transseptal Left-Sided Debulking

Several technical insights emerged from this early experience. First, the transseptal approach using the INARI FlowTriever system proved feasible in all patients, and the telescoping maneuver with inner and outer sheaths allowed sufficient reach and stability at the mitral plane. Second, the systematic use of cerebral protection in all 7 patients was instrumental in avoiding clinically apparent periprocedural strokes. No intraprocedural or postprocedural neurologic events occurred. Third, in all cases, a significant reduction of vegetation or thrombotic mass was achieved without structural injury to native or prosthetic valves. No hemodynamic instability occurred during catheter manipulation or aspiration, and no case of hemolysis or disseminated intravascular coagulation occurred.

Overall, these experiences support the feasibility, safety, and reproducibility of transseptal left-sided debulking in a carefully selected high-risk population not eligible for surgery.

Case 1

The first patient, treated in 2021, was a 69-year-old man who developed severe IE with Streptococcus gallolyticus 2 years after receiving a biological mitral valve implantation.

The mean transmitral gradient increased to 19 mm Hg, accompanied by a severe left ventricular dysfunction (20%) due to ischemic cardiomyopathy (Table 1). Surgical intervention was denied, prompting a transseptal interventional bailout approach. At that time, the only available large-diameter steerable tool was the Triclip guiding system (Abbott) (soft tip inner diameter 5.23 mm). Manual vacuum aspiration was applied under TOE guidance. Aspired blood could not be filtered nor reinfused, limiting this approach. Debulking reduced the transmitral gradient to 5.9 mm Hg. Before clinical application, we have performed an in vitro testing simulating the aspiration of similarly sized material using the Triclip system.

Follow-up

The patient was discharged to his referring hospital after 7 days for continued antibiotic therapy, with an N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) reduction from >35,000 to 9,487 pg/mL. Histopathology confirmed a homogenized thrombus.

Case 2

A 72-year-old woman with a biological mitral valve implanted in 2012 presented with recurrent transmitral gradient elevation (25 mm Hg) and pulmonary congestion (NT-proBNP level 21,487 pg/mL). She had a prior history of IE and an incomplete heart surgery due to severe bleeding complications without treating the infected valve. Conservative antibiotic therapy was initiated. Despite clinical stabilization and recovering, the mitral valve prothesis remained thickened and with elevated pressure gradient. Valve-in-valve implantation was deemed high risk because of potential left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. After 1 year of observation the mean pressure gradient has increased to 25 mm Hg. TOE revealed soft thickening of mitral valve cusps resembling vegetations, but BC results were negative, and there were no signs of active infection. After resuscitation in hospital with ventricular fibrillation, we performed mitral valve debulking with an INARI system. Transseptal debulking reduced the mean transmitral gradient to 5.6 mm Hg (Table 1).

Follow-up

The 3-month follow-up showed a stable gradient above mitral valve prothesis of 8 mm Hg and an NT-proBNP level of 288 pg/mL. Histopathology of the aspirated material showed an amorphous eosinophilic substrate compatible with strongly aggressively altered fibrin. At 18-month follow-up, the gradient was slightly elevated at 12 mm Hg, NT-proBNP levels were stable, and no hospitalizations for congestion occurred.

Case 3

This frail, nearly immobile 75-year-old woman with multiple comorbidities was referred for transseptal debulking due to suspected mitral valve IE after being denied for open heart surgery. The native mitral valve was functionally intact, but TOE revealed a classic vegetation on the P2 segment measuring 9 × 14 mm, prolapsing over the anterior leaflet. BC remained sterile under antibiotic therapy started in the referring hospital.

The vegetation was completely aspirated using the INARI system without damaging the native valve. The patient was then discharged to her referring hospital for continuation of antibiotic therapy with ampicillin-sulbactam.

Case 4

An 82-year-old woman presented with recurrent strokes, diagnosed on cerebral magnetic resonance imaging after severe nausea. TOE showed a hypermobile 10 × 7 mm structure on the posterior mitral valve ring as the likely embolic source, without any signs of IE. Despite therapy with new anticoagulant, the mass continued to grow. Transseptal thrombus aspiration using the INARI system was performed successfully.

Follow-up

She was discharged 8 days later on a vitamin K antagonist.

Case 5

A 72-year-old man, after bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement in 2023, presented with back pain and signs of IE. TOE showed a massive vegetation (22 × 8 mm) prolapsing into the left atrium. BC confirmed Staphylococcus epidermidis. Antibiotic therapy was initiated with daptomycin, rifampicin, and gentamicin. The heart team denied open heart surgery based on a EuroSCORE II of 34.7%. Transseptal debulking using the INARI system was performed under TOE guidance with argatroban. Microbiological examination of the aspired material stayed sterile. New thrombotic material developed periprocedurally and was aspirated at the end of the intervention.

Follow-up

After 6 weeks of antibiotic therapy, the patient could be discharged with a normal transmitral gradient of 5 mm Hg and without neurologic symptoms. Three months after discharge, the patient was diagnosed with a malignant glioblastoma and referred to palliative care.

Case 6

A 69-year-old man with prior mitral valve replacement and tricuspid valve reconstruction developed IE with S gallolyticus 1 year after surgery. Antibiotic therapy with gentamicin, ampicillin and flucloxacillin was initiated. TOE revealed a hypodense, mobile vegetation causing a mean transmitral gradient of 23 mm Hg. The patient developed heart and kidney failure. Surgical intervention was deemed high risk (EuroSCORE II 20.9%). Transseptal debulking using the INARI system reduced the gradient to 10 mm Hg. Despite continued antibiotics and anticoagulation, the gradient increased to 17 mm Hg within 2 weeks. A second retrograde debulking via the subclavian artery was performed with vascular surgical support to aspirate the mitral valve from the ventricular side.

Follow-up

Despite successful procedures, the patient developed multiple organ failure and died 10 weeks later.

Case 7

An 84-years-old man presented with a large, stiff structure (19 × 12 mm) adherent to the posterior mitral leaflet without any signs of infection. BC results remained negative. He received the debulking procedure without any complications and was released from hospital 2 days after the procedure. Tissue cultures of the aspirated material remained sterile.

Follow-up

He experienced no further hospitalization for stroke, cardiac congestion, or IE in the following 6 months.

Discussion

This series describes a heterogeneous but clinically representative cohort of elderly, multimorbid patients with left-sided cardiac masses for whom conventional surgical or interventional options were unavailable. The common procedural strategy—transseptal aspiration under cerebral protection—proved feasible and reproducible, offering a potential therapeutic alternative in a population with otherwise limited options. To our knowledge, this is the largest and currently first series describing interventional aspiration of left-sided mitral valve–adherent thrombotic or infective masses performed on a beating heart without circulatory support.

We initiated this approach in 2021 for patients ineligible for surgery. Initial in vitro testing using the TriClip guiding system successfully aspirated model material, demonstrating feasibility; however, blood could not be filtered or reinfused. These limitations were overcome with the INARI FlowTriever system, which allows adjustable vacuum up to 711 mm Hg and filtration/reinfusion using the FlowSaver system, without extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or perfusionist support.

Historically, percutaneous aspiration of thrombus or IE masses has focused on right-sided masses, mainly at the tricuspid valve and/or attached leads.4 Few case reports have demonstrated successful procedures predominantly using the AngioVac system, which is approved for this indication,5, 6, 7 one using the INARI FlowTriever.4 Large registries, such as CLEAR-IE (Cardiac Lesion Extraction and Aspiration Registry for Infective Endocarditis) for patients with right-sided IE who have undergone catheter-based percutaneous mechanical aspiration, demonstrate high procedural success (89.6%) but involve younger patients (mean age 43 years) with more than 50% having a history of intravenous drug use.8 Death occurred in 9.8%.8 In 2022, Qintar et al5 reported a case series of 10 patients (mean age 58.3 years) from 2 centers undergoing left-sided cardiac mass aspiration using the AngioVac system. Circuits were configured as either arteriovenous or arterioarterial. Procedural success (defined as >70% reduction in mass size) was achieved in 80% of patients without major complications. In the most recent case series by Al-Termanini et al,7 3 of 6 reported cases involved left-sided masses, all treated with AngioVac, 2 patients were protected with the Sentinel device, and 2 underwent cardiac surgery later. The mean age of their cohort was 37.8 years, thus nearly half as old as our population.

Our approach complements the experience with 2 other aspiration tools and adds a new therapeutic option in experienced hands. Registries or larger data collections will support its role in a selected population. Limitations include small sample size, single-center experience, short follow-up, and off-label use. Future studies should evaluate long-term outcomes, multicenter safety, and comparative efficacy vs surgical or conservative management.
