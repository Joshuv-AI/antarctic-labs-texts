Treeline Provides a Unified Strategy for Optimising Phylogenetic Trees Under Alternative Criteria
Authors: Erik S Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe choice of optimality criterion is a key consideration in phylogenetic studies. Recent work challenges the notion that more computationally demanding optimisation objectives result in better phylogenetic trees. This finding underscores the importance of comparing trees across optimisation objectives in addition to different models of evolution and data partitions. It is currently cumbersome to optimise trees for alternative objectives because multiple programmes must be used, each with its own optimisation framework. Here, I introduce Treeline for optimising balanced minimum evolution, maximum likelihood and maximum parsimony trees. Treeline explores the optimisation landscape using a new strategy based on perturbing the patristic distance matrix used to initialize candidate trees, which is shown to be particularly effective for the balanced minimum evolution objective. Tests suggest Treeline can be more accurate, memory efficient or faster than existing programmes designed for a single optimisation objective. Treeline's unified nature facilitates comparison of phylogenetic trees across distinct optimisation objectives and models of sequence evolution. Consistent with prior studies, the balanced minimum evolution objective resulted in gene trees that were more consistent with species trees than maximum likelihood or maximum parsimony objectives. With a case study of species from the family Hominidae, including Homo sapiens , I show how Treeline simplifies the process of constructing trees under alternative optimisation objectives and models of evolution. Treeline is part of the DECIPHER package for R and is available from Bioconductor and online (https://DECIPHER.codes/).Keywords: balanced minimum evolution, maximum likelihood, maximum parsimony, molecular evolution, systematics

Introduction

Phylogenetic trees are a cornerstone of evolutionary biology, and their construction is a foundational step in many analyses. Different optimisation objectives for phylogenetic reconstruction are commonly employed without certainty about which is preferable. Computationally demanding objectives, such as maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference, have long been considered the most accurate methods for phylogenetic tree construction (Huelsenbeck 1995; Guindon and Gascuel 2003). However, simulation and empirical studies indicate fast distance‐based objectives, such as balanced minimum evolution (ME) and neighbour‐joining (NJ), yield more accurate trees (Gonnet 2012; Yoshida and Nei 2016; Spirin et al. 2024). These optimisation objectives are commonly contrasted with maximum parsimony (MP), which is criticised for its lack of statistical consistency (Felsenstein 1978). Comparing trees constructed under alternative criteria would benefit from a unified framework providing a consistent optimisation strategy.

The objective of tree construction is to quickly identify the best topology and associated branch lengths according to an optimality criterion and model of evolution. Finding the best tree amongst the forest of possibilities is a monumental task, because the number of tree topologies increases super‐exponentially with the number of sequences (N). Hence, efficiently searching the immense space of possible trees requires an optimisation procedure coupled with fast algorithms for scoring candidate trees. Classical approaches to this problem entail systematic tree topology rearrangements to explore a single starting tree, whereas modern optimisation approaches begin from many candidate trees to iteratively identify better solutions. Nevertheless, all search strategies are challenged by the immense number of topologies for large trees (N >> 100 sequences), where local tree rearrangements are woefully inadequate to explore a wide variety of topologies in tree space.

Deterministic tree rearrangements include nearest neighbour interchanges (NNIs), subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) and tree bisection and reconnection (TBR). There are O(N) possible NNIs, O(N 2 ) possible SPRs and O(N 3 ) possible TBRs (Catanzaro et al. 2022). Tree building is commonly started from multiple alternative topologies in lieu of thoroughly rearranging a single tree to mitigate the risk of entrapment in local optima. For example, IQ‐TREE introduced the strategy of iteratively rearranging candidate trees through stochastic NNIs and then optimising through hill‐climbing NNIs to potentially arrive at a different topology (Nguyen et al. 2015). This multi‐start approach leverages the scalability of NNIs to gradually improve a set of candidate trees. Alternatives, such as genetic algorithms, have been employed for tree optimisation but rarely outperform multi‐start approaches (Lewis 1998; Lemmon and Milinkovitch 2002; Helaers and Milinkovitch 2010; Gasparin et al. 2023).

A subset of software packages, such as MEGA (Tamura et al. 2021), are able to optimise trees for multiple objectives. Existing R packages, including ape (Paradis et al. 2004) and phangorn (Schliep 2011), can be used in combination to produce different types of trees (Zou et al. 2024). Some software packages wrap together multiple programmes for different objectives rather than offering original implementations. Nevertheless, to my knowledge, there is no single software that is competitive with programmes tailored to individual optimisation objectives in terms of accuracy and/or scalability. Achieving this goal could streamline analyses by offering a unified optimisation framework with a consistent application interface.

Here, I introduce Treeline for generating phylogenetic trees under different character‐based and distance‐based objectives. As shown in Figure 1, Treeline begins from a multiple sequence alignment and/or distance matrix and iteratively constructs then optimises candidate phylogenetic trees with the goal of conceptually moving along the treeline of high scoring trees until the best observed tree is returned. The output is a single bifurcating tree that is locally optimal according to the user‐specified optimisation criterion (ME, ML or MP). I compare a stochastic NNI initialization strategy to an alternative based on structured perturbation of the patristic distance matrix. The best strategy is used to compare the three optimisation criteria implemented in Treeline: ME, ML and MP. Using PhyloBench, I confirm results showing that the ME objective produces trees that are more consistent with species trees than the ML or MP objectives. Finally, I provide a case study for how Treeline can facilitate comparison across optimisation objectives to address phylogenetic questions.

Materials and Methods

Treeline Implementation

Treeline is implemented in the open source DECIPHER (v3.7.1) package (Wright 2024) for the R programming language (R Core Team 2024) available from Bioconductor (Huber et al. 2015) and written in a combination of the R and C programming languages. The input is a sequence alignment (and/or a distance matrix for ME), and the output is a mid‐point rooted bifurcating tree with edge lengths (Figure 1). Specifically, the input is either an AAStringSet, DNAStringSet or RNAStringSet object, and the output is a dendrogram object. The dendrogram class is part of the base R distribution and represents generic (ultrametric or non‐ultrametric) tree structures using nested lists. These trees can be read or written from Newick formatted files using the ReadDendrogram and WriteDendrogram functions provided by DECIPHER.

Given a sequence alignment as input, Treeline begins by calculating a matrix of distances between all sequences. Non‐overlapping sequences in the alignment result in unknown distances that are imputed (Makarenkov and Lapointe 2004). Each iteration starts by generating a tree from a perturbed distance matrix using a heuristic variant of NJ, which provides a greedy heuristic for finding the balanced minimum evolution tree (Eickmeyer et al. 2008). This starting tree then undergoes additional perturbation through stochastic NNIs. The topology of the perturbed tree is optimised with successive rounds of NNIs (‘climbs’), followed by branch length and model parameter optimisation for the ML objective, until no possible NNI improves the tree's score. If the resulting tree has a worse score than the previous best‐scoring tree, partitions containing the same leaves in a different branching order are replaced on the best tree and the score is recalculated (‘grafts’).

The number of possible grafts can be very large even for trees that share relatively similar topologies. To minimise computational costs, distinct subtrees are quickly identified by hashing the ordered sets of leaves (i.e., sequence indices) partitioned by each branch and comparing hashes to quickly identify the smallest subtrees with alternative topologies. Each differing subtree is then replaced on the best tree and the tree's score is recomputed, with the best resulting tree being retained. A hash table is maintained to avoid retesting subtrees. For ML, only the branches adjacent to the node where the subtree was replaced undergo branch length optimisation, and global branch length optimisation is performed sparingly to avoid the added runtime.

Treeline's optimisation strategy requires methods for scoring a tree, calculating the change in score for possible NNIs, and calculating branch lengths. For ME, formulas for balanced minimum evolution are used to calculate branch lengths and overall tree length (Pauplin 2000). For MP, Sankoff parsimony with a user‐specified cost matrix is used to score trees (Sankoff 1975). For ML, branch lengths are optimised using a quasi‐Newton method, and free model parameters are optimised with the BFGS algorithm (Byrd et al. 1995). Treeline uses its own implementation of all underlying algorithms, including likelihood computations—the mathematics of which are described in detail elsewhere (Felsenstein 1981). For the calculation of likelihood on large datasets, double precision scaling is used to avoid underflow (Berger and Stamatakis 2010). Repeated sites in the input sequence alignment are collapsed for computational efficiency (Kobert et al. 2017). Treeline supports the use of SIMD operations and multiple processors through the OpenMP framework.

Candidate Tree Perturbation and Parameter Optimisation

Treeline's two parameters controlling the exploration of tree space are standardDeviation (0.2, by default) and fracRandomNNIs (0.8). These two parameters were determined using PANDIT (v17) alignments containing the same number (3351) of nucleotide and amino acid alignments (Whelan 2006). Alignments were subsampled to have up to onefold (amino acid) or twofold (nucleotide) the number of sites per sequence (L) in order to make optimisation more challenging due to insufficient phylogenetic signal. For each combination of standardDeviation and fracRandomNNIs, trees were iteratively optimised with a p‐distance (i.e., length‐normalised Hamming distance) matrix used for ME, a binary cost matrix for MP and the JC69 (nucleotides) or WAG (amino acid) model for ML. The output trees were compared by their scores for each optimisation objective to identify the best parameter values for use as Treeline's defaults.

Treeline begins by constructing a tree from an unperturbed distance matrix given as input or derived from the input sequence alignment under a user‐specified model of evolution (Figure 1). In subsequent iterations, a new tree is constructed from the best observed tree's patristic distance matrix with the addition of structured noise. I chose to model variability in patristic distances using the formula: d2=elogd1+ϵ=d1*eϵ, where d2 is the perturbed distance, d1 is the original distance and ϵ is a normally distributed random variable with mean zero and standard deviation specified by the user. This formula results in greater magnitude of variation for larger distances and represents simulating distances from a lognormal distribution.

Perturbation is applied to the patristic distance matrix of the best observed tree on a per‐node basis, such that every node is assigned a random value for ϵ. That is, each internal node in the tree partitions the leaves into three sets, one above and two below, and the perturbation formula is applied to patristic distances between the leaves belonging to the two sets below. Perturbation is applied to every node starting from the leaves until reaching an arbitrarily placed root on the tree. This induces structured noise in the matrix (Figure 1) rather than applying random noise to each patristic distance independently.

The perturbed patristic distance matrix is converted into a tree using a heuristic variant of NJ with approximately quadratic empirical time complexity (i.e., O(N 2 )). Briefly, this is achieved by tracking the minimum value in each row of the NJ intermediate matrix and updating it infrequently. Any fast variant of NJ is expected to work equally well for initializing trees (Mailund et al. 2006; Simonsen et al. 2008; Elias and Lagergren 2009), because the error introduced by perturbation is much greater than the imprecision of heuristic NJ. The resulting tree undergoes further perturbation through stochastic NNIs, which is a previously described strategy (Nguyen et al. 2015) that rearranges a fraction of randomly selected branches into one of two alternative configurations.

Treeline has four parameters governing the number of iterations: minIterations (40, by default), maxIterations (400), maxTime (Infinity) and tolerance (0.001%). Iterations are continued for at least minIterations unless maxTime is exceeded. Convergence is achieved when the total score improvement relative to the initial tree falls below tolerance per iteration and minIterations have occurred since a relative improvement of at least tolerance, whereby iterating is stopped and the best observed tree is returned. Forced termination occurs when maxIterations is reached or maxTime is exceeded. In this manner, Treeline is designed to continue searching until the marginal improvement in score is insufficient to justify further iterations.

Comparison Between Programmes on Published Alignments

The complete set of 19,414 previously published gene alignments were downloaded from a prior study that extracted them from 15 other studies (Liu et al. 2024). These published alignments span animal, plant and fungal phylogenomic datasets with between 15 and 1178 taxa, of which 4973 alignments are amino acid and the rest are nucleotides. The alignments were processed by replacing ‘?’ (unknown) characters with ‘‐’ characters, removing sequences with ambiguities to avoid any scoring issues resulting from alternative handling of degeneracies (e.g., N or X) and removing duplicated sequences. Alignments were filtered to those with at least 15 sequences and two (amino acid) or four (nucleotide) sites per sequence (L/N) for consistency with similarly published comparisons (Nguyen et al. 2015). This resulted in a final set of 2705 amino acid and 6925 nucleotide alignments that were used as input for constructing gene trees.

Treeline was compared to three programmes representing popular methods for building ME, ML or MP trees: FastME (https://gite.lirmm.fr/atgc/FastME/) (Lefort et al. 2015), IQ‐TREE2 (https://iqtree.github.io) (Minh et al. 2020) and MPBoot (https://github.com/diepthihoang/mpboot) (Hoang et al. 2018), respectively. The version of Treeline used in benchmarking (v3.7.1) is available in the DECIPHER package from Bioconductor (https://doi.org/10.18129/B9.bioc.DECIPHER) and online (https://DECIPHER.codes).

Treeline was run under the default settings for ME, ML and MP. For ME, the distance matrix derived from FastME was used as input to Treeline to circumvent any differences in distance calculations. For ML, the model was specified as ‘WAG+G4’ for proteins and ‘GTR+G4’ for nucleotides. For MP, the BLOSUM cost matrix described below was used for amino acids, and nucleotide transitions were assigned a cost of 1 whilst transversions were assigned a cost of 2. FastME (v2.1.6.4) was run with arguments ‘‐‐method=B ‐‐nni=B ‐‐spr ‐‐seed 123 ‐‐verbose 1 [‐‐protein=W or ‐‐dna=4] ‐T #’, where ‘#’ was the number of processor threads, ‘W’ represents the WAG model and ‘4’ represents the F84 model. IQ‐TREE2 (v2.3.6) was run with arguments ‘‐T AUTO ‐ntmax # ‐‐seqtype [AA or DNA] ‐m [WAG+G4 or GTR+FO+G4] ‐‐seed 123’. Both programmes' reported log‐likelihoods were verified with the phangorn (v2.11.1) package for R (Schliep 2011) to be within ~6 units across all alignments, implying variance in computed likelihood had a negligible effect on any reported conclusions. MPBoot (v1.1.0) was run with arguments ‘‐st [AA or DNA] ‐cost [cost matrix] ‐seed 123’.

Measuring Scaling on Large Alignments

Testing scalability required alignments with many sequences and sites. To this end, I downloaded the 120 Genome Taxonomy Database (release 09‐RS220) marker gene alignments and concatenated them into a single amino acid alignment with 107,235 sequences and 41,084 sites. A nucleotide alignment was generated by replacing single gaps (‘‐’) by ‘‐‐‐’ and amino acids by their corresponding codon from each coding sequence, such that there was no ambiguity in codon selection. Subsets of N sequences were randomly drawn from the concatenated alignment, and L sites were subsampled with probability proportional to occupancy (i.e., 1 ‐ fraction of gaps). For consistency, the same input alignment was used across all programmes for a given N and L. Time and memory consumption were recorded on a Dell PowerEdge T650 workstation with two Intel Xeon processors (E5‐2690 v4 2.6 GHz) and 792 GB of memory running the Ubuntu operating system. All reported times represent elapsed (i.e., wall) times, and reported memory is the maximum used (i.e., the high water mark).

ME was tested under the F84 and WAG models for nucleotides and amino acids, respectively, which are supported by Treeline and FastME. ML comparisons were made using the GTR + G4 and WAG + G4 models, and MP used the cost matrix described above for nucleotides and the BLOSUM cost matrix described below for amino acids. Programmes were configured to use up to 28 processors, with the exception of MPBoot only allowing a single processor. Scalability with increasing N was performed with a fixed 512 site alignment to approximate a typical number of sites. Scalability with L was performed using a fixed 512 sequences to ensure all programmes completed in a reasonable amount of time. Alignment size (N or L) was increased by twofold increments until a programme exceeded a 24‐h time limit.

Evaluation of Optimisation Objectives on an Empirical Dataset

I used the 1949 amino acid alignments from PhyloBench (Spirin et al. 2024) to estimate the relative empirical accuracy of different optimisation objectives and models of evolution. PhyloBench provides three species trees: the multifurcating NCBI taxonomy and two bifurcating trees generated by ASTRAL (Zhang et al. 2018) with or without enforcing NCBI taxonomy constraints. PhyloBench alignments contain subsets of orthologous sequences selected from 12 original sets of 60 species. For each 45 sequence alignment, a gene tree was constructed with Treeline and compared to the corresponding species tree (from Phylobench) using clustering information distance calculated by the TreeDist (v2.9.1) package (Smith 2020) in R (v4.4). A standard NJ tree derived from a p‐distance matrix was used as a baseline for comparison, since the average magnitude of clustering information distance varies across the three species trees.

For ME, distance matrices were calculated by excluding gapped positions on a pairwise basis. For MP, the codon cost matrix was defined as the average number of nucleotides that must be changed to cause an amino acid substitution under the standard genetic code, rounded to the nearest integer. The BLOSUM cost matrix was derived from the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix standardised to have a diagonal of zero, made symmetric through averaging entries across the diagonal and rounded to the nearest integer value. Both of these cost matrices are non‐negative, have zeros along their diagonal and obey the triangle inequality (Wheeler 1993). Statistical significance was quantified with a two‐sided Wilcoxon signed rank test in R.

Case Study Based on Hominidae Topological Congruence

Amino acid and nucleotide alignments were downloaded from the OrthoMaM database version 12a (Allio et al. 2024) for all genes with relative evolutionary rates greater than 1. Alignments were subset to the species present in the OrthoMaM species tree, sequences without ambiguities and requiring all of the species Gorilla gorilla , Homo sapiens , Pongo abelii , Pan paniscus and Pan troglodytes . Identical sequences were eliminated to ensure a bifurcating topology, resulting in 2897 amino acid alignments with 9 to 156 (average = 133) sequences and 4373 nucleotide alignments with 9 to 158 (average = 137) sequences. Phylogenetic trees were optimised for amino acid alignments under the WAG model for ME/ML and BLOSUM cost matrix for MP, as well as for nucleotide alignments using a HKY85 distance matrix for ME, the GTR model for ML and a cost matrix with a transversion/transition ratio of 2 for MP. The resulting Treeline trees were subset to the five focal species belonging to the family Hominidae, and topologies were tabulated by optimisation objective and alignment type. Statistical significance was quantified with a one‐sided Fisher's exact test in R.

Results

Treeline Provides an Alternative Approach to Exploring Tree Space

The algorithm underlying Treeline was motivated by the observation that optimisation starting from distinct initial trees often leads down different paths to yield alternative final trees (Shen et al. 2020). This absence of convergence to the same tree suggests the global optimum is difficult to reach and leads to irreproducibility (Shen et al. 2020; Kumar et al. 2023). However, alternative local optima often share substantial portions of their topology, which allows subtrees to be contrasted in addition to entire trees. Rather than discarding failed attempts at obtaining a better tree, it is potentially more efficient to replace distinct subtrees to determine whether the overall score improves. Therefore, Treeline uses two types of moves in tree space: rounds of NNIs termed ‘climbs’ and replacement of subtrees termed ‘grafts’.

As shown in Figure 1, the Treeline algorithm repeatedly initializes candidate trees using a heuristic NJ method with approximately quadratic time complexity. This candidate tree is then optimised through successive climbs until reaching a local optimum according to the user‐selected optimisation criterion (ME, ML or MP). Next, any subtrees containing the same leaves in a different branching configuration are replaced on the best observed tree to determine whether it improves the tree's score. A list of failed grafts is maintained to avoid repeatedly testing suboptimal topologies. The process of optimising candidate trees (via climbs and grafts) is iteratively repeated until the score has not substantially improved for a minimum number of iterations, the maximum number of iterations is reached or a user‐specified maximum runtime is encountered. In this way, the Treeline algorithm follows the conceptual treeline of top scoring trees in tree space until reaching an optimum, although Treeline may stop searching before attaining the global optimum (Figure 1).

Treeline Employs a Combination of Strategies for Initializing Candidate Trees

Unlike ML and MP, ME can optimise a distance matrix without needing sequences. Therefore, fully supporting this capability precludes standard tree initialization techniques based on sequences, such as building parsimony trees through stepwise addition. Treeline offers two strategies to generate candidate trees: (1) stochastic NNIs similar to the strategy employed by IQ‐TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015), and (2) structured perturbation of the patristic distance matrix followed by heuristic NJ. I rationalised that NNIs locally rearrange the tree, whereas structured perturbation of the distance matrix may induce larger rearrangements. Since Treeline's two main parameters control the fraction of internal edges undergoing stochastic NNIs and the standard deviation used to perturb the patristic distance matrix, it is important to tune these two parameters for efficient tree optimisation. There exists a trade‐off between inadequately exploring tree space by perturbing the tree too little and perturbing the tree too much for a better tree to be found.

To compare the effectiveness of these two strategies for obtaining candidate trees, I used Treeline to construct trees for alignments in the PANDIT database (Whelan 2006) under different degrees of perturbation. As shown in Figure 2, both structured perturbation of the patristic distance matrix and stochastic NNIs resulted in better trees, but neither was more effective across all three optimisation objectives. Most notably, the use of solely stochastic NNIs was less effective for ME, whereas they worked well for ML and MP. To harmonise the degree of perturbation across optimisation objectives, I set Treeline's default parameters to a standard deviation of 0.2% and 80% stochastic NNIs. These settings yielded the greatest number of highest scoring trees across amino acid and nucleotide alignments (Figure 2).

Comparing Treeline to Alternative Programmes on Empirical Datasets

It is straightforward to evaluate trees based on their score given an optimisation objective and model of evolution. I compared Treeline to popular alternative programmes on a dataset of 19,414 previously published gene alignments (Liu et al. 2024). This dataset was derived from 15 phylogenetic studies of animals, plants and fungi based on DNA or amino acid sequence alignments. I compared Treeline's ME result to FastME (Lefort et al. 2015), ML to IQ‐TREE2 (Minh et al. 2020) and MP to MPBoot (Hoang et al. 2018). Each programme was configured to use the same model of evolution as Treeline. For consistency with prior programme comparisons (Nguyen et al. 2015), alignments were required to have at least twice or four times as many sites (L) as the number of sequences (N) for proteins and nucleotides, respectively.

For ME trees, Treeline found more (> 0.1% better score) optimal trees than FastME for 14%/5.8% of protein/nucleotide alignments, and the reverse was true in 2.7%/0.3% of cases (Figure 3). This shows the advantage of Treeline's multi‐tree optimisation strategy over performing NNIs and SPRs on a single starting tree, as is done by FastME. For ML trees, Treeline found higher (> 0.1%) scoring trees than IQ‐TREE2 in 0.1%/1.3% of cases and vice‐versa in 0.6%/6.7% of cases. As shown in Figure 3, Treeline occasionally found trees with much higher likelihoods than IQ‐TREE2, even for some alignments with many sites per sequence (L/N). For MP trees, Treeline found higher scoring trees in 0.04%/0.06% of cases, whereas MPBoot found more parsimonious trees in 6.1%/14% of cases. I attributed Treeline's poor MP results to the fact that MPBoot optimises multiple initial trees with SPRs, and Treeline does not use SPR rearrangements.

Comparing the Time and Memory Scalability of Programmes

Modern alignments can contain thousands of sequences and even more aligned sites. This size of data presents a challenge for programmes in both time and memory requirements. To compare programmes for scalability, I downloaded the alignments of marker genes used to construct the Genome Taxonomy Database (Parks et al. 2021). These alignments were concatenated to produce a 107,235 sequence alignment with 41,084 amino acid sites and another with 123,252 nucleotide sites. Alignments were subsampled with increasing numbers of sequences (N) or sites (L) and used as input to each programme until a 24‐h time limit was exceeded. To disentangle scaling in both dimensions of size, alignments with increasing N used a fixed L of 512 sites and alignments with increasing L used a fixed N of 512 sequences.

As shown in Figure 4, the ME optimisation objective was the fastest for moderate numbers of sequences but was surpassed by MP for large numbers of sequences. This reversal was due to the fact that ME exhibited approximately cubic time complexity, in contrast to the near‐quadratic or sub‐quadratic empirical scalability of ML and MP. However, the speed of Treeline's ME optimisation objective was largely independent of alignment length, since sequences only need to be considered once to construct a distance matrix and additional sites may accelerate convergence. FastME was about twice as fast as Treeline for large numbers of sequences but used more memory. IQ‐TREE2 was 4.6‐ and 3.2‐fold faster than Treeline for ML optimisation of large nucleotide and amino acid alignments, respectively. MPBoot was 1.9‐fold faster than Treeline for alignments with many nucleotide sequences but 5.5‐fold slower for amino acids. MPBoot displayed erratic memory usage, which was better or worse than that of Treeline depending on the alignment size.

Confirming Minimum Evolution Is the Most Accurate Optimisation Objective on Empirical Data

An advantage of Treeline is the ability to construct trees for different optimisation objectives using a unified optimisation strategy, which potentially lessens the influence of optimisation strategy over differences in outcome when comparing optimisation objectives. Previous work showed that distance‐based methods can outperform character‐based methods at obtaining gene trees that are consistent with a species tree (Gonnet 2012; Spirin et al. 2024). To test this assertion under a uniform optimisation strategy, I used Treeline to compare gene versus species tree congruence on the set of 1949 PhyloBench amino acid alignments containing 45 sequences. PhyloBench provides three species trees (NCBI, ASTRAL and NCBI‐ASTRAL) for comparing gene tree accuracy. I used cluster information distance to quantify accuracy because of its advantages over alternative measures (Smith 2020).

As shown in Figure 5, comparison with the three species trees in PhyloBench generally yielded qualitatively similar results. The ME optimisation objective was more accurate than ML and MP in all cases (p < 0.0003), corroborating prior studies. I further compared popular models of evolution and alternative cost matrices. Most notably, a standardised BLOSUM substitution matrix was competitive with ML and more accurate than a binary (uniform) cost matrix for MP optimisation, although these results were not statistically significant. Binary cost matrices are commonly used for MP because they are less computationally demanding and allow the convenient interpretation of parsimony score as the minimum number of substitutions. The original PhyloBench study only tested a binary cost matrix, which was likely the reason why they observed ML outperforming MP.

ME was the best optimisation objective on empirical data. Treeline Facilitates Comparison Across Optimisation Objectives

To further demonstrate the utility of comparing optimisation objectives, I generated trees for a set of 2897 amino acid and 4373 nucleotide alignments of orthologous mammalian genes from the OrthoMaM database. The alignments were required to include five species from the family Hominidae, including Homo sapiens , because this phylogeny is well‐studied and offers a commonly accepted species topology for comparison. After generating the alignment using all available mammal sequences, I extracted the five focal species and tabulated their topologies. Consistent with the results on PhyloBench (Figure 5), the ME optimisation objective yielded the expected species tree topology for a greater fraction of gene trees than ML and MP (Figure 6), although this result was only statistically significant for ME versus ML optimised trees on amino acid alignments (p = 0.03). This analysis highlights the ease with which Treeline makes it possible to compare optimisation objectives, in addition to standard comparisons across models of evolution and data partitions.

Discussion

Treeline offers a unified strategy for optimising trees for different objectives. The Treeline algorithm implements a new approach for initializing candidate trees and uses subtree replacement to more effectively explore tree space (Figure 1). The biggest advantage of Treeline over popular programmes is the ability to generate multiple types of tree in a single user interface, conveniently within the R ecosystem, which includes packages such as ape (Paradis et al. 2004) and phangorn (Schliep 2011). I anticipate this integration will facilitate comparison amongst optimisation objectives and the teaching of different phylogenetic methods. Treeline supports concurrent (multiprocessor) and data level (SIMD) parallelism, real number (non‐integer) cost matrices for MP and multiple commonly used models of evolution, optionally with automatic model selection for ML trees. Collectively, these features empower users to compare trees across many different criteria.

Treeline's main conceptual advance is the method it uses to initialize candidate trees. IQ‐TREE pioneered the use of stochastic NNIs in lieu of deterministic tree rearrangements. However, I found that stochastic NNIs largely explore local tree space, which IQ‐TREE overcomes by starting from multiple different trees. Initialization of trees through parsimony is incompatible with distance‐based methods that accept a matrix as input instead of sequences. Instead, Treeline relies on distance‐based trees constructed initially from a sequence‐based (or input) distance matrix and later from perturbing the patristic distance matrix of the best observed tree. This perturbation process is specifically designed to induce structured noise that transports the candidate tree to a new location in tree space. In contrast, the addition of unstructured noise insufficiently explores tree space and is akin to stochastic NNIs. This distinction is likely due to NJ eliminating unstructured noise by averaging across many distances, whereas structured noise elicits larger rearrangements in the resulting tree.

Treeline offers a means of more easily comparing trees under multiple models and optimisation objectives. Results on the PhyloBench benchmark (Figure 5) were consistent with prior studies showing that the ME optimisation objective is preferable (Gonnet 2012). Notably, the ME optimisation objective also resulted in more gene trees agreeing with the commonly accepted Hominidae species tree (Figure 6). These results run counter to the assumption that character‐based methods are better than distance‐based methods, and I suspect further validation will be required before wider adoption of ME by the phylogenetic community. Similarly, MP with non‐uniform cost matrices was competitive with ML in this study, contrary to long‐standing wisdom that ML is superior (Felsenstein 1978). The original PhyloBench study (Spirin et al. 2024) only considered MP under a model of uniform costs, and I found surprisingly little literature on non‐uniform cost matrices for MP. The unsatisfactory performance of ML could be due to overfitting the data, since ML includes free parameters for branch lengths.

Treeline was frequently more accurate than FastME and similar in speed for large trees. This provides Treeline with an advantage if ME is the desired optimisation objective. The comparison against IQ‐TREE2 for ML trees was more nuanced. IQ‐TREE2 was faster and sometimes found more likely trees than Treeline, particularly for alignments with relatively few (< 10) sites per sequence. However, Treeline occasionally found trees with far better log‐likelihoods (> 100 units) on nucleotide alignments. In probability terms, an improvement of 100 log‐likelihood units would be e 100, or 1043, times more likely. This discrepancy was so extreme that initially I believed it could be artifactual. However, both programmes' reported likelihoods were independently confirmed by phangorn (Schliep 2011). My interpretation of this result is that Treeline searches more globally in tree space than IQ‐TREE2, although IQ‐TREE2 possibly searches more thoroughly in local tree space. This interpretation is consistent with the notion that stochastic NNIs result in local perturbations that can be efficiently reverted by NNI optimisation, whereas perturbing the patristic distance matrix with structured noise causes larger rearrangements.

Whilst ML is a computationally difficult optimisation objective because there are more free parameters, I found MP to be the most challenging owing to the large number of trees with similar parsimony scores when using integer cost matrices. Treeline was faster, but less accurate than MPBoot for the MP optimisation objective. I credited this result to MPBoot's use of SPRs in addition to starting from multiple trees constructed through random stepwise addition (Hoang et al. 2018). Although Treeline and MPBoot were tied for the same score in the majority of cases, MPBoot often identified better trees than Treeline when the two programmes differed in score. MPBoot implements Fitch parsimony that makes it faster for uniform cost matrices, but only non‐uniform matrices were used in this study due to their higher accuracy on PhyloBench.

Is Treeline finding the top scoring tree along the conceptual treeline? Probably the global optimum is being found in some cases, although this is difficult to prove given the enormity of tree space. As with comparable programmes, Treeline often finds higher scoring trees when allowing for more iterations. Iterations are restricted under the default settings to keep Treeline competitive with other programmes' runtimes (Figure 4). In my mind, the more important question is whether it matters to achieve the global optimum. There are indications that more extensive searching might result in better scoring trees that are nonetheless statistically similar (Haag et al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2023). Similarly, many trees may exist with identical (Sanderson et al. 2015) or nearly identical scores, much like trees at the same height along a mountain treeline. The considerable distance between most gene and species trees reflects a general inadequacy of single gene trees for inferring accurate genealogies. I expect it to be more lucrative to compare many trees and phylogenetic models than to focus on perfecting the score of an individual gene tree. Treeline is designed to simplify this type of comparative analysis.

Phylogenetic studies often consider statistical robustness to the data (e.g., bootstrapping) and sometimes consider concordance across sites or loci (Lanfear et al. 2024). Yet, it is still unusual to consider robustness to the optimisation objective or model of evolution, which represents an opportunity to improve scientific practises. Variability across alternative methods is an important consideration for confidence in results and reproducibility (Gould et al. 2025). It is this type of comparative analysis that Treeline was developed to expedite by offering multiple phylogenetic methods in a single programme for easy accessibility. Furthermore, Treeline's scalability makes it a rational choice for constructing trees on large alignments with many sequences or sites. These features collectively make it possible to perform large‐scale phylogenetic comparisons conveniently from within the R environment.
