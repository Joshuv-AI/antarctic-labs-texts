Scientometric study of learning circles in medical education: Trends, collaborative networking, and citation dynamics
Authors: Abigail Temoche, Juan Alvitez, Diego Galarza-Valencia, Fran Espinoza-Carhuancho, Miguel Cabanillas-Lazo, Gabriel Barriga-Yauri, Frank Mayta-Tovalino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Learning circles in medical education are collaborative, peer-led study groups designed to enhance learning through discussion and shared experiences. These circles create a supportive environment where medical students and professionals can engage in active learning, critical thinking, and problem-solving. This scientometric study aims to analyze the evolution and trends in research on learning circles and medical education. A quantitative methodology was employed to evaluate scientific production and collaborations in this field.MATERIAL AND METHODS:This study included articles published between 2005 and 2024 that addressed topics related to “learning circle,” “group lesson,” “teaching cycle,” “class coaching,” “collaborative learning,” “team-based learning,” “interactive learning,” and their application in “medical education” or “health professions education.” The studies were indexed in the Web of Science database. Articles that did not meet these thematic criteria or were not available in the mentioned database were excluded. Duplicates and studies without complete data for bibliometric analysis were also excluded. The search was conducted in the Web of Science database on October 12, 2024, using the specialized search formula. The selection, extraction, and analysis processes were carried out using R Studio and the Bibliometrix package. Key bibliometric metrics were calculated, and CiteSpace was used to visualize trends and patterns.RESULTS:The analysis covered the period from 2005 to 2024, with 50 documents from 35 sources. An annual growth rate of 8.84% was observed, with an average document age of 6.18 years and an average of 20.38 citations per document. A total of 182 authors were identified, with an average of 3.74 co-authors per document and 10% international co-authorship. Document types included 35 articles, two early access articles, one conference paper, two book reviews, one correction, two editorial materials, one letter, two meeting abstracts, and four reviews.CONCLUSIONS:The analysis highlights the concentration of productivity in a few key journals, as revealed by Bradford’s law, and the common trend of single-document authorship, as shown by Lotka’s law. The dual map overlay graph indicates continuity in the field, with recent articles strongly connected to classic references, while also pointing to emerging areas of innovation.

Introduction

Learning circles consist of groups of individuals who come together on a regular basis to study and converse on a topic of interest in a collaborative and self-directed environment.[1] Learning circles are not hierarchical learning structures like other forms of learning. In a learning circle, there is an equality where every participant is equal participants in contributing to and ultimately learning from one another.[2] This form of structure gives way to a peer-to-peer learning model in which participants support one another, mutually select study materials, and set the learning pace and learning object without an instructor or formalized teaching.[3] Learning circles have been used in a variety of informal educational settings which promotes an environment for active learning through the sharing of knowledge and experience.

The relevance of learning circles in educational settings lies in their ability to foster collaborative learning that boosts participants’ autonomy, participation, and engagement.[4] This model is especially effective in non-formal educational settings, where participants find a safe space to explore and learn without the pressure of formal assessments.[5] The cooperative structure of the learning circles facilitates the development of essential skills, such as critical thinking and team problem-solving, by requiring participants to discuss and reach joint conclusions. Furthermore, studies on this methodology suggest that learning circles increase intrinsic motivation and knowledge retention because learning in this context is perceived as a shared and meaningful process, aligned with the interests and needs of the participants.[6]

Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative research methodology that uses statistical indicators to evaluate the scientific production and impact of research in a given field of study. Through metrics such as publication count, number of citations, impact factor and co-authorship, research trends, emerging topics, and collaborative networks between authors, institutions and countries can be identified. The importance of bibliometric analysis lies in its ability to provide a global and comprehensive view of the development and evolution of a scientific field. This type of analysis is valuable for researchers, science policymakers, and funding agencies because it allows the identification of areas of high productivity, gaps in knowledge, and research priorities. Thus, it facilitates evidence-based decision-making and contributes to the optimization of resources allocated to science and technology.[6]

It is very important to make visible the practical justification behind this study while underlining how it can contribute to a better understanding of the research landscape in relation to learning circles in medical education; and all this as much as it might seem much more appropriate to conduct a clinical trial or a cohort study, as bibliometric analysis offers a specific form of traits for research questions, which is to identify trends, collaborative networks and citation dynamics that contribute to the correct orientation of future research. To strengthen this, one can draw on previous bibliometric studies on education strategies that demonstrate how bibliometrics has been useful in detecting areas of potential improvement, assisting in the strategic allocation of resources, and aiding in the implementation of evidence-based education.[123456]

Therefore bibliometric analyses have been conducted on e-learning,[7] blended learning,[8] and flipped classroom[9]; which has allowed us to address patterns and collaborative networks. For this reason, our objective was to describe the trends, collaborative networks, and citation dynamics in learning circles in medical education through a bibliometric analysis.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This scientometric study was designed to analyze the evolution and trends in research on learning circles and medical education. A quantitative methodology was used to evaluate scientific production and collaborations in this field. This study were reported in accordance with the Reporting and Measurement of Items for Bibliometric or Scientometric Studies in Health Sciences (RAMIBS) guidelines. RAMIBS provides a gold standard for structured reporting of bibliometric data in a systematic way and facilitates providing structure and clarity in identifying research trends, collaboration networks, and citation patterns related to learning circles in medical education.

Selection criteria

While the study is restricted to studies published only in the time frame of 2005–2024, this is based only on the availability and relevance of the data. The data available from 2005 to 2024 represents some significant developments in strategies and methods of education, particularly with medical education, consistent with the focus of the study. The Web of Science database also showed a concentration of publications in these years, making for a cohesive representation of trends and developments in learning circles. This time frame also allows for some level of evolving categorization and, more importantly, allows for the acquisition of the most relevant and recent relevant research. Finally, no language restriction was applied.

Procedure

The selection process was shaped by the definitions and contexts found in each publication. Instead of implementing a single, defined concept of what a study circle is, we considered how each study framed and described the study circle in its own context. This flexible approach allowed us to broaden our horizons and included several different ideas and methods, which were beneficial for our analysis and provided an overall encompassing meaning of study circles in the medical education field.

The search was performed in the Web of Science database on October 12, 2024. The following search formula was used to identify relevant studies: TS = (“learning circle” OR “learning circle” OR “group lesson” OR “teaching cycle” OR “class coaching” OR “collaborative learning” OR “collaborative learning circle” OR “team-based learning” OR “group learning” OR “circle learning” OR “interactive learning”) AND TS = (“medical education” OR “health professions education” OR “medical training” OR “medical studies” OR “medical curriculum” OR “medical pedagogy”). The selection, extraction, and analysis processes were done using R Studio and the Bibliometrix package. A systematic search of the Web of Science database was the first step in identifying relevant studies. Following this, metadata of articles selected in the previous step were extracted (and included authors, citations, and keywords). Finally, the departures of bibliometric information were analyzed, bibliometric metrics were calculated, and trends and patterns of learning circles and medical education literature were visualized using tools available within Bibliometrix.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using the R Studio version 4.2.3 and CiteSpace 6.3 R2 package. Key bibliometric metrics were calculated, such as h-index, g-index, m-index, total number of citations (TC), number of publications (NP), and year of publication start (PY_start). In addition, Bradford’s law and Lotka’s law analyses were performed to assess the distribution of productivity and collaboration among the authors. The CiteSpace dual map overlay graph was used to visualize the evolution and trends in the research.

Results

The bibliometric analysis covered the period from 2005 to 2024, with 50 documents from 35 sources. An annual growth of 8.84% was observed, with an average age of documents of 6.18 years and an average of 20.38 citations per document. In total, 182 authors were identified, with an average of 3.74 co-authors per paper and 10% international co-authorships. Paper types included 35 articles, two early access articles, one conference proceedings article, two book reviews, one correction, two editorial materials, one letter, two meeting abstracts, and four reviews [Table 1].

Discussion

Learning circles are valued as a key tool to foster critical thinking in the field of medical sciences.[1] This methodology can be transformed into specialized associations, such as medical student scientific societies.[10] Although they are of great relevance, this work constitutes the first bibliometric analysis focused on scientific production related to learning circles in the last two decades.

“Medical Teacher” and “BMC Medical Education” stand out among the journals with the highest scientific production, addressing topics such as e-learning and teaching evaluation, according to bibliometric studies,[1112] which evidences their leadership in various fields linked to medical education worldwide. Similarly, the analysis of Bradford’s law identified some journals, such as Academic Psychiatry and Journal of Chiropractic Education, which, although not specialized in medical education, include publications on learning circles, reflecting that specialized fields are incorporating these topics as relevant in their research.

Lotka’s law analyzes the productivity of researchers in a specific area. According to this law, academic research on learning circles in medical education is dominated by a small group of prominent authors, while most contributors publish less frequently. This behavior reflects a characteristic hierarchization in knowledge generation, where the most active actors play a central role in the configuration of collaborative networks and citation dynamics.[13] Therefore, we recommend expanding the network of collaborators in this topic, with the aim that the most productive authors increase their impact on the impact of scientific production and that junior researchers can gain experience in the methodology of elaborating studies with this approach.

The findings from the overlay dual map analysis emphasize the balance of traditional and innovative approaches in medical education research, especially with respect to learning circles. The link between the recent articles in the “2. Medicine, Medical Clinical” cluster and historical references in the “5. Health, Nursing, Medicine” cluster demonstrates that an emerging area of research, such as learning circles, builds upon a robust knowledge base, while also moving the area forward in an appropriate manner.[14] The relationship between tradition and innovation demonstrates the role of working in tandem, with the opportunity to address contemporary issues without being “untethered” from core principles. The synergy provided by integration also means the area can be better positioned to generate relevant knowledge and provide the possibility of a responsive approach to modern change.[15] Finally, the results of this study reinforce the need to make the case for interdisciplinary and continuous approaches to learning circle research in medical education.

The analysis of impact metrics reflects a remarkable diversity in the influence and productivity of journals in medical education, where “BMC Medical Education” and “Medical Teacher” lead in h- and g-indexes, the latter standing out for registering the highest number of total citations (322) and publications (6) since 2013, confirming its relevance as a pillar in the field.[1617] On the contrary, journals such as “Medical Education” and “Medical Science Educator,” although with lower h-indexes, evidence the dynamism of a field that integrates emerging publications with high growth potential, a phenomenon supported by the predominance of low m-indexes, characteristic of young journals that are still building their academic impact. This coexistence of consolidated and developing sources fosters innovation and knowledge diversification, aligning with the need for interdisciplinary collaboration and the strategic use of emerging platforms to address complex challenges.[18] Thus, the bibliometric landscape demonstrates how the balance between tradition and innovation is shaping an expanding academic ecosystem that not only sustains scientific advancement but also redefines the future directions of the field.

The analysis of the most frequent words highlights the predominant focus on strategies centered on collaborative learning within medical education, highlighting terms such as “team-based learning,” “medical education,” and “collaborative learning.” This landscape reflects an evolution toward more holistic educational models, designed to prepare students not only as technically competent professionals but also as effective actors within complex, multidisciplinary environments in today’s healthcare system, which falls within the competencies needed.

Our study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we limited our analysis to papers published in the Web of Science database. Papers published in other databases, such as Scopus, were not included. Second, we only dealt with papers published after 2005 and thus excluded papers published before this year. Third, we limited our analysis to quantitative metrics (word frequency and citation count), thus leaving out important qualitative perspectives on the impact and relevance of the papers investigated and contexts.

This investigation has many advantages compared to previous studies using bibliometrics to explore similar areas of research. This study used a method using advanced tools, specifically RAMIBS,[19] Bibliometrix, and CiteSpace, which offered a detailed and dynamic examination of trends, collaboration networks, and citation dynamics in medical education. Similar to other studies, it emphasizes the clinical relevance of learning circles and effectively provides the reader with actionable insights that may inform the development of collaborative learning models. Findings related to learning circles have implications not only for academic knowledge but could influence practice in a real-world context within healthcare education.

Conclusions

In conclusion, between 2005 and 2024, significant growth and constant evolution in the field was revealed. With an annual increase of 8.84% and an average of 20.38 citations per paper, the research shows a trend toward international collaboration and the diversification of sources. Bradford’s law highlights the concentration of productivity in a few key journals, while Lotka’s law reflects that most authors contribute only one paper. The dual map overlay graph indicates a continuity in research based on established foundations while exploring new emerging areas such as augmented reality and artificial intelligence. Overall, the results underscore the importance of team-based learning and interprofessional education as the predominant approaches in medical education today.
