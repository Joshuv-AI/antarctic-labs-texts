Effects of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium fertilizers on storage root yield, nutrient use efficiency, and soil nutrient balance of sweetpotato
Authors: Zhe Zhao, Baoxi Bai, Zhenyang Qie, Zijuan He, Shanshan Zhang, Xiaomei Zhang, Youjun Li, Wenbang Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) fertilization is widely used to enhance crop productivity. However, the synergistic effects of combined N, P, and K application on sweetpotato yield and nutrient use efficiency are not fully understood. To address this knowledge gap, a field experiment was conducted with five treatments: control (CK), no N (-N), no P (-P), no K (-K), and full NPK application (NPK). We systematically analyzed storage root yield, yield components, and nutrient accumulation characteristics. Additionally, fertilizer use efficiency and soil nutrient balance were evaluated. The NPK treatment significantly increased storage root yield by 34.8–53.1% compared with single nutrient deficiency treatments. The greatest yield reduction was observed under -P conditions, associated with low soil available P and a disordered N and K allocation ratio (5.10–14.40%). Phosphorus application resulted in high agronomic efficiency (187.79 kg kg−1 P2O5) but low recovery efficiency (0.05–0.25 kg kg−1 P2O5), whereas -N and -K treatments led to soil P surplus (50.25–63.06 kg ha−1). A logistic model revealed that NPK treatment increased the maximum and average nutrient accumulation rates compared with deficient treatments. Pearson correlation analysis showed significant positive relationships between yield and yield components, as well as nutrient accumulation in storage roots and whole plants. Random forest regression identified P accumulation in storage roots as the most important predictor of yield. In conclusion, combined NPK fertilization enhances both storage root yield and nutrient use efficiency, with targeted P management playing a critical role in achieving high-yield and high-efficiency sweetpotato production.

Introduction

Sweetpotato (Ipomoea batatas L.) is the seventh most important crop worldwide and serves as a vital staple food in many developing countries, making significant contributions to global food security [1]. As the largest sweetpotato producer globally, China accounted for 2.2 million hectares of planting area and a total output of 47 million tons in 2022, representing 29.76% and 54.19% of the global totals, respectively [2]. In agriculturally constrained regions such as Luoyang, a typical hilly rainfed area, sweetpotato is not only a staple crop but also critical for ensuring local food security and farmer income. Its prominent role in Luoyang’s dryland agriculture arises from its high yield potential, drought tolerance, adaptability to poor soils, and nutritional value. Therefore, sustaining and improving storage root yield is critical for regional and global food systems. Although sweetpotato is known for its relatively high productivity in low-fertility soils, this adaptability often leads to suboptimal fertilization practices, including either insufficient nutrient application due to underestimated demands or excessive use aimed at maximizing yield [3–6]. These imbalanced fertilization practices have emerged as major constraints on sweetpotato productivity.

Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) are essential macronutrients that critically influence the yield formation of storage root crops [4, 7, 8]. For high-yield crop production in modern agricultural systems, the natural nutrient levels in soil are often inadequate to meet crop demand, which severely limits yield potential. Numerous studies have demonstrated that fertilization within appropriate thresholds can significantly increase the storage root yield of sweetpotato: N promotes the differentiation and expansion of tuber primordia, increasing the tuber number per plant by 7.81–10.98% and the average tuber weight by 13.11–24.56% [9–11]; P facilitates the transport of photosynthetic products to sink organs, increasing dry matter accumulation in storage roots by 2.3–2.6 times [12]; and K improves source‒sink carbohydrate partitioning [4], accelerating tuber expansion rates to 0.08–3.18 g day– 1 [13]. While these studies provide valuable insights into individual nutrient management, comparative analyses of the differential effects of N, P, and K on sweetpotato yield remain limited. Moreover, nutrient interactions can exhibit either synergistic or antagonistic effects; for example, the combined application of N and K increases photosynthetic efficiency and yield synergistically [14], highlighting the importance of integrated nutrient management.

Fertilizer use efficiency is commonly assessed using parameters such as fertilizer contribution rate (FCR), agronomic efficiency (AE), and recovery efficiency (RE) [15, 16]. These indicators are well-established in major grain crops such as wheat [17], maize [18], rice [19] and in rapeseed [20], serving as critical tools for evaluating fertilization response and guiding nutrient management. In contrast, sweetpotato research has historically emphasized breeding and cultivation techniques [14, 21–23], with limited attention to fertilizer efficiency, resulting in a significant knowledge gap regarding nutrient management for this crop.

We hypothesize that (1) deficiencies in N, P, or K would hinder nutrient transport and distribution, reducing storage root yield, and (2) any deficiencies in NPK would markedly reduce fertilizer use efficiency and disrupt soil nutrient balance. To test these hypotheses, a field experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of N, P, or K deficiency treatment on sweetpotato yield, nutrient accumulation and distribution, fertilizer use efficiency, and soil nutrient balance. This study aims to provide a scientific basis for optimizing NPK management in support of high-yield sweetpotato production and sustainable soil nutrient use.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and weather conditions

Field experiments were conducted in 2023 at two representative sites in Luoyang city, Henan Province, a major sweetpotato-producing region in China, specifically in Luolong District (34°36’N, 112°25’E) and Ruyang County (33°06′N, 112°28′E). The region has a temperate monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature of 15.1 °C, a precipitation of 903.4 mm, and a sunshine duration of 2192.7 h. Temporal variations in climatic conditions during the sweetpotato growing season are presented in Fig. 1. Soil analysis of the 0–20 cm layer revealed a silty loam texture in Luolong District (41.07% sand, 57.51% silt, and 1.42% clay) and a silt texture in Ruyang County (7.62% sand, 74.38% silt, and 18.00% clay). Key soil chemical properties were as follows: pH 7.55 and 7.49, organic matter 10.57 and 6.13 g kg−1, alkali-hydrolysis N 32.90 and 27.07 mg kg−1, Olsen-P 8.35 and 17.76 mg kg−1, and available K 270.95 and 342.71 mg kg−1 for Luolong District and Ruyang County, respectively.Fig. 1Monthly rainfall, sunshine hours, and maximum and minimum temperatures in the experimental region

Experimental design and management

The sweetpotato seedlings used in the experiment were the cultivar ‘Pushu 32’ (variety registration number: GPD Sweetpotato (2020) 440031) selected by Puning Agricultural Science Research Institute, and designated as the national agricultural leading variety by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs in 2023.

Sweetpotato seedlings, provided by Luoyang Shuxiang Potato Industry Science and Technology Park Co., Ltd. (Luoyang, Henan Province, China), were transplanted and harvested on 10 June 2023 and 10 October 2023, respectively, in Luolong District, and on 30 April 2023 and 10 August 2023, respectively, in Ruyang County. Healthy and uniform cuttings were transplanted at a density of 50,530 plants per hectare using the horizontal cutting method. A double-row triangular planting pattern was adopted with a ridge spacing of 90 cm and a plant spacing of 44 cm.

Five treatments were employed in the field experiment: (1) CK (absolute control): no N, P, or K fertilizer; (2) NPK (complete fertilization): a basal dose at 75 kg N ha−1, 75 kg P2O5 ha−1, and 150 kg K2O ha−1, following local agronomic recommendations; (3) -N (PK alone): P and K applied as in NPK, but with no N; (4) -P (NK alone): N and K applied as in NPK, but with no P; and (5) -K (NP alone): N and P applied as in NPK, but with no K. All fertilizers were applied as a basal dose: urea (46% N), superphosphate (12% P2O5), and potassium sulfate (52% K2O). The experiment used a randomized complete block design with three replications. Each plot was 36 m² (3.6 m × 10.0 m) and contained 182 plants. Field management, including irrigation, weeding, and pest and disease control, was carried out in accordance with local agronomic practices.

Plant sampling and measurements

To minimize edge effects and ensure data representativeness, yield components, specifically the number of tuberous root per plant and the fresh weight per tuberous root, were measured using six plants from the central area of each plot. All the remaining plants within the central sampling area were subsequently harvested to determine the total storage root yield.

For nutrient analysis, two representative plants per plot were sampled at 60, 80 and 120 days after transplanting (DAT) in Luolong District, and at 120 DAT in Ruyang County. The harvested plants were subsequently separated into leaves, petioles, stems, and storage roots. Tissues were dried at 105 °C for 30 min and then at 65 °C to constant weight. Dried samples were ground into a powder, digested with H2SO4–H2O2, and analyzed for the nutrient concentrations [24]. The K concentration was measured by a flame photometer (M-410, SHERWOOD, UK), while the N and P concentrations were measured with a continuous flow analyzer (AA3, SEAL, Germany). The nutrient content of plant tissues was calculated by multiplying the dry matter weight by the corresponding nutrient concentration.

Soil nutrient balance describes the equilibrium between nutrient inputs and outputs in a cropping system [20]. All nutrient accumulation values are expressed as N, P2O5 and K2O for consistency with the fertilizer input units.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Treatment effects were assessed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and means were compared using Duncan’s test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), and figures were generated using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). To evaluate and rank the influence of nutrient accumulation traits on sweetpotato yield, a random forest analysis was conducted in MATLAB R2022a (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) with the ‘’randomForest’’ package.

Dynamic simulation of nutrient accumulation in sweetpotato plants

Although field experiments were conducted at two sites with distinct soil properties, both locations exhibited highly consistent trends in yield response and fertilizer use efficiency across the various treatments. Therefore, the nutrient accumulation dynamics modeled from the Luoyang site are representative of the general physiological patterns of sweetpotato growth under different nutrient supplies. The accumulation of N in sweetpotato followed a logistic growth pattern throughout the growing season, characterized by a slow initial phase, a period of rapid growth, and a final phase where the rate gradually declined before reaching a plateau (Fig. 7A). The NPK treatment achieved peak maximum (Vmax) and mean (Vmean) accumulation rates of 4.12 and 2.75 kg ha–1 d–1, respectively (Table 2). Single-nutrient deficiency (-N, -P, -K) reduced both the Vmax and the Vmean of sweetpotato. Compared with those under NPK, the N active accumulation period (Taas) of sweetpotato plants under -N, -P, and -K treatments decreased by 22.45%, 21.29% and 13.24%, respectively. Discussion

Synergistic effects of NPK fertilization on storage root yield

Rational fertilization is crucial for enhancing sweetpotato productivity, particularly in low-fertility regions [4, 7]. Our field experiments demonstrated that balanced NPK fertilization significantly increased the storage root yield at both sites, with increases of 109.7% in Luolong and 270.0% in Ruyang compared with the CK treatment. This improvement was driven by a simultaneous increase in both storage root number (by 9.1–47.4%) and average storage root weight (by 21.7–75.9%), resulting from basal NPK application that ensured sustained nutrient availability, thereby effectively meeting crop demand during critical developmental stages such as root initiation and bulking. Phosphorus omission led to the most severe yield reductions (48.9–57.4%), consistent with the central role of P in regulating photoassimilate partitioning [28, 29]. Phosphorus was identified as the primary limiting nutrient in this study due to the low soil available P levels at both experimental sites (Luolong District: 8.35 mg kg−1; Ruyang County: 17.76 mg kg−1), both well below the critical threshold of 24 mg kg−1 for optimal sweetpotato production [30]. Furthermore, -P induced systemic nutrient dysregulation, reducing tuber N/K allocation ratios by 5.10–14.40%, highlighting the importance of P in maintaining ion homeostasis through interactions with N and K [31, 32]. Although -N also significantly reduced yields (29.6–39.9%), its impact was less pronounced than that of -P, illustrating nutrient co-limitation in which P deficiency constrained N utilization. Potassium deficiency resulted in relatively moderate effects, which is consistent with high native soil K levels exceeding critical thresholds [5], as sweetpotato yield shows diminished responsiveness to K application when soil available K exceeds 110 mg kg−1. These findings support the nutrient co-limitation theory, which is particularly relevant in nutrient-deficient soils [30, 33–35].

Synergistic effects of NPK fertilization on fertilizer use efficiency and soil nutrient balance

Phosphorus fertilizer resulted in high AE (187.79 kg kg−1 P2O5), yet its RE remained low at only 0.22 kg kg−1 P2O5. This apparent contradiction can be attributed to the strong P fixation capacity of the soil [36, 37], which is also influenced by soil texture and particularly clay content. In Ruyang, a higher clay content (18.00%) promoted P adsorption and fixation, retaining applied P in less available forms. Even in Luolong, with a lower clay content (1.42%), the silty loam texture still facilitated considerable P fixation. Combined with low P mobility, this fixation reduced short-term recovery yet sustained yield by building a slow-release P reservoir. This disparity underscores the need for site-specific P management strategies that align productivity aims with ecological sustainability. In comparison, N fertilizer resulted in a greater RE (1.57 kg kg−1 N), yet the persistent negative soil N balance (− 69.63 to − 39.96 kg ha−1) reflects unsustainable consumption of the soil N pool, suggesting that the current fertilization practices remain insufficient to meet crop demand [5]. The alarmingly low KRE (41.5 kg kg−1 K2O) accompanied by severe K depletion (− 100.77 kg ha−1) under the -K treatment reveals a fundamental underestimation of the role of K in storage root development, especially in regulation of starch synthesis [4, 38, 39]. Notably, even combined NPK application still resulted in a significant K deficit (− 39.96 kg ha−1), which strengthened the need to reassess K fertilizer recommendations for this high-demand crop [40]. The P surplus observed in the -N or -K treatments (+ 50.25–63.06 kg ha−1) requires special vigilance. Although inherent soil P fixation offers a partial explanation [36], these accumulations may pose environmental risks, as reported in maize [41] and oilseed [20]. It should be noted, however, that the nutrient balance was estimated from fertilizer inputs and crop removal rather than direct soil nutrient measurements. The lack of post-harvest soil nutrient data constitutes a limitation of this study, as direct monitoring would provide more conclusive evidence of nutrient depletion or surplus. Future research should include systematic soil sampling to better quantify nutrient dynamics and refine management recommendations.

Synergistic effects of NPK fertilization on nutrient accumulation characteristics

Crop growth and yield formation are fundamentally influenced by nutrient accumulation dynamics [42, 43]. In this study, a logistic model was used to characterize the nutrient accumulation in sweetpotato at the Luolong District, revealing that the synchronization between the absorption kinetics of N, P and K, and the key phenological periods is essential for optimizing yield. Although field experiments were conducted at two sites with differing soil properties, both locations exhibited highly consistent trends in yield response and fertilizer use efficiency across treatments. Therefore, the nutrient accumulation dynamics modeled from the Luolong site are representative of the general physiological behavior of sweetpotato under different nutrient supplies. Under -P, the Taas of P was prolonged by 26.35 d in the whole plant and 32.29 d in the storage root, yet this extension failed to compensate for the reduced Vmax (Vmax decreased by 0.37 kg ha−1 d−1 in the whole plant and 0.18 kg ha−1 d−1 in the storage root). This finding indicates an ineffective physiological compensation mechanism under low P conditions, which is consistent with findings in rapeseed where the transient upregulation of acid phosphatases and organic acid exudation could not sustain long-term P efficiency during reproductive growth [44]. Potassium deficiency not only delayed Taas for K by 12.32 d (whole plant) and 17.72 d (storage root), but also significantly reduced Ym (− 43.17% in the whole plant and − 57.41% in the storage root). This phenomenon may be attributed to the role of K in promoting the translocation of photoassimilates from source to sink organs [4, 39, 45, 46]. The irreversible decline in Ym further suggests the existence of a critical window for K absorption, wherein inadequate basal K supply leads to irreversible yield limitations, consistent with field observations that basal K application is more effective than split applications in sweetpotato [46, 47].

Conclusion

The combined application of N, P, and K fertilizers significantly increased sweetpotato yield by synergistically enhancing tuberous root number (9.1–47.4%) and average tuberous root weight (21.7–75.9%), while improving nutrient absorption and reducing environmental risks associated with soil nutrient imbalances. Phosphorus was the primary yield-limiting factor; -P reduced the storage root yield by 48.9–57.4% relative to that in the NPK treatment. Furthermore, -P disrupted nutrient homeostasis, decreasing tuber N/K allocation ratios by 5.10–14.40%. In contrast, -N and -K had relatively moderate effects. These findings underscore the critical importance of balanced NPK fertilization, particularly P management, for maximizing yield and nutrient use efficiency in sweetpotato production.
