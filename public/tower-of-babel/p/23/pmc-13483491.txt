Design and development of a virtual reality simulation for dental implant prosthesis training

Published: 2026

Introduction

Dental implants have become an established and effective treatment option for replacing missing teeth and managing partial or complete edentulism over the past few decades [1–5]. Since the introduction of the classic implant protocol in the 1980s [6, 7], implant treatment has become widely accepted and is now routinely performed in clinical dental offices. Implant treatment generally involves two main steps: surgical placement of the implant and the subsequent prosthetic phase, which includes impression taking, abutment selection, and restoration fabrication [8]. The accuracy of the prosthetic phase is critical to the long-term success of implant treatment, as errors at this stage may result in biological and mechanical complications, increased costs, and additional clinical interventions.

Dental implant training is typically offered at both the undergraduate and graduate levels. Undergraduate programs primarily focus on theoretical knowledge such as diagnosis, treatment planning, and patient selection, whereas graduate education emphasizes practical clinical training [9, 10]. However, traditional teaching methods in dental education, including face-to-face instruction and live demonstrations, are often limited by factors such as time constraints, cost, patient availability, and patient safety concerns. These limitations can restrict opportunities for repeated practice and potentially lead to student anxiety and inadequate procedural skills [11]. While supplementary approaches like practicing on physical models (e.g., for tasks such as tooth preparation) can also offer opportunities for repeated practice and help mitigate some of these limitations, the complexity and variability of real-world clinical scenarios, particularly in areas like implant prosthetic fabrication, remain challenging to replicate fully. Consequently, there is a growing need for advanced educational tools that allow dental students to safely practice and refine functional skills related to implant prosthetic fabrication in a highly realistic and interactive environment [12].

Recent advances in information technology have led to increased interest in the application of virtual reality (VR) in healthcare education [13, 14]. Virtual reality provides an immersive three-dimensional environment that allows users to interact with virtual objects and perform procedural tasks with a high degree of spatial realism [15–17]. Evidence suggests that virtual reality-based training can improve learners’ knowledge, technical skills, and visuospatial understanding by allowing for repeated practice and immediate feedback in a controlled environment [18–21]. In dentistry, virtual reality simulator have been applied in various clinical procedures, including implant surgery, tooth preparation, and anesthesia training, and have shown positive educational outcomes [22–24]. These systems also allow for the examination and manipulation of anatomical structures in multiple degrees of freedom, facilitating a deeper understanding of complex spatial relationships [25].

Despite these advances, existing virtual reality-based dental simulators have focused mainly on the surgical phase of implant treatment and have paid limited attention to the prosthetic phase. In particular, the implant impression procedure—especially the open-tray (removal) technique—requires high precision and accurate transfer of the implant position from the oral cavity to the laboratory, but has not been specifically simulated in previous VR-based simulator. Given the importance of this step in achieving prosthetic accuracy and treatment success, the present study aimed to design and develop a virtual reality-based simulator to teach the impression process of dental implant prostheses using the open-tray technique. The simulator was developed using a user-centered design approach to support structured, repeatable, and clinically accurate training for dental students and professionals.

Method

Study design

This descriptive-developmental study was conducted using a user-centered design (UCD) framework. The development process consisted of four sequential steps: (1) elicitation of clinical, educational, and technical requirements; (2) validation and prioritization of requirements through expert focus group discussions; (3) scenario design and development of a virtual reality (VR) simulation environment; and (4) qualitative content validation by clinical experts. The present paper reports the design and development stages (Phases 1–3) and the initial content validation prior to quantitative educational evaluation.

Phase 1. requirements extraction

Clinical, educational, and technical requirements were extracted from three main sources: (1) findings from a previously conducted systematic review by the same research team on VR applications in implant dentistry [26]; (2) scientific literature and clinical guidelines related to implant prosthodontics and the open-tray (pick-up) impression technique; and (3) consultations with experienced prosthodontists. Based on these sources, an initial list of requirements was categorized into three domains:

Clinical requirements: including precise sequence of practical steps, required tools (healing, impression coping, analog, tray, etc.), and accuracy in alignment of parts and position of the jaw anatomy.

Phase two. requirements validation and analysis

Focus group discussions

A multidisciplinary focus group consisting of three prosthodontists with more than five years of clinical and academic experience and two medical informatics specialists was convened. Crucially, none of these participants were members of the simulator development team. All participants were faculty members at Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Written and verbal explanations of the study were provided, and informed consent was obtained.

Three focus group sessions (each lasting approximately 60 min) were conducted. During these sessions, the initial requirements list was reviewed, and participants discussed the clinical workflow of the open-tray implant impression procedure, educational priorities, simulator realism, and interaction needs. All sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented with written notes.

Qualitative analysis.

To provide greater methodological clarity, the requirement prioritization process followed a structured expert‑rating procedure. During the final focus group session, all validated requirements were independently rated by the five experts using a three‑level scale (1 = low priority, 2 = medium priority, 3 = high priority). Experts were instructed to assign priority based on three predefined criteria: (1) clinical importance for accurate execution of the open‑tray impression procedure (2), educational relevance for achieving the intended learning outcomes, and (3) technical feasibility within the VR environment.

After individual scoring, ratings were compiled and discussed in a brief consensus round. Requirements with a mean score ≥ 2.5 were classified as high‑priority, those with scores between 1.5 and 2.4 as medium‑priority, and those with scores < 1.5 as low‑priority. In cases where disagreement persisted (defined as a score range > 1 point among experts), a second round of voting was performed, followed by consensus discussion. This structured approach ensured transparency and reproducibility in determining which requirements would guide scenario design and technical implementation.

Phase three: scenario design and simulation environment

Scenario selection and learning objectives

The open-tray (pick-up) implant impression technique was selected due to its clinical importance and frequent use in implant prosthodontics. The simulator focused exclusively on the clinical impression stage; surgical placement and laboratory procedures were not included. Based on validated requirements, the learning objectives included anatomical understanding, familiarity with implant components and instruments, correct execution of procedural steps, management of common errors, and improvement of procedural accuracy under simulated conditions.

Scenario structure

The simulation scenario consisted of 22 sequential steps corresponding to the clinical workflow of the open-tray impression procedure, from removal of the healing abutment to placement of the implant analog. Each step was defined by the expected user action, required instrument, execution prerequisites, system feedback, and error conditions with corrective messages.

Technical development

The simulator was developed for a Meta Quest 3 headset supporting natural hand-tracking interaction. The VR environment was created using Unity 3D, and three-dimensional models of anatomical structures, implant components, and clinical instruments were designed using Blender software. Interaction was based on natural hand movements (e.g., grasping, rotating, and pointing) to simulate real clinical manipulation of instruments. Physics-based interactions and real-time visual and auditory feedback were implemented to enhance procedural realism.

The technical development was carried out by the research team with expertise in Unity-based simulation development, 3D modeling, and user-centered design. All 3D anatomical structures and implant components were modeled internally using Blender, and the interaction logic, physics engine integration, and hand-tracking functionality were implemented within the Unity 3D environment. No external company or institutional software development unit was involved. Prosthodontists on the research team contributed to the clinical scenario specification, iterative evaluation of the procedural accuracy, and validation of the simulated interactions.

To clarify the origin and validation of the anatomical models, it should be noted that the mandibular anatomy, implant fixtures, and prosthetic components were constructed as generic anatomical models rather than being derived from patient‑specific CBCT datasets. The base mandibular structure was modeled in Blender using standard anatomical references from prosthodontic textbooks and published digital anatomy libraries. Implant components, including healing abutments, impression copings, and analogs, were modeled based on manufacturer‑provided dimensional specifications to ensure accurate geometric representation.

Anatomical and geometric accuracy was verified through iterative review by the prosthodontist experts involved in the project. Necessary refinements were applied in multiple review cycles to ensure that the simulated environment met clinical requirements for procedural realism and spatial precision.

Training and assessment modes

The simulator included two operational modes. In training mode, users received step-by-step visual and audio guidance with immediate corrective feedback. In assessment mode, guidance was disabled, and user performance was recorded. Performance indicators included task completion time, procedural accuracy, sequence errors, and tool placement errors. Each procedural step was scored on a predefined scale, and overall performance was reported as a cumulative score for instructor review.

In the assessment mode, user performance was evaluated using a rule-based scoring system aligned with the predefined procedural workflow. Each of the 22 procedural steps was associated with specific evaluation criteria, including: (1) correct sequence execution (2), accuracy of instrument selection and positioning (3), successful task completion, and (4) occurrence of procedural errors.

Each step was assigned a score based on whether the action was completed correctly, partially correctly, or incorrectly. Sequence violations, incorrect tool usage, and improper positioning were recorded as errors and negatively affected the score. In addition, task completion time and total number of errors were tracked as performance indicators.

The overall performance score was calculated by aggregating step-level scores, providing a quantitative summary of user performance for instructor review and learner feedback. Each step was scored on a predefined scale (e.g., 0–2), where 0 indicated incorrect performance, 1 indicated partially correct execution, and 2 indicated correct completion.

Phase four. content validation

Prior to quantitative evaluation, the clinical content and procedural accuracy of the simulator were qualitatively validated by the same expert group. Experts reviewed each simulated step, assessed anatomical accuracy, interaction realism, and educational clarity, and provided feedback for iterative refinement. All suggested modifications were implemented, and final approval was obtained to ensure content validity before learner-based evaluation.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Participation in focus group discussions and content validation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

The results of this study include the user-centered design and development process of a virtual reality-based simulator for dental implant prosthesis impression training. The main findings are presented in four sections: determining simulator design requirements, validated scenario, virtual environment development, and expert content validation.

Requirements of simulator design requirements

Validated simulator requirements

Following thematic content analysis of the literature, a prior systematic review [26], expert consultations, and focus group discussions, a total of 34 requirements were identified and validated. These requirements were categorized into three domains: clinical (n = 12), educational (n = 11), and technical (n = 11). These requirements, categorized into clinical, educational, and technical aspects, are detailed in Supplementary File S1.

To more explicitly reflect the outcomes of the expert-based requirement analysis, the prioritization process resulted in 23 high-priority, 10 medium-priority, and 1 low-priority requirements across clinical, educational, and technical domains. High-priority requirements predominantly encompassed critical clinical workflow steps, accurate spatial positioning and alignment of implant components, and the provision of real-time feedback mechanisms. These requirements directly informed the core structure and interaction design of the simulation scenario.

During iterative discussion and consensus rounds, several requirements were refined to enhance clarity, consistency, and technical feasibility. For instance, the level of detail in anatomical modeling was adjusted to balance visual fidelity with system performance, and certain interaction constraints were simplified to better align with the limitations of hand-tracking technology. Additionally, a small number of initially proposed requirements were either merged due to conceptual overlap or deprioritized based on limited educational impact or technical constraints within the virtual environment.

Content validation was operationalized through structured expert review based on predefined evaluation criteria, including procedural accuracy, anatomical fidelity, interaction realism, and educational clarity. Experts assessed each component using a Likert scale and provided qualitative feedback, which guided iterative refinements of the simulator. The consistently high ratings (mean score > 4.5 out of 5) and final consensus approval indicate a strong level of agreement regarding the validity and educational suitability of the developed simulator.

Clinical requirements focused on accurate representation of mandibular anatomy, correct positioning and orientation of implant components, and adherence to the standard clinical workflow of the open-tray impression technique. Key elements included modeling of implant fixtures and prosthetic components, correct sequencing of procedural steps, simulation of impression materials, spatial stability of components, and appropriate reconnection of healing abutments and analogs.

Educational requirements addressed procedural understanding, tool recognition, execution of the correct clinical sequence, real-time feedback, repeated practice, and performance assessment. Additional requirements included enhancement of three-dimensional spatial perception and support for progressive learning through guided and unguided practice modes.

Technical requirements included support for natural hand interaction, physics-based object behavior, real-time visual and auditory feedback, availability of training and assessment modes, recording of user performance data, system responsiveness, modular design, and usability features to support learner interaction.

Across all domains, clinical accuracy, correct procedural sequencing, interactive realism, and feedback mechanisms were consistently prioritized as high-importance requirements by all participating experts.

Scenario design

Based on the validated requirements, the final simulation scenario was organized into 22 sequential procedural steps, corresponding to the complete workflow of the open-tray (pick-up) implant impression technique. The scenario began with removal of the healing abutment and concluded with attachment of the implant analog to the impression coping.

Each step was defined by the required user action, associated instrument, execution prerequisites, system feedback, and predefined error conditions. Logical dependencies were implemented to ensure that each step could only be performed after successful completion of the preceding step, thereby preserving the clinical sequence of the procedure. Virtual environment development.

The virtual environment was developed using Unity 3D (version 2022), with three-dimensional anatomical structures, implant components, and clinical instruments modeled in Blender software. The environment included a simulated dental clinic setting, a mandibular model with bone, teeth, and soft tissue structures, and all instruments required for the impression procedure. To provide greater transparency into the system’s performance, key interactive components of the simulator were implemented using rule-based logic integrated with the Unity physics engine. Physics-based interactions were achieved primarily through collider components and rigid body dynamics, enabling realistic object behaviors such as gripping, collision detection, rotation, and spatial alignment of instruments and implant components. Object interactions were constrained using predefined positional and rotational thresholds to ensure accurate clinical placement.

Simulation of impression materials was implemented at the functional level rather than through full physical modeling. Specifically, material placement and behavior (e.g., tray filling and impression coping coverage) were displayed using visual changes based on predefined state and interaction conditions, allowing users to follow the correct procedure sequence without computationally intensive fluid simulation.

Feedback mechanisms were enabled through event-based recognition of user actions. Correct or incorrect actions were identified by comparing user interactions with predefined procedural rules, including sequence order, object positioning, and task completion criteria. Based on these conditions, the simulator provided real-time visual cues (e.g., highlights, overlays) and audio prompts to guide the user’s performance and correct errors.

In the evaluation mode, user performance was automatically recorded using predefined criteria, including task completion time, sequence accuracy, number of errors, and object placement accuracy. Each step of the procedure was evaluated against expected conditions, and scores were calculated using a rule-based scoring system. The performance and stability of these systems were qualitatively validated through expert reviews, who assessed the realism, accuracy, and educational relevance of the interactions and feedback during repeated testing sessions.

Although the presented figures illustrate the structural design, components, and workflow of the simulator, it should be noted that the perceived realism of the system primarily emerges from dynamic interaction rather than static visual representation. Specifically, realism is achieved through real-time object manipulation, spatial alignment constraints, sequence-dependent task execution, and immediate feedback during user interaction. These interactive elements, which are difficult to fully capture in static images, play a central role in simulating the procedural aspects of the open-tray impression technique.

The current simulator utilizes a simplified anatomical model focusing on the mandible, without inclusion of the full maxillofacial structures such as buccal soft tissues. While this design facilitates clearer visualization and interaction during early-stage learning, it does not fully replicate clinical working conditions, including restricted access and operator positioning. Future developments should aim to incorporate more comprehensive anatomical features and clinically accurate ergonomics to enhance realism.

The simulator was implemented on a standalone virtual reality headset supporting natural hand-tracking interaction without controllers. Two execution modes were incorporated: training mode, which provided step-specific visual and audio guidance, and assessment mode, in which users performed the procedure without guidance. Disscution

The present study reports the design and development of a virtual reality–based simulator specifically intended for training the dental implant prosthesis impression procedure using the open-tray (pick-up) technique. In contrast to most existing VR applications in implant dentistry, which primarily focus on the surgical phase, this simulator addresses the prosthetic impression stage, a critical yet underrepresented component of implant treatment. According to a previous systematic review conducted by the same research team, VR-based systems have been increasingly applied in implant education, most commonly using head-mounted displays and Unity-based development platforms, with reported improvements in procedural training outcomes [26]. However, these systems have not simulated the implant impression workflow in a structured and comprehensive manner.

A key contribution of this study is the application of a user-centered design framework to model the complete open-tray impression procedure, from removal of the healing abutment to attachment of the implant analog. By integrating validated clinical, educational, and technical requirements, the developed simulator reflects the standard clinical workflow while maintaining alignment with educational objectives. Expert validation confirmed that the simulated steps, anatomical representations, and interaction logic were consistent with current prosthodontic practice, supporting the feasibility of the simulator as a supplementary educational tool in implant prosthesis training.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research highlighting the potential of VR-based simulations in dental education. In particular, dental experts reported positive feedback regarding the simulator’s realism, usability, and effectiveness in supporting implant impression training. While studies have also reported benefits such as increased learner confidence and reduced anxiety, our current study focused on validating the simulator’s technical and pedagogical suitability from an expert perspective. Zhou et al. (2021) reported that VR-assisted implant training improved procedural understanding and learner confidence by allowing interaction with anatomically accurate mandibular models derived from cone beam computed tomography data [27]. Similarly, Mansoory et al. (2022) demonstrated that immersive VR environments enhanced learner engagement and reduced anxiety compared with traditional demonstration-based teaching methods [11]. Although these studies demonstrated the educational benefits of VR in dental training, their focus was primarily limited to surgical procedures or observational learning environments rather than interactive simulation of prosthetic workflows.

The present simulator differs from earlier systems by emphasizing precise spatial relationships between implant components, impression materials, and trays—factors that are essential for accurate transfer of implant position from the oral cavity to the laboratory. The incorporation of natural hand-tracking interaction and physics-based object behavior enabled realistic manipulation of clinical instruments without reliance on handheld controllers, addressing a limitation of earlier VR-based simulatorthat employed joystick-based interactions. This design choice supports more intuitive execution of procedural steps and approximates aspects of real clinical movements.

From an educational perspective, the inclusion of both training and assessment modes allows learners to progress from guided instruction to independent task execution, while enabling instructors to review objective performance indicators. Such a structure aligns with established principles of experiential learning and skill acquisition, which emphasize repeated practice, immediate feedback, and progressive reduction of instructional support. Although quantitative evaluation of learning outcomes was beyond the scope of the current phase, the validated design provides a preliminary foundation for future usability and educational evaluation studies.

While the developed simulator provides an interactive and immersive environment for practicing the procedural steps of the open-tray impression technique, its current capabilities are particularly well suited for familiarization with the procedure, spatial understanding, and cognitive skill development. The use of natural hand tracking, object manipulation, and sequence-dependent interactions allow users to actively engage with the workflow rather than passively observe.

However, the absence of haptic feedback remains a limitation of the current system. Therefore, the simulator should be considered a complementary educational tool that supports skill acquisition and preparation in the early stages prior to real-world or mannequin-based clinical practice, rather than a replacement for conventional practical training.

Despite its contributions, this study represents an early phase of simulator development. The simulator has not yet undergone formal usability testing or learner-based performance evaluation. Future phases of the project will include usability assessment with faculty members and prosthodontists, followed by controlled evaluation with dental students to examine learning outcomes related to procedural accuracy, error reduction, and understanding of the implant impression process. These evaluations will be essential to determine the educational impact and practical integration of the simulator in to dental curriculaum.

It should be noted that the present study focuses on the design and development phase of the simulator and its initial qualitative validation by experts. A comprehensive evaluation of educational effectiveness, including learner-based usability testing, construct validity, and comparison with traditional educational methods, was beyond the scope of this phase.

Future research will include controlled studies with dental students to assess usability, learning outcomes, and skill acquisition using objective performance measures. In addition, comparative studies with conventional educational approaches are planned to evaluate the added value of the VR-based educational environment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, although the simulator was qualitatively validated by prosthodontists and medical informatics experts, no quantitative evaluation was conducted with dental students. Therefore, the educational effectiveness of the simulator has yet to be empirically determined. Future studies should include usability testing, objective performance evaluation, and learning outcome evaluation to determine its impact on procedural accuracy, error reduction, and skill acquisition.

Second, the current simulator lacks haptic feedback, which is an important component of clinical skill development. Tactile sensations such as material resistance, tray placement pressure, and impression material flow control are not replicated in the virtual environment. Incorporating haptic or force feedback technologies in future iterations could enhance procedural realism and improve training effectiveness.

Third, the simulator is an early-stage prototype with certain technical and usability limitations. Although the system operated smoothly during expert validation, prolonged use of virtual reality may result in user discomfort such as nausea or eye fatigue. Furthermore, the current implementation is optimized for a specific hardware platform and may require further adaptation for broader compatibility across devices and different user settings.

Fourth, the expert sample involved in requirements definition, prioritization, and validation was relatively small and selected from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Involving the same experts at different stages may also lead to potential confirmation bias. Future research should include a larger and more diverse group of experts as well as independent external evaluators.

Finally, this simulator uses a simplified anatomical model that does not fully replicate clinical conditions, particularly the absence of surrounding soft tissues and realistic ergonomic constraints. This limitation may impact the transferability of acquired skills to real clinical settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a structured foundation for the development of a virtual reality-based simulator for implant prosthetic training and supports further research into its educational applications.

Conclusion

A virtual reality–based simulator was designed and developed to support training in the open-tray implant impression technique using a user-centered framework. While the simulator offers a structured and interactive environment for procedural practice, this study is limited to design, development, and expert-based validation. Further research is required to evaluate its usability, learning outcomes, and educational effectiveness among dental students.
