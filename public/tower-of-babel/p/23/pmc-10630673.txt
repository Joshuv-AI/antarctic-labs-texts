Protocol to study molecule exchange between venous blood and CSF in live mice using transcranial bioluminescence imaging
Authors: Tina Esmail, Maiken Nedergaard, Felix RM Beinlich
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn bioluminescence imaging (BLI), the biochemical reaction between a substrate and enzyme triggers light emission upon convergence. Unlike fluorescence imaging, BLI does not require excitation. In this protocol, we utilize the high signal-to-background ratio of the reaction between luciferase and its substrate to study the exchange of molecules between blood and cerebrospinal fluid. We outline steps for skull window thinning, cisterna magna infusion, intravascular retro-orbital injection, and imaging.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Møllgård et al. (2023).1Subject areas: Health Sciences, Microscopy, Neuroscience

Expected outcomes

Bioluminescence imaging is a powerful method for non-invasive, highly sensitive visualization of in vivo biomolecular events. Unlike fluorescence imaging, it does not require excitation light, eliminating thickness-dependent autofluorescence and phototoxicity issues. This protocol, wherein the fluorescently tagged luciferase enzyme distributed via the glymphatic system, binds with the i.v.-introduced substrate, provides a new tool for studying compartmentalization and permeability in the brain. We have used the protocol in our description of the subarachnoid lymphatic-like membrane (SLYM) that separates the subarachnoid space into a superficial outer compartment and a deeper inner compartment.

In this protocol, the bioluminescence signal from the reaction between luciferase and its blood-brain-barrier (BBB) impermeable substrate is restricted to the venous wall of the two major sinuses lacking a BBB. This suggests that venous endothelial cells and the nearby appositions of SLYM allow for the exchange of small molecules between CSF and blood. The protocol outlined allows for real-time dynamic in vivo study of molecular transfer between the venous blood and CSF as opposed to conventional ex vivo methods, like Evans blue administration that require longer incubation times and relates more to BBB permeability.

For control experiments, consider omitting the enzyme infusion and image after retro-orbital injection of the substrate. For an additional control experiment consider injecting the enzyme into soft ear tissue and delivering the substrate retro-orbitally (for more on controls see Møllgård et al., 2023).

At the end of the experiment, each mouse should have a set of four recordings. The first white light recording is to document the quality of the thin skull window (Figure 3A). The second recording provides a verification of the distribution of the enzyme by mNeongreen fluorescence (Figure 3B). The third recording is a baseline to later correct for any background intensity (Figure 3C). The fourth recording is the actual signal from the bioluminescence reaction (Figure 3D). It is optional to have an additional recording visualizing the vasculature (Figure 4C).

Limitations

Despite the high signal-to-background ratio of the biochemical reaction described, the signal is dim and requires a sensitive camera and darkness to be adequately visualized. The peak of the signal occurs early, and the signal is short-lived. The experiment, therefore, has little time between retro-orbital injection and the start of the recording.

Furthermore, the signal observed tends to be more concentrated around the transverse sinus area likely because the enzyme is infused into the CSF reaching the substrate in the caudal part of the brain first. Note that infusions into the cisterna magna have been suggested to cause change to ICP. Raghunandan et al., have demonstrated using two cannulas, one of which withdraws the same amount of CSF as injected, that ICP increases 1–2 mmHg during the injection. However, pressure normalizes soon afterward.7

Like with general macroscopic imaging, the study is limited by the trade-off between the field of view and resolution, and the quality of the imaging relies heavily on the quality of the thin skull preparation. Finally, the current protocol describes the steps for the study of molecule exchange only in the anesthetized mouse due to the retro-orbital injection. However, the protocol can likely be adapted to awake study by considering other routes of i.v. injection of the substrate.
