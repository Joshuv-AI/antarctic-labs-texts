Atmospheric Pressure Plasma Functionalization of Sealed PDMS Microfluidics: Application to Capillary Pumping and Enhanced Cell Growth
Authors: A. Zeniou, D. Kefallinou, P. Dimitrakellis, E. Xenogiannopoulou, M. Grigoriou, A. Dimoulas, D. T. Boumpas, A. Tserepi, E. Gogolides
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrofluidic devices serve as essential tools across diverse fields like medicine, biotechnology, and chemistry, enabling advancements in analytical techniques, point‐of‐care diagnostics, microfluidic cell cultures, and organ‐on‐chip models. While polymeric microfluidics are favoured for their cost‐effectiveness and ease of fabrication, their inherent hydrophobic properties necessitate surface functionalization, often post‐sealing. Here, we introduce a versatile apparatus for functionalizing sealed microfluidic devices using atmospheric plasma processing, with a focus on PDMS (polydimethylsiloxane) microfluidics. Through meticulous analysis of surface properties and capillary speed, before and after plasma treatment, along with a comparison between vacuum and atmospheric plasma functionalization methods, we demonstrate the efficacy of our approach. Subsequent experimentation within 3D PDMS microfluidic chambers, combining atmospheric pressure plasma treatment with collagen coating to facilitate mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) growth over five days, reveals enhanced initial cell adhesion and proliferation, highlighting the potential of our method for improving cell‐based applications within microfluidic systems.

Introduction

Surface chemistry is paramount in micro‐ and nano‐fluidic devices composed of organic or inorganic materials. Key surface properties, including hydrophilicity, hydrophobicity, and surface electrostatic interactions, profoundly influence phenomena such as capillary pumping, flow dynamics, and biomolecule or cell adhesion. These properties, depending on the application, may be either advantageous or detrimental. Hence, the capacity to manipulate surface properties within these devices is crucial. Consequently, surface functionalization through various methods emerges as the predominant approach for achieving such control. [1]

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), commonly referred to as silicone, stands out as a prevalent material in microfluidic device fabrication. This synthetic polymer, characterized by a repeating chain of (CH3)2Si−O units, offers advantageous molding properties utilized in crafting intricate fluidic circuits via soft lithography techniques, thus enabling the construction of relatively simple and cost‐effective microfluidic devices. PDMS widespread adoption can be attributed to its array of favorable properties, including gas permeability, optical transparency, flexibility, non‐toxicity, and complete biocompatibility. [2] These attributes render PDMS ideal for cell culture devices. Nonetheless, a significant challenge lies in the inherent hydrophobicity of PDMS, which poses difficulty in its application for cell‐related purposes.

A prominent approach to mitigating PDMS hydrophobicity involves protein immobilization on the PDMS surface, particularly noteworthy in cell‐related applications and widely employed in biomedical research. Notably, extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, such as collagen and fibronectin, are favored for cell culture and growth due to their critical role in mimicking the cellular microenvironment.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] Immobilization of ECM proteins[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] involves oxidizing the PDMS surface through plasma treatment, which has proven to be an effective method for promoting protein binding along microfluidic channels, thereby significantly enhancing cell adhesion and proliferation.[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] Plasma oxidation primarily generates functional hydroxyl groups (−OH) on the PDMS surface.[ 13 , 14 ] Oxygen radicals produced during plasma discharge target the non‐polar methyl groups (−Si−CH3), replacing them with polar silanol groups (−SiOH), [10] or oxygen atoms to form an oxidized “silica‐like” SiOx layer. [11] Furthermore, carbonyl (−C=O) and carboxyl (−COOH) functional groups are generated either through the combination of carbon radicals with plasma‐generated oxygen radicals or upon exposure to the atmosphere. [14] Consequently, the application of collagen (or any ECM protein) coating shortly after PDMS plasma treatment facilitates interfacial interaction between the plasma‐induced functional groups and the collagen polypeptide chains.

Most studies regarding PDMS surface oxidation deploy vacuum plasma treatment, and primarily oxygen[ 14 , 16 ] or air plasma,[ 9 , 12 ] and less, nitrogen plasma, [13] where oxygen radicals are generated as side products. N. Gomathi [15] applied vacuum pressure plasma at 20 Pa with a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen to functionalize the surface of the PDMS in order to enhance cell growth and attachment to the surface. These surface modifications increased the viability of the cells.

In recent years, atmospheric pressure plasma treatment is gaining ground as an alternative method for PDMS surface functionalization, which enables fast and cost‐effective processes, eliminating the need of a vacuum chamber.[ 17 , 18 ] The atmospheric plasma functionalization has been widely used in the alteration of the surface properties of open PDMS surfaces. Farzaneh et al. used a jet type atmospheric plasma device having a rotating head for the modification and functionalization of PDMS surfaces. [19] Recently, efforts to strike the plasma inside a sealed microfluidic channel are made, thus extending the use of plasmas beyond open surfaces to sealed microfluidics.[ 20 , 21 , 39 ] However current methods embed the plasma electrodes inside or outside the microfluidic device employing in some cases even copper tape as electrodes: Yamanishi et al has produced mono dispersed plasma bubbles using an atmospheric pressure Dielectric Barrier Discharge (DBD) plasma device [23] in a PDMS microfluidic on Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) coated glass substrates. Moreover, these devices can be categorized into three main types based on their microfluidic architecture: chip‐based microfluidic plasma, tubular‐based microfluidic plasma, and jet‐based microfluidic plasma. [24] Apart from these scarce works, there is limited research for surface functionalization of sealed microfluidic channels with atmospheric pressure plasma. Finally, although atmospheric pressure plasma has been employed for the surface modification of microfluidic systems, its effect for cell adhesion and proliferation in PDMS‐based microfluidic devices has not been explored.

For addressing these questions, a new general‐purpose apparatus comprising a microfluidic chip acting as a Dielectric Barrier atmospheric plasma applicator is presented herein. This new DBD apparatus can replace vacuum or atmospheric open microfluidic plasma treatment, having the ability to directly treat any sealed microfluidic device, without needing to insert plasma electrodes in the microchannels to be activated. The apparatus is characterized by optical emission spectroscopy (OES) for the identification of the atomic lines of species needed in the functionalization of the PDMS microfluidic device. The surface properties of the plasma‐functionalized and the pristine microfluidic device are compared using X‐ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS).

The benefits of atmospheric plasma treatment inside sealed PDMS microfluidic devices are thereby demonstrated in two areas, capillary pumping and cell growth. Specifically, capillary pumping is largely applied in areas like liquid manipulation in heat pipes, [25] biomolecule surface patterning, [26] and generally, in passively pumped lab‐on‐a‐chip and diagnostic devices.[ 27 , 28 ] Capillary pumping is demonstrated here by capillary speed measurements. As for cell growth, indispensable in a broad range of applications in the biomedical field (e. g. disease modelling and drug testing),[ 29 , 30 ] 3D PDMS‐based microfluidic cell culture chambers (microchambers) are subjected to in‐situ atmospheric plasma treatment using the suggested plasma apparatus prior to collagen coating and are further studied in terms of their ability to enhance mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) development.

Results and Discussion

The Atmospheric Plasma DBD Apparatus for Sealed Microfluidics Functionalization

Figure 1 depicts a detailed cross‐section of the atmospheric plasma apparatus utilized in this study. This apparatus consists of two parallel and flat electrodes. The upper electrode serves as the primary power application point and is constructed from a perforated multilayer printed circuit board (PCB) plate. The PCB layers include: 1. an 80 μm‐thick powered copper film, responsible for conducting electrical current, 2. a 1 mm‐thick FR4 dielectric layer, providing insulation between the copper film and the upper electrode's outer surface, and 3. an additional 80 μm‐thick floating copper grid, enhancing electrical conductivity and stability. On the other side, the bottom electrode comprises a conductive layer of Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) deposited onto a flexible Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) substrate. Positioned between these electrodes is the polymeric microfluidic device, functioning as a dielectric layer. To initiate plasma, an alternating current (AC) high voltage is applied across the two electrodes, inducing ionization of the gas within the chamber. Notably, the flexible PET electrode, grounded for safety, features drilled holes intended for the insertion of gas tubing necessary for functionalizing the microfluidic channel, ensuring efficient gas delivery and plasma generation within the device.

Optical Emission Characterization of the DBD Plasma Apparatus

Figure 3 shows the optical emission spectrum of the He discharge inside the microfluidic device for a wide spectral range between 300 nm and 900 nm. The spectrum is dominated by the He atom electronic transitions, namely the singlet system with emission bands appearing at 502 nm (31P‐21S), 668 nm (31D‐21P), 728 nm (31S‐21P) and the triplet system at 588 nm (33D‐23P), 707 nm (33S‐23P).[ 37 , 38 ] Despite the low intensity, the emission band appearing at 501 nm denotes the presence of He*(21S) metastable species that have high energy (>20 eV) and play critical role in Penning processes. The latter is dominant when oxygen admixtures are present, as the He* can induce O2 ionization and dissociation. The strong emission bands from atomic oxygen lines at 777 nm (3p5P‐3 s5S0) and 845 nm (3p3P‐3 s3S0) indicate the high number density of O species which are playing critical role in the oxidative functionalization of the microfluidic surface. Finally, we also observe the low intensity emission bands assigned to the first negative system (FNS) of N2 ion. [38]

Optical emission spectrum of the plasma inside the microfluidic chamber. Power dissipation during plasma was 40 W and He flow rate was fixed at 650 sccm.

In comparison with an RF plasma, high voltage AC plasma does not contain a secondary positive system (SPS) of the molecular lines of N2 that relates to heating phenomena. It is also observed that, despite the plasma is ignited with He, there is also a considerable amount of atomic Oxygen and Nitrogen, that are imperative in the functionalization of the chamber surfaces.

XPS Characterization of Plasma‐Treated PDMS Microchamber

To delve deeper into the characterization of the PDMS microfluidic chamber and the impact of plasma treatment, X‐ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted on microchambers, which were treated with two types of plasma, atmospheric or vacuum. The analysis was performed with reference to native PDMS surfaces.

Upon conducting a quick scan of the samples to obtain an initial overview of the surface chemistry, the presence of silicon, oxygen, and carbon lines was observed, as expected due to the PDMS composition. Subsequently, a more comprehensive analysis was performed to quantify the intensity of the peaks. All binding energies for carbon were corrected using the C1s peak of hydrocarbons at 284.6 eV. The presence of SiO2 was confirmed by the O1s peak located at 532.9 eV.

Notably, the predominant oxidized species on the plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces were found in the Si2p region (Figure 4). The characteristic Si2p spin‐orbit signal in the native PDMS was observed at 102.1 eV, which exhibited a shift to 103.3 eV and 103.5 eV for the vacuum and atmospheric plasma‐treated PDMS, respectively. Further analysis of the Si2p region for all samples (see supporting information) revealed two component peaks: a primary peak at 101.9–102.1 eV and a secondary peak at 103.3–103.6 eV, consistent with existing literature.[ 34 , 35 ] The primary peak corresponds to the Si peak intrinsic to the PDMS structure, indicative of a silicon compound with a lower number of oxygen atoms (=Si(O)1–2). Meanwhile, the secondary peak corresponds to a SiOx component, suggesting a state closer to silicon oxide and signifying a silicon compound with a higher number of oxygen atoms (=Si(O)3–4). [36]

The impact of plasma treatment on PDMS is evident in the coverage area of the first element of the Si2p spectrum, ranging from (101.95–102.1) eV, which corresponds to the compound (=Si(O)1–2), and a secondary peak at 103.3–103.6 eV, primarily indicative of the presence of SiO4. Application to Capillary Pumping Inside Plasma‐Treated PDMS Microchannels

To assess the wettability changes resulting from plasma treatment and subsequent aging inside PDMS microchannels, a series of microchannel filling flow experiments were conducted within channels of width=1.5 mm, length=50 mm, and depth=0.5 mm. These experiments were specifically designed for measuring the capillary pumping speed of deionized water within the microchannel.

Wettability testing resulting from plasma treatment and subsequent aging were studied inside PDMS microchannels by measuring the capillary pumping speed of deionized water within the microchannel. In specific, six microchannels were subjected to two types of plasma treatment, atmospheric and vacuum and their capillary flow speed was measured at day 0 of treatment, and afterwards, at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7 days post‐treatment, for assessing aging. The capillary flow speed inside the plasma‐treated microchannels was measured with respect to untreated microchannels. Notably, the untreated microchannels exhibit a stable speed of 26.9 mm/s, due to their plasma exposure (more than a month ago) for device sealing.

In Figure 6, the evolution of the pumping speed of the plasma‐treated devices in time (aging) is shown. In detail, a substantial difference in capillary speed between the two methods of plasma treatment is demonstrated on the same day of treatment (day 0), when the atmospheric plasma‐treated microchannel yielded a 133.12 mm/s value, compared to the 39.57 mm/s inside the vacuum plasma‐treated. Notably, the atmospheric plasma‐treated sample exhibited a capillary flow speed three times higher than that of the vacuum plasma‐treated sample and four times higher than the untreated sample. However, with the evolution of time and the occurring PDMS aging of the plasma‐treated surfaces, there was a corresponding decline in the capillary flow speed. This decrease can primarily be attributed to the expected reorganization of PDMS chains and rearrangement of active hydrophilic groups (e. g. silanols, carbonyls) [40] within the microchannels, indicative of aging of the treated surfaces.

Capillary pumping speed of deionized water inside PDMS microchannels, untreated and treated with vacuum and atmospheric plasma, up to day 7. On all days, the flow speeds inside the treated microchannels remain higher compared to the ones inside the untreated. On day 0, a significantly higher speed is presented inside the atmospheric plasma‐treated microchannel compared to the vacuum‐treated one, with decreasing difference the remaining days.

Overall, the atmospheric plasma‐treated channels maintained a higher mean speed value of 41.1±1.0 mm/s from day 1 to day 7, representing an approximately 25 % increase compared to the vacuum plasma‐treated channels, which exhibited a speed of 36.6±1.2 mm/s over the same duration. Moreover, the flow speed for both treatment methods remained higher than the speed inside the untreated microchannel up to the 7th day.

In support of the capillary pumping speed study, the wettability of the plasma‐treated surfaces was quantified by performing WCA measurements on days 0, 1, and 7. In this case, open PDMS surfaces were used that have been exposed to vacuum or atmospheric plasma treatment on the same conditions as the microchannels. The plasma‐treated surfaces were evaluated with reference to native PDMS surfaces exhibiting an average CA value of 112.9°±0.2°. The vacuum plasma‐treated surfaces registered an average value of 16.3°±1.3° on day 0, increased to 44.9°±0.5° on day 1, and to 75.5°±0.6° on day 7. It should be highlighted that published studies, also performing PDMS vacuum plasma hydrophilization, report largely comparable contact angles on day 0,[ 41 , 42 ] and days 1 and 7. Application to UC‐MSCs Proliferation Inside Plasma Treated/Collagen Coated Microfluidic Chambers

3D cell culture PDMS microchambers that were treated with atmospheric plasma followed by collagen coating are examined with regards to untreated and collagen‐free microchambers, in terms of their ability to promote cell adhesion and proliferation.

In order to validate the existence of collagen coating on the atmospheric plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces, a wettability study was performed by conducting WCA measurements. For this reason, two kinds of atmospheric plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces were used, i. e. collagen‐free and collagen‐coated.

The atmospheric plasma‐treated and collagen‐free surfaces recorded an average contact angle of 16.3°±1.3°, while the atmospheric plasma‐treated and collagen‐coated exhibited an even lower contact angle of 5.6°±1.4°. Coating of the plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces with collagen further reduced their contact angle by approximately 11°, rendering them superhydrophilic. This increase in the PDMS surface energy is attributed to the highly water‐interactive nature of collagen. [45] The immobilization of collagen coating on the atmospheric plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces is therefore verified.

Umbilical cord‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC‐MSCs) were seeded at a concentration of 2.7×106 cells/mL and were studied for 5 days. Optical observation (10x magnification) was performed, as a means of evaluating cell growth, representative images of which are shown on Figure 7, from the 1st, 3rd and 5th day for both microchambers.

UC‐MSCs culture inside 3D PDMS microchambers in situ atmospheric plasma‐treated and collagen‐coated (right column), with respect to untreated and collagen‐free (left column) microchambers for 5 days. The atmospheric plasma‐treated and collagen‐coated microchambers favor cell adhesion and uniform tissue formation. The red circles on the 3rd day of culture inside the untreated microchambers depict non‐uniformly organized UC‐MSCs populations. All images are captured under a Leica DM IRE2 inverted light microscope (10× magnification).

On day 1, cells show the expected filament‐like morphology inside both microchambers. It should be noted that given the relatively high initial concentration both microchambers appear to be adequately populated. However, inside the plasma‐treated and collagen‐coated microchambers a mildly greater UC‐MSCs population can be detected, which bears an advantage in terms of a more uniform organization, compared to the untreated microchambers. It is deducted that the treated microchambers promote the initial cell adhesion, favoring cell proliferation, and more evidently, enabling more expanded and well‐organized distribution on the available space. Moreover, early signs of locally formed 3D stromal tissue can be spotted in the treated microchambers, whereas UC‐MSCs still expand in one level in the untreated microchambers.

Moving on to day 3, UC‐MSCs retain their elongated structure having obviously created 3D tissue in both cases. Due to the relatively high initial cell concentration, the population in the untreated microchambers seems to have leveled up the one in the treated. Despite this, the allegations of the previous day are preserved, demonstrating better tissue organization inside the treated microchambers. Except from the existence of more void spaces in the untreated microchambers, some non‐uniformly organized cell populations are detected, indicatively depicted with red circles. On the other hand, in the atmospheric plasma‐treated and collagen‐coated microchambers, UC‐MSCs form a more consistent tissue network with an evident circular trend.

On day 5, UC‐MSCs have reached complete (100 %) confluency inside both microchambers. Nevertheless, the untreated microchambers continue to fall behind in tissue uniformity, resulting in incomplete surface coverage of the microchamber interior, with UC‐MSCs showing in places preference to irregularly organize in stacks, instead of uniformly covering the surrounding areas. Circular tissue arrangement appears in both microchambers, which however is more ordered and uninterrupted inside the treated microchambers.

To quantify 3D tissue formation, its thickness was calculated according to Equation (1) on the 5th day. In particular, stromal tissue thickness of 41.5±4.5 μm and 48.5±0.9 μm was measured inside the untreated and treated microchambers, respectively. Given the 3.3 μm height of a single human mesenchymal stem cell, [32] these thicknesses correspond to approximately 13 cell layers in the untreated microchambers, and to 15 cell layers in the treated ones. In both microchambers, UC‐MSCs have substantially sprouted, being also favored by their relatively high initial concentration. The greater thickness in the treated microchambers can be attributed to the improved cell adhesion at the beginning of the experiment, which promoted cell viability and stable growth in the following days. It is worth noting that the tissue thickness in the untreated microchambers succumbed into variations from one region to another, as a result of the lack of uniformity in cell expansion. This is quantified by the significantly higher standard error of the tissue thickness (i. e. 4.5 μm) in the untreated microchambers, compared to the one (i. e. 0.9 μm) in the treated microchambers, where a more homogenous organization is observed.

Hence, atmospheric pressure plasma treatment, being employed on‐site inside 3D PDMS‐based microchambers, prior to collagen coating, improves the initial cell adhesion and organization, bringing about greater tissue uniformity even 5 days after the treatment. It is therefore concluded that the induced PDMS surface oxidation after atmospheric plasma treatment, as proven from the OES and XPS analysis, leads to successful collagen binding, which in turn contributes to enhanced cell attachment and improved tissue uniformity.

Conclusions

Plasma processing of sealed microfluidic channels represents a pioneering advancement in surface treatment for bio‐based applications, facilitated by our patented Dielectric Barrier Discharge (DBD) technology. [31] Leveraging this innovation, a novel apparatus was engineered specifically tailored for treating sealed microfluidic devices. The treated chambers exhibit a notable increase in the SiOx peak area and a concurrent decrease in the C/Si ratio, indicating successful plasma oxidation.

To evaluate the longevity and stability of this process, we conducted capillary pumping speed measurements over one week on microfluidic channels treated with both vacuum and atmospheric plasma. The results underscore the superiority of atmospheric plasma‐treated channels, particularly evident on day 0, where the speed is three times higher than that of vacuum plasma‐treated channels and four times higher than untreated ones. This way, atmospheric plasma treatment inside sealed microchannels can be successfully used for capillary pumping applications.

Furthermore, in‐situ atmospheric plasma treatment, combined with collagen coating on PDMS‐based microchambers, facilitates the growth of UC‐MSCs during a five‐day culture study. Specifically, UC‐MSCs exhibit improved adhesion and organization on the treated PDMS microchambers, that promote cell proliferation and homogenous tissue formation, resulting in the creation of thicker and more uniform 3D stromal tissue by the 5th day. Thus, atmospheric plasma treatment emerges as a straightforward method for functionalizing sealed PDMS‐based microfluidic devices before collagen (or any extracellular matrix protein) coating, effectively enhancing cell growth. The simplicity and user‐friendliness of the required apparatus further underscore the practicality of this approach.

Materials and Methods

Surface Functionalization

All PDMS (Sylgard 184 silicone elastomer kit, Dow Corning, Midland, MI) surfaces were prepared by mixing the base and the crosslinker at a volume ratio of 10 : 1, followed by heating at 100 °C for 40 min.

Atmospheric plasma treatment was performed to in‐situ functionalize microchambers and microchannels. In specific, both the microchannels and microchambers were treated at conditions of voltage 2 kV, current 7.8 mA (power 15.6 W), frequency 140 kHz, helium (He) flow rate 650 sccm and time 5 min.

Vacuum plasma treatment was conducted using the Covance multi‐system (Femto Science Inc., Korea) low‐pressure reactor at power 100 W, base pressure 5×10−2 Torr (8.27×10−1 Torr after gas insertion), air flow 50 sccm and time 1 min.

All the sealed PDMS microchambers and microchannels have been exposed to vacuum plasma treatment, as the necessary surface activation step that yields irreversible bonding. [46] With that being said, the untreated samples (either microchambers or microchannels) have been plasma‐bonded more than a month before being used, thereby having almost completely recovered their hydrophobicity, as already proven elsewhere.[ 41 , 42 , 44 ] The vacuum plasma‐treated samples were freshly subjected to low pressure plasma treatment at their bonding stage and tested as such. Finally, the atmospheric plasma‐treated samples were exposed in situ to atmospheric plasma treatment after being sealed, meaning that the plasma is ignited inside the microchamber/channel via its inlet and outlet.

Measurement of Capillary Speed Inside PDMS Microchannels

PDMS microchannels are composed of two layers, one bearing the microchannel, and the other one used to seal it atop. Each PDMS layer has dimensions of 60×11.5 mm2, and a height of 3 mm and 2 mm, for the microchannel and its cover, respectively. The microchannel itself has dimensions of 50 mm×1.5 mm×0.5 mm (length×width×depth), yielding a total volume of approximately 40 μL. The microchannels were leakage‐free, as can be seen in Figure 2 of Supplementary Information, where blue‐coloured deionized water fills and remains constrained inside the microchannel.

The microchannels were exposed to two kinds of plasma treatment, atmospheric and vacuum, to study their hydrophilicity and their aging in the course of time. Vacuum plasma treatment was incorporated at the bonding stage of the microchannels, while atmospheric plasma treatment was performed on‐site, inside the microchannels after their bonding. Plasma‐treated microchannels were evaluated with respect to untreated microchannels that had been plasma‐bonded more than one month prior to testing, thereby having almost completely hydrophobically recovered.[ 41 , 42 ]

Wettability and aging of the microchannels was examined by measuring the capillary pumping speed on the same day of plasma treatment (day 0) and within 2 h after treatment completion, and then on the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th day after the treatment. The same time intervals were used for both kinds of plasma‐treated microchannels throughout the days. The measurement of capillary flow speed was obtained using a pipette to inject colored water into the microchannel, allowing capillary forces to draw the water inside (refer to supporting information for details). Simultaneously, high‐speed video footage capturing the procedure was acquired and analyzed using Tracker 5.1.5 software, a tool for video analysis.

Surface Characterization

For Optical Emission measurements, PDMS microfluidic chambers measuring 9 mm in diameter and 0.4 mm in height were utilized. Positioned above the chamber, an optical fiber was placed at a distance of 10 mm and connected to an Ocean Optics QE65000‐ABS spectrometer.

X‐ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to chemically characterize PDMS microchambers that were vacuum or atmospheric plasma‐treated. The microchambers were previously cut along the plane of the chamber, thus exposing their interior for analysis. Native PDMS surfaces that have not been exposed to plasma were used as reference. All surfaces were cut to 1×1 cm2 dimensions to facilitate measurements. Measurements were performed using a SPECS Phoibos100 instrument, equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X‐ray source emitting at 1486.6 eV and a concentric hemispherical analyzer. The spectrometer was calibrated using silver, copper, and gold samples, with the positions of the Ag3d5/2, Cu2p3/2, and Au4f7/2 peaks referenced at 368.3 eV, 932.7 eV, and 84 eV, respectively. XPS spectra were collected at a Θm=38° angle with 7 eV pass energy. Both the vacuum and atmospheric plasma‐treated microchambers have been tested on their functionalization day (day 0), and specifically, mostly 2 h after being treated.

The surface properties of the plasma‐treated samples were evaluated by performing water static contact angle (WCA) measurements on open PDMS surfaces, using the DSA30 Drop Shape Analyzer (KRÜSS GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Specifically, a 2 μL sessile droplet of deionized water was placed on the surface, and the static contact angle was determined using tangent fitting. Each contact angle measurement has a systematic error of 2°. Measurements were taken up to 2 h after sample preparation, when conducted on the treatment day (day 0), and otherwise on days 1 and 7 post‐treatment. All surfaces were tested in triplicates, with at least two measurements performed on different spots of each surface. The results are presented as the average value accompanied by its±standard error.

UC‐MSCs Isolation and Culture

Umbilical cord‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC‐MSCs) were donated by “The Hellenic Cord Blood Bank” (HCBB, Greece), which performed the isolation. Cells are plated in a T75 flask at a 1 : 100 dilution with 10 mL MEM α (Minimum Essential Medium Eagle–alpha modification, Gibco/22571–020) growth medium, supplemented with 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco/15,140–122) and 20 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco/10270–106) (heat inactivated). They are maintained in a humidified incubator (Forma Steri‐Cycle CO2 Incubator, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 5 % CO2 and at 37 °C, while medium is renewed when needed (every 3–4 days). When UC‐MSCs become 80–90 % confluent, they are harvested with 1x trypsin‐EDTA (5–7 min, 37 °C), centrifuged (1200 rpm, 7 min, 25 °C), and re‐plated at 1 : 100 dilution (passage 2). Cells are kept in culture until they reach passage 8.

UC‐MSCs Culture and Characterization Inside Microchambers

Testing of UC‐MSCs growth was carried out inside atmospheric plasma‐treated 3D cell culture PDMS microchambers. Fabrication of the PDMS microchambers has already been described elsewhere. [9] Briefly, the PDMS cell culture microchamber layer is irreversibly bonded on both sides with a pristine PDMS cover and a glass slide, forming a 30 μL volume. The microchambers were leak‐proof, even after the in situ atmospheric plasma treatment, as can be seen in Figure 2c, where the microchamber is filled with culture medium that remains constrained to its interior.

Atmospheric plasma treatment was performed on‐site inside the sealed microchambers. Following the plasma exposure, the microchamber interior was coated with type I bovine collagen coating solution (50 μg/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany). In specific, the microchamber was filled with the collagen solution, which was left to incubate for 2 h, at 25 °C. Afterwards, it was rinsed off twice with serum‐free medium (αMEM), ready for cell seeding. The atmospheric plasma‐treated microchambers were evaluated with reference to untreated microchambers that had been plasma‐bonded more than one month ago, thereby having reached almost complete hydrophobic recovery. Also, no collagen coating was applied on the untreated microchambers, since it was shown in our previous work by Kefallinou et al. [9] that collagen is poorly immobilized on PDMS surfaces that have not been previously hydrophilized. Each kind of microchamber was examined in duplicates.

UC‐MSCs of 2.7×106 cells/mL initial concentration were inserted in the microchambers, which were previously sterilized under UV exposure for 30 min. Cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2 and 37 °C for 5 days, and medium was refreshed every 1–2 days.
