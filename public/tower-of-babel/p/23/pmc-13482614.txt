Direct correspondence between liquid and glass polymorphism in densely packed metallic alloys
Authors: Hongbo Lou, Xiaoxing Ke, Xin Zhang, Lianghua Xiong, Fujun Lan, Aiguo Li, Zhidan Zeng, Hongwei Sheng, Ho-kwang Mao, Qiaoshi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid and glass are two closely related disordered states of matter. Whether each can exhibit two or more structurally distinct yet chemically identical forms have been debated for decades and the relationship between polymorphism in equilibrium liquids and that in non-equilibrium glasses remains elusive. Here, we address these long-standing issues in two prototype glass-forming metallic alloys using in situ high-pressure high-temperature synchrotron X-ray diffraction in diamond anvil cells. We identify distinct liquids above their melting temperatures and corresponding glasses formed upon melt quenching at different pressures, as evidenced by reproducible discontinuous changes in multiple diffraction-derived structural descriptors. Comprehensive structural and compositional characterizations consistently demonstrate that the recovered samples are fully amorphous and chemically homogeneous glasses. By continuously monitoring the formation and transformation pathways with in situ structural probes, our study provides compelling experimental evidence for the more general existence of liquid and glass polymorphism in densely packed metallic alloy systems.

Introduction

From a theoretical perspective, there should be enormous distinct states separated by energy barriers in the configurational space for a macroscopic N-particle condensed matter system with a given composition1. In practice, crystalline materials do commonly exhibit more than one phases with different structures but identical compositions, referred to as polymorphism. Graphite and its allotropes diamond, fullerene, and graphene are well-known examples. However, although disordered structures are expected to be relatively flexible in their atomic arrangement without the restriction of strict crystalline symmetry and could occupy a large portion of the configurational space of a condensed matter system, the analogous polymorphic phenomena in structurally disordered glasses and liquids, namely, polyamorphism or liquid polymorphism, has remained far more controversial, despite decades of theoretical and experimental investigation2.

The concept of polyamorphism was first established experimentally in water by Mishima et al.3, where a low-density amorphous (LDA) and a high-density amorphous (HDA) ice were discovered under high pressure and low temperature. These amorphous states were proposed to originate from a hidden first-order liquid-to-liquid transition (LLT) in the supercooled water, i.e., liquid polymorphism, a hypothesis that has been invoked to rationalize many of water’s anomalous thermodynamic properties4. Subsequently, liquid polymorphism has been reported in some covalent-bonded, network-forming systems, including phosphorus5, silicon6,7, sulfur8, silica9,10, and oxides11,12. With the recent application of advanced diagnostic probes such as inelastic X-ray scattering and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy under extreme conditions, insights into how oxide glasses densify under compression have been substantially advanced, particularly in terms of electronic structure and network entanglement13,14. These earlier advances provided exciting and novel experimental and theoretical results that have improved prospects for understanding polymorphism and polyamorphic transitions in covalent-bonded glasses. Nevertheless, different perspectives remain, highlighting potential challenges in interpreting the observed structural changes. Several important questions are still under active discussion and may benefit from further quantification. These questions include whether true liquid and glass polymorphism exist in experimentally accessible regimes15–17 or whether the glass polymorphism instead reflects non-equilibrium mechanical collapse of an “over-strained” crystal18,19, the long-standing enigma of the glass transition in amorphous silicon20, the validity and generality of the two-liquid model for glass polymorphism21–24, and critically, the existence of a general causal relationship between liquid and glass polymorphism25–28.

Addressing these issues is essential for developing a unified thermodynamic and kinetic understanding of liquids, glasses, and their transitions9,29. Progress has been mainly hindered by serious challenges, including the metastability of the supercooled liquids and glasses against nanocrystallization and/or phase separation, partial melting or chemical reaction in liquids at high temperatures, and limited in situ diagnostics at extreme pressure-temperature (P-T) conditions2,30. As a result, it has remained difficult to unambiguously disentangle intrinsic polyamorphic behavior from extrinsic artifacts. A long-sought experimental benchmark is therefore the direct preparation of distinct, fully amorphous glass states quenched to ambient conditions from structurally distinct stable liquid states above the melting temperature at different pressures31,32. Such an approach would enable comprehensive characterization of the resulting distinct glasses at ambient conditions, and provide reciprocal insight into the nature of the parent liquid states11,33.

Most experimentally established cases of liquid and glass polymorphism involve open network structures and are accompanied by pronounced changes in coordination number, bond angles, or bonding character34,35. Metallic liquids and metallic glasses (MGs), by contrast, are densely packed and dominated by non-directional metallic bonding, offering a comparatively simpler platform for interrogating more general forms of polyamorphism. However, it is only within the past two decades that pressure-induced polyamorphic transitions have been reported in MG systems, followed by reports of LLT in metallic melts at elevated temperatures36–56. These studies in metallic systems have focused either on glass polymorphism or on liquid polymorphism alone, lacking direct and unambiguous connections between distinct liquid states and their corresponding glassy states.

In this work, we address this gap by combining a double-sided laser heating in diamond anvil cells (DACs) with in situ synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) to explore the P-T space of two prototypical MGs, Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 and Mg61Cu28Gd11. By accessing stable liquid states over a wide P-T range (up to ~30 GPa and ~2600 K), we demonstrate that structurally distinct metallic liquids can be directly quenched into distinct, chemically homogeneous glasses. This strategy enables a direct experimental assessment of the correspondence between liquid and glass polymorphism in densely packed metallic systems, while avoiding many of the long-standing ambiguities that have hindered progress in this field.

Results and discussion

Phase stability and transition pathways at high pressure and high temperatures can differ markedly from those at ambient conditions. Therefore, reliable identification of a material’s state in an unexplored P-T space requires in situ structural probes. Figure 1d shows representative in situ laser heating XRD patterns of Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 collected at 29.3 GPa. At room temperature (RT), only a broad amorphous peak from the MG and the sharp Bragg peaks from the pressure-transmitting medium (NaCl) can be observed. Upon heating, extra sharp Bragg peaks emerge at ~1070 K, indicating crystallization. As a quaternary alloy, Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MG’s crystallization behavior is rather complex even at ambient pressure, so we did not attempt to index the observed Bragg peaks, as most of the potential crystalline phases lack established reference data at high-pressure and high-temperature conditions. The onset temperature is substantially higher than the ambient crystallization temperature (Tx ≈ 653 K), underscoring the need for in situ structural probing. Melting initiates near ~1380 K, and the crystallized sample becomes fully molten above ~1640 K, as evidenced by the complete disappearance of its crystalline Bragg peaks. Once a fully molten state is confirmed by XRD, rapid melt-quenching is implemented (details shown in Methods).

A comparison of XRD patterns before heating, in the liquid state (~1640 K), and after melt-quenching reveals unexpected structural signature (Fig. 1e). Specifically, although the diffraction peak intensity decreases obviously by heating as expected, the position of the principal diffraction peak, q1, of the liquid at ~1640 K is almost identical to that of the as-spun MG before laser heating (300 K), showing an anomalously small thermal expansion effect. Here, q1 is used as an empirical structural metric that correlates with average atomic density established in metallic liquids and glasses57. After quenching, the recovered sample remains fully amorphous, showing only diffuse scattering from the MG and Bragg peaks from NaCl, yet its q1 shifts to higher values (from ~3.01 to ~3.05 Å−1) and the principal diffraction peak becomes more intense compared with the initial MG at the same pressure. These changes indicate a quenched amorphous state with considerably reduced average interatomic spacing and enhanced short-/medium-range order relative to the as-spun MG. Notably, after heating and quenching, the local pressure near the sample decreases slightly (~1.7 GPa), as inferred from the NaCl peak shift and the reported equation of state (EOS) of the NaCl B2 phase58, whereas regions away from the hotspot show no measurable pressure change. In addition, this local pressure drop was not detectable after heating-quenching cycles if no noticeable shift in q1 occurs. The concurrent observation of a small local pressure drop in the pressure medium and a higher q1 value in the quenched glass is consistent with a local volume redistribution after heating-quenching (i.e., densification of the quenched glass relative to its surroundings).

These features are reproducible over a wide pressure range in the Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MGs (Supplementary Fig. 1). The experimental protocol is summarized in Fig. 2a, and the entire pathway (1–4) is tracked by in situ synchrotron XRD. To quantify the structural changes, the q1 and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the principal peak of MG and liquid were fitted using the Voigt function with a linear background (examples are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2). During room temperature compression of the as-spun MG (pathway 1), q1 increases smoothly with pressure (open blue squares in Fig. 2b), with no evidence of any first-order structural transition, consistent with the prior report59. However, when the MG sample is fully melted at high pressures (pathway 2), the measured q1 of liquids at ~1600 K (Fig. 2b, open green stars) shows no shift compared with that of the as-spun MG before heating, counter to the shift expected from normal thermal expansion. Using an extrapolated thermal-expansion baseline for the as-spun MG60 and the high-pressure-quenched MG in our work (calculation details are provided in Methods and Supplementary Fig. 3), the q1 values anticipated for a “normal” liquid at ~1600 K fall into a lower-q1 band as a function of pressure (gray region in Fig. 2e), substantially below the values observed in the high-pressure melt above ~20 GPa. Consistent with this classification, the glasses obtained by quenching from the HDL (pathway 3) systematically exhibit unusually large q1 values compared with the as-spun MG at the same pressure (Fig. 2c, open red circles; ~1.3% increase). Combining measured q1 of the quenched glass with the established thermal-expansion baseline at high pressures yields inferred q1 of its parent liquid at ~1600 K, which closely coincides with the observed HDL values (details in Methods and Supplementary Fig. 3). This quantitative consistency supports the direct correspondence between the “unusual” HDL-quenched MGs and the HDL, most likely through a glass transition during quenching at high pressure. During subsequent decompression at room temperature (pathway 4), a kink emerges at ~20 GPa in the pressure dependence of q1 (Fig. 2d, solid red circles), indicative of a transition between distinct amorphous states. After complete decompression, the recovered glass still retains a higher q1 than the as-spun MG at ambient pressure (Fig. 2d, open purple diamonds; ~0.7% increase), reproducible across multiple experiments.

Although direct accurate density determination is challenging for micron-scale DAC samples, diffraction peak shifts can be readily used to estimate relative density changes61. We adopt an empirical relationship established for MGs linking amorphous volume to the principal diffuse peak position61,62, V∝(1/q1)2.5. This scaling yields an estimated average density enhancement of ~3.1% for the HDL-quenched MG relative to the as-spun MG between 20–30 GPa, and a retained enhancement of ~1.7% after decompression to ambient conditions. Based on the estimated density differences, for discussion, we denote the HDL-quenched state at high pressure as ultra-high-density amorphous (uHDA), the recovered ambient-pressure counterpart as high-density amorphous (HDA), and the initial as-spun glass as low-density amorphous (LDA). Unlike the covalent amorphous materials with open network structures, in densely packed MGs, where nearest-neighbor coordination number (CN) is already high (typically CN ~ 12–14)63, even complete crystallization commonly produces density increases less than ~1%64. The substantially larger densification observed here also exceeds values reported for long-time ambient-pressure annealing (<0.5%)65 or thermal treatments under pressure (~0.6–1%)66–68 in comparable bulk MGs, indicating that uHDA and HDA are not accessible via conventional relaxation pathways59,60.

Mechanical property measurements further support the distinctness of the recovered HDA state. The recovered HDA MG is more incompressible than the as-spun MG, with bulk moduli of 175 ± 4 and 166 ± 3 GPa, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4, ~5% increase) consistent with a denser arrested structure. Similar results have also been observed in another prototype MG, Mg61Cu28Gd11, which exhibits a comparable pressure threshold (~18 GPa) and an estimated density increase of ~2.9% after high-pressure melt-quenching (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that closely coupled liquid-glass polymorphic behavior may be generally accessible in densely packed MG systems via pathways in P-T phase space.

A key question is whether the liquid-side crossover (or LLT) can be observed more directly under isothermal conditions. True isothermal compression above the liquidus is challenging in laser-heated DAC experiments. Alternatively, we therefore approximate an isothermal compression path by extracting liquid XRD patterns at matched temperatures from multiple isobaric heating runs. Repeated re-melting of the same sample at different pressures enables robust comparison of q1, peak width, and peak intensity with a nearly constant background (Supplementary Fig. 6). When these liquid data (from 1.6 GPa to 21.7 GPa, at ~1600 ± 100 K) are mapped onto Fig. 2e (solid green stars), the liquids below ~10 GPa fall within the gray area of the LDL band, whereas the liquid at higher pressure coincides with the HDL branch. Concurrent anomalies in peak position (q1, Fig. 2e), peak intensity, and peak width (Fig. 2f) between ~10 and 15 GPa provide consistent evidence for a pressure-driven liquid structural crossover between LDL-like and HDL-like states in Pd40Ni10Cu30P20. Notably, the characteristic pressure range mirrors that of the amorphous-amorphous transition observed in the high-pressure melt-quenched MG upon decompression, supporting a close correspondence between the liquid and glass polymorphic states accessed here.

Moreover, at fixed pressures, it is found that the HDL could transform toward an LDL at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 7). Based on the ensemble of multiple in situ XRD experiments, temperature-pressure metastability boundaries between the various states in the Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 system can be proposed (Supplementary Fig. 8). The liquidus temperature increases monotonically with pressure. The HDL-LDL boundary shows a positive slope. Above a critical pressure (~15–20 GPa), Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 first melts into an HDL-like state and then evolves toward an LDL-like state at higher temperature, whereas rapid melt-quenching at fixed pressure always ends up with a uHDA glass on experimental timescales due to extremely fast dynamics of liquid states (Supplementary Fig. 9). Nevertheless, it should be noted that even with indistinguishable static atomic structures, atomic dynamic behaviors may differ depending on thermomechanical history69–72. Along this HDL-LDL boundary, a second-critical point8 may exist in liquids at high enough pressures, which, however, needs further exploration to clarify in the future.

Because polyamorphism claims are often confounded by nanocrystallization, phase separation, or chemical reactions, we performed extensive diagnostic measurements on the recovered HDA MGs to further address these issues. Synchrotron XRD (Fig. 3), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), selected area electron diffraction (SAED) (Fig. 4a), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping in both TEM (Fig. 4b) and SEM (Supplementary Table 1), three-dimensional atom probe tomography (3DAPT) (Fig. 4c), and reduced pair distribution function, G(r), from SAED (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 10) consistently show a fully amorphous structure and chemical homogeneity within the resolution of each method of the HDA MGs and thus correspondingly of its parent states, i.e., the uHDA and HDL states. As an internal control, the initial as-spun LDA MG was also characterized to be spatially homogeneous in compositions and fully glassy in structures by HRTEM, SAED, and 3DAPT with identical experimental protocols (Supplementary Fig. 11). Moreover, Flash DSC measurement of the recovered HDA sample reveals a clear glass transition signal (Fig. 5a), confirming the glassy nature of the sample. Although recovered HDA sample is stable at ambient conditions, in situ high-temperature synchrotron XRD measurement on the recovered HDA sample at ambient pressure (Supplementary Fig. 12) reveals a well-defined irreversible transition from HDA to LDA. As shown in Fig. 5b, by tracking q1 of the HDA sample as a function of temperature, two distinct glass states characterized by distinct linear thermal expansion behavior can be identified below ~350 K and above ~500 K, separated by a clear transition region in between. Notably, this transition occurs within the temperature region (350–500 K) far below the glass transition temperature of the as-spun Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MG (~560 K). More importantly, in contrast to the typical upward deviation due to relaxation-induced densification near Tg57,73, the q1 value in our study shows a downward deviation between 350 K and 500 K. This expansion-like behavior precludes structural relaxation or the glass transition as the underlying mechanism, pointing instead to a thermally activated polyamorphic transition from the HDA to the LDA state. During cooling, the system remains in the LDA state without reverting to its initial HDA state, suggesting that the transition is irreversible at ambient pressure. This suggests that the HDA state represents a higher-energy metastable configuration, accessible only via HDL quenching and a following decompressing process. Such a higher-density yet higher-energy state is a characteristic feature of many recovered high-pressure metastable phases, similar to the relationship between diamond and graphite. In MG systems, achieving structurally denser but energetically higher states have also been reported to be feasible following specific processing treatments69,74–76. Although multiple amorphous states were reported before in network-forming systems where coordination number and bonding change substantially (e.g., ice77–79 by experiments and silicon by simulations80,81), to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time to experimentally reveal three different polyamorphic states in densely packed MG systems. In summary, by combining in situ high-pressure and high-temperature synchrotron XRD with controlled melt-quenching and comprehensive ex situ diagnostics, we show that structurally distinct liquid states exist in multicomponent metallic alloys and can be quenched into distinct, chemically homogeneous glasses at different pressures, thereby establishing an explicit correspondence between liquid and glass polymorphism. These results demonstrate that such polyamorphism is not restricted to network-forming, strongly directional systems, but more generally exists even in simple, densely packed alloys. By providing a protocol-robust platform that tracks the full liquid-to-glass pathway while ruling out nanocrystallization, phase separation, and chemical heterogeneity, this work provides solid experimental evidence for promoting the understanding of liquid-glass relationships within an energy-landscape framework in metallic systems.

Methods

Sample preparation

The Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 (at.%) MG ribbons with a thickness of ~30 μm were prepared by single-roller melt spinning of the master alloy ingot using a quartz tube. The Mg61Cu28Gd11 (at. %) MG ribbons were prepared by single-roller melt spinning of the Cu-Gd intermediate alloy and Mg metals (about 5 wt.% excessive amount of Mg was added) using a graphite tube. The two alloys are employed as model systems because they have been extensively investigated as good glass formers with homogeneous amorphous structures and moderate melting temperatures82,83.

In situ high-pressure high-temperature synchrotron XRD

The MG ribbon sample was first polished with a thickness down to ~15 μm and then cut into cylinders with a diameter of ~20 μm by Focused Ion Beam (FIB). The cylinder sample was loaded into a symmetric DAC with a culet size of 300 or 400 μm to perform in situ high-pressure high-temperature XRD experiments. Rhenium was used as the gasket material, typically pre-indented to ~20 GPa and then drilled by laser to form a hole of ~100 μm in diameter as a sample chamber. NaCl, KCl, or MgO was loaded as the pressure-transmitting medium and pressure standard, as well as a chemically inert thermal insulator between samples and diamond anvils. Tiny ruby balls were also loaded near the samples as the pressure calibrant using the ruby fluorescence scale84. The MG sample is embedded in the pressure transmission media. Diamond and pressure-transmitting media materials are pretty transparent to both the high-energy X-ray and the optical laser, which enables the collection of XRD patterns during laser heating under high pressure. To guarantee the fully melting of the entire sample, the sample size must be smaller than the laser hot spot, thus, FIB is used to cut samples accurately with designed sizes during sample preparation. The in situ high-pressure high-temperature synchrotron XRD experiments were mainly carried out at the beamline 13-ID-D at Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL). A monochromatic X-ray beam with a wavelength of 0.3344 Å was used with a beam size of ~2.5 × 3.5 μm2. Two-dimensional (2D) diffraction images were collected with a Mar165 CCD detector and a Pilatus 1 M CdTe detector in multiple experimental runs. The software Dioptas85 was used to integrate the 2D images into 1D patterns. A double-sided YLF (wavelength 1064 nm) laser-heating system was used to heat the sample with a focused laser beam size of ~25 μm in diameter, which is comparable to our sample size. Temperatures were determined by fitting the thermal radiation spectra of the heated sample to the Planck radiation function in a given wavelength range. We first compressed the samples to a targeted pressure, then gradually increased the temperature while carefully keeping the temperatures on both sides balanced by adjusting the laser power. Two different laser heating methods were used. In the first method, a typical laser heating lasted 2 seconds at a given laser power for XRD acquisition. After that, the laser was automatically turned off, the sample cooled to room temperature immediately, and another XRD pattern was taken at room temperature. We then manually increased the laser power and performed another XRD measurement during heating. By gradually increasing the laser power in a stepwise manner, the sample was confirmed by in situ XRD to be fully melted within 2 s at a certain power level. The second method was a continuous laser heating method, in which we gradually increased the laser power while the laser remained shining on the sample. Starting with low power, we took XRD patterns after each manual power increase until a fully molten state was achieved. After that, the laser beam was suddenly shut off manually, and the sample was quenched from a high-temperature liquid state to room temperature under high pressure. The exact cooling rate is not measurable in our setups, but the exceptional glass-forming abilities of both Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 and Mg61Cu28Gd11 enable quenching fully amorphous states within the DACs. Finally, the pressure was gradually released at room temperature to check the recovered sample and for further characterization using various modern diagnostic probes outside of the DAC.

In situ high-pressure synchrotron XRD at room temperature

In situ high-pressure synchrotron XRD experiment on the Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MGs was performed at the beamline 12.2.2 at Advanced Light Source (ALS) in Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) with the x-ray wavelength of 0.4959 Å and the beam size of ~10 × 10 μm2. Rhenium was used as the gasket material. Two samples, an as-spun LDA MG and a recovered HDA MG, were loaded together in the same sample chamber in a symmetric DAC with a culet size of 400 μm. Helium was loaded as the pressure medium to ensure an identical hydrostatic pressure environment for both samples, and tiny ruby balls were used as the pressure calibrant.

In situ high-temperature synchrotron XRD at ambient pressure

To invest the relative energy states of HDA and LDA, in situ high-temperature synchrotron XRD experiment on the recovered HDA Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MGs was performed at the beamline BL15U1 at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) with the x-ray wavelength of 0.6199 Å and the beam size of ~3.0 × 4.5 μm2. The HDA Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MG was heated continuously at a rate of 20 K per minute in a Linkam HFS600E-PB4 heating stage with flowing ultrahigh purity Ar atmosphere. The exposure time was set to 5 s, therefore, the temperature resolution of each XRD pattern was around 1.7 K. The 2D diffraction images were collected continuously during heating by a Mar165 CCD detector.

Synchrotron XRD at ambient conditions

Some synchrotron XRD experiments for characterizing the amorphous nature of the uHDA and HDA MGs prepared by an off-line in-house laser heating system were performed at the beamline BL15U1 at SSRF with the x-ray wavelength of 0.6199 Å and the beam size of ~3.0 × 4.5 μm2 and the beamline BL10XU at Spring-8 with x-ray wavelength of 0.4147 Å and the beam size of ~2 × 2 μm2.

HRTEM

The TEM samples were prepared by a focused ion beam/scanning electron microscopy (FIB/SEM) dual-beam system (FEI Helios), combined with Gatan Ion milling. To remove the surface contamination layer and to achieve an ultra-thin layer for HRTEM imaging, a low voltage (1–0.5 kV) accelerated ion beam was used for final cleaning. HRTEM, SAED, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), and STEM-EDS mapping were all acquired using an FEI Titan G2 microscope operated at 300 kV. The microscope is equipped with an aberration corrector for the probe-forming lens and a Bruker SuperX EDX spectrometer. Electron diffraction patterns for PDF analysis were acquired directly on a charge-coupled device camera with a camera length of 245 mm. Exposure time is 0.5 s for each pattern, and 10 patterns were collected and averaged for better statistics. The patterns of thin protective layers of Pt along the samples were also collected as the polycrystalline calibration standard. The reduced pair distribution functions, G(r), were extracted from the 2D electron diffraction patterns using the software packages of eRDF Analyser86.

Large area EDS analysis in SEM of a Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 HDA MG recovered from 30.9 GPa was performed in a scanning electron microscope (FEI Versa) at five different spots.

3DAPT

3DAPT analyses were carried out both on a Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 HDA MG recovered from 27.6 GPa and the as-spun LDA MG. Needle-shaped samples were prepared by FIB milling (FEI Helios 600i). 3DAPT measurements were performed on a local electrode atom probe (CAMECA LEAP 4000 HR) to obtain the spatial distribution of the constituent elements. The data were acquired at 50 K under ultra-high vacuum (4.5 × 10−11 Torr) with a pulse fraction of 0.2 at a repetition rate of 200 kHz. The data analysis and 3D atomic reconstruction were performed with the IVAS 3.6.2 software.

Flash DSC measurement

Calorimetric properties of the tiny recovered Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 HDA MGs from high-pressure and high-temperature treatment were measured by a Flash DSC system (Mettler Toledo DSC2+) from 183 K to 973 K with a heating and cooling rate of 5000 K/s and 10,000 K/s, respectively.

Thermal expansion calculations of MGs and liquids

It was reported that the q1 of the as-spun Pd40Ni10Cu30P20 MG shifts almost linearly with temperature from glass to liquid with a thermal coefficient, α, of approximately −4.71 × 10−5 Å−1K−1 at ambient pressure60. In our work, α of a high-pressure-liquid-quenched MG (uHDA) and its liquids at ~27.7 GPa was also determined to be constant at approximately −2.63 × 10−5 Å−1K−1 over the studied temperature range (up to ~1700 K) by in situ laser heating XRD measurement (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, the q1 of a liquid at any given temperature can be calculated from the initial q1 of the MG using a simple linear relationship, q1(T) = q1(MG at 300 K) + α × (T-300), here α varies from −2.63 × 10−5 to −4.71 × 10−5 Å−1K−1 depending on the pressure and sample states.
