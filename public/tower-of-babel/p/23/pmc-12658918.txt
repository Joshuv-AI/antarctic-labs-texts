Dip‐in Nanomechanical Probes for Mass Characterization of Nanoparticles in Ultra‐Low Volume Liquid Suspensions
Authors: Juan Molina, Javier E. Escobar, Priscila M. Kosaka, Óscar Malvar, José J. Ruz, Javier Tamayo, Álvaro San Paulo, Montserrat Calleja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mass of nanoparticles has a critical influence on their phenomenology and functional performance, driving the need for measurement tools that combine high‐throughput with high‐resolution single‐particle analysis. Mass characterization of large nanoparticle populations under such demands remains a persistent challenge, particularly for nanoparticles of large mass, where consolidated techniques encounter limitations. Here, a dip‐in nanomechanical probe sensing approach for weighing nanoparticles in liquid suspensions which simultaneously provides high mass resolution and sensing rate is demonstrated. The method relies on nanoparticle transfer to nanowire resonator probes via iterative immersion into the suspension, requiring minimal volume and resulting in negligible losses. It also introduces ionic liquids as suspension media, enabling vacuum operation for optimizing mass resolution through single‐mode resonance frequency tracking of the probes. Additionally, implementing acoustofluidic actuation on the suspension regulates nanoparticle adsorption/desorption during immersion, significantly enhancing the sensing rate. Measurements of 30 nm gold nanoparticles demonstrate accurate characterization of their mass distribution with a probe resolution of 0.1 ag, a sensing rate of 38 NPs min−1, and a sample volume of a few nanoliters. The concurrent advances in all these performance characteristics reveal the potential of dip‐in nanomechanical probes for precise and fast characterization of individual nanoparticles over a wide mass range.

Introduction

Nanoparticles (NPs) are key enabling materials across multiple scientific disciplines and applications.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Their most elementary physical properties dictate their interactions with different environments, and ultimately define their functionality. Understanding and controlling these properties requires the availability of characterization techniques suitable for the analysis of NPs of diverse nature, including solid inorganic and polymer nanoparticles, core–shell nanostructures, polymeric micelles, exosomes, liposomes, virus‐like particles, etc.[ 4 , 5 , 6 ] Mass is a fundamental quantity for the analysis of any type of NP. It directly influences physical phenomena such as diffusion, aggregation, sedimentation, thermal transport or mechanical properties.[ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] Also, mass correlates with size, accounting for the whole NP volume and associated density regardless of shape, thus serving as an essential parameter for a comprehensive analysis of size effects. These effects are particularly significant in the interactions between NPs and biological systems. On the one hand, engineered NPs enable otherwise unfeasible technologies in biomedicine, providing unique approaches for interacting with target cells for the delivery of drugs or genetic material, photothermal actuation or imaging purposes.[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] NP mass is crucial in these applications because it influences biodistribution, tissue permeability, cellular uptake and retention, pharmacokinetics, and imaging mechanisms.[ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] On the other hand, NPs pose acknowledged risks in terms of toxicity and environmental impact. Although no physical mechanism can be considered universal in this regard, NP mass determines their interaction with biota through the same paths harnessed for biomedical applications,[ 19 , 20 ] either directly interfering with the growth and behavior of exposed organisms or triggering the effects of co‐occurring contaminants.[ 21 , 22 ] The mass of some naturally occurring NPs is also of great relevance in regard of human health. A paradigmatic example is that of viruses. The mass of aerosolized particles is a crucial factor on the effectiveness of airborne virus transmission,[ 23 , 24 ] which has been recognized as the primary transmission route of SARS‐COV‐2.[ 25 ]

NPs are prone to yield polydisperse populations with highly variable distributions of their properties, which implies the need of accurate characterization methods ideally capable of combining high‐resolution individual NP data collection with high‐throughput performance.[ 5 ] The mass of NPs typically ranges from the picogram scale, for dense metallic NPs with submicron dimensions, down to the zeptogram scale, for low‐density NPs of a few nanometers such as organic or polymer NPs. High mass resolution is essential not only for accurately measuring the mass of individual NPs, but also for reliably characterizing the mass distribution across large NP populations, which implies assessing the degree of mass dispersion within such populations. Consequently, zeptogram resolution is ideally required to effectively span the mass range of NPs. NP mass can be indirectly estimated from size measurements using various high‐resolution methods, with microscopy and light scattering techniques among the most relevant.[ 4 , 5 ] However, describing NPs simply by their diameter often fails to capture critical size effects, especially for NPs that are irregular, hollow, or compositionally heterogeneous. Complementarily, the mass of NPs can be specifically assessed through a few techniques.[ 5 , 26 ] Mass spectrometry (MS) is currently the reference technique for determining the mass, composition, and stoichiometry of nanoparticulate analytes. Although originally conceived for the characterization of macromolecules after fragmentation, the introduction of soft ionization approaches, together with the integration of detection methods able to reach the megadalton (or attogram) range, has extended the application of MS to the analysis of intact NPs. These novel approaches, including charge detection mass spectrometry or inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry,[ 27 ] preserve the sensitivity to the mass/charge ratio of the analytes and the need of sample ionization, which challenges their performance for large NPs that ionize poorly.[ 26 ]

Nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS) offer a unique potential for direct NP mass sensing across a broad range of materials and mass scale.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] NEMS consist of nanomechanical resonators, usually operating in flexural modes, through which the mass of adsorbed NPs is determined regardless of their charge state by harnessing mass‐induced resonance frequency shifts.[ 32 ] A major challenge using NEMS is the transfer of NPs to their surface, given that NPs are typically generated, stored or functional in liquid suspensions. The response of resonators largely degrades in liquids, which has driven the development of different approaches for transferring NPs while circumventing this issue. The two most consolidated methods are based on either hollow resonators, through which NP suspensions can flow, or on the use of nebulization techniques such as electrospray ionization (ESI), which allows the transport of the NPs from the suspensions to conventional solid resonators. Suspended microchannel and nanochannel resonators (SNRs) are hollow beams fabricated by clean‐room processing techniques.[ 28 ] The possibility to operate them in a vacuum allows to optimize mass resolution, which has been pushed to the attogram scale by device miniaturization,[ 33 ] read‐out optimization,[ 34 , 35 ] and advanced data processing,[ 36 ] whereas parallel readout of SNR arrays has provided optimization of throughput performance.[ 37 ] Similarly, transparent microcapillary resonators consist on hollow cylinders obtained by stretching glass capillary tubes,[ 38 , 39 ] implemented as suspended doubly clamped beams that enable optical access to the inner channel.[ 40 ] Complex fabrication and operation, limited size‐reduction, inherent risks to enclosed microfluidics, and buoyant mass sensitivity (rather than inertial mass) limit the performance and evolution of these technologies for NP characterization. Nebulization‐based approaches follow three steps: nebulization of a sample suspension into electrically charged droplets, liberation of NPs from droplets, and transport of the NPs from the atmospheric pressure ionization source to the surface of the NEMS, usually placed in a vacuum. Nebulization enables a versatile approach for inertial mass sensing that can be used with resonators of different geometry, size, and readout schemes, and has been demonstrated for mass measurements of NPs of different natures.[ 29 , 31 , 41 , 42 , 43 ] The difficulty of improving resolution while simultaneously optimizing sensing rate (number of NPs sensed per unit time) represents a significant challenge for this approach. This is a consequence of both capture area and mass resolution scaling with resonator size, such that pursuing high‐resolution by means of device miniaturization leads to reducing capture area. Recently, electrostatic focusing has been demonstrated to enhance the capture of electrosprayed NPs on the surface of NEMS, enabling operation at atmospheric pressure.[ 44 , 45 ] Atmospheric pressure operation represents an advantage in terms of setup simplification, but it hinders the accuracy and resolution of mass measurements. The application of ESI to NP mass characterization with NEMS has allowed to reach sensing rates of a few NP min−1 with a resolution of a few attograms in vacuum. Electrostatic focusing at atmospheric pressure improves sensing rate to tens of NP min−1, but it degrades resolution by a significant factor. Both hollow resonators and nebulization‐based technologies have unveiled the potential of nanomechanical sensing for NP mass characterization, but significant improvements in terms of combined high resolution and throughput are still necessary in order to fully exploit such potential and cover the broadest possible range of NPs with sufficient accuracy and precision.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for measuring the mass of NPs in suspension based on the transfer of NPs to the surface of Si nanowire (NW) resonators by controlled immersion of the free end of the NW into the suspension. The transfer is governed by the adsorption/desorption of the NPs on the NW surface, enabling single‐nanoparticle measurements, high‐throughput sensing and suitable application to ultra‐low volumes with negligible sample loss. Two key elements for optimizing this approach are the use of an ionic liquid (IL) as fluid media for the suspension and the application of acoustofluidic actuation to the suspension during the immersions. ILs are fluids of great interest given their widely tunable properties,[ 46 ] and have already shown advantageous interactions with diverse nanoparticulate analytes, including metal NPs,[ 47 ] proteins[ 48 , 49 ] and viruses.[ 50 ] The extremely low vapor pressure of ILs enables vacuum operation of our approach,[ 51 ] pushing resolution into the sub‐attogram range. Acoustofluidic technologies provide powerful methodologies to manipulate the behavior of particles suspended in liquids, and have demonstrated a transformative impact for biomedical applications.[ 52 , 53 , 54 ] In our case, acoustofluidic actuation optimizes the NP dispersion in the IL, ensuring adsorption/desorption predominantly one NP at a time, and allowing high‐throughput measurements with a sensing rate reaching several tens of NP min−1. We demonstrate our approach oby measuring the mass distribution of 30 nm diameter Au NPs dispersed in nanoliter‐scale droplets of an IL, and develop a theoretical framework that allows to understand, optimize, and anticipate further improvements of the method.

Results and Discussion

Instrumental Setup and Experimental Methodology

The nanomechanical probes used in this work consist of single‐clamp flexural beam resonators constituted by Si NWs vertically grown by the vapor‐liquid‐solid (VLS) mechanism on raw Si substrates (Figure 1 ).[ 55 ] The fabrication process involves the use of catalyst Au NPs of either 150 or 250 nm of specified diameter, which remain at the tip of the NWs after growth as gold‐silicon oxide core–shell NPs with reduced diameter (Experimental Section).[ 56 ] The typical dimensions of the NW resonators are 35–50 µm in length, 150–400 nm in base diameter, and 30–250 nm in tip diameter. The instrumental setup consists of two stages (Figure 1a), one for resonance readout and another for micro‐positioning. The readout scheme is based on laser beam scattering modulation with laser incidence perpendicular to the NWs (Experimental Section).[ 57 ] The NW resonators have a resonance frequency of their fundamental flexural mode in the 200–300 kHz range, a quality factor of 10 000–30 000, and a frequency stability (Allan deviation) down to 0.16 ppm at 1 Hz measurement bandwidth. We use commercial Au NPs with a specified diameter of 30 nm as model sample analytes. We have tested their size distribution by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), which ratifies a spherical shape and a normal distribution of size with an average diameter of 30 nm and a standard deviation of 2.4 nm (Supporting Information). Considering the density of gold (19.3 g cm−3), the resulting nominal average mass is mNOM = 273 ag with a standard deviation σ NOM = 65 ag. We use a hydrophobic IL, either DEME‐TFSI or BMP‐TFSI, as fluid media for the suspension of the NPs with a concentration of ≈1013 NP mL−1 (Experimental Section).

Instrumental setup and experimental methodology. a) Schematic depiction of the instrumental setup used for NW resonator transduction and for controlled dip‐in probing of a suspension of NP analytes in an IL nanoliter‐scale droplet. b) SEM image of a Si NW resonator in the proximity of an IL droplet; scale bar 5 µm. c,d) SEM images of an NW resonator dipping into an IL droplet; scale bar 5 µm in (c), 500 nm in (d). e) Schematic sequence of the three‐step measurement process: 1) beginning of fundamental mode resonance frequency tracking; 2) immersion of the free‐end of the NW resonator into the IL suspension; 3) resonance frequency shift measurements, with three possible results depending on the net balance of adsorption–desorption of the sampled NPs (no shift, downshift, upshift).

The micro‐positioning stage, implemented in high vacuum (10−5 mbar), allows the operation of the NW resonators as dip‐in probes, enabling controlled immersion of the free end of the NWs into a NP suspension droplet. The hemispherical droplet diameter is ≈250 µm, which corresponds to a volume of 4.1 nL. The droplet support is a Pt‐Ir microwire 250 µm in diameter and 1.2 mm in length attached to a small piezo‐actuator for acoustofluidic actuation on the droplet (Experimental Section). The whole micro‐positioning stage is modular, and it was installed into the sample chamber of a SEM in order to check the control of the immersion of the NWs into the IL droplet (Figure 1b–d). Note that SEM imaging is not needed for immersion control during mass measurements, which is provided by a CCD camera integrated in the optical readout setup. The length of the NW immersed into the IL is limited to 1 µm with an error of 10%. In previous studies, we used a similar configuration to study the effect of a bias voltage between the NW and the IL with no suspended analytes.[ 58 ] For immersions at zero‐bias voltage, no flow of IL along the NW is observed. However, upon the first immersion, the IL wets the NW tip where the catalyst NP remains. Subsequent immersions produce small liquid exchanges between the IL‐coated NW tip and the IL reservoir, resulting in mass increments with zero average and a dispersion in the range of 1–10 ag, depending on the NW tip diameter.[ 59 ] All experiments presented in this work were performed at zero bias voltage, so that these liquid mass fluctuations represent an effective mass detection limit.

The experimental methodology is schematically represented in Figure 1e. The measurement process follows three steps: First, the resonance frequency of the fundamental flexural mode of the NW resonator is measured for a time interval Δtmsr with the NW retracted; second, the NW free end is immersed into the droplet for a time interval Δtdip , during which NPs can adsorb or desorb, producing a positive or negative mass increment; third, the NW is retracted and its resonance frequency is measured again, which results in either no shift (if adsorption equals desorption), a negative shift (if adsorption exceeds desorption) or a positive shift (if adsorption is less than desorption). Typical measurement and immersion times are of a few seconds, and this three‐step sequence can be iterated for a large number of immersion events by using each resonance frequency measurement as the reference for a following immersion. Thus, measurements obtained from hundreds of immersion events can be obtained in a few minutes. The mass increment Δm produced in the NW as a consequence of analyte adsorption/desorption for each immersion event is calculated by the point‐mass approximation in the limit of small mass increments: (1) Δm=−2mRΔfRfR where mR is the effective mass of the resonator, fR is the resonance frequency measured before immersion, and ΔfR is the measured shift after immersion. The effective mass is calculated including the effect of cross‐sectional tapering.[ 60 ] The error of the point‐mass approximation increases as the adsorption position of NPs moves from the exact NW free end toward its base. By restricting NW immersion to ≈1 µm from the tip (≈2% of total length), we keep the accuracy of mass measurements to ≈5–10%.[ 58 ] Under the approximation expressed in Equation (1), and from the measurements of NW frequency stability, we achieve a mass resolution of 0.1 ag (Supporting Information).

Statistically relevant measurements require a large number of events. In Figure 2d–g we represent mass increment histograms obtained for series of N = 100 immersion events in different experimental conditions. For clarity, these histograms are displayed in terms of the ratio of the measured mass increment to the nominal average mass of a single NP, Δm/mNOM . The insets represent a zoom for small values of Δm/mNOM and for the same vertical scale. Figure 2d corresponds to a blank test with no NPs suspended in the IL droplet. Consistently, the histogram shows a prominent peak at zero‐mass increment, with a dispersion compatible with previous observations of liquid mass adsorption/desorption fluctuations at the NW tip.[ 59 ] In order to show the importance of acoustofluidic actuation during the immersions, we show two examples of typical results obtained in control experiments where no acoustofluidic actuation is applied. In such case, non‐uniform dispersion of NPs in the suspension leads to two possible scenarios. On the one hand, the immersion can result in null or negligible adsorption of NPs on the NW as a consequence of the absence of NPs in the proximity of the immersion point (Figure 2e). On the other hand, immersion can produce the adsorption of NP clusters resulting from their aggregation in suspension. In consequence, the obtained histogram shows a large number of short peaks that spread from zero‐mass to the mass of several tens of NPs, with a large predominance of positive increments (Figure 2f).

The introduction of acoustofluidic actuation on the NP suspension during the measurements provides optimum conditions for the adsorption/desorption of NPs. An example is shown in Figure 2g. In such conditions, the measured histograms present three main features: 1) All measured mass increments remain within a few times the nominal average mass of a single NP; 2) Well defined peaks appear centered around the mass of integer multiples of the nominal average mass of a single NP, as well as around the zero‐mass increment; 3) The two previous observations hold for both positive and negative increments, although there is a prevalence of positive mass increments. These features suggest a NP transfer mechanism dominated by the adsorption and desorption of NPs one by one. However, the net number of adsorbed or desorbed NPs during each immersion varies from zero to a few NPs, depending on the final balance between adsorption and desorption for each immersion.

In order to support the interpretation of the experimental mass increment histograms, we have performed SEM imaging of NW resonators before and after mass measurements. The results are presented in Figure 2h–k for each of the experimental conditions previously considered. Each set of three images corresponds to the NW before the immersions (left), after the immersions (center), and after the immersion with Z‐number enhanced contrast by backscattered electrons detection (right). The later provides optimized images to discern the Au NPs from any other materials present on the NW (Experimental Section).[ 56 ] Figure 2h displays an example of a blank test, showing the absence of NPs along the NW. The darker contrast at the NW tip after the immersion is interpreted as permanent localized IL wetting, as discussed earlier. Figure 2i,j corresponds to examples with no acoustofluidic actuation. In particular, Figure 2i matches a case equivalent to the histogram in Figure 2e, where only one single NP is adsorbed at the NW tip. In contrast, Figure 2j corresponds to a histogram of the type shown in Figure 2f: the observed non‐uniform coverage of the NW and the formation of NP clusters adsorbed on the NW are consistent with the measured peaks at large positive integer values of Δm/mNOM . Finally, the set of images in Figure 2k reveals that after an experiment with applied acoustofluidic actuation, the adsorbed NPs generate a more uniform coverage on the NW. These results demonstrate the importance of acoustofluidic actuation to produce a uniform NP dispersion that ensures optimum NP transfer.

Theoretical Model

The mass increment measurements performed with acoustofluidic actuation can be used to obtain information about the mass distribution of the NPs. A simple statistical model allows to understand these measurements and to optimize the experimental parameters for the acquisition of meaningful histograms. The assumptions of the model are the following. First, the probability density P of an individual NP to have a mass mNP is given by a Gaussian distribution: (2) PmNP=12πσNP2e−mNP−μNP22σNP2 where µ NP is the average NP mass and σ NP is the standard deviation. Second, individual NPs adsorb randomly one by one during the immersion time Δtdip with a mean adsorption rate ν A . Thus, the probability Pi of having an integer number i ≥ 0 of individual NP adsorption events during NW dip‐in follows a Poisson distribution: (3) PiλA=λAie−λAi! where λ A is the average adsorption number, determined from the product λ A = ν A Δtdip . Analogously, the probability Pj of desorption of an integer number j ≥ 0 of individual NPs during immersion is given by another independent Poisson distribution, characterized by constant mean desorption rate ν D : (4) PjλD=λDje−λDj! with λ D = ν D Δtdip and ν D < ν A . In order to parametrize the balance between adsorption and desorption, we introduce the desorption/adsorption ratio as r D/A = λ D / λ A = ν D /ν A . In consideration of the observed effect of acoustofluidic actuation on the mass measurements, we neglect adsorption/desorption of NP clusters. As a result of these assumptions, the expected distribution of measured mass increments is given by the combination of the three probability distributions introduced above, so that the mass histograms are composed by a series of peaks centered at positive and negative integer multiples of the average mass of a single NP, with maximum values weighted by the Poisson distributions for adsorption and desorption.

In order to determine the capability of the theoretical model to reproduce the features of the experimental data and to establish optimum parameters for precise NP mass distribution analysis, we have performed Monte Carlo simulations based on the assumptions of the model. In such calculations, we estimate the frequency of mass increments resulting from a number N of simulated immersions for each of which the numbers i and j of adsorbed and desorbed NPs are randomly generated from Poisson distributions with fixed λ A and λ D , respectively, and the mass corresponding to these NPs is randomly generated from a Gaussian distribution with fixed average mass µ NP and standard deviation σ NP (Experimental Section). Figure 3 shows simulated histograms of the normalized mass increments Δm/mNOM (with mNOM = µ NP ), performed as a function of the total number on immersion events N (Figure 3a–d), the coefficient of variation σ∼NP=σNP/μNP (Figure 3e–h), the adsorption number λ A (Figure 3i–l) and the desorption/adsorption ratio r D/A (Figure 3m–p).

Default values are N = 1000, σ∼NP = 0.2, λ A = 2, r D/A = 0.8, as represented in (c).

In general, most of the simulated histograms reproduce well the main features of the experimental measurements, showing a series of peaks associated to positive and negative integer multiples of µ NP emerging from a bell‐shaped background that extends to a few times µ NP . However, specific trends are observed for the variation of each parameter. As N increases, the peaks become less prominent, but their position seats more precisely at integer multiples of µ NP . For increasing σ∼NP, the height of the peaks above the background decreases, and their position is not affected. For σ∼NP approaching unity, the peaks vanish except for the zero‐mass increment. λ A contributes to the number of adsorption events, so that an increasing value implies the presence of more peaks. However, the peaks are sharper for lower values of λ A , and higher values result in a wider and more dominant bell‐shaped background, which eventually hides the peaks. Thus, intermediate values of λ A produce a good compromise between the number of peaks and their sharpness. Finally, the main effect of the ratio r D/A is to modify the symmetry of the histograms around the zero‐mass increment, so that the closer to unity, the more symmetric.

The simulations provide valuable insights for optimizing experimental data acquisition and interpretation. We consider histograms optimal when their peaks are well‐defined and seat precisely at integer multiples of µ NP . The experimental parameters that can be tuned to obtain optimal histograms are N and λ A . Figure 3a–d evidences that the larger N, the more accurately the histograms represent the underlying distributions. However, unnecessarily high N implies longer measurements and redundant information. For N equal to a few hundred, the simulated histograms sufficiently exhibit the optimal characteristics. The adsorption number λ A can be varied by tuning the immersion time Δtdip , given that λ A = ν A Δtdip . Figure 3i–l reveals that λ A < 4 produces useful histograms. The rest of parameters, σ∼NP and r D/A , depend on sample properties. The simulated histograms allow to predict what kind of samples, in terms of these parameters, are expected to provide the sharpest results. Obviously, NP suspensions with narrower distributions will result in better histograms. For σ∼NP approaching zero, the histograms are expected to enable a precise analysis which may allow to differentiate among mixed NPs of different nature. In contrast, for σ∼NP approaching unity, the histogram analysis would be subject to significant uncertainty. As r D/A is concerned, a value approaching zero implies slightly narrower peaks because of the lower contribution of desorption to the total variability of the mass increments.

Analysis of Mass Measurements

A precise analysis of the mass distribution and adsorption/desorption dynamics of a suspension of NPs can be performed by fitting experimental histograms to the analytical expression that results from the model, which can be derived as follows. First, we consider that the probability density Pij (Δm) of measuring a mass increment Δm when the number of adsorbed NPs is i and the number of desorbed ones is j is given by the combination of a Gauss distribution for the mass of the NPs with two Poisson distributions for the probability of having such numbers of adsorbed and desorbed NPs: (5) PijΔm=PiλAPjλD2πσij2e−Δm−i−jμNP22σij2

The standard deviation σ ij is calculated under the assumption of uncorrelated mass probabilities for different NPs. In addition, we consider a contribution arising from the dispersion in mass increments caused by fluctuations in the amount of IL wetting the NW tip, which we denote as σ W , so that σij2=(i+j)σNP2+σW2. In general, σ W may represent any other effect that sets a mass detection limit. Then, the probability density Pk (Δm) of obtaining a mass increment Δm produced by the net accretion of a number k = i − j of NPs can be written as: (6) PkΔm=∑i=max0,k∞PiλAPi−kλD2πσik2e−Δm−k·μNP22σik2 where σik2=(2i−k)σNP2+σW2. Finally, the total probability density P(Δm) of measuring an added mass Δm after an immersion event is obtained by considering every possible net number of accumulated particles: (7) PΔm=∑k=−∞∞PkΔm

Equations (6) and (7) provide analytical expressions that can be used to fit experimental mass increment histograms by using µ NP , σ NP and λ A as fitting parameters (Figure 4 ). Figure 4a shows a representative example of this approach for an experiment comprising a set of N = 300 immersion events with Δtdip = 5 s on an acoustofluidic actuated sample of the model Au NP suspension considered in this study. The measured mass increment histogram clearly shows the distinctive features previously identified as characteristic of the Gauss‐Poisson model. The numerical fitting is represented by a red line. For such fitting, we consider that the total mass accreted on the NW after N immersions must correspond to the average net number of adsorbed NPs per immersion multiplied by the number of immersions, so that: (8) λA−λD=1N∑n=1NΔmnμNP where Δmn represents the mass increment measured after the nth immersion. This expression allows to experimentally determine the balance λ A − λ D and thus eliminates the use of λ D as a free fitting parameter.

Consistently with the mass increment dispersion observed in blank tests, a value of σ W = 17.5 ag is considered for the fitting. The fitting results in µ NP = 271 ag, σ NP = 52 ag and λ A = 3.1. The determination of λ D from the mass balance measured in the histogram leads to a ratio r D/A = 0.82. Remarkably, the NP mass distribution parameters are in excellent agreement with the corresponding nominal values. Figure 4c–f show several SEM images that provide evidence of the mostly homogeneous coverage of the adsorbed NPs that takes place only near the free end of the NW also after a large number of immersions, supporting the accuracy of the point‐mass approximation and the assumptions of the model.

Figure 4b represents the total probability density function P(Δm) for the parameter values obtained from the fitting. The graph includes the decomposition of P(Δm) into the peaks corresponding to the individual probabilities Pk (Δm) associated to every possible net number k (positive or negative) of accumulated NPs. This representation clarifies the origin of the width of the peaks observed in the measurements. In particular, the width of the peak resulting from P 0(Δm) is much larger than that corresponding to the fluctuations in the amount of IL wetting the NW tip as given by σ W , contrarily to what could have been intuitively expected when the net number of accumulated NPs is k = i − j = 0. This happens because a net zero accumulation implies multiple combinations of an equal number of adsorbed and desorbed NPs (i = j), so that P0(Δm)=P00(Δm)+P11(Δm)+P22(Δm)+(⋯). Thus, each combination contributes to the resulting total dispersion, and only the contribution P 00(Δm) has a dispersion given by σ W (magenta line). Similarly happens for the peaks at k≠0. For instance, if we focus on the peak for k = 1, the resulting dispersion is larger than that corresponding to a single NP as defined by σ NP . Again, the explanation is that multiple combinations of adsorbed and desorbed NPs result in a net accumulation of just one NP, P1(Δm)=P10(Δm)+P21(Δm)+P32(Δm)+(…), and all these combinations contribute to dispersion. Only P 10(Δm) has a width dominated by σ NP (orange line). The width of the peaks increases for increasing k because larger net numbers of accumulated NPs necessarily imply larger numbers of combined adsorbed and desorbed NPs.

Conclusion

The presented results demonstrate the capability of dip‐in nanomechanical probes to provide an efficient characterization of the mass distribution of NPs dispersed in an IL suspension. The key performance advantages of this approach are its high‐resolution, high‐throughput, ultra‐low sample consumption, and negligible sample losses. The tapered Si NW resonators used in this work feature a reduced effective mass that results in a mass resolution down to 0.1 ag, as limited by frequency stability. Fluctuations in the mass of liquid that wets the tip of the NW set the mass detection limit to the order of 1–10 ag depending on device. These parameters would allow the characterization of inorganic NPs with a density similar to Au for diameters down to 10 nm, and soft organic NPs with a density ≈1 g cm−3 for diameters down to 30 nm. However, none of these represent fundamental limits. Mass detection limit could be minimized by selecting a probe material and IL composition that reduce wetting fluctuations below mass resolution as limited by frequency noise. Also, mass resolution limited by frequency noise, which scales with the resonator dimensions, can approach the zeptogram scale by reducing the NW length and diameter by a small factor with respect to the dimensions used here. Such a reduction would preserve the compatibility with all the technologies presented in this work, and the resulting mass resolution would allow the precise characterization of any type of NP with a diameter down to a few nanometers. Another benefit of our approach relies on the simplicity of the conversion of frequency shifts into mass increments, which can be done in real‐time at very low computational cost without the need of knowing the exact flexural mode shape.[ 45 ] Extracting the mass distribution parameters of the suspended NPs requires some assumptions about the mass distribution: for the monomodal suspension considered in this study, the mass increment histograms are fitted to a Gauss‐Poisson statistical model; other more complex systems, such as NP mixtures, might require specific models for accurate mass analysis. Interestingly, the Gauss‐Poisson model also enables the estimation of additional parameters. In particular, from the determination of the average number of NPs adsorbed per immersion event, λ A , and the applied immersion time, Δtdip , we can estimate the average adsorption rate as ν A = λ A /Δtdip , which results in ν A = 0.63 NP s−1. This value implies an average sensing rate of 38 NP min−1, which allows a robust characterization of a NP suspension for a total measurement time of a few minutes. These estimations evidence the combined high‐resolution and high‐throughput capabilities of our approach, particularly when compared with nebulization‐based measurements (Supporting Information). In addition, our results also demonstrate the ability to operate with an extremely low volume of liquid suspension while ensuring virtually zero sample loss. In the presented experiments, we use a suspension droplet with a volume of a few nanoliters. Nevertheless, there is no fundamental limitation to further reducing such volume. For instance, if the droplet diameter is reduced to ten times the immersed length of the NW, the resulting volume would be in the sub‐picoliter scale. Remarkably, no fraction of this volume is wasted during the measurements, so that the suspension under test can be re‐used for further characterization by other techniques. In comparison, ESI expends suspension volumes in the microliter range, and it has shown detection efficiency below 0.001%, which implies a 99.999% of sample loss.[ 31 , 41 , 44 ]

In terms of further improvements, we envision two particular lines. First, acoustofluidic actuation can be designed to locally enhance NP concentration at the nodes of acoustic trapping modes induced within the liquid. This approach has already been demonstrated in macroscopic volumes of aqueous suspensions, and it is expected that the same underlying mechanism operates at reduced scales.[ 53 ] This would allow either increasing the transfer efficiency or working with a much lower NP concentration. Second, if the full length of the nanomechanical probe is immersed into the suspension, NPs will adsorb at any position. Besides the evident increase in transfer efficiency and sensing rate, this would also enable the implementation of NP stiffness characterization. When NPs adsorb at any position, stiffness sensing can be implemented in several ways as reported in previous studies: first, if adsorption position is controlled or known, adsorption events on specific highly stiffness‐sensitive regions (those with the largest flexural mode shape curvature) can be used to extract stiffness parameters from single mode measurements by neglecting mass effects;[ 61 ] second, if positions are unknown, then mass, stiffness and position can be inferred by application of the inverse method, which can be performed by tracking at least three different modes[ 31 ] or by harnessing the split orthogonal resonances for each mode that occur as a consequence of degeneration breakage.[ 62 ] In conclusion, the NP mass characterization approach presented in this work has shown critical progress with respect to the most significant limitations of nanomechanical sensing. Further engineering of specific ILs, nanomechanical probes and methodologies for particular analytes can be expected to result in the widespread application of dip‐in nanomechanical probes for the characterization of an ample variety of NPs in a broad range of conditions regarding their composition, size, concentration, and distribution parameters.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of Si Nanowire Resonators

Si NWs were grown in a Nanoinnova “CVDCube” atmospheric pressure chemical vapor deposition (AP‐CVD) system by the VLS mechanism. Colloidal Au NPs (Sigma–Aldrich) with nominal diameters of either 150 or 250 nm were diluted in deionized (DI) water to a concentration of 7·106 NP mL−1 and deposited on 5 × 5 mm raw Si (111) substrates to serve as a catalyst. Before catalyst deposition, substrates were thoroughly cleaned in ultrasounds with acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and DI water, and then HF (5%) treated to remove native oxide (10 s immersion). Substrates were then coated by immersion with poly‐L‐lysine to improve Au NP adhesion. Afterward, Au NP deposition near one substrate edge was controlled by nano‐pipetting (Femtotip, Eppendorf) in order to ensure a sufficient separation among NWs to prevent the immersion of more than one NW into the IL reservoir droplet during dip‐in experiments, as well as allowing signal readout from a single NW at a time.[ 58 ] Growth temperature was 825 °C in the tubular quartz reactor of the AP‐CVD system. Liquid precursor SiCl4 was driven to the reactor by flowing 30 sccm of inert Ar gas through a bubbler kept at a constant temperature of 0 °C. H2 was introduced (10% in Argon) with a flow of 120 sccm. Growth time was between 35 and 40 min. Details of the formation of the gold‐silica core–shell nanostructures that remain at the nanowire tips after growth were found elsewhere.[ 56 ]

Nanowire Resonator Transduction

The resonance readout stage uses a fiber‐coupled diode laser (TopMode‐633, Toptica Photonics AG, λ = 633 nm). Output power and polarization were manually controlled with a variable attenuator and birefringence loops, respectively. The values of incident optical power used range from 1 to 20 µW, high enough to resolve the thermomechanical signal of nanowires at practical acquisition times (τ ≲ 1s) but without inducing any observable optomechanical back‐action effect. Polarization was aligned with the longitudinal axis of the nanowires in order to maximize backscattered intensity, optimizing transduction sensitivity.[ 63 ] A triplet lens collimator provides a nearly Gaussian free‐space beam, with its optical axis oriented perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the nanowires. The beam was focused on the Si NWs grown near the edges of the substrate using a 20× objective with 0.42 numerical aperture, which results into a laser spot waist diameter of ≈4 µm. The NW resonators were driven at their nonlinear onset by AC piezo‐actuation. Resonance frequency tracking was performed by acquisition of the optical signal collected by an unsegmented photodetector by a DAQ board and Fourier transformed into frequency spectra by a computer algorithm.

Acoustofluidic Actuation

A frequency‐modulated sine wave, typically centered ≈30 kHz with a modulation bandwidth of 2 kHz, was applied to the piezo‐actuator driving the droplet microwire support. A built‐in function of the signal generator (noisy modulation shape) was used to randomly modulate the center frequency within the specified bandwidth. Mechanical resonances of the support enhance energy transfer from the piezo‐actuator to the droplet. The optimal actuation frequency depends on the geometry, mechanical properties, and clamping configuration of the support, so that it was experimentally determined after reservoir preparation by direct observation of support vibrations under an optical microscope. The actuation voltage, typically within the range 1–10 V, was adjusted to optimize nanoparticle velocity and dispersion within the reservoir.

Preparation of Au NP Suspensions in Ionic Liquid

Two different hydrophobic ILs were used for the NP suspension reservoir, either Diethylmethyl(2‐methoxyethyl) ammonium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (abbreviated as DEME‐TFSI) or 1‐Butyl‐1‐methylpyrrolidinium bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (abbreviated as BMP‐TFSI), both purchased from Merck. DEME‐TFSI was successfully employed in previous open nanofluidics studies involving nanowire resonators;[ 58 ] while BMP‐TFSI exhibits comparable physicochemical properties but offers improved commercial availability with more detailed technical documentation. Both ILs have provided similar results in this work. Au NPs used as a model sample were purchased from Nanopartz Inc, either suspended in DI water (part number C11‐30‐TC‐DIH‐50‐1) or dry (part number C11‐30‐TC‐DRY‐2.5‐S5). Dry NPs were dispersed in 200 µL of IL by standard sonication methods at a concentration of ≈1013 NP mL−1. Nanoparticles suspended in DI water can also be transferred into IL using standard solvent exchange methods based on centrifugation, pipetting, and sonication. However, due to the hydrophobic nature of the ILs used, an intermediate solvent that was miscible with both DI water and the hydrophobic IL was introduced to facilitate efficient phase transfer. Isopropanol was selected as the intermediate solvent due to its complete miscibility with both DI water and BMP‐TFSI, the IL used as the reservoir suspension in this case. This two‐stage transfer process resulted in a stable dispersion of Au NPs in BMP‐TFSI. Starting from 200 µL of the commercial Au NP solution in DI water, with a nominal concentration of 1.8·1013 NP mL−1, each solvent exchange stage involved three sequential cycles of centrifugation (at 25 °C), supernatant removal, and resuspension. In the first stage (from DI water to isopropanol), centrifugation was performed at 6000 RCF for 15 min, and resuspension was achieved by gentle pipetting, with 170 µL of supernatant removed and replaced with 170 µL of isopropanol in each cycle. In the second stage (from isopropanol to BMP‐TFSI), the centrifugation conditions were 6000 RCF for 15 min in the first cycle, 20 000 RCF for 60 min in the second, and 20 000 RCF for 135 min in the third; in each cycle, 170 µL of supernatant was removed and replaced with 170 µL of BMP‐TFSI, followed by 1 min of sonication to ensure uniform nanoparticle dispersion. Once the Au NP suspension in IL was prepared, a small droplet was placed on the 250 nm‐diameter flat top of the cylindrical Pt‐Ir support using of a microinjector syringe (NanoFil, WPI), forming a reservoir with a volume of a few nanoliters.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The microscope used in this work was a FEI Verios 460. In its conventional low voltage operation (2–5 kV), the instrument uses a detector named TLD (through the lens detector) for secondary electron imaging with topographic contrast, and another one named MD (mirror detector) for backscattered electron imaging with material contrast. The general imaging parameters used in this work are the following: acceleration voltage, 2 kV; current, 13 pA; working distance, 4 mm; dwell time, 30 µs; image size, 1536 × 1024 pixels; tilt angle of the NW longitudinal axis with respect to the electron beam, 90° in Figure 1b–d, 45° in all other images.

Monte Carlo Simulations

Monte Carlo simulations were implemented in MATLAB. The algorithm follows these steps during a number N of iterations: 1) a random integer i from a Poisson distribution with average adsorption number λ A was generated; 2) another random integer j from a Poisson distribution with average desorption number λ D was generated; 3) a number i of NP mass values were randomly generated from a Gaussian distribution with mean µ NP and relative standard deviation σ∼NP; 4) a number j of the previously generated and registered adsorbed mass values in the present or any preceding iteration were randomly removed from the register, such that only previously added NPs could be removed; 5) the added mass at each iteration was determined as the balance of added and removed mass values. For each iteration, the mass of each adsorbed or desorbed NP was added or subtracted one by one in random order, so that adsorption and desorption events were treated individually even within a single immersion.
