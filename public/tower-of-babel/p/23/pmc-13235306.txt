Peripapillary scleral stiffening increases optic nerve head shear deformation in porcine eyes during IOP elevation
Authors: Manqi Pan, Zihao Chen, Xueliang Pan, He Zhang, Matthew Reilly, Sayoko E Moroi, Jun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputational models predicted that peripapillary sclera (PPS) stiffening may reduce intraocular pressure (IOP) induced stresses and strains at the optic nerve head (ONH). Experimental studies in animal models of glaucoma however did not confirm such benefit. In this study, we used high-resolution ultrasound elastography to quantify PPS and ONH strains to evaluate biomechanical changes associated with PPS stiffening. Sixteen pairs of porcine globes were used. One globe in each pair was randomly assigned to the stiffening or the control group. Inflation tests were performed before and after treatment with 1.25 % glutaraldehyde (stiffening group) or saline (control group). IOP was raised from 5 to 30 mmHg while 2D cross-sectional images of the PPS and ONH were obtained using a 50 MHz ultrasound probe. PPS and ONH displacements and strains were calculated using a validated ultrasound speckle tracking algorithm. In the stiffening group, there was an increase in ONH shear strain (by 11.8 %, P = 0.025) and an increase in the posterior displacement of the ONH relative to PPS (P < 0.001). In contrast, ONH shear strain decreased (by 8.1%, P=0.006) and the relative ONH posterior displacement did not change in the control group. ONH tangential strains were reduced in both groups, but more so in the stiffening group. These results suggest that the biomechanical effects of PPS stiffening are complex. While a reduction of ONH tangential strain could be protective, the concomitant increase in ONH shear strain and ONH posterior displacement could be detrimental. Future studies are needed to optimize PPS biomechanical properties for neuroprotection.Keywords: Peripapillary sclera, Sclera stiffening, Optic nerve head, Mechanical deformation, Shear strains, Ultrasound elastography

Introduction

Glaucoma is the second leading cause of irreversible blindness (Tham et al., 2014). Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) is a major risk factor, and the optic nerve head (ONH) is the primary site of damage (Quigley et al., 1983). Computational models predicted that the peripapillary sclera (PPS) stiffness and thickness are major factors influencing stresses and strains induced by elevated IOP at the ONH (Norman et al., 2011; Sigal et al., 2005). Several glaucoma risk factors such as older age, African American (AA) race, or myopia, are associated with structural and biomechanical changes of the posterior sclera (Coudrillier et al., 2015; Grytz et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2011). These observations have motivated the pursuit of novel therapies to optimize scleral biomechanics for neuroprotection (Girard et al., 2015; Quigley, 2015; Strouthidis and Girard, 2013).

One debated question is: what scleral biomechanical properties are desirable to protect the ONH from IOP-induced stresses and strains? A stiffer PPS is often thought to be beneficial because it can reduce certain deformations at the ONH (Coudrillier et al., 2016a; Thornton et al., 2009). However, its neuroprotective benefits have not been confirmed by experiments in rodent models of experimental glaucoma with scleral stiffening. Kimball et al. (2014) showed an increased susceptibility to retinal ganglion cell damage in IOP-elevated mice that underwent glyceraldehyde crosslinking of the sclera. In a more recent study, Gerberich et al. (2022) showed that scleral stiffening did not yield neuroprotection in ocular hypertensive rats. Although many factors could have played a role, these results motivate us to ask the question: are there potentially adverse biomechanical effects with PPS stiffening that are not recognized? Previous numerical studies using computational models have also suggested that the effects of PPS stiffness on ONH could be complex (Sigal and Ethier, 2009; Sigal et al., 2011).

Intuitively, the weaker ONH is largely “carried over” by the typically much stiffer PPS in the deformation within the same (curved) plane of the PPS. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that a stiffer PPS would reduce IOP-induced tangential stretch and thus protect the ONH from in-plane expansion. However, the ONH is also displaced posteriorly relative to the sclera shell, and our previous experimental studies have shown that the out-of-plane compression and shear are typically dominant and larger than the in-plane deformation (Kwok et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2019, 2020a; Pavlatos et al., 2018b). This is also consistent with the clinical hallmark of glaucoma: the posterior bowing or cupping of the ONH (Burgoyne, 2015). The out-of-plane deformation after PPS stiffening has not been well characterized and methods with sufficient tissue penetration depth are necessary to evaluate such deformation.

In this study, we applied a high-frequency ultrasound elastography technique to characterize ONH and PPS mechanical responses to IOP with an expanded field of view both in depth to cover full tissue thickness (a few mm) and in width to simultaneously image the ONH and the surrounding PPS centered at the ONH (~10 mm total width). This expanded field of view allows us to evaluate the out-of-plane deformation and the displacement of the ONH relative to the PPS. The high-frequency ultrasound at 50 MHz provides an axial resolution of 35 μm and a lateral resolution of 75 μm, and a displacement sensitivity approaches 10s of nanometers owing to the advantage of radiofrequency (RF) analysis and interpolation (Pavlatos et al., 2018a). In addition, the elastography method computes the in-plane tangential stretch and the out-of-plane compression and shear in both PPS and ONH.

Using the high-frequency ultrasound elastography method, we measured ONH and PPS mechanical deformation before and after PPS stiffening in paired porcine globes (one eye served as a control with a sham treatment). Our goal was to generate a comprehensive characterization of ONH and PPS’ mechanical responses through tissue thickness to better understand the biomechanical consequences of PPS stiffening.

Methods

Porcine globe preparation and inflation testing

Sixteen paired globes from 16 pigs (6 months–1 year old) were obtained 1–2 h after slaughter (Heffelfinger’s Meats Inc., Columbus, OH, USA). The globes were immersed in cold 0.9 % saline and kept cold (~4 °C) prior to experimental use. The extraocular tissues were removed, and the optic nerve was trimmed to approximately 1–2 mm from the surface of the sclera. The globes were secured to a custom-built holder with two spinal needles and immersed in saline with the ONH facing up. To minimize postmortem changes, the immersion solution was kept cold by periodically adding saline ice cubes. Two 20G needles were inserted into the anterior chamber of the eye, one connected to a programmable syringe pump (PhD Ultra; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA) to control the IOP, and the other connected to a pressure sensor (P75; Harvard Apparatus) to continuously record and monitor the IOP (Fig. 1A).

IOP control and monitoring were achieved through cannulation to the anterior chamber. B. Two rounds of inflation testing were conducted in all eyes, one before and one after PPS treatment (chemical crosslinking in the stiffening group and sham treatment in the control group; see details in 2.2). For each inflation testing, the globe was first preconditioned with 20 pressure cycles from 5 to 30 mm Hg at 4 s per cycle and left to equilibrate at 5 mm Hg for 30 min (Fig. 1B). Inflation testing was performed by increasing IOP from 5 to 30 mmHg with pressure steps of 0.5 mmHg. The IOP was held constant at each level for 30 s before ultrasound scans using a 50 MHz probe (MS700, Vevo2100, FujiFilm VisualSonics, Inc., Toronto, Ontario, Canada). Control of the testing apparatus and data acquisition were implemented using customized LabView software (National Instruments, Austin, TX). Each inflation test took about 25 min to complete for a total of 51 pressure steps. The globes were scanned along either the nasal-temporal (NT) or superior-inferior (SI) capturing approximately 9.7 mm in width centered at the ONH. The same scanning orientation was used for the globe pair from a given animal.

PPS treatment in stiffening and control groups

For each pair of globes, one eye was randomly selected for PPS stiffening and the contralateral eye was assigned to the control group. Each eye underwent a 5-min treatment. For the stiffening group, 1.25 % glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was used as the treatment solution, as this concentration showed minimal fixative diffusion into the ONH based on previous reports (Coudrillier et al., 2016a). Treatment with PBS was used for the control group.

After the first round of inflation testing, each eye underwent a 5-min treatment by placing an annular gauze (inner diameter 4.5 mm, outer diameter 12.0 mm) soaked with treatment solution on the PPS surface while keeping IOP at 5 mmHg. A cylinder ring of waterproof tape was placed around the ONH to physically block the fixative from leaking into the ONH. Treatment solution was periodically added to the gauze during treatment. The rest of the globe was immersed in saline. The globe was thoroughly rinsed with PBS after treatment to remove any residual treatment solution before a second round of inflation testing was conducted following the same protocol.

Ultrasound speckle tracking

A correlation-based ultrasound speckle tracking algorithm (Tang and Liu, 2012) was used to compute tissue displacements and strains at each step of IOP elevation. The accuracy of the ultrasound elastographic technique for displacement and strain measurements has been validated and reported previously (Pavlatos et al., 2018a; Tang and Liu, 2012). Digitized ultrasound RF data were sampled at 1.5 μm × 19 μm (axial × lateral). Regions of interest (ROI) were defined in the reference RF data obtained at the initial IOP. The ROIs for the ONH and the PPS were defined by manual segmentation. The RF data were divided into kernels, each containing 51 × 31 pixels (axial × lateral), or approximately 75 μm × 570 μm in size, with a 50 % overlap, following our previous protocols for ONH/PPS strain mapping. The parameters for kernel size and overlap were optimized in our previous studies to achieve the best combination of signal-to-noise ratio and strain resolution.

The displacement of each kernel was computed by cross-correlation within a search window between RF frames acquired at consecutive IOP levels. Maximum correlation coefficient indicated the best match and the new location of the kernel, and spline interpolation was used to refine the search to achieve subpixel resolution in displacement tracking. The cumulative displacement vectors were calculated with respect to the kernel location at the initial IOP.

Regional analyses of tissue displacements and strains

The PPS, ONH, and lamina cribrosa (LC) were manually segmented based on tissue boundaries in ultrasound images. The ONH/PPS junction is defined as a 2-mm width area across the boundaries between PPS and ONH on both sides (Fig. 2). We have previously validated the repeatability of the manual segmentation on ultrasound images, showing excellent interobserver repeatability (Ma et al., 2020a). Average strains and displacements within each region were calculated. The difference between axial displacements between the ONH and the PPS was also calculated for each tested eye before and after treatment.

Illustration of the imaged regions of PPS, ONH, LC, and ONH/PPS junction area in a representative porcine eye. The ONH/PPS junction is defined as a 2-mm width area across the boundary between PPS and ONH on either side of the ONH (shaded in orange).

Tensile and compression tests of dissected posterior scleral specimens

Porcine globe pairs were obtained (Heffelfinger’s Meats Inc. Columbus, OH, USA) to estimate the extent of stiffening (i.e., change in tensile and compressive moduli) induced by the adopted stiffening protocol. Ten pairs were used to estimate changes in tensile modulus by testing on sclera strips, and another 10 pairs were used to estimate changes in compressive modulus by testing on scleral discs collected by trephine punch. One of the whole globes from each pair was randomly assigned to the stiffening group and the other assigned to the control group. These globes were treated following the same protocol for inflation testing, as described earlier, using either 1.25 % glutaraldehyde (stiffening) or PBS (control). After treatment in whole globes, scleral strips (12 mm × 3 mm) or discs (diameter = 7.5 mm) were dissected from the superior sclera about 3 mm away from the ONH. Scleral specimens were immersed in saline and kept cold (~4 °C) prior to uniaxial tensile or compression tests.

For uniaxial tensile tests, scleral strips were preloaded to about 50 mN using a mechanical testing system (ElectroForce Planar Biaxial TestBench, Bose Corp., Eden Prairie, MN). Ten load-unload cycles were applied from 20 to 300 kPa, with a load rate of 5.6 kPa/s. The first nine cycles were for preconditioning and the tenth cycle was used as the actual testing. Secant modulus at 250 kPa was used to calculate tensile Young’s modulus.

A custom compression system (Alzoubi et al., 2024) was used for scleral compression tests. This instrument used a rotary motor (BLM-N23-50-1000-B; Galil Motion Control; Rocklin, CA) to drive a high-precision linear stage (6734K4 with 1/8” pitch; McMaster-Carr; Aurora, OH). The applied load was measured using a load cell with 50 g-force capacity (FSH02667; FUTEK Advanced Sensor Technology, Inc.; Irvine, CA). Samples were immersed in saline solution during testing. Samples were preloaded to 20 mN and then underwent 10 preconditioning cycles from 0 to 15 % strain with 0.5 % strain per second. After preconditioning, the samples were preloaded at 20 mN and then ramped from 0 to 15 % strain with 0.5 % strain per second. Secant modulus at 10 % strain was used to calculate compressive modulus.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviation of radial, tangential and shear strains at 30 mmHg were summarized for each region (ONH, PPS, LC and ONH/PPS junction) before and after glutaraldehyde treatment in the stiffening group and before and after saline treatment in the control group. Since the “before and after treatment” measures were from the same eye, paired t-tests were used to detect differences for each group. Since globe pairs of the same animal randomly received either stiffening or control treatment, the comparisons between treatment were also conducted using paired t-tests. Sensitivity analysis, using linear mixed model for repeated measures to account for the association of eyes from the same pig, was conducted to ensure the conclusion is robust to the model selection. Model fitness, such as normal distribution of residuals, was also evaluated. If normality distribution is violated, alternative non-parametric methods will be used for sensitivity analysis to ensure conclusions are robust.

The difference between the average axial displacements of the ONH and the average axial displacement of the PPS (referred to as “the posterior displacement of ONH relative to PPS”) was also compared before and after treatment in the same group using paired t-tests. The association of the before/after changes and before-treatment measures was illustrated using scatter plots and summarized using Pearson correlation coefficients.

A sample size of 13 pairs of eyes provides at least 90 % power to detect 1 standard deviation (Cohen’s effect size d = 1.0) in terms of strain change before/after treatment or strain difference between treatment groups based on paired t-tests at a significance level of 0.05. This sample size also provides at least 80 % power to detect associations of R > 0.7 at the significance level of 0.05. Additional 3 pairs of globes were acquired to account for potential experiment attrition or missing data during data processing.

Results

Mechanical strip tests showed that the average scleral secant tensile modulus at 250 kPa was 4.20 ± 1.39 MPa after glutaraldehyde treatment in the stiffening group and 2.88 ± 0.36 MPa after sham treatment in the control group (P = 0.010). Unconfined compression tests in scleral discs showed that average scleral compressive modulus at 10 % strain was 15.6 ± 3.1 kPa after glutaraldehyde treatment in the stiffening group and 12.3 ± 3.0 kPa after sham treatment in the control group (P < 0.001).

Table 1 shows the comparison of strains at 30 mmHg in the PPS, ONH, LC, and ONH/PPS junction regions before and after PPS stiffening in the stiffening group and before and after a sham treatment in the control group. In the stiffening group, PPS radial and tangential strains significantly reduced by 30.9 % and 18.1 % on average after PPS stiffening (P = 0.002 and 0.003, respectively), whereas PPS shear strains did not change (P = 0.625). ONH tangential strain also significantly decreased by 15.0 % on average (P = 0.002), but ONH shear strain significantly increased by 11.8 % on average (P = 0.025) and ONH radial strain did not change (P = 0.830). LC tangential strain significantly decreased by 21.5 % on average (P = 0.002), whereas LC radial and shear strains did not change (P = 0.396 and 0.434, respectively). All three strains changed significantly in the ONH/PPS junction region: radial and tangential strains significantly decreased by 14.5 % and 17.0 % on average (P = 0.011 and P = 0.004 respectively) but shear strain significantly increased by 9.9 % on average (P = 0.046). In the control group, most strains showed no difference before and after the sham treatment, but a few strains showed statistical differences. The ONH shear strain reduced by 8.1 % on average (P = 0.006). ONH/PPS junction shear also significantly reduced in the control group (P = 0.045). In addition, ONH and LC tangential strain reduced slightly but statistically significantly in the control group (P = 0.012 and 0.014, respectively).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the changes in the biomechanical responses of the PPS, ONH, LC, and ONH/PPS junction regions before and after PPS stiffening using high-frequency ultrasound elastography. We quantified radial, tangential, and shear strains in these regions as IOP raised from 5 to 30 mmHg. Our main finding was that most strains were reduced after PPS stiffening as expected, but ONH and ONH/PPS junction shear strain increased after PPS stiffening. In addition, there was a significant increase in the relative posterior displacement of the ONH with respect to the PPS in the stiffening group but not in the control group, and this relative displacement was significantly correlated with the ONH/PPS junction shear.

Our study confirmed a significant reduction of the tangential strains in PPS, ONH, LC, and ONH/PPS junction (Table 1), consistent with computational models’ predictions (Norman et al., 2011; Sigal et al., 2005) and previous experimental results (Coudrillier et al., 2016a; Thornton et al., 2009). Our full tissue depth imaging using high-frequency ultrasound elastography allowed us to quantify the out-of-plane deformation including the radial compression and the out-of-plane shear which is the gradient of the radial displacements along the tissue curvature. This led to the observation of a significantly increased ONH shear and ONH/PPS junction shear after PPS stiffening. To our best knowledge, this result has not been previously reported. The shear deformation is a more dominant form of deformation in both PPS and ONH with a magnitude larger than tensile stretch, as reported in our previous experimental studies (Ma et al., 2019; Pavlatos et al., 2018b) and predicted by computational models (Sigal et al., 2007, 2009). Therefore, the pathophysiological implications of this mechanical deformation could be significant and warrant future study.

We observed a significant increase in the posterior displacement of the ONH relative to the PPS after PPS stiffening, suggesting the ONH bowed more outward at a given IOP elevation after PPS stiffening. This observation was made possible by imaging both regions simultaneously. The clinical hallmark of glaucoma is the posterior bowing or cupping of the ONH, and this relative motion could subject the ONH/PPS junction area to detrimental mechanical stresses and strains such as those leading to LC defects or optic disc hemorrhage frequently observed in glaucoma patients (Sharpe et al., 2016; You et al., 2013). We found a strong correlation between the relative displacement and the junction shear (Fig. 5). Together, the increased ONH shear, the increased relative displacement between ONH and PPS, and the increased shear strain in the junction area all point to potentially negative biomechanical effects of PPS stiffening. This result also suggests caution for using sclera stiffening as a potential treatment for progressive myopia (Gawargious et al., 2020; Wollensak and Spoerl, 2004; Zhang and Lai, 2021). Myopia is associated with elevated glaucoma risk (Haarman et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2016). Future studies are needed to evaluate whether scleral stiffening further increases the risk for glaucoma in myopic eyes and if so, optimal levels of scleral stiffening need to be identified to prevent myopia progression without increasing glaucoma risk.

We also observed from the ultrasound images at different IOP levels that the PPS bent more posteriorly at higher IOP (Supplementary Fig. S1), consistent with the scleral rotation or bending reported previously (Sigal et al., 2009). It is noted that the shear strain reported in this study represents a distinct mode of tissue deformation that occurs concurrently with rotation but is not resulting from simple angular displacement (Supplementary Fig. S2). Instead, this shear arises from complex differential motions within the ONH and PPS, underscoring the complex mechanical responses of these tissues to elevated IOP. We also validated the strain calculation algorithm in terms of correctly detecting and calculating true shear deformation (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Fig. S4). Although not causal, the significant correlation between ONH/PPS junction shear and ONH displacement relative to PPS (Fig. 5) suggests that increased shear may concur with greater PPS rotation, assuming larger relative displacement indicates greater rotation. In addition, both relative displacement and shear increased significantly after PPS stiffening (Fig. 4), also suggesting consistency. Previous studies have reported a more pronounced posterior bowing of the PPS in glaucoma (Wang et al., 2022) and older age (Tun et al., 2019). Although speculative, it seems plausible that the shear strains and PPS rotation/bending, experienced during IOP elevations in glaucoma or throughout a lifetime at every ocular pulse or other IOP fluctuations, could affect PPS connective tissue remodeling leading to shape change and posterior bowing. Whether this posterior bowing and an increased ONH/PPS shear are associated with glaucoma vulnerability remains to be determined in future studies.

It is also noted that the absolute changes in all types of strains were small in the present study, and this is consistent with previous experimental study using the same stiffening protocol (Coudrillier et al., 2016a), and theoretical predictions (Sigal et al., 2011). We implemented a 5-min treatment of 1.25 % glutaraldehyde for PPS stiffening. Mechanical testing confirmed scleral stiffening as tensile modulus increased from 2.88 MPa to 4.20 MPa (46 % increase) and compressive modulus from 12.3 kPa to 15.6 kPa (27 % increase). Theoretically, a higher level of stiffening may be induced if we increase the treatment duration or fixative concentration. The faster and lower concentration treatment was intentional to minimize two potential experimental confounding factors: 1) fixatives leakage into the ONH; and 2) postmortem tissue changes. The choice of 1.25 % glutaraldehyde was based on a previous study that confirmed the absence of leakage to ONH at this concentration with a 5-min treatment (Coudrillier et al., 2016a). The brevity of the 5-min treatment minimized the total postmortem time before the second round of inflation testing in the same eye after treatment. Although the physiological consequences of the reported strain change remain to be elucidated, the mixed trends observed in this study (some strains reduced whereas others increased) suggest the need to further clarify the biomechanical effects of PPS stiffening. Our data from this ex vivo porcine eye model suggests that not all biomechanical changes are neuroprotective: could the increased ONH bowing and the increased shear within the ONH and at the ONH/PPS junction negate the benefit from reduced tensile stretch after PPS stiffening (Gerberich et al., 2022), or even contribute to neural damage and increased axonal loss reported in the rodent model of glaucoma (Kimball et al., 2014)?

We also observed a few significant changes in the control group with a sham treatment, including a reduction in ONH, LC, and ONH/PPS junction shear strains, and a reduction in ONH and LC tangential strains (Table 1). Although on average the absolute change of these strains was very small (0.001–0.003), the trends were statistically significant suggesting possible systematic effects such as tissue change due to postmortem time or the first round of inflation. Notably, the shear strains were reduced in the control group, but increased in the stiffening group, suggesting that the increase we observed in the stiffening group could have been larger if the unknown tissue change were avoided. It is noted that before treatment none of the strains was significantly different between the control and the stiffening groups. After treatment, ONH shear strains were significantly larger in the stiffening group as compared to the control group (0.033 ± 0.005 vs 0.029 ± 0.005, P = 0.021), further confirming an increase in ONH shear after PPS stiffening.

To further understand what factors may influence the change in ONH shear, we evaluated the relationship between the change in ONH shear (defined as “ONH shear after treatment – ONH shear before treatment”) and the initial ONH shear before treatment. Fig. 6 shows a strong negative correlation between the change in ONH shear and the initial ONH shear. This result suggests that eyes with a smaller ONH shear at baseline would experience a larger increase in ONH shear after PPS stiffening. Caution should be used when considering PPS stiffening in these eyes.

This study has a few limitations. First, ex vivo studies do not fully replicate the in vivo mechanical environment due to the absence of cerebrospinal fluid pressure, retinal artery pressure, and optic nerve traction. These physiological forces will undoubtedly modulate ONH and PPS mechanical responses, and future studies are needed to investigate the interactions. The present study focused on the most dominant load, the IOP, which is a primary risk factor for glaucoma. Second, only one cross-section of the posterior eye was imaged which does not permit evaluations of regional variations. We have developed a 3D ultrasound elastography method (Cruz Perez et al., 2016; Kwok et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2020b); however, our current 3D imaging protocol is 4–5 times slower than 2D and not suitable due to the lengthy experimental time and expected postmortem tissue changes. We plan to develop fast 3D protocols to map the regional variances. A typical challenge of 2D methods is the uncertainty of the through-plane motion and deformation when imaging one 2D plane. Our 2D and 3D data from the same eyes showed generally good agreement in strain distribution and magnitudes, although not identical. Similar observations were also reported by others when comparing digital image correlation (2D) and digital volume correlation (3D) results of pig posterior eye strains (Coudrillier et al., 2016b). Third, the porcine eye model has the advantage of shorter postmortem time, larger availability, and lower cost than human donor eyes. The pig eye is also anatomically similar to the human eye and has a collagenous LC. However, the conclusions from this study cannot be generalized to the human eye without further validation. In general, the human sclera is thinner and stiffer than the pig sclera. Future studies will test the effects of PPS stiffening in human donor globes. Fourth, the experiments were performed at cold temperature (~4 °C), which could affect tissue biomechanics. The main reason we kept the tissue cold was to minimize tissue degradation. Since our experiments lasted for hours, some level of degradation (particularly for the ONH) would be expected if tissue were kept at room temperature. In the past, we performed faster 2D inflation tests at room temperature and did not observe significant difference in behavior or strain pattern between room temperature and cold.

In conclusion, we used high-frequency ultrasound to quantify the changes in ONH and PPS biomechanical responses before and after PPS stiffening in an ex vivo porcine eye model. Our results showed an ONH tangential strain reduction after PPS stiffening, consistent with previous reports. We also found an increase in both ONH shear strain and its posterior bowing relative to the PPS. These new findings suggest that the effects of PPS stiffening on ONH are more complex than previously thought and may be protective in one way but detrimental in another. Future studies are needed to validate these results in human eyes to provide guidance for developing effective strategies of neuroprotection through PPS biomechanical optimization.
