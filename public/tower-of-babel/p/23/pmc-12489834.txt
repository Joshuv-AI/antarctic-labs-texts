Prospective associations of obesity heterogeneity, serum proteins, and carotid atherosclerosis risk
Authors: Haili Zhong, Jieteng Chen, Shize Jia, Hanzu Chen, Yue Xi, Yan Yan, Zilong Lu, Congmei Xiao, Fengzhe Xu, Jun Tang, Ju-Sheng Zheng, Yu-ming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundObese individuals exhibit considerable heterogeneity in developing cardiovascular diseases, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. We aimed to investigate the prospective associations between obesity phenotypes, serum proteomics, and carotid atherosclerosis (CAS) incidence.MethodsThis cohort study included 3162 participants from the Guangzhou Nutrition and Health Study, with 413 proteins profiled from 6803 serum samples collected at three time-points. Obesity phenotypes included metabolically healthy non-obesity (MHNO) and metabolically healthy/unhealthy obesity (MHO/MUO).FindingsWe identified 11 proteins influenced by MUO (vs. MHO) over time, with their combined score positively associated with incident CAS (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.01–1.34). Additionally, 8 proteins were prospectively associated with MHO-to-MUO transition, which increased the performance of traditional risk factors in predicting this transition (P < 0.001). Among the 8 proteins, bidirectional mediation effects were observed between pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) (36.8%; P = 0.025) and the MHO-to-MUO transition (20.5%; P = 0.010) on CAS incidence. The PEDF genetic risk score was positively associated with MHO-to-MUO transition (OR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.00–1.43). Our main findings were validated in both the internal and external validation cohorts.InterpretationThis population-scale proteomics study broadens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying obesity heterogeneity and CAS, providing potential targets for the prevention of CAS.FundingThis study was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China, the Key Research and Development Program of Guangzhou, “Pioneer” and “Leading goose” R&D Program of Zhejiang, and the 5010 Program for Clinical Researches of the Sun Yat-sen University.Keywords: Obesity heterogeneity, Circulating proteins, Carotid atherosclerosis, Prospective study

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Obese individuals exhibit considerable heterogeneity in developing CVD, such as metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO). Previous studies suggest that circulating protein biomarkers may mediate the association between obesity and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) risk. However, the role of circulating proteins in the transition from MHO to MUO and the association between obesity heterogeneity and CVD risk remain unclear.

Added value of this study

This prospective cohort study demonstrates the temporal associations between obesity heterogeneity, serum proteomics, and incident carotid atherosclerosis (CAS). Some proteomic biomarkers are identified as prospective predictors of the transition from MHO to MUO. Additionally, it shows that proteomic biomarkers of obesity heterogeneity are associated with incident CAS risk.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying obesity heterogeneity and its association with CAS risk, suggesting potential proteomic targets for risk prediction and early intervention of CAS.

Introduction

Obesity is a leading cause of cardiometabolic diseases (CVDs), yet individuals with obesity could exhibit remarkable heterogeneity in their health status.1 Growing evidence indicates that identifying obesity phenotypes, such as metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO), may improve early risk stratification and provide precision management strategies for CVDs.2, 3, 4, 5 However, the current reliance on BMI-based obesity phenotypes ignores the difference between muscle and fat mass, leading to misclassification bias.6,7 Moreover, a large proportion of MHO individuals may transition to MUO over time,4,8 yet the molecular mechanisms underlying this transition remain largely unexplored.

Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS), characterised by the accumulation of lipids and fibrous elements in the carotid arteries, is a major contributor to CVDs.9 Approximately 27% of Chinese adults aged ≥ 30 years have CAS and the prevalence increases with age, resulting in a public health burden.9 Recent evidence suggests that the transition from MHO to MUO is associated with a higher risk of CAS,2 but the underlying mechanisms are unclear. Proteomics technology has been utilised in previous studies to investigate the mechanisms linking obesity and cardiometabolic diseases.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 For example, observational studies conducted in East Asian populations10,11 and European populations12, 13, 14 have reported associations between adiposity-associated proteomic signatures and cardiometabolic risk. Yet, these studies were limited by cross-sectional designs, largely focused on Western populations, or a limited set of candidate proteins. To our knowledge, the role of circulating proteins in the association between obesity heterogeneity and CAS risk is still unknown.

Therefore, in this community-based prospective cohort study included 3162 middle-aged and elderly adults from the Guangzhou Nutrition and Health Study (GNHS), we profiled 6803 serum samples for proteomics across three time points over a 9-year follow-up. We defined obesity phenotypes based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-derived adiposity and metabolic health status. We aimed to identify proteomic biomarkers for different obesity phenotypes and examine their temporal relationships. Then, we examined the associations of these identified proteomic biomarkers with the risk of incident CAS, and evaluated the performance of proteomic biomarkers in predicting the MHO-to-MUO transition during follow-up.

Methods

Ethics

The study protocol of the GNHS was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at Sun Yat-sen University (No. 2018048) and Ethics Committee of Westlake University (No: 20190114ZJS0003). All participants provided written informed consent.

Study design and participants

This study was based on the GNHS (ClinicalTrials.gov, ID: NCT03179657), a community-based prospective cohort study in South China. Participants were enrolled from diverse communities across Guangzhou city, primarily through local advertisements, health talks, and referrals. Sex was considered as a biological variable during study design and was recorded as assigned at birth. Briefly, 4048 participants aged 40–75 years were recruited from 2008 to 2013, and followed up every 3 years. Sociodemographic information, lifestyle factors, physical activity, and dietary intake were collected through face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires. Anthropometric measurements, body adiposity, carotid intima-media thickness, and blood samples were also obtained.15

The data utilised in this study were collected between 2008 and 2023. After excluding participants without baseline measurements for circulating proteomics profiling (n = 223), DXA-derived body adiposity (n = 606), blood pressure (n = 9), fasting glucose (n = 43), or blood lipids (n = 5), 3162 participants were finally included in the current study for subsequent analysis (Fig. S1).

For proteomic analyses, participants from the GNHS multi-omics subcohort were set as discovery cohort (n = 1857), while the remaining GNHS participants comprised the internal validation cohort (n = 1305), in accordance with our predefined study design.16 Proteins were standardised separately within each cohort for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1). Additionally, we included an independent external cohort, in which serum proteomics were profiled for 74 participants in 2017 (Supplementary methods).

Overview of the study cohort and design. This study included 3162 participants from the Guangzhou Nutrition and Health Study (GNHS), who were followed up every 3 years from 2008 to 2023. Body fat percentage (BF%) was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) and carotid plaque were assessed with carotid ultrasonography (US). Proteomics for 6803 serum samples collected across three timepoints was profiled using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS), with participants divided into discovery (n = 1857) and internal validation (n = 1305) cohorts. Participants were characterised as metabolically healthy non-obesity (MHNO), metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), and metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO) based on obesity and metabolic health status. We identified proteomic biomarkers for different obesity phenotypes and examined their temporal relationships. Then, we examined the associations of obesity phenotype-associated proteomic biomarkers with CAS incidence, and evaluated whether MHO-to-MUO transition-assoicated proteomic biomarkers could improve the predictive performance for the transition from MHO to MUO. The main findings were further replicated in an external validation cohort includded 74 participants.

Definition of obesity phenotypes

Body fat percentage (BF%) was determined using DXA (Discovery W, Hologic Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). DXA-derived obesity was defined as BF% ≥ 25% for men and ≥ 35% for women.17 For comparison, BMI-derived overweight/obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Metabolic health was defined according to the most widely used criteria.6,18,19 Participants were considered metabolically healthy with fewer than two of the following components: 1) hyperglycaemia: fasting blood glucose ≥ 6.10 mmol/L (110 mg/dL) or confirmed diabetes under treatment; 2) hypertension: blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or confirmed hypertension under treatment; 3) hypertriglyceridaemia: fasting triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or confirmed elevated triglycerides under treatment; 4) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) hypolipidaemia: fasting HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) or confirmed HDL-C hypolipidemia under treatment. Participants were characterised as metabolically healthy non-obesity (MHNO), metabolically unhealthy non-obesity (MUNO), MHO, or MUO according to their obesity and metabolic health status. Given the study's focus on obesity heterogeneity, only individuals classified as MHNO (reference group), MHO, or MUO were included in the subsequent analyses.

Assessment of carotid intima–media thickness and plaques

Carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) is a well-validated surrogate measure of subclinical atherosclerosis, and was measured at the common carotid artery using a high-solution 7.0–12.0 MHz linear-array transducer system (Aplio TOSHIBA, Japan), as detailed in our previous study.20 According to the European Mannheim consensus,21 cIMT thickening was defined as cIMT ≥ 1.0 mm, while plaque was defined as a focal cIMT ≥ 1.5 mm. The cIMT from the right and left common carotid arteries were averaged to determine cIMT thickening, with reliability coefficients of 0.98 for intra-operators and 0.85 for inter-operators.22 Participants without cIMT thickening or plaque at baseline who developed respective condition during follow-up were considered as incident CAS cases.

Circulating proteome profiling

Serum proteomics were profiled using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS), as detailed in our previous study.16,23 After quality control, 413 proteins were profiled for 6803 serum samples from the baseline, 2nd and 3rd follow up in the discovery and internal validation cohorts, and for 74 samples in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1). Detailed methods were provided in the Supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2) and Python (version 3.8.5). Baseline characteristics of study participants were presented as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Missing values of covariates at baseline were imputed using multiple imputation methods (missing rates: 0.3%–1.2%).

We performed Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine the associations of obesity phenotypes with cIMT thickening and plaque incidence separately among all participants in the GNHS cohort, adjusting for age, sex, marital status, education, income, smoking, alcohol drinking, tea consumption, physical activity, and dietary energy intake at baseline as presented in Table 1. Right censoring was applied to participants lost to follow-up or who did not develop the outcome by the end of the study. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of baseline assessment to the date of the first event, or the last follow-up visit. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was employed to assess the overall differences in serum proteins across different obesity phenotypes at each visit separately in the discovery and internal validation cohorts. To identify serum proteomic biomarkers for different obesity phenotypes, we employed generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) separately in the discovery and internal validation cohorts, adjusting for above-mentioned covariates. Serum proteins with a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 in the discovery cohort and confirmed in the internal validation cohort (P < 0.05 with the same association directions as in the discovery cohort) were identified as proteomic biomarkers for obesity phenotypes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed with the identified proteins to explore the biological functions or processes associated with different obesity phenotypes using clusterProfiler (v4.12.2).24

We conducted cross-lagged panel models to investigate the temporal relationships between the obesity phenotypes and proteomic biomarkers identified from the GLMM models, using repeated measurements of serum proteins and obesity phenotypes at baseline and the 2nd follow-up (Supplementary methods). Proteomic biomarkers with FDR <0.05 in the discovery cohort and P < 0.05 in the internal validation cohort with the same association directions were considered significant.

To assess the overall impact of obesity phenotype-influenced proteomic biomarkers on CAS risk, we constructed protein risk score (ProRS) based on biomarkers that were temporally influenced by obesity phenotypes, as identified through the cross-lagged panel models. The ProRS was calculated as follows: ProteinRiskScore=∑(proteinlevel)×(coefficientfromGLMMmodel). In both the discovery and internal validation cohorts, we performed Cox regression models and restricted cubic splines (RCS) to evaluate the prospective associations of baseline ProRS with cIMT thickening and plaque incidence separately, adjusting for age, sex, marital status, education, income, smoking, alcohol drinking, tea consumption, physical activity, and dietary energy intake at baseline as presented in Table 1. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Additionally, we also examined the associations between individual proteins and CAS incidence using Cox regression models as sensitivity analysis.

In predicting the transition from MHO at baseline to MUO at the 2nd follow-up (vs. stable MHO), we constructed two machine learning models based on Light Gradient Boosting Machine (lightGBM). The traditional model was developed with seven clinical risk factors: age, sex, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides, HDL-C, and fasting glucose at baseline. The combined model incorporated these clinical risk factors along with MHO-to-MUO transition-associated proteomic biomarkers. In the discovery cohort, predictive performance was assessed using the average area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROC) from 100 iterations of five-fold cross-validation, and Wilcoxon test was used to compare differences between models. Furthermore, the trained model from the discovery cohort was validated in the internal validation cohort, and DeLong's test was employed to compare the differences.

We employed structural equation models (SEM) to investigate the bidirectional mediation effects of proteomic biomarkers and the transition from MHO at baseline to MUO at the 2nd follow-up on the CAS incidence in the discovery cohort (Supplementary methods). To explore the potential causal associations of proteomic biomarkers with MHO-to-MUO transition, we constructed genetic risk scores (GRS) for proteomic biomarkers using the genotype frequencies of our Han participants and cis protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL; P < 1 × 10−5) identified in a Chinese population.25 Linear regression models were applied to validate the instrument in the discovery cohort, and the assumptions of linear regression were tested. Logistic regression models were employed to assess the associations between baseline GRSs and the transition from MHO to MUO (vs. stable MHO) among all participants in the GNHS cohort, adjusting for age and sex.

In the external validation cohort, we conducted logistic regression models to validate the associations of the identified proteomic signatures with obesity phenotypes and the risk of CAS prevalence, adjusted for age and sex. Due to the small sample size, significant associations were determined at a threshold of P < 0.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

The overview of the study design was presented in Fig. 1. The median age (IQR) at baseline was 60.4 (56.7, 64.5), 60.7 (56.6, 65.7), and 74.3 (68.5, 77.2) years for participants in the discovery, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively (Table 1). Compared to MHO, participants with MUO were older and exhibited more severe metabolic and disease conditions in the three cohorts (Tables S1–S3).

Comparison of BF%-based and BMI-based obesity phenotypes in risk stratification of CAS incidence

After 9 years of follow-up, 462 (22.5%) participants developed cIMT thickening and 876 (48.3%) developed carotid plaque among those free of corresponding conditions at baseline in the GNHS cohort. Compared to MHO, BF%-based MUO was associated with a higher risk of cIMT thickening (HR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.02–1.59), while BMI-based MUO showed no association (HR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.68–1.35) after adjusting for potential confounders (Fig. 2A). Similar results were observed for the incidence of carotid plaque (Fig. S2).

Identification of proteomic biomarkers for BF%-based obesity phenotypes

In both the discovery and internal validation cohorts, PLS-DA models indicated altered compositions of serum proteins across different obesity phenotypes over the three timepoints (Fig. S3). In GLMM models, we identified 37 MHO-MHNO proteins (Fig. 2B), 53 MUO-MHNO proteins (Fig. 2C), and 54 MUO-MHO proteins (Fig. 2D) (FDR discovery < 0.05 and P validation < 0.05 with the same association directions). Spearman correlation showed high consistency in the effect sizes of proteins for obesity phenotypes between the discovery and internal validation cohorts (R2 > 0.5; Fig. S4). Notably, 21 proteins were consistently identified across the MHO-MHNO, MUO-MHNO, and MUO-MHO comparisons (Fig. S5). Among them, apolipoprotein F (APOF), apolipoprotein A-I (APOA1), and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) were consistently among the top five down-regulated proteins, and complement factor H (CFAH) was among the top five up-regulated proteins (Fig. 2; Tables S4–S6). GO pathway enrichment analyses with the identified proteins indicated inflammation and dysfunction of lipid metabolism for MHO, while further disruptions in sterol homeostasis and oxidative stress for MUO (Fig. S6).

Consistent results were observed for BMI-based obesity phenotypes (Fig. S7). However, only nine proteins were identified for BMI-based MUO (vs. MHO), whereas an additional 45 proteins were identified for BF%-based MUO (vs. MHO) (Fig. S7).

Temporal relationships between obesity phenotypes and the identified proteomic biomarkers

In the direction from baseline obesity phenotypes to the 2nd follow-up proteins, cross-lagged models revealed that 22 of the 37 MHO-MHNO proteins (Fig. 3A), 44 of the 53 MUO-MHNO proteins (Fig. 3B), and 11 of the 54 MUO-MHO proteins (Fig. 3C) were significantly associated with corresponding obesity phenotypes (FDR discovery < 0.05 and P validation < 0.05 with consistent association directions). In the direction from baseline proteins to the 2nd follow-up obesity phenotypes, we did not find any protein that was significantly associated with MHO or MUO when compared to MHNO (Fig. 3A–B; Tables S7–S8). Notably, we found that eight proteomic biomarkers were prospectively associated with MHO-to-MUO transition (Fig. 3C; Table S9), including APOF, SHBG, pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3), Gelsolin (GELS), zinc finger protein Rlf (RLF), zinc finger protein 879 (ZN879), and pleckstrin homology-like domain family B member 2 (PHLB2).

Prospective associations of proteomic signatures with carotid atherosclerosis incidence

We constructed three ProRS based on the 22 MHO-MHNO proteins, 44 MUO-MHNO proteins, and 11 MUO-MHO proteins that were temporally influenced by obesity phenotypes, respectively. In the discovery cohort, baseline ProRS were positively associated with the incidence of cIMT thickening, with HRs (95% CIs) per SD increase of 1.21 (1.04, 1.42) for MHO-MHNO ProRS (Fig. 4A), 1.24 (1.07, 1.44) for MUO-MHNO ProRS (Fig. 4B), and 1.16 (1.01, 1.34) for MUO-MHO ProRS (Fig. 4C). Consistently, we observed positive associations of MHO-MHNO ProRS (HR: 1.11; 95% CI: 1.01–1.22; Fig. S8A) and MUO-MHNO ProRS (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.05–1.30; Fig. S8B) with carotid plaque incidence. Nevertheless, the association between MUO-MHO ProRS and carotid plaque incidence was not statistically significant (HR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.92–1.11; Fig. S8C). In the internal validation cohort, similar association patterns were observed between ProRS and both cIMT thickening (Fig. S9A) and plaque incidence (Fig. S9B), while the associations were marginally significant in the Cox regression models (P for trend: 0.057–0.313).

Among the 44 proteins included in the above ProRS construction, Cox regression models showed that 14 were significantly associated with cIMT thickening incidence in the discovery cohort (P < 0.05), such as APOF (HR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.72–0.90), APOA1 (HR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.72–0.91), and RLF (HR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.76–0.95) (Table S10). Additionally, five proteins were significantly associated with plaque incidence, such as CFAB (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.04–1.20) and GELS (HR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.83–0.97) (Table S11).

Associations of proteomic biomarkers with the transition from MHO to MUO

After 3.2 years of follow-up, 128 (19.3%) participants with MHO at baseline transitioned to MUO at the 2nd follow-up in the discovery cohort (Fig. S10). In predicting the 3.2-year transition from MHO to MUO (vs. stable MHO), the average AUROC of the combined model developed with traditional factors and eight MHO-to-MUO transition-associated proteins (0.70; 95% CI: 0.69–0.71) was higher than that of the traditional model (0.64; 95% CI: 0.61–0.67) in discovery cohort (P Wilcoxon < 0.001; Fig. 4D). Consistent results were observed in the internal validation cohort, with AUROCs of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.69–0.82) for the combined model and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.57–0.71) for the traditional model (P < 0.001; DeLong test; Fig. 4D).

In the discovery cohort, bidirectional mediation effects were observed between PEDF and MHO-to-MUO transition on cIMT thickening incidence (P indirect effect < 0.05 for both directions) among the eight identified proteins. Follow-up PEDF mediated 36.8% of the association between baseline MUO (vs. MHO) and cIMT thickening incidence (P indirect effect = 0.025), and MHO-to-MUO transition mediated 20.5% of the association between baseline PEDF and cIMT thickening incidence (P indirect effect = 0.010) (Fig. 4E).

Among the eight identified proteins, cis-pQTLs were available only for PEDF in the Chinese GWAS summary data, and the PEDF-GRS was constructed with nine cis-pQTLs after linkage disequilibrium clumping (R2 < 0.01) (Table S12). Linear regression model indicated that PEDF-GRS was a strong instrument in the discovery cohort (F = 34.2; variance explained: 2.20%) (Fig. 4F). Logistic regression model showed that baseline PEDF-GRS was positively associated with MHO-to-MUO transition (vs. stable MHO) among participants in the GNHS cohort (per SD OR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.00–1.43) (Fig. 4F).

Replication in the external validation cohort

In the external validation cohort, we validated the associations of some proteomic biomarkers (e.g., GPX3, PEDF, and PHLB2) with obesity phenotypes among 74 participants using logistic regression models (P < 0.1; Fig. S11). Notably, ProRS was significantly associated with an increased risk of cIMT thickening, with OR (95% CI) of 21.19 (1.42, 316.27) for the upper median (vs. lower median) of MHO-MHNO ProRS (Fig. S12), and 4.45 (1.16, 17.08) for MUO-MHO ProRS (Fig. S12). However, the association between MUO-MHNO ProRS and cIMT thickening was not statistically significant (OR: 1.44; 95% CI: 0.39–5.26; Fig. S12).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults, we identified a panel of proteomic biomarkers associated with BF%-based obesity phenotypes, which were linked to the risk of CAS incidence. Notably, eight proteomic biomarkers (i.e., APOF, SHBG, PEDF, GPX3, GELS, RLF, ZN879, and PHLB2) significantly improved the predictive performance for the transition from MHO to MUO. Among the eight proteins, we observed bidirectional mediation effects between PEDF and MHO-to-MUO transition on CAS incidence, and the genetic risk score of PEDF was associated with an increased risk of this transition. Our main findings were validated in both the internal and external validation cohorts.

Previous efforts to refine the definition of MHO have largely focused on metabolic health,6,8 with less attention on obesity definition. Although BMI is widely used as a screening tool to diagnose overweight or obesity, it is not a precise indicator for the impact of excess adiposity on health.26 In addition, previous studies indicated that the prevalence of MHO was underestimated when defined based on BMI compared to DXA-derived BF%.7 Notably, our DXA-based obesity phenotypes outperformed BMI-based phenotypes in CAS risk stratification and exhibited superior sensitivity in capturing proteomic differences between MHO and MUO. These findings provide insights into the identification of obesity phenotypes that can more accurately predict CAS risk.

The proteins we identified for both MHO and MUO were consistent with previous findings on adiposity,27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 such as increased levels of complement 3 (C3), complement factor H (CFAH), complement factor B (CFAB), complement factor I (CFAI), and transcriptional coactivator YAP1, as well as decreased levels of complement 7 (C7), APOF, APOA1, APOD, SHBG, and inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain H3 (ITIH3). Additionally, we identified 19 differential proteins between MUO and MHO that were not observed between MUO and MHNO, which appear biologically plausible. For instance, the positive associations of retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) with metabolic dysfunction in obese individuals align with its known roles in regulating energy homeostasis and metabolism.33

Interestingly, we observed that the majority of identified proteins were temporally influenced by obesity phenotypes, and their corresponding protein risk scores showed significant associations with incident CAS risk. Moreover, some individual proteins included in the score, such as APOF, APOA1, SHBG, and C3, also were significantly associated with CAS incidence. These findings aligned with previous studies showing positive associations of CFAH, C3, APOC2, and APOC3, and negative associations of APOF, APOA1, SHBG, and GPX3 with cardiometabolic health.34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40 The enrichment analysis of identified proteins suggested dysregulation in lipid metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress, in accordance with the view of CAS as a lipid-driven chronic inflammatory disease.41,42 Our findings extend previous evidence demonstrating that circulating protein biomarkers mediated the associations between obesity and CVD risk,10, 11, 12, 13 revealing potential targets for the early prevention of CVD.

Although previous studies have proposed genetic and hormonal factors as potential mechanisms underlying obesity heterogeneity,6 there is limited evidence on other biomarkers. In this study, we found that decreased levels of RLF, ZN879, APOF, gelsolin, SHBG, GPX3, and PHLB2, as well as increased level of PEDF temporally contributed to the transition from MHO to MUO. It is known that APOF, SHBG, and GPX3 could improve lipid functionality, and reduce insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress.43, 44, 45 Both RLF and ZN879 belong to zinc finger proteins, the largest family of transcription factors, which play a crucial role in regulating white adipogenesis.46 Gelsolin, one of the most abundant actin-binding proteins, protects against oxidative stress and the toxic effects of actin released from damaged cells and tissues, serving as potential therapeutic target for atherosclerosis.42,47 Depletion of PHLB2 (also known as LL5β) in pancreatic β-cells impairs the first phase of insulin secretion by disrupting focal adhesion signalling.48 PEDF, a non-inhibitory serine protease inhibitor, has been implicated in inducing insulin resistance in both animal and human studies.49,50 Interestingly, among the eight proteins, we observed bidirectional mediation effects between PEDF and MHO-to-MUO transition on CAS incidence. Moreover, we observed a positive association between the GRS of PEDF and this transition. However, we are unable to examine the potential causality of other proteins with this transition due to the lack of available cis-pQTLs in the East Asian population. Therefore, future studies are needed to validate the causality of these proteins in relation to the MHO-to-MUO transition.

The present study has several strengths. First, DXA scanning, as the gold standard for assessing body composition within research settings, addressed the limitations of BMI in distinguishing between lean and fat mass, thereby minimising the misclassification bias of obesity phenotypes. Second, the longitudinal design with repeated serum proteomics profiling reduced bias from temporal protein changes and enabled the assessment of temporal relationships among obesity phenotypes, proteomic biomarkers, and incident CAS risk. Third, our main findings were consistently validated in both the internal and external validation cohorts.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the definition of metabolic health status remains debated, although we used the most widely accepted criteria. Second, although our MS-based proteomics workflow provides unbiased, hypothesis-free, and relatively high-abundance proteome analysis,16,23 the number of proteins identified is relatively small compared to other proteomics platforms, such as the SomaScan and Olink assays. Third, as participants in both the discovery and validation cohorts were limited to middle-aged and elderly adults in South China, and the sample size of the external validation cohort was relatively small, independent validation in larger and more diverse populations is required to generalise our findings. Finally, the absence of overlapping cis-pQTLs for certain proteins limited our ability to investigate their potential causal relationships with obesity heterogeneity.

In summary, our present study has identified various important circulating protein biomarkers associated with obesity heterogeneity, and these circulating proteins are predictive of CAS incidence. Our findings provide insights into the potential mechanisms underlying both obesity heterogeneity and CAS progression, suggesting potential intervention targets for cardiovascular disease.
