High‐intensity statin therapy is associated with reduced coronary inflammation on CT in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Bryan Wu, Koen Nieman, Ryan Sandoval
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) attenuation, assessed by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), is a biomarker of coronary inflammation. Mean PCAT attenuation ≥ −70.5 Hounsfield Units (HU) corresponds to elevated inflammation and a higher future risk of myocardial infarction. While statins are known to possess anti‐inflammatory properties, the impact of statin intensity on vascular inflammation remains underexplored in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and coronary atherosclerosis. We aim to evaluate whether high‐intensity statins are associated with lower PCAT attenuation in this high‐risk cohort.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 203 CCTAs performed at our institution between 1/1/2019 and 9/1/2024 in patients with T2DM who met these key criteria: evidence of coronary atherosclerosis on CCTA, treatment with ≥1 oral hypoglycaemic agent, and no history of prior coronary interventions. PCAT attenuation was measured in the proximal right coronary artery.ResultsAmong the three statin intensity groups, patients receiving high‐intensity statins (n = 75) had the lowest PCAT attenuation (high‐intensity: −75.0 ± 6.8 HU, moderate‐intensity: −71.8 ± 8.2 HU, no statin: −71.6 ± 7.4 HU; adjusted p = 0.027). In addition to high‐intensity statins, treatment with glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist was independently associated with a lower likelihood of having elevated PCAT attenuation (≥ −70.5 HU), with an odds ratio of 0.344 (95% CI: 0.132–0.900).ConclusionsIn patients with T2DM and coronary atherosclerosis, high‐intensity statin therapy was associated with lower coronary inflammation. Our findings emphasize the need for aggressive cardiovascular prevention in this high‐risk cohort.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, diabetes complications, GLP‐1, lipid‐lowering therapy, type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Coronary atherosclerosis is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is driven by vascular inflammation which plays a pivotal role in plaque initiation, progression and destabilization. In high‐risk atheromatous plaques that are prone to rupture, a dynamic interplay between activated T cells and pro‐inflammatory macrophages accelerates the degradation of interstitial collagen in the fibrous cap and enhances plaque vulnerability. 1 In recent years, there have been several large randomized controlled trials aiming to identify therapeutic strategies to target this inflammatory pathway of atherothrombosis and yield improved cardiovascular outcomes. The LoDoCo2 trial demonstrated lower cardiovascular events in patients who had a history of chronic coronary disease and were treated with low‐dose colchicine, an anti‐inflammatory agent traditionally used in the treatment of gout. 2

Pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) attenuation, measured by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA), is an emerging imaging biomarker that reflects the local coronary inflammation. The PCAT is a subtype of the epicardial adipose tissue and is the fat deposit located adjacent to the outer wall of the coronary arteries. 3 The PCAT is hypothesized to engage in a bidirectional relationship with the coronary vessel wall by both releasing inflammatory cytokines into the vessel wall and responding to signals from vulnerable plaques that may impair lipid maturation. 3 As such, vascular inflammation leads to more immature, smaller adipocytes, which corresponds to higher Hounsfield Units (HU) on computed tomography. 3 While there is no universal definition of where PCAT attenuation should be measured, the proximal right coronary artery (RCA) has been the most validated site due to the lack of side branches and abundance of adipose tissue. 3 A PCAT attenuation ≥ −70.5 HU in the proximal RCA has been associated with a higher risk of future myocardial infarction. 4

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major risk factor for coronary atherosclerosis by promoting vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Statins, a class of 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐coenzyme A reductase inhibitors, are lipid‐lowering agents recommended for primary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in patients with diabetes mellitus. Beyond their cholesterol‐lowering properties, statins have been demonstrated to possess pleiotropic effects that can reduce inflammatory serum biomarkers, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein 5 and stabilize atherosclerotic plaques. 6 There have been a few small prospective and observational studies demonstrating the beneficial effect of statin therapy on lowering PCAT attenuation 7 , 8 , 9 but they did not specifically focus on patients with T2DM, who have a higher risk of coronary atherothrombosis. The impact of statin intensity on PCAT attenuation is also underexplored in this high‐risk cohort. The 2023 American Diabetes Association's guidelines recommend treatment with high‐intensity statin therapy in patients aged 40 to 75 years with diabetes mellitus, and one or more risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 10 As such, the primary objective of our retrospective observational analysis is to assess whether high‐intensity statin therapy is associated with lower PCAT attenuation in patients with T2DM and coronary atherosclerosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

In this retrospective study conducted at Stanford Health Care, consecutive patients with a history of T2DM who underwent CCTA from 1 Jan 2019 to 1 Sept 2024 were screened. Inclusion criteria are: aged between 40 and 80 years, diagnosis of T2DM with most recent haemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) ≤ 9.0%, body mass index (BMI) ≤ 35.0, and evidence of coronary atherosclerosis on CCTA (as defined by Coronary Artery Disease‐Reporting and Data System [CAD‐RADS] grading ≥1), computed tomography (CT) tube voltage 100–120 kV, and treatment with ≥1 oral hypoglycaemic agent for at least 6 months. To qualify for the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia, the patients must have the condition clearly documented in a physician note and meet these clinical criteria: (1) type 2 diabetes: prior fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, HbA1C ≥6.5%, random glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or a blood glucose level of ≥200 mg/dL on a 2‐hour oral glucose tolerance test; (2) hyperlipidaemia: either already on a lipid‐lowering medication or had a prior lipid panel with low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) ≥130 mg/dL and/or non‐high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL; (3) hypertension: either already on antihypertensive therapy or have documented blood pressure ≥130/80 mmHg during 2 or more clinic visits. Patients with haemoglobin HbA1C >9.0% were not included as some studies have demonstrated a link between medication non‐adherence to cardioprotective and hypoglycaemic therapies and very poor glycaemic control with HbA1C >9.0%. 11 , 12 Exclusion criteria included: history of prior coronary interventions and presence of non‐dominant right coronary artery, anomalous coronary anatomy, or obstructive stenosis in the proximal right coronary artery where the PCAT attenuation is assessed. As studies have shown higher lesion‐specific PCAT attenuation in culprit lesions of acute coronary syndrome and functionally significant lesions by fractional flow reserve, we excluded patients with obstructive lesions in the proximal RCA, which could misrepresent inflammation across the entire coronary system. 13 , 14 This study was reviewed and approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board and informed consent was waived.

Clinical and CT Imaging Data Acquisition

Patient's baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race and BMI, and clinical risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and smoking) were collected. Medication history, including statin intensity, classes of hypoglycaemic agents, and aspirin use, was obtained and verified for our study analyses if the treatment duration exceeded 6 months. High‐intensity statins are defined as atorvastatin 40–80 mg daily and rosuvastatin 20–40 mg daily, while moderate‐intensity statins are defined as atorvastatin 10–20 mg daily, rosuvastatin 5–10 mg daily, and other statins including simvastatin, pravastatin, lovastatin, fluvastatin and pitavastatin. Lipid profiles, if available within 6 months prior to the CCTA, were recorded.

The CCTAs were obtained on a third generation, dual‐source, 384 (2 × 192) slice CT scanner (Siemens Somatom FORCE, Forchheim, Germany) with a rotation time of 250 ms and a slice thickness of 0.75 mm. The scans were retrospectively EKG‐gated, and mid‐diastolic images were obtained. Patients were instructed to perform a breath‐hold during the imaging acquisition. Oral beta blockers were administered if their resting heart rate exceeded 70 beats per minute. The severity of coronary artery disease as defined by the CAD‐RADS classification system and coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores were recorded.

PCAT Analysis

The PCAT analysis was performed on the Terarecon Aquarius Intuition 4.4.13 software by a cardiologist who is board‐certified in cardiac CT and blinded to patients' medication profiles. The proximal RCA was the primary site of analysis, specifically a 4‐cm‐long segment extending from 1 cm distal to the origin of the coronary artery. The width of this segment is the diameter of the coronary artery (Figure 1). The PCAT attenuation is defined as the mean Hounsfield Unit (HU) within the range from −190 to −30 HU in this outlined region. The PCAT attenuation was adjusted for tube voltage and divided by a correction factor of 1.11485 for CCTAs with tube voltage of 100 kV as previously described. 15

Illustration of a patient's pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) in the proximal right coronary artery (4‐cm long segment extending from 1 cm distal to the coronary origin). Red and orange indicate more inflamed adipose tissue with higher PCAT attenuation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0.0. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared with unpaired Student's t‐test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables are presented as percentages of patients and analysed with chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. In the comparison of mean PCAT attenuation across three statin‐intensity groups (none, moderate or high), we performed both univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses, adjusting for age, sex, clinical risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, HbA1C and presence of microvascular complications), and severity of coronary artery disease (CAD) based on CAD‐RADS grading. In a secondary analysis, a multivariate logistics regression model was performed to identify independent predictors of elevated PCAT attenuation (≥ −70.5 HU). A two‐sided P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 1,138 patients were screened. As illustrated in Figure 2, after excluding 492 patients due to either missing information or failure to meet the baseline patient criteria, and an additional 443 patients due to failure to meet the CT imaging parameters, 203 patients were included in the final study cohort. In total, 75 patients were on high‐intensity statin therapy (H‐statin), 83 patients were on moderate‐intensity statins (M‐statin) and 45 patients were on no‐statin therapy (N‐statin). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the N‐statin patients, M‐statin patients and H‐statin patients had similar ages (65.1 ± 7.1, 64.0 ± 9.3, and 66.4 ± 9.4, respectively; p = 0.226) and proportions of male patients (66.7%, 67.5% and 69.3%, respectively; p = 0.947). H‐statin patients had a higher CAC score than M‐statin and N‐statin patients (980 ± 1306, 459.3 ± 582.8, and 497.0 ± 585.5, respectively; p < 0.001) and a greater prevalence of hyperlipidaemia (96%, 92.8% and 68.9%, respectively; p < 0.001). The most common indication for CCTA across all groups was chest pain evaluation (N‐statin: 60.0%, M‐statin: 60.2%, H‐statin: 69.3%; p = 0.420).

The majority of patients in all groups were treated with metformin (N‐statin: 91.1%, M‐statin: 90.4%, H‐statin: 94.7%; p = 0.581), and no significant differences were observed in the use of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT‐2) inhibitors (Table 1). However, aspirin use was significantly higher in the H‐statin group than in the N‐statin and M‐statin groups (53.3%, 26.7% and 36.1%, respectively; p = 0.009).

Of the entire cohort, 174 patients (86%) had lipid panel available within 6 months of CCTA acquisition. A significant difference was observed in LDL levels among the groups (H‐statin: 69.2 ± 29.9 mg/dL, M‐statin: 76.2 ± 26.8 mg/dL, N‐statin: 98.9 ± 51.0 mg/dL; p < 0.001). High‐density lipoprotein cholesterols (HDL) were comparable across the groups (H‐statin: 48.2 ± 15.4 mg/dL, M‐statin: 47.1 ± 13.9 mg/dL, N‐statin: 46.5 ± 15.3 mg/dL; p = 0.832), as were triglyceride levels (H‐statin: 133.3 ± 66.6 mg/dL, M‐statin: 149.9 ± 106.8 mg/dL, N‐statin: 159.2 ± 89.2 mg/dL; p = 0.322).

For the comparison of mean PCAT attenuation between the groups, we performed both univariate analysis and multivariate linear regression analysis, adjusting for age, sex, clinical risk factors and severity of CAD. Of the three groups, H‐statin patients had the lowest mean PCAT attenuation as shown in Figure 3 (H‐statin: −75.0 ± 6.8 HU, M‐statin: −71.8 ± 8.2 HU, N‐statin: −71.6 ± 7.4 HU; unadjusted p = 0.011; adjusted p = 0.027). With the direct two‐group comparisons, a significant difference was noted between H‐statin and M‐statin groups (unadjusted p = 0.007; adjusted p = 0.015) but no difference was observed between M‐statin and N‐statin groups (unadjusted p = 0.925; adjusted p = 0.999).

An elevated PCAT attenuation, defined as ≥ −70.5 HU in the proximal RCA, is linked to a higher future risk of myocardial infarction. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 2), high‐intensity statin therapy and treatment with GLP‐1 receptor agonist were independently associated with a lower likelihood of having elevated PCAT attenuation (high‐intensity statin: odds ratio [OR]: 0.382; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.189–0.772; GLP‐1 receptor agonist: OR 0.344; 95% CI: 0.132–0.900). Such association was not found for metformin, SGLT‐2 inhibitors, and aspirin therapy. Men had numerically higher odds of having elevated PCAT attenuation, but this did not reach statistical significance (odds ratio 1.85; 95% CI: 0.899–3.827).

DISCUSSION

In our cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and coronary atherosclerosis on CCTA, treatment with high‐intensity statin therapy was associated with lower PCAT attenuation compared with their counterpart. While several prior studies have suggested an anti‐inflammatory effect of statin therapy by PCAT attenuation, 7 , 8 , 9 they had small sample sizes and lacked specific focus on the populations with diabetes, who have a higher risk for coronary atherothrombosis. It is important to note that in this high‐risk cohort, the inflammatory modulating potential was only demonstrated with the high‐intensity statin therapy, as we found no difference in PCAT attenuation between patients on moderate‐intensity statin therapy and those who were not on statins. This association remained significant in our multivariate analyses adjusting for the severity of coronary artery disease and baseline cardiovascular risk factors.

The pleiotropic anti‐inflammatory property of statins has been presented in earlier studies and is more pronounced for high‐intensity statins. In the JUPITER trial, treatment with rosuvastatin 20 mg daily yielded a 37% reduction in their high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (CRP) level. 5 In a more recent study done by Kim et al., high‐intensity statin therapy lowered hs‐CRP significantly more than non‐high‐intensity therapy did in high‐risk patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention. 16 However, CRP is less representative of local vascular inflammation and less predictive of future myocardial infarction than PCAT attenuation. 17 The effect of different statin intensities on PCAT attenuation remains underexplored, but related research has been done with Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)‐Positron Emission Tomography (PET). A meta‐analysis by Jamialahmadi et al. reported that high‐intensity statins yielded a significant reduction in arterial wall Fluorine‐18‐FDG uptake, but the reduction by moderate‐intensity statin was non‐significant. 18 Our relatively small sample size (83 M‐statin patients and 45 N‐statin patients), in conjunction with the modest reduction in vascular inflammation associated with moderate‐intensity statin, as noted in prior studies, may explain the lack of difference in PCAT attenuation between M‐statin and N‐statin groups.

Interestingly, we also observed that patients on high‐intensity statin had higher coronary calcium scores. While this finding could represent the higher risk phenotype of patients on high‐intensity statin, it may also reflect the plaque‐stabilizing property of this therapy. Calcified plaques are representative of stable plaques, while non‐calcified, low‐attenuation plaques are more prone to rupture and cause acute myocardial infarction. In a small, prospective study conducted by Mátyás, patients who were treated with high‐intensity statins developed an increase in calcified plaque volume and a concurrent decrease in non‐calcified plaque volume. 9

Clinical guidelines emphasized aggressive lipid lowering efforts in patients with diabetes mellitus. The 2023 American Diabetes Association's guidelines recommend treatment with high‐intensity statin therapy in patients aged 40–75 years, with diabetes mellitus and one or more risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 10 In our patient cohort, only 37% of patients were on high‐intensity statin therapy, yet 99% of them had at least one risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) beyond type 2 diabetes mellitus. While this undertreatment could be explained by patient data collected prior to the 2023 guideline and possible statin intolerance, the importance of treating high‐risk, diabetic patients with aggressive lipid‐lowering therapy needs to be emphasized.

In addition to high‐intensity statin therapy, we found that treatment with GLP‐1 receptor agonist was an independent factor associated with lower likelihood of having elevated PCAT attenuation (adjusted odds ratio OR 0.344; 95% CI: 0.132–0.900). GLP‐1 receptor agonists have been shown to reduce major adverse cardiovascular outcomes, 19 , 20 , 21 and their effect on coronary inflammation has been explored in several small studies. In a sub‐study of the LIRAFLAME trial (n = 30), Jensen et al. showed that 26‐week treatment with liraglutide was associated with a reduced uptake of [64Cu]Cu‐DOTATATE, a ligand for the somatostatin receptor subtype‐2 receptor which is expressed in active M1 macrophages, in coronary vessels as measured by PET‐CT. 21 In a retrospective analysis, Biesenbach et al. reported that patients on liraglutide treatment (n = 53) had lower PCAT attenuation in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) after adjusting for baseline comorbidities but not in the RCA. 22 This study also noted a higher mean plaque burden in the LAD compared with other coronary arteries, and the authors proposed that this difference could have accounted for the more pronounced anti‐inflammatory effect associated with liraglutide in this region. Although the proximal RCA has been a standard site for PCAT evaluation, these study findings underscore the limitations of RCA‐PCAT in capturing the heterogeneous plaque burden and inflammatory changes across the entire coronary system. A more recent retrospective study by Li et al. demonstrated that patients on semaglutide treatment (n = 93) had lower PCAT attenuation in all three arteries after propensity score matching. 23 Dulaglutide has been shown to yield greater reduction in hs‐CRP than placebo but no study has evaluated this drug's effect of plaque composition. 24 Our findings contribute to the hypothesis that GLP‐1 receptor agonists may potentially lower coronary artery inflammation.

SGLT‐2 inhibitors have been shown to decrease epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) volume, 25 and recent meta‐analysis by Bao et al. showed that SGLT‐2 inhibitors may reduce EAT to a greater extent than GLP‐1 receptor agonists. 26 While EAT reflects the overall cardiac adiposity, PCAT is more specific to local coronary inflammation. In our study, treatment with SGLT‐2 inhibitors was not associated with a lower risk of having elevated PCAT attenuation. It is noteworthy that while SGLT‐2 inhibitors have shown to improve cardiovascular mortality outcomes, the benefits are more pronounced in reducing the risk of heart failure hospitalizations than in preventing myocardial infarction. 27 , 28 However, as the effect of SGLT‐2 inhibitors on coronary inflammation has not been deeply explored, further studies are warranted in this area.

Our study has several limitations. As a single‐centred, retrospective observational study, it lacks the rigour of a placebo‐controlled, randomized trial design and effect on clinical outcome. Additionally, the sample size is relatively small with 203 patients. While we focused on the impact of statin therapy on PCAT attenuation, the effects of other lipid‐lowering agents, particularly proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors and selective cholesterol absorption inhibitors, were not fully assessed, given the limited number of patients receiving these therapies (1 patient was on evolocumab and 4 patients were on ezetimibe). Furthermore, the PCAT analysis was only conducted by one imaging specialist, though previous studies have reported good interobserver variability. 29 , 30 We have also eliminated cases (n = 55) with suboptimal imaging quality, including step artefacts and blooming artefacts from major calcified plaques, that could lead to an inaccurate drawing of the vessel contour, which is a major contributor to interobserver variability. Finally, our results are not generalizable for the entire population with type 2 diabetes mellitus. As we excluded patients with a history of prior coronary interventions and only 17% of our patients required coronary interventions after the CT was completed, our results are more relevant for patients with non‐obstructive coronary artery disease and multiple ASCVD risk factors where aggressive preventative therapy is crucial. The findings are also less applicable to patients with very poorly controlled risk factors, as we excluded patients with BMI >35 or HbA1C >9.0%.

In conclusion, treatment with high‐intensity statins was associated with lower risk of elevated coronary inflammation on CCTA in our cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Aggressive cardiovascular prevention must be emphasized in this high‐risk patient cohort.
