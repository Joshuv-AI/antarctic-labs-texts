Gaining confidence in in vitro toxicity data with measured exposure concentrations
Authors: Luise Henneberger, Sophia Bardehly, Maria König, Beate I. Escher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Exposure can be measured in multiwell plate format.•Measured exposure concentrations provide information on bioavailability.•Identification of unstable chemicals with uncertain exposure.•Data generated can be used for quantitative in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation.

Introduction

Chemical risk assessment is currently transitioning from conventional animal-based testing approaches to new approach methodologies (NAMs). NAMs include a variety of different methods and tools like high-throughput in vitro test systems based on human or animal cell lines (Schmeisser et al., 2023). Optimization of cell-based in vitro bioassays has historically been conducted from a biological perspective for example by choosing certain cell types and developing detection systems for different toxicological endpoints. The chemistry behind the bioassays has so far often been neglected. The major shortcomings of most bioassay protocols from a chemist’s perspective include scarce reporting of exact medium composition (i.e., protein and lipid content) and pH value (Michl et al. 2019) and disregard of solubility limits (Aardema et al. 2011). Furthermore, the common use of animal-derived materials of unknown and variable composition like fetal bovine serum (FBS) leads to undefined chemical exposure (van der Valk et al. 2018). The fact that chemical exposure in cell-based in vitro bioassays matters has been acknowledged in the 1980s already, when several groups observed that cytotoxicity of organic chemicals and metals measured in vitro is dependent on the protein or serum content of the applied cell culture medium due to differences in protein binding (Benoit et al. 1987; Fischer 1985).

The concentration of test chemicals in cell-based in vitro bioassays is not only determined by protein binding, but partitioning to medium lipids, cells and diffusion into the well plate plastic can influence the exposure concentration as well (Groothuis et al. 2015). In addition, chemicals can be degraded during the bioassay by abiotic processes like hydrolysis or autoxidation or by cellular metabolism (Huchthausen et al. 2022). In the past decades, a variety of experimental tools as well as computational models has been developed to account for chemical partitioning and loss processes and suggestions have been made which concentration of the tested chemicals is the most relevant. A review of available in vitro distribution models is provided in Proença et al. (2021). There is consensus in the literature that the nominal concentration (Cnom), mathematically determined from the total amount of chemical that is dosed at the beginning of the assay, is not a reliable concentration metric, but it is unfortunately the only reported concentration for most of the in vitro toxicity data. Other concentration metrics that can be used are the experimentally determined total medium concentration, which differs from Cnom, if the chemical is lost from the medium during the assay or is depleted due to cellular uptake, or the cellular concentration (Ccell), which reflects the amount of chemical that has reached the cells and can either be normalized to cell count, cell mass or cell volume. Furthermore, the freely dissolved concentration (Cfree) in the aqueous phase of the medium can be used as a measure of the bioavailable concentration of the test chemical, which is especially important for hydrophobic and highly protein-bound chemicals. A more detailed explanation of the different concetration metrics can be found in the publications of Kisitu and co-workers (Kisitu et al. 2019, 2020).

The roadmap provided by Nicol et al. (2024) is intended to give guidance on which concentration metric should be used depending on the physicochemical characteristics of the tested chemical including volatility, abiotic stability, hydrophobicity, diffusion into plastic, solubility in culture medium and binding to medium proteins and lipids. Nicol et al. (2024) concluded that Cnom can often be used for next generation risk assessment, but a > 2-fold difference between nominal and total concentration needs further investigations. The determination of Cfree in the assay medium is suggested for chemicals with low bioavailability. Cfree is recommended by many other studies as well, as it reflects the concentration of the chemical that can be taken up by the cells (Escher and Hermens 2004; Groothuis et al. 2015; Heringa et al. 2004; Kramer et al. 2012). Using Cfree instead of Cnom for the extrapolation of in vitro assay data to in vivo may result in different modelling outcomes especially for chemicals that are instable and for those that show concentration dependent binding to medium and plasma proteins (Henneberger et al. 2021). For experimental determination of Cfree, solid-phase microextraction is the method of choice, because it can be applied to protein and lipid-rich samples, requires only small sample volumes and can be conducted in high-throughput using a 96-well plate format (Boyacı et al. 2018; Huchthausen et al. 2022; Roy et al. 2021). Several studies have measured exposure concentration in cell-based in vitro bioassays (De Oro-Carretero and Sanz-Landaluze 2025; Dimitrijevic et al. 2022; Groothuis et al. 2019; Hewitt et al. 2022; Kramer et al. 2012; Stadnicka-Michalak et al. 2014; Tanneberger et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2020). However, all of these studies used lower-tier well plate formats (e.g., 24-well) and larger assay volumes. Few studies have reported measured concentrations in 96- and 384-well plates (Henneberger et al. 2022; Henneberger et al. 2019b; Huchthausen et al. 2023; Huchthausen et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2023). Abiotic stability of chemicals is also not assessed routinely. While a comprehensive data set has been generated for chemical stability in DMSO stock solutions (Richard et al. 2025), a comparable data set for chemical stability in cell culture medium is missing.

The present study aims to demonstrate the usefulness of measured exposure concentrations as complementary readout in cell-based in vitro bioassays that helps to identify chemicals with low bioavailability and limited stability. A SPME method in 96-well format that is well established in the field of analytical chemistry was used (Boyacı et al. 2018; Roy et al. 2021; Vuckovic 2013) as it has the same format as the bioassays and therefore a comparable throughput. Furthermore, it requires only 200 µL of medium which allows the exposure assessment in individual wells. Four bioassays that use adherent, mammalian cell lines were selected to represent different assay types, i.e., reporter gene bioassays and image-based bioassays, and exposure media with different FBS content, i.e., FBS-free, 2 % and 10 % FBS. The first assay (PPARγ) uses genetically modified HEK293H cells and detects activation of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (Neale et al. 2017). The second assay (AREc32) is based on MCF7 AREc32 cells and is used to measure oxidative stress response (Escher et al. 2012) and the third assay (AhR) detects activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor in the rat hepatoma cell line H4L7.5c2 (Brennan et al. 2015). The fourth assay (Neurotox) detects neurotoxic effects by measuring the neurite outgrowth inhibition in the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y (Lee et al. 2022). Six chemicals were tested in each assay and Cfree was measured at the time point of dosing (0 h) and detection (after 24 h of exposure). Test chemicals with diverse physicochemical properties were selected to demonstrate, how much exposure concentrations can vary depending on the hydrophobicity and stability of the chemicals.

Methods

Test chemicals and solvents

For each of the four bioassays six test chemicals were selected (Table 1) that showed specific activity in the respective assay in previous studies or in the data from the Tox21 database (Attene-Ramos et al. 2013). Further information on the chemicals including logarithmic octanol-water partition constants (log Kow) as well as provider and purity can be found in Table S1. The chemicals represent various chemical classes including neutral as well as acidic and basic structures and cover a wide range of hydrophobicity with log Kow ranging from 0.16 (atenolol) to 5.81 (chrysene). Stock solutions of all test chemicals were prepared in methanol (Honeywell), except for chrysene and pyrene, which were dissolved in DMSO (Carl Roth), because the solubility of these chemicals was too low. The stock concentrations ranged from 2.5 g/L to 10 g/L. Solvents and chemicals used for the instrumental analysis were LC-MS grade acetonitrile, isopropanol (Chemsolute), formic acid and ammonium acetate (Honeywell). Cell-based in vitro bioassays

Four bioassays were selected for the present study (for details see Table S2). The workflow of the cell-based bioassays with exposure measurement is shown in Fig. 1. On the first day, the cells were seeded in 100 µL assay medium into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 and 100 % humidity for cell attachment. On the second day, dosing solutions were prepared for all chemicals in assay medium by either directly dissolving the test chemicals in the medium or by spiking a small volume of a stock solution in methanol or DMSO. The final methanol concentration in the bioassay was kept <1 % and the DMSO concentration was <0.1 %. No solubility issues or precipitation were observed for any of the chemicals in the tested dosing solutions. For the more hydrophobic compounds complete dissolution was achieved by ultrasonic treatment prior to the bioassay dosing. Absence of precipitates after 24 h of exposure was checked again using the images from the IncuCyte S3 (for details see below). Serial dilutions were prepared from the dosing solutions in glass-coated 96 deep-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) either manually (AREc32 and PPARγ) or using an Assist Plus System from Integra (Neurotox). For the AhR assay one replicate was prepared manually and another one with the automated procedure and no differences between both replicates were observed. 50 µL medium was removed from each well of the cell plate and 170 µL from the dosing plate were transferred to the cell plate manually or using the Assist Plus System leading to a final volume of 220 µL per well after dosing. On each plate a serial dilution of the respective reference compound was added in the last two rows of the cell plate (rosiglitazone for the PPARγ assay, tert‑butylhydroquinone for the AREc32 assay, 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin for the AhR assay and narciclasine for the Neurotox assay). The cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 and 100 % humidity. The confluency of the cells was measured before dosing the chemicals and 24 h after dosing using an IncuCyte S3 Automated Life-Cell Imager (Sartorius) to determine the cytotoxicity for the PPARγ, AREc32 and AhR assays. For the Neurotox assay the cells were seeded at low cell densities, which allows a better observation of neurite formation, but hampers the cytotoxicity detection based on cellular confluency. Therefore, the cells were stained with Nuclear Green™ and propidium iodide 24 h after dosing and fluorescence was measured using the IncuCyte S3 to detect the number of total and dead cells. For the detection of the specific endpoints the cell plates were then prepared for the different treatments. In case of the PPARγ assay, the LiveBLAzer™ FRET-B/G Loading-Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to quantify the β-lactamase produced by the viable cells, following the protocol described by Neale et al. (2017). For the AREc32 assay and AhR assay the cells, which contain a luciferase reporter gene, were treated with lysis buffer and luciferase activity was subsequently quantified using a reagent consisting of substrate buffer and luciferin (Escher et al. 2012). For the Neurotox assay the neurite length was measured with phase-contrast imaging using the IncuCyte S3 and the effect on the neurite outgrowth was expressed by the neurite length relative to the control (Lee et al. 2022). At least three independent replicates were conducted for each assay and exposure concentrations were measured for two of the replicates as described in Section 2.3 Exposure concentration measurements.Fig. 1Workflow of cell-based bioassays of the present study. Exposure concentration measurements

Exposure concentrations were measured for all test chemicals with SPME. The reference compounds were excluded, because of their extremely low dosing concentrations (see also Fig. S9). Furthermore, tert‑butylhydroquinone is a redox cycling compound, making its exposure assessment very complex (Tentscher et al. 2021). Medium aliquots from each well of the dosing plate (DP, t = 0 h) and each well containing the test chemical after 24 h incubation in the assay plate (AP, t = 24 h) were transferred to a separate plate (Axygen 96 deep-well plate) for SPME (Fig. 1). The optimization of the pin device SPME method is described in the SI section S3. Method optimization included the determination of pin-water distribution ratios (Dpin/w), sorption kinetics (Fig. S1+2) and isotherms (Fig. S3). In the final protocol, 155 µL of the medium aliquots from the DPs were transferred to the SPME sample plate, which already contained 45 µL of blank medium to adjust the dilution factor to the APs. From the APs 200 µL of the medium was transferred without further dilution. The SPME sample plates were shaken for 3 min (Bioshake iQ, Q Instruments) and preheated to 37 °C for 15 min. For SPME a Supel™ BioSPME 96-Pin Device from Supelco coated with C18/PAN (parameters according to the manufacturer: pin diameter 1 mm, coating length 1.5 mm, coating thickness 17.6 µm and coating volume 80 nL) was employed. The workflow for the 96-pin SPME from Huchthausen et al. (2022) was used. In short, the workflow consisted of four steps. First, the pin device was conditioned in a glass-coated deep-well plate containing 800 µL of isopropanol per well for 20 min, followed by a wash step in a glass-coated deep-well plate containing 900 µL of MilliQ water per well for 10 s. After conditioning and wash, the pin device was immersed into the SPME sample plate containing 200 µL of the medium aliquot per well and shaken at 37 °C and 1000 rpm for 1 h (Bioshake Q1, Q Instruments). The time for the transfer was <6 s to avoid drying of the pin coating. A separate desorption plate with 200 µL of the respective desorption solvent (Table S3) per well was prepared. After transferring the pin device to the desorption plate, the plate was shaken at 10 °C and 1000 rpm for 2 h (Bioshake Q1, Q Instruments) to ensure complete desorption for all test chemicals. To avoid evaporation losses from the sample and desorption plates, the pin devices were fixed on top of the plates with laboratory sealing film (Parafilm) during the shaking process. After the desorption step the pin device was removed from the plate, the plates were sealed and stored at 4 °C until instrumental analysis.

Instrumental analysis

The chemical concentration in the samples was analyzed and quantified using a liquid chromatography instrument (1260 Infinity II, Agilent). The detection was performed using either a fluorescence detector (1100/1200 FLD), a diode array detector (DAD) or a triple quad mass spectrometer (Triple Quad 6420, Agilent). The LC columns used included a Kinetex® C18, 1.7 µm, 50 × 2.1 mm column, a Luna® Omega Polar C18, 1.6 µm 50 × 2.1 mm column and a Kinetex® EVO C18, 1.7 µm 50 × 2.1 mm column. More details on the instrumental analysis can be found in the SI, Tables S4-S6. Calibration standards with a concentration range of 1–10.000 ng/mL in desorption solvent or PBS (for the determination of isotherms and pin-water distribution) and acetonitrile blanks were measured together with the samples.

Data evaluation

Calculation of freely dissolved concentrations and fractions

The concentration of the test chemicals in the coating of the pin device (Cpin) was calculated from the measured concentration in the desorption solution (Cdes), the volume of the desorption solution (Vdes) and the volume of the pin coating (Vpin) using Eq. (1).(1)Cpin=Cdes×VdesVpin

Cpin was used to calculate the freely dissolved concentration in the extracted sample (Cfree). Cell-based in vitro bioassays

All concentration response curves (CRCs) were fitted twice, first, using the nominal concentrations to derive the nominal effect concentrations (IC10,nom, EC10,nom and ECIR1.5,nom) and second, using the measured freely dissolved concentrations to derive the freely dissolved effect concentrations (IC10,free, EC10, free and ECIR1.5, free).

For the PPARγ assay, the AREc32 assay and the AhR assay the cell viability was determined by measuring and comparing the confluency of the exposed and unexposed cells 24 h after chemical exposure (ratio of confluency of exposed to average confluency of unexposed cells). The concentration, which led to a reduction of the cell confluence of 10 % is the IC10 and was calculated from the linear range of the CRCs as described by Escher et al. (2018). For the Neurotox assay the cell viability was derived by dividing the number of viable cells (total – dead cells) with the number of total cells. A log-logistic model was used for the analysis as previously described by Lee et al. (2022).

For the AREc32 assay the induction ratio (IR) was calculated by dividing the luminescence of the samples with the average signal of the unexposed cells. The concentration, which led to an IR of 1.5 (ECIR1.5) was used as an activity benchmark and was calculated from the linear range of the concentration response curves below the IC10 (Escher et al. 2012). The IC10 was used as a cut-off to avoid artifacts in the interpretation of the reporter gene assay results due to the cytotoxicity burst phenomenon (Judson et al. 2016). For the AhR and PPARγ assays the CRCs of the reference compounds were used to determine the maximum effect. The concentration which led to 10 % of the maximum effect (EC10) was calculated from the linear range of the concentration response curves below the IC10 (Escher et al. 2018). For the Neurotox assay the effect on the neurite outgrowth was expressed by calculating the neurite length of the exposed cells relative to the unexposed cells. All effect data were included for the analysis and the EC10 was derived using a log-logistic model as described by Lee et al. (2022).

Specificity analysis

The specificity ratio (SR) for each chemical was calculated using the IC10 for cytotoxicity and the specific effect concentrations (EC10 or ECIR1.5) (Eq. (12)), either derived from nominal (SRnom) or freely dissolved effect concentrations (SRfree).(12)SR=IC10EC10orECIR1.5

Effects with SR ≤ 1 are considered non-specific, 1 ≤ SR ≤ 10 moderately specific, 10 ≤ SR ≤ 100 specific, and effects with SR ≥ 100 are highly specific (Escher et al. 2020). If no cytotoxicity is detected, SR can also be calculated from the IC10,baseline derived by (9), (10) (SRbaseline).

Results

Method optimization

The SPME method was successfully optimized for all test chemicals. The times to reach 95 % equilibrium between the pin coating and either PBS or medium as well as the slope and intercept of the sorption isotherms and the determined Dpin/w are summarized in Table S3. The majority of the chemicals reached equilibrium between the pin coating and the medium samples within 1 h, allowing a fast extraction of the analytes from the sampled medium (Fig. S1). Only some chemicals showed longer equilibration times in medium: indomethacin (2.4 h), citalopram (3 h), metolachlor (3.7 h), 4C3MP (5.9 h), quinoxyfen (7.6 h) and atorvastatin (14.8 h). An extraction time of 1 h was chosen for the medium aliquots sampled from the dosing and assay plates. For metolachlor, 4C3MP, quinoxyfen and atorvastatin this sampling point was definitely not in equilibrium, but because the medium composition was the same in all samples, the uptake to the pin coating can be assumed to be the same in all samples (Kramer et al. 2007). Equilibration times were usually longer in PBS for hydrophobic chemicals (Table S3. and Fig. S2). Atorvastatin was the only chemical that showed much faster uptake kinetics in PBS (0.25 h) compared to medium (14.8 h). Hydrophobic chemicals with log Dlip/w > 5 (quinoxyfen, pyrene and chrysene) did not reach equilibrium between pin coating and PBS within 24 h and Dpin/w was determined using an indirect method as described in SI section S3.

Sorption to the pin coating was found to be concentration dependent for 9 of the 24 chemicals (Table 2). For those chemicals, Dpin/w was not constant, and the concentration dependence had to be considered for the calculation of Cfree (see (6), (7) in Section 2.5.1). Nonlinear sorption was mainly observed for acidic chemicals (e.g., clofibric acid and bezafibrate), which is in line with previous studies using C18-coated SPME metal fibers (Henneberger et al. 2019a; Huchthausen et al. 2020). In general, the sorption to the pin coating increased with increasing hydrophobicity. The determined log Dpin/w ranged from 0.9 (atenolol) to 3.96 (chrysene). For 13 of the 24 chemicals the pin extraction depleted the concentration in the medium sample and Cfree had to be calculated based on the mass balance (see (5), (7) in Section 2.5.1). The highest fraction in the pin coating was measured for metolachlor (24.6 %).

Measured exposure concentrations

Exposure concentrations, i.e. Cfree, were measured for all test chemicals at two time points (t = 0 h and t = 24 h) with two independent replicates each (for all data see Table SA in the Supplementary Excel file and Fig. S10-S13) and the average ffree was calculated (Table 2). At the beginning of the exposure the measured ffree of the hydrophilic test chemicals with log Dlip/w< 2.5 was > 70 % (Fig. 2A). With increasing hydrophobicity of the test chemicals, ffree in the medium decreased due to protein and lipid binding. The most hydrophobic test chemical of the present study (chrysene) had a ffree of only 1.4 % at the time point of dosing. Interestingly, the pesticide methiocarb showed the lowest ffree of only 0.4 % at the time point of dosing, even though this chemical is not very hydrophobic (log Dlip/w of 3.54). The measured ffree in the dosing plates agreed within a factor of 8 with ffree predicted by the mass balance model (Eq. (8)) for most of the chemicals (Fig. 2B). After 24 h of chemical exposure the ffree decreased >20 % for 12 of the 24 chemicals in the assay plates, including all chemicals with to log Dlip/w > 3.5, but also several hydrophilic chemicals (Fig. 2C). For dichlorvos, carbaryl and methiocarb the ffree decreased >80 % most likely due to abiotic degradation processes. Methiocarb could only be detected in the highest dosed concentrations close to the quantification limit of the LC-MS/MS method. Despite the observed losses for half of the chemicals, the measured ffree in the assay plates still agreed within a factor of 10 with the ffree predicted by the mass balance model. Only the measured ffree of carbaryl and methiocarb were a factor of 36 and >8000 below the predictions (Fig. 2D).

Some of the hydrophilic chemicals (chloridazon, benzothiazolone, bezafibrate, atenolol and 2A3M3HIQ) showed consistently too high ffree >100 % at both times points. This phenomenon has been observed previously with C18-coated metal fibers and is most likely caused by medium constituents that influence the sorption of the chemicals to the SPME material (Huchthausen et al. 2020). Nevertheless, the determined ffree at both time points can be compared to assess chemical stability.

Cell-based in vitro bioassays

The dose response curves of the tested chemicals were fitted twice, using either nominal (Fig. S5-S8) or freely dissolved concentrations (Fig. S14-S17). The derived effect concentrations of all test chemicals can be found in Tables S7 and S8. Dose response curves of the reference compounds are shown in Fig. S9. Cytotoxicity could be measured for the majority of the chemicals. Chrysene and 2A3M3HIQ showed no cytotoxicity at the highest dosed concentrations in all replicates. Further increase of the concentration was not possible due to visible precipitation. 7D4MC and pyrene did not show cytotoxicity in the replicates with SPME measurement, but were slightly cytotoxic in the replicate without SPME. Therefore, only IC10,nom, but not IC10,free could be calculated.

For most of the chemicals the IC10,nom and IC10,free agreed within a factor of 10 (Fig. 3A). For the unstable chemicals dichlorvos, carbaryl and methiocarb, the IC10,free was more than a factor of 10 below the IC10,nom. For the hydrophobic chemical quinoxyfen, IC10,free was 53-times lower than IC10,nom. Specific effects were detected for all chemicals in the respective bioassays as expected from the literature data (see Table S1). The TRs calculated from the IC10,nom and IC10,free agreed well for most of the chemicals, only for methiocarb and carbaryl the TR increased by more than a factor of 10 when using the IC10,free compared to IC10,nom (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, the SR did not change, if freely dissolved effect concentrations were used instead of nominal concentrations (Fig. 3D). However, for both chemicals, no IC10,free for cytotoxicity could be determined and SRfree was calculated based on the predicted IC10,free.baseline, while SRnom was derived from the experimental IC10,nom. For pyrene the difference between SRnom and SRfree has two different reasons: (i) the measured IC10,nom is higher than expected (TR 0.3) leading to a higher SRnom, (ii) the measured EC10,free is nine times higher than predicted by the MBM leading to a lower SRfree. Discussion

The data of the present study confirm that binding to medium proteins and lipids reduces the ffree and therefore the bioavailability of many test chemicals. The lowest ffree were observed for hydrophobic chemicals in media containing 10 % FBS. For example, pyrene and chrysene had ffree of only 3.39 and 0.67 % in the assay plates (Table 2). The medium of PPARγ assay had the lowest protein content (Table S2) and consequently all chemicals tested in this assay except quinoxyfen had high ffree. The 24 test chemicals of the present study are covering more than five orders of magnitude in hydrophobicity, with log Kow ranging from 0.16 (atenolol) to 5.81 (chrysene) and include also acidic and basic structures (Table S1). The SPME method performed well for all but the extremely hydrophilic compound, for which Cfree was consistently too high (Table 2). The method is therefore especially suitable for moderately hydrophobic and hydrophobic neutral and charged chemicals.

Half of the tested chemicals also showed a decrease of >20 % in concentrations over time, which indicates that other loss processes are relevant as well. While a loss of 20–50 % might not be of high significance as this is typically within the error of the bioactivity measurements, the substantial losses observed for dichlorvos, methiocarb and carbaryl cannot be neglected. For hydrophobic chemicals the loss over time is most likely caused by slow diffusion into well plate plastics and/or cellular uptake. However, loss of hydrophilic chemicals cannot be explained by sorption to plastic or cells and must be due to degradation or partitioning to the air. Partitioning to the air is unlikely the cause for the observed losses, because all of the chemicals are non-volatile (Henry’s Law constants of all chemicals can be found in Table S1). The observed degradation of dichlorvos, methiocarb and carbaryl complicates the interpretation of the in vitro bioassay results. All three chemicals showed specific activation of oxidative stress response (dichlorvos) or inhibition of neurite outgrowth (carbaryl and methiocarb). The question that arises is, whether the observed effects are caused by the rapidly decreasing parent compound or the formed transformation products (TPs) or by the mixture of both. Because dichlorvos, methiocarb and carbaryl are data-rich chemicals with known degradation pathways, it is possible to make suggestions on the concentration metric that should be used for QIVIVE and hazard assessment.

Dichlorvos rapidly hydrolyzes in aqueous solutions, which leads to the formation of dimethylphosphoric acid and the highly reactive dichloroacetaldehyde (DCA) (Oncescu and Oancea 2007). DCA can probably induce oxidative stress response as well, similar to chloroacetaldehyde, which is known to induce oxidative stress by glutathione depletion (Pourahmad et al. 2012). The effects observed in the present study are therefore most likely caused by dichlorvos and at least one of its degradation products. Reporting the effect concentration based in the measured Cfree of dichlorvos is therefore not recommended, but rather the total/nominal concentration dosed should be used or the mixture of parent and TP needs to be further evaluated.

Methiocarb and carbaryl are carbamates that are slowly hydrolyzed in water, but the hydrolysis rate increases in the presence of serum albumin (Fujino et al. 2016; Sogorb et al. 2007). Methiocarb is hydrolyzed to methiocarb sulfoxine phenol and methylcarbamic acid and carbaryl is degraded to 1-naphthol and methylcarbamic acid. The observed inhibition of neurite outgrowth is most likely caused by the parent chemicals carbaryl and methiocarb only, because both carbamates inhibit acetylcholinesterase. In this case the potency of carbaryl and methiocarb is underestimated, if the nominal effect concentration is used, because the concentration of both chemicals is decreasing over time, while the measured Cfree after 24 h of exposure may overestimate the potency. As an alternative the area under the curve of Cfree over time might be used.

Unstable chemicals with no further information on degradation pathways and activity of the TPs are more challenging to assess. Computational models may be used to predict possible TPs, but these models are not tailored to in vitro bioassays (Huchthausen et al. 2022). Exposure assessment for unstable chemicals should therefore be complemented by suspect and non-target screening approaches. If standards of TPs are commercially available, those should be tested in the in vitro bioassay as well. Further information on the activity of the TPs may also be gained by varying the exposure conditions, e.g., by testing unstable chemicals with and without pre-incubation of the medium or by forcing degradation processes like autoxidation by adding oxidizing agents like abiotic CYP enzymes (Henneberger et al. 2024).

The cell lines used in the AREc32 (MCF7) and AhR (rat hepatoma cells) bioassays may also be able to metabolize some of the test chemicals like the PAHs pyrene and chrysene. The MCF7 AREc32 cells were found to metabolize benzo[a]pyrene in a previous study (Fischer et al. 2020). To confirm this hypothesis, the medium from the assay plates should be analyzed for the hydroxylated metabolites of pyrene and chrysene.

Interestingly, specificity of effects expressed as SR did not change for the chemicals of the present study, if Cfree was used for the calculation instead of Cnom (Fig. 3D). This is reasonable, because all chemicals had a comparable ffree at both effect concentrations (IC and EC). This means that the absolute IC and EC values change, but not their ratio. This is not always the case. Chemicals that show highly concentration dependent binding to medium proteins like perfluorinated acids and certain pharmaceuticals may have different SRnom and SRfree (Henneberger et al. 2021; Qin et al. 2023). While SRnom and SRfree are similar for many chemicals, the absolute potency of the highly bound and rapidly degrading chemicals can change by orders of magnitude. For 7 of the 24 chemicals the effect concentrations calculated from the measured Cfree after 24 h of exposure differed more than a factor of 10 from the nominal effect concentration (Fig. 3B).

Conclusion

Application of NAMs for regulatory purposes requires exact knowledge of the bioavailable chemical concentrations in these tests. Measured exposure concentrations can be used as additional readout for cell-based in vitro bioassays and help to choose the correct dose metric for QIVIVE and chemicals hazard assessment. Fig. 4 illustrates how much the concentration can differ for different substance classes depending on the chemical stability and hydrophobicity. The Cnom that is dosed at the beginning of the bioassays is only a suitable dose metric for hydrophilic chemicals that do not degrade during the bioassay. For chemicals that show strong binding to proteins and/or lipids like organic acids or hydrophobic chemicals (log Kow > 3.5), Cfree in the assay medium should be measured. SPME is a useful method for the determination of Cfree, but other methods like equilibrium dialysis can be used as well. Furthermore, for the majority of neutral organic chemicals mass balance models should provide estimates of Cfree within one order of magnitude (see also Fig. 2). Less accurate predictions can be expected for charged chemicals, for which protein binding cannot easily be predicted. The most problematic chemicals are those that undergo chemical transformation processes irrespective of their hydrophobicity. Choosing the correct dose metric for these chemicals does not only require the quantification of the parent chemical, but the formation of TPs needs to be considered. In many cases, degradation will lead to less toxic TPs, but the formation of equally or even more potent TPs is possible and needs to be taken into consideration. Furthermore, computational tools for the prediction of abiotic stability have limited applicability for in vitro bioassays (Huchthausen et al. 2022). Special attention should also be paid to chemicals with poor solubility. Solubility does not always correlate with hydrophobicity and experimental values are often missing. It is therefore advisable to measure the total concentration in the medium for poorly soluble compounds as precipitation may not always be visible.

For future implementation of exposure assessment into high-throughput in vitro toxicity screening, we recommend to use a tiered approach, e.g., the one suggested by Nicol et al. (2024). Chemical stability in bioassay medium should be tested first using a simple extraction method, e.g., protein precipitation. Measuring Cfree is only necessary for chemicals that show strong sorption to proteins and lipids in the medium. Even though we found a decrease in Cfree over time, this loss was not substantial for stable chemicals. It is therefore not always necessary to measure the binding to medium components in samples from the assay plates. A direct measurement of Cfree in the assay plates is only required, if loss processes other than protein and lipid binding are expected to significantly contribute to the mass balance, e.g. for assays that use very low contents of FBS in the medium or assays that use cell lines with high metabolic activity. Exposure assessment can therefore run in parallel to the bioassay screening for many test chemicals and can also be conducted “offline” in simulated dosing medium in a separate lab or institute.
