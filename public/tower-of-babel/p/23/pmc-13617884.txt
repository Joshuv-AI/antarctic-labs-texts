Targeting the cGAS-STING pathway alleviates neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment induced by chronic infection of Toxoplasma gondii
Authors: Yihui Xing, Huiling Lv, Peixuan He, Yongyao Xu, Weifan Shen, Ziyan Gu, Fan Zeng, Yifan Zhang, Kai Sui, Yurui Shi, Yinghua Yu, Wei Pan, Cheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic infection of Toxoplasma gondii has been established as a contributor to cognitive impairment via inducing sustained neuroinflammation and synaptic damage. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. As a key regulator of both neuroinflammation and cellular senescence, Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway is implicated in pathogenesis induced by T. gondii infection. Here, we found that cGAS-STING pathway was activated in the cerebral cortex of mouse chronically infected with T. gondii, as indicated by the elevated protein levels of cGAS and STING, and increased phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3. Pharmacological inhibition of this pathway with RU.521 and H151, specific inhibitors of cGAS and STING, significantly alleviated T. gondii-induced cognitive impairment and neuronal damage. Moreover, chronic T. gondii infection was shown to trigger senescence characterized by increased expression of senescence markers P16, P21 and P53, and senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs), including Il-1β, Il-6, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, Cxcl10 and Mmp9. In addition, elevated expression of β-galactosidase, a senescence marker, was predominantly observed in neurons compared to microglia and astrocytes, indicating a primary role for neurons in infection-associated senescence. Notably, these phenotypes of senescence were rescued by inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that chronic infection of T. gondii activates the cGAS-STING pathway, which in turn drives neuroinflammation and cognitive dysfunction in which neuronal senescence plays a contributory role. Introduction

With the global population aging at an unprecedented rate, neurodegenerative diseases pose an escalating socio-economic burden worldwide [1, 2], underscoring an urgent need to elucidate their underlying mechanisms and identify therapeutic targets. Beyond classical genetic and metabolic risk factors, a pathogen-centric perspective has gained traction, highlighting chronic pathogenic infections as potent drivers of cognitive decline [3, 4]. Among these, Toxoplasma gondii, a highly prevalent neurotropic parasite [5], offers a compelling model to study infection-mediated neurodegeneration. Mounting clinical evidences show that chronic infection with T. gondii is associated with an elevated risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment across multiple domains [5, 6]. During persistent infection, T. gondii forms cysts in host brain, preferentially within neurons [7], inducing sustained neuroinflammation, synaptic damages, and ultimately cognitive deficits [8, 9]. These observations prompt a deeper investigation into the cellular and molecular pathways through which this parasite disrupts neural homeostasis.

Cellular senescence, a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest driven by DNA damage, oxidative stress and other stressor, has recently emerged as a key mechanism in aging and neurodegeneration [10–12]. Senescence-associated SASPs are recognized to propagate inflammatory signaling and accelerate neuronal damages [13]. As an orally infected pathogen, T. gondii initially infects the host intestine and is indicated to induce colonic cellular senescence, as evidenced by the upregulation of serval senescence-associated markers and SASPs [14]. Notably, this senescent response is positively associated with impaired intestinal barrier function characterized by reduced mucus layer thickness and downregulated tight junction proteins [14]. Consistent with this association, amelioration of cellular senescence by butyrate supplementation contributes to the restoration of intestinal barrier damage induced by chronic T. gondii infection [15]. Here, T. gondii further disseminate to all organs of the body [16], especially the brain [17], to establish persistent infection, triggering excessive neuroinflammation [18] and neuronal impairments [19]. However, despite being the primary target cells of T. gondii in host brain, it remains unknown whether neurons undergo senescence during chronic infection and how such senescence contributes to the associated neuropathological damages.

Contrary to the long-held view that senescence exclusively affects proliferating cells, recent studies indicate that neurons can also enter a senescent state in response to stressor such as genomic instability and mitochondrial dysfunction [20]. These senescent neurons not only exhibit classic senescence markers such as P16 and P21, but also secrete SASPs thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation. Notably, senescent neurons have been identified via single-cell sequencing in the brains of AD patients [21]. Moreover, experimental models reveal that chronic T. gondii infection drives neuroinflammatory processes and cognitive deficits via inducing an excessive H2A.X-dependent DNA double-strand break signaling response [22], a key hallmark of cellular senescence [23, 24]. These observations indicate neuronal senescence as a plausible mechanistic link between chronic T. gondii infection and neurodegeneration. However, the upstream signaling events initiating this senescence program during chronic T. gondii infection remain poorly defined.

The cGAS-STING pathway has recently been implicated in both neuroinflammation and cellular senescence [25]. cGAS can detect cytosolic DNA from invading pathogens or damaged mitochondria, and then activates STING, leading to the phosphorylation of IRF3 and NF-κB [26]. This cascade stimulates type I interferon production and proinflammatory cytokine release [27, 28]. Beyond its canonical role in pathogen infection, persistent cGAS-STING activation is reported to promote cellular senescence through IRF3-retinoblastoma protein interactions that induce cell-cycle arrest [29]. Importantly, this pathway has been linked to AD pathogenesis, as demonstrated by the ability of genetic or pharmacological inhibition of cGAS or STING to mitigate amyloid pathology, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficits in preclinical models [30, 31]. Interestingly, T. gondii-derived pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMP) is reported to activate the cGAS-STING pathway to facilitate its replication, thereby causing pathology [32]. Moreover, T. gondii infection leads to mitochondrial damage, characterized by cristae disruption, swelling and membrane rupture, resulting in mitochondrial DNA leakage into the cytosol, which may act as an endogenous cGAS activator [33, 34]. Together, these observations raise a plausible model whereby T. gondii activates cGAS-STING signaling. However, it is still unknown whether this pathway drives neuronal senescence and cognitive impairment during chronic infection of T. gondii.

Here, using a model of chronic T. gondii infection, we observed concomitant activation of the cGAS-STING pathway with neuronal senescence in the prefrontal cortex. Using selective inhibitors of cGAS (RU.521) and STING (H-151), we showed that pharmacological disruption of this pathway attenuates both neuronal senescence and cognitive deficits in mice chronically infected with T. gondii. Overall, our findings identify the cGAS-STING pathway as a critical mechanistic link between T. gondii infection and neuronal senescence, revealing new therapeutic avenues for mitigating infection-associated neurodegeneration.

Result

cGAS-STING activation induced by chronic T. gondii infection positively correlates with neuronal damage and cognitive deficits

Given the association between chronic infection of T. gondii and neurodegeneration [35], we sought to elucidate the underlying mechanism involved in this process. To achieve this goal, we employed a chronic T. gondii infection model established by orally gavaging cysts (Fig. 1A), and conducted transcriptomic profiling of the prefrontal cortex, a key brain region for cognition (Fig. 1A). Intriguingly, Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of these transcriptomic data revealed that cGAS-STING pathway was significantly enriched in the prefrontal cortex of mice chronically infected with T. gondii compared to controls (Fig. 1B). Consistently, multiple genes within this pathway were also found to be significantly upregulated in the infected mice (Fig. 1C). Subsequent experimental validation confirmed the activation of cGAS-STING pathway in the prefrontal cortex induced by chronic T. gondii infection, as indicated by the elevated protein levels of key signaling molecules, including cGAS, STING, p-TBK1 and p-IRF3 (Fig. 1D-K).

In view of the potential roles of chronic T. gondii infection and cGAS-STING pathway activation in cognitive impairment [36–38], we therefore investigate the expression of PSD95 and SYN, two robust indicators of cognitive function [39], in the prefrontal cortex of infected mice. As a result, we observed that chronic T. gondii infection significantly reduced the protein levels of PSD95 and SYN (Fig. 1L-O), and their expression was negatively correlated with the upregulation of key cGAS-STING components (Fig. 1P-S). Taken together, these results suggest that cGAS-STING pathway is involved in T. gondii-induced synaptic dysfunction and consequently, cognitive deficits.

Activation of cGAS-STING pathway contributes to T. gondii-induced cognitive impairment and neuronal damage

To investigated the essential roles of cGAS-STING pathway in promoting cognitive deficits and neuronal dysfunction during chronic T. gondii infection, we interrogated its pathological role by employing the cGAS-specific inhibitors, RU.521 and H151, in chronically infected mice (Fig. 2A-B). After blocking the activation of cGAS-STING pathway with RU.521 (Fig. 2C-G), the cognitive impairment induced by chronic T. gondii infection was revealed to be rescued, as evidenced by restored performance in temporal order memory test, novel object recognition test, Y-maze alternation working memory and nesting experiment (Fig. 2H-O). Accordingly, this functional recovery was consistent with robust amelioration of neuronal and synaptic pathology. In the hippocampus, RU.521 significantly enhanced neuronal complexity, reflected by increases in total neurite length, branch points, and Sholl intersection numbers (Fig. 2P-T). Synaptic integrity was also restored, as indicated by significantly elevated levels of PSD95 and SYN proteins (Fig. 2U-W). These findings demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of cGAS attenuated neuropathology and cognitive decline caused by chronic T. gondii infection.

To further elucidate the essential roles of cGAS-STING pathway in regulating T. gondii-induced cognitive dysfunction, we also inhibited the downstream effector STING using H151 (Fig. 3A-D). Similar to RU.521, H151 treatment not only rescued cognitive deficits in all behavioral tests (Fig. 3E-L), but also restored neuronal morphology and synaptic protein expression (Fig. 3M-T), reinforcing that blockade of the cGAS-STING pathway mitigates infection-induced synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline. Taken together, our findings identified the activation of cGAS-STING pathway as a key contributor to T. gondii-induced cognitive deficits and neuropathology.

Inhibition of cGAS-STING pathway exerts no effect on parasite burden during chronic T. gondii infection

Given cGAS-STING-associated proinflammatory factors, including IFNγ/TNFα-dependent immune effector, are reported to control the replication of T. gondii during acute infection [40, 41], we investigated the expression of these genes in prefrontal cortex of chronically infected mice following cGAS or STING inhibitor treatment. As a result, we found administration of the cGAS inhibitor RU.521 significantly reduced the elevated mRNA levels of Gbp1, Gbp2, Gbp5, Irgb6, Isg15, and iNOS induced by chronic T. gondii infection, whereas the expression of Gbp3, Gbp4, and Irga6 showed no obvious change (Fig. 4A-B). Similarly, treatment with the STING inhibitor H151 also suppressed a subset of these effectors, including Gbp1, Gbp2, Isg15, and iNOS (Fig. 4C-D). These results suggest that pharmacological inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway partially shuts down cGAS-STING-associated immune responses against chronic T. gondii infection.

In contrast, neither RU.521 nor H151 treatment altered the number of brain cysts or the abundance of parasite genomic DNA in chronically infected mice (Fig. 4E-H). In parallel, inhibition of the cGAS -STING pathway had no significant effect on the transcriptional levels of the tachyzoite marker SAG1, the bradyzoite marker BAG1, or their ratio (Fig. 4I-N). These results indicate that blockade of the cGAS-STING pathway is insufficient to drive cyst conversion toward a replicative and lytic form. Taken together, despite partial suppression of cGAS-STING-associated immune effectors, this reduction does not disrupt the host-parasite immune equilibrium, thereby failing to alter the cyst burden.

Chronic T. gondii infection induces neuronal senescence in mice prefrontal cortex

Accumulation of senescent cells is one of the key drivers of neurodegenerative disease progression [42]. Interestingly, GSEA analysis of cortical transcriptome data revealed significant enrichment of cellular senescence (Fig. 5A) and upregulation of senescence-related genes in the prefrontal cortex of mice chronically infected with T. gondii (Fig. 5B). Subsequently validation confirmed that chronic T. gondii infection not only elevated the expression of three key senescence markers, P53, P16 and p21 (Fig. 5C-G), but also upregulated the transcriptional levels of several SASPs-related molecules, Il-1β, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Mmp9 (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results indicated chronic infection of T. gondii resulted in cell senescence in the prefrontal cortex of mice. As β-galactosidase (β-gal) is a well-characterized senescence-associated marker [43], we employed β-gal co-staining with markers for microglia, astrocytes and neurons to determine the specific senescent cell populations responsible for the cognitive deficits induced by chronic T. gondii infection. As a result, we found neuronal senescence was most predominant in the cerebral cortex of chronically infected mice, as evidenced by the highest number of senescent neurons (β-gal+NeuN+ cells) (Fig. 5I). Collectively, these findings established that cortical neuronal senescence was a key pathological hub linking chronic T. gondii infection to cognitive dysfunction.

Inhibition of cGAS-STING pathway attenuates neuronal senescence induced by chronic T. gondii infection

To dissect the link between the cGAS-STING pathway and neuronal senescence, we assessed senescence-related markers after RU.521 intervention. Administration with RU.521 significantly reduced the co-staining ratio of β-gal and NeuN in the cerebral cortex of T. gondii-infected mice (Fig. 6A-B). Consistently, it markedly downregulated the expression levels of P53, P16 and p21 (Fig. 6C-G) and suppressed the mRNA expression of several SASPs-related molecules, including Il-1β, Il-6, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Mmp9, in T. gondii-infected cortical tissue (Fig. 6H-M), indicating that cGAS inhibition alleviates infection-induced neuronal senescence. In parallel, the subsequent application of the STING inhibitor H151 recapitulated the effects of cGAS inhibition, demonstrating a consistent suppression of neuronal senescence, as evidenced by a reduced β-gal/NeuN co-staining ratio (Fig. 7A, B), and decreased expression levels of the above senescence markers and SASPs-related factors (Fig. 7C-M). These findings confirmed that STING inhibition also effectively mitigated cortical neuronal senescence caused by chronic T. gondii infection. In summary, pharmacological inhibition of cGAS-STING with both RU.521 and H151 consistently attenuated neuronal senescence in prefrontal cortex of mice chronically infected with T. gondii, thereby establishing a causal role for this pathway in infection-driven neuronal senescence.

To identify the cell types responsible for cGAS-STING-mediated neuronal senescence, we co-stained STING with cell-specific markers in the prefrontal cortex of mice chronically infected with T. gondii. Consistent with the above findings, chronic infection significantly increased both the number and the colocalization ratio of STING+NeuN+ neurons (Fig. 8A-C), indicating activation of the neuronal cGAS-STING pathway. Notably, we found that chronic infection did not alter the proportion of STING+IBA1+ cells, but significantly increased the total number of STING+ microglia, which is consistent with the well-established microgliosis induced by chronic T. gondii infection [44] (Fig. 8D-F). This result suggests that the increase in STING⁺ microglia largely reflects microglial expansion rather than selective activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in this population. Moreover, no significant differences in STING expression or colocalization were observed in astrocytes (Fig. 8G-I). Collectively, these findings indicate that chronic T. gondii infection preferentially activates the cGAS-STING pathway in neurons, but not in microglia or astrocytes, supporting a predominantly neuron-localized role of this pathway in mediating neuronal senescence during chronic infection.

Discussion

Although accumulating evidence has emphasized the pivotal role of chronic T. gondii infection in driving the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment [5, 6, 42, 45–47], the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. In this study, we demonstrate that chronic infection of T. gondii activates the cGAS-STING pathway, while pharmacological inhibition of this pathway alleviates T. gondii-induced cognitive impairment, indicating that targeting cGAS-STING pathway contributes to mitigating infection-induced cognitive deficits. Furthermore, chronic T. gondii infection was revealed to preferentially activate the cGAS-STING pathway in neurons, a response that was associated with neuronal senescence, and thereby contributed to cognitive impairment. However, it remains unclear whether neuronal cGAS-STING activation originates from neurons parasitized by chronic Toxoplasma gondii infection or from uninfected neuronal cells, which warrants further investigation. Interestingly, previous studies have reported that chronic T. gondii infection leads to increased number of microglia [48]. In line with this, we observed that chronic infection increased the absolute number of microglia positive for both IBA1 and STING. However, their colocalization ratio remained unchanged, implicating the increase in STING-positive microglia is likely driven by microglial proliferation rather than by a selective upregulation of microglial STING expression. Further neuron- or microglia-specific genetic manipulation would help dissect their distinct contributions to cognitive impairment caused by chronic T. gondii infection.

Chronic infection of T. gondii is reported to drive mouse hippocampal DNA double-strand breaks in neurons [49]. Consistent with this, elevated expression of γ-H2AX, a classic biomarker of DNA damage response, was also detected in the prefrontal cortex of mouse chronically infected with TgCtwh6. The presence of aberrant double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm is recognized as the primary trigger for activating the cGAS-STING pathway [50]. In this study, we identified activated cGAS-STING pathway in response to chronic infection of T. gondii initiates excessive neuroinflammation and cognitive deficits. Interestingly, although T. gondii has been shown to exploit the host cGAS-STING pathway to enhance its replication during acute infection [32, 51], our study found that pharmacological blockade of this pathway did not affect the cyst burden in mouse chronically infected with T. gondii. These results suggest that inhibition of cGAS-STING is dispensable for the maintenance of established cyst burden during the chronic infection of T. gondii. Therefore, we propose that cGAS-STING blockade mitigates T. gondii-induced behavioral deficits primarily by suppressing excessive neuroinflammation, rather than by affecting parasite persistence in the brain.

Beyond its pro-inflammatory role in driving neuroinflammation [25], the cGAS-STING pathway is also critical for anti-parasite immunity [52]. For example, T. gondii employs virulence factors, including TgROP16 and TgROP18, to dampen cGAS-STING signaling, thereby evading anti-parasite immunity and promoting its survival [38, 53, 54]. Notably, both TgROP18 and TgROP16 are reported to be highly expressed in tachyzoites but expressed at low levels in bradyzoites [55]. Consequently, we propose that T. gondii adopts a stage-dependent strategy towards the cGAS-STING pathway. Intriguingly, in this study we found that inhibition of this pathway significantly down-regulated several factors previously reported to be required for parasite control, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α dependent immune effectors [56, 57], yet this did not affect cyst burden during chronic infection. Therefore, a partial reduction in cGAS-STING-associated IFN-γ/TNF-α signaling does not compromise immune control of established chronic infection. However, the suppression of excessive neuroinflammation resulting from cGAS-STING inhibition is adequate to ameliorate cognitive impairment. Together, we propose that targeting this pathway alleviates behavioral deficits primarily by attenuating excessive neuroinflammation.

In recent years, there is compelling evidence that neuronal senescence plays a causal role in the progression of cognitive dysfunction. For example, senescent neurons can exhibit SASPs [58], which is proposed to drive chronic mild neuroinflammation [59], thereby inducing synaptic damage and ultimately contributing to cognitive impairment [60]. While clearance of senescent cells can prevent the degeneration of cortical and hippocampal neurons and ameliorate cognitive function [61]. Intriguingly, our study not only substantiates the role of neuronal damage in T. gondii-induced cognitive impairment, but also provides the evidence that chronic infection of T. gondii drives neuronal senescence, which in turn mediates cognitive deficits in mice. Unfortunately, although we establish a causal link between T. gondii-mediated neuronal senescence and cognitive impairment, we have not yet determined the specific neuronal subtypes that are most vulnerable and primarily responsible for the deficits.

Given that inflammation is a critical factor driving cell senescence [62], the activated cGAS-STING pathway, a key mediator of chronic inflammation [63], likely promotes neuron senescence, thus contributing to cognitive dysfunction during chronic infection of T. gondii. Indeed, our study demonstrate that inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway significantly alleviates T. gondii-induced neuronal senescence and cognitive impairment. Nevertheless, given the potential contribution of infiltrating inflammatory cells and microglia to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the precise role of neuronal cGAS-STING-mediated SASPs secretion in cognitive impairment warrants further investigation. Here, our results indicate that pharmacological suppression of cGAS-STING activation effectively ameliorate neuronal senescence, thereby nominating this pathway as a potential therapeutic target for treating cognitive dysfunction associated with chronic T. gondii infection. However, further studies are required to determine whether the cognitive benefits yielded by blocking the cGAS-STING pathway are comparable to those achieved by directly targeting cell senescence. Additionally, given the distinct roles of cGAS-STING pathway across different stages of T. gondii infection, therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway should be predicated on a clear distinction between acute and chronic phases of infection.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that chronic T. gondii infection activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in the prefrontal cortex of mouse. The sustained activation of this pathway is associated with cortical neuronal senescence characterized by increased expression of P53 and P16, and elevated release of SASPs factors, leading to neuronal structural destruction and synaptic dysfunction, and ultimately triggering cognitive impairment. Pharmacological inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway with inhibitors, RU.521 or H151, significantly alleviate neuronal senescence, restores neural structure and function, thereby rescuing T. gondii-induced cognitive deficits (Fig. 9). Our findings thus establish neuronal senescence driven by the cGAS-STING pathway serves as a core pathological axis linking chronic T. gondii infection to cognitive impairment, revealing a novel mechanism and a promising therapeutic target.

Chronic infection of T. gondii results in mouse neuron damage and cognitive impairment, accompanied by the activation of cGAS-STING pathway and the induction of neuronal senescence in the prefrontal cortex. Pharmacological inhibition of cGAS-STING pathway not only alleviates these neuron damage and cognitive deficit, but also rescues the senescent phenotype of neurons, as evidenced by downregulated expression of senescence markers, P16, P53 and p21 and multiple SASPs factors, including Il-1β, Il-6, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Mmp9. Therefore, infection-induced activation of cGAS-STING pathway promotes neuronal senescence, which in turn contributes to neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment

Materials and methods

Animals

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, China). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. After one week of adaptation under laboratory conditions, the mice were grouped randomly to perform further experiments in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xuzhou Medical University.

Establishment of chronic T. gondii Infection model and inhibitor treatment

C57BL/6 mice aged 8 weeks were gavaged with 10 cysts of TgCtWh6 per mouse to establish chronic T. gondii infection model [64], and the exact process of infection was performed as previously described [65]. After 4 weeks post infection, these mice were euthanized to collect brain and blood samples for subsequent analysis unless specified otherwise. To further investigate the essential roles of cGAS-STING pathway, mice chronically infected with T. gondii were further treated with cGAS-STING pathway inhibitors, namely RU.521 (MCE, HY-114180) and H-151 (MCE, HY-112693), which selectively target cGAS and STING. RU.521 and H-151 were administrated intraperitoneally three times weekly for two consecutive weeks at doses of 5 mg/kg and 750 nmol per mouse, respectively.

RNA sequencing and GSEA analysis

RNA-Seq sequencing and analyses were performed by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., (Guangzhou, China) as described previously [66]. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from fresh prefrontal cortex tissues of chronic T. gondii-infected and control mice using Trizol Reagent Kit (Vazyme, R401-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, the Illumina sequencing and generation of transcriptome data were conducted at Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. The acquired raw sequencing reads was processed by removing adaptor, low quality, and unknown nucleotides to produce high-quality clean reads. The resulting clean reads were then assembled to further generate unigenes. Read counts of unigenes were calculated and standardized to the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), which were used to quantify the expression levels of unigenes. Statistical significance of differentially expressed genes between T. gondii-infected and control groups were evaluated with an absolute log2 FC ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), the GSEA and MSigDB were employed to identify significantly enriched pathways in the T. gondii-infected group compared to the control group. Gene ranking was performed using the Signal2Noise normalization method. Significance thresholds were set at |NES| ≥ 1, p-value < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25. The results were visualized using enrichment plots and heatmaps of core enriched genes to illustrate expression patterns across sample groups.

Cognitive behavioral assessments

To assess the impact of T. gondii infection and cGAS-STING inhibitor treatments on cognition, temporal order memory test, novel object recognition test, nesting experiment and Y-maze alternation working memory were performed.

The temporal order memory test was conducted as previously described [67]. Briefly, the temporal order recognition task consisted of two training phases and a single test phase. At the training phase 1, two identical copies of object A were placed in the open-field arena and the mouse was allowed to explore for 10 min. One hour later, the training phase 2 commenced, during which the animal was presented with two identical copies of a different object B for 10 min. After a further 1 h delay, the test phase was conducted: one copy of object A (the temporally remote stimulus) and one copy of object B (the temporally recent stimulus) were simultaneously placed in the arena and exploration was recorded for 10 min. Exploration was defined as the mouse directing its snout towards the object within a 2 cm distance. Climbing or sitting on the object was excluded from scoring. Videos were analyzed offline by experimenters who were blinded to object identity and animal treatment. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated using the following formula: DI = (tA - tB)/(tA + tB), where tA and tB represent the time spent exploring objects A and B, respectively. Simultaneously record the total exploration time of mice to rule out differences in exploration time caused by motor impairments.

The NOR was performed as previously described [68]. Briefly, before NOR test, each mouse was allowed to freely explore an empty open-field for 5 min to habituate to the testing apparatus. Twenty-four hours later, the training phase began, in which two identical objects were placed symmetrically in the arena, and the mouse was allowed to explore for 5 min. One hour after training, the retention phase was performed, during which one of the familiar objects was replaced by a novel object, and the mouse was again allowed to explore for 5 min. The recognition index was calculated using the following formula: (Time spent exploring the novel object / Total time spent exploring both objects) × 100%. Simultaneously record the total exploration time of mice to rule out differences in exploration time caused by motor impairments.

The nesting behavior test was carried out according to previous protocol. In brief, the mice will be transferred to individual testing cages with free access to standard food and water. One hour before the onset of the dark phase, mice were individually housed in clean cages containing wood-chip bedding. A 3.0 g pressed-cotton square nestlet was placed in each cage. The following morning, nest construction was evaluated by an experimenter blind to genotype/treatment using a validated 5-point scale [69]. All loose fragments were collected and weighed on an analytical balance (0.1 mg precision), with the untorn nestlet weight recorded.

Y-maze spontaneous alternation test was performed as previously described [70]. The Y-maze consisted of three identical arms (29.5 cm long × 3 cm wide × 14.5 cm high) labeled A, B and C, which were positioned at 120° intervals. Each animal was gently placed in arm A facing the distal wall, and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. An arm entry was scored when all four paws had crossed a virtual line 10 cm from the central triangle. Spontaneous alternation was defined as three consecutive entries into different arms (e.g., ABC, BCA, CAB) and expressed as: Spontaneous alternation rate = [Number of alternations / (Total arm entries − 2)] × 100%. After each trail, all experimental arenas were wiped clean with 70% ethanol and ddH2O.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cortical brain tissue using TRIzol (Vazyme, R401) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized with 1 µg RNA using a high-capacity reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R223) to further analyze the transcription level of genes (p16, p21, Il-1β, Il-6, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, Cxcl10, and Mmp9). qPCR was then performed using SYBR Green (Vazyme, Q311) and determined on LightCycler® 480 II Real-time PCR Instrucment (Roche, Swiss). The relative mRNA expression level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and β-Actin was used as an internal control for normalization. Primers used in this study were synthesized in Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the primer sequences are listed in supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Cortical tissue was lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (X-blot, Xblot-021), and the extracted protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay using kit (GIPBIO, GK10009). Equal amounts of protein were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gelelectrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difuoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milkat at room temperature for 2 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies. These primary antibodies were included: anti-SYN (Abcam, ab32127, 1:50000 dilution), anti-PSD95 (Santa Cruz, sc-32290, 1:200 dilution), anti-BDNF (Abomone labs, ANT-010, 1:200 dilution), anti-cGAS (HUABIO, HA500023, 1:500 dilution), anti-STING (HUABIO, HA722832, 1:3000 dilution), Anti-γ-H2AX (Servicebio, GB111841-100, 1:1000 dilution), anti-p-TBK1 (Zenbio, R30260, 1:1000 dilution), anti-TBK1 (Proteintech, 83686-3-RR, 1:1000 dilution), anti-p-IRF3 (Alomone, AP1412, 1:1000 dilution), anti-IRF3 (Proteintech, 66670-1-IG, 1:2000 dilution), anti-P-53 (Wanleibio, WL01919, 1:500 dilution), anti-P-16 (Santa Cruz, sc-1661, 1:200 dilution) and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 81115-1-RR, 1:20,000 dilution). Following washing 3 times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-inked anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB23303, 1:3,000 dilution) or HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB23301, 1:3,000 dilution) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing 3 times with TBST, the protein bands were detected with Clarity™ ECL western blot substrate (Bio-Rad, 1,705,060) and visualized using the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad). Protein levels were normalized to β-actin.

Immunofuorescence analysis

Mice were subjected to transcardial perfusion using saline, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio, China). Brain tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose solutions, embedded in tissue embedding medium (epredia, Neg-50) and sectioned into 20 μm coronal sections. Sections were then blocked with a buffer prepared by mixing 10% BSA, normal goat serum (NGS), PBS and 0.6% Triton X-100 at a volume ratio of 1:1:3:5 ; thereafter, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-GFAP (Servicebio, GB12096-100, 1:500 dilution), anti-IBA1 (Abcam, ab178847, 1:300 dilution), anti-NeuN (Proteintech, 26975-1-AP, 1:500 dilution), anti-β-gal (Proteintech, 66586-1-Ig, 1:200 dilution), anti-STING (HUABIO, HA722832, 1:500 dilution). After washing, sections incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies Goat Anti-Mouse IgG Dylight 488 (Abbkine, A23210, 1:500 dilution) or Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG Dylight 594 (Abbkine, A23420, 1:500 dilution). Images were acquired using an Olympus IX51 microscope after coverslipping with mounting medium. The number of β-gal positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software, and the ratio was determined as the percentage of target cells relative to the total number of cells in the field at 20× magnification.

Golgi-cox staining and image analysis

Following transcardial perfusion with physiological saline, brains were promptly collected and processed using the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit (PK401, Nanjing Well-Offex Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, whole hemispheres were immersed in equal volumes of Solutions A and B, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 14 days, with the solution replaced every 24 h. Subsequently, tissues were transferred to Solution C, and stored for 48 h at room temperature in the dark, with daily solution changes. Brain was then sectioned into 100 μm coronal slices, which were further mounted on slides pre-coated with 0.3% gelatin and air-dried overnight at room temperature. After being briefly rinsed with distilled water, they were stained for 10 min in a mixture of Solution C, Solution D, and distilled water (1:1:2). After washing, the sections were dehydrated with graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and final coverslipped with Permount mounting medium (Solarbio, G8590).

Images were acquired using a digital scanning microscope (OLYMPUS 21 VS120). Neurites were traced using the NeuronJ plugin in ImageJ to quantify total neurite length and number of branches. Subsequently, Sholl analysis was performed using the Sholl plugin, with concentric circles drawn from the soma at intervals of 10 μm (0 μm to 400 μm radius). The number of intersections between neurites and each circle was recorded. The total number of intersections and the maximum number of intersections were calculated to characterize neuronal morphology.

Cell and parasite culture

Human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) and HT22 was maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (solarbio, 01-139) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (solarbio, P1400) at 37℃ with 5% CO2. All parasites used in this study were maintained in vitro by serial passages in HFF monolayers. Freshly egressed parasites purified with 3 μm nucleopore filters were used to infect HT22 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3.

Cyst burden analysis

To count the cyst number, the brain tissues of mice were homogenized in 1.2 mL PBS. Then, a 10 µL aliquot of each homogenate was examined under a light microscope (OLYMPUS CX21), and the total cyst number per brain was calculated. The cyst number was counted in a blind manner, with each sample counted at least three times. To quantify the parasite DNA, qPCR was performed on genomic DNA extracted from 20 mg of brain tissue used per sample using the genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, DC102-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. As described previously [71], TgB1 gene was amplified and the number of TgCtwh6 per µg of tissue DNA was estimated by comparison with a standard curve, established with a known number of TgCtwh6 tachyzoites. The primer sequences are listed in supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± Standard Error of the Mean (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test (non-paired test). one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey-Kramer post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. *p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
