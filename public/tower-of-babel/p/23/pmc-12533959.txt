Recombinant Mycobacterium smegmatis expressing antigen 85B for immunotherapy of Mycobacterium tuberculosis-infected mice
Authors: Yuxiao Chang, Huanhuan Ning, Xuan Liang, Yanzhi Lu, Jian Kang, Xue Li, Shangqi Liu, Leran Hao, Yanling Ma, Fanglin Zhang, Yinlan Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculosis is a serious, chronic infectious disease worldwide, primarily caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). Antigen 85B is a potential vaccine candidate for Mtb. In this study, we constructed a recombinant strain expressing Antigen 85B (rMs-Ag85B) in the Mycobacterium smegmatis (Ms). The immunological characteristics and the therapeutic efficacy of the vaccine were evaluated in mice. The results showed that rMs-Ag85B exhibited slow growth under static culture conditions, with a significant reduction in biofilm formation compared to the Ms strain. Immunologically, subcutaneous immunization with rMs-Ag85B induced stronger humoral and cellular immune responses. As a therapeutic vaccine, rMs-Ag85B modulated the aggregation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in spleen and lung, promoted Th1/Th2-type cytokines secretions, and increased inflammatory cytokines releases and alleviated pathological damages in the lung of Mtb infected mice. Moreover, rMs-Ag85B significantly reduced the Mtb loads in the spleen of mice. In conclusion, rMs-Ag85B vaccine provided an immunotherapeutic effect against Mtb infection and could be used as a candidate vaccine for the immunotherapy of tuberculosis.KEYWORDS: Tuberculosis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Ag85B, Mycobacterium smegmatis, therapeutic vaccine

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a devastating global health threat, with approximately a quarter of the world’s population harboring latent infections.1,2 While standard chemotherapy exists, the protracted treatment duration and the escalating crisis of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) TB underscore the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.3,4 In this context, therapeutic vaccines emerge as a promising avenue to augment host immunity, potentially curtailing treatment time and mitigating drug-related adverse effects.5,6

Therapeutic vaccines offer a promising approach by inducing a specific immune response against Mtb, correcting the Th1/Th2 immune imbalance in infected individuals. Furthermore, such vaccines can mitigate immunopathological damage, promote immune clearance of Mtb, shorten the duration of TB treatment, and enhance survival rates.7 Therefore, the development of novel TB therapeutic vaccines holds significant potential for improving both prevention and treatment outcomes. Antigen 85B (Ag85B), an early-secreted protein, is a leading candidate for TB vaccines.8,9 This protein is present in all pathogenic Mycobacterium species and exhibits mycobacterial acid-transferase activity, which is crucial for cell wall biosynthesis.10 Additionally, Ag85B as plays a vital role in Mtb’s ability to adhere to and invade macrophages, positioning it as a key virulence factor.11 Numerous studies have demonstrated that Ag85B-based subunit and DNA vaccines elicit robust humoral and cellular immune responses, thereby establishing Ag85B as an immunodominant antigen.12,13

However, the efficacy of a vaccine is contingent on its delivery system. Mycobacterium smegmatis (Ms), a fast-growing and nonpathogenic mycobacterium, presents a unique advantage as a vaccine vector. Its intrinsic adjuvant properties and ability to trigger broad, mycobacterium-specific immune responses akin to Mtb make it an ideal candidate for presenting TB antigens.14 We hypothesized that engineering Ms to express and secrete the immunodominant Ag85B antigen would create a potent therapeutic vaccine that combines the robust immunogenicity of Ag85B with the favorable immunostimulatory profile of the Ms vector.

To test this hypothesis, we constructed a recombinant Ms strain over-expressing Mtb Ag85B (rMs-Ag85B). This study aimed to evaluate the immunotherapeutic potential of rMs-Ag85B in a murine model of Mtb infection, assessing its capacity to reduce bacterial burden and modulate protective immune responses. Our findings provide foundational evidence for rMs-Ag85B as a novel strategy for TB immunotherapy.

Methods and materials

Mice and bacterial strains

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Animal Center of the PLA Air Force Medical University. The Ms MC2-155 strain was obtained from ATCC (Catalog No. 700,084). The Mycobacterium shuttle expression plasmid PW27 is preserved in our laboratory. The Mtb H37Ra attenuated strain was obtained from the China National Institute for Food and Drug Control.

Construction of rMs-Ag85B strain

The Ag85B gene of Mtb (Rv1886c) was amplified by PCR from the chromosomal DNA of Mtb H37Rv and cloned into Escherichia coli–mycobacterium shuttle vector PW27 with Hind III restriction enzyme site (Table S1). The plasmid of PW27-Ag85B was sequenced and transformed into Ms by electroporation. Recombinant Ms clones were screened on Middlebrook 7H10 plate supplemented with 10% OADC and 25 μg/mL kanamycin. Positive clone was confirmed by PCR (Supplementary Table S1) and subsequently cultured in 7H9 complete medium (Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.5% glycerol, 10% OADC, and 0.05% Tween-80).

Western blot

Bacterial proteins of rMs-Ag85B were extracted by sonication, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio). Total proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore) for Western blot analysis. The primary and secondary antibodies used were: anti-Ag85B polyclonal antibody (prepared in our laboratory) and fluorescent secondary antibodies (LICOR).

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was extracted from strains using the Total RNA Kit (OMEGA). cDNA synthesis was carried out using reverse transcription reagents (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. mRNA levels were quantified by qPCR using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Data were presented as fold change and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Growth curve determination

1 × 106 CFU of Ms or rMs-Ag85B were inoculated into 96-well plates or 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 7H9, 7H9 complete medium, LB, or Sauton’s medium, respectively. The 96-well plates were cultured statically at 37°C, while the 50 mL centrifuge tubes were incubated with shaking at 37°C and 80 rpm. OD600 was measured at regular intervals, and the growth curves were plotted.

Biofilm determination

Ms and rMs-Ag85B strains were inoculated into 7H9 complete medium and cultured until OD600 reached to 0.8–1.0. The strains were washed with Sauton’s medium and adjusted to OD600 = 0.2. Then adjusted strains were inoculated into 24-well plates and cultured statically at 37°C to allow biofilm formation. The biofilm was quantified using the crystal violet staining method. The amount of biofilm formation was directly proportional to the OD570.

Acid-fast staining

Ms and rMs-Ag85B bacteria were coated onto glass slides. The slides were stained with carbolic acid fuchsin solution, decolorized with 3% hydrochloric acid alcohol, and then stained with 0.3% alkaline methylene blue solution.

Animal immunization

Female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks were divided into three groups (n = 4 per group): Naïve (blank control), Ms, and rMs-Ag85B groups. Mice were inoculated subcutaneously on the back with 1 × 107 CFU of Ms and rMs-Ag85B strains twice at an interval of 2 weeks. Mice in the Naïve group were inoculated with the same volume of PBS solution. Immune responses were assessed 6 weeks after the second immunization. All mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC) within a Biosafety Level 2 Laboratory (BSL2).

Animal infection and immunotherapy

Female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks were intranasally inoculated with 1 × 104 CFU of Mtb H37Ra per mouse. Four weeks post-infection, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4 per group): Naïve (blank control) group, infection untreated (UN) group, and Ms and rMs-Ag85B treatment groups. Each treatment group was subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 107 CFU twice at a two-week interval. After 6 weeks, mice were sacrificed for immunological assays.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sera were collected and diluted for antibody titers detection with Ag85B protein-coated ELISA plate. HRP-labeled secondary antibody (Solarbio) was added as second antibody. OD450 reading was measured to determine the antibody titers.

1 × 106 splenocytes were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. About 25 μg/mL Ms and Mtb bacterial protein or 5 μg/mL Ag85B protein was added to stimulate the cells for 3 d. The culture supernatant was collected, and cytokine levels were measured using ELISA kits (Invitrogen).

Frequency of splenocytes secreting IFN-γ by ELISPOT

5 × 105 splenocytes were added to each well of 96-well plates, along with Ag85B protein at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL. The plates were incubated and detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions of ELISPOT Kit (Mabtech).

Flow cytometry

2 × 106 splenocytes were resuspended in 100 μL of staining buffer (BioLegend) containing TruStain FcX™ PLUS (anti-mouse CD16/32) (BioLegend). The cells were stained with fluorochrome-labeled anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 antibodies (BioLegend).

For intracellular staining analysis, 3 × 106 splenocytes were placed in a 24-well plate and stimulated with Ag85B (5 μg/mL) for 12 h, then stained with anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 antibodies. After fixed and permeabilized, the cells were stained by fluorescent dyes labeled anti-IFN-γ and anti-IL-10 antibodies (BioLegend).

Finally, cells were resuspended in 500 μL of staining buffer for flow cytometry analysis (BD FACSCanto). FlowJo V10 software was used to analyze the results.

Pathological and immunohistochemical (IHC) detection

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For IHC detection, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD4 and CD8 (BioLegend) and then processed using standard IHC protocols.

Bacteria counting in organs

Tissues were placed on a 40 μm cell strainer and grinded to obtain a homogeneous cell solution. Subsequently, 250 μL of lung and 500 μL of spleen tissue homogenate were separately spread onto Middlebrook 7H10 plates containing antibiotics (80 μg/mL polymyxin B and 5 μg/mL azlocillin sodium). The plates were incubated at 37°C for 3 weeks until visible colonies were formed. The results were showed as Log₁₀ CFU.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A t-test was used to compare results between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used to compare results across multiple groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with the following notation: *P < .05; **P < .01; ***P < .001.

Result

rMs-Ag85B construction and its biological characteristics

The vector with Ag85B was constructed and transferred into Mycobacterium smegmatis (Ms) by electroporation, resulting in the recombinant strain rMs-Ag85B. qPCR showed that the expression level of Ag85B in rMs-Ag85B was significantly higher than that in Ms (Figure 1(A)). Western blot analysis further confirmed that the Ag85B expression in rMs-Ag85B (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S1), demonstrating the successful construction of rMs-Ag85B.

(A) the expression levels of Ag85B in Ms and rMs-Ag85B were analyzed using RT-qPCR. (B) the amounts of Ag85B in intracellular of Ms and rMs-Ag85B were quantified via Western blot. (C) growth curves of Ms and rMs-Ag85B under static or shaking conditions in 7H9 medium, 7H9 complete medium, LB medium, or Sauton’s medium were determined. (D) representative images of biofilms formed by Ms and rMs-Ag85B after 48 and 72 hours of growth. (E) the quantification of biofilms formed by Ms and rMs-Ag85B at 48 and 72 hours was performed using the crystal violet staining method.

The growth curves of rMs-Ag85B were measured in four cultures under different conditions. Under shaking culture conditions, the growth patterns of both strains in all four media were similar. However, under static culture conditions, rMs-Ag85B exhibited slower growth in 7H9 complete and Sauton’s media compared to Ms, with a prolonged logarithmic phase (Figure 1(C)).

After 48 h of culture in 24-well plates statically, both Ms and rMs-Ag85B formed a yellowish biofilm with a granular texture. The biofilm formed by Ms appeared more wrinkled, while that of rMs-Ag85B was thinner and less wrinkled (Figure 1(D)). Crystal violet staining followed by statistical analysis revealed that the biofilm formation of rMs-Ag85B was significantly lower than that of Ms at 72 h (Figure 1(E)). There were no significant changes on morphology by acid-fast staining (Supplementary Figure S2).

rMs-Ag85B induced high level of humoral immunity

Female BALB/c mice were immunized and tested as Figure 2(A). Both Ms and rMs-Ag85B immunization induced an increase in Ms-specific IgG compared to the Naïve group and not statistically significant between two groups (Figure 2(B)). As expected, rMs-Ag85B induced significantly higher levels of Ag85B-specific antibodies compared to both the Ms and Naïve groups. Although the antibody level in the Ms group was higher than that in the Naïve group, this may be due to the cross-reactivity caused by the presence of the homologous genes MS MEG_2078 and MSMEG_6398 in the MS (Figure 2(C), Supplementary Table 2). Compared to the Naïve group, the levels of IgG1, IgG2a, and IgG2b were significantly increased following immunization with Ms and rMs-Ag85B. Moreover, the levels of IgG2a induced by rMs-Ag85B were significantly higher than those induced by Ms (Figure 2(D)). In conclusion, rMs-Ag85B induces a significantly higher level of humoral immune response compared to Ms, mainly due to the overexpressed Ag85B protein.

Mice were inoculated subcutaneously on the back with 1 × 107 CFU of Ms, rMs-Ag85B strains or PBS twice at an interval of two weeks. Immune responses were assessed six weeks after the second immunization. (B–D) Quantification of cellular immune indices in mice immunized with Ms and rMs-Ag85B. rMs-Ag85B induced stronger cellular immunity

Splenocytes from immunized mice were stimulated in vitro with antigens (Ms bacterial protein or Ag85B) for 3 d, and the secretion levels of Th1/Th2-type cytokines were measured. The results showed that after stimulation with Ms bacterial protein, the secretion levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-10 were significantly higher in the rMs-Ag85B group compared to the Naïve group, whereas only IFN-γ and IL-2 levels were significantly increased in the Ms group. However, no significant differences were observed between the immune groups (Figure 3(A)). After stimulation with Ag85B, the IL-2 level in the rMs-Ag85B group was significantly higher than that in both the Naïve and Ms groups. However, no increase in the levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, or IL-10 was observed in the Ms group (Figure 3(B)). These findings indicate that rMs-Ag85B can induce the increasing secretion of Th1/Th2 type cytokines in mice.

(A–E) Assessing humoral immune indicators in Ms or rMs-Ag85B immunized mice. (A) Evaluation of Th1/Th2 cytokine levels in splenocytes stimulated with Ms bacterial protein. (B) Assessment of Th1/Th2 cytokine levels in splenocytes stimulated with Ag85B protein. (C) The ELISPOT assay was used to detect the frequency of cells secreting IFN-γ in spleen cells. (D–E) Quantification of inflammatory factors following stimulation of splenocytes with (D) Ms bacterial protein and (E) Ag85B protein.

Subsequently, the frequency of IFN-γ secretion by splenocytes was analyzed using ELISPOT. The frequency of IFN-γ secretion was higher in the rMs-Ag85B group than in the Ms group, suggesting that rMs-Ag85B enhances IFN-γ secretion in splenocytes following subcutaneous immunization (Figure 3(C)). We also examined Th1/Th2/Th17-type cytokines transcription in lung tissue, but no significant differences were found between groups (Supplementary Figure S3(A)).

ELISA results showed that the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 in the rMs-Ag85B group was also significantly higher compared to the Naïve group. No significant differences were found between the Ms and Naïve groups (Figure 3(D)). After stimulation with Ag85B protein, the IL-6 secretion in the rMs-Ag85B group was significantly higher than other groups (Figure 3(E)). These results suggest that rMs-Ag85B enhances the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6. Furthermore, cytokines transcription analysis in lung tissue revealed that rMs-Ag85B significantly upregulated the transcription of IL-1β and iNOS (Supplementary Figure S3(B)).

rMs-Ag85B reduced the pathological lesion in lung and bacteria loads in spleen of Mtb infected mice

A mouse model of Mtb infection was established to assess the immunotherapeutic effects of rMs-Ag85B (Figure 4(A)). Pathological examination of lung tissue revealed significant differences between groups. Compared to the Naïve group, the UN group exhibited pulmonary bullae, severe destruction of alveolar structure, and moderate inflammatory cell infiltration. The Ms group showed intact alveolar tissue with mild inflammatory reactions, hemorrhages in lung tissue, and thickening of the alveolar septa. The lung tissue of the rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy group only exhibited partial damage and inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 4(B)). These findings indicate that rMs-Ag85B treatment alleviated the pathological changes in lung tissue caused by Mtb infection to some extent.

Next, the bacterial load in various organs was assessed. The lung bacterial counts showed no significant differences between three groups. However, rMs-Ag85B immunization significantly reduced bacterial numbers compared to the UN group, while the Ms group did not (Figure 4(C)). These results suggest that rMs-Ag85B treatment significantly reduced bacterial numbers in the spleen, demonstrating its potential as an effective immunotherapeutic agent.

rMs-Ag85B elevated humoral immunity of Mtb-infected mice

Serum antibody analysis showed that the levels of Ag85B- and Ms-specific antibodies in the UN group were similar to those in the Naïve group. In contrast, rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy significantly increased the levels of both Ag85B- and Ms-specific IgG antibodies compared to the Naïve and UN groups. The Ms treatment group only significantly increased the level of Ms-specific IgG, and this increase was notably lower than that observed in the rMs-Ag85B group (Figure 4(D)). These findings indicate that rMs-Ag85B can comprehensively enhance the production of specific IgG antibodies in Mtb-infected mice. Further analysis of serum IgG subclasses revealed that, after Mtb infection, the antibody subclass levels in the UN and Naïve groups were comparable. The rMs-Ag85B group induced significantly higher levels of IgG1, IgG2a, and IgG3 compared to the UN group. However, the Ms group only induced significant increases in IgG1 and IgG2a levels, with the IgG2a level significantly lower than that in the rMs-Ag85B group (Figure 4(E)). In conclusion, rMs-Ag85B induces a stronger humoral immune response in Mtb-infected mice compared to the Ms treatment.

rMs-Ag85B enhanced the cellular immune response of Mtb-infected mice

To further evaluate the impact of rMs-Ag85B on cellular immunity in Mtb-infected mice, splenocytes were isolated and stimulated with Mtb bacterial protein or Ag85B protein in vitro for 3 d, followed by measurement of Th1/Th2 cytokine secretion. The results demonstrated that, following stimulation with Mtb bacterial protein, rMs-Ag85B immunization significantly increased the secretion of IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-10 compared to the Naïve group. In contrast, only IL-2 levels were significantly elevated in the Ms group when compared to the Naïve and UN groups (Figure 5(A)). When splenocytes were stimulated with Ag85B protein, rMs-Ag85B immunization significantly promoted IL-10 secretion compared to the Naïve, UN, and Ms groups, with a trend toward increased IFN-γ secretion. The other cytokine levels exhibited trends similar to those observed following Mtb bacterial protein stimulation (Figure 5(B)). Additionally, the ELISPOT assay indicated that rMs-Ag85B subcutaneous immunization significantly enhanced IFN-γ secretion by splenocytes from Mtb-infected mice (Figure 5(C)). These findings suggest that rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy can significantly induce Ag85B-specific Th1/Th2 cellular immunity in infected mice, whereas Ms only induces Th1-type cytokine secretion, and rMs-Ag85B elicits a stronger cellular immune response in Mtb-infected mice.

(A) Quantification of Th1/Th2 cytokines in splenocytes stimulated with Ms bacterial protein. (B) Quantification of Th1/Th2 cytokines in splenocytes stimulated with Ag85B protein. (C) The ELISPOT assay was used to detect the frequency of cells secreting IFN-γ in spleen cells. The cytokine secretion profile of splenocytes stimulated with antigen was further examined. No significant differences were observed in TNF-α secretion between the groups following Mtb bacterial protein stimulation (Figure 5(D)). However, IL-6 secretion was significantly elevated in the rMs-Ag85B group compared to the Naïve group (Figure 5(E)). After Ag85B stimulation, the levels of both TNF-α and IL-6 cytokines in the rMs-Ag85B group were significantly higher than those in the Naïve and UN groups. Furthermore, IL-6 levels in the rMs-Ag85B group were significantly higher than those in the Ms group (Figure 5(F)). Collectively, these results further demonstrate that rMs-Ag85B can significantly enhance cellular immunity in Mtb-infected mice.

rMs-Ag85B enhanced T-cell immunity to eliminate Mtb in mice

CD4+ T cells primarily regulate immune responses through cytokine secretion, while CD8+ T cells directly eliminate infected cells by cytotoxic activity. Together, they maintain immune homeostasis and contribute to the defense against infections.15 Immunohistochemical staining revealed accumulations of CD4+ and CD8+ in lungs of Mtb-infected mice after immunotherapy, especially in rMs-Ag85B group (Figures 6(A–C)). Flow cytometry analysis showed a tendency for increased CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the rMs-Ag85B group compared to the UN and Ms groups (Figure 6(D–E)). Noteworthy, CD4+ T cells secreting IFN-γ were significantly elevated in the rMs-Ag85B group compared to both the Ms and UN groups, suggesting an enhanced immune response against Mtb intracellular infection, and no significant differences were observed in CD8+ T cells (Figure 6(F)). In conclusion, rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy activated the accumulation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a particularly pronounced effect on CD4+ T cells, which may contribute to the reduction of Mtb infection.

Discussion

Therapeutic vaccination represents a promising approach by using immunomodulators to enhance immune responses in established disease.16,17 Ms, with its short growth cycle and high efficiency in expressing foreign proteins, serves as both an effective vaccine vector and a natural immune adjuvant.18 Ag85B is an immunodominant antigen of Mtb for TB vaccines.19 Our prior studies showed that the Ag85B DNA vaccine provided immune protection in mice infected with Mtb H37Rv.20 In this study, rMs-Ag85B was administered by subcutaneous injection and showed therapeutic immune effects on mice infected with respiratory Mtb, offering a promising treatment strategy to enhance anti-tuberculosis immunity.21

The reduction of biofilm formation can reduce drug resistance and bacterial virulence.22 In this study, the biofilm formation of rMs-Ag85B was significantly reduced compared to Ms. It may be due to high exogenous protein expression consuming cellular energy. Additionally, excessive Ag85B may disrupt intrinsic cell wall lipid metabolism.23,24 This reduction in biofilm formation suggests that the recombinant vaccine has lower virulence, ensuring a better safety profile as a potential TB vaccine. At the same time, a reduction in biofilm may facilitate the secretion or release of Ag85B and more effectively stimulate the immune responses.

High antibody levels enhance bacterial clearance and reduce pathology.25 Previous studies show recombinant Ms overexpressing Ag85B epitopes induce strong antibody responses.26 In this study, rMs-Ag85B increased Ms- and Ag85B-specific antibodies, including IgG1, IgG2a, and IgG2b, further confirming the secreted Ag85B could increase humoral immunity and enhance the immunogenicity of Ms.

Cellular immunity is crucial for the elimination of intracellular bacteria such as Mtb. Th1 cells activate macrophages via IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α.27–29 Th2 cytokines like IL-10 modulate immunity and can regulate the intensity of the inflammatory responses to avoid immune pathological damage.30 In the current study, rMs-Ag85B significantly induced more Th1/Th2-type cytokines releases in vivo. A strong Th1 response helps to clear Mtb but may cause tissue damage. IL-10 acts as an immunoregulatory ”brake,” suppressing excessive inflammation and limiting immunopathology. IL-10 induced by rMs-Ag85B may improve outcomes by controlling Th1-related damage. This effect was attributed to Ms vector, as well as overexpressed Ag85B in it.

After Mtb infection, the adaptive immune response is essential for control and clearance of the pathogen.31 We measured key pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in splenocytes stimulated with Ms or Ag85B protein. These cytokines mediate critical anti-Mtb mechanisms: macrophage activation, granuloma formation, and T/B cell responses.32–35 In this study, splenocytes from rMs-Ag85B immunized mice exhibited significant or trending increases in TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Elevated transcript levels of IL-1β and iNOS were also detected in the lungs, which collectively underscore the strong immunogenicity of rMs-Ag85B.

CD4+ T cells resist Mtb via IFN-γ and TNF-α to activate macrophages, while CD8+ T cells directly kill infected cells via perforin and granulysin.36 In this study, rMs-Ag85B increased both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in lungs and spleen, with a stronger effect in the spleen. Additionally, rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy significantly elevated the frequency of IFN-γ and IL-10-secreting cells in spleen. IL-10 plays a complex dual role in Mtb infected hose. While its primary function is to suppress immune responses and reduce tissue damage, excessive expression weakens host defenses. In this study, rMs-Ag85B was shown to increase IL-10 expression while significantly reducing Mtb in the spleen. However, pulmonary pathology showed improvement, indicating that rMs-Ag85B played a role in balancing Th1/Th2 in combination immunotherapy.

Bacteria load in organs and the pathological changes are important indicators for evaluating the therapeutic effect of vaccine. rMs-Ag85B immunotherapy reduced organ damage in Mtb-infected mice. Mtb loads were significantly lower in spleen. However, rMs-Ag85B did not significantly reduce Mtb loads in the lungs of infected mice, likely due to organ-specific differences in bacterial distribution and immune microenvironments. As a secondary lymphoid organ, the spleen efficiently clears bacteria and activates T cells,37 whereas the lung exhibits an immunosuppressive microenvironment with regulatory T cells and anti-inflammatory macrophages that may inhibit immune function.38 Nevertheless, rMs-Ag85B induced protective pulmonary CD4+ IFN-γ+ T cell responses and improved histopathology, supporting its immunotherapeutic potential against tuberculosis.

In conclusion, rMs-Ag85B vaccine could effectively promote both humoral and cellular immunity, reducing the bacterial load in organs, and be used as an adjunct to chemotherapy of TB. Further studies with larger samples and additional animal models were needed to be carried out for verification of observations of these beneficial effects.
