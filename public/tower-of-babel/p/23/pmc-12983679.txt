Trajectories of metabolic risk clusters and adverse outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease: a prospective cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Wei-Cheng Lo, Mei-Yi Wu, Yi-Hsuan Roger Chen, Mai Szu Wu, Chung‑Shun Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between longitudinal trajectories of metabolic risk clusters and the risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and major adverse kidney events (MAKEs) in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD).DesignProspective registry-based cohort study.SettingSecondary and tertiary care settings in Taiwan, using data from a multidisciplinary pre-ESKD care programme.ParticipantsA total of 1494 adult patients with CKD stages 3b–5 enrolled in a structured pre-ESKD care programme.Primary and secondary outcome measuresTime to initiation of dialysis (primary outcome) and time to MAKE, defined as a composite of dialysis initiation or all-cause mortality (secondary outcome). Similar patterns were observed for MAKE outcome, confirming the association between trajectory groups and adverse kidney events.ConclusionLongitudinal trajectories of metabolic risk cluster are associated with differential risks of CKD progression to ESKD and death. Our findings provide valuable insights into the monitoring of metabolic risk profiles over time in patients with CKD.Keywords: Chronic renal failure; Nephrology; Blood Pressure; Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2; Lipid disorders

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major public health concern associated with a substantial medical and financial burden on societies and healthcare systems. In Taiwan, the estimated prevalence of CKD is 15.46%, which is substantially higher than the global average of 13.4%,1 leading to the highest prevalence of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) worldwide in patients undergoing renal replacement therapy (RRT). Among the factors that increase the prevalence and incidence of advanced CKD and ESKD worldwide are ageing, high rates of diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension and low rates of early-stage CKD detection.2 3

Metabolic risks such as high blood pressure, elevated blood glucose levels and hyperlipidaemia play a key role in the onset and progression of CKD, with many patients with CKD simultaneously presenting with metabolic syndrome. Compared with the general CKD population, patients with metabolic syndrome are at a higher risk of CKD progression, cardiovascular complications and mortality.4 5 However, because of the complex bidirectional interaction between changes in metabolic risk clusters and kidney function during CKD progression, the relationship between metabolic risk components and CKD progression remains unclear; thus, the causal effects of metabolic risk components on CKD progression are difficult to isolate.6 Clinical outcomes and disease progression patterns can vary widely depending on disease risk factors or combinations of metabolic components. Although certain metabolic components are associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, few studies have examined the collective effects of or alterations in the status of multiple metabolic components on CKD progression and mortality.

Group-based multitrajectory modelling (GBMM) is a useful approach for categorising individuals into relatively homogeneous groups depending on their trajectories of multiple biomarkers.7,10 A fundamental assumption of GBMM is that a population is composed of a mixture of distinct groups defined by their developmental trajectories. Previous studies focusing on individual metabolic components measured at single time points have often failed to capture the effects of long-term exposure changes. Therefore, evaluating the long-term trajectories of metabolic risk clusters may represent a unique opportunity to determine the effect of risk exposure on disease progression and clinical outcomes. Particularly in a real-world clinical dataset with good accessibility to healthcare and long-term follow-up, identifying the true patterns of patients’ long-term trajectories of metabolic risk clusters may be crucial for improving clinical outcomes in patients with CKD. In this study, we investigated the associations between the trajectories of metabolic risk clusters and the risk of progressing to dialysis and experiencing major adverse kidney events (MAKEs) in a longitudinal cohort of patient with CKD. Based on existing literature and accumulated evidence, we selected systolic blood pressure (SBP), fasting blood glucose and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol as the three key biomarkers representing metabolic risk clusters.

Methods

Study population and setting

In 2002, the National Health Insurance Administration of study country launched the Project of Integrated Care for Chronic Kidney Disease, with an initial focus on patients presenting with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or with proteinuria (defined by a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) greater than 1 g/g creatinine). In 2007, the programme was expanded to include a multidisciplinary paradigm, specifically targeting patients with CKD stages 3b to 5, referred to as pre-ESKD. This integrated CKD care programme involves a multidisciplinary care approach that comprises nephrologists, renal nursing specialists, pharmacists and dietitians to formulate individualised care plans tailored to patients with CKD, aligning with the therapeutic objectives outlined by the National Kidney Foundation Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines.11 12 In this programme, participants are continuously monitored until the initiation of RRT, until death or until they withdraw from the programme. In this study, patients were recruited from a multidisciplinary pre-ESKD care programme at a tertiary medical centre, conducted between December 2008 and October 2022. To ensure compliance with the care quality standards mandated by the national health insurance reimbursement policy, biochemical parameters such as blood pressure, blood glucose and lipid profile were regularly measured at least annually or more frequently. Patients younger than 20 years old, patients with a history of dialysis and pregnant women were excluded from the study. A 2-year index period was defined for trajectory construction. Only patients who had at least three measurements (quarterly average) taken for SBP, fasting blood glucose and LDL cholesterol within the defined index period were eligible for inclusion. Among included patients, the median (IQR) number of quarterly averaged measurements during the index period (excluding baseline) was 7 (2) for SBP, 7 (2) for fasting blood glucose and 6 (2) for LDL cholesterol. Patients who initiated dialysis, experienced death or had less than 2 years of follow-up during the index period were excluded. The end of the 2-year index period was treated as a predefined landmark (time 0) for outcome analyses. Follow-up for dialysis and MAKE commenced after completion of the trajectory window to ensure temporal separation between trajectory classification and outcome assessment. Ultimately, a total of 1494 patients were included for analysis (online supplemental figure S1).

Assessment of metabolic risks and blood biochemical parameters

Based on clinical guideline emphasis, data completeness and measurement consistency requirements for trajectory modelling, we selected SBP, fasting glucose and LDL cholesterol as our primary metabolic risk components. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure was determined using oscillometry (Omron HBP-9020; Omron). To conduct blood biochemical measurements, all patients were required to fast for at least 8 hours before they underwent blood tests. Blood biochemical data including haemoglobin (Hb), haematocrit (Hct), white blood cell (WBC), fasting blood glucose, albumin, uric acid, triglyceride, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, potassium (K), sodium (Na), calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) levels were determined using an automated chemistry analyser (UniCel DxC 800; Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA). The patients’ eGFR values were estimated using the following abbreviated Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation: eGFR=175 × creatinine−1.154×age−0.203×1.212 (if black)×0.742 (if female).13 Serum creatinine levels at enrolment were used to define the baseline eGFR and corresponding CKD stages with the following cut-off values: >90, 60–89.9, 30–59.9, 15–29.9 and <15 mL/min/1.73 m2. After the quarterly average degree of metabolic risk was calculated in cases in which the patient had more than one measurement within a 3-month period, the metabolic risk trajectory of each individual was modelled on the basis of these serial quarterly average metabolic risk measures (figure 1).

Outcomes and other covariate measurements

The primary outcomes of this study were the initiation of dialysis and the occurrence of MAKE. A MAKE was defined by a necessity for RRT, such as dialysis, and the occurrence of death. Survival status and date of death were determined through proactive surveillance. Dialysis was defined as the beginning of peritoneal dialysis or haemodialysis. All participants were followed up from the index date until the initiation of dialysis or death, with kidney transplantation and loss to follow-up treated as censoring events, or until 31 October 2022, whichever occurred first. During the enrolment interviews, data on the sociodemographic characteristics of age, sex, educational level, marital status, body mass index (BMI), smoking habits, alcohol consumption, physical activity and herbal medicine intake were collected. Marital status was classified as either ‘married or cohabiting’, ‘never married’ or ‘other’ (widowed, divorced, separated or living as a single parent). Educational level was classified as ‘below elementary school’, ‘high school’ or ‘college or above’. Smoking habits and alcohol consumption were classified as ‘never’, ‘former’ (quit smoking or drinking) or ‘current’. Baseline comorbidities such as DM, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, cardiovascular disease (congestive heart failure, ischaemic heart disease and stroke), gout, chronic liver disease and cancer were defined through physicians’ clinical diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision codes and the use of relevant medications in electronic medical records (EMRs) 1 year before enrolment in the pre-ESKD programme.

Statistical analysis

Group-based trajectory model

GBMM was used to identify distinct subgroups among patients with CKD that shared longitudinal patterns of metabolic risk clustering over time. Longitudinal changes in SBP, fasting blood glucose and LDL were modelled using the Proc Traj procedure in SAS statistical software (SAS Institute).7 9 This procedure involves a semiparametric mixture model that enables the concurrent modelling of trajectories for multiple outcomes through the maximum likelihood method. We selected SBP, fasting blood glucose and LDL cholesterol because they represent clinically relevant and modifiable risk factors in CKD management and were measured with sufficient consistency and frequency to support reliable longitudinal trajectory modelling, whereas other metabolic components lacked comparable longitudinal completeness. No imputation was performed for missing longitudinal measurements. Proc Traj accommodates unbalanced repeated measures by allowing individuals to contribute all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption within the maximum likelihood framework. Given the uncertainty regarding the optimal number of groups and the order of trajectory polynomials (eg, linear, quadratic and cubic), we systematically compared models with 3, 4 and 5 trajectory groups, allowing polynomial terms up to cubic order. Model selection was based on three criteria: (1) Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values, with values approaching 0 indicating better model fit; (2) sufficient sample sizes in each multitrajectory group (exceeding 5% of the study sample) and (3) average posterior probability of assignment equal to or exceeding 0.7 for all groups, indicating adequate classification certainty. The 4-group model was selected as optimal, yielding the best BIC value (BIC=−154 879.3) compared with the 3-group (BIC=−155 293.0) and 5-group (BIC=−154 895.9) models. Average posterior probabilities of group assignment for the 4-group model were Group I (0.88), Group II (0.95), Group III (0.93) and Group IV (0.88), all exceeding the 0.7 threshold. Each patient was allocated to a trajectory group based on the highest estimated probability of assignment. Details of trajectory models with 3, 4 and 5 groups are presented in online supplemental figures S2–S4, respectively.

Baseline factors by trajectory group

We characterised the patients within each trajectory group by tabulating their baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics depending on their assigned group according to their posterior probabilities. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented in terms of the mean±SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. The percentage of missing data for all baseline variables is provided in online supplemental table S1. For continuous variables, distributional assumptions were assessed using visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots, together with formal testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables demonstrating approximately normal distributions were analysed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas variables showing substantial departure from normality were analysed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Differences in the distributions of patient characteristics between trajectory groups were analysed using ANOVA or a Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and a χ2 test for categorical variables.

Risk of dialysis and mortality by trajectory group

Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests were used to compare dialysis-free and MAKE-free survival across trajectory groups. For the dialysis outcome, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were applied, treating death prior to dialysis as a competing event. For MAKE, which included dialysis and death as composite outcomes, Cox proportional-hazards models were used. Kidney transplantation was not considered a component of MAKE and was handled as a censoring event in all time-to-event analyses. HRs and their corresponding 95% CIs are reported as outcome measures. The trajectory group with the lowest risk of dialysis or mortality was selected as the reference group. Hierarchical adjustments were made to all hazard models except for Model 1, which was used as a crude model. Model 2 had adjustments for age, sex, BMI, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, educational level, marital status, physical activity, herbal medicine intake, baseline CKD stage and baseline comorbidities. Model 3 was further adjusted for baseline creatinine and selected baseline biochemical markers that were significantly associated with outcomes in univariate analyses. These baseline biochemical markers (measured prior to or at the beginning of the trajectory modelling period) were conceptualised as potential confounders reflecting pre-existing kidney function, nutritional status and metabolic state that may have influenced both subsequent trajectory patterns and clinical outcomes. A directed acyclic graph illustrating our assumed causal structure is provided in online supplemental figure S5.

Results

Characteristics of the four trajectory groups

The mean age of the participants at enrolment was 73.0 years (SD=12.8), with a median follow-up duration of 46.3 months. After systematic model comparison based on BIC values and classification quality, a 4-group trajectory model was selected as optimal for characterising metabolic risk patterns (figure 1). The four trajectory groups consisted of Group I (n=384, 25.7%), Group II (n=233, 15.6%), Group III (n=629, 42.1%) and Group IV (n=248, 16.6%). Group I had stable blood pressure below the suggested target of 130 mm Hg and fasting blood glucose levels below the cut-off of 130 mg/dL, with stable LDL levels above 100 mg/dL. Group II had slightly high SBP, LDL levels immediately below the cut-off of 100 mg/dL (borderline-high) and high but gradually decreasing fasting blood glucose levels. Group III exhibited well-controlled fasting blood glucose levels (below 130 mg/dL) and relatively low LDL levels (below 90 mg/dL), with borderline-high SBP (above 130 mm Hg). Group IV had well-controlled fasting blood glucose levels (below 130 mg/dL) but elevated SBP (above 140 mm Hg) and LDL levels (above 110 mg/dL).

Baseline characteristics revealed that Group IV tended to be young at enrolment, to be current alcohol consumers and to have a high mean BMI. Group III tended to be male and married or cohabiting. Group II tended to be current smokers with reduced physical activity. Group I tended to be highly educated with low herbal medicine intake. Group I also exhibited improved kidney function, with more patients in stage 3 than in the other trajectory groups. In terms of comorbidities, hypertension was prevalent among Group IV, and diabetes and hyperlipidaemia were prevalent among Group II. In terms of biochemical profile, Group I had higher baseline urine creatinine (U-Cr) levels and lower blood pressure, fasting blood glucose levels, blood urea nitrogen levels and UPCR compared with the other groups. Group II exhibited high baseline triglyceride levels, fasting blood glucose levels, WBC count and UPCR. Group III had low total cholesterol levels, LDL levels, triglyceride levels, U-Cr levels, eGFR, Hb levels, Hct levels and WBC count but high blood urea nitrogen levels. Group IV exhibited high baseline blood pressure, total cholesterol levels, LDL levels, eGFR, Hb levels, Hct levels, WBC count and UPCR (table 1).

Risk of dialysis and MAKE

In total, 221 ESKD events and 146 deaths occurred over 6580 person-years of follow-up. The incidence rates for ESKD and MAKE were 33.6 (95% CI (29.5 to 38.3)) and 55.8 (95% CI (50.4 to 61.8)), respectively, per 1000 person-years. Various dialysis-associated and MAKE-associated risks were observed with trajectory group membership. As indicated by the unadjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curve depicted in figure 2, Group I, characterised by well-controlled SBP and fasting blood glucose levels, exhibited significantly longer dialysis-free and MAKE-free survival (log-rank test, p<0.001) compared with the other groups. The incidence rate of dialysis during follow-up was 19.5 per 1000 person-years in Group I, 33.6 in Group II, 38.8 in Group III and 46.7 in Group IV, with Group IV exhibiting the highest rate and Groups II and III showing intermediate rates. A similar pattern was observed for MAKE outcome.

Kaplan-Meier plot of (A) dialysis and (B) MAKE in each trajectory group. Time 0 represents the end of the 2-year trajectory (index) period, after which follow-up for outcomes began.

Table 2 presents the HRs and 95% CIs of dialysis and MAKE for each trajectory group. In adjusted models, trajectory group membership was significantly associated with the risk of dialysis and MAKE. After baseline sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, baseline CKD stage and comorbidities were adjusted for, Group II (HR=1.32, 95% CI (0.79 to 2.21)) showed elevated HR that did not reach statistical significance, whereas Group III (HR=1.67, 95% CI (1.09 to 2.54)) and Group IV (HR=2.16, 95% CI (1.32 to 3.54)) exhibited a significantly higher risk of dialysis compared with Group I (table 2). These association patterns remained largely unchanged after further adjustment for baseline creatinine and additional biochemical markers included in Model 3, with HRs in Model 2 and Model 3 being highly comparable, supporting the robustness of the findings. Group IV remained significantly associated with a higher risk of dialysis, whereas the HRs for Groups II and III were elevated but not significant. Similar results were obtained for MAKE outcome. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the risk of MAKE was higher in Group II (HR=1.37, 95% CI (0.87 to 2.16)) and Group III (HR=1.36, 95% CI (0.95 to 1.94)) compared with Group I, although neither reached statistical significance. Group IV showed a significantly higher risk (HR=1.90, 95% CI (1.23 to 2.94)).

Stratification analysis

Exploratory stratified analyses were conducted to assess whether associations between metabolic trajectory groups and the risks of incident dialysis and MAKE outcome varied across baseline demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors and comorbidities. These analyses were hypothesis-generating in nature and should be interpreted cautiously due to reduced sample sizes within strata and wide CIs for some estimates. For the dialysis outcome, the overall pattern of associations across trajectory groups was generally consistent across most subgroups. Stronger relative associations were more apparent among participants younger than 75 years, among females and among individuals with BMI ≤24 kg/m², with higher-risk trajectory groups showing elevated risks compared with Group I (online supplemental figure S6). For the MAKE outcome, among individuals aged below 75 years, trajectory Groups II, III and IV were associated with higher risks of MAKE compared with Group I. Elevated relative risks were observed among former or current smokers and drinkers, particularly in the highest-risk trajectory group. In addition, among patients without hypertension or without hyperlipidaemia, higher-risk trajectory groups, especially Groups III and IV, showed more pronounced associations with MAKE compared with Group I. No clear or consistent sex-specific differences were observed for MAKE across trajectory groups (online supplemental figure S7).

Discussion

Among all patients with CKD in this study, we identified four metabolic risk trajectory groups. Group I had blood pressure and blood glucose levels within the recommended targets but had high LDL levels. Group II had borderline-high blood pressure and high blood glucose levels. Group III had controlled blood glucose levels and low LDL levels but borderline-high blood pressure. Group IV had controlled blood glucose levels but elevated blood pressure and high LDL levels. Group IV had HRs for progression to dialysis and MAKE of 1.96 and 1.90, respectively, and was characterised by elevated SBP and high LDL levels. Overall, information on the trajectories of metabolic risk clusters provides a clear understanding of CKD progression. Previous studies have identified metabolic syndrome as a risk factor for CKD.14,20 In this study, we examined three key components of metabolic syndrome, namely SBP, fasting blood glucose and LDL, which strongly correlated with CKD. Although these metabolic risk factors may be associated with renal injury, their contribution to CKD progression and their developmental trends remain to be fully understood.

High-normal blood pressure is significantly associated with microalbuminuria, which leads to tubular cell injury, interstitial inflammation and fibrosis.21 22 Multiple studies have indicated that high blood pressure is an independent risk factor for CKD progression and blood pressure control, particularly with renin-angiotensin system blockade in proteinuric disease as an intervention that can slow CKD progression and reduce MAKE.23 24 In this study, we discovered that SBP remained above the recommended target in Groups II, III and IV, leading to an increased risk of CKD progression and MAKE. These findings underscore the importance of blood pressure control as a key metabolic factor in CKD prognosis.

Hyperglycaemia or impaired fasting blood glucose is associated with hyperfiltration and intraglomerular hypertension, leading to albuminuria and reduced glomerular filtration.25 26 Hyperglycaemia can also cause oxidative stress and trigger an inflammatory response that promotes vascular cell damage.27 28 According to a previous study, poor glycaemic control increases the risk of CKD progression and mortality in patients with diabetes and CKD.29 In this study, we discovered that Group II, characterised by significantly high fasting blood glucose levels (although with a downward trend), was at a slightly increased risk of CKD progression and MAKE. We also discovered that the majority of patients in this group had baseline diabetes, indicating ongoing medication treatment and medical interventions. In a cohort study conducted in the United States, Hb A1c levels below 6% and above 9% were found to be associated with an increased risk of mortality.30 According to the ADVANCE trial, intensive glycaemic control reduces the long-term risk of ESKD in patients with diabetes, particularly when started earlier in the disease course and when blood pressure is optimally controlled.31

High LDL cholesterol levels are associated with the development and progression of atherosclerosis, which may affect renal arteries and contribute to kidney damage. Dyslipidaemia, particularly characterised by high LDL cholesterol levels, may exert proinflammatory and profibrotic effects on the kidneys, potentially accelerating the progression of CKD.32 33 The relationship between LDL cholesterol and outcomes in advanced CKD is complex and stage-dependent. In this study, we observed that both low LDL levels in Group III and high LDL levels in Group IV were associated with higher risk of CKD progression and MAKE, though likely through different mechanisms. In advanced CKD populations, low cholesterol levels often reflect the malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS) or illness-related lipid suppression, rather than indicating protective effects or adverse consequences of lipid lowering therapy. Low serum cholesterol (<100 mg/dL) is recognised as one of the biochemical criteria for protein-energy wasting, inflammation and catabolic illness, all of which are strongly associated with mortality in patients with CKD. Patients in our low LDL trajectory (Group III) exhibited biochemical features compatible with MICS, suggesting chronic catabolic or inflammatory states. In contrast, the high-LDL/high-SBP trajectory in Group IV may reflect persistent atherogenic and haemodynamic stress, consistent with known pathophysiology. These findings highlight the complexity of lipid pattern in advanced CKD, where nutritional and inflammatory status may contribute to observed lipid levels. Rather than viewing low LDL as universally beneficial or high LDL as uniformly harmful, lipid trajectories likely reflect a combination of underlying disease states and treatment effects, which may require different clinical and therapeutic approaches.34 35

Few long-term studies have evaluated the prognostic effect of metabolic risks in patients with advanced CKD. In a large study, Navaneethan et al36 relied on a CKD registry based on electronic health records to examine 25 868 patients with stage 3 and 4 CKD, with a mean follow-up period of 2.3 years. They discovered an association for metabolic syndrome with ESKD progression, with an HR of 1.33 (95% CI (1.08 to 1.64)), but not with all-cause mortality. In a study of hypertension and kidney disease in 842 hypertensive, nondiabetic African-American patients with CKD, Lea et al37 reported an association for metabolic syndrome with proteinuria, but not with CKD progression. In a study involving patients with moderate CKD (mean eGFR=49.4±18.2 mL/min/1.73 m2), Pammer et al38 discovered that hyperglycaemia was associated with a major increase in the risk of all-cause mortality. In a study conducted in Taiwan, Lin et al39 identified hypertension as the most significant factor affecting the renal outcomes of patients with CKD and metabolic syndrome. Randomised and long-term follow-up studies of multifactorial interventions show that an intensified, combined approach targeting glycaemia, blood pressure, lipids and lifestyle factors produces greater reductions in nephropathy and ESKD risk than single-factor strategies in high-risk populations. Taken together, these findings indicate the multifactorial nature of CKD progression and cluster-based management addressing multiple concurrent metabolic abnormalities may be a more effective clinical approach for many patients with CKD to improve renal outcomes.

One of the strengths of this study is its well-designed patient registry, in which EMRs were used to substantially enhance the aggregation of biochemical data. In addition, we used GBMM to simultaneously explore the long-term profiles of three metabolic risk factors: blood pressure, blood glucose and lipid profile. Through real-world data investigation, we were able to understand the authentic patterns of these clustered metabolic risk factors under conditions of good accessibility to healthcare. Although further research is needed to investigate the underlying causes of these trajectories, we believe that examining the long-term trajectories of these metabolic risk factors will provide reliable and robust indicators for CKD progression and clinical outcomes in patients with CKD.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single tertiary medical centre in northern Taiwan, which limits the generalisability of our findings. Our cohort focused on advanced CKD stages (3b–5) in patients enrolled in a structured pre-ESKD care programme, which may not reflect patients with CKD in community settings, those not participating in disease management programmes or patients with earlier disease stages. Regional variations in CKD prevalence within Taiwan and differences in population characteristics, healthcare systems and metabolic profiles across regions suggest caution is needed when extrapolating our findings to other settings. Multicentre validation studies are needed to confirm the broader applicability of our trajectory-based risk stratification approach. Second, we did not fully collect data on our patients’ lifestyles, particularly their dietary patterns. Although we included several crucial metabolic risks, we did not consider long-term changes in obesity. In a study involving a cohort of 453 946 veterans from the USA, Lu et al40 reported an association between high BMI and CKD progression in patients with stage 3 CKD. By contrast, in this study, we discovered that individuals with BMI ≤24 kg/m2 were at an increased risk of incident dialysis, although further research is required to comprehensively explore this finding. In addition, while we focused on three key metabolic components, we recognise that other elements of metabolic syndrome (HDL, triglycerides, waist circumference) may provide additional prognostic information. Our selection prioritised parameters with established treatment targets and consistent longitudinal measurement, though future studies incorporating a broader metabolic profile may offer complementary insights. Third, we adjusted for baseline biochemical markers as potential confounders reflecting pre-existing disease severity and physiological state. While we conceptualised these baseline measurements as temporally preceding trajectory development, we acknowledged that some biological overlap existed between these markers and the metabolic processes captured by our trajectories. Importantly, the HR patterns and effect sizes were largely consistent between Model 2 (without biochemical adjustment) and Model 3 (with biochemical adjustment), suggesting that the overall associations were similar across different adjustment strategies and that potential over-adjustment had limited impact on the results. Fourth, our trajectory model was developed and applied within a single cohort without external validation. Internal validation through bootstrapping or split-sample approaches, and external validation in independent CKD cohorts, would provide additional confidence in the stability and generalisability of our trajectory classification. Fifth, our study focused on kidney-related outcomes and did not systematically capture major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) during follow-up. Given that MACE often represents a competing and higher-risk outcome than kidney failure in CKD populations, the lack of comprehensive cardiovascular event data limits our understanding of the full clinical impact of metabolic trajectory groups. Future studies linking metabolic trajectory data with comprehensive cardiovascular surveillance would provide important insights into the broader prognostic implications of these metabolic phenotypes in CKD populations. Finally, we did not evaluate the effects of medication, including the longitudinal and adherence patterns of drug use and the effectiveness of drugs in controlling blood pressure, blood glucose levels or lipid profiles. This represents a significant limitation, as antihypertensive agents, glucose-lowering medications and statins may independently affect kidney outcomes beyond their effects on metabolic parameters. The absence of medication adjustment means we cannot distinguish whether observed trajectories reflect underlying disease severity, treatment effectiveness or medication adherence patterns. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting real-world metabolic control patterns—the net result of disease and treatment—rather than isolated disease effects. Future studies incorporating comprehensive, time-updated medication data with adherence measures would provide valuable insights into the relative contributions of disease biology vs treatment effects on CKD progression.

Conclusion

Our findings emphasise the importance of monitoring longitudinal metabolic risk clusters in patients with CKD, indicating that distinct trajectories of blood pressure, glucose levels and LDL levels are associated with differential risks of CKD progression to ESKD and death. These results highlight that tracking metabolic risk profiles over time can inform early risk stratification and individualised patient management. Incorporating regular assessment of these metabolic factors into routine CKD care may support timely interventions to slow disease progression and improve clinical outcomes. Future studies should validate these trajectory-based risk patterns in broader CKD populations and explore their integration into clinical decision-making frameworks.
