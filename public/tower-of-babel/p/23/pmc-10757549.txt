Correction to: Comparative Safety and Effectiveness of Sotalol Versus Dronedarone After Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation
Published: 2023

In the article by John Marcus Wharton, et al, “Comparative Safety and Effectiveness of Sotalol Versus Dronedarone After Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation,” which was published online January 21, 2022 (J Am Heart Assoc. 2022;11:e020506. DOI: 10.1161/JAHA.120.02050639) and appeared in the February 1, 2022 issue of the journal, corrections were needed.

After publication of the article, the authors discovered an error in the data analysis. Although the Methods section indicated that ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes were used for cohort selection, the ICD‐10 codes were inadvertently not used for the analysis, which resulted in the reporting of inaccurate results. In addition, the reported study period and patient selection criteria were incorrect. The specific corrections to the article are as follows:

In the Abstract, the Methods and Results section read “A comparative analysis of propensity‐score–matched retrospective cohorts was carried out using IBM MarketScan Research Databases. Patients treated with dronedarone after AF ablation were matched 1:1 to patients treated with sotalol between January 1, 2013 and March 31, 2018. Outcomes of interest included cardiovascular hospitalization, proarrhythmia, repeat ablation, and cardioversion. This study was exempt from institutional review board review. Among 30 696 patients who underwent AF ablation, 2086 were treated with dronedarone and 3665 with sotalol post‐ablation. Propensity‐score matching resulted in 1815 patients receiving dronedarone matched 1:1 to patients receiving sotalol. Risk of cardiovascular hospitalization was lower with dronedarone versus sotalol at 3 months (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.77 [95% CI: 0.61–0.97]), 6 months (aHR, 0.76 [95% CI: 0.63–0.93]), and 12 months post‐ablation (aHR, 0.70 [95% CI: 0.66–0.93]). Risk of repeat ablation and cardioversion generally did not differ between the 2 groups. A lower risk of proarrhythmia was associated with dronedarone versus sotalol at 3 months (aHR, 0.76 [95% CI: 0.64–0.90]), 6 months (aHR, 0.80 [95% CI: 0.70–0.93]), and 12 months post‐ablation (aHR, 0.83 [95% CI: 0.73–0.94]).” This has been corrected to read “A comparative analysis of propensity‐score–matched retrospective cohorts was carried out using Merative™ (formerly IBM) MarketScan Research Databases. Patients treated with dronedarone after AF ablation were matched 1:1 to patients treated with sotalol between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2017. Outcomes of interest included cardiovascular hospitalization, proarrhythmia, repeat ablation, and cardioversion. This study was exempt from institutional review board review. Among 37 589 patients who underwent AF ablation, 2230 were treated with dronedarone and 3986 with sotalol post‐ablation. Propensity‐score matching resulted in 2224 patients receiving dronedarone matched 1:1 to patients receiving sotalol. Risk of cardiovascular hospitalization was lower with dronedarone versus sotalol at 3 months (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.73 [95% CI: 0.61–0.87]), 6 months (aHR, 0.73 [95% CI: 0.62–0.85]), and 12 months post‐ablation (aHR, 0.79 [95% CI: 0.69–0.90]). Risk of repeat ablation and cardioversion generally did not differ between the 2 groups. A lower risk of proarrhythmia was associated with dronedarone versus sotalol at 3 months (aHR, 0.76 [95% CI: 0.66–0.89]), 6 months (aHR, 0.79 [95% CI: 0.70–0.91]), and 12 months post‐ablation (aHR, 0.84 [95% CI: 0.75–0.95]).”

In the Methods section, under the heading Patient Selection, selection criteria were described as follows: “Patients with a diagnosis of AF were identified within the study period between January 1, 2013 and March 31, 2018 using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐9 and ICD‐10 codes. Patients aged >18 years who were undergoing an initial index ablation for AF during this period were identified.” This has been corrected to read “Patients with a diagnosis of AF were identified within the study period between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2017 using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐9 and ICD‐10 codes. Patients aged ≥18 years who were undergoing an initial index ablation for AF during this period were identified.”

In the Results section, under the heading Cohort Formation and in Figure 1, the number of patients at each step of cohort formation was revised, including number of patients excluded from the analysis, number of patients who met enrollment criteria, and number of patients in each treatment group.

In the Results section, under the heading Patient Characteristics and in Table 1, the baseline characteristics of patients in each treatment group (demographics, comorbidities, procedures and health care utilization, and concomitant medications) were updated.

In the Results section, under the heading Cardiovascular Hospitalization and in Supplemental Table S1, the rates of hospitalization for cardiovascular disease (eg, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and atrial fibrillation), follow‐up procedures (repeat catheter ablation, cardioversion, and pacemaker insertion), and proarrhythmia (eg, ventricular proarrhythmia and bradycardia) for each treatment group were revised. Analyses of these data, shown in Figures 2 through 5, Table 2, Supplemental Figures S1 through S5, and Supplemental Tables S2 and S3, were updated accordingly.

In the Discussion section, under the heading Effectiveness, the following sentence was added to the first paragraph: “The difference in hospitalizations due to heart failure were also greatest in the first 3 months after ablation. Studies have demonstrated that the risk of heart failure immediately post ablation is high, which could possibly be due to an ablation‐induced injury to the atrial myocardium.”

In the Discussion section, under the heading Effectiveness, the first sentence of the third paragraph read “After the recovery phase, dronedarone treatment was associated with lower 1‐year rates of ATA‐related hospitalization, relative to sotalol treatment. The rates of ATA‐related hospitalization were the same between dronedarone and sotalol treatment groups 6 months after AF ablation.” This has been corrected to read “After the recovery phase, dronedarone treatment was associated with lower rates of ATA‐related hospitalization at 6 months, relative to sotalol treatment. The rates of ATA‐related hospitalization were the same between dronedarone and sotalol treatment groups at 12 months after AF ablation.”

In the Discussion section, under the heading Safety, the first 4 sentences of the first paragraph read “There was a higher risk of composite proarrhythmia with sotalol compared with dronedarone after AF ablation. This difference was driven predominantly by a higher risk of significant bradyarrhythmias and pacemaker implantation in patients treated with sotalol. This is consistent with the known bradycardic effects of sotalol. There was a marginal increase in ventricular proarrhythmia at the 3‐month follow‐up, but no subsequent differences in ventricular proarrhythmia at 6 and 12 months.” This has been corrected to read “There was a higher risk of composite proarrhythmia with sotalol compared with dronedarone after AF ablation. This difference was driven predominantly by a significantly higher risk of bradycardia at 3 months or by higher rates of pacemaker implantation at 3, 6 or 12 months of follow‐up in patients treated with sotalol. This is consistent with the known bradycardic effects of sotalol, thus requiring pacemakers. There was a marginal increase in ventricular proarrhythmia at the 3‐month and 6‐month follow‐up, but no subsequent differences in ventricular proarrhythmia at 12 months.”

In the Discussion section, under the heading Safety, the first 4 sentences of the second paragraph read “As mentioned, the dominant driving factor for the difference in the proarrhythmia composite at 3, 6, and 12 months was the increased risk of bradyarrhythmias with sotalol. The composite proarrhythmia curve diverged mostly during the recovery phase after ablation, probably due to the higher use of AADs within that phase. Of interest, the pacemaker insertion curves for dronedarone and sotalol diverged continuously during the follow‐up period out to 1 year, becoming significant only at 6 and 12 months of follow‐up. The higher incidence of significant bradycardia and pacemaker implantation in the recovery phase with sotalol compared with dronedarone in our study raises concern that acute changes after ablation may alter the safety profile of a drug.” This has been corrected to read “As mentioned, the dominant driving factor for the difference in the proarrhythmia composite at 3, 6, and 12 months was the increased risk of pacemaker implantation with sotalol. The composite proarrhythmia curve diverged mostly during the recovery phase after ablation, probably due to the higher use of AADs within that phase. Of interest, the pacemaker insertion curves for dronedarone and sotalol diverged continuously during the follow‐up period out to 1 year, becoming significant at 3, 6 and 12 months of follow‐up. The higher incidence of pacemaker implantation in the recovery phase with sotalol compared with dronedarone in our study raises concern that acute changes after ablation may alter the safety profile of a drug.”

The first sentence of the Conclusions section read “Patients treated with dronedarone after AF ablation had lower risk of cardiovascular hospitalization, compared with patients treated with sotalol, predominantly due to lower rates of ATA‐related hospitalization.” This has been corrected to read “Patients treated with dronedarone after AF ablation had lower risk of cardiovascular hospitalization, compared with patients treated with sotalol, predominantly due to lower rates of ATA‐related hospitalization and hospitalizations due to heart failure.”

The authors regret the errors.

The correction has been made to the current online version of the article, which is available here: https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.120.020506.
