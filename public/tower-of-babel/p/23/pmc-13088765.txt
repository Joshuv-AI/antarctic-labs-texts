Enhanced myelination potential of human mature oligodendrocytes by TNF and IFNG combination
Authors: Abdulshakour Mohammadnia, Qiao-Ling Cui, Moein Yaqubi, Gabriela J Blaszczyk, Chao Weng, Jeffery A Hall, Roy Dudley, Jo Anne Stratton, Stephanie Zandee, Timothy E Kennedy, Myriam Srour, Jack P Antel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInitial phases of multiple sclerosis (MS) are characterized by recurrent focal inflammation within the central nervous system associated with demyelination and variable subsequent remyelination, indicating that both injury and repair are ongoing within the same microenvironment. An array of pro-inflammatory molecules including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF) and interferon γ (IFNG) are implicated as contributing to oligodendrocyte (OL) lineage cell injury in MS and its models. Using OL lineage cells derived from human surgical samples, we observed that TNF and IFNG in combination enhanced ensheathment of synthetic nanofibers by mature OLs compared to those observed under control conditions or in the presence of individual cytokines, reaching levels comparable to those of A2B5+ late-stage progenitor cells. The combination reversed the individual cytokine mediated inhibition of A2B5+ cell ensheathment. Molecular analysis of the mature OL population identified increased expression of myelination relevant genes in the cytokine combination treated cells, of STAT and IRF transcription factors (TFs) that regulate many of these genes, and of mature OL structural genes (MOBP and CNP). TNF and IFNG combination also reduced expression of OPC signature genes in the mature OLs compared to IFNG alone. The combination effect on ensheathment was reversed using a JAK/STAT pathway inhibitor. The combination of TNF and IFNG induced a stronger immune signature in mature OLs compared to individual cytokines. These results emphasize the complex positive and negative interplay of inflammatory responses in the CNS and the potential for therapeutic modulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03753-y.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is characterized in its initial phases by recurrent focal regions of inflammation associated with demyelination and variable degrees of subsequent remyelination, indicating that both injury and repair are ongoing within the same microenvironment. Demyelination would predominantly reflect insults to the existent myelinating oligodendrocytes (OLs), whereas repair is most frequently attributed to the response of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) existent in the CNS parenchyma. However, mature OLs are now variably implicated in contributing to myelin repair in both the human brain as shown by C14 based studies of human tissues [1] and demyelination in Rhesus monkeys [2, 3]. Within initial MS lesions, there is relative preservation of the numbers of mature myelin producing OLs but with retraction of their processes, referred to as dying back [4, 5]. Within such lesions, there is a relatively greater loss of progenitor cells with increases in the lesion perimeter [6]. Previous studies have identified a population of late OL lineage cells recognized by the ganglioside directed A2B5 antibody that can be isolated from the developed human CNS and that is able to myelinate the dysmyelinated mouse CNS in xenogeneic transplantation studies [7, 8]. Our in vitro comparative analysis of ensheathment capacity of human late OL lineage cells indicate superior response of these cells compared to human mature OLs, consistent with the results in animal models [9, 10].

Inflammation within the CNS is recognized to be capable of contributing both to tissue injury and protection/repair [11]. In cases of multiple sclerosis, remyelination is most apparent early on when peak inflammation is still ongoing [4]. An array of pro-inflammatory molecules derived from the systemic compartment and accessing the CNS by crossing blood or CSF: brain barriers and/or produced by endogenous sources (astrocytes, microglia) are implicated as contributing to the injury of OL lineage cells in multiple sclerosis and its models [12, 13]. Prominent amongst the pro-inflammatory cytokines are tumor necrosis factor α (TNF) and interferon γ (IFNG). TNF is reported to kill primary rodent OL-lineage cells in vitro and in vivo [14], although others observe a block in differentiation rather than killing [15]. Baerwald and Popko showed that developing and mature rodent OLs respond differently to IFNG [16]. IFNG induced morphological changes and cell death in developing OPC cultures after 2 days, and in mature oligodendrocytes after 4–7 days [16, 17]. Developing OPCs underwent apoptotic cell death in the presence of IFNG, but mature OLs died of necrosis. Additional reported effects of IFNG on rodent OPCs include inhibiting proliferation and differentiation, as well as inducing of immune signatures [18, 19]. We observed previously that both TNF and IFNG when added individually to mature human OLs in dissociated cell culture induced process retraction over a 4–6 day period without cell death [20]. Examples of immune-linked protection/repair include mice completely lacking an adaptive immune system, having impaired remyelination [14, 15] and soluble products from “M2” polarized microglia supporting remyelination [16]. TNF promotes proliferation of OPCs and remyelination capacity in vivo in TNF-receptor 1 knockout mice [21].

A number of studies have examined the combined effects of TNF and IFNG on rodent OL lineage cells. Lentferink et al. [22] found that TNF counteracted the reduced arborization of neonatal rat forebrain grey matter derived OPCs induced by IFNG [22]. Zveik et al. found that TNF in vitro reversed the IFNG induced block in differentiation of OPCs derived from neonatal mice, attributing the effect to TNFR2 dependent signaling [23]. TNF also reversed MHC-II expression by OPCs; the latter was not affected by anti-TNFR2 antibody. Simultaneous TNF and IFNG exposure to more mature cells (defined by expression of O1+) resulted in higher levels of endpoints/cell index and branch length /cell compared to their separate applications [23].

The initial aim of the current study was to evaluate the combined effect of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF and IFNG on the injury response of human OL lineage cells derived from surgically resected brain tissue samples as measured by survival and ensheathment of synthetic nanofibers. The primary cells used in this study were human OLs derived from either adult or pediatric donors and separated into sub-populations using A2B5 antibody coated immunomagnetic beads. Non-selected cells (90–95% of the total OL fraction) are comprised of mature OLs and are referred to as A2B5- (or A-); the A2B5 antibody selected cells (5–10%) comprise the late progenitor pool (A2B5+ cells) [6], some refer to these cells as pre-OLs. Previous RNA sequencing studies indicate that both these cell types express all canonical mature OL myelin structural genes and low levels of progenitor markers (e.g. PDGFRα, PTPRZ1) compared to cells earlier in the OL lineage [9, 24]. Progenitor gene expression, however, is higher in A2B5+ cells versus mature OLs and in pediatric versus adult tissue derived cells [9]. Our observation that the combined cytokine effect induced enhanced ensheathment of mature OL compared to effects of the individual cytokines led to subsequent bulk RNA-seq studies to identify the underlying molecular basis for this response in the mature OL population. The cytokine combination induced the most extensive changes in overall gene expression followed by IFNG and then TNF alone. To specifically examine the effects of cytokine exposure on mature OL and OPC associated gene signatures in these cells, we derived these signatures from publicly available single-nucleus RNA (snRNA-seq) datasets. The cytokine combination induced enrichment of the mature OL gene signature while reducing expression of the OPC signature when compared to IFNG alone. As motif-based analysis indicated the importance of interferon-regulatory factor (IRF)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) transcription factors (TFs) in regulation of the mature human OL gene signature, we used an inhibitor of JAK-STAT signaling to document the role of this and related signaling pathways in the ensheathment induced by these cytokines. Both cytokines, individually and in combination, induced MHC Class I gene expression in the mature OLs, whereas MHC Class II genes were most prominently and significantly upregulated in the combinatorial treatment compared to individual cytokine treatments. To relate our in vitro findings to conditions encountered in MS lesions, we used publicly available snRNA-seq datasets from MS cases [25–27] to demonstrate the increased expression of IRF/STAT TFs in OLs in active lesions in which both cytokines are present. These results emphasize the complex positive and negative interplay of inflammatory responses in the CNS.

Results

Combined TNF and IFNG treatment significantly increases nanofiber ensheathment in mature human oligodendrocytes

To determine the distinctive functional effects of TNF and/or IFNG on ensheathment capacity and survival of human mature OLs (A2B5- cells) and A2B5 antibody selected late progenitors (A2B5+ cells) derived from surgically resected tissue samples, we examined these using synthetic nanofiber containing cell cultures. Our study included 40 individual patient samples (Supplementary Table S1) although not all could be tested under individual cytokine alone conditions due to limitations of cell numbers. As previously described, under control conditions, the % of ensheathing cells was greater for A2B5+ versus A2B5- cells (Figs. 1A and 14.3 ± 1.1 vs. 7.3 ± 0.4%, p < 0.0001, n = 40). The mean percentage of ensheathment in response to the combination of TNF and IFNG on mature cells (A2B5- cells) was significantly increased compared to control conditions (16.0 ± 1.2% versus 7.3 ± 0.4%, p < 0.0001, n = 40) and to cells treated with either TNF (8.6 ± 0.7%, p < 0.0001, n = 29) or IFNG (11.4 ± 1.4, p < 0.001, n = 28) alone (Fig. 1A). The levels induced by the cytokine combination were comparable to the A2B5+ cells under control conditions (N1 media only) (14.3 ± 1.1% Fig. 1A). TNF alone induced a modest increased in the level of ensheathment in the mature cells that did not reach to a significant level in comparison with control (8.6 ± 0.7% vs. 7.3 ± 0.4%, n = 29); IFNG alone induced a significant increase in ensheathment in comparison with the control group (11.4 ± 1.2 versus 7.3 ± 0.4%, p < 0.01, n = 28). All statistical significance shown above was determined by two-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons. In addition to enhanced percentage of ensheathing cells, the length of ensheathed segments and the number of ensheathed segments per cell were increased in response to combination treatment, as is the alignment with the nanofibers (Fig. 1B, C). The majority of the O4 + cells also express MBP (Fig. S2).

Similar patterns of cytokine effects for both A2B5- and A2B5+ cells were seen when data was divided into age-subgroups (< 12 y, 13–18 y, > 20 y) (Fig. S1A-C). The two-week treatment with each cytokine alone or in combination did not induce significant cytotoxicity of the overall OL population (Fig. S1D).

Comparison of individual versus combined effects of TNF and IFNG on overall mature human oligodendrocyte (A2B5-) cell transcriptome

Since the most apparent enhanced functional ensheathment response was in mature OLs, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of mature OLs exposed to individual and combined cytokine treatments as well as control conditions to assess overall transcriptomic response of these mature OLs. Principal component analysis of normalized read counts from treated and control human mature OL cells revealed transcriptomic changes under all treatment conditions (Fig. 2A). Although treated cells under all treatment conditions showed changes in gene expression compared with control cells, quantification of differentially expressed genes (fold change > 1.5 and P < 0.05) indicated the highest number of differentially expressed genes in the combinatorial treatment group (3053 genes), followed by IFNG-treated cells (2721 genes), and then TNF-treated cells (1105 genes); these differentially expressed genes from each cytokine alone and combinatorial treatment showed only modest overlap (Fig. 2B). Gene ontology (GO) term analysis indicated that immune related processes were prominent amongst the top enriched biologic processes (pathways) that involve these differentially expressed genes (Fig. S3, listed in Extended Data Table 1).

Comparison of individual versus combined effects of TNF and IFNG on mature OL and OPC gene signatures in mature OLs

To examine how cytokines affect mature OL and OPC associated gene signatures expressed within the mature OLs, we generated these gene signatures from publicly available single-nucleus RNA (snRNA-seq) datasets. We leveraged snRNA-seq data from two independent studies [25, 26], which include robust numbers of OLs and OPCs, to define the molecular signatures of mature OLs as well as of OPCs (Fig. S5) [25, 26]. We defined OLs and OPCs by lineage-specific markers - PLP1, MOG, MBP, and MAG for OLs; CSPG4, PTPRZ1, and PDGFRA for OPCs. After analyzing both datasets and defining cell clusters (including OLs and OPCs), as visualized by UMAP (Fig. S5), we compared all OLs with all OPCs in each dataset, identified the upregulated genes for each population, then overlapped the two studies to delineate OL and OPC gene signatures found in both. We defined 530 genes as the mature OL signature and 737 genes as the OPC signature based on their presence in both studies (Fig. 3A, Extended Data Table 2).

We used GO analyses to identify biologic processes, derived from these signature genes (Fig. 3B-C). The OL signature genes contribute to myelination-relevant process terms that include myelin assembly and axon ensheathment, as well reflecting lineage stage (OL differentiation), while OPC signature genes are enriched for a wide range of biological processes, including cell proliferation and PDGFR signaling pathway, indicating their early stage in the lineage (Fig. 3B-C).

To show the actual effect of each treatment condition on the mature OL signature genes of our mature OLs, we plotted fold changes for all mature OL signature genes which showed a clear shift from single cytokine treatment conditions to combinatorial treatment. TNF or IFNG alone treatments suppressed expression of mature OL signature genes (Fig. 4A, top and middle panels) compared to control conditions, but the cytokine combination reversed this effect (Fig. 4A, lower panel). When we categorized up- and downregulated genes based on P < 0.05, we observed a higher ratio of significantly down versus upregulated genes in single treatments (52 down vs. 9 up for TNF; 77 down vs. 27 up for IFNG), but a reversed trend emerged with the cytokine combination (79 upregulated vs. 16 downregulated genes) (Fig. 4B). We used pattern analysis to define the relation of changes in expression of signature genes by individual and combined cytokine treatment. 73 genes down regulated by TNF alone were upregulated by the combination treatment; IFNG alone induced a mixture of up and downregulation of these genes (Fig. 4C). Other patterns of changes in gene expression by these cytokines alone or combinations are shown in Fig. S6A. GO pathway analysis of the 79 genes upregulated (list of genes provided in Extended Data Table 3) when exposed to both cytokines indicated they are linked to processes relevant for the myelination process including cytoskeleton properties and cell differentiation (Fig. 4D). As regards lineage stage, the combination treatment increased expression of late OL lineage structural genes MOBP, MOG, CNP, and MBP (Fig. 4A).

Differentially expressed genes within mature oligodendrocyte and oligodendrocyte progenitor signatures are regulated by distinct transcription factor profiles

To identify transcription factors (TFs) that could regulate significantly upregulated genes in mature OL and OPC signature genes, we applied motif-based analysis using the iRegulon plugin of Cytoscape to these genes. From the list of all TFs implicated by motif analysis in regulating these signatures, we retained those that were upregulated (read counts > 50, with an adjusted P < 0.05) after TNF, IFNG and/or combinatorial treatments. We identified 10 TFs in the mature OL signature that were significantly upregulated upon combinatorial cytokine treatment compared to control and individual cytokines alone (Fig. 5A, B). Some of these were also upregulated by individual cytokines compared to control conditions (TNF - HMGA1, IRF1; IFNG – all except IRF4 and JUNB) (Fig. 5C). Most were members of the STAT and IRF families (Fig. 5C). All these TFs had relatively greater increased expression under combination cytokine exposure compared to TNF or IFNG alone (Fig. 5C). The regulatory network of these TFs (Fig. 5A) indicates the relationship between the TFs and their regulated genes, featuring the overlapping genes regulated by the STAT and IRF family TFs. HMGA1 mainly regulates a set of independent genes. As regards mature OL signature genes noted to be downregulated by IFNG alone (Fig. S7A-B), most were regulated by members of the SOX family rather than by STAT and IRF.

For the OPC signature genes upregulated by combinatorial cytokine treatment, we identified 10 significantly upregulated TFs (Fig. 5D) with limited overlap with those observed for mature OL signature genes (Fig. 5A vs. D) (only overlap is with HMGA1). Some of these were also upregulated by individual cytokines compared to control conditions ( TNF - HMGA1, ELK1; IFNG – all except EGR2, EGR3, and EGR4); the number of the OPC signature genes upregulated by each of these TFs and the regulatory networks involved are presented in Fig. 5D, E and F. IFNG alone induces a profile of TFs including IRFs (Fig. S7C and D) which upregulate the OPC signature genes; some of these are also amongst those which downregulate mature OL signature genes (Fig. S7A, B and E), indicating crosstalk between these signatures at the TF level. For example, TF SOX4 in the OPC signature, which is induced by IFNG alone, downregulates some mature OL signature genes. Combination cytokine treatment suppressed this upregulation, potentially contributing to upregulation of mature OL genes (Fig. S7E).

Inhibition of JAK-STAT signaling pathways reverses the effect of TNF and IFNG on ensheathment of human oligodendrocytes

Our computational analysis showed the importance of STAT and IRF TF family members in regulation of the mature human OL gene signature in response to TNF/IFNG. Motif-based analysis showed that these TFs share the same binding motifs (for example a motif called homer-M00095) for the target genes, potentially enabling stronger gene expression regulation. Genes upregulated by the cytokine combination treatment include JAK2 (Fig. 5D) and STAT3 (Extended Data Table 1). We thus used an inhibitor of JAK-STAT signaling to determine its role in the ensheathment induced by these cytokines. Peficitinib, a pan-JAK inhibitor [30] was applied to the individual and combined cytokine conditions as well as the control group. Peficitinib significantly reduced the O4 + cell ensheathment levels under each cytokine alone or the combination treatment with the two cytokines, indicating the important role of JAK-STAT signaling pathways in mediating of ensheathment of oligodendrocytes by these cytokines (Fig. 6A-C).

Comparison of individual versus combined effects of TNF and IFNG on immune related signatures in mature OLs

Both cytokines, individually and in combination, induced MHC Class I gene expression, whereas MHC Class II genes were most prominently and significantly upregulated in the combinatorial treatment compared to individual cytokine treatments (Fig. S8A-B).

Cytokine-treated mature oligodendrocytes mirror oligodendrocyte transcriptomes in active MS lesions

To relate our in vitro findings to conditions observed in MS lesions, we utilized publicly available datasets from Absinta et al. [26], Jakel et al. [25] and Trobisch et al. [27] to document the presence of TNF and IFNG in immune cells, the expression of TFs, and the molecular properties of oligodendrocytes in MS lesions. After defining distinct cell populations, we examined IFNG and TNF expression across various cell types (Fig. 7B-C, Trobisch et al. dataset; Fig. S7A, B Jakel et al.). Microglia are present in control and all lesion samples, but T cells and B cells were markedly enriched in active and chronic active lesions compared to controls and chronic inactive lesions (Fig. 7D, Fig. S7B).

To assess which type of MS lesions show the most similar expression of TFs and TF regulated gene expression in mature OLs with our in vitro combinatorial treatment of cytokines on OLs, we re-clustered the in situ mature OLs and annotated them based on lesion pathology (Fig. 7E, F). The clusters separated clearly into control, active/chronic active, and inactive groups. We found significant upregulation of members of STAT and IRF TFs, including the key regulators STAT1 and IRF1 in oligodendrocytes from active/chronic lesions (Fig. 7E). An independent study also demonstrated elevated IRF1 in oligodendrocytes from chronic active lesions, reinforcing our findings [28]. In this analysis we also observed that several mature OL genes, such as CNP, PLP1 and MAG, showed increased expression in two or three of these datasets (Fig. 7E). MHC class I and II expression patterns were also highly consistent across studies, with significantly higher MHC class I and II expression in oligodendrocytes in the active/chronic clusters compared to controls and inactive lesions (Fig. 7E, F), mirroring our in vitro results under combinatorial TNF and IFNG treatment.

Discussion

The current study emphasizes the complexity of the injury and repair responses of OL lineage cells dependent on their molecular signatures and how these can be modulated by interactions with environmental regulators. The primary cells used in this study were human OLs derived from either adult or pediatric donors and separated into sub-populations using A2B5 antibody coated immunomagnetic beads. As described previously the selected cells (O4+A2B5+ positive) are referred to as pre-OLs by some study [7]. The non selected cells have the molecular profile of mature cells [24, 29]. We previously showed the superiority of the A2B5+ cells to ensheathe synthetic nanofibers [9]. Here we show the contrasting effect of adding our selected cytokines TNF and IFNG individually versus in combination on A2B5- and A2B5+ cells. Combination of TNF and IFNG had an additive effect on ensheathment by mature OLs compared to each cytokine alone, whereas the cytokine combination reversed the inhibitory effects on ensheathment by each cytokine alone in the A2B5+ cells. The inhibitory effects of each cytokine alone on the A2B5+ cells but not on the A2B5- cell fraction would seem consistent with the increased susceptibility of less mature OL lineage cells to inflammation mediated injury [15, 16].

In this study, we initially used bulk sequencing data derived from our mature human OLs subjected to cytokines in vitro to demonstrate that the cytokine combination induced the most extensive changes in overall gene expression followed by IFNG and then TNF alone. To specifically link our functional results with changes in the mature and OPC gene signatures expressed by these mature OL cells, we derived these signatures from publicly available single-nucleus RNA (snRNA-seq) datasets. The combinatorial treatment induced changes in a greater number of mature OL signature genes in these cells compared to cells treated with individual cytokines or to control conditions. Expression was increased in the majority of these mature OL signature genes by the combinatorial treatment whereas expression was reduced in the majority that significantly changed in response to single cytokines, most apparent with IFNG. These cytokine combination responsive genes included myelin specific genes (MOBP, CNP) and those that would be relevant for the observed functional ensheathment responses e.g. cell adhesion, development, and morphology. The most common relation of cytokine-induced changes in expression of individual genes as detected by pattern analysis was that genes upregulated by cytokine combination were downregulated by TNF alone; for IFNG, both up and downregulated genes were detected. These findings indicate crosstalk between the two cytokines i.e. can regulate the same set of genes in different directions. There was no significant change in expression of IFNG or TNF receptor genes in response to any individual or cytokine combination treatment (data not shown).

Although as reported previously [30], genes that define OPCs are much less expressed in our mature OLs versus OPCs. IFNG alone induced upregulation of the highest number and proportion of genes contained in the OPC signature whereas the cytokine combination induced increased numbers of both down and upregulated genes compared to control conditions. Pattern analysis indicated that for genes upregulated by IFNG alone, the majority were downregulated by the combination, again indicating crosstalk between the two cytokines. As regards a contribution of the cytokine effects on the OPC signature to the relation of the molecular and functional data, TNF induced the fewest changes in molecular OPC and mature OL signature markers compatible with having the observed limited effects on ensheathment. IFNG alone induces expression of OPC signature genes that would favor initiation of myelination by the mature OLs, consistent with our observation of modestly enhanced ensheathment in presence of IFNG, but this response would be limited by the downregulation of the mature OL signature genes. The cytokine combination overcomes some of the downregulation of the mature OL signature genes by these cytokines alone.

To identify the major TFs regulating the mature OL gene signature under combination conditions, we reconstructed a gene regulatory network. The analysis revealed a significant contribution of STAT and IRF family TFs in controlling these genes. Motif-based analysis showed that these TFs share the same binding motifs (for example a motif called homer-M00095) for the target genes, potentially enabling stronger gene expression. As we show, TNF itself significantly only induced IRF1 but in combination with IFNG an expanded array of IRFs and STATs were significantly induced, even to a higher level than by IFNG alone. TFs (SOX and FOX families) involved in downregulating mature OL signature genes by IFNG alone were distinct from those underlying the cytokine combination responses (IRF and STAT). However, some of these TFs including SOX family members were upregulated by some of IRFs induced by IFNG alone, indicating crosstalk of the two cytokines in OLs at the TF level. In addition to STAT and IRF family TFs, HMGA1 also responds to TNF and IFNG stimulation, upregulating multiple OL-related genes, including ASPA and MOBP, as observed previously [31]. As regards the expression of TFs regulating OPC signature genes, the cytokine combination induced a wider array TFs compared to single cytokine with only a limited overlap with mature OL signature linked TFs. Interestingly, those TFs (SOX) involved in upregulate the OPC signature genes by IFNG alone are the ones that downregulate mature OL signature genes in IFNG alone condition. Our reconstructed gene regulatory network showed distinct differences from that observed with mature OL gene signature responses.

Both TNF and IFNG can activate signaling cascades, the Janus-activated kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and STAT pathways are the most well studied [32]. The JAK-2/STAT-3 is documented to regulate OL differentiation and myelination [33, 34]. JAK-2 is upregulated in the OPC signature both by IFNG alone and by the cytokine combination. STAT-3 was upregulated in mature and OPC gene signatures by the cytokine combination (Extended Data Table 1). IFNG is also known to stimulate non-canonical STAT signaling pathways through formation of STAT1- (IRF)-9 complexes. IFNG-induced transcriptional activity also depends on association of STAT1 with other proteins, apart from the STAT1-phosphorylation observed in the canonical JAK-STAT pathway [32]. TNF induction of STAT-1 and IRF is also described [35, 36]. We provide data that blocking the STAT signaling pathway with an inhibitor decreases the combinatorial cytokines mediated effect on ensheathment by mature OL.

Our analysis of publicly available molecular data sets derived from MS tissues confirm that IFNG and TNF are both present in active MS lesions. We need also consider that lymphocytes and myeloid cells provide an array of additional factors that could modulate both the myelination and immune properties of OLs. As mentioned, both acute injury and maximal repair responses occur at time of peak inflammation. Transcription factors IRF and STAT are increased in chronic active MS lesions compared to inactive lesions and control tissues, an independent study also demonstrated elevated IRF1 in oligodendrocytes from chronic active lesions, reinforcing our findings [28]. OL lineage cells are shown to express immune-related molecules under pro-inflammatory conditions [37]. OLs in chronic active lesions show upregulation of MHC class I and II genes [23, 38, 39]. We observed that both cytokines individually induced immune-linked genes including both MHC Class I and II genes; greater expression was observed with the combinatorial treatment. Overall, our current findings regarding the induction of myelination related responses by combination of cytokines while also potentially contributing to injury reflects on the concept of the two-faced (Janus) response of inflammation within the CNS and the potential to modulate these to favor the clinical condition.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

As described in more detail by Luo et al. [9], tissue samples obtained and prepared for the functional ensheathment and bulk RNA sequencing studies were derived from adult and pediatric age patients undergoing elective neurosurgical procedures to alleviate non-tumor related medically resistant epilepsy. Adult surgical samples were obtained at the Montreal Neurological Institute and Hospital (MNIH). Pediatric samples were obtained from the Montreal Children’s Hospital. The use of adult tissues was approved by the MNI Neurosciences Research Ethics Board and the use of pediatric tissues by the Montreal Children’s Hospital Research Ethics Board. Patient age, sex, and diagnoses are provided in Supplementary Table S1. During surgery, samples from normal superficial tissue “corridors” that had to be removed to access the area of underlying pathology, were collected into separate CUSA (Cavitron Ultrasonic Surgical Aspirator) bags. Aliquots of these samples were provided for histologic analyses.

Cell isolation

Upon receipt of the CUSA bag (within 2 h of collection), these ex vivo samples were subjected to trypsin and DNase digestion followed by Percoll gradient centrifugation, as previously described [9]. To obtain enriched populations of mature OLs and A2B5+ cells, the total dissociated cell sample was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada) containing N1 (Sigma, Oakville, ON, Canada), 0.01% bovine serum albumin, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen, Burlington, ON, Canada) overnight and the floating cells were collected, leaving behind the adherent microglia. A2B5 antibody conjugated microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA) were then used to select A2B5+ cells. Non-selected cells, referred to as A2B5- (or A-), comprise the mature OLs. The A2B5+ (or A+) cells comprise ~ 5–10% of the total OL fraction [40, 41]; the high majority (~ 90%) express O4 [40, 41]. These cell preparations are devoid of astrocytes and neurons and have a small proportion of microglia as documented by FACS [41] and/or single cell mRNA sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 1 in Luo et al.) [9]. The 2 cell fractions (A+ and A-) express comparable levels of mature myelin genes (MBP and PLP) based on bulk RNA sequencing whereas progenitor genes (PDGFRa and PTPRZ1) are more highly expressed in the A+ cells (Fig. 3 in Luo et al. [9]. The co-expression of MBP with O4 in oligodendrocyte culture is illustrated in supplementary Fig. 2 (Fig. S2).

Nanofiber ensheathment cultures

Aliquots of A2B5+ and A2B5- cells were plated in multi-well aligned Nanofiber plates (The Electrospinning Company Ltd., Didcot, Oxfordshire, UK) (10,000 cells per microwell) and cultured in DMEM/F12 + N1 medium supplemented with B27 and T3 (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY), TNF (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), IFNG (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and combination of TNF and IFNG, with or without Peficitinib (Selleckchem, Burlington, Ontario, Canada) for two weeks. Cytokines were added every 2 days; N1 medial alone was the control. Cell cultures were immunolabelled with O4 (R&D Systems, Oakville, ON) and MBP (Avantor, Quebec, Canada) antibodies followed by corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 or 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining was used to verify the survival state under each treatment. To determine the percentage of O4+ cells in a culture ensheathing a nanofiber, a blinded rater counted individual O4+ cells whose processes could be identified as being connected to their cell bodies (Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining of nuclei). Ensheathed processes were recognized by their straight alignment with underlying nanofibers and increased thickness of the segments (Fig. 1E, F). Cells were assigned as ensheathing (one or more segments) by a blinded observer; segment lengths and the number of segments per cell were analyzed by Plugin Cell Counter ImageJ program.

Bulk RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

RNA was extracted from untreated cells or cells treated with TNF and IFNG and their combination for 2 days. Sequencing was conducted at the Génome Québec Centre using the Illumina platform and the NovaSeq 6000 PE100 machine. The GenPipes workflow facilitated alignment to the GRCh38 human genome and read counting. Specifically, raw FastQ files were aligned to the GRCh38 genome reference using the STAR aligner with default parameters [42], and raw reads were quantified using HTseq count. The DESeq2 package in R was employed for normalization, variance stabilization, and transformation of raw read counts [43]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between sample populations using DESeq2, considering P values, adjusted P < 0.05, log2 fold change > 0.58 or 1, and an average basal expression higher than 50 based on the analysis described in the results. This threshold for basal expression levels ensured the inclusion of expressed genes while excluding non-expressed ones.

For clustering analysis and heatmap visualization we used normalized read counts for gene expression data. Gene expression data were loaded using pandas [44] and log2-transformed with numpy [45] by adding 1 to avoid log-zero issues. Row normalization was performed using a custom min-max normalization function to scale each gene’s expression to the range [0, 1]. Two custom diverging colormaps (blue-white-red and blue-white-yellow) were generated with LinearSegmentedColormap from matplotlib [46]. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to the normalized data using seaborn’s clustermap function [47] with average linkage and correlation as the distance metric, clustering both genes and samples or only genes in separate analyses.

For principal component analysis (PCA), we used normalized read counts derived from our bulk RNA-seq studies. Gene expression data and corresponding metadata were loaded using pandas [44]. PCA was performed using scikit-learn’s PCA function to reduce the dimensionality to two components, and the percentage of variance explained by each component was calculated. PCA scatterplots were generated using seaborn [47] and matplotlib [46] with custom legends and sample labels to visualize clustering of samples according to treatment groups.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of significantly upregulated genes was conducted using gProfiler [48], with pathways showing an adjusted p-value of < 0.05 considered as significant. Bubble plots were generated using the negative log10 transformed p-values and the number of upregulated genes for the top GO biological process terms. For the summary plots, upregulated GO biological process terms obtained from gProfiler were summarized using REVIGO [49] with default parameters, and two-dimensional scatterplots were extracted. For the specific pathways induced by TNF, IFNG, and combinatorial treatments, GO terms were summarized using REVIGO and visualized in Cytoscape [49, 50]. For the mature OL and OPC signature genes upregulated by combinatorial treatment, we used the ClueGO v2.5.10 plugin of Cytoscape v3.10 for gene ontology analysis. Only significant terms (Gene Ontology biological processes, p < 0.05) were considered, and network specificity was set between medium and global.

To generate bubble plots, dependent on bubble plot type, gene set enrichment and gene comparison data were processed using pandas [44] and numpy [45] by reshaping the data into long-form DataFrames using functions such as np.tile and np.repeat to repeat pathway or gene terms for each treatment group or study. Missing values were imputed by setting p-values to 0 (or 1, as appropriate) and intersection sizes or gene counts to a minimal value, and bubble sizes were computed as -log10 (P) to reflect significance. For each bubble plot, colormaps were normalized using TwoSlopeNorm from matplotlib.colors [46] with parameters selected to center the color scale at the median or at zero, thereby enhancing contrast. Bubble plots were generated using seaborn’s scatterplot function [47], with the x-axis representing treatment groups or study names, the y-axis representing pathway or GO terms (or genes). Bubble sizes are proportional to intersection sizes or gene counts; bubble colors indicate-log10 (adjusted P) or average log2 fold change, depending on the dataset. Independent figures for color keys and size legends were produced using matplotlib to facilitate interpretation. This approach was applied uniformly across all bubble plot analyses, including those for pathways, gene comparisons, and GO term enrichment.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis

For these analyses, we have used three independent Single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) datasets including Jakel et al. [25], Absinta et al. [26] and Trobisch et al. [27]. For the Trobisch et al., we have used author’s generated object. For the Jakel et al. [25] and Absinta et al. [26] analysis was performed using the Seurat package [51]. Gene expression data were imported using Read10X and Seurat objects were created with CreateSeuratObject with a minimum of 3 cells per gene and 200 features per cell. The percentage of mitochondrial genes was calculated using PercentageFeatureSet with the pattern “^MT-“. Cells were filtered to retain those with nFeature_RNA between 200 and 2500 and with percent.mt below 20. Data normalization was conducted using NormalizeData, and variable features were identified using FindVariableFeatures with the “vst” method, selecting 2000 features. Data scaling was performed with ScaleData, followed by dimensionality reduction via RunPCA on the variable features. The neighborhood graph was constructed using FindNeighbors (dims = 1:20) and clusters were defined using FindClusters at a resolution of 0.1. UMAP visualization was generated with RunUMAP (dims = 1:20). Cluster identities were manually annotated and renamed with RenameIdents based on established lineage markers. Finally, UMAP plots were generated using DimPlot to visualize the distribution of cell types across the datasets.

Mature and immature oligodendrocyte gene signature generation

These signatures were generated using datasets from Absinta et al. [26] and Jakel et al. [25]. Mature OLs were defined using lineage-specific markers including PLP1, MAG, MOG, CNP, MOBP, and MBP, while OPCs were identified by CSPG4, PDGFRA, and PTPRZ1. After cluster annotation in the main Seurat object, clusters corresponding to OLs and OPCs were subset. Significantly upregulated genes were identified using the ‘FindMarkers’ function in Seurat with a mean.pct threshold of 0.1 and (adjusted P < 0.05), generating separate lists for OLs versus OPCs from each dataset. For similar comparisons (OLs versus OPCs and OPCs versus OLs from both studies), gene lists were overlapped using Venn diagrams to define common OL and OPC signatures.

For the pattern analysis of the generated signatures, we used regular DESeq2 log2 fold changes (log2FC). Each gene was screened separately for TNF, IFNG, and their combination, and kept if it passed p < 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 0.58 in any one of those contrasts. For retained genes, the three log2FC values (TNF, IFNG, and combination) were assigned to one of the eight possible response patterns for three treatments. We only called one condition “higher” or “lower” than another if their log2FCs differed by at least 0.25. If the difference was smaller than 0.25, we treated them as essentially the same.

Gene regulatory network analysis

To generate gene regulatory networks for the OL and OPC signatures and the 79 upregulated genes we identified upon combinatorial treatment of cytokines, we applied the same method. For the OL and OPC signatures, the gene lists were provided to the iRegulon plugin of Cytoscape [50, 52] using the following criteria: a 10k motif collection for human, minimum score > 2, ROC threshold for area under curve calculation (%) 0.03, and false discovery rate < 0.01. For the list of 79 genes, default settings of iRegulon were used to identify the most significant TFs. The results were then extracted and mapped with gene expression data to exclude non-significant transcription factors. Only TFs with normalized read counts > 50 and an adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant. Gene regulatory networks were reconstructed based on only significantly upregulated TFs and their corresponding target genes in Cytoscape. To assess the contribution of each TF in the regulation of target genes, we performed centrality analysis using CentiScape [53] on a directed network, considering out-degree as the edge direction (from TFs to target genes). Network visualization was conducted in Cytoscape [50].

Statistics

For nanofiber ensheathment assay, one way or two ANOVA followed with multiple comparisons except where noted (paired t -test). In all plots and across the text stars mean * p ≤ 0.05, ** for p ≤ 0.01, *** for p ≤ 0.001, and **** for p ≤ 0.0001.
