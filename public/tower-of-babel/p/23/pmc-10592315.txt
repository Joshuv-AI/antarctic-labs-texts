Controlling Superhydrophobicity on Complex Substrates Based on a Vapor-Phase Sublimation and Deposition Polymerization
Authors: Yu-Ming Chang, Yun-Shan Wang, Hsien-Yeh Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe superhydrophobic properties of material surfaces have attracted significant research and practical development in a wide range of applications. In the present study, a superhydrophobic coating was fabricated using a vapor-phase sublimation and deposition process. This process offers several advantages, including a controllable and tunable superhydrophobic property, a dry and solvent-free process that uses well-defined water/ice templates during fabrication, and a coating technology that is applicable to various substrates, regardless of their dimensions or complex geometric configurations. The fabrication process exploits time-dependent condensation to produce ice templates with a controlled surface morphology and roughness. The templates are sacrificed via vapor sublimation, which results in mass transfer of water vapor out of the system. A second vapor source of a polymer precursor is then introduced to the system, and deposition occurs upon polymerization on the iced templates, replicating the same topologies from the iced templates. The continuation of the co-current sublimation and deposition processes finally renders permanent hierarchical structures of the polymer coatings that combine the native hydrophobic property of the polymer and the structured property by the sacrificed ice templates, achieving a level of superhydrophobicity that is tunable from 90° to 164°. The experiments demonstrated the use of [2,2]paracyclophanes as the starting materials for forming the superhydrophobic coatings of poly(p-xylylenes) on substrate surfaces. In comparison to conventional vapor deposition of poly(p-xylylenes), which resulted in dense thin-film coatings with only a moderate water contact angle of approximately 90°, the reported superhydrophobic coatings and fabrication process can achieve a high water contact angle of 164°. Demonstrations furthermore revealed that the proposed coatings are durable while maintaining superhydrophobicity on various substrates, including an intraocular lens and a cardiovascular stent, even against harsh treatment conditions and varied solution compositions used on the substrates.Keywords: vapor sublimation and deposition, multicomponent, physical structuring and patterning, surface modification, biointerface coating

Introduction

Since the mechanism behind the non-wetting properties of lotus leaves in the 1970s was discovered through the use of scanning electron microscopy, it began attracting more and more attention and interest toward replication of such an appealing property for application in various fields.1 To reach the non-wetting or superhydrophobic state, the water contact angle (WCA) must be greater than 150° to enable the droplets to roll easily on surfaces. The hierarchical micro- and nanoscale structures of the surfaces have been found to be the primary reason for superhydrophobicity, as seen in lotus leaves.2,3 Over the last few decades, a variety of techniques have been studied and investigated to produce superhydrophobic surfaces for different applications, including electrospinning,4 phase separation,5 emulsion,6 plasma method,7 template method,8 solution immersion,9 chemical vapor deposition (CVD),10 sol–gel processing,11 lithography,12 spray coating,13 three-dimensional (3D) printing,14 etc. Among these methods, CVD possesses several superior features over other techniques, including an extremely uniform and conformal coating result, high purity, fine controllability, and solvent-free processing, making it suitable for a wide range of material-processing applications, especially candidating a promising treatment for miniaturized and sensitive materials/devices.15 Even with those superior features of the CVD method, however, the previous studies are focusing on applying the CVD method to a pre-made rough template by means of decreasing its surface energy to create a superhydrophobic surface, while the pre-made rough template that was not created by the vapor-phase process made the advantages of the CVD process disappear or be limited.16 Recently, some approaches have been developed to create rough templates via the vapor deposition method; however, the requirement of specific materials (e.g., silane for SiO2 nanoparticles and C2H2 for carbon nanofiber or carbon nanotube) and harsh conditions (e.g., high temperature and toxic chemicals) for creating roughness through the vapor-phase process limits the availability of various substrate materials.17 Although some template-free approaches using initiated chemical vapor deposition (iCVD) were demonstrated to overcome these issues,18 the requirement of using an initiator has a potential hazard to the environment.

Herein, we propose a simple and versatile fabrication process to produce superhydrophobic coatings based on the vapor sublimation and deposition polymerization process. Unlike the conventional ice templating technique that was mainly utilized to fabricate porous materials,19 the process exploits self-sacrificing ice/water templates, which feature the control of the time-dependent ice condensation and crystallization structure on the substrate surface that provides a native and tunable morphology from the surface and from bottom up. Subsequently, a transformation of the iced templates as well as the constructed templated morphology occurs and resulted in a final and permanent polymeric morphology during the fabrication process, which includes the following: (i) a sublimation of water/ice vapor escapes out of the coating system, at the same thermodynamic conditions; (ii) a second vapor of monomers is then introduced to the system, in which an opposite physiochemical behavior of deposition upon polymerization occurs on the iced templates, and the resultant polymeric structures replicate the same morphology of the evaporated ice templates; and finally, (iii) the combination of the hydrophobic nature of the vapor-deposited polymer with the same time-constructed hierarchical structure and morphology delivers the proposed superhydrophobic coating on the substrate subface. In the demonstration, we used [2,2]paracyclophanes as the starting materials to form the vapor monomers and produce superhydrophobic coatings of poly(p-xylylenes) on the surfaces.20 In contrast, in comparison to existing approaches of superhydrophobic treatments that required additional and complicated methods to create surface roughness and are limited to flat substrates and simple geometry, our proposed fabrication process enables the production of controlled coating structures in hierarchy and from bottom up and yields a tunable surface superhydrophobicity on complex substrates with ease.

Results and Discussion

The proposed fabrication process utilized a home-built vapor deposition system, which allowed for a temperature-controlled sample stage in the deposition chamber and to accommodate ice templates and the polymer precursors for the described sublimation and deposition polymerization. Theoretically, commercially available equipment that is designed for polymer vapor deposition can also be used to realize the process. During the fabrication, 200 mTorr of pressure was ensured to maintain throughout the process, and a key parameter of the temperature was controlled between −4 and 25 °C for the sublimation (ice template, water molecule) and deposition (polymer precursor, quinodimethane monomer) substances to behave. Under the thermodynamic conditions, the ice template and water molecule tend to undergo an endothermic phase transition of a sublimation process and from a solid ice to vaporized water molecules. In contrast, the vaporized quinodimethane monomers favor deposition and polymerization at the same thermodynamic conditions, thus forming solid-phase polymers of poly(p-xylylenes) on the substrates.21 The two processes, namely, the sublimation of ice and the deposition of poly(p-xylylene), occur contentiously and simultaneously while competing with mass transfer flow and complimenting each other with the same volume space.22,23 With fine control of thermodynamic conditions, the deposited polymer on such an iced template would result in a porous and 3D polymer structure instead of a conventional dense thin film, as shown in previous work.23 We therefore hypothesize that the controlling of morphology and roughness of the ice templates can result in the controls of the constructed structures with thereafter deposited polymers based on the described vapor sublimation and deposition process and are applicable on a variety substrate materials with complex geometries. Also, to prevent the formation of porous structures that would affect the morphology of constructed structures, we suppose that the manipulation of the ratio of the deposition rate to the sublimation rate during the fabrication process can result in the non-porous structure of deposited polymers. On the basis of previous discoveries,22,24 the architectural and structural fidelity was found precisely controllable; i.e., fine replica structures were obtained for the deposited polymers compared to their ice templates. During the experiment, substrates were placed at a controlled humidity of approximately 75%, and lowering the substrate temperature with a nitrogen bath can instantly obtain an iced template via a condensation process. Interestingly, the structures of the formed ice template by condensation appear to be fractal as a result of aggregation, and the degree of the aggregation was found to be a time-dependent process.25 Illustration of the fabrication process is shown in Figure 1a that revealed that a substrate surface is subjected to the controlling crystallization of an iced template, and the proposed superhydrophobic coating is constructed subsequently by the sublimation and deposition process. Theoretically, a wide range of substrates are applicable for the fabrication process, and for the demonstration in the study, substrates, including papers, ceramics, metals, and polymers, are used and tested. By examination of the surface morphology of the iced template (ice crystalline structure, before the process) and the superhydrophobic coating (replicated polymer structure, after the process), the optical images in panels b and c of Figure 1 also showed high similarity for two surfaces with a surface roughness (Rq) of 2.89 and 2.52 μm, respectively, and a WCA of approximately 164° was observed on the fabricated coating. The important vapor compositions during the fabrication process were also examined in detail, and as revealed in Figure 2a, the analysis results of residual gas using a real-time mass spectrometric gas analyzer indicated that the characteristic peaks observed at 103 and 138 amu correspond to the presence of the monomer resulting from the pyrolysis of the precursor. The peak observed at 18 amu is attributed to the water vapor escaping from the ice template under reduced pressure, and the peak at 40 amu represents the carrier gas argon. To verify the chemical structures of the prepared coatings, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was also performed, and the results were compared to those of the coatings prepared using the same vapor deposition procedure but without the ice template (see Figure S1 of the Supporting Information). The FTIR data for both coatings displayed similar characteristic bands of poly(p-xylylene), including an alkyl C–H stretch around 2900 cm–1, an aromatic C–C stretch around 1500 cm–1, and a C–Cl stretch around 800 cm–1, indicating that the presence of the ice template did not affect the chemical structures of the coatings. Furthermore, the absence of the O–H peaks around 3500 cm–1 confirms the consumption of the ice template by the sublimation process and that the overall FTIR data verified the formation of the same poly(p-xylylene) polymer during the proposed fabrication process. Verifications of the hypothesis that forming condensed ice template fractal structures and the same deposited poly(p-xylylene) structures can render a tunable hydrophobic property on a substrate surface were further examined. The WCA of the resulting coatings was found to increase when the condensation time also increased (Figure 2b). Specifically, the contact angle increased from 135.1° ± 4.7° for coatings with 1 min of condensation time to 164.4° ± 1.9° for coatings with 4 min of condensation time. Notably, the contact angle of coatings without ice templates was measured to be 89.4° ± 0.7°, indicating a remarkable improvement in water repellency of conventional vapor deposition coatings with the assistance of ice templates. Intriguingly, the analysis of contact angle hysteresis revealed that only coatings with a condensation time of 4 min exhibited a contact angle hysteresis of 8.3° ± 1.7°, while coatings with a condensation time of less than 4 min displayed a “sticky” state,26 in which water droplets adhered firmly to the surface without any slipping, irrespective of its tilt (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information). These findings suggest that the water repellency of the coatings is highly dependent upon the condensation time and that the superhydrophobic properties of the coatings can only be achieved under specific conditions. To further investigate the wetting behavior of the coatings, the surface morphology of coatings with varying condensation times was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and 3D profile imaging. The results shown in Figure 3b indicate that, with longer condensation times, the profile features left by the ice template became increasingly dense and obvious and the surface roughness of the coatings increased accordingly. Young’s model, the Wenzel model, and the Cassie–Baxter model are used to explain how the variation in surface roughness lead to the various wetting behaviors27 (Figure 3a). For smooth surfaces of the coatings without the ice template, the wetting regime is physically and chemically homogeneous as a result of the low surface roughness. Therefore, the wettability is mainly determined by the surface chemistry, in accordance with Young’s model. As surface roughness increases as a result of the formation of ice templates, the real solid–liquid contact area gradually increases, while the wetting regime remains homogeneous as a result of the relatively large spacing of profile features, as depicted in the Wenzel model. This wetting behavior results in a higher contact angle and higher adhesive force between the solid surface and liquid droplet, as observed in the “sticky” state of coatings with the condensation time less than 4 min. As surface roughness continues to increase and the spacing of profile features keeps decreasing with rising condensation time, the droplets eventually cause the air to be trapped in the spaces of profile features beneath it. This results in the wetting regime becoming heterogeneous with a decrease in the solid–liquid contact area and an increase in the solid–gas contact area. As a consequence, the coatings exhibit ultralow adhesive force and ultrahigh contact angles, namely, the superhydrophobic state as predicted by the Cassie–Baxter model. More quantitatively, we can apply the mathematic model regarding the hierarchical roughness proposed by Feng et al.28 to simulate the superhydrophobicity.

The fractal dimension D in 3D space is 2.2618. For the coating with 4 min of condensation time, the upper and lower limit scales of the surface, L and l, were 10 and 1 μm, respectively. The surface fraction under the water droplet occupied by solid material and air, fs and fv, was estimated to be 0.1 and 0.9, respectively. The value of θ was measured to be 89°. Therefore, the value of θf can be calculated by eq 1 as 161.1°, which is close to the measured value (164.4° ± 1.9°). The result also indicates that the surface roughness induced by ice templates plays a key role in determining the wetting behavior of the coatings.

One important aspect in achieving superhydrophobic state is the multiscale roughness of surfaces, because it helps increase the apparent contact angles and reduce the contact angle hysteresis.3 To better understand the mechanism underlying the formation of hierarchical structures of ice templates, the growing process of ice crystals under different conditions was investigated. The schematic illustration of two different patterns of the condensation process resulting from the different substrate temperatures is shown in Figure 4a, with the results presented in Figure 4b. At a classic condition of the substrate temperature around −20 °C, ice crystals on the substrate were observed to grow slowly in terms of the crystal size over time. The results are in accordance with previous research that focused on the growth pattern of ice crystals. Specifically, the growth of ice crystals occurs at the solid–gas interface when saturated vapors contact the surface of ice crystals and subsequently condense to form a new layer of ice crystal.29 The size distribution analysis of ice crystals using ImageJ also reveals that the dimensions of each ice crystal continued to develop over time, whereas the number of ice crystals in the same area remained approximately constant (Figure S3 of the Supporting Information). However, when the temperature of substrates was reduced to below −100 °C by immersion in a liquid nitrogen bath, ice crystals exhibit a distinct growth pattern from that described above. Specifically, ice crystals on the substrate begin to grow in terms of numbers rather than size. The corresponding analysis of the size distribution also confirmed the increasing number of ice crystals with an almost constant crystal size (Figure S3 of the Supporting Information). The different growth patterns of ice crystals resulting from varying the substrate temperature can be explained by the different locations where water vapor condenses to ice crystals. In the case of substrates at around −20 °C, the water vapor pressure while decreasing as it approaches the cooled surface remains sufficiently high (approximately 171 Pa) for condensation to occur on the surface of ice crystals. Conversely, the saturated vapor pressure in the case of substrates maintained below −100° is nearly 0 Pa (below 0.02 Pa), implying that nearly all of the water vapor in proximity to the cooled substrate would instantaneously condense to ice crystals in the air and lead to the formation of a thin layer of ice fog near the surface of the substrates. Subsequently, the newly formed ice crystals will land and attach onto the cooled substrates as a result of gravitational force, gradually stacking to form larger structures. As indicated in the SEM images in Figure 4b, the structures left by the assembly of plate-like ice crystals resulted in disordered arrangement, confirming that these small structures were constructed via random attachments of ice crystals floating in the air. Furthermore, the sizes of these small structures exhibit no significant variation between different condensation times, directly verifying that, as the temperature of the substrate remains below −100 °C, the position where water vapor condenses to ice crystals will be located in the air instead of the solid–gas interface of ice crystals. Overall, by cooling substrates to below −100 °C, water vapor will condense into small ice crystals in the air and stack to form larger structures on cooled substrates, eventually achieving the preparation of ice templates with hierarchical surface roughness.

The chemical durability of the coatings prepared with ice templates was demonstrated through various treatments, including exposure to heat, ultraviolet (UV) light, and acid, alkali, and corrosive solutions, as shown in Figure 5a. It was observed that the coatings retained their high contact angles even after being exposed to these conditions, indicating the exceptional ability to maintain water repellency under a harsh environment. The results were also in accordance with the inherent chemical properties possessed by poly(p-xylylenes), including heat and UV resistance and chemically inertness, demonstrating that the coatings with the usage of the ice template still retain their inherent chemical properties.30 The chemical properties of coatings mainly depend upon the outcome of the vapor deposition and polymerization process, in which the propagation of the polymer chain is of great importance.31 While ice templates undergo sublimation during the process, the produced water vapor basically does not participate in the propagation reaction of the monomer as a result of the fact that water is commonly used as an solvent in the radical polymerization process.32 Therefore, the existence of an ice template does not interfere with the polymerization throughout the whole process and eventually renders the formed polymer its inherent chemical properties. Furthermore, the versatility of coatings with superior superhydrophobicity was demonstrated through their wide applicability to various substrate materials, including silicone dioxide ceramic, polycarbonate plastic, aluminum metal, and cellulose filter paper. As shown in Figure 5b, the coated substrates consistently displayed the WCA ranging from 156° to 164°, which was higher than that observed on uncoated substrates, irrespective of the substrate material. Meanwhile, it was also demonstrated that the superhydrophobicity of prepared coatings is independent of the surface roughness from different substrate materials. Moreover, droplets from commonly available solutions, including deionized water (denoted as water), whole milk (denoted as milk), black coffee (denoted as coffee), apple juice (denoted as juice), Coca-Cola (denoted as cola), and soy sauce (denoted as sauce), were tested on the proposed superhydrophobic coatings, and the WCA was measured to be within the range of 153° and 161°, as shown in Figure 5c, indicating that the superhydrophobic properties of the prepared coatings are independent, regardless of the solution compositions. Furthermore, with acknowledging the distinctive and widely adopted features of the vapor deposition coatings being their coating fidelity and precision on intricate surfaces and devices, i.e., complex 3D or curved configurations and geometries, we finally confirmed the hypothesis of rendering the superhydrophoibic coating technology with the proposed vapor sublimation and deposition process onto complex conditions of substrates, and the results in Figure 5d showed that the superhydrophobic coatings were successfully applied on complex device surfaces, such as an intraocular lens and a cardiovascular stent, using the proposed fabrication process. A high contact angle of approximately 155.3° was measured on these surfaces with consistency. In parallel to its sibling of conventional vapor deposition coating being a versatile and precise treatment for delicate and complex materials and devices, the present process in the study agreed with the same processing versatility, additionally facilitated the control of the important surface hydrophobicity property, and achieved the level of superhydrophobicity on a variety of encountered substrate conditions.

Conclusion

The introduced superhydrophobic coating technology is versatile and can be applied to a wide range of substrate conditions, from two-dimensional (2D) to 3D and curved surfaces. It is also applicable regardless of the solution compositions. The technology is based on a vapor-phase sublimation and deposition process, which offers the advantages of being a dry and solvent-free process as well as providing conformal and precise coating fidelity on complex dimensions and geometries of substrates. The process can theoretically be applied to a wide range of substrate materials. In this study, we used an ice template for sublimation and poly(p-xylylene) (parylene, U.S. Pharmacopeia Class VI as highly biocompatible) for deposition. These materials agreed well and are compatible with applications in sensitive and delicate substrate conditions. The fabricated coatings exhibited a WCA of 164° compared to the conventional poly(p-xylylene) coating of approximately 90°, demonstrating superior hydrophobicity. The coatings also showed high durability, maintaining above 150° after exposure to harsh conditions. The proposed fabrication process and coating technology have significant potential for unlimited application developments in the fields of energy materials, aerospace and defense materials, and biomaterials that require stringent and tunable interface properties.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Dichloro-p-cyclophane (≥92%, Galxyl C, Galentis, Italy) was purchased from La Chi Enterprise Co., Ltd. (Taiwan). Hydrochloric acid (36.5–38.0%, ACS reagent) was purchased from Honeywell Fluka (Charlotte, NC, U.S.A.). Sodium chloride (≥99.0%, ACS reagent) and sodium hydroxide (≥98%, reagent grade) were ordered from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.). Blue dye (Brilliant Blue FCF, Warner-Jenkinson Co., St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.) was ordered from Echo Chemical Co. (Taiwan). Whole milk was purchased from Kuang Chuan Dairy Co., Ltd. Black coffee (Starbucks, Seattle, WA, U.S.A.), apple juice (Tree Top, Selah, WA, U.S.A.), cola (Coca-Cola, Atlanta, GA, U.S.A.), and soy sauce (Wan Ja Shan Brewery Co., Ltd.) were purchased locally. All of the materials were used without further purification.

Fabrication Process

For the fabrication of superhydrophobic coatings with a hierarchical surface morphology, first ice templates were prepared through the following steps: The substrate from a certain material or device was properly cleaned before using. Then, the substrate was placed on a cold stage, which has been precooled by immersion in liquid nitrogen, and was placed in a sample chamber. After the substrate and cold stage achieved thermal equilibrium, wet air was introduced into the chamber using a custom-built humidity controller to provide a humid environment for the initiation of the condensation process of water vapor, eventually causing the growth of ice crystals on the substrate.

After a specific time of the condensation process, the inlet of wet air ceased, and the chamber was connected to home-built CVD equipment for the subsequent process. Substrates with the formulated ice templates were placed in a custom-built vacuum chamber for the vapor sublimation and deposition process to occur. The CVD equipment is composed of a quartz tube through a three-stage furnace and connected to a reaction chamber, which is connected with a cold trap cooled with liquid nitrogen, followed by a vacuum pump (E2M40, Edwards, U.K.). The CVD processing conditions were maintained at a vacuum pressure of approximately 200 mTorr and a sample stage temperature of approximately −4 °C. For the vapor deposition of poly(p-chloro-xylylene) on the substrate, the precursor of dichloro-[2,2]-paracyclophane (Galxyl C, Galentis, Italy) was vaporized and transported by the carrier gas of argon [15 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm)]. The vapor precursor was pyrolyzed after passing through a furnace (670 °C) and forming the reactive quinodimethane monomers, which later deposited on the ice template through an adsorption process and caused the polymerization to occur.

In Situ Vapor Composition Analysis

The monitoring of the vapor compositions during the sublimation and deposition process was conducted by real-time mass spectrometry with a residual gas analyzer (RGA, HAL RC 511, Hiden Analytical, Warrington, U.K.), and the operating conditions were at a pressure of 1 × 10–7 mbar with 70 eV electron ionization energy and 20 μA emission current.

Chemical Property Analysis

FTIR was performed to identify the chemical structure of polymeric coatings with or without assistance of the ice template. FTIR spectra were recorded with a liquid nitrogen-cooled (MCT) spectrum 100 FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) equipped with an advanced grazing angle specular reflectance accessory (AGA, PIKE Technologies, Fitchburg, WI, U.S.A.). The scanning range was from 500 to 4000 cm–1 with 4 cm–1 resolution and 128 scans.

Surface Morphology Analysis

SEM was performed to analyze the surface morphology of the prepared coatings. Images of the interested area were recorded using Nova NanoSEM (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, U.S.A.) with a primary energy of 10 keV and an operating pressure of 5 × 10–6 Torr. The sample was coated with a conductive layer of platinum before SEM analysis.

To measure the surface roughness of prepared coatings, a 3D profile of the interested area was taken by a laser confocal 3D profile microscope VK-9500 (Keyence, Japan), and the results of surface roughness as well as the 3D profile images were constructed by a Keyence analyzer (version VK-H1-A9).

Surface Hydrophobicity Analysis

The contact angle measurements were conducted to confirm the superhydrophobicity of prepared coatings. Imaging and calculation of contact angles were performed using a JY-PHb contact angle meter (Xian Yima Optoelec, China). Each measurement was performed by placing a 10 μL deionized water droplet on the prepared coatings and was repeated in triplicate for different samples. The measurement of contact angle hysteresis was performed by the tilting method with a tilting rate of 1° s–1. The image of the droplet right before it started to move was recorded, and the corresponding advancing and receding contact angles were measured. Contact angle hysteresis was determined by the difference between the advancing (θa) and receding (θr) contact angles.

Growth Process of Ice Crystal Analysis

To analyze the growth process of ice crystals on the cold substrate, the optical images of the substrate surface were taken at different condensation times by digital microscope B013 (Supereyes, China). The image processing was conducted with ImageJ (version 1.53a) to obtain the results of the size distribution of ice crystals within a given area (1400 × 1050 μm2).

Durability Test Environment

A thermal durability test was performed by placing coated substrates on a hot plate (PC-420D, Corning, Corning, NY, U.S.A.) and maintained at 100 °C. An UV light durability test was performed by exposing coated substrates to a compact UV lamp (UVGL-25, UVP, Upland, CA, U.S.A.) with a power of 1240 W/m2. An acid, alkali, and corrosive solution durability test was performed by immersing coated substrates in 0.1 M hydrochloride, 0.1 M sodium hydroxide, and 3.5 wt % sodium chloride solution, respectively. All of the tests were conducted at normal conditions for 2 h.
