Establishment of a high-throughput field defoliation data survey strategy combined with genome-wide association studies to reveal the genetic basis of defoliation in cotton
Authors: Bowei Xu, Le Liu, Rumeng Zhao, Jiajie Yang, Bin Wu, Lili Lu, Xiantao Ai, Jingshan Tian, Fuguang Li, Kai Zheng, Liqiang Fan, Zuoren Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-harvest defoliation of cotton is a key agricultural measure to improve mechanical harvesting efficiency and raw cotton purity. Collecting data on cotton defoliation traits for genetic localization and thus breeding defoliation-prone varieties is an essential alternative to traditional defoliant spraying. Nevertheless, it is hampered by low throughput and artificial error in manual field surveys. In this study, a framework for collecting high-throughput defoliation data in large fields was established. Three spectral indices (MTCI, VDVI, CI) and leaf area index (LAI) were first screened as core predictors through hierarchical segmentation analysis in three levels: leaf number (LN), leaf number difference (LND), and defoliation rate (DR). Four deep learning architectures (CNN, BiGRU, CNN-BiGRU, and CNN-BiGRU-Attention) were developed, and the CNN-BiGRU-Attention hybrid model demonstrated superior performance at all three levels, with R2 values exceeding 0.85. Importantly, the inversion accuracy of this model at the LN and LND levels was superior to that at the DR level, which was also confirmed by the results of the genome-wide association study (GWAS). We combined GWAS and transcriptome results to identify a new gene, GhDR_UAV1, associated with defoliation traits. The overexpression of GhDR_UAV1 significantly promoted the wilting of cotton leaves, indicating that GhDR_UAV1 plays a positive regulatory role in cotton defoliation. This study proposed a strategy to invert cotton defoliation data at three levels using deep learning fusion of UAV remote sensing data and LAI data and confirmed that LND can provide accurate phenotypic data for GWAS analysis. This study provides a new theoretical basis for cotton defoliation regulation and genetic improvement by integrating cotton high-throughput defoliation phenomics and genomics from an innovative perspective.Keywords: Defoliation, UAV, Genome-wide association study, Deep learning, LAI

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.), as one of the most economically significant crops on a global scale, plays a crucial role in various industries and economies [1]. In recent years, along with the gradual expansion of mechanized cotton production in China, the planting area of machine-harvested cotton has also increased significantly [2,3]. The total cotton planting area in China reached 2,838,300 ​ha, with Xinjiang region accounting for 2,447,900 ​ha, representing approximately 86 ​% (National Bureau of Statistics, China, 2024). Xinjiang is the largest and most representative cotton-growing region in China. According to the types of cotton cultivated and various indicators such as fiber length, fineness, and yield, the primary cotton varieties in this region are upland cottons (Gossypium hirsutum), including Yuanmian 8, Tahe 2, and Xinluzhong 62 [4]. With the expansion of large-scale production and increased mechanisation in Xinjiang's cotton industry, mechanical harvesting has become widely adopted to enhance efficiency. The primary cotton varieties cultivated in Xinjiang are particularly well-suited for mechanical harvesting due to specific traits, such as the compact plant architecture of cotton [5]. Cotton leaves remaining on the plants can reduce the purity of cotton harvested mechanically. Therefore, to facilitate an efficient and effective cotton harvest, ensuring high-quality defoliation is crucial before the mechanized harvesting process. Effective defoliation can reduce the phenomenon of residual leaves hanging on branches to significantly improve the picking rate, and further decrease the residual leaves and impurities mixed into the machine-picked cotton to improve the fiber quality of cotton [6]. Natural defoliation of cotton is insufficient for timely and complete mechanical harvesting. Thus, defoliant application is essential to improve defoliation efficiency and support mechanized harvesting [7]. Defoliants are widely used in modern agriculture, but their application also incurs environmental and economic costs and may pose a risk to human health [8,9]. Breeding cotton varieties with improved natural defoliation traits can significantly reduce reliance on chemical defoliators and thus advance sustainable agriculture.

The sensitivity of cotton to defoliants is a key trait that significantly influences both defoliation efficiency and mechanically harvested fiber quality [10]. Identifying genes and creating new material is critical for breeding new defoliant-sensitive cotton varieties that can significantly reduce the use of defoliant [11]. Pan et al. manually investigated defoliation rate data of 328 upland cotton accessions in four environments at two locations after 5 days of defoliant treatment and further analyzed the data with resequencing data by GWAS, and found that the gene GhDS-A06 may be involved in the development of the abscission zone [12]. Li et al. manually surveyed defoliation rate data of 383 upland cotton accessions in four environments at three locations over two years. They identified two genes, GhRDR7 and GhRDR13, which were significantly associated with defoliation rate by GWAS analysis, and further confirmed their function in enhancing defoliation sensitivity by gene silencing experiments [13]. These studies provide a critical foundation for the potential broad application of key genetic loci in breeding machine-pickable cotton. Unfortunately, there are still few studies on the genetic localization and functional validation of defoliation traits in cotton due to the disadvantages of manual survey of defoliation data, such as low throughput and high artificial statistical errors [14,15]. Therefore, investigating cotton leaf defoliation efficiently and accurately has become a research hotspot. Remote sensing technologies can effectively replace traditional manual measurement methods [16], enabling high-throughput, high-precision collection of leaf defoliation data, which greatly facilitates the identification of leaf defoliation genes.

Recently, with the rapid rise of unmanned aerial low-altitude remote sensing (UAV-LARS) technology in agricultural applications [17], it has emerged as a useful tool for evaluating the effectiveness of defoliation strategies in cotton [18]. RGB and multispectral data acquired through UAV remote sensing can accurately predict photosynthetically active radiation (FPAR) of cotton leaves to assess leaf numbers and determine the optimal defoliant dosage [19], as well as the defoliant droplet distribution pattern to determine defoliant dosage for unmanned aerial spraying systems (UASSs) [20]. Furthermore, spectral indices can be calculated using UAV multispectral data to assess cotton defoliation effects [21]. These assessments of defoliation effects could not meet the accuracy required for use in genome-wide association study (GWAS) analyses because UAVs can only capture information about the cotton canopy from a top-down perspective. This has led to the absence of successful cases in current research where leaf fall phenotype data obtained through remote sensing technology have been combined with genotype data to identify genes. The integration of multiple remote sensing technologies to replace single remote sensing technologies has become a tool for breaking the deadlock by improving the accuracy of leaf defoliation data acquisition. To comprehensively investigate the amount of cotton defoliation in the canopy, defoliation data can be detected by bottom-up measurement of leaf area index (LAI) using a plant canopy analyzer. LAI refers to leaf surface area per unit of ground surface area, which is directly correlated with plant canopy structure [22] and is a key indicator for evaluating plant growth [23,24]. Changes in cotton LAI are often closely related to the process of leaf abscission following defoliant application [25]. Combining top-down scanning from a UAV high-throughput platform with bottom-up detection from a plant canopy analyzer provides a robust and feasible method for accurately estimating cotton defoliation.

This study aims to fuse UAV remote sensing and LAI data to precisely and effectively acquire defoliation data of cotton, followed by GWAS analysis to identify and validate the functions of key candidate genes. The innovative aspects of this work are as follows: (1) Two remote sensing technologies (UAV and plant canopy analyzer) were used to detect the surface and internal conditions of cotton canopies from both top-down and bottom-up perspectives, thereby obtaining high-throughput and high-precision leaf defoliation data. (2) Three types of data reflecting cotton leaf defoliation (leaf number, leaf number difference, and defoliation rate) were comprehensively evaluated to establish the optimal input strategy for GWAS analysis. (3) Four deep learning algorithms (CNN, BiGRU, CNN-BiGRU, and CNN-BiGRU-Attention) were employed to identify the optimal model, thereby enhancing the accuracy of defoliation phenotypic data. (4) Integrating high-throughput cotton defoliation phenomics with genomics from an innovative perspective, new genes related to defoliation were identified and genetically validated, creating new materials for efficient mechanical harvesting of cotton.

Materials and methods

Site description and field experimental design

In this study, 419 upland cotton (G. hirsutum L.) accessions that possess diverse genotype data [26] were utilized. These accessions are primarily employed for investigating the genetic mechanisms underlying various agronomic traits and the specific information is provided in Table S1. The study was conducted in Shihezi, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China (86°0′04″E, 44°18′27″N) (Fig. 1A). The experimental field was divided into 419 plots, each measuring 4.5 ​m ​× ​5 ​m and consisting of one row and six columns (Fig. 1B). During the leaf defoliation survey conducted in the late growth stage of cotton, we found that some of the 419 cotton accessions had thinner stems that were unable to support the weight of the cotton bolls, resulting in varying degrees of lodging. The morphological changes in cotton plants after lodging can affect the monitoring of defoliation. Therefore, we carefully observed the plants in the field and ultimately selected 214 cotton accessions with good plant structure and morphology for the study. The sampled plots were sprayed with a defoliant after data collection on September 15, 2023, and data collection was repeated on September 22 and 29, 2023, respectively (Fig. 2A). Data collection consisted of three levels: UAV surveys, LAI 2200C canopy analyzer measurements, and manual surveys.

UAV-based remote sensing and preprocessing

The RGB data were captured using a DJI Mavic 3E UAV equipped with an RGB camera, and the multispectral data were collected using a DJI Mavic 3M UAV equipped with a multispectral camera covering four spectral bands. UAV-based image acquisition using RGB and multispectral sensors was conducted at a relative flight altitude of 15 ​m between 12:00 and 16:00 and UAVs were configured with a forward overlap rate of 80 ​% and a side overlap rate of 70 ​%. We can efficiently collect cotton defoliation data on 214 plots using UAVs, with a total flight time of approximately 20 ​min.

Agisoft Metashape v2.1.0 and Pix4Dmapper Pro v2.0.104 were employed to process RGB and multispectral data. The primary preprocessing steps included image alignment to ensure spatial consistency, camera alignment optimization to enhance geometric accuracy, dense point cloud generation to extract image information, and digital orthophoto model (DOM) development to stack different spectral bands. Nine spectral indices were derived from the DOM of RGB and multispectral imagery by computing distinct band combinations to estimate cotton defoliation (Table 1). These indices comprised five color indices (generated from RGB data) and four vegetation indices (calculated from multispectral data), each targeting specific physiological or morphological traits linked to defoliation.

LAI data acquisition

Leaf area index (LAI), defined as half the total one-sided leaf surface area per unit ground area, is a critical biophysical parameter to quantify plant canopy structure, and thus LAI can be used as an important indicator for assessing defoliation in cotton. In this study, LAI values of 214 sample plots were measured by an optical sensor-based instrument, the LAI 2200C plant canopy analyzer (LI-COR Ltd., United Kingdom). It utilizes a light interception model to estimate leaf area based on canopy radiation interception measurements and is widely used for LAI assessment in agricultural applications [36,37]. This instrument can perform light scattering correction to improve accuracy under direct sunlight. LAI measurements require reading from above and below the canopy to calculate LAI values based on radiation difference. When using a plant canopy analyzer for LAI measurement, each plot takes approximately 27 ​min.

Manual survey of leaf number

Leaf number (LN) was manually surveyed in 214 sample plots using a five-point random sampling method. The number of leaves of a single plant was randomly surveyed in the four corners and the center area of each sample plot, and the average value was calculated to represent the final number of leaves in a single plot. Each plant takes approximately 3 ​min to count leaf number manually, and it takes a total of 222 ​min approximately to survey the entire plot.

Three statistical levels for evaluating cotton defoliation

Defoliation rate is currently calculated from manual surveys of cotton defoliation numbers and analyzed as phenotype data with genotype data for GWAS [13]. However, whether high-throughput inversion of defoliation rate by remote sensing is the most suitable phenotypic data for GWAS analysis has not been studied. In this study, cotton defoliation was counted for the first time through three levels (Level 1: leaf number (LN); Level 2: leaf number difference (LND); and Level 3: defoliation rate (DR)). Specifically, leaf number refers to the average number of leaves in the sample plots at the three sampling time points, leaf number difference indicates the value of the change in leaf number between the first and the last two sampling time points, and defoliation rate was calculated using leaf number and leaf number difference (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical segmentation and convolutional neural network (CNN) hybrid model

The UAV-based spectral indices and plant canopy analyzer-derived LAI were assessed by a hierarchical segmentation method implemented through the “glmm.hp” package [38,39]. The significance of the variables in the random forest regression was measured using the percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE) [40] to determine the contribution of each factor to influencing the defoliation rate.

The convolutional neural network (CNN) model typically consists of the input layer, the convolutional layer, the pooling layer, and the full connection layer. A larger convolution kernel results in stronger network performance, while a smaller convolution kernel leads to faster convergence. The versatility of CNN architectures enables their application across various domains, including image classification and segmentation, object detection, and regression prediction tasks [41]. Bidirectional gated recurrent unit (BiGRU) simplifies bidirectional gated long short-term memory (BiLSTM) by merging gates and unifying memory with the hidden state, offering a computationally efficient alternative [42]. The CNN-BiGRU model combines CNN and BiGRU. CNN is used to extract spatial features from the input data, while BiGRU can process time series data from both forward and backward directions simultaneously, capturing bidirectional temporal dependencies [43]. An attention mechanism was integrated into the CNN-BiGRU framework to selectively emphasize task-critical features (Fig. 2B). By dynamically weighting the hidden states of the BiGRU, this mechanism refines feature representations and significantly enhances forecasting accuracy [44]. In this study, we explored various architectures of CNN by adjusting the size of the convolution kernels to identify an optimal configuration (Table S2). Based on the test results, a convolution layer utilizing 5x1 filters was ultimately selected. During the training process, the size of the filter is automatically calculated and adjusted to ensure that the output of the layer matches the input size. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was utilized as the activation function, thereby improving the nonlinear fitting capability of the neural network. In the pooling layer, a 2x1 convolution kernel was employed using the max-pooling sampling method, with a convolution stride of 2. The output size of the fully-connected layer was 32. In the BiGRU module, the number of hidden units was set to 10. In the attention mechanism module, the weights were processed through the softmax function to obtain the final attention output. Besides, the Adam adaptive gradient descent algorithm was employed to enhance the efficiency of the models. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, a dropout layer was also generated with a random discard rate of 0.1. This study employed K-fold cross-validation to assess the generalization performance of the model. By partitioning the dataset multiple times, this method mitigates bias that may arise from a single random division.

To evaluate model performance, the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) were calculated to compare predicted values with manually measured values. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a game theory-based framework designed to interpret the behavior of machine learning models. It employs SHAP values as an additive feature attribution method to quantify the contribution of each feature by representing the model's predictions as a linear combination of binary variables [45,46]. This study quantified feature contributions through SHAP values to evaluate the influence of input feature variations on the cotton defoliation estimation model. A total of 300 SHAP values were computed and normalized to assess the relative impact of features, reflecting their positive and negative effects on model predictions. Beeswarm plot is a visualization tool designed to rank feature importance and illustrate the magnitude and direction of each feature's impact on the model's predictions. The summary plot provides a global interpretation of feature importance, derived by computing the mean absolute SHAP values for each feature. In contrast, the heatmap plot visualizes the underlying structure of the dataset through supervised clustering and heatmap representation. Supervised clustering is based on the log-transformed SHAP values of data points rather than their raw feature values. The line plot visualizes variation in the supervised clustering results of SHAP values for each feature, highlighting trends in feature contributions over an X-axis.

Genome-wide association studies for the defoliation trait in 3 levels

The defoliation effects of 214 cotton accessions exhibited rich diversity, with the highest defoliation rate exceeding 90 ​% and the lowest below 50 ​%. This meets the requirements of GWAS analysis and can more effectively identify genetic variations closely related to leaf defoliation. We extracted 214 cotton accessions from 419 upland cotton genome resequencing data and filtered them to obtain 1,901,549 high-quality single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (MAF >0.05, missing rate <10 ​%) using PLINK [47] software, which was analyzed by GWAS through the EMMAX software. The genome-wide significance thresholds of all tested traits were set as −log10 (p) ​= ​5 (control threshold). Manhattan plots of GWAS results were plotted using the “CMPlot” package in the R software.

RNA library construction and sequencing

Defoliant-sensitive (BL34) and defoliant-insensitive (LM28) cotton materials were treated with defoliant (Thidiazuron, TDZ) at the 10-leaf stage. Samples were collected at 0, 36, and 72 ​h post-treatment, and total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit for cDNA library construction and high-throughput sequencing using the Illumina platform. Raw reads were quality filtered using fastp software and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 software. Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package, and genes with p-values ≤0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ​≥ ​1 were considered differentially expressed genes and selected for further analysis.

Vector construction and cotton transformation

The GhDR_UAV1 gene fragment was amplified from Gossypium hirsutum cDNA using standard PCR procedures. The amplified product was inserted into the WMV067 vector containing the CaMV 35S promoter via double digestion with the restriction enzymes BamHI and SalI. The recombinant plasmid was verified by sequencing and subsequently introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Positive clones were identified using colony PCR. Finally, transgenic cotton plants overexpressing GhDR_UAV1 were generated using the shoot apical meristem transformation (SAMT) system.

Results

The high efficiency and effectiveness of the defoliation investigation in this study

By integrating a top-down scanning method using a UAV platform with a bottom-up detection method utilizing a plant canopy analyzer, the efficiency and effectiveness of cotton defoliation assessment have been enhanced. To validate this method, a comparative analysis was performed across various methods used for investigating cotton defoliation (Table S3). Based on total time expenditure, assuming that only one person collects data from 214 plots, traditional manual surveys would take approximately 33 days, while the method established in this study would require only 20 ​min of UAV flight time, plus 4 days of LAI surveys. Based on survey efficiency, manual surveys would take approximately 222 ​min for one plot, whereas LAI and UAV surveys require only 27 ​min and 5.6 ​s, respectively. Based on survey effectiveness, both the manual survey and the LAI survey demonstrated high precision. The medium accuracy achieved by UAV surveys is due to the unavoidable loss of some accuracy in the indirect prediction of cotton defoliation by remote sensing data in the acquisition of great efficiency advantages. However, a combination of UAV and LAI survey can make up for the deficiency in UAV accuracy. Due to the low efficiency of manual surveys, they are unsuitable for collecting leaf defoliation data across large populations and cannot be applied on a large scale in GWAS analysis. This study combined UAV surveys with LAI surveys, achieving better results in the GWAS analysis.

Hierarchical segmentation results of three statistical levels for cotton defoliation evaluation

Hierarchical segmentation analysis was used to reveal the contribution of nine spectral indices and LAI to cotton defoliation traits in three statistical levels and to further screen the optimal indices at each level for defoliation monitoring. In level 1, six spectral indices and leaf area index (LAI) were significantly associated with leaf number with mean squared error (%IncMSE) values of MTCI (17.06), CI (16.21), VDVI (13.80), LAI (11.34), NDVI (9.33), MGRVI (8.75), and NGRDI (8.46) (Fig. 3A). In level 2, five spectral indices and LAI were significantly correlated with leaf number difference with %IncMSE values of VDVI (22.88), MTCI (21.45), CI (21.15), LAI (9.96), NDVI (7.66), and EXGR (5.80) (Fig. 3B). In level 3, four spectral indices and LAI were significantly associated with defoliation rate with %IncMSE values of LAI (37.38), MTCI (30.89), VDVI (13.1), RGBVI (12.26), and CI (9.01) (Fig. 3C). The intersection of the significant indices obtained at these three levels was obtained and MTCI, LAI, VDVI, and CI were all significantly associated with cotton defoliation traits as the core predictors of the defoliation monitoring model (Fig. 3D).

Estimation of cotton defoliation traits at three levels based on CNN, BiGRU and CNN hybrid models

The number of leaves, the difference of leaves number, and the defoliation rate were trained and tested with significant indices screened by hierarchical segmentation on the 1d-CNN model, BiGRU model, CNN-BiGRU model, and CNN-BiGRU-Attention model, respectively, and the scatter plots combined with the marginal histograms illustrated the distribution of measured and predicted values (Fig. 4). The results demonstrated that the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model outperformed others in estimating cotton defoliation at three statistical levels, with R2 values all exceeding 0.85, which is particularly evident at level 1 (R2 ​= ​0.91) and level 2 (R2 ​= ​0.89). Similar trends were observed in both the 1D-CNN and CNN-BiGRU models. The estimation performance for level 1 (R2 ​> ​0.88) and level 2 (R2 ​> ​0.80) was relatively superior compared to level 3 (R2 ​< ​0.80). The BiGRU model had satisfactory prediction results at level 1 (R2 ​= ​0.81) but exhibited poor predictive performance at level 2 and level 3 (R2 ​< ​0.60). Model accuracy metrics, including mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE), were summarized in Table S4. Overall, the accuracy of each model at Level 1 (LN) is significantly higher than at Levels 2 (LND) and 3 (DR). This discrepancy may be attributed to the differing sizes of training sets across these levels: Data for LN was collected from three time points, whereas LND and DR data were sourced from only two time points, which were calculated from three time points. This observation highlights the impact of numerical operations on prediction results. Additionally, it is worth noting that although the training samples for leaf number differences and leaf defoliation rates are the same, the predictive performance of leaf number differences is superior to that of leaf defoliation rates, indicating that the effectiveness of predictions also depends on the nature of the data itself.

SHAP analysis results of CNN-BiGRU-Attention model

To assess the contribution of different features to the estimation of cotton defoliation traits, we conducted an interpretability analysis of the input features in the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model using the SHAP method, which elucidates the nonlinear relationships between the raw feature values and their corresponding SHAP values, thereby quantifying feature importance and directional impacts on predictions. Beeswarm and summary plot revealed that feature importance rankings follow the order of VDVI ​> ​MTCI ​> ​CI ​> ​LAI in the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model, and the mean absolute SHAP values are +1.23 (VDVI), +1.18 (MTCI), +1.04 (CI), +1.02 (LAI). Notably, VDVI exhibited the widest SHAP value range, indicating its strong interpretative power for target features, whereas LAI showed the narrowest range, reflecting weaker influence (Fig. 5A and B). The color gradient in the plots corresponds to the raw feature values (not SHAP values) at each data point. MTCI demonstrated an inverse relationship with SHAP values (higher MTCI values correlated with lower SHAP values), contrasting with the positive correlations observed for VDVI, CI, and LAI. The heatmap combined with the line plot revealed the underlying structure of the dataset based on supervised clustering, demonstrating that the four significant indices have similar trends of SHAP value according to the clustered results and the line trend plot (Fig. 5C and D). It indicated that these four indices had similar contributions to the model. In the heatmap plot, MTCI exhibited significantly higher values within the instance range of 0–20, whereas VDVI demonstrated notably lower values in the instance range of 184–201. This indicates that their contributions to the model were more pronounced compared to CI and LAI. Furthermore, based on the results presented in the line chart, it is evident that VDVI displayed a more stable trend with less volatility than the other three indices across the overall instance interval. Consequently, it can be concluded that VDVI contributes most substantially to the model, which aligns with findings from the summary chart. VDVI can effectively reflect the phenotypic structure of cotton [48] and shows a significant correlation with cotton defoliation prediction [49]. Therefore, VDVI can be considered a reliable indicator for estimating defoliation when using a single index. Furthermore, both the MTCI and CI indices are highly correlated with chlorophyll levels. This suggests that chlorophyll information is associated with defoliation, indicating that chlorophyll can also serve as an effective indicator for estimating defoliation.

GWAS results based on LND data are in the best consistency with those obtained based on manual data

GWAS analysis was carried out using the data of 214 cotton accessions at three levels of leaf number, leaf number difference and defoliation rate at two time points. At time 1, the results for level 1 and level 2 showed consistency, with intervals of more significant signaling for chromosomes A08 and A10, and no regularity in the results for level 3. At time 2, these two intervals became more significant across the 3 levels, with the effect of level 2 being the most representative. A total of 515 SNPs were significantly associated with cotton defoliation in significant correlation peaks of chromosomes A08 (∼2.60–∼2.70 ​Mb) and A10 (∼99.89–∼99.94 ​Mb) (Table S5). To verify the reliability of the level 2 results, we performed a GWAS analysis of the manually detected leaf number difference values with the genotype data and detected peaks located at the same positions on chromosomes A08 and A10 (Fig. 6D). Further gene annotation was performed for the A08 and A10 significant intervals, and 15 genes were found to be annotated (Table S6).

GhDR_UAV1 is a potential defoliation-sensitive gene for cotton

To comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms of cotton in response to defoliant thidiazuron (TDZ), we selected two representative cotton cultivars, BL34 and LM28, which display pronounced differences in TDZ sensitivity. BL34 is highly sensitive to TDZ, while LM28 exhibits strong tolerance. For transcriptomic profiling, leaf samples from both cultivars were collected at 36 and 72 ​h with TDZ treatment or no treatment, and subjected to high-throughput RNA sequencing (Fig. 7A and B). Phenotypic observations revealed that BL34 plants began to exhibit visible leaf wilting as early as 36 ​h post-treatment, with the severity of wilting and the number of desiccated leaves increasing dramatically by 72 ​h. In contrast, LM28 showed weaker signs of wilting or dryness throughout the observation period (Fig. 7C–E). These results confirm the different physiological responses to TDZ in both cultivars.

(F) Heatmap showing the expression patterns of the GhDR_UAV1 gene in cotton leaf at two stages in LM28 and BL34 after TDZ treatment.

To identify candidate genes involved in TDZ response, we focused on 15 genes in the interval localized by GWAS. The RNA sequencing results showed that the expression level of Gh_A10G2109 was significantly downregulated in both BL34 and LM28 after TDZ treatment, and the decline in sensitive BL34 was more obvious (Fig. 7F). This gene, a member of the P450 family encoding photosensitive pigments, was named GhDR_UAV1 in this study. The transcript level of GhDR_UAV1 decreased rapidly and significantly after TDZ treatment, indicating that it may act as a negative regulatory factor for TDZ-induced leaf defoliation. Differences in GhDR_UAV1 expression may be the molecular basis for the differences in sensitivity between BL34 and LM28.

GhDR_UAV1 positively regulates cotton defoliation

To further substantiate the functional role of GhDR_UAV1 in modulating cotton response to TDZ, we generated multiple independent transgenic cotton lines with constitutive overexpression of GhDR_UAV1 (GhDR_UAV1-OE) using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The expression of the transgene was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR, which showed a more than 10-fold increase in GhDR_UAV1 transcription levels in GhDR_UAV1-OE lines compared to wild-type (WT) plants (Fig. 8B). Independent homozygous T3 lines with high GhDR_UAV1 expression were selected for further phenotypic and physiological analysis.

GhDR_UAV1 positively regulates cotton defoliation. (A) Phenotypes of ZM24 and GhDP_UAV1-OE under TDZ treatment at 0 ​h, 36 ​h, 72 ​h, and 108 ​h post-treatment (Treat), compared to control groups (No Treat). (B) Detection of the GhDR_UAV1 expression in ZM24 and GhDP_UAV1-OE lines. (C–E) Quantification of leaf death in ZM24 and GhDP_UAV1-OE lines at 36 ​h ​(C), 72 ​h (D), and 108 ​h ​(E) after TDZ treatment.

Upon TDZ treatment, GhDR_UAV1-OE lines exhibited a significant acceleration of leaf wilting, with visible symptoms appearing as early as 36 ​h post-treatment, while WT plants remained largely unaffected at this stage (Fig. 8A–C). The progression of leaf senescence and cell death was quantitatively assessed by measuring the number of dead leaves at multiple time points (36 ​h, 72 ​h, 108 ​h). The GhDR_UAV1-OE lines exhibited significantly more dead leaves at all time points compared to WT, and statistical analysis confirmed the robustness of these differences (Fig. 8A–D, E). Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence that GhDR_UAV1 acts as a positive regulator of TDZ-induced leaf senescence and abscission, and its overexpression renders cotton more sensitive to TDZ-induced cell death and leaf defoliation.

Discussion

High-throughput phenotypic monitoring technology in the field facilitates the application of GWAS analysis for cotton defoliation

Cotton production in Xinjiang is characterized by large-scale cultivation and highly mechanized production systems [50], and mechanized harvesting efficiency and quality depend largely on achieving high defoliation rates [51]. High-throughput and high-precision phenotyping data acquisition and analysis of cotton defoliation dynamics are essential for defoliation-prone cotton variety screening and enhancing mechanized harvesting workflows. Currently, extensive research has focused on gene identification and functional resolution of traits such as cotton fiber quality and resistance [[52], [53], [54]], and research on cotton defoliation traits remains scarce [12,13], which is mainly due to the low throughput of manual surveys in the field. More importantly, protracted manual assessments have the potential to exaggerate the observed defoliation rate due to the continuous leaf shedding, thus biasing the results obtained from phenotypic data used in GWAS to accurately identify defoliation genes. To meet the demand for GWAS analysis of defoliation traits, high-throughput and synchronized phenotypic investigations of defoliation in the entire cotton population are necessary.

The traditional GWAS analysis used manually surveyed leaf numbers converted to defoliation rate as input data, however, there is no previous study to prove the effectiveness of remote sensing inversion of defoliation rate data directly for GWAS analysis. In this study, we innovatively integrated UAV remote sensing technology with canopy structure analysis technology to achieve a high-throughput collection of defoliation traits in a large field, which is effective for GWAS analysis and for identifying novel genes associated with defoliation. To facilitate subsequent studies on defoliation traits in different crops, we comprehensively constructed three strategies: inversion of leaf number, leaf number difference, and defoliation rate, respectively. The results of all four deep learning models proved to be much more effective in model building for both leaf number and leaf number difference than defoliation rate, which is supposed to be since defoliation rate is due to the conversion of data twice, which exceeds the performance of the modeling algorithms. The model performance results indicated that the number of leaves was the most effective in estimating cotton defoliation, followed by leaf number difference, and defoliation rate was the least effective. However, the accuracy of the model does not necessarily show an absolute positive correlation with the GWAS analysis results. Further validation is needed to determine which type of defoliation data can produce better results in GWAS analysis.

So far, no studies that obtained defoliation rates through high-throughput phenotyping techniques have been validated by genotypic data. In this study, the robustness of the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model was confirmed by GWAS analysis of three levels of data obtained from model inversion. More importantly, defoliation-regulated genes were successfully identified and experimentally validated through the combination of GWAS and transcriptome data, which will greatly enhance the confidence of researchers in the technology of high-throughput investigation of defoliation traits in crop fields.

A CNN-BiGRU-Attention model enables high-precision multi-level cotton defoliation assessment

To enhance the accuracy and reliability of high-throughput collection and analysis of defoliation phenotypes, four deep learning models were constructed in this study to invert the leaf number, leaf number difference, and defoliation rate, respectively, to develop an optimal input strategy for GWAS analysis. CNN algorithm showed a strong ability to estimate continuous biological parameters such as cotton leaf nitrogen content [55] and canopy Verticillium wilt severity parameters [56]. Therefore, we would like to apply the CNN model to cotton defoliation analysis to improve the inversion ability. The effectiveness of this model was proved based on the results of R2 values above 0.78 for all three levels. The BiGRU algorithm can extract temporal information about the input variables in both forward and reverse directions while capturing bi-directional temporal features of the data [57], but the BiGRU model performs well only at level 1 (R2 ​= ​0.81), and at the other two levels the R2 value is below 0.60. This discrepancy may be attributed to a larger number of training sets available at level 1 compared to levels 2 and 3. The CNN-BiGRU model integrates the spatial feature extraction from CNN and the temporal feature extraction from BiGRU, aiming to improve data prediction capability [43]. The accuracy of the CNN-BiGRU model in this study was significantly higher than that of the BiGRU model, but similar to the CNN model. To address this limitation, we incorporated an attention mechanism into the CNN-BiGRU model, which dynamically prioritizes task-critical features across hierarchical levels while addressing class imbalance within multi-level training sets [58]. The results confirm that the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model maintains consistently high performance (R2 ​> ​0.85) at all three levels, demonstrating robust generalization capability. This is because the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model effectively mitigates the impact of imbalanced sample sizes across multi-level training datasets, resulting in an accurate and robust defoliation assessment. The defoliation data obtained by inversion of the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model and the data obtained from manual field measurements were subjected to GWAS analysis with the genotypes, respectively, and the results all proved the performance of this model. In summary, when using CNN and BiGRU algorithms separately, the CNN model performs well, while the BiGRU model performs poorly. The accuracy of the CNN-BiGRU framework is significantly higher than that of the BiGRU model, but similar to that of the CNN model. Combining the attention mechanism with the CNN-BiGRU framework enables the most accurate prediction of defoliation data.

Limitations and future directions

This study successfully identified a defoliation gene through the defoliation phenotype of natural cotton populations. The reliability of enhancing defoliation sensitivity was demonstrated by creating gene overexpression materials. Currently, this gene has not been applied to cotton varieties for large-scale commercial cultivation, limiting its practical application. Future work will focus on targeted improvement of cotton varieties to increase their defoliation rates. Given that varieties with high defoliation rates are more suitable for mechanical harvesting, this study is expected to make significant contributions to future practical production.

Conclusion

In this study, defoliation traits data of 214 cotton accessions at three levels, leaf number (LN), leaf number difference (LND), and defoliation rate (DR), were acquired by deep learning algorithms using UAV remote sensing data and LAI data. We successfully developed a framework for high-throughput collection and application of cotton defoliation data in the field: (1) using MTCI, LAI, VDVI, and CI as the core predictors; (2) inverting leaf difference data with the CNN-BiGRU-Attention model; and (3) identifying genes through GWAS and transcriptome results and experimentally creating new cotton materials susceptible to defoliation. This framework will bridge the gap between abundant genotype data and limited field phenotypic data of defoliation, thereby facilitating the integration of genotypic and phenotypic research in crop improvement.
