Techniques of Arthroscopic Anterior Syndesmosis Stabilization
Authors: Dhivakaran Gengatharan, Wayne Yong Xiang Foo, Wen Xian Png, Chong Keen‐Wai, Eric Wei Liang Cher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnkle sprains are among the most common sports‐related musculoskeletal injuries. Injury to the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament, resulting in anterior syndesmotic instability, is frequently overlooked and inadequately treated. The purpose of this technical note is to (1) describe clinical examination maneuvers used to assess anterior syndesmotic stability; (2) review radiologic features suggestive of syndesmotic instability; and (3) present 2 arthroscopically assisted techniques for anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament reconstruction. The surgical techniques are described stepwise, with emphasis on indications, technical pearls, and potential pitfalls.

Ankle sprains are among the most frequently encountered sports‐related musculoskeletal injuries, with the majority involving the lateral ankle ligament complex. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) is most commonly injured, followed by the calcaneofibular ligament and posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL). 6 Recently, there has also been increased interest in the presence of concomitant anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL) injuries. 6 , 7

The AITFL is a primary stabilizer of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis and is essential for maintaining ankle mortise congruency. 8 , 9 , 10 Originating from the anterolateral tibial tubercle and inserting onto the anterior distal fibula, the AITFL restrains excessive external rotation and lateral translation of the fibula. Among the syndesmotic ligaments, it is the weakest and therefore most susceptible to injury during rotational ankle trauma. 11 , 12

AITFL injury typically occurs through a forced external rotation mechanism combined with ankle dorsiflexion. 12 Ligament disruption results in syndesmotic widening, lateral talar translation, and loss of ankle mortise congruency, leading to abnormal ankle joint loading. Clinically, patients often present with localized anterolateral ankle pain approximately 1 to 2 cm proximal to the distal fibular tip, tenderness along the syndesmosis, pain reproduced with external rotation stress, and a positive squeeze test. 13 , 14

Radiologic assessment helps confirm the diagnosis and assess injury severity. Plain radiographs may show widening of the tibiofibular clear space and reduced tibiofibular overlap, which may be accentuated on stress views obtained in dorsiflexion. Dynamic ultrasonography has shown increased syndesmotic widening during dorsiflexion‐external rotation, whereas magnetic resonance imaging allows direct evaluation of AITFL integrity and associated syndesmotic instability. 15

Despite its biomechanical importance, AITFL injury is frequently overlooked during the surgical management of lateral ankle instability. Isolated ATFL repair or reconstruction is commonly performed, even in the presence of concomitant syndesmotic pathology. Emerging evidence suggests that recognition and treatment of anterior syndesmotic instability at the index procedure are associated with improved clinical and functional outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 The purpose of this technical note is to describe 2 arthroscopically assisted techniques for AITFL reconstruction in patients with anterior syndesmotic instability.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Intraoperative Evaluation of AITFL Injuries

The authors recommend performing 2 stress maneuvers under general anesthesia to assess dynamic widening of the syndesmotic joint. Both maneuvers may be performed under real‐time fluoroscopy to enhance visualization of the apparent widening of the tibiofibular space.

1.Lateral translation of the talus: With the tibia stabilized, a valgus force is applied at the heel to translate the talus laterally against the fibula. In the presence of syndesmotic instability, the fibula may be displaced laterally, resulting in widening of the distal tibiofibular joint and an increased tibiofibular clear space.

Lateral translation of the talus: With the tibia stabilized, a valgus force is applied at the heel to translate the talus laterally against the fibula. In the presence of syndesmotic instability, the fibula may be displaced laterally, resulting in widening of the distal tibiofibular joint and an increased tibiofibular clear space.

2.Ankle dorsiflexion: With the ankle stabilized, the foot is moved from neutral into maximal dorsiflexion, positioning the widest portion of the talus within the ankle mortise. In an unstable syndesmosis, this maneuver stresses the syndesmosis, resulting in observable widening as depicted in Figure 1.

Ankle dorsiflexion: With the ankle stabilized, the foot is moved from neutral into maximal dorsiflexion, positioning the widest portion of the talus within the ankle mortise. In an unstable syndesmosis, this maneuver stresses the syndesmosis, resulting in observable widening as depicted in Figure 1.

3.Ankle arthroscopy: Ankle arthroscopy allows direct visualization of the distal tibiofibular joint and assessment of syndesmotic stability. A positive “drive‐through sign,” whereby an arthroscopic probe can be easily advanced into the distal tibiofibular space, is highly suggestive of syndesmotic injury. In some cases, frayed ligamentous tissue or insertional avulsion fragments may also be identified.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine on a radiolucent table with a thigh tourniquet applied. A towel roll beneath the calf facilitates arthroscopic instrumentation.

Portal Placement

Standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals are established. Landmarks include the interval between the tibialis anterior tendon and the medial malleolus, and the interval between the peroneus tertius and the lateral malleolus, respectively. A diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to assess the joint and address concomitant pathology.

Syndesmotic Reduction

Reduction is achieved using a pointed reduction clamp placed between the distal tibia and fibula, as shown in Figure 2. Adequate reduction is confirmed fluoroscopically, with restoration of at least 1 mm of tibiofibular overlap on ankle mortise view. A 1.6 mm K‐wire is used to transfix the tibia and fibula; following this, the clamp may be removed.

Technique 1: All‐Inside Arthroscopic AITFL Reconstruction

The fibular AITFL footprint is identified approximately 1 cm proximal to the distal fibular tip and just inferior to the ankle mortise. After debridement and preparation of the osseous bed, a 1.8 mm Q‐Fix Ultra all‐suture anchor with Ultratape suture (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is inserted into the prepared fibula footprint. It is crucial to avoid a low‐lying fibular insertion, as this increases the risk of graft verticalization, which can lead to postoperative anterolateral impingement.

The distal anterolateral tibial footprint is then identified, and a 1.2 mm K‐wire may be used to ensure appropriate placement of the anchor. The suture tape inserted into the distal fibula is anchored to the distal tibia footprint using a 3.5 mm Footprint mini‐PK suture anchor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA), with the appropriate tension. It is essential to remove the reduction clamps before tightening the tape to minimize the risk of over‐tensioning. The key operative steps are highlighted in Figure 2.

Technique 2: All‐Inside Arthroscopic AITFL Reconstruction with Additional Accessory Portal

A small (approximately 0.5 cm) accessory incision is made directly over the fibular AITFL footprint, allowing direct anchor insertion with minimal debridement, as the anchor used may be inserted directly through the stab incision. After insertion, the suture is then passed back into the ankle joint and retrieved from the anterolateral portal. The steps are illustrated in Figure 3. The remaining steps mirror Technique 1.

Assessment of Syndesmosis Stability After Surgery

After the reconstruction, the stability of the syndesmosis complex is confirmed by the absence of the arthroscopic drive‐through sign and presence of at least 1 mm tibio‐fibula overlap on the ankle mortise view during lateral talus translation and forced dorsiflexion of the ankle.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The postoperative care protocol includes immediate, supervised weight‐bearing and range‐of‐motion exercises performed by a qualified physiotherapist. Patients are advised to avoid foot eversion and external rotation for 4 weeks to prevent excessive stress on the reconstructed AITFL. This is followed by progressive strengthening and a return to daily activities and sports in accordance with the patient's lifestyle.

DISCUSSION

This Technical Note describes 2 arthroscopically assisted techniques for the management of anterior syndesmotic instability resulting from injury to the AITFL. The distal tibiofibular syndesmosis plays a critical role in maintaining ankle mortise stability, and untreated or inadequately managed syndesmotic injuries may lead to chronic pain, persistent instability, and post‐traumatic osteoarthritis secondary to altered ankle biomechanics and abnormal load distribution. 16 , 17 Recent contemporary arthroscopic reports have further emphasized the importance of recognizing subtle anterior syndesmotic instability, particularly in athletic populations. 18 , 19

Accurate clinical assessment of ankle sprains, particularly high ankle sprains, is essential to avoid under‐recognition of syndesmotic instability. Stress maneuvres such as lateral talar translation, forced dorsiflexion–external rotation, and arthroscopic evaluation of the distal tibiofibular joint reliably identify dynamic syndesmotic widening. 13 , 14 , 15 Arthroscopy further allows direct visualization of syndesmotic competence, with a positive drive‐through sign serving as a useful intraoperative indicator of instability. 18 , 19 These same maneuvres may be repeated following reconstruction to confirm restoration of syndesmotic stability.

Growing evidence suggests that failure to address concomitant AITFL injury in patients with lateral ankle instability may compromise clinical outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 Several studies have shown improved functional outcomes when anterior syndesmotic instability is recognized and treated at the index procedure rather than relying solely on isolated ATFL repair or reconstruction. 8 , 9 , 10 , 16 On this basis, we advocate consideration of AITFL reconstruction when clinical, radiologic, and intraoperative findings indicate anterior syndesmotic instability.

The primary objective of this technical note was to describe 2 reproducible arthroscopically assisted techniques for AITFL reconstruction, each with distinct advantages and limitations, allowing surgeons to tailor their approach based on experience and patient‐specific anatomy. Both methods are consistent with previously described arthroscopic strategies for syndesmotic stabilization while emphasizing precise restoration of the native AITFL footprint and orientation. 18 , 19 , 20

The first technique is entirely arthroscopic and does not require additional incisions beyond standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals. This approach allows direct visualization of the fibular and tibial footprints, and facilitates assessment of anchor placement. However, adequate exposure may necessitate extensive debridement, which carries the risk of unnecessary soft‐tissue disruption. Additional limitations include the potential for anchor crowding or blowout during concomitant ATFL reconstruction and the risk of low‐lying fibular anchor placement if proximal exposure is insufficient.

The second technique involves a small accessory incision over the fibular footprint, enabling more precise anchor placement at the ankle mortise. This approach minimizes the need for extensive arthroscopic debridement and preserves surrounding soft tissue. It facilitates a more horizontal orientation of the reconstructed ligament, resulting in a shorter and potentially more biomechanically favorable construct. These advantages may be particularly relevant when AITFL reconstruction is performed in conjunction with other lateral ligament procedures. Nevertheless, this technique introduces an additional incision and carries potential risks of superficial irritation, scar sensitivity, or localized hardware prominence.

Postoperatively, early weight‐bearing with a protected range‐of‐motion that avoids eversion and external rotation is recommended to promote recovery while minimizing stress on the reconstructed AITFL. Rehabilitation protocols should be individualized, particularly in cases involving concomitant procedures such as fracture fixation, deltoid ligament repair, or lateral ligament reconstruction, which commonly coexist with syndesmotic injuries. 12 , 16

Both techniques described are safe, reproducible, and technically straightforward. Awareness of their respective advantages and potential pitfalls allows surgeons to select the most appropriate approach for their practice. Further comparative, outcome‐based studies are warranted to determine whether one technique offers superior biomechanical stability, better functional outcomes, or lower complication rates.
