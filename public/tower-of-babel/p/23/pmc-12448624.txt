Dynamic sperm proteome remodeling during epididymal maturation in a marsupial, Macropus giganteus†
Authors: Sarah Kunkel, Stephen D Johnston, Amanda Nouwens, Taylor Pini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) are widely used in domestic animals, successful implementation of ARTs to conserve wildlife species remains challenging. In macropods, crucial aspects of fundamental reproductive biology, including changes induced by epididymal maturation, remain unknown, limiting the development of ARTs. In this context, we performed a proteomic analysis of spermatozoa from the caput, corpus, and cauda epididymis of Eastern Grey Kangaroos (n = 6) to profile changes over epididymal maturation. Samples prepared by filter-aided sample preparation digestion were analyzed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry with sequential window acquisition of all theoretical acquisition. A total of 4304 proteins were identified, with significant overlap across epididymal regions. Highly abundant proteins in common across caput, corpus, and cauda spermatozoa had strong enrichment for tubulins and included four histone proteins. The most significant proteomic remodeling was observed in the corpus to cauda transition, late in epididymal transit (728 differentially abundant proteins). Overall proteomic changes across epididymal maturation (1131 differentially abundant proteins) suggested a loss of sperm glycosidases and an increase in flagellar proteins, including tubulins and dyneins. These findings serve to highlight both consistencies with eutherian sperm epididymal maturation (e.g. bias toward protein loss over transit, transfer of proteins via extracellular vesicles) and elements which are likely unique to marsupials (e.g. reduced chromatin stability, potential use of β-oxidation as a major metabolic pathway). This critical information can now be leveraged to further develop ARTs in marsupials.

Introduction

Despite their iconic status, macropods (kangaroos, wallabies, and potoroos) face an uncertain future in Australasia. Of the 63 extant species in Australia and New Guinea, 60% are currently under threat due to habitat loss, climate change, and/or introduced predators [1]. While the use of gene banking and assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) can potentially play an important role in conservation efforts, these methods are yet to be fully developed in any marsupial. Compared to more invasive and challenging techniques such as in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer, artificial insemination with frozen semen is a readily applicable technique requiring significantly less optimization. However, to date, there has been very limited success in macropod sperm cryopreservation despite extensive optimization, empirical experiments, and studies of cryodamage [2, 3]. This limited success appears to be driven by lack of a suitable low toxicity penetrating cryoprotectant [3, 4], and limited research into suitable handling media compared to other marsupials [5]. Addressing these limitations to allow for further application of macropod sperm cryopreservation and artificial insemination requires a deeper knowledge of molecular sperm biology in this family.

Post-testicular maturation in the epididymal duct is a pre-requisite for fertility, involving intense, dynamic remodeling of sperm [6]. While functional and molecular changes in sperm over epididymal transit have been characterized extensively in eutherian mammals, much less is known about marsupial sperm maturation. Seminal work characterized epididymal maturation in macropods using microscopy [7–9], with more recent work exploring lipidomic changes [10]. Interestingly, macropod sperm enter the epididymis in a T-shape, with the head lying perpendicular to the tail, followed by reorientation to perpendicular to the tail during epididymal maturation [9, 11]. Such extreme post-testicular reorganization of the sperm head appears to be a peculiarity of the Marsupialia, and suggests that the period of epididymal maturation may drive key species differences in mature sperm function.

The objective of this study is to explore proteomic changes in spermatozoa over the course of epididymal maturation in a model macropod species, the Eastern Grey kangaroo (Macropus giganteus). While proteomics has been used extensively to characterize sperm, only two studies have focused on epididymal maturation, employing mouse models [12, 13]. We employ a global proteomic approach, utilizing liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) with label-free acquisition by SWATH-MS (sequential window acquisition of all theoretical mass spectra [14]) for reliable protein quantification. Our bioinformatic approach includes the use of neural networks for protein identification (DIA-NN [15]), a recent advancement in proteomic data analysis. This in depth molecular profiling will provide important information on sperm biology in macropods, potentially giving insight into whether the physical remodeling during epididymal transit is driven by protein loss or gain. In addition, as sperm become fertilization competent during post-testicular maturation, exploring changes in the proteome over this period may help to pinpoint cellular processes that are key to reproductive success.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Unless otherwise stated, all reagents were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Castle Hill, Australia).

Animals

Wild sexually mature eastern grey kangaroo males (M. giganteus, n = 6) were euthanized by a commercial shooter on private land in Leyburn, Queensland. Intact scrota were sealed in a plastic bag containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transported (2 h) at 4°C to the University of Queensland’s Gatton campus for further processing. All methods were carried out under approval from The University of Queensland’s Animal Ethics Committee (approval number 2023/AE000055) and the Queensland Department of Environment and Science (approval number WA0050140). All procedures followed the Australian code for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes, 8th edition.

Isolation and preparation of spermatozoa

Spermatozoa were isolated and prepared individually for each male (n = 6). The left and right epididymides were isolated and cut into three sections (caput, corpus, and cauda) based on gross anatomical differences. Each section was rinsed in PBS to remove excess blood, minced in 2 mL of tris–citrate buffer (300 mM Tris, 95 mM citrate, 28 mM fructose, pH 7.4) containing 0.3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at 37°C for 15 min to allow sperm to “swim out” from epididymal tissue. Following incubation, tissue was discarded and isolated sperm from the left and right epididymides were pooled within male and maintained at 37°C. Cauda epididymal samples were subjectively assessed for motility, and all samples met the minimum threshold of >60% motile (data not shown). Samples were then washed with tris–citrate buffer without BSA and centrifuged (1000 x g, 5 min). The supernatant was discarded and the sperm pellet was stored at −80°C until further processing.

Preparation of samples for mass spectrometry

Lysis buffer (100 μL; 10 mM Tris, 2% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulphate, 1× Sigma COmplete protease inhibitor, pH 8) was added to each frozen sperm pellet and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Samples were centrifuged (7500 x g, 15 min) and supernatant protein concentration was determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat # 23225). A standardized load of 20 μg total protein was prepared by filter-aided sample preparation. Spin filters with 30 kDa molecular weight cut off (MWCO; Amicon Ultra, Merck, cat # UFC5030) were washed with 8 M urea in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (UA) (13 000 x g, 15 min). Lysate was added to prepared filters (13 000 x g, 15 min) and washed twice with UA (13 000 x g,15 min). Samples were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (30 min) and then alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (30 min in the dark). Samples were washed thoroughly with 8 M urea, followed by 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (3× 13 000 x g, 15 min each).

Samples were digested with trypsin (1:50 trypsin:total protein, in 0.01% (w/v) ProteaseMax, Promega, cat # V5113, V2071) overnight at 37°C. Resulting peptides were eluted with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The flow-through was dried by vacuum centrifuge and resuspended in 0.5% (v/v) formic acid (FA). A final cleanup was performed with C18 ziptips (~5 μg binding capacity, Millipore, cat # ZTC18S). Peptides were eluted with 0.1% (v/v) FA, 80% (v/v) acetonitrile, dried by vacuum centrifuge, and resuspended in 20 μL 0.1% (v/v) FA prior to injection.

LC–MS/MS and SWATH acquisition

All biological replicates were analyzed individually by LC–MS/MS. Samples were separated using reversed-phase chromatography on a Waters M-Class ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system. Samples were loaded onto a Waters NanoEase HSS T3 column (100 A, 1.8 μm, 300 μm × 150 mm). Chromatography was performed at 5 μL/min and column set at 40°C, with LC conditions as follows: 0–0.5 min = 3% B, 0.5–30.5 min = 3%–30%, 30.5–34.5 min = 30%–60%, 34.5–36.5 min = 60%–97%, held at 97% for 4 min followed by re-equilibration for 4 min. Pump A = 0.1% (v/v) FA in water, and pump B = 0.1% (v/v) FA in acetonitrile. Eluted peptides were directly analyzed on a ZenoTof 7600 instrument (ABSciex) using an OptiFlow Micro/MicroCal source. Curtain gas = 35 psi, CAD gas = 7, Gas 1 = 20 psi, Gas 2 = 15 psi, source temp = 150°C, spray voltage = 5000 V, DP = 80, CE = 10.

For Zeno-SWATH acquisitions, time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MS TOF) scan across 400–1500 m/z was performed (0.1 s). For MS2, variable windows spanning 399.5–750.5 m/z were chosen for fragmentation (0.013 s) with fragment data acquired across 140–1750 m/z with Zeno pulsing on, with threshold set to 100 000 cps. Dynamic collision energy was used. A BSA standard was employed for quality control between runs.

Protein identification, quantification, and statistical analysis

Protein identification and quantification were performed using DIA-NN (Demichev et al. [15], v1.9.2). An in silico ion spectral library was generated using two Uniprot FASTA files for the closely related Koala (Phascolarctos cinereus) and Wombat (Vombatus unsinus) (downloaded January 2025), with default search parameters. Raw files were converted to MzML using Proteowizard and searched against the generated library, with output filtered at 0.01 FDR, cysteine carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification, match between runs enabled, heuristic protein grouping and fixed mass accuracy of 20 ppm (MS1 and MS2).

The DIA-NN output report was reformatted using the R package MSstats_labelfree_preprocessing and subsequently the R package MSstats [16] was used to perform filtering and statistical analysis of protein abundance in R Studio (v 2021.09.0). The dataProcess function was run with default settings, and processed data was statistically analyzed by pairwise comparisons employing a linear mixed model. Proteins with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and a log2 fold change >\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\left|1\right|$\end{document} were considered significantly different between groups. Manual data curation was performed for proteins which were identified in duplicate from both Koala and Wombat reference proteomes, by different proteotypic peptides. Those where both IDs were significant were counted only once and those where the ID was significant in only one species were retained as significant. In the case where one ID was significantly decreased and the other was absent, the ID was counted as significantly decreased only. Proteins were only considered as absent from an epididymal region (i.e. “exclusive” to the region being compared), if they were not detected in any biological replicate from that region. If a protein was detected in at least one biological replicate from the region, it was considered present.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and graphed in R Studio using FactoMineR, factoextra, and tidyverse. Volcano plots were generated in R Studio using ggplot to visualize pairwise comparisons. Venn diagrams were generated to visualize conservation of proteins across epididymal regions and to compare between our data and published mouse epididymal sperm proteomes [12, 13] using JVenn [17].

Gene ontology and functional protein associations

Significantly different proteins were analyzed for gene ontology (GO) through DAVID bioinformatics software (Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp, v 6.8). DAVID annotation results grouped by molecular function were considered significant if the modified Fisher Exact p-value (EASE score) was <0.05 and if the enrichment score was >3.0. GO enrichment figures were generated in R Studio using ggplot2.

LC–MS/MS using SWATH acquisition detected 4305 proteins in spermatozoa across the three sections of the epididymis (Supplementary Table S1; caput 4213, corpus 4237, cauda 4017). Quantitative comparison based on epididymal region

Despite a high degree of common proteins, spermatozoa from each epididymal section showed considerable proteomic differences (Supplementary Tables S2–S4, Figure 3). There were significant changes between caput to corpus (216) and corpus to cauda (728), with the most obvious alterations to the proteome when comparing caput and cauda sperm (1131) (Figure 4, Table 2). Many proteins appear to be lost entirely (228) or newly gained (61) during epididymal transit. More subtly, roughly equal numbers of proteins significantly increased and decreased in abundance within each section comparison, with the exception of corpus to cauda, where 1.5× fold more proteins were significantly decreased. Of note, for those proteins which were detected in only one epididymal section, 9.6% in the caput, 23.2% in the corpus, and 15.8% in the cauda were detected in only one biological replicate.

Gene ontology of significantly different proteins

All GO raw data are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Caput to corpus transition

GO analysis using DAVID did not show any significantly enriched functional clusters in proteins which increased/were gained or decreased/were lost between spermatozoa recovered from the caput and corpus epididymides.

Corpus to cauda transition

Sperm proteins which decreased or were lost from the corpus to cauda epididymis showed little functional enrichment, save for a significant cluster of proteins with glycosidase activity (enrichment score 3.8, e.g. GAA, MAN1A1, MAN2B1, MAN2B2, HEXA, HEXB). Sperm proteins which increased or were gained from corpus to cauda showed moderate significant functional enrichment for flagellar proteins (5.8, e.g. CFAP proteins, dyneins).

Overall changes from caput to cauda

Proteins which were significantly different in abundance between caput and cauda spermatozoa showed several significantly enriched functional clusters (Figure 5), including flagellar proteins (19.9), dyneins/microtubule function (5.5), acyl-CoA enzymes (3.8), TCA cycle (3.3), and cytoskeletal/motor proteins (3.1). Looking specifically at proteins which decreased or were lost from the caput to cauda epididymides, there was stronger enrichment for glycosidases (4.5), as well as a significantly enriched cluster of proteins involved in endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi transport via vesicles (3.3). In comparison, proteins which increased or were gained from caput to cauda were also strongly enriched in flagellar proteins (9.2, e.g. CFAP proteins, RSPH9), microtubule/tubulins (5.4, e.g. TEKT4, HOOK1, tubulin alpha, and beta isoforms) and dyneins (3.4, e.g. DNAH1, 2, 10, 12, 17, DRC1).

Cross-species comparison

Comparing our data with published proteomes for mouse epididymal spermatozoa revealed a moderate to high degree of protein conservation across species in caput, corpus, and cauda sperm (73.2%, 24.6%, and 48.9%, respectively, as a percentage of the kangaroo proteome, Figure 6). Examining functional clusters for proteins which were only identified in our dataset, there was evidence of enrichment for mitochondrial proteins (5.6), tubulins (4.4), and histones (3.3) in caput sperm; ATP binding (16.3) and dyneins (4.3) in corpus sperm; and ATP binding (7.4) and fatty acid metabolism (4.6) in cauda sperm (Figure 6).

Discussion

Here, we present the first proteomic profile of spermatozoa from the Eastern Grey kangaroo (M. giganteus). This is the only published sperm proteome for a macropod, and the second for a marsupial [47], representing a significant step forward in the study of molecular reproductive biology in non-model species. The use of SWATH MS acquisition allowed for a high-depth proteome to be generated, with a total of 4304 proteins identified, on par with recently published sperm proteomes in mammalian species (e.g. boar [48], bull [49]).

Our study focused on exploring proteomic changes over the landscape of epididymal development. This remodeling was biased toward loss of proteins over epididymal transit, similarly to mouse epididymal spermatozoa [12, 13]. Sperm from the proximal sections of the epididymis were more similar to each other than mature cauda spermatozoa, suggesting that the final portion of the epididymis is where most proteomic remodeling occurs. This appears to be different to mouse [13], which showed balanced changes across the three major epididymal sections. This difference may be attributed to improvements in mass spectrometry in the last decade, generating increased data depth and revealing previously unappreciated remodeling of low abundance proteins. Our results include a number of interesting findings, including a high abundance of histone proteins, a notable increase in flagellar proteins, suggestions of a unique macropod sperm metabolic preference and a loss of glycosidases over epididymal transit.

When examining the Eastern Grey kangaroo sperm proteome across the caput, corpus, and cauda epididymis, histone proteins were particularly abundant. Interestingly, our cross-species comparison also highlighted that histones as a functional cluster were enriched in proteins identified in kangaroo but not mouse caput sperm. Our workflow identified H2A, H2B, and H3 histones in kangaroo sperm, which are typically constituents of DNA packaging nucleosomes [50]. These histones are replaced by protamines during spermatogenesis in eutherians, with as little as 1%–15% of histones retained following this transition, depending on the species [51]. This histone-to-protamine transition appears to be significantly different in marsupial sperm, continuing over the process of epididymal maturation and resulting in up to 25% of histones being retained in cauda epididymal sperm [52]. Histone retention appears to be localized, creating two structurally different nuclear zones with differences in chromatin condensation [53]. However, this work on histone retention was completed in only one species; a dasyurid marsupial, Sminthopsis crassicaudata. Given the high abundance of histone proteins in our results, we speculate that a similar process may occur in macropods and other marsupials more widely. This observation is further supported by related findings that sperm from marsupials, including the macropod tammar wallaby (Macropus eugenii), apparently have little chromatin stability when compared to eutherian spermatozoa [54]. This lack of chromatin stability has been proposed to increase the susceptibility of marsupial spermatozoa (including that of M. giganteus) to DNA damage, as shown under conditions of oxidative stress [55]. While further work is required to fully elucidate sperm chromatin packaging in macropods, this is clearly an important area in the context of applied reproduction. Modifying sperm handling and cryopreservation protocols to account for reduced nuclear stability and increased susceptibility to DNA damage could lead to significant improvements in the historically poor ART outcomes in macropods.

Combining label free, SWATH based protein quantification and GO, we also saw significant enrichment of flagellar proteins, tubulins, and dyneins in proteins which were gained during epididymal transit. Spermatozoa are thought to be transcriptionally quiescent following spermatogenesis, though there is ongoing speculation of de novo post-testicular transcription [56]. However, there have been no studies of transcriptional silencing in marsupial sperm. Given that tight chromatin packaging with protamines drives transcriptional silence in mature eutherian sperm [50] and the differences in histone-to-protamine transition described above, the prospect of de novo transcription and translation in mature marsupial sperm is an interesting one. While this is a potential explanation underlying our observed increase in flagellar proteins, a more widely investigated explanation is the contribution of proteins by epididymal extracellular vesicles (EVs) (previously referred to as “epididymosomes”).

Studies in mice and humans have demonstrated the transfer of proteins from somatic epididymal tissue to sperm via EVs [57, 58], and presumably a similar process operates in the marsupial epididymis. Interestingly, Nixon et al. [57] showed more difference in epididymal EVs from the corpus and cauda compared to the caput, mirroring our results. Looking specifically at motility related proteins, there are some similarities to published work, with increases in flagellar proteins (TEKT3, CFAP91, TUBB4A) in mouse cauda epididymal sperm [12] and dynein DYNC2H1 in cauda epididymal EVs [57]. However, it is unclear whether the particular enrichment for flagellar proteins in those increasing over epididymal transit we observed is a peculiarity of marsupial spermatozoa, or simply not previously been captured in studies of eutherian spermatozoa.

Our data provided potential evidence of a macropod sperm metabolic preference. We observed significant increases in several acyl-CoA dehydrogenases in spermatozoa in the distal epididymis (e.g. ACAD9, ACAD11, ACADM, ACADSB, ACADVL), as well as an enrichment for acyl-CoA activity in proteins found in kangaroo but not mouse cauda epididymal sperm. These enzymes catalyze the initial step of β-oxidation of fatty acids to produce ATP. Spermatozoa largely use ATP generated via glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to power motility, with the predominant energy generation pathway being species specific [59]. β-oxidation of lipids offers an alternative energy source; while this is responsible for only a small amount of ATP production in most mammalian species, a notable exception are the toothed whales, in which sperm motility is primarily driven by lipid metabolism [60]. Marsupial spermatozoa have a higher usage of endogenous metabolic substrates than eutherian sperm [61], allowing undiluted cauda epididymal sperm to remain motile for at least 12 h post collection [8]. While marsupial sperm can employ glycolysis [62], there has been no investigation into β-oxidation of lipids as a major energy source in these species. While eutherian sperm metabolism has been shown to increase over the course of epididymal transit [63], similar studies of changes in marsupial spermatozoa are yet to be performed. Given the importance of supporting sperm metabolism during in vitro handling, there is clearly further need to investigate whether β-oxidation of lipids represents a major metabolic pathway in marsupial sperm. As there is evidence that targeting the major metabolic pathway yields the best sperm performance [64], a transition to a macropod sperm medium containing fatty acids as an energy source rather than simple sugars may be warranted.

We also found that glycosidases, the enzymes responsible for cleaving sugars, significantly decreased from the proximal to distal kangaroo epididymis. Sugars on the sperm surface of both eutherian and marsupial sperm undergo substantial remodeling during epididymal transit [65–67], driven in part by the action of glycosidases. The activity of various glycosidases have been profiled in eutherians and appear to typically peak in the distal epididymis [68], though there have been no studies of glycosidase activity in marsupial spermatozoa during epididymal maturation. Our finding that glycosidases decreased in abundance is supported by Skerrett-Byrne et al. [12], showing a significant decrease in the fucosidase FUCA1 in cauda compared to caput mouse sperm. Interestingly, FUCA1, along with GLB1L3, HEXB, MANBA, MAN2BA, and TREH, significantly increased in EVs from the distal epididymis [57]. Given that EVs likely also remove proteins from sperm [69], there is some support for the idea that while the activity of glycosidases increases, their abundance decreases over epididymal transit, potentially driven by removal via EVs. Given the importance of the sperm glycocalyx in navigating the female reproductive tract and achieving fertilization [65], further investigation of glycan enzymatic remodeling over marsupial epididymal transit is warranted.

There are several important limitations of our investigation worth noting. While proteins which were not detected in any biological replicates of a given epididymal section were classified as “lost,” this may instead reflect a protein abundance below the limit of MS detection. Conversely, between 9% and 23% of proteins identified as being unique to a region were identified in only one biological replicate, suggesting that some of these proteins may be very lowly abundant rather than absent. In addition, there is no published reference genome or proteome for M. giganteus. We were able to generate an in silico spectral library based on koala (P. cinereus) and wombat (Vombatus ursinus) reference proteomes. While these species are also in the Diprotodontia order of marsupials, they belong to different suborders (Vombatiformes vs Macropodiformes), which diverged approximately 52 million years ago (TimeTree [70]). This evolutionary distance limits the identification of macropod specific proteins which emerged following divergence and may lead to poor identification of proteins with low sequence homology across species. Finally, while a cross-species comparison to published mouse epididymal sperm proteomes offers interesting insights into potential species differences, there are significant limitations in comparing sperm proteomic data across studies [71]. One is the difference in proteomic depth, which is highlighted in our results; given the low depth of the mouse corpus sperm proteome [13] (1720 proteins), the low conservation compared to the kangaroo corpus sperm proteome (24.6%) is unsurprising. While this limitation means that our cross-species results are likely only modestly accurate, this is still a useful approach in the space of discovery proteomics, which builds the groundwork for further empirical research.

Our findings regarding chromatin packaging, gain of flagellar proteins, loss of glycosidases, and potential preference for lipid metabolism have important implications for assisted reproduction. These physiological changes over the course of epididymal maturation may make sperm more susceptible to damage during in vitro processing and cryopreservation, warranting further investigation of potential protective solutions. In addition, metabolic preference for β-oxidation may require a shift from carbohydrate to fatty acid-based handling and cryopreservation media. This study has applied cutting edge molecular techniques to investigate sperm physiology in a marsupial. Such an approach is sorely needed to advance our understanding of fundamental reproductive sperm physiology in non-model species, driving further targeted work with applied outcomes for artificial breeding and conservation more broadly.
