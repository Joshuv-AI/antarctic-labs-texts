Oxycodone self-administration and genetic background exert community-specific effects in the gut microbiome
Authors: Eamonn P. Duffy, John D. Sterrett, Luanne H. Hale, Jonathan O. Ward, Laura M. Saba, Daniel N. Frank, Ryan K. Bachtell, Marissa A. Ehringer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiome and genetic factors contribute to several opioid-related phenotypes in humans and animal models. Here, we examined the effects of genetic background and oxycodone self-administration on the gut microbiome in two genetically divergent rat strains. ACI/EurMcwi and M520/N rats were trained to self-administer oxycodone or saline. Males and females from both strains acquired and escalated oxycodone self-administration, with M520/N rats administering more oxycodone than ACI/EurMcwi rats. Saline self-administration was influenced by a strain-sex interaction, with M520/N females consuming more than any other saline-administering animals. Following the self-administration period, fecal and cecal samples were collected to examine the effects of genetic background, sex, and oxycodone on the gut microbiome. Measures of alpha diversity in the fecal and cecal microbiomes were influenced by strain and sex, but not oxycodone. Beta diversity analyses demonstrated that strain and oxycodone alter the structure of the fecal and cecal microbiomes, while sex had more limited effects. Both oxycodone and strain exerted community-specific effects on gut microbiome composition, with 15 taxa differing between self-administration groups and 51 taxa differing between strains. These findings demonstrate that genetic background influences self-administration phenotypes, and both host genetics and oxycodone influence the diversity and composition of the gut microbiome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41666-1.

Introduction

A growing body of research has described the bidirectional relationship between substance use disorders (SUDs) and the gut microbiome1. Opioid consumption is highly prevalent, and approximately 12% of adults in the United States have used prescription opioids in the past year2. Long-term opioid use confers heightened risk for several adverse outcomes, including opioid use disorder (OUD) and gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction3–6. Disruptions to the gut microbiome alter behavioral responses to opioids, reducing morphine-associated reward and enhancing fentanyl self-administration7,8. Conversely, findings describing the effects of opioid use on the microbiome have been more inconsistent, as reviewed in9. These disparities between studies may arise from a number of factors, such as differences in the gut microbial communities being studied, drug administration protocols, and host genetic differences.

The gut microbiota is composed of several distinct microbial communities throughout the GI tract10,11. Within the GI tract, variability in intestinal physiology and biochemical content leads to unique ecological niches which drive the growth of microbial communities with distinct compositions and functions10,12,13. The fecal and cecal microbiota communities, in particular, have been extensively studied in rodent models14. The composition of these communities can be influenced by a number of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including host genetics and drug use9. Although environmental factors, such as diet and stress, induce community-specific effects in the gut microbiome, the community-specific effects of substance use remain understudied13,15,16. For example, cocaine self-administration can exert similar effects on specific taxa within the fecal and cecal microbiomes17. However, there is a general dearth of research on whether individual communities in the gut microbiome are especially vulnerable to drug-induced alterations.

A limited number of studies in humans have characterized the relationship between the gut microbiome and opioid use in a clinical setting. In substitution therapy, the opioid agonist methadone is frequently used to attenuate or prevent withdrawal symptoms in individuals with OUD18. Individuals undergoing methadone treatment display lower fecal alpha diversity compared to individuals who do not use opioids19. In patients with chronic health conditions, such as cirrhosis or type 2 diabetes (T2D), medical use of prescribed opioids also alters fecal microbiome composition compared with matched controls20,21. Together, these findings demonstrate that chronic opioid use is associated with large, potentially deleterious effects on the gut microbiome. Animal models offer greater control over certain environmental variables (e.g., polysubstance use, diet, and health status), but findings have been inconsistent on how opioid self-administration affects the gut microbiome. For example, measures of alpha diversity can be impacted by opioid self-administration, but the effects depend on the exact opioid being studied, self-administration paradigm, and rat strain, as reviewed by9.

Genetic background also plays a major role in shaping the diversity and composition of the gut microbiome22. Genetic mapping studies in humans and mice have identified specific quantitative trait loci (QTLs), genes, and gene ontology pathways that contribute to the abundance of specific bacterial taxa23–28. For example, genetic loci and gene ontology pathways related to immune signaling have consistently been associated with the abundance of specific taxa within the fecal microbiome27,29. Some of the genes identified in microbiome GWAS have also been associated with risk for psychiatric disorders, suggesting that gene-microbiome interactions are important for mediating complex behaviors22,30. With recent technological advancements and improvements to the rat reference genome, there has been a growing interest in forward genetic studies in rats31. Before performing these genetic mapping studies, however, it is essential to demonstrate that isogenic strains vary in a phenotype of interest32.

Using genetically divergent, inbred strains from the Hybrid Rat Diversity Panel (HRDP), we found that oxycodone-induced analgesia and oxycodone self-administration phenotypes depend on genetic background and sex33,34. For this study, we selected the ACI/EurMcwi and M520/N strains that consume relatively high, consistent levels of oxycodone to examine how genetic background, sex, and oxycodone self-administration influence the gut microbiome. We analyzed both fecal and cecal microbiome samples to examine how genetic background and oxycodone self-administration exert community-specific effects in the gut microbiome.

Methods

Animal subjects

Adult (PND 60+) male and female rats (n = 76) from the ACI/EurMcwi (RRID: RRRC_00284) and M520/N (RRID: RRRC_00168) strains were used for these experiments. Strains and sample sizes are described in Table 1. Sample sizes were determined using published guidelines for microbiome research35–37. All rats were obtained in multiple cohorts from the Medical College of Wisconsin (provided by Dr. Melinda Dwinell, R24OD024617). Upon arrival, rats were housed in quarantine conditions for approximately 4 weeks then allowed to habituate in standard housing for at least one week before testing. Rats were single-housed in a temperature (22 °C) and humidity (40%) controlled animal vivarium on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Rats were provided with standard rat chow (Teklad 2918, Envigo, Indianapolis, IN) and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines outlined by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Colorado Boulder, an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International accredited institution. .

Baseline somatosensory & analgesia tests

Following the quarantine and habituation period, animals underwent the von Frey and tail immersion tests to measure somatosensory sensitivity and oxycodone-induced analgesia, as described previously34. During the tail immersion test,

animals from both intravenous self-administration (IVSA) groups were administered oxycodone (1 mg/kg, i.p.).

Surgery

Chronic indwelling intra-jugular catheters were implanted as described previously33,38. Briefly, animals were anesthetized (2–4% isoflurane), and intravenous (i.v.) catheters were implanted in the right jugular vein under aseptic conditions. Immediately prior to surgery, animals were administered carprofen (5 mg/kg, s.c.) and enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg, s.c.). Animals received daily subcutaneous injections of carprofen (5 mg/kg) for two days following surgery. Catheters were flushed daily with 0.2 mL heparinized (20 IU/ml) bacteriostatic saline containing gentamicin sulfate (0.33 mg/ml; Hospira). Prior to the first self-administration session, animals were randomized in matched pairs to oxycodone (“OXY”) or saline (“SAL”) IVSA groups based on behavioral responses during the tail immersion test. Experimenters could not blinded to rat strain due to differences in coat colors, nor sex due to differences in body weight, nor IVSA group due to the behavioral effects of oxycodone. Data analysis was conducted in an unbiased manner, as described below. Catheter patency was evaluated for all animals using 0.2 mL propofol (10 mg/mL propofol, Sagent) solution before the acquisition phase of oxycodone self-administration, before the escalation phase, and after the final session of the escalation phase.

Self-administration

Self-administration procedures were conducted as previously described33 in operant chambers (Med Associates, St. Albans, VT) containing two retractable levers (an active, infusion-paired left lever and an inactive right lever), stimulus cue lights above each lever, and a sound-attenuating fan. A two-phase self-administration paradigm was used to measure the acquisition and escalation of oxycodone intake (Fig. 1). All self-administration sessions began at the start of the dark, active circadian phase of the light/dark cycle. Body weights were collected immediately before each self-administration session to calculate the oxycodone unit dose and monitor overall health. During the acquisition phase, rats were trained to self-administer oxycodone (Oxycodone HCl; B&B pharmaceutical, Englewood, CO) or saline (0.9%) on a fixed-ratio 1 (FR1) reinforcement schedule in ten 2-hour “short-access” (ShA) sessions. Responses on the active lever resulted in an oxycodone (0.15 mg/kg/infusion) or saline (0.15 mL/infusion) infusion delivered over 5-s and a cue light (7.5-W white light, 20-s). Each infusion was followed by a 20-s timeout period (TO20). Responses on the inactive lever were recorded but had no consequences. Rats were then transitioned to the escalation phase that included ten 12-hour “long-access” (LgA) sessions under the same FR1:TO20 schedule of reinforcement.

Somatic withdrawal tests

Following the last self-administration session, animals underwent another set of von Frey and tail immersion tests to measure withdrawal-induced hypersensitivity and oxycodone tolerance. Briefly, animals underwent the von Frey test the day after the last self-administration session, and the tail immersion test two days after the last session. During the tail immersion test, animals from both IVSA groups were administered oxycodone (1 mg/kg, i.p.). Results from these tests will be presented in future manuscripts by our research group.

Fecal and cecal sample collection

Fecal samples were collected prior to the last tail immersion test, approximately 36 h after the last self-administration session. Each rat was individually placed in a sterile chamber cleaned with 70% ethanol, and at least two fecal pellets were collected per animal within a 1-hour window. Samples were collected in sterile RNAse/DNAse-free microcentrifuge tubes, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C. Approximately 60 h after the last self-administration session animals were euthanized by rapid decapitation. The cecal contents were collected, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C. Frozen samples were transported to Dr. Daniel Frank’s laboratory at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus for high-throughput DNA sequencing.

High-throughput DNA sequencing for microbiome analysis

Bacterial profiles were determined by broad-range amplification and sequence analysis of 16S rRNA genes in two sequencing runs following our previously described methods39,40. In brief, amplicons were generated using primers that target the V3V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene: primers 357 F (5’-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). PCR products were normalized using a SequalPrepTM kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), pooled, lyophilized, purified and concentrated using a DNA Clean and Concentrator Kit (Zymo, Irvine, CA). Illumina paired-end sequencing of the amplicon pool was performed on the MiSeq platform using MiSeq Control Software (v2.4) and of MiSeq Reporter (v2.4; MiSeq software available at https://support.illumina.com/sequencing/sequencing_instruments/miseq/downloads.html), using a 600-cycle version 3 reagent kit. This resulted in sequences in a 2 × 300 paired-end format. Paired end sequence data were deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive (BioProject accession number PRJNA1216623).

Microbiome sequencing data was processed using QIIME2 v2023.5.141. Sequences were joined and denoised using DADA242 with a trim length of 20 bp, 290 bp truncation length for forward reads, and 211 bp truncation length for reverse reads. Supplementary Table 1 presents additional details about read counts before and after processing with DADA2. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using SATé-enabled phylogenetic placement (SEPP) with the SILVA 128 reference tree43,44. Taxonomy was assigned to reads using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA 138.1 database44. To maintain consistency, all taxonomic names in this manuscript are the ones used in the SILVA database. A total of 125 samples were sequenced (average sample depth: 35,062 reads; minimum depth: 10,380 reads; maximum sample depth: 72,427 reads). One sample was discarded due to low sampling depth, and the remaining samples were rarified without replacement to 10,300 reads per sample to account for unequal sequencing depth45.

Outcome measures

Self-administration

The average hourly intake was calculated by dividing the number of infusions within a session by the duration of that session (hr). This measure was used to normalize intake across the acquisition (ShA; 2-hr) and escalation (LgA; 12-hr) sessions and depict the overall pattern of self-administration across both phases of self-administration. The broadest measure of self-administration behaviors was total infusions. This was calculated by adding the cumulative infusions received throughout acquisition and escalation. The total oxycodone dose was calculated by multiplying the total infusions received by the unit dose (0.15 mg/kg) per infusion.

Body weight

Body weights were tracked daily throughout the self-administration protocol to calculate oxycodone unit doses and track overall health. The body weight prior to each self-administration session was used to describe overall patterns of weight fluctuation throughout the protocol. To evaluate body weight changes during the acquisition and escalation phases, a difference score was calculated by subtracting session 10 body weight from session 1 body weight within each phase.

Alpha diversity

Several measures of alpha diversity were calculated using the core-metrics-phylogenetic function in QIIME2. We evaluated the number of observed ASVs, Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD), Pielou’s evenness, and the Shannon Index. Analyses were conducted separately for fecal and cecal samples.

Beta diversity

Beta diversity was described using unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances46. Distances and ordinations were calculated using phyloseq v1.46.047. Analyses were conducted separately for fecal and cecal samples.

Relative abundance

To describe differences in microbiome composition, the mean relative abundance was calculated within strain and IVSA groups. Stacked barplots were generated using the microshades R package v1.1148. Separate barplots were generated for fecal and cecal samples.

Differential abundance

ANCOM-BC2 from the ancombc R package v2.4.049 was used to identify bacterial taxa at the phylum and genus levels that were differentially abundant between strains, sexes, and IVSA groups. Exploratory analyses (data not shown) indicated that no taxa were differentially abundant between sexes, so subsequent tests collapsed across sex. Analyses were conducted separately for fecal and cecal samples. ANCOM-BC2 includes an additional, more conservative sensitivity analysis to account for the effects of pseudo-counts on the log-transformed taxa counts49. Results from the sensitivity analysis are included in Supplementary Tables S3-6.

Co-occurrence networks

SCNIC (q2-SCNIC v2018.8.0) was used to detect modules of co-occurring ASVs50. Samples were filtered to remove features with an average abundance of less than 2 across all samples50,51. A minimum R-value of 0.35 was used to determine that a correlation between features was significant. The module feature table was imported into R, and ANCOM-BC2 was used to detect modules that were differentially abundant between strains and IVSA groups. Analyses were conducted separately for fecal and cecal samples.

Statistical analysis

All phenotypic data are presented as mean ± SEM. Longitudinal data for hourly intake and body weight were analyzed using a repeated measures ANOVA with session as the within-subject factor and strain, sex, and IVSA group as the between-subject factors. Two-way ANOVAs were run to test the effects of strain, sex, and their interaction for the total infusions of oxycodone or saline. Three-way ANOVAs were run to test the effects of strain, sex, IVSA group, and their interactions for change in body weight and alpha diversity measures. If an interaction term was significant, Tukey’s HSD test was performed. For beta diversity measures, statistical differences for strain, sex, and IVSA group were calculated through pairwise permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations using the adonis2 function from the vegan package v2.6-452. ANCOM-BC2 was used to identify phyla, genera, and modules of ASVs that were differentially abundant between strains and IVSA groups, and an FDR correction was applied to account for multiple testing. For differential abundance analyses, statistical significance was set as q < 0.05. For self-administration and body weight data, outliers were identified and winsorized, and missing data (< 2% of session data) was imputed prior to statistical analysis as described previously33. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05. All statistical analysis was conducted in R v4.3.2.

Results

Strain differences in self-administration phenotypes

Male and female rats from two genetically divergent, inbred strains underwent a behavioral protocol designed to measure the acquisition and escalation of i.v. oxycodone self-administration (Fig. 1). The average hourly intake (Fig. 2a) was calculated to describe and compare the general pattern of oxycodone or saline consumption during the acquisition and escalation phases.

We observed that hourly intake was influenced by sex-session (F8.08,541.53 =.4.46, p < 0.001) and strain-IVSA group-session (F8.08,541.53 = 3.49, p < 0.001) interactions. In general, males and females within the same strain displayed similar patterns of oxycodone intake, and both strains increased oxycodone intake throughout the procedure. Saline-administering animals typically responded at lower levels than their oxycodone-administering counterparts, although M520/N females showed enhanced responding for saline at the start of the escalation period. To simplify these comparisons, we calculated the total infusions for oxycodone-administering (Fig. 2b) and saline-administering (Fig. 2c) animals, as well as the total oxycodone dose (Fig. 2b). In oxycodone-administering animals, the total infusions and total oxycodone dose were influenced by a main effect of strain (F1,39 = 36.27, p < 0.001), with M520/N rats administering more oxycodone than ACI/EurMcwi rats. In saline-administering animals, the number of total infusions was influenced by a strain-sex interaction (F1,28 = 4.72, p = 0.04), with M520/N females administering more saline than any other animals (M520/N female & ACI/EurMcwi male, p = 0.002; M520/N female & ACI/EurMcwi female, p = 0.002; M520/N female & M520/N male, p = 0.047).

Body weight throughout the self-administration protocol (Fig. 3a) was influenced by a sex-IVSA group-session interaction (F4.67, 317.81 = 13.37, p < 0.001) and a strain-IVSA group-session interaction (F4.67, 317.81 = 4.05, p = 0.002). In general, oxycodone-administering animals in both strains lost more weight than saline-administering counterparts, with oxycodone-administering females losing less weight than oxycodone-administering males during the escalation phase.

Unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances were used to describe beta-diversity.

Fecal microbiome alpha diversity

The number of observed ASVs (Fig. 4a) was influenced by a strain-sex interaction (F1,57 = 7.23, p = 0.009). M520/N males displayed lower number of ASVs compared to ACI/EurMcwi males (p = 0.001), ACI/EurMcwi females (p < 0.001) and M520/N females (p = 0.006). We did not observe a main effect of IVSA group (F1,57 = 0.97, p = 0.33) nor its interactions on the number of observed ASVs. Faith’s PD (Fig. 4b) was influenced by a strain-sex interaction (F1,57 = 5.04, p = 0.03). M520/N males displayed lower phylogenetic diversity than ACI/EurMcwi males (p = 0.002), ACI/EurMcwi females (p < 0.001) and M520/N females (p = 0.01). We did not observe a main effect of IVSA group (F1,57 = 0.62, p = 0.43) nor its interactions on Faith’s PD. No strain, sex, nor IVSA group differences were observed in Pielou’s evenness or the Shannon Index (Supplementary Figure S1).

Beta diversity

We analyzed beta diversity using PERMANOVAs performed on unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance matrices. Unweighted UniFrac distances use the presence or absence of ASVs to describe a community, while weighted UniFrac distances also account for the abundance and phylogenetic placement of each ASV53. Unweighted UniFrac distances in fecal samples (Fig. 5a) showed a main effect of IVSA group (R2 = 0.026, pseudo-F1,57 = 1.88, p = 0.001) and a strain-sex interaction (R2 = 0.019, pseudo-F1,57 = 1.36, p = 0.04). Weighted.

UniFrac distances in fecal samples (Fig. 5b) showed strain-IVSA group interaction (R2 = 0.036, pseudo-F1,57 = 2.25, p = 0.02). Unweighted UniFrac distances in cecal samples (Fig. 5c) showed main effects of strain (R2 = 0.093, pseudo-F1,51 = 5.97, p = 0.001) and IVSA group (R2 = 0.023, pseudo-F1,51 = 1.47, p = 0.02). Weighted UniFrac distances in cecal samples (Fig. 5d) showed a strain-IVSA group interaction (R2 = 0.023, pseudo-F1,51 = 1.72, p = 0.04). Interestingly, there was greater separation between strains in the unweighted UniFrac metric. This may indicate that strains differ in the presence of lower abundance taxa but show overall similarity in the “core” gut microbiome.

Genetic background and oxycodone influence gut microbiome composition

To describe the composition of the fecal and cecal microbiome, the mean relative abundances of bacterial taxa were plotted within strains and IVSA groups (Fig. 6). ANCOM-BC2 was used to examine the differential abundance of bacterial taxa at the phylum and genus taxonomic levels. An exploratory analysis (data not shown) was performed testing the effects of IVSA group, strain, and sex on bacteria at the phylum and genus taxonomic levels. Discussion

In this study we found that the composition and diversity of the fecal and cecal microbiomes are influenced by genetic background, sex, and oxycodone self-administration. Measures of alpha diversity in the fecal and cecal microbiome were influenced by strain and sex, indicating that genetic background plays a major role in the richness and phylogenetic diversity present in the gut microbiome. Measures of beta diversity were consistently influenced by strain and IVSA group, suggesting that these factors play a major role in defining the composition of the fecal and cecal microbiomes. We also identified 15 taxa that differed between IVSA groups and 51 taxa that differed between strains. We also found that genetic background contributes to oxycodone self-administration, as described previously in33, while the interaction between genetic background and sex influences saline self-administration. Together, these results demonstrate the need to account for genetic background when studying the effects of substance use on the gut microbiome.

We observed that genetic background plays a major role in the diversity and composition of the gut microbiome. Measures of richness (Observed ASVs) and phylogenetic diversity (Faith’s PD) were influenced by both strain and sex. When analyzing the differential abundance of bacterial taxa, we observed a large number of genera differed between M520/N and ACI/EurMcwi strains. Because these strains were obtained from the same vendor and lived in the same colony room, these effects are likely due to genetic differences between the M520/N and ACI/EurMcwi strains. Previous research in humans and rodents demonstrated that genetic background plays a major role in controlling gut microbiome composition and mediating host-microbiome interactions, as reviewed by9. The collaborative cross (CC) is a panel of recombinant inbred mouse strains frequently used for the genetic mapping of complex phenotypes55. CC mouse strains vary in fecal and cecal microbiome composition, and several quantitative trait loci (QTLs) have been identified that are associated with the abundance of specific taxa23,27,56,57. For example, multiple QTLs are associated with Ruminococcaceae abundance in CC mice23,57. In our model, M520/N rats displayed higher levels of Ruminococcaceae than ACI/EurMcwi rats in both the fecal and cecal microbiomes. Future genetic mapping studies in rats may determine whether similar QTLs or homologous candidate genes regulate the abundance of these taxa. The CC model has also demonstrated that gut microbiome composition influences the effects of host genetics on complex traits such as anxiety-like behaviors and sleep architecture23,58. We expect to observe a similar relationship in our model, in which genetically derived differences in the gut microbiome influence oxycodone self-administration and oxycodone-induced alterations to the microbiome.

Oxycodone induced shifts in beta diversity and microbiome composition in fecal and cecal samples across both strains. We identified 15 taxa that were differentially abundant between oxycodone and saline IVSA groups. Oxycodone self-administration decreased the abundance of the phylum Patescibacteria in the cecal microbiome. Interestingly, long-access heroin self-administration increases the abundance of Patescibacteria in the fecal microbiome of outbred heterogenous stock (HS) rats59. This phylum of ultrasmall bacteria, also referred to as the Candidate Phyla Radiation (CPR), is widely distributed throughout environmental and mammalian microbiomes and can act as parasites or symbionts for other bacteria60. We also found that oxycodone had no effects on alpha diversity, consistent with previous fentanyl and heroin self-administration studies8,59. However, the effects of opioids on alpha diversity may depend on the specific class of opioid, dose, and sex. For example, in outbred Sprague-Dawley rats, only males display a dose-dependent increase in alpha diversity following fentanyl self-administration61. Future research should address whether oxycodone influences the gut microbiome in a strain-specific manner, and whether pre-existing differences in the gut microbiome mediate risk for acquiring and escalating oxycodone intake.

Physiological side effects of chronic opioid use likely contribute to oxycodone-induced shifts in gut microbiome community structure and composition. For example, oxycodone may indirectly influence gut microbiome composition by influencing feeding behavior, hydration, and body weight. Congruent with previous findings, we observed that oxycodone-administering male and female rats lost weight during the escalation phase of the study62–64. Caloric restriction and weight loss influence several of the same taxa as oxycodone or heroin self-administration59,65–67. In drug-naïve Fisher 344 rats, caloric restriction increases the abundance of Prevotella and Muribaculaceae (previously known as S24-7), taxa that metabolize complex carbohydrates and produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)65,67–70. We observed that oxycodone-administering rats displayed an increased abundance of Prevotella in both the fecal and cecal microbiomes, and co-occurrence network analysis identified that oxycodone self-administration altered several modules containing ASVs from the Muribaculaceae family. The abundance of Muribaculaceae is also influenced by heroin self-administration, although the directionality depends on the specific ASV59. Together, these results suggest that some of the effects of oxycodone on the gut microbiome may be mediated through alterations in body weight and feeding.

Changes in GI transit time are one of the most reported side effects of opioid use in humans and rodents, with individuals frequently experiencing constipation during opioid use and diarrhea during withdrawal4,9,71. Opioids reduce GI motility by suppressing the excitability of enteric neurons, decreasing the activity of smooth muscles in the intestines72. In humans, longer GI transit time is associated with increased alpha diversity and increased abundance of several bacterial taxa73,74. This increase in bacterial growth may occur due to reduced selective pressure against slow-growing taxa and increased fermentation of complex carbohydrates73,75. Although we did not quantify intestinal function or GI transit time in the current study, this factor likely plays a role in the compositional differences observed between oxycodone- and saline-administering animals.

We examined the effects of strain, sex, and oxycodone self-administration in both the fecal and cecal microbiomes. When analyzing alpha and beta diversity, we observed similar effects in both communities, suggesting that large-scale effects on community richness and structure are similar between the fecal and cecal microbiomes. However, differential abundance analysis revealed that the specific taxa that are impacted by oxycodone or genetic background are highly community specific. Similar patterns have been observed in studies on stress and diet, where the interventions cause compositional changes that are highly community-specific15,16. These findings accentuate the need to investigate the impact of opioids throughout the whole gut microbiome, rather than only the fecal or cecal microbiomes.

Previous work from our research group demonstrated that genetic background and sex contribute to several oxycodone self-administration phenotypes in the HRDP33. We found that strain and sex also influence saline self-administration, corroborating previous work in mouse models that saline self-administration is highly heritable (H2 = 0.62)76,77. Our saline IVSA group experienced similar conditions as the light sensory reinforcement (LSR) or operant novelty seeking (ONS) paradigms, in which the active lever controls a visual or auditory sensory stimulus78,79. These paradigms are often used to measure aspects of sensation seeking, which has been associated with the initiation of drug use in humans and rodent models79–81. Future studies from our group will elucidate shared behavioral and genetic factors between saline and oxycodone self-administration.

Together, our results show that genetic background, sex, and oxycodone self-administration shape both the diversity and composition of the gut microbiome. Strain and sex influenced microbial richness, while strain and IVSA group defined community structure, with numerous taxa differing across these factors. Importantly, genetic background also contributed to oxycodone intake and interacted with sex in saline self-administration. These findings underscore the critical need to account for genetic background when studying the effects of substance use on the gut microbiome. Understanding how the interactions between host genetics and oxycodone use impact the microbiome will be critical for understanding the mechanisms that contribute to opioid use disorder and guide the development of novel treatment options.
