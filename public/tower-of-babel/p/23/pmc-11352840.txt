The TaWAK2-TaNAL1-TaDST pathway regulates leaf width via cytokinin signaling in wheat
Authors: Dejie Du, Zhaoju Li, Jun Yuan, Fei He, Xiongtao Li, Naijiao Wang, Renhan Li, Wensheng Ke, Dongxue Zhang, Zhaoyan Chen, Zihao Jiang, Yunjie Liu, Lingling Chai, Jie Liu, Zhaorong Hu, Weilong Guo, Huiru Peng, Yingyin Yao, Qixin Sun, Zhongfu Ni, Mingming Xin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Leaves play a crucial role in photosynthesis and respiration, ultimately affecting the final grain yield of crops, including wheat (Triticum aestivum L.); however, the molecular mechanisms underlying wheat leaf development remain largely unknown. Here, we isolated a narrow-leaf gene, TaWAK2-A, through a map-based cloning strategy. TaWAK2-A encodes a wall-associated kinase (WAK), for which a single Ala-to-Val amino acid substitution reduces the protein stability, leading to a narrow-leaf phenotype in wheat. Further investigation suggests that TaWAK2 directly interacts with and phosphorylates TaNAL1, a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease. The phosphorylated TaNAL1 is then involved in the degradation of the zinc finger transcription factor TaDST, which acts as a repressor of leaf expansion by activating the expression of the cytokinin oxidase gene TaCKX9 and triggering in vivo cytokinin degradation. Therefore, our findings elucidate a signaling cascade involving TaWAK2-TaNAL1-TaDST that sheds light on the regulation of wheat leaf development.

INTRODUCTION

The leaf is a crucial component of plant architecture that forms from leaf primordia located at the flank of the shoot apical meristem (1, 2). Leaf morphology and size are important agronomic traits that affect photosynthesis and respiration (3, 4), with optimal leaf shape and size being essential characteristics for efficient absorption of sunlight energy and its conversion into grain yield in important crops such as wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) (5). Understanding the molecular basis underlying the regulation of leaf size will therefore contribute to the improvement of wheat plant architecture, biomass formation, and ultimately grain production.

Leaf width is determined by cell division and cell expansion (5). Extensive studies have identified several genes involved in the regulation of leaf width in rice (Oryza sativa L.), including NARROW LEAF1 (OsNAL1), OsNAL2, OsNAL3, OsNAL7, OsNAL9, OsNAL21, SHALLOT-LIKE1 (OsSLL1), ABNORMAL VASCULAR BUNDLES (OsAVB), and WIDE LEAF1 (OsWL1) (6–14). OsNAL1 encodes a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease that influences polar auxin transport, thereby controlling lateral leaf growth. Mutations in this gene result in narrow leaves and reduced longitudinal veins (6, 15). OsNAL2 and OsNAL3 are paralogs that encode the OsWOX3A transcription activator, homologous to the PRESSED FLOWER protein in Arabidopsis thaliana. The osnal2/osnal3 double mutants exhibit narrow-curly leaves, increase tillers, and narrow grains (7). The loss of OsNAL21, which encodes a ribosomal small subunit protein RPS3A, leads to notable reductions in leaf width and length as well as plant height. Ribosome-targeting antibiotics resistance assay suggests that OsNAL21 primarily controls leaf development by altering the translation efficiency of AUXIN RESPONSE FACTORs (OsARFs) and OsWOX3A (OsNAL2/OsNAL3) genes (11). Rice OsWL1 encodes a Cys2/His2-type zinc finger protein and acts as a negative regulator of leaf width, with mutations in this gene causing wider leaves than that of wild type (WT) by enhancing the number and spacing of vascular bundles. The OsWL1 protein negatively regulates leaf development by repressing the expression of OsNAL1, basically through recruiting the co-repressor TOPLESS-RELATED PROTEIN (OsTPR) (14).

The wall-associated kinases (WAKs) have been extensively studied in Arabidopsis. These proteins have extracellular domains that tightly bind to pectin and the cytoplasmic protein kinase domains (KDs) (16, 17) and are involved in both pathogen responses and cell expansion (18, 19). In Arabidopsis, the loss of function of AtWAK2 leads to shorter roots owing to the reduced activity of vacuolar invertase (20). Similarly, the knockdown of AtWAK4 expression confers smaller leaves and inflorescences than that of the WT, primarily owing to defects in cell elongation (19). In rice, the transcriptional repression of OsWAK1 leads to a dwarf phenotype with smaller leaf and shorter internodes, highlighting the vital role of this gene in leaf development and plant growth (21). Despite these insights, the precise mechanisms by which WAKs regulate plant development remain largely unknown.

In this study, we found that narrow leaf1 (nl1) wheat mutant exhibits narrow-leaf phenotype. Map-based cloning revealed that a single Ala-to-Val amino acid substitution in TaWAK2-A, a WAK gene derived from the A subgenome, is responsible for this phenotypic variation. Further analyses demonstrated that TaWAK2-A regulates leaf development through a signaling cascade involving TaNAL1, DROUGHT AND SALT TOLERANCE (TaDST), and CYTOKININ OXIDASE/DEHYDROGENASEs (TaCKXs). Consistent with this, the knockout of TaWAK2-A and TaNAL1, as well as overexpression of TaDST and TaCKX9, led to narrow leaf shape resembling the nl1 phenotype. These findings may greatly expand our understanding of leaf development and providing important genetic resources for the design of leaf architecture.

RESULTS

Map-based cloning of the candidate gene underlying narrow-leaf phenotype

We began by screening a mutant library generated by subjecting the winter wheat cultivar Jinmai47 (JM47) to the ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS). We identified a narrow-leaf mutant (nl1; Fig. 1, A and B), which showed an ~50% reduction in leaf width, accompanied by decreased leaf length and plant height compared with the WT (JM47) under field conditions (Fig. 1, A and B). In addition, nl1 mutant had a slender appearance, shorter spikes, and smaller grains (decreased length and width) compared with the WT (Fig. 1, A and B). A histological analysis of transverse sections of leaf blades and culms revealed significantly fewer large veins and small veins, lower total cell numbers, and small vein areas in the nl1 mutant than in the WT (Fig. 1, A and B). Specifically, in the leaves, the numbers of big vascular bundles (BVs) and small vascular bundles (SVs) in nl1 mutant were reduced by 27.8 and 56.1%, respectively, and the average area of the SVs was reduced to 75% of the WT (Fig. 1, C and D). In addition, the cross section of the second culm of nl1 displayed just 59.5% of the number of cells in the WT, while the numbers of BVs and SVs were reduced to 83.2 and 68.4% of WT, respectively (Fig. 1, C and D). Together, these results suggest that both cell division and expansion in the leaf tissue of nl1 have been largely compromised.

To dissect the genetic basis underlying the narrow-leaf phenotype of nl1, we performed crosses between nl1 and three different wheat varieties, including JM47 (the genetic background of nl1), Jing411 (J411), and Bainong207 (B207), generating three distinct mapping populations. All F1 plants exhibited a normal leaf phenotype (fig. S1); in addition, the segregation ratios of normal leaf phenotype to narrow-leaf individuals in the F2 populations follow a 3:1 ratio, fitting a chi-square distribution [χ2 < χ20.05,1 (3.84); table S2]. These results indicate that the narrow-leaf phenotype of nl1 is likely controlled by a recessive mutation at a single gene locus.

To isolate the causal gene responsible for the nl1 phenotype, we selected 473 F2 individuals displaying a narrow-leaf phenotype derived from nl1 × B207 population for genetic analysis. This allowed us to narrow down the candidate gene to a region between the markers 40-5 and 12-60 on the short arm of chromosome 1A (Fig. 2A). Further fine mapping with newly developed markers refined the target region to a 156-kb interval between the markers A2-2 and A2-3 (Fig. 2A). Only three high-confident genes were annotated within this region using the Chinese Spring reference genome IWGSC Refseq v1.1. A sequencing analysis of these three genes revealed a single C-to-T nucleotide variation at position 1577 from the start codon of TraesCS1A02G058200 (Fig. 2A). This C-to-T variation led to a single amino acid substitution from Ala to Val at the 525th amino acid of the deduced protein (Fig. 2A and fig. S2); therefore, we considered TraesCS1A02G058200, which encodes a wall associate kinase, as the most likely candidate for nl1 mutation, and designated the gene as TaWAK2-A (with “A” representing the A genome copy of TaWAK2). WAK family proteins are involved in the regulation of cell expansion, cell wall biosynthesis, and responses to environmental stresses in plants (22, 23), which provides further evidence that TaWAK2-A is the most likely candidate for the nl1 mutation.

Functional validation of TaWAK2 during wheat leaf development

Bread wheat is a typical hexaploid species containing A, B, and D subgenomes; thus, there are three TaWAK2 homoeologs, with the TaWAK2-B and TaWAK2-D homoeologs (TraesCS1B02G075700 and TraesCS1D02G058800, respectively) encoding proteins having 83.91 and 88.21% amino acid identity, respectively, with TaWAK2-A (fig. S3). To investigate the spatial expression patterns of the TaWAK2 homoeologs, we performed a reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction PCR (RT-qPCR) using homeolog-specific primers in five developing tissues. TaWAK2-A exhibited a dominant expression pattern in all examined tissues, whereas TaWAK2-B and TaWAK2-D were barely expressed (Fig. 2B). This functional differentiation of the homoeologs explains the observation that the single mutation in TaWAK2-A results in the narrow-leaf phenotype despite being a recessive allele. Moreover, in situ hybridization assays revealed prominent accumulation of TaWAK2-A transcript in the vascular bundles of young leaves (Fig. 2C), indicating a potential role for TaWAK2-A in manipulating leaf development and vascular bundle formation.

To validate the biological function of TaWAK2-A, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 strategy and designed single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) specific to TaWAK2. Through screening, we obtained two independent Tawak2-A mutant lines (#1 and #2) in the spring wheat “Fielder” background, which knocked out only the A-homeolog of TaWAK2 without affecting the TaWAK2-B and TaWAK2-D homeologs (fig. S4). Both Tawak2-A lines exhibited significantly narrower leaves than the WT plants, with leaf width reductions of 45.2 to 54.0% in the seedling stage and 27.6 to 31.0% at the mature plant stage. (Fig. 2, D and E). In addition, we introduced the full-length cDNA of TaWAK2-A into WT (Fielder) plants under the control of the ubiquitin promoter using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method. These TaWAK2-A–overexpressing lines showed wider flag leaves than that of Fielder at the mature plant stage (Fig. 2E). Together, these findings suggest that TraesCS1A02G058200 is the causal gene that explains the narrow-leaf phenotype of nl1.

Moreover, the TaWAK2-A–overexpressing lines exhibited slightly longer spikes, but the spikelet number per spike was unaffected (Fig. 2, F and G). Further investigation demonstrated that the TaWAK2-overexpressing lines had a higher thousand-grain weight and a 12.7 to 14.2% increase in grain weight per spike than WT in the field trials (Fig. 2, H and I). Collectively, these results suggest that TaWAK2 coordinately regulates leaf blade development and grain weight in wheat.

The A525V substitution in TaWAK2 facilitates its protein degradation

To examine the significance of A525V amino acid substitution, we first analyzed the subcellular localizations of TaWAK2-A and the nl1 mutation TaWAK2-AA525V by co-expressing p35S::TaWAK2-A–green fluorescent protein (GFP) or p35S::TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP with the plasma membrane marker p35S:: phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2)–red fluorescent protein (RFP) in the epidermal cells of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves and observed that the fluorescence from the TaWAK2-A–GFP and TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP fusion proteins overlapped with the red fluorescence of PIP2-RFP (Fig. 3A). These observations were further supported by the results of plasmolysis assays (Fig. 3B), indicating that TaWAK2-A and TaWAK2-AA525V are membrane-associated and that the A525V amino acid variation in TaWAK2-A does not affect its subcellular localization on the plasma membrane.

To investigate the effects of the A525V substitution on the stability of the TaWAK2-A protein, we next expressed TaWAK2-A–GFP and TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP in N. benthamiana leaves and observed a lower accumulation of TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP proteins than that of TaWAK2-A–GFP (Fig. 3C). Notably, the addition of MG132 (a 26S proteasome inhibitor) partially inhibited this degradation (Fig. 3C). To confirm this finding, we conducted a cell-free degradation assay by incubating the glutathione S-transferase (GST)–tagged TaWAK2-A (GST–KD–TaWAK2-A) or TaWAK2-AA525V (GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V) in the total protein extracts of 20-day-old Fielder seedling leaves. We found that GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V was degraded more rapidly than GST–KD–TaWAK2-A, and this degradation was largely blocked by treatment of a MG132 (Fig. 3D). Therefore, we concluded that the A525V mutation has reduced the protein stability of TaWAK2 via 26S proteasome pathway.

TaWAK2-A interacts with and phosphorylates trypsin-like protease TaNAL1

Previous studies have shown that OsNAL1, a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease, plays a crucial role in the regulation of vein patterning, cell division, leaf width, and plant height in rice and barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) (6, 24, 25). Notably, the loss of OsNAL1 confers a 50% reduction in leaf width and length in rice, largely resembling the phenotypes of nl1 (6). These findings prompted us to investigate whether the wheat ortholog of NAL1 (TaNAL1) is functionally related to TaWAK2-A in controlling leaf expansion (fig. S5). To address this, we performed a split–luciferase (LUC) complementation imaging (LCI) assay and confirmed that TaNAL1 interacts with TaWAK2-A in plant cells (Fig. 4A). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), pull-down, and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays also confirmed the interaction between TaWAK2-A and TaNAL1 (Fig. 4, B to D). TaWAK2-A is a transmembrane protein with a transmembrane region and an intracellular KD; therefore, we divided TaWAK2-A into the N-terminal transmembrane region (TaWAK2-AN) and the C-terminal KD (TaWAK2-AC) to determine which part of TaWAK2-A mediates the physical interaction with TaNAL1 (fig. S6). We observed a strong interaction signal between TaWAK2-AC and TaNAL1 but not between TaWAK2-AN and TaNAL1 (Fig. 4E), indicating that the TaWAK2-A–TaNAL1 interaction occurs in the cytoplasm and is likely mediated by the KD of TaWAK2-A.

To further confirm the biological function of TaNAL1 in wheat, we generated two independent Tanal1 mutants by simultaneously knocking out the B and D homeologs through CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. These were referred as Tanal1-b/d#1 and Tanal-b/d #2 (fig. S7). Phenotypic analyses revealed that the two transgenic lines phenocopied the Tawak2-a mutants, exhibiting reduced leaf blade widths, plant heights, and spikelets per spike compared with the WT (fig. S8).

As TaWAK2-A is a protein kinase, we next performed in vitro autophosphorylation assays using the GST-tagged KD of TaWAK2-A (GST–KD–TaWAK2-A) and the mutated KD of TaWAK2-AA525V (GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V), revealing that both exhibit strong autophosphorylation abilities with similar intensities (fig. S9). This suggests that the A525V mutation does not influence the kinase activity of TaWAK2-A. Next, we conducted in vitro phosphorylation assays using His-tagged TaNAL1 (His-TaNAL1) as the substrate protein. As expected, both GST–KD–TaWAK2-A and GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V effectively mediated the phosphorylation of His-TaNAL1 (Fig. 4F). Together, these findings reveal that TaWAK2-A can directly phosphorylate TaNAL1 and that the A525V amino acid substitution does not affect this kinase activity.

To determine the specific residues of TaNAL1 that are phosphorylated by TaWAK2-A, we performed a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. Ser562 and Ser568, located in the C terminus of TaNAL1, were found to be potentially phosphorylated (fig. S10 and table S7). To experimentally validate their potential functions, we introduced point mutations in TaNAL1, generating the TaNAL1S562A,S568A (TaNAL1AA) form with Ser-to-Ala substitutions at the 562nd and 568th positions to mimic the non-phosphorylated form of TaNAL1. Correspondingly, TaNAL1AA cannot be phosphorylated by TaWAK2 (Fig. 4G). Therefore, we concluded that Ser562 and Ser568 in TaNAL1 are the two major residues phosphorylated by TaWAK2. We next transformed plasmids expressing TaNAL1-GFP, TaNAL1AA-GFP, or TaNAL1DD-GFP (the phosphorylation-mimic form of TaNAL1) into Fielder protoplasts to investigate whether TaNAL1 phosphorylation changes its subcellular localization. We found that all the transformations showed similar subcellular locations, suggesting that Ser562 and Ser568 phosphorylation does not affect the subcellular localization of TaNAL1 (fig. S11).

Ser562 and Ser568 phosphorylation modulates the activity of TaNAL1 in the regulation of TaDST protein degradation

In rice, the zinc-finger transcription factor drought and salt tolerance (OsDST) is a negative regulator of leaf width (14). In this study, we next investigated whether the regulation of leaf width by TaNAL1 is also related to the function of TaDST in wheat (fig. S12). Using in vitro pull-down assays and in vivo LCI assays, we confirmed that TaNAL1 directly interacts with TaDST (Fig. 5, A and B). Subcellular localization studies further revealed that TaDST also co-localizes with TaNAL1 in the nucleus (Fig. 5C and fig. S13). TaNAL1 is a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease that may facilitate protein degradation; thus, we next investigated whether TaNAL1 targets TaDST for degradation. To test this hypothesis, we co-expressed TaNAL1-GFP and TaDST-MYC in N. benthamiana leaf epidermal cells, which revealed that the expression of TaNAL1-GFP leads to the strong repression of TaDST-MYC protein accumulation compared with the control samples expressing GFP alone (Fig. 5, D and E).

Next, we performed LCI assays to examine the interaction between TaNAL1 and TaDST. We found that the S562A and S568A substitutions in TaNAL1, creating a non-phosphorylation mimic (TaNAL1AA), completely abolished its interaction with TaDST (Fig. 5F). Consistent with that result, the degradation of TaDST was blocked by TaNAL1AA, whereas its degradation was enhanced by the phosphorylation-mimic form of TaNAL1 containing the S562D and S568D substitutions (Fig. 5, D and E). These findings support the hypothesis that the phosphorylation of TaNAL1 at S562 and S568 is essential for its protease activity, which triggers TaDST degradation. Consistent with this result, we observed a higher accumulation of the TaDST protein in the nl1 mutant than in the WT (Fig. 5G).

To better understand the biological significance of TaDST in regulating leaf development, we generated transgenic wheat lines overexpressing TaDST, referred as TaDST-OE (#1 and #2) (fig. S14). Phenotypic analyses revealed that both of the two independent TaDST-OE lines had significantly narrower leaves than the WT (Fig. 5, H and I). Together, these findings indicate that the TaWAK2-A–mediated phosphorylation of TaNAL1 is a prerequisite for the activation of TaNAL1 activity, leading to the degradation of TaDST and resulting in narrow-leaf phenotype.

Transcriptome profiling revealed TaDST-mediated TaCKX9 gene expression changes in nl1 mutants

We next explored the influences of the TaWAK2-A mutation on transcriptome reprogramming in nl1. We performed a transcriptome profiling assay using the young leaf tissues of nl1 and WT at 30 days after germination. A total of 1615 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between two genotypes, including 831 up-regulated and 784 down-regulated genes in nl1 compared with those in the WT (Fig. 6A). The gene ontology (GO) enrichment assay revealed that several signaling processes were enriched among the DEGs, including “photosynthesis and light harvesting,” “plant-type cell wall organization or biogenesis,” “cell wall macromolecule catabolic process,” and “gibberellin (GA) biosynthetic process” (Fig. 6B).

Notably, we observed a significant enrichment of DEGs in the “cytokinin (CK) metabolic process (GO:0009690, P value of 0.000955) category (Fig. 6B). CK is a crucial plant hormone involved in the regulation of axillary bud initiation, leaf development, and various aspects of plant growth (26–29). Therefore, we assumed that TaWAK2-A mutation may affect CK metabolism in wheat. To this end, we quantified the amounts of different types of CKs in the young leaf tissues of nl1 and the WT and found that the levels of the bioactive CK forms, including isopentenyladenine and cis-zeatin (cZ), were all significantly less abundant in the nl1 mutant than in the WT (Fig. 6C). In addition, the contents of CK ribosides, including cZ roboside and trans-zeatin riboside, were also markedly decreased in nl1(Fig. 6C). These results suggest that the accumulation of bioactive CK is partially compromised in the absence of functional TaWAK2-A. Consistent with these observations, several TaCKX genes, which encode key CK dehydrogenases responsible for CK degradation, were significantly up-regulated in response to TaWAK2-A mutation in nl1 (Fig. 6D); for example, TaCKX9 (the ortholog of OsCKX9, Os05g0374200) and TaCKX11 (the ortholog of OsCKX11, Os08g0460600) were up-regulated by 300 and 80%, respectively, in nl1 (Fig. 6E). To confirm the hypothesis that the narrow-leaf phenotype in nl1 mutant is ultimately caused by reduced CK levels, we performed exogenous application of 6BA to nl1 mutant. The results revealed that exogenous application of 6BA leads to wider leaves than the control in nl1 mutant, albeit slightly narrower than those of the WT (fig. S15). This finding supports the notion that the narrow-leaf phenotype observed in nl1 is a result of reduced endogenous CK.

Previous studies have revealed that OsDST recognizes and binds to the TGNTANN(A/T)T sequence, a cis element named DST-binding sequence (DBS) (30–32). We identified several DBSs within the 2-kb promoter upstream of TaCKX9 gene (fig. S16). An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) confirmed the direct binding of TaDST to these DBS motifs (Fig. 6F). A transient transcriptional activity assay revealed that TaDST efficiently enhances the expression of TaCKX9; however, the presence of TaNAL1-B suppresses the ability of TaDST to activate TaCKX9 (Fig. 6G). Notably, the transcript level of TaCKX9 was also up-regulated in the TaDST-overexpressing lines (fig. S17), providing further evidence that TaDST acts as an upstream transcriptional activator of TaCKX genes in wheat. To validate the biological function of TaCKX9, we generated TaCKX9 overexpression wheat lines (TaCKX9-OE), using Fielder as the genetic background (Fig. 6H and fig. S18). A phenotypic analysis revealed that the overexpression of TaCKX9 significantly reduced the leaf width in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6H). In summary, we concluded that the nl1 narrow-leaf phenotype is attributed to the genetic activation of TaCKX genes and, consequently, leading to the degradation of bioactive CK in vivo.

DISCUSSION

Leaf morphology is one of the most important features of plant architecture, which directly affects a plant’s photosynthetic capacity and influences agronomic traits such as yield and stress responses (33, 34). Leaf development initiates from the shoot apical meristem, which acts as a stem cell niche responsible for generating all aboveground organs during plant postembryonic development (2). During leaf development, distinct regions undergo cell division and expansion processes. The direction and rate of cell expansion, crucial for leaf morphology, are influenced by the interplay between turgor pressure and cell wall mechanics (35, 36). Composed of polysaccharide polymers like cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, as well as glycoproteins and lignin, these components not only contribute to the physical strength and rigidity of the cell walls but also play crucial roles in signaling and defense mechanisms (35–37). Previous studies in Arabidopsis and rice have demonstrated the involvement of WAKs in the regulation of cell expansion (20, 21, 38, 39); for example, OsWAK11 directly interacts with and phosphorylates the brassinosteroid receptor OsBRI1 and inhibits its interaction with the co-receptor OsSERK1/OsBAK1, thereby blocking the brassinosteroid signal transduction pathway and regulating cell elongation in rice (39). In addition, the knockdown of AtWAK4 transcription results in the inhibition of cell elongation (38), and the knockout of AtWAK2 reduces seedling growth (20). Moreover, transgenic rice plants overexpressing OsWAK1 also exhibited dwarf phenotype due to decreased leaves, flag-leaves, internodes, and panicles (21).

WAK proteins are also reported to bind to cross-linked pectin and pathogen- or damage-induced pectin fragments and oligo-galacturonides in the cell walls (38, 39), suggesting their integral role in recognizing environmental cues and in initiating signaling pathways that contribute to the plant’s defensive responses. Consistently, the WAK-like protein RESISTANCE TO FUSARIUM OXYSPORUM 1 (RFO1) has been shown to play a critical role in the early defense response against Fusarium oxysporum by detecting changes in pectin methylation levels within the cell wall (40), underscoring the significance of pectin methylation status as a crucial factor in activating WAK functions in plant defense mechanisms. In addition, in rice, the stability of OsWAK11 is influenced by light conditions, with degradation occurring in darkness and stability maintained under light conditions, which is triggered by alterations in the ratio of methylated pectin to demethylated pectin (31). This observation suggests that OsWAK11 may also function as a sensor of pectin methylation status, similar to RFO1. The light-dependent stability of OsWAK11 indicates a sophisticated regulatory mechanism where environmental factors, such as light, can affect the protein’s stability and, consequently, its signaling functions.

Through map-based gene cloning, we isolated TaWAK2-A as the causal gene responsible for the narrow-leaf phenotype of nl1 mutant. A sequencing analysis revealed a C-to-T single-nucleotide variation at position 1577 from the start codon of TaWAK2-A in nl1. The substitution leads to a single amino acid change from Ala to Val at the 525th amino acid of the deduced protein. Notably, TaWAK2-AA525V showed a decreased accumulation compared with WT TaWAK2-A–GFP when expressed in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, cell-free degradation assay confirmed that GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V degrades faster than GST–KD–TaWAK2-A (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that the A525 amino acid residue plays a critical role in controlling the protein stability of TaWAK2-A. Both GST–KD–TaWAK2-A and GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V were found to directly phosphorylate TaNAL1 (Fig. 4F), suggesting that the A525V amino acid substitution in nl1 reduces TaWAK2-A protein stability but not alter its kinase activity. TaWAK2-A can affect the in vivo concentration of CK through regulation of TaCKX9 gene expression (Fig. 6), which provides a previously unidentified example in wheat to explain the essential role of WAKs in regulating CK homeostasis and leaf expansion. Therefore, our findings highlight a sophisticated signaling pathway that transmits plasma membrane cues to the intracellular transcription events, thereby regulating CK metabolism and leaf development in wheat. Considering that WAK family proteins are generally considered as cell wall sensors by respond to differential pectin methylation status, which can be altered by environmental changes, we hypothesize that TaWAK2 might also plays a crucial role in response to environmental challenges, which merits further study.

In summary, this study supports a working model to explain the fundamental role of TaWAK2-A in regulation of leaf expansion in wheat (Fig. 7). Briefly, in the normally developing leaf, the phosphorylated TaWAK2-A interacts with and phosphorylates a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease TaNAL and enhances its activity. This, in turn, leads to the degradation of TaDST, a zinc finger transcription factor; then, the degradation of TaDST suppresses the expression of TaCKX genes, leading to an increase in CK accumulation, thus promoting leaf development (Fig. 7A). Whereas in the nl1 mutant, the protein stability of TaWAK2-A is reduced due to Ala-to-Val substitution, leading to repression of TaNAL1 activity because of decreased phosphorylation levels. Then, decreased activity of TaNAL1 results in an increased amount of TaDST protein, which causes an up-regulation of TaCKX9/11 expression and down-regulation of CK concentration, and lastly leads to the narrow-leaf phenotypic variation in wheat (Fig. 7B).

Proposed model for the TaWAK2-TaNAL1-TaDST-TaCKX9/11 meditated CK signaling in regulation of leaf width in wheat.

(A) In WT plant, TaWAK2 protein phosphorylates and activates TaNAL1, a trypsin-like serine/cysteine protease; the activated TaNAL1 will lead to the degradation of its downstream substrate TaDST, a zinc finger transcription factor, resulting in the repression of CK oxidase (TaCKX9 and TaCKX11) gene expression and the accumulation of in vivo CK; last, the accumulated in vivo CK promotes wheat leaf cell division and leaf formation. (B) In the nl1 mutant, the protein stability of TaWAK2 is reduced, which, in turn, represses the protein activity of TaNAL1 due to decreased phosphorylation levels. This repression leads to an increase in the amount of TaDST protein, which causes an up-regulation of TaCKX9 and TaCKX11 expression. Consequently, the levels of CK in vivo decrease, which is correlated with the development of a narrow-leaf phenotype in wheat.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

The nl1 mutant was isolated from an EMS-mutagenized population of common wheat cultivars JM47. JM47 was used as the WT control in this study for phenotypic observation. Three F2 populations were generated by crossing nl1 with common wheat varieties Bainong207 (B207), Jing411 (J411), and JM47 for map-based gene cloning. For the transgenic experiments, Fielder was used as the WT control. Wheat plants were planted in the experimental field in Shangzhuang, Beijing, China. The transgenic plants were planted in pots with soil in a greenhouse under a photoperiod of 16-hour light (24°C) and 8-hour dark (20°C). The seedlings were stratified at 4°C for 1 month and then were cultivated under 22°C in a growth chamber with 60% relative humidity and 3000 l× light intensity (Master GreenPower 558 CG T 400W E40; Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands).

Map-based cloning

To isolate the causal gene mutanted in nl1 mutation, insertion-deletion markers were first developed on the basis of the genomic resequencing data of JM47 and B207. These markers were further used to screen 473 plants selected form the F2 population of nl1/B207 that displayed the nl1 mutant phenotype. Further fine mapping with newly developed markers refined the target region to a 156-kb interval between the markers A2-2 and A2-3. On the basis of resequencing data and Sanger sequencing, we performed sequence analysis of the genes in the candidate interval and ultimately identified the candidate gene. The primer sequences used for gene mapping were listed in table S1.

Expression vector construction and gene transformation

To knock out TaWAK2 and TaNAL1 genes in wheat, the sgRNA target sequences were designed on the basis of the exon sequences of these genes using E-CRISP (www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/). The MT1T2 vectors were amplified using two pairs of primers containing the sgRNAs and then cloned into the CRISPR-Cas9 vector pBUE411. In addition, to generate OE–TaWAK2-A, TaNAL1-OE, TaDST-OE, and TaCKX9-OE transgenic plants, the full-length coding sequences (CDSs) of TaWAK2-A, TaNAL1-B, TaDST-D, and TaCKX9-B were separately fused with GFP-CDSs and cloned into the plant binary vector pWMB110. All these recombinant expression vectors were separately transformed into the bread wheat cultivar Fielder using the Agrobacterium-mediated gene transformation (40). The primer sequences used for vector construction were listed in table S1.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were extracted from wheat leaf tissues using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by reverse transcription using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was performed using the AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) in a real-time PCR system (CFX96, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, USA). The wheat β-actin gene was used as an internal control. The primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in table S1.

Subcellular localization analysis

The CDSs of TaWAK2-A and TaWAK2-AA525V were separately amplified and cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector to generate TaWAK2-A–GFP and TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP expression vectors. These vectors were separately expressed in N. benthamiana epidermal leaf cells through Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101)–mediated transient expression. The plasmolysis assays were conducted by incubating the samples in 20% mannitol or water for 30 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. PIP2-RFP was co-expressed as a membrane marker. The fluorescence signal was detected at 488 and 546 nm using a confocal microscope (LSM880; Carl Zeiss, Heidenheim, Germany).

The GFP, TaNAL1-GFP, TaNAL1AA-GFP, TaNAL1DD-GFP, and TaDST-GFP plasmids were separately co-transfected with TF3-mCherry into wheat protoplasts. The GFP fluorescent signals were imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope as described above. At least 10 cells showing a consistent localization pattern were observed.

RNA in situ hybridization

The probe for TaWAK2 was labeled using digoxigenin-11-ddUTP (Dig Gel Shift Kit; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using the SP6/T7 promoter and RNA polymerase. The section of pretreatment, hybridization, and immunological detection were carried out as described previously (41). The primers used for analysis are listed in table S1.

Split-LCI assay

For the LCI assays, the indicated genes were separately fused with sequences encoding the N- or C-terminal regions of the reporter LUC. The CDSs of TaNAL1 and TaNAL1AA were cloned into the p1300-35S-nLUC vectors, and the CDSs of TaWAK2-A, TaWAK2C-A, were separately cloned into p1300-35S-cLUC vectors. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens (strain GV3101) and then infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves according to the indicated combinations. The LUC signal was analyzed 48 to 72 hours after infiltration using a NightSHADE LB985 in vivo plant imaging system (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany), after spraying 1 mM d-luciferin on the leaves (Coolaber, Beijing, China). The primer sequences are listed in table S1.

BiFC assay

The BiFC assay was performed by using n-YFP and c-YFP vectors harboring the fragments encoding the N- and C-terminal halves of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), respectively. The CDSs of TaWAK2-A and TaNAL1 were separately cloned into the n-YFP and c-YFP vectors, and the combinations of plasmids were transformed into the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. The YFP signal was imaged after 48 hours of infiltration using a confocal microscope (LSM880; Carl Zeiss). The primer sequences are listed in table S1.

GST pull-down assay

For pull-down assay, the KD–TaWAK2-A and KD–TaWAK2-AA525V and TaDST sequences were amplified and introduced into pGEX-4 T-2, while TaNAL1-A was cloned into pET-32a. Protein expression, purification, and pull-down assay were performed as previously described with minor modifications (42). The GST or GST-fusion proteins were separately incubated with GST beads in 1 ml of binding buffer [50-mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.2% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.6% (v/v) Triton X-100] at 4°C for 2 hours and then washed four times with the same buffer. The beads were resuspended in 1 ml of binding buffer before the His-fusion prey proteins. The mixture was incubated at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by washing four times. The immunoprecipitated proteins were detected using anti-GST (30901ES50; YEASEN Biotech, Shanghai, China) and anti-His (30401ES50; YEASEN Biotech) antibodies, where appropriate. The primer sequences are listed in table S1.

Co-IP assay

The Co-IP experiments were performed as described previously (43). GFP, TaWAK2-A–GFP, and TaNAL1-myc vectors were separately transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co-expressed in N. benthamiana with indicated combinations. Total proteins were extracted using lysis buffer (20131ES06, YEASEN Biotech), after which the lysates were incubated at 4°C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 13,000g for 20 min. The supernatants were incubated with 30 μl of anti-GFP magnetic beads (HT801-01; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) at 4°C for 2 hours for immunoprecipitation. The obtained immunoprecipitated samples were isolated on SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels, and the proteins were detected using anti-GFP (HT801-01; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and anti-Myc (HT101-01; TransGen Biotech) antibodies, respectively. The primer sequences are listed in table S1.

RNA-seq and data analysis

Young leaf tissues of 30-day-old nl1 and WT (JM47) plants were collected for RNA extraction and library construction. Three biological replicates were performed. Barcoded cDNA libraries were constructed using Poly-A Purification TruSeq library reagents (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and were sequenced on NovaSeq platform (Illumina). After screening and trimming, the clean reads were mapped to the wheat reference genome (IWGSC REFSEQ v.1.1) using TOPHAT2 software. The bioconductor package DESEQ2 was used for determination of the DEGs with estimated log2 (fold changes) ≥ 1 and a P value of <0.05. MORPHEUS was used to construct the heatmaps (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus), and Triticeae-Gene Tribe was used to determine the GO term enrichment (44). The RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data are deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; PRJNA1053490).

Cell-free protein degradation assay

A cell-free protein degradation assay was performed as described previously (45). Briefly, as TaWAK2-A encodes a membrane protein, it is challenging to purify membrane proteins in bacteria. Therefore, we opted to purify the KD of TaWAK2-A and TaWAK2-AA525V for further analysis. First, total proteins were extracted from 10-day-old WT plants with native protein extraction buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 M sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)]. Approximately 300 ng of GST–KD–TaWAK2-A and GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V proteins were separately incubated with equal amounts of total WT protein extracts of and 10 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) at 25°C for the indicated times with or without 50 μM MG132. The amounts of GST-tagged proteins were determined with an immunoblotting assay using the anti-GST antibody.

In vitro and in vivo phosphorylation assays

The in vitro phosphorylation assay was performed as reported previously (46). Briefly, 1 μg of the GST–KD–TaWAK2-A or GST–KD–TaWAK2-AA525V proteins was incubated with 2 μg of His-TaNAL1 protein in 20 μl of kinase reaction buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 12 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP] at 37°C for 1 hour. The reactions were boiled with 2× SDS loading buffer and loaded on a 6% SDS-PAGE gel containing 100 μM MnCl2 and 50 μM Phos-Tag (NARD, Kai City, Japan). The proteins were detected using immunoblotting assay with anti-His antibodies (HT501; TransGen Biotech). The in vivo phosphorylation assay was performed as described previously (46) with minor modifications. The indicated combinations of GFP, TaWAK2-A–GFP, TaWAK2-AA525V–GFP, and TaNAL1-MYC constructs were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaf cells, after which the total proteins were extracted using 2× SDS loading buffer for a further immunoblot analysis on 6% SDS-PAGE gel with 100 μM MnCl2 and 50 μM Phos-Tag.

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry

To identify the putative site of TaNAL1 that is phosphorylated by TaWAK2-A in vitro, 1 μg of purified His-TaNAL1 proteins and 10 μg of purified GST–KD–TaWAK2-A proteins were incubated in 20 μl of a protein kinase reaction buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 50 μM ATP] at 30°C for 30 min. The reaction products were reduced by DTT, alkylated by iodoacetamide, and then digested with trypsin (pH 8.5) at 37°C for 12 hours. The samples were analyzed using LC-MS/MS as described previously (47). The Mascot search engine (Mascot 2.6.0; Matrix Science) was used to analyze the raw MS/MS data. The UniProt wheat sequence database was used to search the peptides, using the parameters listed previously (48). The positive peptides and identified phosphorylation sites are listed in table S7.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The CDS of TaDST was cloned into pGEX-4 T-2, and the resulting vector was transformed into Escherichia coli (strain BL21). Protein expression and purification were performed described previously (32). Oligonucleotide probes were synthesized and labeled with biotin at their 5′ end. An EMSA was performed using a Light Shift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). GST and GST-TaDST were added to the reaction mixture [5 ng of biotin-labeled annealed oligonucleotides, 2 μl of 10× binding buffer, 1 μl of 50% (v/v) glycerol, 1 μl of 100 mM MgCl2, 1 μl of poly(dI-dC) (1 mg ml−1), and 1 μl of 1% (v/v) Nonidet P-40]. The reactions were then incubated at 25°C for 20 min, followed by an electrophoretic analysis. The primer sequences are listed in table S1.

LUC reporter assay

The CDS of TaDST was cloned into pCAMBIA-1300-35S-GFP.Approximately 2 kb of promoter sequence of TaCKX9-A was introduced into Plant Gateway Binary Vector vector to fuse with the LUC reporter gene and then transiently expressed with the indicated effector vectors in N. benthamiana leaves for 48 hours. The luminescence was detected using the Night SHADE LB985 system (Berthold Technologies) with Indigo software. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in table S1.

CK measurement and CK assay

The CKs were extracted from the wheat leaves and analyzed according to a previously described method (49). For the CK treatment assay, an exogenous CK, 6-benzylaminopurine (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was dissolved in 0.5 ml 1M NaOH and then diluted with distilled water to a concentration of 300 μM. All water control treatments used a similar solution of 0.5 ml 1M NaOH and were diluted synchronously with the CK solution. All plants were grown in an incubator with 75% relative humidity, day/night temperatures of 24°C/20°C, and a light intensity of 3000 lux (Master GreenPower CG T 400 W E40; Philips). Two days after germination, the seedlings were transplanted into square pots containing a soil medium and subjected to daily hormone or water treatments. After 20 days of growth, the width of the third fully expanded leaf was measured, and the data were analyzed. Each treatment was set up with 15 independent replicates.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise differences between genotypes or treatments were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. For comparisons involving more than two genotypes, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check for data normality. If the data were normally distributed, then statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. When the normality assumption was not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunnett’s post hoc test were used to compare genotypes or treatments.
