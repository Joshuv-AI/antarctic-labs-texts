Prevalence and experiences of sexual harassment among Saudi female nursing students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wafa Hamad Almegewly, Haya Abu Saleh, Alanoud Alasmari, Amirah Alnashery, Atheer Alanazi, Fatima Alqahtani, Rawan Alharbi, Rahaf Alotaibi, Shefaa Alanazi, Taif Alanazi, Rasha Mahfouz, Savvato Karavasileiadou, Christos Kleisiaris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale nursing students are at risk of various types of sexual harassment, including physical, emotional, psychological, and visual abuse. This study aims to assess the prevalence and dimensions of sexual harassment experienced by female undergraduate nursing students.MethodsA cross-sectional quantitative design was employed. A convenient sample of undergraduate nursing students (n = 236) enrolled in the Bachelor of Science in Nursing Program at the selected university was recruited. The Sexual Experiences Questionnaire was administered as an online survey via Google Forms to collect data from February to March 2022. Both descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted to analyze the data.ResultsAmong the 236 students, 62.2% reported experiencing harassment at least once. The most prevalent form of sexual harassment identified was unwanted sexual attention, particularly reflected in uncomfortable looks, which affected 41.1% of participants. Several reasons were noted for the failure to report incidents, including fear, lack of knowledge, and feelings of shame. Participants also suggested that educational lessons and awareness campaigns emphasizing the importance of reporting incidents could help reduce the incidence of sexual harassment.ConclusionMore than half of nursing students reported experiencing sexual harassment during clinical training, highlighting a serious concern. Academic institutions and health policymakers must ensure a safe clinical environment through awareness sessions, clear reporting procedures, and education on legal protections under Saudi law.Keywords: Knowledge, Attitude, Sexual harassment, Sexual abuse, Nursing students

Background

Sexual harassment refers to any unwelcome sexual conduct, whether verbal, physical, psychological, or visual. It involves not only sexual behavior but also power imbalances [1]. It is a serious issue faced by any individual who comes into direct contact with others, especially staff nurses and nursing students, during the care process.

According to WHO, 6% of women aged 15–49 and those 15 years and older have experienced non-partner sexual violence at least once in their lifetime, with Western Asia showing the lowest reported prevalence at 4% [2]. Sexual harassment among Saudi nurses is a pervasive issue that has gained increased attention in recently. Despite efforts to address this problem, many nurses continue to experience various forms of harassment in clinical settings. In Saudi Arabia, 15.5% of healthcare workers experience sexual harassment, and verbal harassment was the most common form, at 66% [3]. 19.3% of Saudi female residents experienced sexual harassment [4]. 51.4% of Saudi nurses, midwives, and other health specialists reported sexual violence in the workplace [5]. Globally, according to a systematic review, the prevalence of sexual harassment against female nurses was 43.15% [6]. In Korea, Europe, Ethiopia, Gambia, China, and Taiwan, the rates are 22%, 16.2%, 13.02%, 10%, 63.4%, and 12.9%, respectively [7].

Recent studies highlight that sexual harassment among nursing students during clinical training is a global concern, with prevalence rates varying widely across regions. According to a meta-analysis, the prevalence of sexual harassment among nursing students in the Middle Eastern region ranges from 2.1 to 53.3% [8]. Two-thirds of nursing students in Egypt reported they had been sexually harassed while undergoing clinical rotations [9]. In Jordan, 2.5% of nursing students reported experiencing sexual harassment during their clinical training [10]. Similarly, in Tanzania, 11% of nursing students reported such experiences during clinical practice [11].

The impact of sexual harassment on nursing students is profound and far-reaching. Victims often experience psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, fear, sleep disturbances, panic attacks, and diminished self-esteem [8]. They may also suffer from social withdrawal, burnout, and feelings of isolation, particularly when institutional responses are inadequate or unsupportive [9, 12]. These consequences can impair clinical learning, reduce academic performance, and diminish motivation to continue in the nursing profession, ultimately affecting the quality of patient care [12, 13]. Furthermore, fear of retaliation or inaction often prevents students from reporting incidents, thereby perpetuating a culture of silence within clinical settings [14].

In Saudi Arabia, sexual harassment is religiously prohibited. This prohibition is rooted in Islamic teachings that emphasize respect for women and their dignity. Any violation of these principles is not only considered as a social taboo but also a religious offense. While gender segregation in Saudi Arabia can reduce opportunities for sexual harassment by limiting mixed-gender interactions in public and professional spaces, exposure to clinical environments may present increased risks for nursing students. In hospitals and healthcare settings, where gender mixing is often necessary for practical training and patient care, nursing students, particularly females, may face heightened vulnerability to harassment [15]. These environments can challenge the protective boundaries of segregation, potentially exposing students to power imbalances and inappropriate behavior from patients, colleagues, or supervisors [3, 16].

Power dynamics often leave nurses feeling unable to report misconduct, particularly when it involves patients or individuals in authority [12]. Organizational shortcomings—such as insufficient training, lack of managerial support, and a prevailing culture of silence—further exacerbate the issue. Feminist theory explains how power imbalances, rigid gender roles, and institutional silence contribute to sexual harassment, especially in male-dominated and hierarchical healthcare environments [17]. Additionally, the Social Ecological Model (SEM) provides a framework for understanding how harassment is influenced by factors across multiple levels: individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and societal.

The initial action for reducing sexual assaults and improving case response is through reporting, though many students refrain from doing so for several reasons. Such as lack of education, uncertainty about when behavior crosses the line, power imbalances, fear of negative consequences, and feelings of guilt for not preventing the harassment [18, 19].

Several strategies have been suggested to tackle sexual harassment in the workplace; these include the introduction of preventive training programs during the internship year [20], the establishment of systematic reporting mechanisms, the enforcement of strict penalties for perpetrators, the enhancement of public perceptions of nurses, and the provision of support from head nurses and female counseling officers [7, 21–23]. It is also essential for senior nurses to mentor new nurses on how to manage “difficult” patients, assert boundaries, and seek assistance when needed [21]. Furthermore, the use of multimedia interactive e-books has been suggested as an effective tool for improving nursing students’ knowledge and competence in preventing sexual harassment [24].

The government has enacted the Saudi Anti-Harassment Law, which criminalizes all forms of harassment, including any violation of honor, body, or modesty. It imposes maximum penalties of up to 5 years in prison and a fine of 300,000 SAR on perpetrators [25]. Saudi Arabia has recently amended its anti-harassment laws to include the naming and shaming of individuals found guilty, requiring them to publish their sentences in local media at their expense [26]. The anti-harassment law aims to foster a healthy and safe environment. This approach aligns with WHO’s strategy for preventing and responding to sexual misconduct (2023–2025), which involves creating a culture change within the organization by addressing gender inequity, lack of diversity, and power imbalances [27].

In individual cases, courts have imposed significant penalties, underscoring the government’s commitment to combating harassment [28]. Also, the rate of sexual harassment among nurses was reduced from 12 to 3% after launching the anti-harassment legalization in 2018 [29]. Despite the legal framework, several challenges hinder the effective implementation of the anti-harassment law, such as cultural stigma and fear of social repercussions that discourage victims from reporting harassment [30].

The research gap in studying sexual harassment against nursing students is substantial, as most existing Saudi literature tends to focus on work violence in general [15, 29] and not specifically on sexual harassment. Also, the current Saudi literature emphasizes healthcare workers [3, 5, 16], leaving the unique experiences of nursing students underexplored. Nursing students, often in the early stages of their careers, are particularly vulnerable to anxiety, depression, turnover, poor learning experiences, and low self-esteem due to their dual role as learners and future professionals. They navigate clinical environments where power imbalances, hierarchical structures, and gendered dynamics can increase the likelihood of harassment. Additionally, the structure of nursing education, which combines theoretical learning with hands-on clinical experience, exposes students to situations where they may encounter inappropriate behavior from patients, physicians, or colleagues. The negative impact of sexual harassment on students’ mental health, academic performance, and work burnout and eventually on the quality of the provided care [31].

The novelty of studying sexual harassment among nursing students lies in addressing the prevalence and factors that make them more vulnerable. This study illuminates how existing institutional cultures, along with inadequate policies or a lack of effective reporting mechanisms, may unintentionally perpetuate harassment. It also offers a unique opportunity to evaluate the disconnect between policy and practice within academic and clinical settings and how these environments may fail to protect students. Furthermore, understanding the broader impact of harassment on students’ mental health, academic performance, and career progression could encourage the development of targeted interventions, improved reporting systems, and educational frameworks designed to prevent harassment and promote safer clinical and academic spaces for nursing students. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence and various aspects of sexual harassment experienced by undergraduate nursing students.

Methods

Research design

A cross-sectional quantitative design was used that included both closed- and open-ended questions.

Setting

The study was conducted at a selected governmental university in the Riyad region, Saudi Arabia.

Sampling technique

A convenient sample of undergraduate nursing students was recruited from the second year through the internship.

Participants

The study participants were female undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a Bachelor of Science in Nursing program at the Nursing College during the data collection period. First-year nursing students were excluded because they had not yet been fully exposed to nursing or clinical practice. The sample size was determined using the Epi Info program, with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval. Based on this, the required sample size was calculated to be n = 229 from a population of N = 562, while the final collected sample size was n = 236.

The data measurement and collection process

Socio-demographic and academic profile

The first section of the questionnaire included demographic characteristics of respondents, such as age, Grade Point Average (GPA), and academic year. The GPA categories were Acceptable (2.0-2.74), Good (2.75–3.74), Very Good (3.75–4.74), and Excellent (4.75-5.0).

The sexual experiences questionnaire

The adapted, validated Arabic version of the Sexual Experiences Questionnaire (SEQ) [32], originally developed by Fitzgerald et al. (1988) [33], was utilized. According to Spencer et al., (2022), the Arabic adaptation of the Sexual Experiences Questionnaire (SEQ) involved a thorough translation and cultural modification process to ensure it accurately reflects experiences of sexual harassment in Arabic-speaking environments like Jordan [32]. It underwent rigorous validation, including face and content validity of students and experts, reliability, and factor analysis, confirming its alignment with the original SEQ’s structure—gender harassment, unwanted sexual attention, physical-contact sexual harassment, and sexual coercion [32].

Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett were used to verify the validity of the questionnaire. The KMO value of the questionnaire is 0.917, and the significance of Bartlett’s spherical testing is 0.000. Therefore, the questionnaire meets the requirements of factor analysis. Reliability testing using Cronbach’s alpha for each dimension in the questionnaire yielded a value of 0.910 (> 0.75), indicating high reliability for the dimensions. The SEQ employed a 3-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from “never” [1], “once” [2], and “more than once” [3]. The questionnaire consists of 22 items divided into four sections: the first part, “gender harassment,” contains three questions; the second part, “sexual coercion,” contains nine questions; the third part, “unwanted sexual attention,” includes seven questions; and the fourth part, “physical sexual harassment,” contains three questions. Two open-ended questions were also included: the first asked, “What are the reasons behind the failure of nursing students to report sexual harassment?” and the second asked, “What suggestions have nursing students made to address sexual harassment issues during clinical training?”

The survey was uploaded to Google Forms, and the Student Affairs unit distributed the survey link to all targeted nursing students via their university email addresses. Data collection took place during February and March 2022.

Ethical consideration

Approval from the institution’s Review Board (IRB) has been granted under reference number 22–0090. All participants provided written consent to participate in the research, which was collected online on the initial page of the questionnaire. They were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. All participants remained anonymous, and no identifying information was collected at any stage of the data-gathering process to ensure participant confidentiality and privacy.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 24. The respondents’ age group and academic profile, as well as the extent of experience for each item on the scales, were described using frequency and percentage. The prevalence of sexual harassment and the specific scales were determined using the epidemiological formula for prevalence rate. Furthermore, bivariate statistics, including the Pearson correlation coefficient, were applied to assess the hypothesis, specifically the relationship between participants’ demographic profiles and sexual harassment on specific scales. The analysis was conducted with a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Shapiro and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests were used to detect the normality of the data.

Content analysis was employed to analyze the narrative responses of the two open questions, which included “reasons for failure to report sexual harassment” and “suggestions by nursing students to solve the sexual harassment problems in clinical training.” Participants’ responses were collected from these two sections. Content analysis was used rather than thematic analysis due to the nature of the data collected, which were generally brief and concise, often limited to short phrases or single sentences. Given this format, content analysis was better suited for systematically categorizing and quantifying recurring responses rather than developing deeper interpretive themes [34]. This approach allowed us to summarize patterns across the data while remaining faithful to the brevity of participants’ input. The first and second authors independently coded the data, compared their findings, and refined the codes throughout the coding process. Coding was organized into categories and subcategories using an inductive approach [35]. Descriptive statistical analysis was also conducted to measure the frequency of the most commonly reported answers.

Discussion

The most prevalent form of harassment reported was being “looked at uncomfortably,” which is a form of non-verbal sexual harassment. These results align with a study among Pakistani students that reported a high prevalence rate of unwanted sexual attention among female nursing students [36]. Suggestive staring has been identified as the most frequent type of sexual harassment in Egypt [37]. This alignment may stem from the cultural norms and societal attitudes toward gender and sexuality in the region, which can perpetuate behaviors that objectify and demean individuals based on their gender [12]. Non-verbal sexual harassment is difficult to document, as it is often dismissed as harmless, and its nature and frequency in daily interactions contribute to its widespread occurrence and underreporting [38]. From a feminist theory perspective, sexual harassment often reflects deeper gender dynamics. In Saudi Arabia, female healthcare workers may face challenges rooted in traditional norms, where certain behaviors—though seen as normal—can quietly reinforce inequality and discourage speaking out. The evidence suggests that interventions targeting cultural norms and attitudes may effectively reduce instances of non-verbal sexual harassment. The most common forms of sexual coercion involve unwelcome attempts to draw someone into sexual discussions. Several studies have indicated that nursing students are more likely to experience verbal sexual harassment than unwanted sexual attention [9, 10, 13, 39, 41]. Its prevalence can be attributed to its subtlety, social tolerance, and the difficulty of proving intent. Cultural norms and gendered power imbalances further normalize inappropriate language, particularly in male-dominated environments. In some cultures, individuals subjected to verbal harassment may not recognize it as inappropriate [38]. In this study, some students were verbally abused because of their gender. This result is congruent with the Greek and Jordanian studies, which found that 70% of female nurses experienced sexual harassment, with the most frequent type being gender-related harassment [10, 42]. The discrimination and inequality between males and females may contribute to the increasing incidence of harassment experienced by females [43]. Hierarchical structures allow those in authority to use verbal harassment to exert control or manipulate subordinates without overtly violating policies, where consultants are regarded as superiors and nurses as subordinates, increasing the likelihood of harassment by physicians [13]. This form of coercion undermines ethical standards, creates power imbalances, and violates personal autonomy, potentially leading to long-term emotional and professional consequences for victims. When viewed through the lens of feminist theory, this highlights how institutional hierarchies may facilitate gender-based abuse of power, often under the proper use of authority.

The current study revealed that physical harassment was the least frequently reported type of harassment among participants. This result aligns with studies from Australia, the United States, and Norway, which found that many participants were female nurses exposed to various forms of sexual harassment, such as being “inappropriately touched, hugged, or kissed” [44, 45]. Female respondents considered physical contact and sexual orientation statements unacceptable [36], which may help explain these findings. In Arab cultures, where traditions and values strongly uphold individual identity, physical harassment is viewed as a societal crime that negatively affects one’s acceptance within the community. Consequently, perpetrators in these contexts may be more inclined to use nonverbal forms of harassment instead of physical contact [46].

The current findings suggest that senior students, who spend more time in clinical training, report experiencing sexual harassment more frequently than junior students. This aligns with previous Saudi studies conducted among medical and nursing students [13, 41, 47, 48]. The increase can be attributed to several factors, including greater exposure to power imbalances, as senior students interact more with authority figures, making them more vulnerable to harassment. Their proximity to supervisors and senior staff also places them within a hierarchical structure where harassment is more likely to occur [12, 13]. Additionally, prolonged exposure to clinical settings where harassment is normalized or overlooked may condition nurses, allowing such behavior to persist and act passively [38]. These findings emphasize the need for improved reporting mechanisms and a cultural shift to address harassment in clinical environments.

The present study identified four primary reasons for the failure to report sexual harassment, with fear being the most frequently cited. These results align with earlier studies that have linked fear to societal perceptions significantly influencing behavior [9, 21, 23, 48]. Additionally, fear of potential disadvantages during clinical practice and a lack of supporting evidence have been identified as key reasons for the silence of nursing and medical students [19]. Another crucial reason for not reporting was a lack of knowledge, as participants expressed a desire for educational resources to guide them on how to act, report, and respond appropriately. Several studies have indicated that students lacking knowledge are more vulnerable to sexual harassment and less equipped to confront such behavior [21, 23, 47]. Some Saudi female adolescents reported receiving no education about sexual violence from their parents [49].

According to the Social Ecological Model (SEM), sexual harassment can be analyzed across multiple levels. At the individual level, victims may lack awareness or confidence to respond; at the interpersonal level, power imbalances between students and supervisors can create environments of vulnerability. At the organizational level, weak enforcement of anti-harassment policies further enables such behavior. Finally, societal-level norms, including cultural silence around gender-based issues, can discourage victims from speaking out. Recognizing these interconnected levels provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to sexual harassment and suggests that interventions must address each of these domains to be effective. The participants suggested various measures to reduce the risk of sexual harassment, including conducting educational sessions before the start of their training and enforcing strict laws to prevent such incidents. These findings align with those of [21, 22], who proposed introducing training programs to prevent sexual harassment and creating a systematic reporting system. E-books were one of the innovative methods that advanced nursing students’ knowledge about sexual harassment and enhanced their coping skills [24].

Recommendations

A multidimensional response—combining education, policy enforcement, and research—is essential to creating safer and more equitable environments for nursing students. There is a need for robust institutional mechanisms to integrate comprehensive sexual harassment education into the nursing curriculum. Training should also cover legal and ethical responsibilities and include strategies to empower students to act when they witness misconduct. Realistic clinical scenarios can help students practice appropriate responses and build confidence in managing such situations.

Policymakers implement clear policies that reflect a zero-tolerance approach to sexual harassment. These policies should be well-publicized, easily accessible, and consistently applied to all members of the academic and clinical community, including faculty and preceptors. Transparent and confidential reporting mechanisms must be established, along with assurances that students can report incidents without fear of retaliation. Policies should be regularly reviewed and updated based on student feedback and emerging best practices.

Support services are crucial for students who face harassment. Institutions should offer access to counseling and psychological services, as well as trained advocates who can help students navigate the reporting process and utilize available resources. Peer support programs can also provide valuable guidance and alleviate feelings of isolation.

Additionally, there is a need for further research to examine the context in which these behaviors occur, the profiles of individuals most at risk, and the organizational or cultural factors that may enable such conduct. Longitudinal studies could also be valuable in assessing the impact of sexual coercion on mental health, academic or job performance, and retention, particularly in hierarchical environments such as clinical settings and universities.

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths of this study lies in its originality. As the first known research to specifically examine the issue of sexual harassment among nursing students within this context, it addresses a critical and often overlooked area of student well-being. Additionally, the study contributes valuable insights by capturing the voices and lived experiences of female nursing students, shedding light on the forms of harassment encountered and the reasons behind underreporting. This depth of understanding not only fills a gap in existing literature but also provides a foundation for future research, policy development, and targeted interventions aimed at creating safer learning environments for nursing students.

This study has several limitations. First, the ability to generalize the findings is constrained because male students were excluded. Second, conducting interviews with participants proved challenging due to the sensitive nature of the topic. As a result, open-ended questions were included at the end of the questionnaire to allow participants to share additional insights. Given the significance of the issue, further research involving additional interviews would yield more accurate and comprehensive information on the topic of sexual harassment.

Conclusions

The findings of this study reveal that sexual harassment remains a significant concern within the nursing student community. The experiences shared point to a pattern of discomfort and boundary violations that often go unreported due to emotional, social, and informational barriers. These challenges highlight the urgent need for institutional action.

Participants emphasized the importance of proactive measures to address this issue, particularly through education and awareness. Creating a supportive environment where students feel empowered to speak up and access resources is critical. By implementing comprehensive prevention strategies and fostering a culture of accountability, educational institutions can take meaningful steps toward reducing harassment and promoting student well-being.
