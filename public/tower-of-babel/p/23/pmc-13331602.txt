Tracking Spatiotemporal Extracellular Matrix Evolution and Tissue Fusion in 3D Microtissues via Click Chemistry‐Based Metabolic Labelling
Authors: Theresa Koenig, F Max Yavitt, Laura Veenendaal, Tim B F Woodfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe extracellular matrix (ECM) is a complex network of macromolecules that forms the cellular microenvironment. Proper regulation of ECM formation is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis, and understanding the intricate processes involved in tissue growth, maturation, and degeneration is vital for developing functional regenerative solutions. However, investigating large‐scale ECM dynamics within cell‐dense engineered 3‐dimensional (3D) tissues remains a challenge. To address this, we introduce a technique to metabolically label nascent proteins in 3D cartilage microtissues and track the spatiotemporal evolution of ECM. Non‐canonical amino acids (L‐azidohomoalanine) are incorporated into newly secreted proteins and fluorescently labeled, enabling visualization of the nascent matrix. Labeling cartilage microtissues at early (Day 1), mid (Day 7) or late (Day 14) time points during microtissue formation revealed increased nascent protein deposition at the microtissue periphery. Comparing the protein secretion over time, we observed significant protein deposition at late times compared to minimal ECM formation at early time points. Furthermore, protein deposition during microtissue fusion was studied using a 3D bioassembly model, enabling tracking of tissue integration over time. Ultimately, this approach is a powerful tool for uncovering mechanisms involved in tissue formation in dynamic 3D microenvironments at a clinically relevant scale, with potential implications for new regenerative strategies.Keywords: 3D in‐vitro models, cartilage, metabolic labeling, nascent protein deposition, spheroids

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) plays an essential role in the formation and maintenance of healthy tissue. Subsequently, proper regulation of ECM and cell‐matrix interactions is important for maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing disease [1, 2, 3]. To develop robust tissue regeneration strategies, it is therefore crucial to fully understand the complex biological processes that contribute to healthy tissue formation and disease progression [4, 5]. However, common quantitative and qualitative techniques to analyze ECM formation often lack spatial resolution or temporal information. For example, biochemical assays and gene expression profiling provide quantitative bulk data but lack spatial information that is especially important in large, 3‐dimensional (3D) constructs [6]. Meanwhile, histological and immunohistochemical techniques offer qualitative insights into matrix formation but lack temporal information and typically only reveal the state at the experimental endpoint.

Recently, metabolic labeling was reported as a promising approach that enables a combination of spatial and temporal analysis of ECM formation on a single‐cell level [7, 8]. Here, non‐canonical amino acids containing biorthogonal functional groups are added to media in discrete timeframes and metabolized by cells to incorporate into newly secreted proteins, including ECM components. The presence of the biorthogonal functional group permits fluorescent labeling to visualize the spatial distribution of nascent matrix deposition. Importantly, only proteins that include the non‐canonical amino acids are visualized, enabling insight into the temporal dynamics of ECM formation. Metabolic labeling approaches were previously used in hydrogel films [7, 9] and 3D hydrogels [8, 10] to study the spatiotemporal ECM deposition in single cells and the impact of early protein deposition on cellular behavior. Moving beyond single‐cell analysis, Eiken et al. [11] applied the method in alveolar organoids to assess nascent matrix secretion in developed organoid culture models. However, little is known about spatiotemporal matrix deposition and development in high‐cell‐density 3D tissues.

3D culture systems, such as microtissue spheroids and organoid culture, allow for complex, multicellular interactions in native‐like microenvironments, and thus provide a widely adopted model platform to study cell‐matrix dynamics, drug interactions, and disease models in 3D, physiologically relevant, and high‐throughput environments [12]. Furthermore, modular bioassembly of these cell‐dense units allows for precise control over 3D tissue architecture, while maintaining physiological cellular microenvironments [13]. However, successful biofabrication of large‐scale tissues from modular microtissue units requires microscale fusion events involving cell migration and cohesive ECM integration [14, 15]. Therefore, understanding interactions between tissue modules is essential for producing scalable human tissues, and yet, our understanding of these specific tissue fusion events and mechanisms of ECM formation are not well‐established and poorly investigated. The ability to monitor tissue maturation and integration spatiotemporally within 3D constructs is increasingly recognized as a key bottleneck in large‐scale tissue engineering, biofabrication, and 3D in vitro spheroid and organoid models [14, 16, 17]. As such, metabolic labeling of nascent ECM formation is a promising approach to investigate the spatial and temporal development of tissue growth and matrix deposition across microtissues and during tissue fusion. By monitoring and quantifying tissue development over time using metabolic labeling as a complementary methodology, new knowledge on the mechanisms of spatiotemporal tissue formation and tissue fusion can be generated.

Specifically, cartilage microtissues provide an ideal platform to recreate the cartilage tissue microenvironment. Chondrocytes are aggregated in high densities, resembling cell condensation mechanisms in early‐stage cartilage development seen in vivo [18], and begin to deposit ECM proteins, including collagens and proteoglycans, which form a structurally organized network. Initially, cartilage ECM is dominated by the presence of collagen I and is low in GAG content. However, as chondrocytes mature, the ECM becomes enriched in collagen II and GAGs [19, 20], representing a hyaline cartilage phenotype. The changes in ECM composition in collagen II and GAG occur rapidly during development and are necessary to build and maintain healthy cartilage tissue [21, 22], and thus, it is important to understand the spatiotemporal dynamics of ECM formation in 3D to develop regenerative therapies.

In this study, we aim to investigate tissue growth and matrix deposition in a spatiotemporal manner using a click chemistry‐based metabolic labeling approach in 3D cartilage microtissue spheroids as a model. Given the difficulty of tracking ECM development in large, cell‐dense 3D cultures and scaffolds with conventional analysis methods, we adopted and modified a previously reported metabolic labeling approach [8] to identify the spatial and temporal dynamics of nascent protein deposition in engineered 3D cartilage microtissues. The emphasis is on the implementation of metabolic labeling as a complementary tool to overcome analytical limitations associated with large, cell‐dense 3D constructs, enabling the spatiotemporal visualization of newly synthesized extracellular matrix and the monitoring of tissue development over time. Human‐derived articular chondrocytes were aggregated into microtissue spheroids and allowed to grow in cell culture media supplemented with the non‐canonical amino acid, L‐azidohomoalanine (AHA), which contains biorthogonal azide functional groups. AHA is metabolized by the chondrocytes and incorporated into newly secreted ECM components. Microtissues containing reactive azide groups were labeled with a dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) functionalized fluorophore, which reacts spontaneously through a strain‐promoted azide alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) reaction to visualize nascent matrix deposition (Figure 1). We systematically investigated the spatial and temporal matrix evolution in cartilage microtissues to understand large‐scale protein deposition. We found spatial differences across 3D microtissues and revealed insights into the onset of protein secretion and the dynamics of temporal protein deposition. Revealing information on large‐scale 3D ECM formation using this novel metabolic labeling technique provides a new approach to understanding the complex processes involved in 3D tissue formation and development in vitro.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

Proteinase K, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt hydrate (Di‐sodium‐EDTA), 1,9‐dimethyl‐methylene blue zinc chloride double salt (DMMB), chondroitin‐4‐sulfate, glycine, hyaluronidase, sodium chloride (NaCl), phosphate buffered saline (PBS), ITS+ media supplement (100x), L‐proline, L‐ascorbic acid‐2‐phosphate sesquimagnesium salt (AsAp), L‐methionine, L‐cysteine, dexamethasone, 10% neutral buffered formalin, fast green, safranin‐O, Triton X‐100 and hydrochloric acid (37%) were all purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St Louis, MO). DNAse free RNAse A, Gibco 4‐(‐2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), Gibco non‐essential amino acids (NEAA), penicillin‐streptomycin (containing 10.000 units mL−1 penicillin and 10.000 µg mL−1 streptomycin), Gibco fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) high‐glucose glutaMAX media (10569‐010), Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) high‐glucose—no glutamine, no methionine, no cystine media (21013024), sodium pyruvate, glutaMAX supplement, bovine serum albumin (BSA), CyQuant cell proliferation assay kit, Click‐iT Plus TUNEL assay kit, 0.25% trypsin/EDTA, AlamarBlue, 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (D1306, DAPI), goat‐anti‐mouse secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488), HCS CellMask stain and Prolong glass antifade mountant were all obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Auckland, NZ). Transforming growth factor beta‐1 (TGF‐β1) was purchased from R&D systems (Mineapolis, USA). Di‐sodium hydrogen phosphate (NA2HPO4), Acetic acid (glacial, 100%), Gill's haemotoxylin, and 96% ethanol were purchased from Merck Life Science (Auckland, NZ). Type II collagenase was purchased from Worthington (Lakewood, USA). L‐Azidohomoalanine and AZDye 488 DBCO were purchased from Vector Laboratories (Newark, CA, USA). Biosystems Tissue freezing medium (Ref: 14 020 108 926) was purchased from Leica (Mt Waverley, VIC, Australia). Donkey‐anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 594) and primary antibodies for collagen I (Ab34710) were purchased from Abcam (Melbourne, Australia). Primary antibodies for collagen II (II‐II6B3‐C) were purchased from DSHB (Iowa City, USA).

Cell Isolation, Expansion, and Microtissue Formation

Human articular chondrocytes (hACs) were isolated from macroscopically healthy regions of the articular cartilage from consenting patients undergoing anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committees, URB/07/04/014/ AM02). The tissue was cut in 0.5–1 mm pieces and digested in chondrogenic expansion media (Gibco DMEM high glucose glutaMAX supplemented with 0.4 mm L‐Proline, 10 mm HEPES, 0.1 mm NEAA, 100 U mL−1 penicillin, 0.1 mg mL−1 streptomycin, 0.1 mm AsAp and 10% FBS) supplemented with 0.15% type II collagenase at 37°C in an incubator (5% CO2) for 16–20 h, as previously described [23, 24]. The solution was filtered through a cell strainer (100 µm). Isolated hACs were expanded (3.000 cells/cm2) into high‐density monolayers, cultured in chondrogenic expansion media, and collected for the experiments at passage 3. High‐density spherical microtissues were formed from human articular chondrocytes using a high‐throughput 96‐well plate centrifugation process, described previously [25]. In brief, hACs suspended in chondrogenic expansion media were seeded in v‐bottom 96‐well plates at a concentration of 250,000 cells/well. Well plates were centrifuged at 200 g for 4 min to allow cellular condensation and incubated overnight (37°C, 5% CO2, 5% O2). The next day, the cell clusters were gently detached from the well bottom by pipetting.

Microtissue Culture and Nascent Protein Labeling

To optimize the metabolic labeling approach in 3D microtissues, spheroids were cultured for 10 days (37°C, 5% CO2, 5% O2) in methionine‐free (MF) chondrogenic differentiation media (Gibco DMEM high‐glucose, no glutamine, no methionine, no cystine media supplemented with 0.4 mm l‐proline, 10 mm HEPES, 0.1 mm NEAA, 100 U mL−1 penicillin, 0.1 mg mL−1 streptomycin, 0.2 mm AsAp, 10 µL mL−1 ITS+, 1.25 mg mL−1 BSA, 0.1 µm dexamethasone, and 10 ng mL−1 TGF‐β1), refreshed every other day. During the microtissue growth over 10 days, newly synthesized proteins were fluorescently labeled using a click chemistry approach. The media was supplemented with either 50 or 100 µm of the non‐canonical amino acid, L‐azidohomoalanine (AHA) for varying lengths of time (1, 3, 7, or 10 days). After 10 days, AHA‐incorporated microtissues were stained with a dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO, 30 µm) functionalized fluorophore in MF chondrogenic differentiation media for 4 h to fluorescently label nascent proteins. Regular chondrogenic differentiation media (Gibco DMEM high‐glucose glutaMAX media supplemented with 0.4 mm l‐proline, 10 mm HEPES, 0.1 mm NEAA, 100 U mL−1 penicillin, 0.1 mg mL−1 streptomycin, 0.2 mm AsAp, 10 µL mL−1 ITS+, 1.25 mg mL−1 BSA, 0.1 µm dexamethasone, and 10 ng mL−1 TGFβ‐1) and MF chondrogenic differentiation media with the addition of 100 µm l‐methionine (MET) and without AHA supplementation served as a negative control. To investigate spatiotemporal ECM deposition, microtissues were cultured for 14 days (37°C, 5% CO2, 5% O2) in MF chondrogenic differentiation media, refreshed every other day. The microtissues were treated with 100 µm AHA for 24 h during the first, the seventh or the last day of culture over 14 days. After 14 days the microtissues were collected and incubated in DBCO solution (30 µm) for 4 h. MF chondrogenic differentiation media with addition of 100 µm MET and without AHA supplementation served as a negative control.

Scaffold Fabrication

To study ECM formation during tissue fusion over time, two microtissue building blocks were bioassembled in 3D‐printed polycaprolactone (PCL) cages. Using an R‐GEN 200 (RegenHU, Switzerland), PCL polymer was heated to 100°C and extruded in a bi‐layered fashion under 500 kPa pressure using nitrogen gas, through a 0.3 mm nozzle. Printing offset was set to 0.35 mm and fiber spacing was measured at 1.4 mm in X‐direction, 2.8 mm in Y‐direction, and 1 mm apart in Z‐direction with fiber diameters measuring 0.2 mm. The fiber spacing can be adjusted to suit specific needs of the bioassembly model, tailored to various sizes of spheroids to achieve repeatability and to prevent spheroids from moving or falling out, allowing for long‐term retention of the spheres within the fusion boxes, as previously detailed [6, 13]. In this study, the length of each pore within the scaffolds was 2.8 mm, suitable for spheroids of ≈1.4 mm in diameter. Fabricated 3D scaffolds were sterilized overnight in 70% EtOH. Scaffolds were then washed three times with PBS prior to use.

3D‐Bioassembly of Tissue Fusion Construct and Fusion of Microtissues

A 1 mL adjustable pipette was used to carefully transfer precultured (10‐day) spheroids from well‐plates to 3D printed scaffolds. Two spheroids were press‐fitted into the fabricated PCL cage and cultured for 14 more days to facilitate tissue fusion. Each construct was inspected under a brightfield microscope following assembly to ensure adjacent and close contact for all samples. Throughout the culture period, digital images of spheroids were captured with a brightfield microscope (Olympus CKX41, Japan). During spheroid fusion, microtissues were treated with 100 µm AHA for 24 h each on Days 1, 3, 7, and 14 to assess ECM formation over time during microtissue fusion.

Biochemical Analysis

Microtissues were collected, washed three times in PBS, and digested at 56°C in 1 mg mL−1 proteinase K, dissolved in 10 mm Tris‐HCl and 1 mm disodium EDTA solution. For glycosaminoglycan (GAG) quantification, samples were diluted 5–20 times and allowed to react with DMMB dye (16 µg mL−1, pH 3). The absorbance of the solution was measured in triplicates immediately after DMMB addition at 520 nm with a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Varioskan Flash Multimode Reader). Chondroitin sulphate was used to generate a GAG standard curve to calculate the total GAG content. Total DNA content was determined using a CyQUANT Cell Proliferation Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions and as described previously [23, 25]. Prior to analysis, samples were treated with DNase‐free RNase A (135 Kunitz units mL−1) for 1 h to eliminate cellular RNA. Fluorescence of the final solution was measured in duplicates with the spectrophotometer at 480/520 nm (excitation/emission). A DNA standard curve was used to calculate total DNA content.

Gel Electrophoresis

SDS‐PAGE followed by fluorescence detection was performed to detect covalent incorporation of DBCO dye into AHA‐labeled nascent proteins. Therefore, 3D spheroids were cultured for 10 days (37°C, 5% CO2, 5% O2) in methionine‐free (MF) chondrogenic differentiation media with supplementation of 100 µm L‐azidohomoalanine (AHA) for the last 24 h of culture. After 10 days AHA‐incorporated microtissues were stained with a dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO, 30 µm) functionalized fluorophore for 4 h to fluorescently label nascent proteins. Covalent incorporation of DBCO dye into AHA‐labeled nascent proteins was evaluated by SDS–PAGE followed by fluorescence detection. For each condition, two cartilage spheroids were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and briefly homogenized utilizing a pestle, followed by lysing in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and benzonase. Approximately 7 or 3.5 µg of microtissue lysate was mixed with 4× Laemmli sample buffer. To assess the effect of protein denaturation, paired boiled and unboiled samples were prepared, with boiled samples heated to 95 °C for 5 min prior to electrophoresis. Samples were resolved on Criterion TGX Stain‐Free precast gels (4%–15%) at 180 V for 45 min. Fluorescence of the incorporated substrate was imaged at 488 nm using an iBright FL1500 imaging system. Precision Plus Protein standards (Bio‐Rad) were visualized at 488 and 647 nm (Figure S1).

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis

Microtissues were washed three times in PBS, fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 1 h at room temperature (RT), and washed in 0.3 m glycine in PBS and an additional two times in PBS. Samples were incubated in tissue freezing medium at 4°C overnight and cryo‐sectioned into 30 µm slices using a Leica Biosystems cryostat microtome (CM1860). Cartilaginous tissue formation was evaluated by visualizing GAGs and collagen types I and II. The presence of GAGs was visualized with Safranin‐O staining. Tissue sections were stained in Gill's haematoxylin for 4 min, fast green solution (0.005%) for 3 min, and safranin‐O solution (0.1% w/v) for 3.5 min, to visualize cell nuclei, collagens, and GAGs. Collagen types I and II were stained with immunohistochemical techniques. Sections were first treated with 1% hyaluronidase for 30 min at 37°C, washed in PBS, and blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 min at RT. Samples were incubated in primary antibodies for collagen type I (1:230) and collagen type II (1:48) diluted in blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. After washing three times in blocking buffer, donkey‐anti‐rabbit (Alexa Fluor 594) and goat‐anti‐mouse (Alexa Fluor 488) secondary antibodies, diluted1:400 in blocking buffer, were applied in the dark for 1 h at RT. Microtissue sections were co‐stained with HCS CellMask to visualize the cell plasma membrane. Tissue sections were permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 15 min at RT and washed three times in PBS. HSC CellMask (1:5000 in PBS) was applied in the dark for 30 min at RT. After collagen type I and II and cell plasma staining, sections were washed three times in PBS and incubated in DAPI solution (1:1000 in PBS) in the dark for 2 min at RT. Samples were washed a further three times in PBS, and all histological and immunohistochemical stained sections were imaged using a Zeiss Axioimager Z1 microscope.

TUNEL Assay

To assess apoptotic cells throughout the microtissue depth, a TUNEL assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Click‐iT Plus TUNEL Assay Kit). In brief, tissue sections (30 µm) were fixed in 10% formalin and permeabilized with Proteinase K for 15 min. Tissue sections treated with DNase to induce TUNEL‐positive DNA strand breaks served as a positive control. Sections were incubated in TdT reaction buffer and TdT reaction mixture for 10 min and 60 min, respectively, at 37°C. The Click‐iT Plus reaction was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions and total cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS) for 2 min. Stained tissue sections were imaged using a Zeiss Axioimager Z1 microscope.

Image Analysis and Nascent Protein Quantification

All image quantification was performed using ImageJ software (v1.54f). The microtissue size was determined by measuring the area at the midsection of each microtissue. The fluorescence intensity of collagen types I and II was quantified by measuring the mean gray value. A region without any cells was selected and used to measure the mean gray value of the background. The value of the background was then subtracted from the gray value for each microtissue. The collagen II/I ratio was calculated by dividing the corrected mean gray values of collagen type II by collagen type I. To quantify nascent extracellular proteins, the fluorescence signal of the cells (stained with CellMask) was subtracted from the nascent protein stain in all images (Figure S2). The radial intensity was measured using an ImageJ line‐scan function across the mid‐section of microtissues (n = 3 line scans in each microtissue, n = 3 microtissues per condition) and normalized to the microtissue radii. In each microtissue section, 10 regions of interest (ROIs) of equal size were defined, spanning from the core to the periphery of the microtissue. In each ROI, the mean gray value of the nascent protein stain was measured, cells were counted, and the mean gray value was normalized to the cell number to adjust nascent protein deposition to cell density. Two areas of interest were defined to quantify temporal extracellular protein evolution, at both the centre and the periphery of the microtissue (Figure S3). The mean gray value was measured at 6 different locations (n = 3 in the centre and n = 3 in the periphery) in each microtissue (n = 3 microtissues for each experimental condition). For nascent protein quantification during microtissue fusion, the fusion region was defined for each fusion construct. Colocalization of nascent proteins and collagen was performed using the ImageJ function ‘Colocalization Threshold’. The colocalization was quantified by reporting the Pearson's correlation coefficient and visualized in the colocalized pixel map and scatter plot for each sample.

Statistical Analysis

All studies were performed in triplicates for each experimental condition (n = 3). GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 software was used for all data analysis and graph plotting. Data outliers were identified using the ROUT method (Q = 1%). Differences between the two experimental groups were determined using unpaired t‐tests. Significance between more than two groups were assessed using one‐way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey testing when a Gaussian distribution of residuals and equal variance were assumed (according to the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality of residuals and the Brown–Forsythe test, respectively). Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests were performed when the variance was significantly different (p < 0.05). Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparison test were used when the normality test was not passed (alpha = 0.05). All values are reported as the mean and standard deviation of the mean. Significance between groups is indicated in figures with * when p < 0.05.

Results

Cartilage Microtissue Formation and Growth

First, cartilage microtissue formation was analyzed to understand how cartilage ECM develops within microtissues. Human‐derived articular chondrocytes were aggregated into spheroids and allowed to grow over 28 days in chondrogenic differentiation media. Samples were collected on Days 3, 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28, and stained for Safranin‐O (Figure 2A), and collagen type I and II (Figure 2B) to visualize cartilage‐specific ECM components. Visible differences were observed in Safranin‐O‐stained sections within the first 10 days of chondrogenic culture. Microtissues lacked GAG staining after 3 days of chondrogenic differentiation but showed positive red staining at the 10‐day timepoint indicating greater glycosaminoglycan (GAG) deposition, consistent with previous studies in 3D microtissues using human articular chondrocytes [6, 26]. The average microtissue diameter increased monotonically between the 3‐ and 28‐day timepoints (Figure 2C), corresponding to a similar increase in GAG deposition (Figure 2D). Interestingly, the quantitative analysis of DNA content revealed no significant differences in cellular DNA between the 3‐day (1.5 ± 0.2 µg) and the 28‐day timepoints (1.8 ± 0.2 µg), indicating that the initial number of chondrocytes (250,000 cell/microtissue) stayed constant, and no significant proliferation occurred (Figure 2E). GAG deposition was normalized to the DNA content (GAG/DNA), further demonstrating a significant increase in GAG‐rich matrix formation as a function of time (Figure 2F). IHC staining of collagen type I and collagen type II was performed and quantified to evaluate the quality of the cartilage matrix deposited by hACs. The collagen II to collagen I (Col II/I) ratio increased significantly after 10 days of cartilage microtissue formation, indicating the formation of hyaline collagen II‐rich cartilage matrix (Figure 2G). These findings demonstrate that microtissues undergo growth and deposit hyaline cartilage when cultured in chondrogenic differentiation media for four weeks. Furthermore, viability assays revealed minimal apoptotic cell presence throughout the spheroids at both 14 and 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation, with no significant increase in apoptosis over time, indicating sustained cell viability (Figure S4). The uniform distribution of GAGs and collagens across both the core and periphery of the constructs further suggests active ECM production, supporting the conclusion that cells remain viable and metabolically active throughout the entire spheroid, with no evidence of reduced viability in the core of spheroids. Notably, the most substantial changes occur within the first 10 days of differentiation, with collagen II‐rich cartilage formation observed after 10 days. Therefore, it was valuable in this study to focus on the initial 10 days of microtissue development for a more detailed understanding of early cartilage matrix deposition.

AHA Affects Microtissue Development in a Time‐ and Dose‐Dependent Manner

To apply the metabolic labeling approach in 3D microtissues, it is necessary to first investigate the effect of AHA treatment on the formation, growth, and chondrogenic differentiation of cartilage microtissues. Therefore, we sought to determine the optimal concentration and pulse duration of AHA to maintain microtissue health, while importantly retaining a sufficient fluorescence signal of nascent proteins. Microtissues were formed over 10 days in methionine‐free (MF) differentiation media with the addition of either 100 µm AHA or 50 µm AHA + 50 µm methionine (MET). These concentrations were chosen to be consistent with the other nascent protein labeling methodologies described in the literature [8, 9]. AHA treatment was pulsed for 1, 3, 7, or 10 days at different time points during the 10‐day culture period (Figure 3A). Prior to fixation, AHA‐treated microtissues were stained with a DBCO functionalized fluorophore to label any AHA incorporated into nascent deposited proteins. A comparison of staining procedures revealed that DBCO‐fluorophore labeling was not affected by diffusion limitations (Figure S5). Furthermore, covalent incorporation of DBCO into AHA‐labeled proteins was confirmed by SDS‐PAGE followed by fluorescence detection (Figure S1). The deposition of nascent proteins was visualized using the previously described metabolic labeling technique and analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively to evaluate the time‐ and dose‐dependent changes in nascent protein expression and the effect on microtissue health and cartilage formation. The fluorescence signal increased with AHA incubation time, with a 2.3‐fold increase for a 3‐day AHA treatment, a 5.6‐fold increase for a 7‐day treatment, and a 14.5‐fold increase for a 10‐day treatment compared to a 1‐day incubation (Figure 3B and Figure 3C). This increase in the mean gray value demonstrated the time‐dependent metabolic incorporation of AHA into newly secreted ECM. A similar trend was observed when microtissues were treated with 50 µm AHA, with a continuous increase of the nascent protein expression as the AHA incubation time extended (Figure S6A,B). Notably, a significantly lower staining intensity was observed with 50 µm AHA compared to a treatment with 100 µm AHA. These results indicate that both AHA concentrations are sufficient for visualizing and quantifying nascent protein deposition, though the 100 µm AHA concentration provides more distinct and quantifiable differences. To assess microtissue health with AHA supplementation, GAG matrix deposition was visualized using Safranin‐O staining and quantified (Figure 3D). For treatment times longer than 1 day, microtissues treated with 100 µm AHA showed a significant decrease in GAGs with increasing AHA treatment time. Notably however, a 1‐day pulse of AHA did not significantly affect GAG content (22.8 ± 1.2 µg) compared to the non‐treated control (25.3 ± 2.3 µg). Furthermore, when GAG deposition was normalized to the DNA content, a significant decrease in GAG‐rich matrix as a function of time was observed in microtissues treated for 3 days or longer (Figure 3F). Microtissue size also decreased with AHA incubation time, with the mean area reducing by 65% following 10 days of 100 µm AHA treatment (Figure 3G). Interestingly, AHA supplementation for 1 day did not significantly affect GAG deposition, independent of the time point when the pulse was administered (Figure 3H). Overall, these results highlight that a 1‐day pulse with AHA is sufficient to label nascent protein deposition in cartilage microtissues without any significant effect on cartilage formation capacity.

To mitigate the observed decrease in GAG deposition and microtissue size in response to prolonged treatments of 100 µm AHA, microtissues were instead treated with a 50:50 combination of 50 µm AHA and 50 µm MET. Microtissues treated with this 50:50 AHA:MET combination maintained GAG‐rich matrix deposition for up to 10 days of AHA incubation, with no significant differences in cartilage formation observed across different pulse durations (Figure S6C,D). To further validate the quality of the deposited matrix, immunofluorescence staining of collagen type I and II was performed on microtissues treated with 100 µm AHA or 50 µm AHA /50 µm MET for 10 days (Figure S7A). Quantification of the collagen type II/I ratio revealed a significant decrease for microtissues treated with 100 µm AHA for 10 days, indicating decreased deposition of healthy hyaline cartilage matrix (Figure S7B). Notably, hyaline cartilage formation similar to AHA‐free culture was achieved in microtissues treated with the 50:50 ratio of 50 µm AHA and 50 µm MET for 10 days. These results highlight the ability of the 50:50 AHA:MET mixture to facilitate nascent protein labeling over long time periods without compromising microtissue quality. Overall, as we were interested in early ECM deposition, treatment with 100 µm AHA for 1 day was sufficient to identify rapid changes in nascent protein deposition throughout the remainder of the study.

Spatial Protein Deposition in Microtissues

Following the application of the metabolic labeling technique to cell‐dense 3D microtissues, we aimed to investigate the spatial deposition of proteins and the distribution of newly synthesized ECM within microtissues. Toward this goal, microtissues were cultured for 14 days in MF chondrogenic differentiation media, with the addition of 100 µm AHA on the final day of culture (Figure 4A). To quantify and differentiate the nascent extracellular proteins from intracellular proteins, the fluorescence signal of the cellular boundary was subtracted from the nascent protein stain in all images prior to analysis (Figure S2). Initially, we sought to assess the distribution of chondrocytes and newly secreted proteins throughout the microtissue. Staining for nascent proteins revealed widespread protein deposition across the microtissue sections, with a noticeable increase in staining intensity at the periphery, suggesting enhanced protein secretion at the microtissue edge (Figure 4B,C). Quantitative analysis of the intensity distribution corroborated these observations, showing a consistent level of protein expression across the microtissue radius, with nascent protein expression peaking at the periphery. Notably, the distribution of cell nuclei remained uniform and was not significantly different across the radius of microtissues (Figure 4D and Figure S8). The mean fluorescence intensity of the nascent protein stain was normalized to cell nuclei (cell number) to assess protein secretion per chondrocyte. This analysis revealed that, while nascent protein secretion per cell remained consistent with increasing distance from the centre, there was a 3.3‐fold upregulation of protein deposition at the periphery. We hypothesize that this increased protein expression is not attributable to a higher density of active cells in the periphery, but rather to greater cellular activity in this region (Figure 4E). These findings highlight that nascent protein deposition significantly increases at the microtissue periphery. Moreover, there were no detectable differences in protein expression across the microtissue radius, except at the periphery. Therefore, subsequent analyses will focus on comparing the nascent protein evolution in the centre and periphery of the microtissues.

Temporal ECM Evolution During Microtissue Growth

To investigate temporal changes in ECM evolution during microtissue development, microtissues were cultured for 14 days in MF chondrogenic differentiation media, with the addition of 100 µm AHA for 1 day at early‐ (Day 1–2), mid‐ (Day 7–8), and late‐ (Day 13–14) time‐points (Figure 5A). When comparing protein deposition over time, a noticeable reduction in ECM formation was observed during the early phase of microtissue development, as compared to the mid and late time‐points, with no significant differences between the mid and late stages (Figure 5B). Quantification of nascent extracellular protein staining revealed a progressive increase in protein deposition at the microtissue centre over time. ECM formation was nearly absent within the first 24 h, followed by a significant increase between the early and mid/late time‐points. Notably, a 1.6‐fold increase in fluorescence intensity was observed between 7‐ and 14‐days time‐points (Figure 5C). In the periphery, a similar trend can be observed, with minimal extracellular protein deposition during the first 24 h, followed by an 11‐fold increase at the 7‐day time‐point. Interestingly, although protein secretion remained high at Day 14, it was decreased by 30% compared to the 7‐day time‐point (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that while ECM formation in the periphery remains active, its relative activity shifts downward as the spheroid develops.

Tracking of Tissue Formation During Microtissue Fusion

Following the analysis of spatial and temporal protein deposition during microtissue formation, we applied the metabolic labeling approach to monitor newly synthesized proteins during the fusion of 3D microtissues. Two precultured (10 days) microtissue building blocks were assembled into 3D‐printed polycaprolactone (PCL) cages and cultured for an additional 14 days to facilitate tissue fusion. During spheroid fusion, microtissues were treated with 100 µm AHA for 24 h each on Days 1, 7, and 14 of fusion to assess ECM formation over time (Figure 6A). Microtissues treated with AHA on Day 14 of fusion were used to visualize the fusion region, which showed a distinct boundary that indicates minimal chondrocyte migration into the fusion region (Figure 6B). Despite the lack of migration, nascent protein staining revealed widespread protein deposition across both microtissues and within the fusion zone.

Nascent protein tracking was then used to characterize the dynamics of tissue fusion over time. Newly formed proteins in the fusion region were visualized on Day 1 (Figure 6C) and Day 7 (Figure 6D) of fusion and compared with total collagen type I (red) and collagen type II (magenta) deposition after 14 days of fusion. Colocalization between nascent proteins and collagen I or II was quantified on Days 1 and 7. On Day 1, minimal protein formation was detected, occurring primarily at the microtissue periphery, with little new ECM in the interstitial space between microtissues (Figure 6C). Colocalization analysis showed a significantly stronger association of newly formed proteins with type II collagen, as indicated by the greater number of white pixels in the colocalized pixel map (Figure 6E) and a higher Person's correlation coefficient (Figure 6G). Consistent with this, collagen I was largely absent from the fusion region, with scatterplot intensities clustering near the x‐axis (nascent proteins) rather than the y‐axis (collagen I). In contrast, scatterplots for nascent proteins versus collagen II showed intensities shifted toward the y‐axis, reflecting a stronger collagen II fluorescence signal (Figure 6E). On Day 7 of microtissue fusion, a substantial increase in nascent protein deposition was visible both within the microtissues and throughout the fusion region (Figure 6D). Colocalization patterns remained consistent with Day 1, showing a higher degree of overlap between nascent proteins and collagen II than with collagen I, as supported by the colocalization map (Figure 6F) and the Pearson's correlation coefficient (Figure 6G). The scatterplot of nascent proteins versus collagen II on Day 7 displayed a more central distribution of pixel intensities, indicating a higher overall intensity of nascent protein staining compared to Day 1 and thus increased protein deposition in the fusion region (Figure 6F). Comparing Day 1 to Day 7, both collagen I and collagen II exhibited higher colocalization with nascent proteins at the later time point, as evident by a higher Pearson's correlation coefficient on Day 7(Figure 6G).

Discussion

Human‐derived cartilage microtissue spheroids are important 3D diagnostic and therapeutic tools in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. For example, microtissues, as opposed to single cell suspensions, have been directly delivered to cartilage defect sites via injection or implantation [27, 28, 29], and they have also been used as modular building blocks in tissue engineering [13, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34] or as 3D model platforms for drug screening and disease modeling [35, 36, 37, 38]. Considering their wide applicability, it is important to thoroughly understand the spatiotemporal dynamics of ECM formation in microtissue spheroids. This study focuses on ECM development in cartilage spheroids as an example of a highly clinically relevant tissue model. Self‐assembled cartilage microtissues effectively replicate key events that occur during mesenchymal condensation and chondrogenesis in vivo, recapitulating chondrogenic differentiation and cartilage formation by synthesizing their own matrix [18, 39, 40, 41]. The assembly of cell‐rich modules into larger constructs offers a versatile strategy for engineering 3D tissues with controlled architecture, while preserving physiologically relevant cellular niches [13]. However, scaling these modular systems into functional tissues relies on microscale fusion mechanisms that involve active cell migration and coordinated ECM remodeling [14]. A deeper understanding of how individual tissue modules interact during these processes is therefore crucial for the advancement of scalable human tissue engineering. In this regard, metabolic labeling of newly produced ECM provides a valuable tool for probing the spatial and temporal progression of ECM development.

In this study, we applied metabolic labeling to track nascent protein deposition in 3D cartilage microtissues, allowing investigations into the spatiotemporal evolution of nascent matrix during microtissue development and fusion. Compared to conventional analysis methods, metabolic labeling of ECM molecules provides a powerful tool for observing both, spatial and temporal changes in matrix formation in an easy and tunable manner, while preserving ECM structure and integrity. This method enables precise time‐specific analysis that would otherwise be challenging and supports both long‐term in vitro cultures and in vivo applications. For example, Yoon et al. used a metabolic labeling approach to fluorescently label chondrocytes, which were subcutaneously implanted into mice and tracked in vivo for 4 weeks with minimal effects on cellular health and cartilage formation [42]. Metabolic labeling is widely applicable and can also be used to label other ECM components, such as nascent proteoglycans, by using modified sugars instead of amino acids [10]. Furthermore, this method has been applied to investigate nascent matrix deposition in various tissue types, including skeletal muscle stem cells [43] and alveolar organoids [11]. Although click chemistry has been used to label nascent ECM components secreted by individual cells and organoids embedded in hydrogels, it has not been applied to investigate larger‐scale ECM formation in complex high‐cell‐density microtissues. Especially for large, engineered 3D tissues and constructs, it is essential to track ECM formation and evolution at specific time points to gain insights into tissue maturation, regeneration, and degeneration. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first application of metabolic labeling in complex, high‐cell‐density 3D spheroid cultures. In contrast to existing literature [8], we observed that concentrations of 100 µm AHA applied for more than 1 day negatively impacted cartilage microtissue health and hyaline cartilage formation in 3D microtissues (Figure 3). Regardless, our results show that the concentration and incubation time of AHA can be easily adjusted to balance fluorescent signal strength and cellular health. 100 µm AHA can be used for treatment for up to 1 day without impairing the microtissue health, whereas a 50:50 ratio of AHA:MET supports healthy microtissue growth over extended periods of up to 10 days.

The analysis of nascent protein deposition in 3D microtissues revealed significant differences in matrix secretion between the centre and the periphery of microtissues, with cells at the periphery secreting nascent matrix more actively. This can be partly attributed to the diffusion limitations of oxygen and nutrients in the centre of the microtissue. For example, in large spheroids (>0.5 mm in diameter), it is well‐established that oxygen diffusion gradients influence cell metabolic activity and create heterogeneity in cell morphology and ECM deposition [44]. Bhattacharjee et al. described three distinct layers in mesenchymal stem cell microtissues after two weeks of chondrogenic differentiation: a surface layer composed of collagen I‐rich matrix and elongated cells, a middle layer containing round cells that produce types I, II, and X collagen along with chondroitin sulphate, and a necrotic centre marked by minimal or absent matrix, with apoptotic and/or dead cells [45]. Our results partially support these findings, where ECM deposition was increased at the periphery at all time points, with collagen I predominantly deposited at the periphery. Collagen II was synthesized primarily in the peripheral regions of microtissues within the first 10 days, but became more homogeneously distributed throughout the microtissue from Day 14. This relative shift in protein production within a single spheroid was visualized using nascent protein staining, providing detailed insights into the spatial onset of protein secretion across microtissues (Figure 5C,D). Moreover, we did not observe a necrotic core, but instead observed nascent ECM production by chondrocytes in the centre of the microtissue at various stages during a two‐week culture period (Figure 5B,C). The absence of a necrotic core could be due to the relatively short culture times used, or the formation of a specific cell niche. In cartilage, which is an avascular tissue, oxygen levels are naturally low (1%–5%) compared to other tissues in the body [46, 47]. Chondrocytes reside in these hypoxic conditions in vivo, and it is well‐established that low oxygen culture stimulates chondrogenic differentiation [47].

Metabolic labeling cannot only be used to identify spatial differences but also to reveal temporal dynamics. Visualizing the onset of nascent protein production, we observed minimal ECM deposition during the first 24 h of microtissue formation. This is consistent with typical 3D cartilage formation in vitro, where cell condensation occurs within the first 24 h, characterized by elevated cell‐cell contacts and low ECM deposition, mainly consisting of collagen type I, fibronectin, and various proteoglycans [40, 48]. As cells commit to chondrogenic differentiation, the production of collagen I decreases and shifts toward an increase of collagen type II and sulphated proteoglycans [18]. In this study, we showed increased collagen I deposition in microtissues within the first 3 days, followed by a time‐dependent increase in collagen II and GAG deposition, particularly from Day 10 onward, indicating the formation of healthy, cartilage‐specific matrix (Figure 2). The cellular DNA content of microtissues plateaued over 28 days of chondrogenic differentiation, indicating that the number of cells remained constant, and chondrocytes did not proliferate significantly during this time (Figure 2E). However, the size of microtissues increases significantly, suggesting that chondrocytes are synthesizing and depositing ECM while migrating away from each other.

The application of metabolic labeling during microtissue fusion in our 3D bioassembly model enables quantitative assessment of nascent matrix formation at the fusion interface over time. Understanding ECM dynamics during tissue fusion is crucial for engineering large‐scale 3D constructs, as successful fusion and the establishment of a cohesive ECM network are essential for recreating functional tissues at scale [14]. Metabolic labeling of newly synthesized proteins allows tracking of protein formation over time, providing insight into the onset and progression of tissue formation within larger constructs. In this study, we observed distinct temporal differences in matrix deposition during spheroid fusion, characterized by minimal early protein formation followed by a pronounced increase at later stages (Figure 6). Fusion capacity is influenced by multiple variables, including cell type. For example, Kosheleva et al. reported that epithelial spheroids fused more rapidly than mesenchymal spheroids, likely due to differences in ECM remodeling and cell migratory behavior [49]. The maturation state of spheroids also affects fusion outcomes. Spagnuolo et al. demonstrated that immature human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cell spheroids exhibited enhanced fusion and produced a superior cartilage‐like phenotype [50]. Embedding spheroids within a supporting matrix may further modulate fusion, as cell–matrix interactions can play a decisive role alongside cell type, differentiation status, and maturation. Ultimately, metabolic labeling within 3D spheroid systems complements traditional analytical methods, providing additional spatial and temporal resolution in highly dynamic microenvironments. In future studies, this approach can be further leveraged to dissect cell–cell and cell‐matrix interactions governing tissue fusion in biofabricated constructs. Furthermore, it can support identifying spatiotemporal changes in ECM development in diseased tissue models (such as in osteoarthritic spheroids) and examining the integration between engineered cartilage and native tissue, leading to new insights into cartilage ECM development, regeneration, and long‐term tissue integration.

Conclusions

We present an innovative metabolic labeling approach to study spatiotemporal matrix evolution in complex, multicellular 3D cartilage microtissues, which has not been achieved with conventional analytic methods. The ECM network serves as a crucial signal transmitter between cells and their microenvironment, playing a key role in regulating chondrogenesis and cartilage formation. Studying ECM deposition and evolution during cartilage development will reveal valuable information on tissue growth, maturation, and degeneration, leading to a better understanding of the complex processes involved in 3D tissue formation and development in vitro. Ultimately, understanding large‐scale ECM formation and matrix interactions in engineered 3D constructs will help to develop functional tissue‐engineered solutions for next‐generation regenerative medicine strategies.
