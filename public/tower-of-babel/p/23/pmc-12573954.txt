Comparative genomic analysis of hypervirulent group B streptococcus of ST12/serotype Ib suggests potential virulence factors
Authors: Kaixin Zhou, Zhewei Sun, Jingyong Sun, Xiaogang Xu, Ming Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGroup B streptococcus is a leading cause of neonatal invasive diseases. ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolate, the second most prevalent lineage in East Asia, has aroused increasing attention, as a growing body of reports suggests they may be more virulent and fatal than the ST17/serotype III strain.ResultsSag37, an ST12/serotype Ib isolate, was recovered from a fatal neonatal case and exhibited features suggestive of hypervirulence. To define this lineage’s prevalence and virulence, we analyzed 432 non-duplicate GBS isolates collected in Shanghai (2016 ~ 2022), and subtyped them by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and capsular serotyping via a multiplex polymerase chain reaction. MLST revealed 50 sequence types, with ST10 and ST19 being most common. Nine clonal complexes were identified using the eBURST program, dominated by CC12 and CC19. Among seven serotypes identified, Ib was most prevalent, followed by III, V and Ia. Guided by the epidemiological data, additional clinical ST12/serotype Ib isolates, along with representative strains from other STs/serotypes, were selected for virulence assessment. For survival, 8-week-old CD1 mice were challenged subcutaneously with 1 × 108 CFU and monitored for 7 days. ST12/serotype Ib isolates caused 100% mortality within 48 h, whereas other GBS subtypes resulted in delayed or no mortality. For the median lethal dose (LD50), mice received intraperitoneal doses from 103 to 108 CFU; logistic regression estimated LD50 values ranging from ≤ 103 to ≥ 108 CFU within ST12/serotype Ib, indicating marked intra-lineage heterogeneity. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the hypervirulence, whole-genome sequencing was conducted on 33 ST12/serotype Ib GBS strains identified herein. A pyseer-based genome-wide association study was performed using LD50 as a quantitative trait. Multiple genetic variations potentially related to virulence were identified, providing insights into the molecular basis of hypervirulence of ST12/serotype Ib GBS.ConclusionsThis study underscored the correlation between ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolates and hypervirulence, and further and revealed multiple candidate loci potentially associated with this phenotype. Background

Group B streptococcus (GBS; Streptococcus agalactiae) resides as a commensal of the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts of humans [1]; however, it has the capacity to cause severe invasive diseases in neonates during pregnancy and delivery [2, 3]. It also recognized as an opportunistic pathogen affecting the elderly, the immunocompromised, as well as healthy adults with an increasing incidence [4, 5]. Maternal screening and intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) have reduced the incidence of early-onset GBS disease (EOD, occurring within 7 days after birth); however, the rate of late-onset disease (LOD, occurring between 7 and 90 days) has remained unchanged.

Several methods have been described to characterize molecular epidemiology of GBS. The multilocus sequence typing (MLST) method was developed by identify allelic variations among housekeeping genes to determine the sequence types (STs) [6]. The capsular polysaccharide (CPS), a major virulence factor of GBS, also determines the serotype [1].

Numerous studies have highlighted the association between ST-17/serotype III and invasive neonatal disease [7–10], especially its close relationship with the onset of meningitis [11]. Meanwhile, the ST-12/serotype Ib GBS strain has gained attention due to its increasing incidence in East Asia, where it ranks second in predominance only to serotype III [7]. It accounts for 19.8 to 28.7% of invasive isolates collected from a mixed population of pregnant women, neonates, and nonpregnant adults [7, 12–15]. The ST-12/serotype Ib was also reported to be characterized with a significantly higher rate of severe sepsis and sepsis-attributable mortality than other serotypes [16, 17]. Therefore, continuous monitoring of the epidemiology and research on the virulence factors and pathogenesis should be conducted of ST-12/serotype Ib GBS.

So far, a number of studies have dug the ST-17/serotype III strains to describe the related virulence factors and pathogenesis during GBS infection. However, the bacterial determinants of ST-12/serotype Ib GBS strains are poorly understood. The present study aimed to characterize the molecular epidemiology and elucidate the potential virulence determinants of the hypervirulent ST-12/serotype Ib GBS strains.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

GBS strain Sag37 (accession No.: CP019978.1) was isolated from the blood sample of a newborn with bacteremia in a case of nosocomial infections occurred in Shanghai in 2014 [18]. A total of 432 nonduplicated GBS isolates were recovered during 2016–2022 from three different hospitals in Shanghai, of which 74.8% (323/432) were isolated from non-pregnant adults and 25.2% (109/432) from pregnant women and newborns. All isolates were identified using MALDI-TOF MS (bioMérieux, Paris, France) and grown in Todd-Hewitt broth (THB, BD) or Todd-Hewitt agar (THA) with 6% (v/v) sheep blood at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator.

Molecular subtyping

MLST was performed as previously described [6]. Briefly, seven housekeeping genes were amplified and sequenced, and the S. agalactiae MLST database (http://pubmlst.org/sagalatiae) was used for assigning allele numbers and obtaining the STs. Using the eBURST program, the STs were grouped into clonal complexes (CCs) whose members shared at least five of the seven MLST loci [19]; otherwise, the ST was considered a singleton. Serotyping was performed as previously described [20]. Briefly, a multiplex PCR was performed using a mix of 19 primers for CPS loci and each serotype was identified by the analysis of the unique bands pattern on agarose gel.

Mouse infection model

We utilized a mouse infection model to assess the GBS virulence as described previously with slight revision [21, 22]. 8-week-old male CD1 mice were purchased from Shanghai Jie Si Jie Laboratory Animal CO. LTD (Shanghai, China). For the survival assay, mice were subcutaneously challenged with 1 × 108 CFU of GBS strains and closely monitored for 7 days to record the end-points as survival or death. To assess the median lethal dose (LD50), mice were intraperitoneally challenged with 1 × 103 to 108 CFU of GBS strains and the survival records were taken. The LD50 was estimated using a logistic regression model as described by SAS software (version 15.1) [23].

Genome sequencing, assembly, annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted using TaKaRa MiniBEST Bacteria Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Ver.3.0) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The genomic library was constructed according to the standard Illumina TruSeq DNA sample preparation guide. All strains were sequenced using Illumina® NovaSeq™ platform. Short read sequences were assembled de novo using SPAdes with minimum thresholds for contig size and coverage set at 200 bp and 40×, respectively [24]. Additionally, strains with low virulence were sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies MinION platform in combination with the Illumina short-read sequencing platform. Long-read data was assembled using Flye and Unicycler (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler) assemblers. The Nucleotide Sequence Database was used for sequence alignment. GeneMarkSwas used for coding sequence (CDS) prediction. The predicted CDSs were annotated from NR database using BLASTP and Diamond with minimum threshold set to 1e-6 [25, 26].

Phylogenetic tree construction

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using core SNPs alignment and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/) [27]. Variants were considered to be positively associated with bacterial virulence only if the P value was < 0.05.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

Whole-genome alignment was performed based on consensus sequences of each isolate against the complete reference genome (Sag37) using Snippy (v.4.6.0) and Gubbins (v.2.3.4) [28]. After obtaining the core genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), pyseer (v.1.3.0) was further used to identify the genome variants of k-mer, SNP and COG association with bacterial virulence [29]. LD50 was used as the outcome variable. A minor allele frequency threshold of 5% was set. A population structure matrix was generated from the Gubbins phylogeny for the linear mixed model.

Screening of large gene segment deletions

The large gene segment deletions were visualized via circos plot generated by the BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG) software. The circular genome of Sag37 was compared to strains of low virulence traits (LD50 > 8) using BLAST.

Statistical analysis

Time-to-event outcomes (7-day survival) were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using the log-rank test. Median lethal dose (LD50) was estimated by logistic regression of death on the log10-transformed inoculum (CFU); isolates with no deaths at 108 CFU were considered right-censored and reported as LD50 > 8. All tests were performed using SAS software (version 15.1).

Results

Outline of the outbreak caused by GBS

During a nosocomial outbreak of GBS infection in the neonatal intensive care unit of a local hospital, strain Sag37 was isolated from the blood culture of a neonate. The infant developed septic shock and respiratory distress within 4 h after birth and ultimately died from the infection (Fig 1). The same strain was also detected in vaginal secretions from the mother, indicating an early-onset disease due to vertical transmission. Subsequently, two additional neonates in the same ward developed severe infections with similar clinical features, including fever, sepsis, and pneumonia. Clinical data showed that two neonates developed symptoms on days 9 and 13 after birth respectively, consistent with late-onset disease. Blood cultures confirmed invasive GBS bacteremia in both cases. However, no isolate was detected from the vaginal secretions of either mother. MLST and serotyping identified all isolates as ST12/serotype Ib. Whole-genome sequencing confirmed that the strains were genetically identical.

Epidemiological characteristic of GBS strains

In total, 432 non-repetitive GBS strains were collected. Midstream urine (220/50.9%) and vaginal secretions (125/28.5%) accounted for the main specimens, along with sputum (21/4.9%), blood (14/3.2%), semen (11/2.5%), etc. Fifty different STs were detected in all the strains, of which ST10 (18.71%) was most pervasive, along with ST19 (15.15%). The following sequence types included ST24 (8.16%), ST12 (7.69%), ST17 (7.23%), ST1(6.76%), ST23(6.53%), etc. Fig 2A listed the 10 most prevalent ST subtypes identified in this study, accounting for 82.9% of the total number of strains. eBRUST was further used to classify the strains into clonal complexes (CCs). A total of nine distinct clonal complexes were identified, with CC12 and CC19 being the most prevalent, collectively accounting for over 50% of the strains. Furthermore, singletons were detected, including ST862, ST4 and ST103 (Table 1). Seven serotypes were detected: Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V, and VI, of which serotype Ib (143/33.1%) ranked first, followed by serotypes III (112/25.9%), V (85/19.7%), and Ia (53/12.3%) (Fig 2B).

Associations between ST and serotype was observed in some specific subtypes. Serotype Ib was the dominant serotype among ST10 and ST12 isolates, accounting for more than 80% of the strains, whereas serotype III accounted for more than 90% of the ST19 and ST17 isolates (Fig. 3). It is noteworthy that some strains exhibited serotype inconsistencies, with the detection of multiple distinct serotypes. For example, serotype III and V were both of relatively high frequency in ST27 strains, and multiple serotypes were detected in ST1 strains, including Ib, II, V, and VI.

Distinct virulence traits of GBS strains of different STs/serotypes

Sag37, isolated from a case of nosocomial invasive infection, was considered to be hypervirulent and able to spread through clonal dissemination. To further validate its hypervirulence, additional clinical ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolates were randomly selected, as well as strains of different STs/serotypes, to assess their virulence phenotypes. Virulence was characterized by mortality rate of mice in 7 days post intraperitoneal inoculation. Infection with ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolates typically led to 100% mortality within 60 h, whereas mice infected by the other GBS subtypes all survived without exception (Fig 4).

Virulence traits of GBS strains of different STs/serotypes. Subtypes and isolation sources are indicated in the set

To further confirm whether the ST12/serotype Ib GBS strains which Sag37 belongs to were associated with hypervirulence, we randomly selected GBS strains of different subtypes based on the preliminary epidemiological data and assessed their virulence phenotypes. These stains represented the most prevalent 8 STs and 6 serotypes detected in this study. Significant variations in virulence traits were observed between different subtypes (Fig. 5). Mice injected with ST12/serotype Ib isolates became moribund much quicker than the other subtype strains. By 48 h post-infection, all mice infected with ST12/serotype Ib strains had succumbed to infection compared to none of those infected with the other strains expect with ST24/serotype V, which mice death occurred in the later stage of the experiment.

Variations in virulence phenotype between ST12/serotype Ib GBS strains

In this study, a total of 33 GBS strains of ST12/serotype Ib were identified (S-T1). To further verify whether all these strains exhibit hypervirulence similar to Sag37, CD1 mice were subjected to intraperitoneal challenge to determine the concentration of log10(LD50) (abbreviated as LD50 in the following text). Sag37 exhibited the highest level of hypervirulence, requiring the lowest concentration to achieve an LD50 of 3.25, followed by Sag-HS20-56 with an LD50 of 3.48 (Fig. 6/S-T1). The LD50 values for the majority of ST12/serotype Ib GBS strains ranged from 4.5 to 7.9. Additionally, some strains demonstrated low virulence, as indicated by LD50 values exceeding 8. No mortality was observed within seven days following intraperitoneal inoculation with a bacterial load of 10⁸ CFU.

The LD50 was estimated using a logistic regression model with the factors for strain and inoculum (CFU/mL). There is no border for partial strains since their LD50 exceeded 8

Phylogenetic analysis of ST12/serotype Ib isolates

To explore the evolutionary relationships among the ST12/serotype Ib isolates, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on whole-genome sequences of the 33 isolates identified in this study. The analysis revealed that Sag-HS20-56, which also exhibited a high virulence phenotype, was located in a different branch from Sag37 (Fig. 7). This suggested that the molecular mechanisms underlying hypervirulence in these strains might differ. Notably, strains within the same phylogenetic branch as Sag37 displayed heterogeneous virulence phenotypes, indicating that phylogenetic clustering alone could not fully explain the variation in virulence.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

To further investigate the genotype–phenotype correlation, a GWAS was performed on 33 ST12/serotype Ib genomes. Three levels of variation were analyzed: SNPs, gene insertions and deletions, and k-mers. In the full-cohort screen, no genome-wide significant associations were detected at the prespecified threshold. Guided by the core-SNP phylogeny, the hypervirulent isolate Sag-HS20-56 (distinct branch from Sag37) was excluded. We then restricted the analysis to isolates clustering with Sag37; despite clear variation in LD50 within this branch, no associations reached significance. Because genetic determinants of virulence might be obscured by isolates with moderate virulence (LD50 = 4 ~ 7), a targeted comparison was conducted exclusively between Sag37 and strains exhibiting low virulence traits (LD50 > 8). No common large gene segment insertions or deletions were observed (S-F1). At the SNP level, however, 46 shared variants were identified (40 coding, 6 non-coding; 32 nonsynonymous, 8 synonymous) (Table 2). Variants clustered into four functional groups: transcriptional regulation (LysR and MerR transcriptional family regulators), transport (peptide/multidrug ABC transporters), metabolism (galactokinase, starch synthase and serine hydroxymethyltransferase), and stress/host interaction (molecular chaperone DnaJ and nuclease). Frameshift changes were also observed in mobile-element genes (site-specific recombinase and transposase). Together, these patterns highlight biologically plausible candidate loci for Sag37 hypervirulence.

Discussion

MLST and serotyping are crucial assays for the epidemiology surveillance of GBS, providing valuable insights for prevention and diagnosis strategies. In this study, ST10/serotype Ib and ST19/serotype III were the most prevalent subtypes identified overall. Notably, serotype Ib exhibited the highest frequency, rather than the commonly observed serotype III. This may be attributed to the mixed population and specimen types involved. The primary population in this study consisted of nonpregnant adults and colonizing pregnant women, with midstream urine and vaginal secretions being the predominant specimen types. Pediatric patients with bloodstream infection or meningitis were relatively underrepresented. Similarly, Lin et al. reported that serotype Ib was the most common serotype collected from a mixed population of pregnant women, newborns, and nonpregnant adults in Taiwan [30]. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that serotype Ib ranks second in terms of neonatal disease incidence within East Asia, following serotype III [7]. Maternal screening for GBS has revealed regional differences in prevalence, with Mexico and South America showing significantly higher rates of serotype Ib maternal colonization compared to the United States, the United Kingdom, and France [7, 31]. These findings suggest that the subtypes of GBS strains vary according to geography, population, and disease, particularly among nonpregnant adults and neonates. Therefore, continuous surveillance of GBS strains is warranted, which will provide crucial insights for vaccine development, genetic evolution research and transmission route tracking.

Associations between specific ST and serotype were observed. Serotype Ib predominated in ST10 and ST12, whereas the majority of ST19 and ST17 isolates were serotype III. Similar trends have been observed within the MLST database [1]. However, certain STs exhibited discordance with their corresponding serotypes, as evidenced by the variation between serotypes III and V within ST27, and the detection of multiple serotypes, including Ib, II, V, and VI, in ST1 strains. Consequently, the correlation between ST typing and serotypes cannot be universally generalized. While some ST strains appear to be associated with specific dominant serotypes, other cases involve the detection of multiple serotypes with no single dominant serotype.

Significant variation in virulence was observed among GBS strains of different STs/serotypes, as demonstrated by the mice challenge assay. Sag37, reported in our previous study [18], was proved to represent distinctly hypervirulence phenotype. Additionally, ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolates, to which Sag37 belonged, were also shown to be closely associated with hypervirulence. Although ST17/serotype III GBS strain is well known for its strong association with neonatal invasive disease, the in vivo experimental data from this study indicated that the virulence of ST12/serotype Ib GBS strain was higher than that of ST17/serotype III. This was evidenced by a shorter onset duration and higher mortality rates in mice. Recently, ST/serotype Ib GBS isolate has aroused increasing attention, as a growing body of reports suggests they may be more virulent and fatal than the ST17/serotype III strain [14, 32–35]. Hsu et al. also emphasized its association with a significantly higher rate of severe sepsis and sepsis-attributable mortality compared to other serotypes [17]. In conclusion, ST12/serotype Ib GBS strain represents an emerging hypervirulent GBS clonal type. Therefore, enhanced monitoring and the development of novel prevention and control strategies are imperative.

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the high virulence of ST12/serotype Ib GBS, we performed whole genome sequencing on 33 ST12/serotype Ib GBS strains identified in this study, and determined their virulence phenotype using LD50 assessment. Notably, there was still variation in virulence among ST12/serotype Ib strains, with some mice succumbing at a challenge dose of 103 CFU, while others survived even at the highest challenge concentration of 108 CFU. Among them, the isolate Sag37 in the first part caused neonatal death was the most lethal to mice. The results suggested that virulence traits still varied among different strains within the ST12/serotype Ib clade.

In order to understand the molecular mechanisms mediating the differences in virulence, genome-wide association study (GWAS) was conducted. GWAS is a research method used to identify genetic variations associated with specific traits or diseases by scanning the entire genome of many individuals [36]. The initial screening did not reveal any statistically significant specific differential genes, which may be attributed to the limited number of strains in the data set. It is also plausible that the hypervirulence phenotype is mediated by multiple differential genes, reflecting the polygenic nature of bacterial virulence [37]. Subsequently, we refined the scope of the data set and focused solely on analyzing the genetic differences between Sag37 and strains exhibiting low virulence traits (LD50 >8), which surfaced shared variants at SNP level.

Our genetic screen points to several candidate genes whose functions include transcriptional regulation, transport metabolism, and stress/host interaction, as candidate drivers of hypervirulence in this lineage. Notably, several candidates align with established GBS virulence mechanisms. For example, nuclease A degrades the neutrophil extracellular traps and is required for full virulence [38]. LysR family transcriptional regulators (e.g., mtaR) link amino-acid uptake and metabolic control to the regulation of pathogenesis programs [39]. ABC transport systems modulate virulence traits and virulence-gene expression in GBS [40]. We also identified genes with documented roles in virulence in streptococci and in bacteria from other genera. The molecular chaperone DnaJ is associated with stress adaptation and virulence, whereas MerR family transcriptional regulator commonly mediate metal/oxidative stress responses and drug efflux, influencing the pathogenicity of Gram-positive bacteria [41, 42]. Consistent with this, metabolism–virulence coupling—via core enzymes involved in nutrient utilization and carbohydrate metabolism—has been documented in pathogenic streptococci [43]. However, some genes remain weakly supported and are best considered observational, low-priority candidates. Translation-related factors such as TruA and the ribosome recycling factor may influence global proteostasis, yet no direct link to GBS virulence has been demonstrated. Mobile-element–associated variants (e.g., site-specific recombinase and transposase) suggest genomic plasticity, but their regulatory consequences remain unclear. Virulence is likely multifactorial and polygenic. Larger cohorts and orthogonal datasets will be needed to validate these candidates and refine genotype–phenotype relationships. Further experimental validation is required to establish their roles in the hypervirulence and pathogenesis of ST12/serotype Ib GBS.

Conclusions

This study underscores the correlation between ST12/serotype Ib GBS isolates and hypervirulence, surpassing that of other clinically prevalent strains. GWAS highlighted candidate loci potentially linked to virulence. These findings support continued epidemiologic surveillance and focused functional studies to define the virulence factors and pathogenesis of ST12/serotype Ib GBS.
