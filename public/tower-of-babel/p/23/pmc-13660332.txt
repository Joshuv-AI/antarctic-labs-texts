Subjective and objective workload of critical care nurses in a tertiary hospital in Switzerland: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Sebastian Zölle, Stefan Ruf, Seraina Wyss, Stefan Christ, Patrick Broer, Philipp Karl Bühler, Benedikt Florian Scherr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNursing workload in intensive care units affects outcomes and staff well-being. Objective measures such as Nine Equivalents of Nursing Manpower Score (NEMS) capture task demands, whereas subjective ratings reflect nurses’ workload; how these relate is unclear (1–4).MethodsAll shifts over 6 months in an adult ICU of a tertiary hospital were analyzed. For each shift, staffing (number of nurses, patients, patient-to-nurse ratio PNR), number of interventions, admissions and discharges, cumulative Richmond agitation sedation scale RASS score and intensivist identity were recorded. Shift workload was measured as cumulative NEMS and subjective rating on a 1–7 scale (LEP). Analyses used Pearson correlations, linear regression, one-way ANOVA, and multiple regression.Results531 shifts showed a mean NEMS of 347.7 and subjective rating of 4.6 with a mean PNR of 1.42. Early shifts had the highest NEMS; the highest subjective ratings occurred in late shifts (4.8 late, 4.6 early, 4.4 night; p < 0.0001). Subjective workload correlated weakly with NEMS overall (r = 0.254, p < 0.0001), but moderately in early (r = 0.31) and late (r = 0.43) shifts with no association at night. PNR correlated with subjective workload in early (r = 0.37) and late (r = 0.24) but not night shifts. Multiple regression including NEMS, PNR, RASS, admissions and discharges and consultant identity explained 12.4% of variance in subjective workload (p < 0.001). NEMS (B = 0.0030, p < 0.0001), admissions (B = 0.0687, p = 0.048) and interventions (B = 0.0603, p < 0.008) were independent predictors. PNR, cumulative RASS, discharges and consultant identity were not independently associated.ConclusionNEMS, admissions and interventions were related to perceived workload but explained little of what makes a shift demanding. The NEMS-subjective workload association was confined to early and late shifts, suggesting night-time workload is driven by other factors. ICU workload is multidimensional and cannot be adequately captured by objective task counts alone. Routinely recorded key performance indicators from intensive care units can only partially reflect the fluctuations in the subjective workload perceived by nurses.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04844-0.Keywords: NEMS, Subjective, Workload, ICU, Shifts, Staffing

Background

Intensive care nursing involves complex, high-stakes patient care, making workload a key concern for patient safety and nurse well-being. High nursing workload has been linked to medical errors, poor patient outcomes (e.g. infections, pressure ulcers, unplanned extubations and higher morbidity and mortality in general) and nurse burnout [1–10]. In high-volume intensive care units ICUs, workload must be managed through adequate staffing and support; conversely, lighter caseloads may help nurses maintain care quality. However, findings on workload and outcomes are mixed. A large Austrian ICU registry study found that, within a well-resourced system, ICU mortality was generally unaffected by ordinary fluctuations in workload and occupancy, except during extraordinary surges (e.g. COVID-19) [11]. This suggests that moderate workload variation alone may not threaten outcomes in well-staffed units. Previous research has highlighted the critical role of emphasizing the adjustment of nursing staff levels to match the complexity of care in the ICU [12]. Nonetheless, perceived workload influences nurses’ stress and work behavior. For example, subjective stress levels (measured at shift end) were associated with elevated cortisol in critical care nurses, whereas an objective workload measure (derived from LEP (“Leistungserfassung in der Pflege” – a standardized tool in Switzerland for recording and documenting nursing activities based on recording the nursing workload using standard catalogs of services)) did not significantly predict cortisol [13]. This implies that subjective workload captures emotional strain beyond task counts. Another study by Fischbacher et al. demonstrated that in unpredictable nursing scenarios, perceived workload is not primarily determined by task quantity but is critically shaped by soft factors [14]. Traditional workload metrics are insufficient as they fail to capture the complexity of dynamic clinical environments.

Objective workload in ICUs is often quantified by tools like Therapeutic Intervention Scoring System-28 (TISS-28) or its simplified derivative, the Nine Equivalents of Nursing Manpower Score (NEMS) as routinely recorded key performance indicators [15]. NEMS allocates points for interventions (e.g. ventilation, monitoring tasks) and correlates with TISS-28. It provides a patient-centric measure of task load that can guide staffing and benchmarking, reliably reflects the general objectifiable workload, is accurate [16] and both NAS and NEMS show high correlation for individual measurements and for the total workload measurement in the unit [17].

In contrast, subjective workload relies on nurses’ perceptions. The LEP® Nursing Workload Management System (widely used in Switzerland) includes a subjective item rated on a 1–7 scale, reflecting the nurse’s self-assessed workload [18]. Prior work emphasizes that subjective assessments capture nurses’ emotional burden and cognitive effort, elements that objective scores may miss [19]. The single-item Likert scale identical to the scale used in our publication was used in establishing the STRAIN-EOS instrument [20]. Despite well-recognized limitations of single-item measures and the absence of explicit psychometric validation of the LEP® scale, such instruments are established in clinical practice, show acceptable reliability [21], have been shown to correlate with salivary cortisol in critical care nurses (although this has not been demonstrated for the LEP item specifically) [13], justifying their use as a pragmatic surrogate for multidimensional subjective stress.

The simple ratio of nursing staff to patients is not sufficient to fully capture the complex nursing workload [4]. Different types of stress are closely linked and affect both the health and job satisfaction of nursing staff and patient safety. These stresses include high physical workload, cognitive and emotional stress, long shifts and staff shortages (which increase the likelihood of errors), and systemic factors (including team communication, shift planning, equipment, and management). Workload in intensive care nursing is a systemic problem that can only be sustainably improved through a holistic approach to work organization, communication, and ergonomic conditions [22].

Despite multiple measures, how well objective and subjective workloads align in day-to-day ICU operations remains unclear. Some authors argue that reliance on task counts alone is insufficient: individual experience, teamwork, unexpected events and other “soft” factors shape perceived workload [23, 24]. Greater individual experience significantly reduces perceived mental demand and workload. Effective teamwork also acts as a buffer, mitigating workload perception and stress. These factors becomes most pronounced in unpredictable and complex situations [14]. Moreover, organizational variables (staffing ratios, shift type, attending intensivist presence, patient acuity) may modulate workload perception [14], for example higher Nursing Activities Score (NAS) values were observed during patient admission and the morning shift [25]. Understanding these relationships is crucial for designing staffing dashboards and interventions.

Our study retrospectively analyzes six months of ICU shift data to test the hypothesis that objective workload (NEMS) correlates positively with subjective workload (LEP rating), and to quantify the strength of this relationship. We also explore secondary factors (shift timing, staffing levels, intensivist presence) to see how they influence subjective workload.

Methods

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study at the adult ICU of a tertiary Swiss hospital, including all nursing shifts (early 07:00 to 15:30, late 15:00 to 22:00, night 22:00 to 07:30) over six consecutive months (October 2024 until March 2025). The Cantonal Ethics Committee Zurich (KEK-ZH) issued a Declaration of Non-Responsibility under BASEC-ID Req-2025-00997; informed consent was waived. All data originated from routine quality-assurance records, fully aggregated and anonymized at shift level. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Objective workload was quantified per shift as the cumulative NEMS score (workload index based on nursing procedures [15, 16] assigning patients to a score between 0 and 56 based on nine nursing care interventions). Subjective workload was the mean of nurses’ end-of-shift self-ratings on the LEP® 1–7 scale (higher equals heavier workload [18]). For each shift, we additionally recorded number of nurses, number of patients, patient-to-nurse ratio (PNR), shift type, number of interventions performed (according to the intensive care unit’s internal definition; see the list of interventions in the supplemental material), cumulative Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score (defined as the sum of the highest individual scores for patients within a shift), number of admissions and discharges and identity of the on-site consultant intensivist (one of 14 consultants; at least one always present per unit policy).

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism v10. Pearson correlations and simple linear regressions were computed across all shifts and stratified by shift type. Pearson r was chosen despite the ordinal subjective scale given the large sample (n = 531) and near-symmetrical distribution (mean 4.6, SD 0.75); Spearman ρ was reported for confirmation (Supplemental Table 1). Shift-type differences were tested by one-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc; variance homogeneity was assessed by Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett tests. A multiple linear regression model was fitted with subjective workload as the dependent variable and NEMS, PNR (as composite staffing measure to avoid multicollinearity), interventions, cumulative RASS, admissions, discharges, and consultant presence (C1–C14, binary indicators without reference category since multiple consultants could be present per shift) as predictors. Bonferroni correction was applied to the 14 consultant comparisons (α = 0.05/14 = 0.0036); the threshold for all other tests was α = 0.05. No multiplicity correction was applied across the substantive predictors (NEMS, PNR, admissions, discharges, RASS, interventions), as each addressed an a-priori-specified, conceptually independent hypothesis. Missing data (n = 15, 2.7%) were handled by listwise deletion; no imputation was performed.

Results

Over six months, we identified n = 546 shifts; after excluding 15 with missing data, n = 531 were analyzed (n = 177 early shifts, n = 176 late shifts, n = 178 night shifts). On average in each shift a mean of 9.3 (SD 1.0) nurses cared for 13.1 (SD 1.4) ICU patients (patient-to-nurse ratio 1.4 SD 0.2).

The mean NEMS score per shift was 347.7 (SD 52.6), translating to a mean NEMS score of 26.7 per patient. The mean subjective workload rating was 4.6 (SD 0.75) on the 1–7 scale.

The mean nursing staffing was 9.7 nurses in early shifts, 9.4 nurses in late and 8.7 nurses in night shifts corresponding to a PNR of 1.41 in early, 1.37 in late shifts and 1.48 in night shifts.

Early shifts had significantly higher objective workload (NEMS early 363.8 vs. late 343.6 vs. night 335.8 points, p < 0.01) and late shifts had significantly higher subjective ratings (early 4.6 vs. late 4.8 vs. night 4.4 points p < 0.01) than early and night shifts.

Correlation was weak but significant between NEMS (r = 0.254, p <0.0001) and subjective workload as well as between number of patients and PNR and subjective workload (Fig. 1). NEMS correlated moderately with staffing (r = 0.393, p <0.0001) whereas the correlation of subjective workload with staffing was very low and not significant (Table 1; Spearman ρ values were confirmed to be within ±0.07 of reported Pearson r values for all primary comparisons, supplemental Table 1).

Higher objective workload, more admissions and more interventions were associated with higher subjective workload in this model. NEMS (B = 0.0030, p < 0.0001), admissions (B = 0.0687, p < 0.05) and interventions (B = 0.0603, p < 0.01) showed a positive regression coefficient, predicting an approximately linear increase in subjective workload.

Patient-to-nurse ratio as well as cumulative RASS score and discharges were not independently associated with subjective workload. Regarding consultant intensivists, in each case, the coefficient is the difference in mean subjective workload between shifts with that consultant on duty versus shifts without that consultant (on-duty – off-duty). Consultant-specific effects were generally small (point estimates − 0.44 to + 0.26 scale points). No consultant reached statistical significance after Bonferroni correction for 14 simultaneous comparisons; the nominally lowest p-value was observed for C1 (B = − 0.252, uncorrected p = 0.01) and C9 (B = − 0.217, uncorrected p = 0.044), but both did not survive correction.

NEMS and interventions showed acceptable variance inflation factors (all VIF < 1.45), indicating no relevant multicollinearity despite interventions being a component of NEMS.

Formal tests of residual normality were statistically significant (e.g. D’Agostino–Pearson K² = 26.1, p < 0.0001; Shapiro–Wilk W = 0.98, p < 0.0001), suggesting deviations from a perfect normal distribution.

Discussion

In this single-center ICU study, we found a statistically significant but weak correlation between objective nursing tasks (NEMS) and nurses’ own perceived workload. The average NEMS score per patient was 26.7 points, which was roughly in line with the European average used to develop the NEMS score [15, 26], while the average subjective workload rating was 4.6 (SD 0.75) on the 1–7 scale, indicating a moderate-to-high perceived workload. Thus, although nurses do rate shifts with more nursing tasks as harder, most variation in their ratings is not captured by NEMS. This finding is in line with prior research showing that patient-based (objective) and operator-based (subjective) workload measures are often only moderately correlated or even uncorrelated, underscoring the gap between documented task counts and personal strain [27].

NEMS explained only part of the variance in subjective scores. In practical terms, a cumulative increase of 100 NEMS points per shift would raise the 1–7 workload rating by only 0.3 units. The unexplained variance is compatible with influences operating below the shift-aggregate level - including individual cognitive load, acute clinical events, and team composition [14, 27–31].

“Soft” factors reinforce the concept that ICU workload is a multidimensional construct influenced by tasks, organization, technology, and environment [14, 32]. Myny et al. suggested that the development or revision of a workload measurement tool should take into account the number of work interruptions, patient turnover rate, and mandatory registrations [33].

High numbers of patient admissions are another significant association with workload and correlate with increased subjective nursing workload. This constant churn contributes to subjective workload. Interestingly, only admissions were associated with an increased subjective workload, not discharges similar to the findings of Li et al. [25]; incoming patient flow appears to contribute to shift-level perception, while outgoing flow does not.

Cumulative RASS spans deep sedation to agitation rather than agitation specifically; the absence of a signal here does not contradict the established link between delirium and nursing workload [34].

NEMS was originally designed as a simplified therapeutic intervention index and captures only nine categories of medical interventions [15]. It does not account for nursing-specific activities such as mobilization, psychosocial support, family communication, documentation, transport, or especially demanding patients, all of which contribute substantially to perceived workload [33, 35]. A more comprehensive objective workload measure such as the Nursing Activities Score (NAS), which covers 23 activity categories and explains up to 80% of nursing time [26], might yield stronger associations with subjective ratings. Future studies should consider using NAS alongside or instead of NEMS to better capture ICU nursing work. Our study found that interventions both within and outside of intensive care units are also a factor contributing to subjective workload. Despite conceptual overlap, the intervention count showed no relevant collinearity with NEMS. The independent contribution of the intervention count beyond NEMS is mechanistically plausible: the internal intervention register (Supplemental Material) covers categories that NEMS does not consider. NEMS thus systematically under-represents both transport-related and time-intensive bedside work, which the intervention count captures.

There was no significant correlation between staff numbers and perceived workload; in early shifts, more staff showed a non-significant negative association. Existing evidence on the association between ICU nurse staffing and nurse outcomes is inconsistent, although lower staffing has generally been linked to adverse outcome [36].

However, the number of patients and the patient-to-nurse ratio showed a significant correlation with increased subjective workload in the simple linear regression analysis. Higher PNR has been linked to worse nurse and patient outcomes in cohorts with substantially wider staffing ranges [37–39].

Notably, the PNR in our ICU varied within a narrow range (mean 1.42; range across shift types 1.37–1.48), reflecting the comparatively well-resourced staffing norms typical of Swiss tertiary ICUs. This limited variability may have attenuated the observable association between PNR and subjective workload through range restriction. In many international ICUs, PNRs of two to three or higher are common, and the relationship between PNR and perceived workload or adverse outcomes may become apparent only across a wider range of staffing conditions. Our narrow PNR band is therefore not informative about the question whether staffing ratios drive perceived workload at PNR ≥ 2.

Late shifts carried the highest subjective workload despite the lowest PNR and intermediate NEMS. Structural features plausibly converge in this shift: deferred tasks generated during early shifts, the second-lowest staffing complement of the three shifts and the shortest shift duration of the three. Where similar volumes of objective work and admissions concentrate into a shorter window with intermediate staffing, perceived task density rises while PNR does not. This is consistent with our finding that the intervention count predicted subjective ratings. The absence of a difference between early and night shifts is noteworthy, given that night work is often assumed to be especially strenuous; one possible explanation is that lower activity levels at night may partly compensate for the circadian strain. Consistently, NEMS was significantly associated with subjective workload in the early shifts, but not at night indicating that night-time perceived workload was largely independent of NEMS. Notably, the association between PNR and subjective workload reversed during night shifts (non-significant), possibly because night-time care might involve fewer competing demands or because circadian factors and fatigue may override task-related workload perception; these factors require further study.

As each shift had a consultant intensivist on site, we could not assess the effect of not having an attending physician present. A Meta-analysis and Swiss multicenter intensive care unit study demonstrated that good interprofessional teamwork is associated with lower levels of burnout, even after adjusting for workload [40, 41]. We did, however, explore whether individual consultants on duty influenced nurse perceptions. No consultant was independently associated with perceived workload after correcting for multiple comparisons. Although C1 and C9 showed the lowest nominal p-value, this did not survive Bonferroni correction and is likely a chance finding.

Notably, we observed that NEMS correlated strongly with staffing. This indicates that scheduled task load and personnel are not independent: elective procedures, mobilization and transport are typically scheduled when staffing permits, while during low-staffing periods such activities are deferred. NEMS therefore behaves partly as an outcome of staffing rather than as an exogenous exposure. Any causal interpretation of a NEMS-workload association must account for this endogeneity, which is not addressable in a retrospective design.

Operationally, the data supports recording subjective and objective workload as complementary rather than substitutive measures; the subjective input cannot be reconstructed from intervention-based scores.

Limitations: This retrospective, single-center study has inherent constraints. Being single-center and observational, we cannot infer causality. Of the 546 identified shifts, 15 (2.7%) lacked subjective workload ratings and were excluded. Most of these missing ratings occurred toward the end of the period analyzed, suggesting that data were not missing completely at random. Although the proportion of missing data was small, selective non-response cannot be excluded.

Our subjective workload measure - a single-item LEP rating on a 1–7 scale - represents a substantial methodological constraint. Unlike multi-dimensional instruments such as the NASA-TLX, which differentiates mental, physical and temporal demand, frustration, effort, and performance, a single item cannot distinguish between these dimensions and may lack the sensitivity to detect variation. Furthermore, the LEP item was designed for routine documentation rather than as a validated research instrument, and its psychometric properties are unknown. The low correlation observed may therefore partly reflect measurement imprecision rather than an independence of subjective and objective workload constructs. Equally important, our analysis included the shift level rather than the individual nurse. Subjective ratings were averaged, which eliminates both within-nurse and between-nurse variability. Factors that the literature identifies as key moderators of perceived workload - including individual clinical experience, coping strategies, fatigue levels, and personal stressors [14] - operate at the individual level and cannot be captured in a shift-level aggregate. This design precludes inference about individual-level associations and may have attenuated true correlations.

We also relied on accurate NEMS coding; any under- or overreporting of tasks would directly affect the objective measure. A further limitation of our analytical model is the absence of patient-level acuity. We did not collect illness severity scores, diagnostic categories, the occurrence of deaths or palliative care situations. All have been shown to influence workload independently of intervention-based scores [28, 31, 32]. The omission of these variables likely contributes to the large unexplained variance in our model and limits our ability to identify the specific drivers of perceived workload. The very low R² values caution against over-interpretation of observed correlations. While literature does link high PNR to adverse outcomes, the present study did not measure patient outcomes or test different staffing ratios. Additionally, cultural and organizational factors specific to our ICU (e.g. staffing norms, familiarity with LEP scoring) may limit generalizability to other settings.

Conclusion

In a tertiary Swiss ICU, nurses’ subjective perception of workload is only weakly aligned with an objective task-based index (NEMS). The positive correlation between NEMS and subjective ratings confirms they are related, but the routinely collected key performance indicator NEMS reflects only a narrow aspect of the variability in nursing staff’s subjectively perceived workload. While PNR was associated with perceived workload in univariate analysis, this association did not persist in the multivariable model. In addition to NEMS, the number of patient admissions and interventions show a significant correlation with subjective workload. Consultant identity, discharges and cumulative RASS score had no statistically significant independent association with perceived workload. The large unexplained variance is compatible with the influence of unmeasured factors (teamwork, stress, fatigue, etc.). Subjective and objective workload are correlated but non-redundant; recording both is required if perceived workload is to be tracked.
