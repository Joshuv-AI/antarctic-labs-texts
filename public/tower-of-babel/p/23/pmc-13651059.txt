Paid work participation and cognitive function trajectories among older adults in China: evidence from a longitudinal study
Authors: Ziting Zhao, Shiyi Xu, Jiayuan Jiang, Yanxia Lyu, Qian Wang, Kan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEvidence on the association between work and cognitive ageing has been mixed, and research from China remains limited, particularly with respect to heterogeneity in cognitive function trajectories. This study examined whether paid work participation in later life is associated with distinct cognitive function trajectories among older Chinese adults.MethodsData were obtained from four waves (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The analytical sample comprised 3,590 adults aged 60 years or older. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct trajectories of cognitive function over time. Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations between paid work participation and trajectory membership. The Karlson-Holm-Breen (KHB) method was applied to assess the mediating role of digital access. Additional analyses further explored work type and regional heterogeneity.ResultsThree cognitive function trajectories were identified: a Low-level rapid decline group, a Moderate-level slow decline group, and a High-level stable group. Compared with nonparticipants, older adults engaged in paid work were more likely to belong to the Moderate-level slow decline and High-level stable groups. Mediation analyses indicated that digital access partially mediated the association with membership in the High-level stable group, whereas no clear mediation effect was observed for the Moderate-level slow decline group. Across different types of paid work, participation was consistently associated with more favorable cognitive function trajectories, with variations in effect magnitude across work types. The positive association between paid work participation and favorable cognitive function trajectories appeared stronger among older adults living in central-western regions.DiscussionIn this nationally representative longitudinal study, paid work participation in later life was associated with more favorable cognitive ageing trajectories among older Chinese adults. These findings underscore the importance of moving beyond a binary distinction between working and not working, and of considering work characteristics and supportive employment environments when assessing the role of paid work in promoting cognitive health in later life.Keywords: Older adults, Paid work, Cognitive function, Group-based trajectory modeling, Digital access

Introduction

Cognitive impairment has emerged as a major global public health challenge, particularly in the context of rapid population ageing [1]. Epidemiological evidence suggests that the prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) among adults aged 60 years and older is approximately 23.7% worldwide [2], while in China the prevalence of MCI and dementia reaches about 19.6% and 6.0%, respectively [3, 4]. Cognitive decline not only compromises quality of life and functional independence in later life [5], but also increases family caregiving demands and places sustained pressure on health-care and social support systems [6]. Against this backdrop, identifying modifiable factors that may help slow cognitive decline has become a central priority in ageing research and policy agendas.

Among a range of potentially modifiable factors, paid work participation represents a salient social exposure that has received growing attention in relation to cognitive health in later life [7]. Within the frameworks of active and productive ageing, continued engagement in paid work is often viewed as an important pathway through which older adults may remain socially engaged and economically productive. By maintaining social roles and facilitating regular social interaction, paid work may provide sustained cognitive stimulation and be linked to cognitive functioning in later life [8, 9]. Empirical evidence from different countries generally suggests that labour force participation at older ages is associated with better cognitive performance and slower cognitive decline; however, findings remain inconsistent [10, 11]. Cognitive ageing is increasingly understood as a dynamic process, and individuals may experience heterogeneous patterns of cognitive change over time. Accordingly, the association between paid work and cognitive ageing may vary across individuals, rather than following a uniform pattern [12].

Despite growing interest in the relationship between work participation and cognitive ageing, important limitations remain in the existing literature, particularly in the Chinese context [13, 14]. First, many studies rely on cross-sectional designs or single-point cognitive assessments, which are unable to capture the dynamic nature of cognitive change or the heterogeneity of cognitive ageing processes [15]. Second, even when longitudinal data are available, cognitive outcomes are often modelled in terms of average levels or average rates of decline, potentially obscuring distinct subgroups that follow different cognitive function trajectories [16, 17]. Third, although some life-course studies have examined how work history patterns during early and mid-adulthood (for example, ages 18–60) relate to cognitive outcomes in later life, longitudinal evidence focusing specifically on current paid work participation in old age and its association with heterogeneous cognitive function trajectories remains limited in China [18]. Against the backdrop of ongoing retirement reform and policies promoting later-life employment, addressing this gap is of substantial practical and policy relevance.

In parallel with these gaps, rapid digitalisation has made digital access an increasingly important contextual factor shaping work participation and health among older adults in China. Prior research suggests that continued work engagement or delayed labour market exit may be associated with a lower risk of digital exclusion [19, 20], which has in turn been linked to slower cognitive decline [21, 22]. However, few studies have examined household digital access as a potential pathway linking paid work participation to cognitive ageing outcomes, particularly from a longitudinal and trajectory-based perspective.

In this context, the present study draws on four waves (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020) of the CHARLS. Using GBTM, we identify heterogeneous cognitive function trajectories among adults aged 60 years and older, and examine the association between later-life paid work participation and trajectory membership. We further assess the potential mediating role of household digital access. By adopting a trajectory-based perspective, this study provides longitudinal evidence on the association between paid work participation and cognitive ageing in China, with implications for understanding supportive employment environments in later life.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

Data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese residents aged 45 years and older that uses a multistage, probability-proportional-to-size cluster sampling design [23]. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers. The national baseline survey was conducted in 2011–2012, followed by follow-up waves in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. In the present study, we used four waves of data from 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The 2013 wave was selected as the baseline because it provided complete information on the key variables required for this study and allowed longitudinal follow-up through 2020. We sequentially excluded individuals who (a) had missing baseline cognitive assessments, (b) had fewer than two cognitive measurements across follow-up waves, (c) had missing information on paid work participation, or (d) had missing values in key covariates. The analytical sample included 3,590 participants aged 60 years or older at baseline after sequential exclusions (Fig. 1).

Variable Selection

Paid work

Paid work was the core explanatory variable and referred to participation in income-generating activities among older adults. Based on the original CHARLS questionnaire and coding rules, paid work was classified into three subtypes: agricultural self-employed work, agricultural wage employment, and non-agricultural work. Agricultural self-employed work referred to engagement in agricultural production or household business activities for one’s own household for at least 10 days in the past year. Agricultural wage employment referred to paid agricultural work for other households or employers for at least 10 days in the past year. Non-agricultural work referred to engagement in non-agricultural work activities for at least one hour in the past week, including wage employment, self-employment, or unpaid work in a family business. According to the CHARLS interviewer instructions, participants engaged in both agricultural and non-agricultural business activities were classified as agricultural self-employed workers. Respondents who answered “yes” to any of these activities were coded as participating in paid work (1); otherwise, they were coded as 0 [24–26]. This operationalisation was based on the available CHARLS questionnaire items and has been used in previous CHARLS-based studies on later-life productive activity and paid labour participation. Because CHARLS does not provide detailed information on working hours or work intensity for all work categories, paid work participation in this study should be interpreted as engagement in work-related productive activities rather than as a measure of employment intensity.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was the outcome variable and comprised two dimensions: episodic memory and mental status [27, 28]. Episodic memory was assessed using immediate and delayed word recall, each with scores ranging from 0 to 10. Mental status was assessed using selected items from the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS), including orientation, attention (each scored from 0 to 5), and visuospatial ability (scored from 0 to 1). Orientation was assessed by asking participants to report the year, month, date, day of the week, and season. Attention was assessed using a serial subtraction task in which participants subtracted 7 consecutively from 100 five times. Visuospatial ability was assessed using a figure-drawing task. The total cognitive function score was calculated as the sum of scores across the two dimensions, ranging from 0 to 31. Higher scores indicated better cognitive function.

Digital access

Digital access was considered the mediating variable in this study [29, 30]. Digital access was defined based on participants’ responses to the question, “Does your household have access to broadband internet?” If respondents answered “yes”, the digital access variable was coded as 1; otherwise, it was coded as 0.

Covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, household characteristics, health status, and lifestyle behaviours [31, 32]. Sociodemographic characteristics comprised age, sex, place of residence, educational attainment, marital status, medical insurance participation, and pension insurance participation. Health status and lifestyle behaviours included smoking, alcohol consumption, self-rated health, and number of chronic diseases. Household characteristics included household size and financial support from children. Financial support from children was measured as the total monetary support received from children in 2013. The variable was log-transformed using log(x + 1) to reduce skewness and winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Statistical analysis

All data management, statistical analyses, and figure generation were conducted using StataMP version 18. GBTM was used to model cognitive function trajectories. Using maximum likelihood estimation, the model identified latent patterns of cognitive change over time and classified individuals into distinct groups with similar developmental trajectories. Trajectory models were initially fitted with higher-order polynomial terms. If these terms were not statistically significant, models were refitted with lower-order terms in a stepwise manner. Model evaluation criteria included the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the average posterior probability (AvePP), and the proportion of individuals in each trajectory group. The optimal model was selected based on lower BIC and AIC values, an AvePP greater than 0.7, and a minimum group membership of at least 5% for each trajectory [33].

χ² tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare baseline characteristics across cognitive function trajectory groups. Multinomial logistic regression models were used to examine the association between paid work and cognitive function trajectories. Models were sequentially adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, household characteristics, health status, and lifestyle behaviours. Robustness analyses were conducted by varying sample selection, controlling for baseline cognitive differences, and substituting alternative outcome measures. The KHB method was used to test the mediating effect of digital access in the association between paid work and cognitive function trajectories [34]. Indirect effects were supplemented by conducting 1,000 repeated bootstrap samples. Additional analyses examining labour type segmentation and regional heterogeneity were also conducted. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Grouping of cognitive function trajectories

Using GBTM, models with one to five cognitive function trajectories were fitted with different functional forms. Model fit indices are shown in Table 1. Both AIC and BIC decreased with increasing numbers of cognitive function trajectories, indicating improved model fit. However, reductions in AIC and BIC diminished substantially beyond the three-group solution, suggesting only marginal gains in model fit with additional groups. The optimal number of cognitive function trajectories can be identified by the inflection point (or “elbow”) in the decline of model fit indices, such as AIC and BIC, as described by Asparouhov et al. [35]. In the present study, the four-group solution represented only a further subdivision of the three-group model, and the additional trajectory did not reflect a distinct pattern of cognitive change or offer new theoretical insights [28]. When multiple models show limited differences in theoretical interpretability, a more parsimonious model should be preferred. On the basis of model fit indices and interpretability, the three-group solution was selected as the optimal number of cognitive function trajectories.

Three distinct cognitive function trajectories were identified, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Trajectory 1, labelled the Low-level rapid decline group (n = 969, 27.0%), was characterized by relatively low baseline cognitive scores and a pronounced decline over time. Trajectory 2, the Moderate-level slow decline group (n = 1,460, 40.7%), showed intermediate cognitive performance at baseline with a more gradual decline across the follow-up period. Trajectory 3, the High-level stable group (n = 1,161, 32.3%), maintained consistently high cognitive scores with minimal fluctuation over time.

Baseline characteristics across cognitive function trajectories

A total of 3,590 older adults were included in the analysis. As shown in Table 2, most baseline characteristics differed across cognitive function trajectories (p < 0.05), except for pension insurance status, household size, and financial support from children. Compared with the Moderate- and High-level groups, individuals in the Low-level rapid decline group were older, more likely to be female, live in rural areas, have lower educational attainment, and be without a spouse. Individuals in this trajectory were also more likely to report poor self-rated health and be uninsured. Although the number of chronic conditions differed across the three groups, the magnitude of these differences was small. With respect to health behaviours, the Low-level rapid decline group had higher proportions of non-smokers and non-drinkers.

Association between paid work and cognitive function trajectories

Participation in paid work was specified as the independent variable, and cognitive function trajectories as the dependent variable. Multinomial logistic regression models were fitted, with the Low-level rapid decline trajectory as the reference category. Results are presented in Table 3. In Model 1, without adjustment for covariates, older adults engaged in paid work were more likely to be classified into the Moderate-level slow decline and High-level stable trajectories than those not engaged in paid work. The corresponding relative risk ratios (RRRs) were 1.62 (95% CI 1.19–2.20; p = 0.002) and 1.93 (95% CI 1.37–2.73; p < 0.001), respectively. In Model 2, with additional adjustment for sociodemographic and household characteristics, the associations remained significant (p < 0.05). Model 3 further adjusted for health status and lifestyle behaviours, with similar results.

Robustness test

To assess the robustness of the findings, additional analyses were conducted addressing sample selection, baseline differences, and outcome substitution. Results are shown in Table 4. First, we re-estimated the regression models after excluding individuals with baseline limitations in activities of daily living (ADL). The association between paid work participation and cognitive trajectory membership remained consistent in both direction and significance. Second, the main models were further adjusted by controlling for baseline cognitive scores. The association between paid work and cognitive function trajectories persisted. Third, the outcome variable was replaced with cognitive impairment status at the final follow-up. Cognitive impairment was defined using education-specific cognitive function thresholds. Older adults engaged in paid work had a lower risk of cognitive impairment (OR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.23–0.90). These findings were consistent with the main conclusions of the study.

Further analyses

Mediation analysis

To explore potential pathways linking paid work to cognitive function trajectories, the KHB method was used to examine the mediating role of digital access. Results are shown in Table 5. In comparisons between the Low-level rapid decline and Moderate-level slow decline trajectories, no mediating effect of digital access was observed. In contrast, in comparisons between the Low-level rapid decline and High-level stable trajectories, the differential effect of digital access was significant (p = 0.031). Given the ordered structure of the cognitive function trajectories, an additional robustness check was conducted by treating the trajectories as an ordinal variable coded from 1 to 3 and estimating a linear mediation model. A bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples was used to estimate the indirect effect [36]. The indirect effect of paid work on cognitive function trajectories through digital access was 0.006 (95% CI 0.001–0.011; p = 0.022). Results of this supplementary analysis were consistent with the KHB decomposition analysis in both direction and significance. Given that cognitive function trajectories represent a multi-categorical outcome, the aforementioned linear mediation analysis serves solely as a robustness check for the methodology.

Examination of different types of paid work

To examine associations between different types of paid work and cognitive function trajectories, paid work was disaggregated into non-agricultural work, agricultural wage employment, agricultural self-employment, and multiple types of paid work (defined as engagement in two or more types). These categories were included in multinomial logistic regression models. Results are shown in Table 6. Compared with older adults not engaged in paid work, participation in any of the four types of paid work was associated with higher relative risks of belonging to the Moderate-level slow decline and High-level stable trajectories, although the magnitude of association varied by work type. Specifically, for the High-level stable trajectory, the relative risk ratios were 5.05 for non-agricultural work, 4.01 for agricultural wage employment, 2.62 for engagement in multiple types of paid work, and 1.83 for agricultural self-employment. Interestingly, engagement in multiple types of paid work was not associated with substantially stronger cognitive advantages than some single work categories. One possible explanation is that participation in multiple work types may reflect fragmented or economically driven labour participation rather than greater cognitive stimulation.

Regional heterogeneity analysis

Given substantial regional differences in economic development, labour market structures, and public resource allocation across China, regional heterogeneity in the association between paid work and cognitive function trajectories was further examined. The sample was stratified into eastern and central-western regions, with results presented in Table 7. Discussion

Using four waves of longitudinal data from CHARLS (2013–2020), we identified three heterogeneous cognitive function trajectories among older adults through GBTM. Overall, compared with those who did not participate in paid work, older adults engaged in paid work were more likely to be classified into the Moderate-level slow decline and High-level stable trajectories, suggesting that paid work participation in later life is associated with more favorable cognitive function trajectories. These associations remained consistent across multiple robustness analyses, supporting the stability of the findings. In contrast to previous studies that primarily relied on cognitive function measured at a single time point or on life-course work histories [15], the present study adopts a trajectory-based approach to characterize the long-term dynamic association between later-life paid work participation and cognitive function changes over time.

Previous studies have reported substantial heterogeneity in cognitive function trajectories among older adults, and the trajectory patterns identified in the present study are broadly aligned with those reported in prior longitudinal research [28, 37]. Evidence from multi-country studies suggests that labour market participation in later life is generally associated with better cognitive performance or slower cognitive decline [7, 8, 15]; however, this association varies across institutional and social contexts. Re-entry into paid work at older ages has been linked to cognitive improvement in South Korea, whereas comparable benefits have not been observed in the United States [38]. Mäcken et al., analysing longitudinal data from the United States, the United Kingdom, and several European countries, showed that retirement was associated with accelerated memory decline in some national contexts but not in others, with stronger negative associations observed in countries characterised by more rigid retirement regimes and fewer opportunities for continued labour market participation or cognitively stimulating alternatives after retirement [39]. Evidence from China similarly presents a complex picture: studies conducted in the context of institutionalised delayed retirement have suggested that continued work may be associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment, particularly among women and individuals with lower educational attainment [40], whereas others report slower cognitive decline among older adults engaged in paid work [15, 32]. These mixed findings may partly reflect the unique social and institutional context of China. Compared with many Western countries, older adults in China, particularly those in rural areas, are more likely to continue working in later life due to limited pension coverage, financial necessity, and family responsibilities. At the same time, substantial urban–rural disparities in socioeconomic resources and digital access may further shape the relationship between paid work and cognitive aging. Heterogeneity in work type, work intensity, employment quality, and regional labour market conditions may therefore contribute to the inconsistent findings reported in previous Chinese studies. Our findings on differences across work types and regions provide further support for this interpretation. Importantly, participation in multiple types of paid work may not necessarily indicate greater cognitive stimulation, but may instead reflect fragmented labour participation or continued work driven by financial necessity among some older adults in China.

Against this background, by adopting a trajectory-based approach, the present study suggests that participation in paid work in later life is associated with more favourable cognitive function trajectories, with the positive association most pronounced in trajectories characterised by higher cognitive levels and relative stability. The findings of this study further suggest that paid work may confer cognitive benefits in later life. Beyond being an economic activity, paid work represents continued engagement in social roles and, by maintaining structured daily routines, task-related responsibilities, and social interactions, may provide sustained cognitive stimulation that helps preserve higher levels of cognitive function among older adults [8, 40].

Mediation analyses revealed a differentiated mediating role of digital access in the association between paid work participation and cognitive function trajectories. Specifically, digital access exhibited a statistically significant mediating effect in the comparison between the Low-level rapid decline trajectory and the High-level stable trajectory, whereas no significant mediation was observed between the Low-level rapid decline and the Moderate-level slow decline trajectories. This pattern suggests that digital access may function more as an amplifying rather than a compensatory factor in the relationship between paid work and cognitive ageing.

From the perspective of social interaction and peer effects, digital access among older adults is unlikely to represent an isolated individual choice; instead, it is embedded within work-related and community-based social networks. Continued participation in paid work may enhance perceived usefulness and familiarity with digital technologies through peer influence, thereby increasing the likelihood of digital access and use [41]. Conversely, withdrawal from the labour market has been shown to substantially reduce opportunities for digital engagement [20]. At the same time, digital engagement can provide sustained cognitive stimulation through information acquisition and online learning [42, 43]; however, its cognitive benefits appear to be contingent on educational attainment and access to complementary resources. As a result, digital access may be more effective in maintaining higher levels of cognitive function rather than universally compensating for existing cognitive decline [44]. Viewed through a trajectory-based lens, differences between High-level stable and Moderate-level slow decline trajectories reflect not only variation in the rate of cognitive decline, but also broader disparities in cognitive reserve and functional maintenance capacity. Older adults with higher cognitive reserve may be better positioned to translate digital access into meaningful cognitive stimulation, thereby reinforcing the positive association between paid work participation and cognitive health. Taken together, these findings suggest that digital access operates as a conditional resource, amplifying the cognitive benefits of paid work primarily among older adults who already possess relatively higher levels of functional capacity and social role engagement.

Further analyses distinguishing types of paid work revealed differences in their associations with cognitive function trajectories, suggesting that the cognitive implications of paid work in later life may vary according to job content and task characteristics. Compared with other forms of work, non-agricultural employment and agricultural wage employment are more likely to involve complex information processing, rule adherence, and social interaction, which may provide older adults with more sustained and multidimensional cognitive stimulation. Consistent with the “use-it-or-lose-it” hypothesis and cognitive reserve theory, prolonged engagement in cognitively demanding activities may contribute to the accumulation and maintenance of cognitive reserve, thereby slowing cognitive decline in later life [8]. Evidence from a life-course perspective further supports this interpretation, as prior research has shown that long-term participation in non-agricultural employment is associated with better cognitive function in old age [18]. In contrast, although agricultural self-employment also exhibited a protective association, its cognitive demands are often more limited and may rely predominantly on physical labour. Under conditions characterized by physically demanding tasks, low-skilled work, and limited social protection, the potential cognitive benefits of work may be partly offset by increased work-related burden and health risks [45]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the cognitive health effects of paid work in later life cannot be adequately captured by a simple dichotomy of working versus not working. Rather, it is important to consider differences in labour type and job characteristics in order to more comprehensively understand how paid work participation relates to heterogeneous cognitive function trajectories among older adults.

Regional heterogeneity analyses showed that the association between paid work participation and entry into more favorable cognitive function trajectories was more pronounced in the central-western regions. In areas with relatively limited resources, the income supplementation, social connections, and structuring of daily activities associated with paid work may yield greater marginal benefits. By contrast, in the more resource-rich eastern regions, broader access to social protection, public services, and alternative sources of cognitive stimulation may weaken the relative role of paid work. These findings indicate that the cognitive benefits of paid work participation may be shaped by regional resource environments and broader social disadvantages in later life [46].

This study still has several limitations. First, as an observational study, although a wide range of covariates were controlled for and robustness analyses were conducted, it remains difficult to fully rule out reverse causality and unobserved confounding. Second, paid work was measured using available CHARLS questionnaire items, which did not provide detailed information on working hours, work intensity, or job quality. Therefore, differences across work categories may partly reflect variation in work frequency or intensity, and the observed differences across paid work categories should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future studies may incorporate richer indicators of work characteristics to more finely characterize the association between paid work participation and cognitive function trajectories. In addition, digital access was measured as a household-level proxy; future research could further integrate more direct indicators, such as digital skills, frequency of use, and usage contexts, to validate and refine the pathways through which the digital environment may operate.

Despite these limitations, our findings have implications for practice and policy. Later-life paid work may reflect active engagement associated with more favorable cognitive function trajectories, suggesting relevance for clinical awareness and community-based cognitive health promotion. At the policy level, the results highlight the importance of focusing on the quality and conditions of later-life work, alongside complementary social and digital support, rather than paid work participation alone. In China and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), integrated approaches linking age-friendly employment, digital inclusion, and community support may help promote healthier cognitive ageing, and warrant further exploration among older cohorts across diverse LMIC settings.

Conclusion

Using four waves of longitudinal data from CHARLS, this study examined the association between paid work participation and cognitive function development among older adults from a trajectory-based perspective. The results show that engagement in paid work is associated with more favorable cognitive function trajectories, and that digital access mediates the maintenance of High-level stable cognitive function trajectories. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of moving beyond a binary view of working versus not working to consider job quality and supportive environments in understanding cognitive ageing in later life.
