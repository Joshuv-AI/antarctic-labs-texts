Impact of Chemical Composition on Aerosol Scattering: Insights from the Surface Particulate Matter Network and Aerosol Robotic Network
Authors: Sujin Eom, Sang Seo Park, Xuan Liu, Christopher R. Oxford, Randall V. Martin, Jhoon Kim, Chang-Keun Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Using the Surface Particulate Matter Network (SPARTAN) and Aerosol Robotic Network (AERONET), we investigated the relationship between aerosol chemical composition and optical properties across 14 global sites. The mass concentrations of ammonium sulfate (AS), ammonium nitrate (AN), fine soil (FS), and black carbon (BC) from SPARTAN were collocated with aerosol optical properties (aerosol optical depth (AOD), fine mode fraction (FMF), and single scattering albedo (SSA)) from AERONET. Significant differences in the optical properties of samples from 2016 to 2023 were identified based on the mass and mass ratios of the chemical components. For the BC–FS relationship in the data set used in this study, increased FS mass lowered FMF and dSSABC–FS (SSA440–SSA870) by approximately 0.033 and 0.005 per 1 μg/m3, respectively. Higher ratios of nonabsorbing components (AS and AN) to BC (w = (AS + AN)/(AS + AN + BC)) increased SSA at all wavelengths. The correlation between w and SSA was stronger at longer wavelengths, with SSA440 and SSA1020 increasing by approximately 0.026 and 0.046 per 10% increase in w from the data set, respectivley. Site-specific results showed that dSSA w and rSSA (SSA440/SSA1020) increased as the BC proportion rose (w decreased). This study emphasizes the utility of both dSSA and rSSA, alongside single-wavelength SSA, in understanding aerosol behavior. Integrating columnar optical data with surface-level chemical measurements, the combined SPARTAN and AERONET approach offers valuable insight into aerosol classification, behavior, and atmospheric impacts.

Introduction

Understanding the types and characteristics of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is essential for evaluating its roles in atmospheric processes, radiative forcing, and environmental and health impacts. − PM2.5 includes aerosols such as black carbon (BC), sulfate, and nitrate, each with distinct optical and chemical properties. − BC absorbs solar radiation, while sulfate scatters light and induces cooling. , Thus, detailed analyses are necessary to understand their complex behaviors and interactions.

Aerosol type analyses rely on two main approaches: field techniques and optical remote sensing. Field techniques provide accurate chemical composition and help clarify regional distributions, − while remote sensing enables broader-scale observations , and is useful for identifying specific aerosol types like dust, which shows strong shortwave absorption and large particle sizes. , For example, Cappa et al. revised earlier classification methods proposed by Cazorla et al. to focus on carbonaceous aerosols using the scattering Ångström exponent (SAE), while Lee et al. applied fine mode fraction (FMF) and single scattering albedo (SSA) to distinguish among dust, BC and nonabsorbing aerosols (NA). Liu et al. reported that the absorption Ångström exponent (AAE) of BC is influenced by mixing with non-BC components.

However, optical-based type classification cannot provide accurate chemical information, and studies quantitatively linking optical properties to chemical composition remain limited. ,− This is primarily due to the challenges associated with long-term field sampling and the limited spatial and temporal coverage of chemical. These limitations pose significant obstacles to fully understanding aerosol types and their impacts on climate and health. Therefore, continuous and multiregional efforts that systematically integrate optical and chemical data, as well as coordinated long-term observational frameworks across multiple regions, are required to clarify these relationships and bridge this gap.

This study addresses a research gap by integrating optical and chemical data. By combining data from the Aerosol Robotic Network (AERONET) and the Surface Particulate Matter Network (SPARTAN), we evaluate the effectiveness and limitations of optical–chemical matching as a means of understanding aerosol types through their optical characteristics and propose SSA as a useful parameter for distinguishing absorbing and nonabsorbing aerosols. The results of this study are expected to enhance understanding of aerosol impacts on atmospheric processes and radiative forcing, thereby supporting global efforts to address climate and air quality challenges.

Data and Methods

Ground-Based Data

SPARTAN

SPARTAN is a long-term research project for characterizing the chemical and physical properties of aerosols using globally collected filter samples. The project seeks to improve remote sensing simulations and support air quality management. With 29 active sampling sites worldwide, chemical analyses are primarily conducted using filter-based observations, focusing on PM2.5, including water-soluble ions, BC, and elements, and the reconstruction of chemical speciation data for identifying major aerosol constituents. − For this study, we focused on PM2.5, BC, ammonium sulfate (AS), ammonium nitrate (AN), and fine soil (FS).

SPARTAN observations began in 2013, and many sites continue to operate as of 2024, highlighting the project’s commitment to long-term data collection, allowing identification of trends and insights that are inaccessible through short-term studies. More importantly, filter-based observations allow for direct analysis of the physical and chemical properties of aerosols, providing precise compositional information that cannot be adequately resolved using remote sensing techniques.

AERONET

AERONET provides high-quality measurements of aerosol optical properties (AOPs) at more than 400 global stations, covering diverse climatic regions, including urban, desert, marine, and mountainous environments, facilitates the analysis of global aerosol distributions and temporal trends. As part of AERONET, CE-318 sun-sky radiometer data were used in this study. CE-318 automatically tracks the sun to provide aerosol optical depth (AOD) at eight wavelengths ranging from 340 to 1640 nm and retrieves columnar optically effective aerosol properties (e.g., SSA, volume size distribution, and refractive index) from sky radiance measurements at four wavelengths: 440, 675, 870, and 1020 nm. AERONET retrieval employs hybrid sky scans that combine azimuthal and zenith movements to provide intermediate data between almucantar and principal plane scans, and derive inversion products, including SSA. −

This study employed Version 3 data with robust quality control measures to ensure a comprehensive analysis. , While Level 2 data are typically used for accurate analyses owing to their stringent quality control, this study utilized cloud-screened Level 1.5 data to maximize the data set. Additional filtering was applied (Text S1) to enhance the data quality and align with the objectives of this study.

Collocation Method

Location and Products

Because SPARTAN stations are colocated with those of AERONET, this study obtained simultaneous data from the same locations (Table S1). We first collected all data that met Conditions 1 and 2, then selected only those sites with at least five matched data points available (Text S1). As a result, a total of 215 data points were obtained from 14 observation sites located in various cities worldwide (Figure S1). Sites with fewer than five data points after data filtering and collocation were then excluded from the site-specific analysis. The total effective data span from 2016 to August 2023 (as of October 2024).

To investigate the relationship between chemical and optical properties, mass concentration (μg/m3) data were obtained from SPARTAN, and AOPs were sourced from AERONET. This study focused on key components: AS, AN, FS, BC, and total PM2.5, selected for their relevance to aerosol scattering and absorption processes. AS and AN are major contributors to light scattering, which affects visibility and radiation balance. In contrast, BC is a light-absorbing component that has a substantial warming effect. Soil primarily affects absorption in the UV range, and total PM2.5 serves as a comprehensive indicator of particulate matter pollution. This study utilized AERONET data, including AOD as an aerosol amount information along with FMF and SSA, which offer insights into aerosol size and scattering properties. The integration of these data from SPARTAN and AERONET enabled the investigation of the relationship between absorption and scattering properties.

Analysis

This study used AOPs measured by AERONET and chemical composition data from SPARTAN during overlapping periods. Default SPARTAN protocol collects PM2.5 chemical species for a 24-h duration over 3-h sampling periods occurring every 27 h, forming a 9-d sampling cycle. Several sites (Table S1) associated with the MAIA satellite mission collect more frequent 24-h samples every 2–3 days. , In a similar approach, Liu et al. also analyzed 1-d sampling data from the MAIA protocol alongside all data collected using the existing protocol. However, given that the 9-d sampling data can provide a more stable representation of overall variation trends, it was deemed more appropriate to prioritize the analysis of this data set. Consequently, the more frequent 24-h samples were excluded from the analysis in this study to avoid the additional variability of regional effects.

AERONET provides high-frequency data and reports daily averages. As shown in Figure S2, the daily data from AERONET are collocated and averaged according to the SPARTAN sampling periods to harmonize the two data sets. However, due to difference in sampling timeSPARTAN has the 24-h sample duration while AERONET data are daytime-onlydirect comparisons are challenging. Although limiting comparisons to the same time periods would provide more accurate results, this approach would lead to the loss of approximately half of the SPARTAN data. To address this limitation and retain as much SPARTAN data as possible while ensuring consistency and reliability, we applied the data filtering conditions explained in Text S1.

To link columnar properties with surface PM data, it is important to consider both PM2.5 and PM10 components. However, this study used PM2.5-based chemical composition data due to limitations in the temporal resolution and PM10 data availability and processing. In particular, fine soil (FS), typically related to dust, has to be considered to the influence of coarse-mode particles. Nevertheless, considering its optical characteristics and typical covariation with PM10 under normal atmospheric conditions, FS was considered a reasonable proxy for dust (Text S2).

Results and Discussion

Absorbing Aerosols

Differences in SSA and FMF

Many studies have focused on absorbing components such as soil and BC (e.g., refs , , and ). Because of the SSA definition, it can also serve as a valuable tool for comparing the absorption contributions of various components. Derimian et al. compared the mass concentrations of iron and BC using sampler data and analyzed their relationship with the SSA differences between short and long wavelengths from AERONET. From Eck et al., the difference between SSA at long and short wavelengths (defined in their study as SSA870 – SSA440) near or below 0 suggests BC dominance, whereas a difference greater than 0.05 typically indicates dust-dominated aerosols. In this study, we adopt the similar definition (SSA440 – SSA870, hereafter referred to as dSSABC‑FS) for consistency with the increasing wavelength axis and to facilitate interpretation in terms of absorption characteristics. Following this definition, we examined the relationship between the dSSABC‑FS and the mass difference between BC and FS using SPARTAN to assess differences in scattering behavior across wavelengths.

In Figure , the dSSABC–FS exhibits an increasing trend with the BC–FS mass difference, with most data points concentrated within the ranges of −0.05 to 0.05 and −5 to 5 μg/m3, respectively. The average values of dSSABC–FS varied between a maximum of 0.0298 and a minimum of −0.0246. For BC–FS values below −5 μg/m3, dSSABC–FS remained relatively stable (around −0.04). It could be due to limited data points or the possibility that once the mass difference exceeded this threshold, further increases did not lead to a linear increase in dSSABC–FS. This tendency reflects the physical properties of aerosols; iron in soil enhances absorption at shorter wavelengths, whereas BC is strongly absorbing at longer wavelengths. ,

Using the characteristic of size information, it has been used in several studies to identify aerosol sources, such as those of natural and anthropogenic origins. − In this study, we used FMF to compare the size characteristics of FS and BC. The FMF varied with the mass difference between the FS and BC (Figure b). When averaging FMF within each bin, decreasing trend was observed as the FS mass increased, particularly in the range of −5 to 5 μg/m3, where most data points were concentrated. The FMF values ranged from 0.843 to 0.548, showing an evident decrease. Similar to dSSABC–FS, FMF showed a flattening trend as the mass of FS increased significantly, suggesting reduced variability. It confirms that the mass difference between BC and FS leads to significant differences in their absorption and size characteristics, in agreement with prior studies that emphasize the role of aerosol composition and size in determining optical properties. − By performing a 1-dimensional fitting, we found that dSSABC–FS changed by 0.005 per 1 μg/m3 difference in the range of −5 to 5 μg/m3, while FMF changed by 0.033. When the mass difference exceeded a certain threshold, the difference flattened; this occurs when the FS mass becomes higher than the BC mass. Although these results were statistically noticeable, caution is needed in interpretation, as vertical distribution and meteorological conditions were not fully accounted for in this analysis.

The data were grouped into four categories based on the BC–FS mass difference (Figure S3). Within the most populous range (−5 to 5 μg/m3), the group with high BC mass exhibited lower SSA values across all wavelengths. Additionally, owing to the influence of BC, the difference at the longer wavelength of 1020 nm was more pronounced than that at shorter wavelengths. In contrast, the groups with high FS masses exhibited particularly low SSA values at 440 nm. Giles et al. reported generally lower SSA440 values in dust source regions and lower SSA1020 values in BC source regions. Furthermore, the SSA values at 675, 870, and 1020 nm of the FS-dominant groups (blue and green) were above 0.95, similar to the tendency of the dust group. The SSA values between the green and blue groups did not significantly differ, which suggests that when the mass difference between the two components exceeds a certain threshold, the dSSA difference no longer becomes apparent. The relationships between the mass difference, SSA, and FMF suggest a range in which this mass increase effectively influences the optical properties. Given the strong optical influence of FS, the following section focuses on fine-mode aerosols, excluding high-FS cases for clearer interpretation.

Fine-Mode Aerosols

Understanding the influence of fine-mode aerosols is crucial for assessing their optical and radiative effects. By excluding cases with significant contributions from coarse-mode particles (FS > 5 μg/m3), we evaluated the interplay between fine-mode aerosols more effectively. To ensure the consistency of our analysis, we performed further analyses by restricting the data set to cases where the FS/PM2.5 mass fraction did not exceed 20% and 10%, respectively (Figures S4 and S5). We focused on three components with contrasting optical properties: BC for absorbing aerosol, and AS and AN for NAs. We hypothesized that the interaction and relative contributions of these aerosol types significantly influenced optical characteristics, particularly the SSA.

Following Ramana et al. who showed that solar absorption efficiency increases with the BC-to-sulfate mass ratio, we extended this concept by examining the relationship between the relative proportion of NAs (AS and AN) and scattering properties such as SSA. We calculated the mass fraction of NAs as w=AS+ANAS+AN+BC 1

This approach allowed us to analyze the fraction of NAs in relation to the SSA, thereby providing insights into the scattering efficiency and its dependence on aerosol composition.

SSA Correlation

Figure shows the correlation between the SSA values at the four wavelengths and the mass fraction of NAs (w). The correlation coefficients show 0.38, 0.46, 0.44, and 0.45 at 440, 675, 870, and 1020 nm, respectively. The stronger correlation at longer wavelengths is likely attributed to the greater influence of BC at these wavelengths. According to hypothesis, SSA values should increase as the w increases; however, this tendency was not evident in the data. The absence of a pronounced trend may stem from methodological differences between the in situ and remote sensing observations. In-situ measurements sample the atmosphere near the surface, whereas remote sensing instruments, such as AERONET, provide column-integrated data. Such differences could explain the discrepancies observed in the relationship between the chemical composition and optical properties. Although PM2.5 constitutes an important portion of atmospheric aerosols, it does not fully represent the entire aerosol population. Factors such as vertical concentration profiles, humidity variability, and the contribution of coarse particles can all influence column-integrated optical properties, making it difficult to fully interpret these characteristics based solely on PM2.5 data. − Nevertheless, previous studies have reported meaningful correlations between surface PM2.5 and AOD, with a global average correlation coefficient of approximately 0.55. Furthermore, PM2.5 generally shows a stronger correlation with AOD than PM10, suggesting that AOD is less sensitive to changes in coarse-mode mass. In addition, several studies , reported specific atmospheric conditions lead to improve between surface and columnar variables. Previous findings suggest that PM2.5 data can still provide valuable insight into columnar optical behavior.

To minimize these discrepancies between the in situ and remote sensing observations, we attempted to resolve the differences in aerosol characteristics by using the PM2.5/AOD500 value. Although specific characteristics are difficult to quantitatively explain using only PM2.5/AOD500, a higher value generally suggests that aerosols are closer to the surface. Based on this relationship, we sequentially divided the data from cases with lower to higher PM2.5/AOD500 values for the analysis.

In Figure a, the PM2.5/AOD500 cases were sequentially excluded from the bottom 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% groups (Ex20, Ex40, Ex60, and Ex80, respectively), showing the results for SSA and w. The SSA at 440 and 870 nm showed a high correlation (>0.6) under Ex60 conditions (Figure b,d). It indicates that grouping by PM2.5/AOD500 effectively reduced the gap between surface observations and remote sensing data by mitigating the discrepancies caused by extreme values or measurement inconsistencies. However, for Ex80, the correlation became difficult to confirm because of the excessive removal of data points, which overly narrowed the data set and potentially masked meaningful trends.

In Ex20 and Ex40, the SSA was notably high (>0.95) in some cases, even when w was relatively low (<0.7). However, these outliers were excluded for Ex60, leading to an improved correlation. The correlation is particularly more reliable at longer wavelengths, with the SSA reaching a higher value of 0.69. When w was binned in increments of 0.05, the correlation improved significantly, with R values of 0.87 at 440 nm, 0.91 at 870 nm, and 0.92 at 1020 nm for Ex60 (Figure and Figure S6). The relationship between binned w and SSA in Ex60 showed more reliable correlations compared to those in Ex20 and Ex40. At 440 nm of Ex60, when w was 0.6, the SSA was approximately 0.87, and when w increased to 0.9, the SSA increased to approximately 0.95 (Figure c). This increase was more pronounced at 1020 nm, where the SSA was around 0.80 at a w of 0.6 and approximately 0.94 at a w of 0.9 (Figure S6, Ex60). Based on the data set used in this study, within the w range of 0.6–0.9, it can be estimated that for every 10% increase in the w, the SSA increased by approximately 0.026 at 440 nm and 0.046 at 1020 nm.

Therefore, the observed correlation between w and SSA indicates that accounting for extreme values within the data set can play a significant role in effectively bridging the gap between in situ measurements and remote sensing observations. Furthermore, the larger variations in the SSA observed at longer wavelengths emphasize its stronger sensitivity to changes in w at these wavelengths, underlining the importance of considering spectral dependencies.

Multiwavelength Analysis

Multiwavelength analyses provide more information than single-wavelength approaches by accounting for the spectral dependence of absorption characteristics. Previous studies have utilized multiwavelength methods to explore aerosol properties, such as chemical mass ratios, SSA curvature for emission source comparison, and dust characteristics.

We used two wavelengths to analyze the AOPs, focusing on dSSA w (SSA440–SSA870) and rSSA (SSA440/SSA1020) (Figure ). The 440–870 nm wavelength pair has been widely used as a standard indicator of the spectral dependence of SSA. To maintain consistency with the parameters introduced in Figure , the same metrics are applied to the y-axes of Figure a,b, enabling a coherent interpretation of spectral dependence. In this study, correlations between w and SSA at each wavelength exhibited relatively stable behavior not only at 870 nm but also at the longer wavelength of 1020 nm. This finding is particularly relevant for BC, as SSA at longer wavelengths tends to more effectively capture its optical characteristics. , Accordingly, in addition to utilizing a wavelength difference, we introduced a ratio-based metric incorporating the longer wavelength to better reflect the optical influence of BC and to interpret the spectral behavior of SSA from a broader perspective. By combining dSSA w and rSSA, the spectral dependence of SSA can be evaluated more comprehensively, and it can capture variations in the absorbing and nonabsorbing components across wavelengths, enhancing both the stability and depth of this analysis.

In Figure , both dSSA w and rSSA were negatively correlated with w. This is because as the proportion of nonabsorbing components in fine particles increases (when scattering characteristics dominate), the SSA decreases less significantly at longer wavelengths. This relationship was observed not only for dSSA w but also for rSSA, with the average correlation reaching >0.6 when considering absolute values. The best results in this regard were found in Ex60 case, similar to the result of the single-wavelength SSA (Figure ).

The correlations observed in Figure a,c (R = −0.6 and −0.63) are weaker than the results at SSA870 (0.69; Figure d). However, when using the mean of the w bins, the correlation significantly improved, reaching −0.95 (Figure b,d). Based on the data set used in this study, within the w range of 0.6–0.9, it can be estimated that for every 10% increase in the w, dSSA w and rSSA decreased by approximately 0.014 and 0.024, respectively (Table , Ex60 case). Since these values are not normalized, this study does not aim to compare the absolute magnitudes of the sensitivity of optical property. Instead, the focus was on identifying which parameter is more sensitive to changes in nonabsorbing components. Accordingly, both dSSA w and rSSA were employed to enhance the interpretability of spectral variations in SSA. This approach is supported by the fact that, as mentioned earlier, dSSA w effectively captures the spectral dependence of SSA by utilizing the absolute difference between short and midwavelengths, while rSSA responds sensitively to the presence of BC, which exhibits stronger absorption at longer wavelengths. Both parameters demonstrated reliable correlations in this study, and their relationships with binned w were found to be slightly improved (Δ|R| = 0.04) over those observed for single-wavelength SSA, thereby enabling enhanced characterization of the wavelength-dependent variations associated with w.

The average values for each site in Figure a,b show rSSA values ranging from 1 to 1.1 and dSSA w values from 0 to 0.05, corresponding to the w values in the range of 0.7–0.9. In Figure c, the dSSABC–FS shows a continuous variation from 0.05 to −0.05. These results also revealed distinct site-specific characteristics. Mexico City exhibited the highest dSSA w and rSSA values when w was <0.7 (Figure a,b). Abu Dhabi and Ilorin had the lowest dSSABC–FS values (Figure c), and the smallest average value of BC–FS because these two sites had relatively high concentrations of FS (Figure S7).

To further examine the site-specific properties, box plots were used to visualize the distributions of dSSA w and rSSA, and pie charts depicted the mass fractions of the components (Figure a,b). The sites were arranged based on the median dSSA w and rSSA values to facilitate a more precise comparison. Both figures demonstrated consistent patterns; as dSSA w and rSSA increased, the combined AS and AN mass fraction decreased, whereas the proportion of BC increased. Although the tendency was not perfectly linear, consistency was observed for the dSSA w and rSSA. If only a single wavelength of the SSA was considered, identifying this trend would be more challenging (Figure c,d).

Seoul, which had the highest fraction of NAs, exhibited the lowest dSSA w and rSSA values. As highlighted by Liu et al., sulfate and nitrate aerosols have been consistently significant in East Asia, emphasizing the influence of substantial natural and anthropogenic emissions on the region’s atmospheric composition. This further supports the trends observed in the urban aerosol characteristics in Seoul. Furthermore, Seoul and Beijing exhibited high proportions of sulfates and nitrates, with Seoul having a particularly high proportion of AN. This finding is consistent with the results observed in 2020, in which Qiu et al. reported that the nitrate fraction in Seoul was approximately 1.5–2 times higher than that in Beijing, thereby supporting our results.

Mexico City exhibited a high BC fraction, which appeared to have resulted in high dSSA w and rSSA values owing to the greater impact of BC on longer wavelengths than on shorter wavelengths in SSA. Another study found that Mexico City was affected by carbonaceous aerosols and had low SSA. Although this outcome may be attributed to the BC concentration, other factors may have also played a role. The average BC concentration in Mexico City was 2.09 μg/m3 (Figure S7), which was not particularly high compared with those in other regions, where this value is often exceeded. BC emissions are known to be significantly higher in East Asia. Wang et al. reported that the BC emissions in East Asia during the 1960s and 2000s were more than ten times higher than those in Mexico. Notably, the surface sulfate mass concentration in Mexico City was less than half that in East Asia. This may have contributed to the relatively high BC fraction observed in the SPARTAN data. This low sulfate concentration is likely due to Mexico’s lower SO2 emissions compared with other regions. Thus, the relatively low proportion of NAs and the higher dSSA w and rSSA values observed in Mexico City likely reflect the influence of regional pollutant distribution and local characteristics.

This study provides valuable insights into the differences in aerosol properties between Mexico City in North America and East Asia, particularly with respect to the distinct optical characteristics of absorbing and nonabsorbing components. These results emphasize the importance of jointly assessing both types of aerosols to better understand the spectral variability of SSA. Considering the contrasting optical properties of these components allows for a more comprehensive understanding of aerosol characteristics across wavelengths.

Implication for Further Study

Based on the results, we were able to explain the unique characteristics of each region through comparisons of the ratios of nonabsorbing and absorbing components. Although our detailed analysis focused on two contrasting regions in terms of aerosol composition-Mexico City and Seoul, reasonable inferences could be made regarding how specific environmental factors influenced their distribution. The mass ratios and corresponding scattering characteristics exhibited a continuous pattern across sites, suggesting that aerosol composition follows a regional trend influenced by local aerosol characteristics, atmospheric conditions, and climate-related elements. This provides valuable insights into aerosol physical properties and behavior, offering foundational data for future studies.

SSA was shown to be a valuable parameter for understanding the influence of NAs and BC, especially when using multiple wavelengths. Our results highlight the importance of considering continuous characteristics rather than fixed aerosol types. Although determining the exact mass of each component is difficult, it may be possible to estimate scattering characteristics using component ratios, or infer ratios based on scattering values. These general patterns could be extended to other sites and satellite analyses as more data become available. However, since an AOD threshold of 0.2 was applied in the present data set, direct application of these findings to regions with generally low AOD levels, such as the United States, may be limited, and care should be taken when generalizing to these regions.

Despite differences in temporal resolution and filtering between AERONET and SPARTAN, our approach allowed us to harmonize the data sets and effectively capture broad trends in AOPs and chemical composition, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating distinct observational platforms.
