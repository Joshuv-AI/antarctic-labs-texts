Association between postoperative nocturnal blood pressure rising and mortality in patients receiving cardiac surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wen-wen Song, Yi Liu, Qin-dong Liu, Long-he Xu, Han Gao, Yong-zhe Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePostoperative blood pressure (BP) patterns may provide prognostic information in cardiac surgery patients, yet their clinical significance remains unclear. We investigated the association between early postoperative nocturnal BP rising and mortality in this population.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study used the MIMIC-IV database (2008–2019) including adult patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or valvular surgery. Patients with an intensive care unit (ICU) stay less than 24 h, insufficient BP records, or indeterminate ICU admission were excluded. Nocturnal BP rising was defined as nocturnal mean arterial pressure (MAP) exceeding diurnal MAP within the first 24 h postoperatively. Outcomes included 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality. Restricted cubic splines, Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations between nocturnal BP rising and mortality, adjusting for confounders. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also conducted to confirm consistency and robustness.ResultsA total of 6139 patients were included in this study. Among them, 1997 (32.5%) exhibited nocturnal BP rising. Patients with nocturnal BP rising experienced higher mortality rates at 90-day (3.3% vs. 2.0%) and at 1-year (5.9% vs. 3.9%). Adjusted Cox models revealed that nocturnal BP rising was statistically associated with 90-day (HR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.11–2.12) and 1-year mortality (HR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.08–1.74), but not 30-day mortality. In subgroup analysis, there was no significant interaction effect between nocturnal BP rising and mortality across different subgroups. After a series of sensitivity analyses, the association remained consistent.ConclusionsEarly postoperative nocturnal BP rising is common and independently associated with an increased risk of medium- and long-term mortality after cardiac surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05720-0.Keywords: Cardiac surgery, Intensive care unit, Blood pressure, Nocturnal blood pressure rising, Mortality

Introduction

Blood pressure (BP) is a fundamental vital parameter essential for preserving adequate tissue perfusion and organ function. In 1733, an Englishman named Stephen Hales measured BP for the first time and suggested that it was dynamically variable, which was neglected for hundreds of years [1]. With advances in medical technology, invasive arterial BP monitoring has become standard practice for continuous hemodynamic assessment, particularly in cardiac surgery and intensive care unit (ICU) settings. Despite this capability, the temporal characteristics of BP in critically ill patients remain insufficiently studied. Among these, the circadian BP pattern represents a potentially important yet underexplored dimension of hemodynamic regulation.

Under physiological conditions, BP exhibits a circadian rhythm, characterized by a 10%-20% decrease in nocturnal average BP compared to diurnal BP, also known as the dipping pattern [2]. However, in the ICU, interventions such as sedation, analgesia, mechanical ventilation, and medications may substantially disrupt normal BP regulation [3]. Consequently, BP fluctuations observed in the ICU should not be interpreted as purely physiological circadian rhythms but rather as time-structured hemodynamic patterns influenced by the interaction of pathophysiological processes and therapeutic interventions. Identifying populations in which these patterns may have prognostic value remains a critical yet inadequately explored area of research.

Among critically ill patients, those undergoing cardiac surgery represent a particularly high-risk subgroup. During the early postoperative period, alterations such as systemic inflammatory responses and myocardial dysfunction frequently disrupt BP regulation, rendering these patients especially susceptible to hemodynamic disturbances [4–6]. Abnormal BP has long been recognized as a significant factor contributing to adverse postoperative outcomes in this population. Nevertheless, existing management strategies have predominantly concentrated on overall or absolute BP values, while the influence of early postoperative BP patterns in the ICU on prognosis has received considerably less attention and their prognostic significance remains unclear [7, 8].

In this context, we collected postoperative BP within the first 24 h from patients undergoing cardiac surgery, compared their nocturnal and diurnal BP, and evaluated the correlation between nocturnal BP rising, which represented early postoperative ICU BP patterns, and postoperative mortality. The objective of our study was to investigate the potential utility of this early ICU BP patterns as indicators of postoperative mortality risk, thereby providing evidence to inform clinical BP management strategies for patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) participants aged 18 years or older; (2) individuals who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valvular surgery or a combination of these procedures; (3) patients admitted to the ICU after surgery. The exclusion criteria were: (1) an ICU length of stay less than 24 h; (2) BP monitoring records during the first 24 h fewer than 24 measurements, defined as at least one measurement per hour after exclusion of abnormal BP values; (3) inability to determine the exact time of ICU admission. For patients who underwent multiple cardiac surgeries, only the first procedure was considered. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and ICD-10 codes were used to identify diagnoses and surgeries.

Data source

This is a retrospective cohort study used data extracted from a publicly accessible database, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV, version: V2.2). The database contains comprehensive clinical data for ICU patients at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2019, including demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory and radiology results, medications, comorbidities, nursing notes, physician discharge summaries, and survival outcomes [9]. One of the authors completed the online training for the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative program of the National Institutes of Health and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology to access the database and acquired the data of MIMIC-IV. Given that all patient records in the MIMIC-IV database were anonymized, the requirement for individual patient consent was waived by the institutional review board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center.

Data extraction and process

The following data were extracted from the MIMIC-Ⅳ database: baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, race and body mass index (BMI); lifestyle factors (smoking, drinking); comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), congestive heart failure (CHF), malignant tumor, chronic lung disease (such as chronic bronchitis and asthma), chronic liver disease (such as autoimmune hepatitis and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) and chronic kidney disease; complete invasive mean arterial pressure (MAP) records; hematological test results within one month before surgery (such as erythrocyte count, hemoglobin, albumin and creatinine); use of vasopressor medications (such as norepinephrine) and vasodilator medications (such as nicardipine) during the first 24 h of ICU admission; first Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS); mechanical ventilation status and survival outcomes (30-day, 90-day, 1-year mortality). Missing data in covariable and baseline characteristics were handled using multiple imputation. Specifically, five imputed datasets were generated, each undergoing 10 iterations. Notably, no imputation was conducted on the MAP time-series data. This decision was made to prevent potential artificial alterations to BP fluctuation trajectories, which could subsequently distort exposure variables such as BP variability (BPV). Consequently, patients with insufficient MAP recordings were excluded from the analysis. Comprehensive information regarding the degree of missingness and the specific imputation method employed for each variable is provided in the supplementary appendix (Supplementary Table S2). Data were extracted using SAS 9.4.

Definition and outcomes

In this study, the exposure factor was identified as nocturnal BP rising, characterized by a nocturnal MAP exceeding the diurnal MAP within the initial 24 h post-cardiac surgery in the ICU. The calculation formula was as follows: (nocturnal average MAP minus diurnal average MAP) divided by diurnal average MAP. When the result was ≥ 0, the patient was classified as exhibiting nocturnal BP rising that we analyzed in this article [10]. Our definition of nocturnal BP rising was informed by the concept of the reverse-dipping pattern observed in ambulatory BP monitoring. It is important to note, however, that while nocturnal BP rising in this study is mathematically similar to the traditional reverse-dipping definition, it was assessed using early postoperative ICU MAP measurements rather than through ambulatory monitoring. Consequently, it should be understood as an ICU-specific hemodynamic pattern rather than a genuine circadian BP phenotype. Additionally, we used MAP collected from 7 am to 11 pm to calculate mean diurnal BP and the other to calculate mean nocturnal BP [11]. MAP measurements < 25 mmHg or > 250 mmHg were excluded to ensure the reliability of our analysis. We defined the 30-day, 90-day and 1-year mortality as the study outcomes, with follow-up starting at ICU admission. 30-day mortality was defined as short-term mortality. 90-day and 1-year mortality were defined as medium- and long-term mortality.

Statistics analysis

Data for continuous and categorical variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges and frequencies with percentages, respectively. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney test and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, respectively. First, we used restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves and Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves to explore the relationship between nocturnal BP rising and mortality. RCS models, with three knots placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution, were used to evaluate potential nonlinear relationships between nocturnal BP rising and mortality. Then KM survival analysis was performed for 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year mortality, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between nocturnal BP rising and mortality. First, we conducted a univariable analysis. Subsequently, multivariable models (models 1–5) were then constructed with stepwise adjustment for confounding factors to examine the independent effect of nocturnal BP rising on postoperative mortality. All models were assessed for multicollinearity and tested for the proportional hazards assumption. The results indicated that there was no multicollinearity among the variables, and all models adhered to the proportional hazards assumption; details are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Table S1, S3, S4, S5). The association between nocturnal BP rising and mortality may vary in different groups, which encouraged us to conduct subgroup analyses by age, BMI, sex, race, and presence of hypertension and DM. Finally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Analyses conducted following imputation rigorously complied with Rubin’s rules. Specifically, point estimates were derived by averaging the results across the imputed datasets, while standard errors were calculated by incorporating both within-imputation variance and between-imputation variance. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2, employing the following packages: car, dplyr, flextable, ggplot2, gtsummary, mice, officer, rms, survminer, and survival.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 6139 patients were included in this analysis, among whom 74 death records occurred within 30 days, 148 within 90 days and 279 within 1 year. Figure 1 shows the selection process for patients. Of the included patients, 1997 patients (32.53%) exhibited nocturnal BP rising. The median age of the included patients was 69 years (IQR: 61–76 years), and 28.56% were female. Patients with nocturnal BP rising were more likely to have a higher BMI, to be admitted more urgently and to undergo combined surgery. The mean 24 h MAP did not differ significantly between groups, while patients without nocturnal BP rising showed a higher diurnal MAP and a lower nocturnal MAP. Association between nocturnal BP rising and mortality

As shown in Table 1, 30-day, 90-day and 1-year mortality rates in patients with nocturnal BP rising were higher than in those without (1.7% vs. 1.0%, 3.3% vs. 2.0%, and 5.9% vs. 3.9%, respectively). RCS curves were initially used to investigate the relationship between nocturnal BP patterns and postoperative mortality, which revealed a generally linear association (30-day: P for overall = 0.0014, P for non-linearity = 0.4586; 90-day: P for overall < 0.0001, P for non-linearity = 0.5902; 1-year: P for overall < 0.0001, P for non-linearity = 0.0932) (Figs. 2, 3 and 3). Subsequently, KM curves were plotted and revealed that patients with nocturnal BP rising exhibited significantly higher postoperative mortality than those without, as evidenced by the log-rank test (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). In univariable Cox model, nocturnal BP rising was significantly associated with postoperative mortality (30-day: HR:1.62, 95%CI, 1.04–2.52; 90-day: HR:1.72, 95%CI, 1.25–2.36; 1-year: HR:1.54, 95%CI, 1.22–1.95). After adjusting for potential confounders, the association remained statistically significant for 90-day and 1-year mortality across models 1–5 (90-day: HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.11–2.12; 1-year: HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.74), whereas the association with 30-day mortality was no longer significant (30-day: HR:1.42, 95%CI, 0.91–2.23) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. After excluding participants with missing covariate data (n = 5610), the association between nocturnal BP rising and 90-day and 1-year mortality remained consistent (Supplementary Table S6). Excluding patients who did not receive vasopressors during the first 24 h in the ICU (n = 5120) or those undergoing combined surgical procedures (n = 5158) yielded similar results (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, landmark analyses confirmed that nocturnal BP rising was consistently associated with higher 90-day and 1-year mortality (Supplementary Table S6). Finally, given that variability in ICU admission time may result in unequal sampling density of diurnal and nocturnal BP measurements, potentially introducing bias in the estimation of average BP values, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis. Only patients with a diurnal-to-nocturnal BP measurement ratio within the 5th to 95th percentile were retained (n = 5545). The results remained robust (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

In our study, we explored the correlation between nocturnal BP rising and postoperative mortality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. It turned out that nocturnal BP rising was relatively common and a statistically significant association was observed between nocturnal BP rising and 90-day and 1-year mortality. This relationship showed no significant interaction across different populations and remained robust in sensitivity analyses.

Over an extended period, BP has consistently been recognized as a vital hemodynamic parameter impacting the prognosis of patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Previous research has extensively documented the correlation between intraoperative and early postoperative hypotension or hypertension and adverse outcomes [12–15]. Nevertheless, these studies have predominantly neglected the temporal structural characteristics of postoperative BP, specifically the BP pattern during the initial phase in the ICU. This study was undertaken precisely within this context.

Several mechanisms may potentially explain the observed relationship between early postoperative nocturnal BP rising and postoperative mortality. Firstly, within the ICU environment, surgical stress, inflammatory responses, fluctuations in volume status, and medications collectively influence BP patterns, which may result in acute hemodynamic imbalances [3]. This multifactorial condition may disrupt neuroendocrine homeostasis, potentially leading to short-term fluctuations in autonomic and neurohumoral functions, which have been shown to elevate the risk of morbidity and mortality [16, 17]. Although the exposure in this study is mathematically analogous to the conventional reverse-dipping definition, it reflects an ICU-specific postoperative hemodynamic pattern rather than a physiological circadian phenotype. While most prior evidence derives from ambulatory or chronic hypertension populations, similar hemodynamic stress pathways may also operate in the acute postoperative setting. Specifically, the association between nocturnal BP rising and increased mortality may be mediated by increased cumulative hemodynamic burden on target organs, which is related to ventricular remodeling such as ventricular hypertrophy in the heart, and an increased incidence of arrhythmia, heart failure and other adverse cardiovascular events [2, 18]. In the kidney, nocturnal BP rising has been demonstrated to be linked to renal dysfunction events such as decreased glomerular filtration rate and proteinuria [2]. Regarding the central nervous system, nocturnal BP rising has been associated with increased cerebral perfusion pressure and vascular load, which may contribute to cerebrovascular atherosclerosis and an increased risk of stroke and other acute cerebrovascular events [19]. Finally, the study identified a notable increase in nocturnal BPV among patients exhibiting nocturnal BP rising. This finding suggests that nocturnal BP rising may heighten the risk of organ dysfunction by affecting sympathetic nerve activity [20–22]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms remain speculative in the current study and require further mechanistic investigation.

Importantly, this study did not identify a significant correlation between nocturnal BP rising and short-term mortality while the impact on medium- to long-term mortality remained evident. This pattern may indicate that the observed relationship is more closely related to cumulative or progressive organ damage rather than immediate postoperative events [23]. It is also worth noting that, due to the relatively low incidence of short-term mortality events, the study may lack sufficient statistical power to detect early effects. Consequently, future research with larger sample sizes is necessary for validation.

Our findings provide new evidence for BP management after cardiac surgery. Most patients are transferred to the ICU for continuous monitoring after cardiac surgery. While achieving target BP values remains important, its dynamic fluctuation especially nocturnal BP rising may reflect a higher-risk hemodynamic phenotype according to the results of this study. Therefore, continuous BP monitoring for at least 24 h after cardiac surgery may facilitate more comprehensive comparison of diurnal and nocturnal BP, thus assisting in identifying patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes. Moreover, it is particularly significant that, although nocturnal BP rising was not associated with short-term mortality, its correlation with medium- and long-term mortality indicates that transient BP abnormalities should not be routinely dismissed as compensatory physiological responses. Finally, although these findings highlight the potential value of incorporating BP pattern assessment, in addition to absolute BP targets, into postoperative risk stratification, prospective validation is required before clinical implications can be drawn.

This study has several limitations. First, the impact of vasopressors and vasodilators on the relationship between nocturnal BP rising and prognosis remains ambiguous. In this study, adjustments were made solely based on the presence or absence of vasoactive drug use, without accounting for dosage, duration, or the temporal sequence of exposure, thereby limiting the ability to infer underlying mechanisms. From a causal structural perspective, the use of vasoactive drugs may indicate underlying circulatory instability, acting as a confounding factor, or it may serve as an intermediate variable in specific pathways. However, during the early postoperative ICU period, nocturnal BP rising typically occurs following the initiation of vasoactive drugs and is more likely to represent a phenotypic manifestation of hemodynamic instability. Future research should integrate high-temporal-resolution BP data with detailed drug exposure information to elucidate the causal relationship. Additionally, some studies have pointed out that nocturnal BP rising occurred before cardiac surgery [24], but we could not collect preoperative BP, and the influence of patients’ preoperative nocturnal BP pattern on our results and the specific reasons for postoperative nocturnal BP rising are still unknown. The exclusion of patients with an ICU stay of less than 24 h may introduce selection bias and potentially distort the true association between nocturnal BP rising and mortality risk. Furthermore, in the multivariable model used to exploring the correlation between nocturnal BP rising and mortality, we failed to adjust the history of cardiovascular medications due to the limitation of the database. Its influence on our outcomes needs to be further studied. Finally, as a retrospective, single-center study, selection bias is inevitable.

Conclusion

Early postoperative nocturnal BP rising occurs frequently in ICU patients after cardiac surgery and is independently associated with higher 90-day and 1-year mortality. These findings underscore the importance of continuous 24-hour BP monitoring and suggest that dynamic BP patterns, rather than absolute BP targets alone, should be considered in postoperative hemodynamic management.
