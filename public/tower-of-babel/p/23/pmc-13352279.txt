Heterogeneous Composite Design and 3D Printing Parameter Optimization of Al2O3 Ceramic Triply Periodic Minimal Surface Structures
Authors: Wei Liu, Kai Ren, Jinglei Zhou, Jinchao Zhao, Qi Liu, Liang Chen, Shanchen Zhou, Zongbo Zheng, Lei Tu, Yanlong Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS) Al2O3 ceramic structures hold significant potential for applications in biomedical engineering, catalytic systems, and thermal management technologies, which can be fabricated with high precision using vat photopolymerization-based digital light processing (DLP). However, conventional homogeneous designs for TPMS often do not fully meet advanced performance requirements, while the curing depth, a key parameter governing the dimensional accuracy and mechanical performance, has not been thoroughly investigated. To address these limitations, this study proposes an algorithm based on target porosity for designing complex graded and heterogeneous TPMS structures, followed by fabrication via DLP with optimized process parameters. The results demonstrate that the fabricated gradient-porosity TPMS scaffold under optimal parameters exhibited high performance, including dimensional accuracy exceeding 98.5%, a surface roughness as low as 1.21 μm, and a compressive strength of 38.44 MPa. These findings provide valuable references for both the design optimization and reliable DLP strategies of complex ceramic heterogeneous TPMS structures.

Introduction

Al2O3 ceramics possess outstanding mechanical properties, chemical stability, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature durability, and have thus been widely adopted in various engineering fields such as aerospace, electronics, thermal management, and biomedical engineering. Triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structure is a periodic implicit porous scaffold characterized by zero mean curvature, highly interconnected pores, and infinitely extensible minimal surfaces. Its distinctive geometric features, including ultrahigh specific surface area and high specific strength, enable exceptional energy absorption capacity as well as superior heat transfer and sound insulation performance. These advantages have led to widespread applications in catalysis, thermal management, filtration, lightweight design, and medical tissue engineering. Consequently, TPMS-structured Al2O3 ceramics, which integrate the advantages of high specific surface area, excellent mechanical properties, and superior thermal stability, have attracted increasing research attention and hold promising application prospects.

The design parameters of TPMS structures, including cell type, porosity, and unit cell size, significantly influence comprehensive performance metrics such as mechanical properties, heat transfer efficiency, and catalytic effectiveness. However, a single TPMS unit type cannot fulfill all desired performance criteria simultaneously. Consequently, graded and heterogeneous TPMS structures present greater practical value and application potential in engineering fields, particularly in the design of functionally graded structures featuring porosity gradients, multitopology combinations, and stiffness tailoring in hybrid TPMS lattice regions. Nevertheless, due to structural disparities between distinct TPMS units, the continuity and smoothness of hybrid transition zones can substantially impact the overall mechanical performance. Therefore, establishing rational volume fraction control rules for transition regions becomes critically important.

Existing studies have predominantly focused on the fundamental design of TPMS structures. Karakoç et al. proposed a TPMS scaffold generation algorithm based on specific surface function domains, which differs from the conventional structural design approach of thickening minimal surfaces defined by f(x,y,z) = 0, enabling the fabrication of TPMS scaffolds with targeted porosity. Zhang et al. achieved control and optimization of structural parameters such as pore size and porosity by modifying the mathematical implicit equations of TPMS structures. They fabricated six hydroxyapatite (HAp) scaffolds with distinct TPMS architectures using SLA (stereolithography), conducted finite element analysis of their mechanical properties via COMSOL software, and performed experimental validation through compression tests. These studies underscored the paramount importance of parametric design optimization for enhancing the overall performance of TPMS structures. Song et al. designed two types of TPMS porous scaffolds with gradient porosity variations along different spatial orientations, evaluating the influence of TPMS design parameters on biomechanical performance through compression tests and in vitro culture experiments. Gain et al. designed pore-gradient TPMS structures using the MSLattice software and, through systematic analysis, demonstrated that these structures can effectively mimic natural bone, exhibiting significant application potential in the field of orthopedic implants. Samson et al. employed the classical Gyroid TPMS structure as a turbulence-enhanced heat dissipater, investigated the mechanism of heat transfer enhancement by normalizing thermal and hydraulic performance metrics against specific sample volume and wetted surface area, with particular focus on the effects of pore size and porosity among TPMS design parameters.

However, the intricate geometry of TPMS structures and the outstanding mechanical properties of alumina ceramics, including superior strength and hardness, pose significant challenges for processing and forming. Conventional subtractive manufacturing methods exhibit limited machining accuracy while being characterized by high mold dependency, elevated production costs, and prolonged manufacturing cycles. These constraints substantially hinder the high-precision fabrication of complex TPMS Al2O3 ceramic components.

The rapidly advancing Additive Manufacturing (AM) technology in recent years has offered a novel technological pathway to address these issues. It substantially shortens the production time of fabricated components by directly translating 3D design models to printers for AM, facilitating high-precision rapid prototyping of high-performance ceramic parts with complex structures. In principle, AM process is not limited by material or structural complexity while maintaining the forming quality and manufacturing accuracy of fabricated components. Ceramic 3D printing technologies can be classified into three categories based on the form of raw materials used: powder processing (Selective Laser Sintering), slurry processing (Stereo Lithography Apparatus), and bulk solid processing (Deposition Modeling and Laminated Object Manufacturing). Among these, Digital Light Processing (DLP) has emerged as one of the most commercially valuable and promising AM techniques in recent years due to its notable advantages, including high dimensional accuracy and resolution, high printing speed, and a high degree of automation. These characteristics render it particularly suitable for fabricating ceramic components with intricate structures and stringent dimensional tolerance requirements.

The AM process parameters, which typically include laser power, exposure time, and photoabsorber concentration, are critical factors determining the dimensional accuracy and surface quality of fabricated parts. Numerous researchers have extensively investigated and discussed the optimization of AM process parameters. Wu et al. optimized the 3D printing process parameters and carbon content to enhance the performance of Si/SiC ceramic triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures, achieving dimensional accuracies predominantly below 6%. Similarly, Huang et al. systematically investigated the influence of layer thickness and binder saturation in binder-jetting 3D printing, successfully fabricating slender alumina ceramic cores with an optimal top-surface roughness of 18.1 ± 1.3 μm.

First, for ceramic vat photopolymerization 3D printing, particularly in the fabrication of microcomponents with complex geometries, selecting an appropriate layer thickness is crucial. This requires a comprehensive consideration of slurry viscosity, fabrication accuracy, and printing efficiency. Li et al. clarified the underlying mechanisms governing the effects of layer thickness and curing depth on ceramic slurry systems and elucidated their impact on the printing quality of vat-photopolymerized ceramic structures. Using the optimized process parameters derived from their investigation, they successfully achieved superior forming quality, attaining a surface roughness of just 1.3 μm. Second, in DLP fabrication, the selection of process strategies and exposure intensity significantly influences the overall performance of the final printed parts. Chen et al. investigated the influence of process parameters and found that the optimal combination of layer thickness, exposure time, and sintering time significantly enhances the mechanical properties of DLP-fabricated specimens. This optimal parameter set simultaneously improves curing efficiency and increases material densification. Fu et al. demonstrated through experimental data that the build orientation in stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing substantially affects the mechanical properties of fabricated parts. Shen et al. successfully fabricated high-performance TPMS ceramic structures, such as Gyroid and Primitive, using DLP technology. They also investigated the influence of exposure time and build orientation on curing outcome, revealing that varying exposure times alter the curing depth of ceramic slurries, thereby further affecting 3D printing quality. Additionally, numerous studies indicate that incorporating photoabsorbers into ceramic slurries can effectively control curing depth, thereby improving the correlation between curing depth (C d ) and layer thickness (C s ). Schmidt et al. observed that adding azo dyes as photoabsorbers to alumina-filled silicone prepolymer resins during DLP fabrication significantly enhances the z-axis resolution of printed parts, leading to improved dimensional accuracy. Therefore, the precision manufacturing of alumina ceramic TPMS structures with complex geometries and enhanced performance is closely linked to the process optimization of the DLP technology. Adjusting and optimizing DLP process parameters are critical to achieve target dimensional accuracy and mechanical properties.

In summary, current research on TPMS complex structure design has predominantly focuses on metals and biocomposite materials, with a scarcity of literature addressing ceramic materials, particularly regarding the integrated process investigation encompassing slurry formulation, structural fabrication, and performance characterization for complex alumina ceramic TPMS architectures. Furthermore, studies on heterogeneous TPMS functionally graded structures have predominantly emphasized design aspects, while analyses of their manufacturing processes and processing quality remain scarce. Based on these investigations, this study focuses on the high-performance alumina ceramic TPMS complex structures fabricated via DLP process. Specifically, the work (1) developed a theoretical model to design the functional TPMS structures; (2) investigated the appropriate curing depth and other DLP process parameters to achieve optimal comprehensive performance; (3) successfully realized the high-precision fabrication of a heterogeneous functionally graded TPMS structure. This work provides critical insights into the high-density, high-fidelity rapid manufacturing and engineering applications of porous alumina ceramic structures.

Methodology

Theoretical Fundamentals

Design of Al2O3 Gyroid Lattice Structures

The TPMS regional extremum generation algorithm proposed in this study takes the structural surface function and target porosity as inputs. It calculates the regional minimum (f min) and maximum (f max) values of the TPMS surface function within prescribed boundary conditions. Subsequently, a bisection method is employed to numerically solve for the upper bound ζ of the surface function corresponding to the input porosity parameter. This process ultimately generates TPMS porous scaffolds defined within the implicit region f min ≤ f ≤ ζ that satisfy the target porosity requirement, as illustrated in Figure .

Framework of the TPMS scaffold region extremum generation algorithm based on target porosity.

The gyroid surface partitions three-dimensional space into two fully interconnected subdomains. By selecting a specific subdomain or thickening the gyroid isosurface, both strut-based and sheet-based gyroid structures can be obtained. The surface function of TPMS gyroid structures can be mathematically described by an implicit function equation in the form of f(x,y,z) = c, as in eq : 1 cos⁡2πxTsin⁡2πyT+cos⁡2πyTsin⁡2πzT+cos⁡2πzTsin⁡2πxT=c

Here, T represents the period of the TPMS structure in the X, Y, and Z directions, controlling the size of a single TPMS unit cell; c is the level-set constant that governs the surface morphology and surface area, with different valid value ranges for different TPMS structures. Design of Graded and Heterogeneous Lattice Structures

The requirements for porosity and TPMS unit morphology may vary across different structural regions in specific applications such as bone tissue repair, heat dissipation, and fluid permeation. Therefore, a complex functionally graded and heterogeneous TPMS structure featuring regionally designed gradient porosity and multitopology combinations has been proposed.

Based on the TPMS regional extremum generation algorithm proposed in this study, a linear function along the Z-axis direction was incorporated at the implicit regional boundary determining the layer thickness of the surface function. This approach generated TPMS scaffolds with gradient porosity variations while satisfying the target volume fraction, as illustrated in Figure c. To systematically evaluate the influence of the curing depth parameter on the comprehensive performance of printed specimens, this research selected TPMS Gyroid models with a volume fraction of 0.6 and gradient porosity variations for DLP printing.

To adjust the volume fraction of hybrid regions in heterogeneous nonuniform complex TPMS structures, thereby improving and enhancing their mechanical properties and manufacturability, this study proposes an SF-based algorithm for volume fraction adjustment of multicell morphology substructures in hybrid regions to achieve smooth transitional connections, as introduced in the following paragraphs.

The Voronoi cell is a polyhedral unit formed by randomly distributed seed points in space and their interconnections, enabling the partitioning of three-dimensional space. Material Preparation

The photocurable ceramic slurry employed in this study consists of solid particles, photosensitive resin, photoinitiator, and dispersant. Al2O3 powder (purity: ≥ 98.5%, Aluminum Corporation of China Limited) was selected as the ceramic filler. Trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA, 1.1 g/cm, Changshu Hengrong Trading Co., Ltd., China), 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA, 1.01 g/cm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), and acryloylmorpholine (ACMO, 1.122 g/cm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as reactive diluents and photosensitive resin monomers. I819 served as the photoinitiator to induce monomer cross-linking reactions. Additionally, BYK was incorporated as a dispersant to enhance slurry stability. The aforementioned raw materials were mixed at specific ratios to prepare photocurable ceramic slurry with a solid loading of 48 vol % at 405 nm wavelength.

The photosensitive resin monomer mixture was prepared by blending TMPTA, ACMO, and HDDA at a ratio of 1:1.8:4.3, followed by adding 1.0 wt % of photoinitiator I819 relative to the monomer mixture. Additionally, 3.3 wt % of dispersant BYK was incorporated to enhance the homogeneity of the mixed slurry. Finally, aluminum oxide (Al2O3) powder was added in batches. To prevent agglomeration in the ceramic slurry, planetary ball milling was performed using a zirconia grinding ball-filled mill (ZQM-2L, Changsha Mitr Instrument and Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 500 rpm for 3 h, with a slurry-to-ball mass ratio of 2:1, ultimately yielding the desired ceramic slurry.

TPMS Modeling Design

The TPMS modeling process using ceramic slurry is as follows: The TPMS regional extremum generation algorithm proposed in section is executed to obtain the 3D model of the designed structure, which are further sliced into a set of two-dimensional images at 50 μm thickness. Commercial software Wolfram Mathematica and Materialise Magics were utilized during the modeling process.

DLP-Process

The images generated in the TMPS modeling procedure are then imported into a DLP-based 3D printing device (BL3D-50-C2, Breuck3D Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China) with appropriate process parameters selected. After the ball-milled ceramic slurry is poured into the material tank, it undergoes layer-by-layer curing under UV light irradiation. Key printing parameters used in the DLP process include: light wavelength of 405 nm, layer thickness set at 50 μm, resolution of 1920*1080, and printing accuracy of 25 μm. By utilizing selected process parameters including printing layer thickness and exposure energy, five sets of Gyroid gradient porous structures with dimensional scaling relationships were fabricated in this study, each measuring 4π × 4π × 4π mm.

The optimal matching relationship between curing depth and printing layer thickness serves as a crucial benchmark for process parameter selection. To investigate the impact of the multiple relationship between curing depth and printing layer thickness on the forming accuracy of printed samples, five scaling factors (3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, and 5) corresponding to the five curing depths (150 μm, 175 μm, 200 μm, 225 μm, and 250 μm) were selected in the experiment. The corresponding exposure energy was then determined based on the Beer–Lambert law, as shown in section .

Degreasing and Sintering

After the DLP process, the printed specimen was removed from the forming platform, cleaned, and dried to obtain the alumina ceramic green body. Based on TG/DSC curves analysis, appropriate debinding parameters were established. The green body was then placed in a debinding furnace (FMJ-07/13, Hefei Facerom Thermal Equipment Co., Ltd., China) for oxidative degradation to remove cross-linked monomers and other organic components from the printed sample. To further enhance the postdebinding specimen strength, it was subsequently sintered in a furnace (FMJ-05/17, Hefei Facerom Thermal Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Measurement and Characterization

In this work, curing characteristics, characterization of gradient-porosity TPMS scaffold and heterogeneous TPMS scaffold are used to comprehensively evaluate the DLP process and the built component properties, which are introduced in this section.

Curing Characteristics

For DLP based vat photopolymerization technology, appropriate process parameters are crucial for precise control of structural characteristics in TPMS architectures, including pore size, porosity, and overall dimensions. Process parameter optimization can significantly enhance comprehensive performance metrics such as dimensional accuracy, surface quality, and mechanical properties of fabricated structures. Considering the dual requirements of high dimensional precision and manufacturing efficiency for printed specimens, this study selected 50 μm as the layer thickness. Subsequently, the curing depth was determined as 3–5 times the layer thickness, with corresponding exposure energy calculated based on the Beer–Lambert law, as shown in eq : 5 Cd=Dpln⁡(E0Ec) where C d (μm) represents the curing depth, D p denotes the penetration depth, E 0 is the incident laser energy (mJ/cm), and E c signifies the critical energy (mJ/cm), which corresponds to the minimum energy required for slurry solidification. Notably, D p and E c are solely determined by material properties. Consequently, the working curve between C d and E 0 can be established by measuring the cured thickness of each layer and fitting the experimental data. Each experimental group was repeated three times with averaged results.

Characterization of Gradient-Porosity TPMS Scaffold

To evaluate the fabrication quality of ceramic structures fabricated via DLP printing, it is essential to develop postprocessing procedures for debinding and sintering of ceramic suspensions, determine the curing behavior curves, and perform a comprehensive analysis of the printed components. This includes dimensional accuracy assessment, surface quality evaluation, 3D deviation measurement, macroscopic structural examination, as well as microscopic morphology and mechanical property characterization.

Thermogravimetric analysis was performed using a synchronous thermal analyzer TG-DTA (TGA/DSC3+, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) with a heating rate of 9.5 °C/h in air atmosphere from 25 to 1200 °C.

The curing depth and dimensions of all printed specimens were measured using a digital micrometer (resolution: 0.001 mm, tolerance: ±0.003 mm, measuring range: 0–25 mm, Syntek Co., Ltd., China). The dimensional accuracy was calculated by comparing the design model with the printed specimens using eq : 6 Rm=L−LmLm×100% The dimensional accuracy is expressed as R m (%), where L m (mm) represents the designed length of the model and L (mm) denotes the measured dimension of the green body from the stereolithography-printed specimen.

Surface roughness was observed using a digital microscope (Keyence VHX-7000, Japan) with ultradepth-of-field capability, offering magnification ranging from 20X to 6000X and Z-axis resolution ≥ 0.1 μm. Measurement positions were consistently selected at identical locations on the sample’s top surface. Three-dimensional models were reconstructed through industrial computed tomography (ICT, nanoVoxel-4000, Sanying Precision Instruments Co., Ltd., China) scanning. Dimensional deviations between designed and measured models were quantified, while the actual porosity of fabricated specimens was determined. Cross-sectional images of the printed sample at identical positions were obtained from CT scans. The top surface area of the reconstructed model was calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, NIH).

The micromorphology of samples was observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Gemini SEM 500, ZEISS, Germany). Structural characteristics, defects, and fracture surface morphology (including wall surface defects such as cracks and uncured residues) under different process parameters were characterized. It needs to be pointed out that due to the electrically insulating properties of alumina ceramic materials, the formed samples exhibited charging effects. Therefore, gold sputtering was applied to establish conductive pathways and eliminate noise interference, thereby obtaining clear, reliable, and high-quality imaging results. Quasi-static compression tests were performed on TPMS scaffold printed samples using a universal material testing machine (Instron 5982, 100 kN capacity, USA). Each process parameter group was tested with three replicates at a loading rate of 0.2 mm/min to obtain stress–strain curves. Average values were calculated from measurements, with compressive strength (σ) and elastic modulus (E) determined as follows: 7 σ=FA 8 E=σε

Furthermore, to further evaluate the DLP photopolymerization outcomes of porosity-gradient graded TPMS structures, this study conducted a comparative analysis of porosity distribution between printed specimens and the original design models using CT.

Characterization of Heterogeneous TPMS Scaffold

Employing the optimal process parameters determined in Section , two types of heterogeneous nonuniform structures were fabricated: (1) scaffolds combining Gyroid unit cells with varying rotational orientations, and (2) hybrid scaffolds combining Gyroid and Diamond unit cells. The microstructural morphology of these heterogeneous TPMS scaffold was examined using scanning electron microscopy, following the same characterization methodology applied to the gradient-porosity TPMS structures.

Comparison of the Mechanical Performance of Al2O3 Ceramics and TPMS Structures

From the typical fracture morphology of sintered specimens shown in Figure a, it can be observed that the fracture surface is dense and uniform without obvious pores. This indicates that the employed photopolymerization process parameters and sintering schedule successfully enabled complete and accurate formation of the designed structure, thereby ensuring consistent mechanical performance. Further analysis of high-magnification scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the fracture surfaces revealed characteristic intergranular fracture features, evidenced by a rock candy-like morphology, along with numerous continuous and flat transgranular cleavage facets. These observations confirm that the sintered specimens exhibited typical brittle fracture behavior. A representative high-magnification micrograph of the fracture surface is presented in Figure b.

Fracture photographs (a) and high-magnification images (b) of the struts for gradient-porosity TPMS scaffolds with different curing depths.

As alumina ceramics are intrinsically brittle materials, their fracture behavior is predominantly controlled by crystal structure and grain-boundary bonding strength. In Gyroid-type triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) samples, applied stress is primarily concentrated at curved surface junctions during loading. This geometric feature further facilitates cleavage fracture at relatively low strains. The observed fracture characteristics are fully consistent with established mechanisms of brittle failure in ceramics with complex topological architectures.

Conclusions

This research is significant for optimizing the comprehensive performance of TPMS structures. Building upon complex heterogeneous TPMS microarchitectures, we propose a scaffold generation algorithm capable of achieving target porosity while adjusting the volume fraction of hybrid regions. For the selection of curing depth, a critical parameter in DLP fabrication, this study employs a comprehensive analysis based on multiple criteria, including manufacturing accuracy, surface quality, microstructural morphology, and mechanical properties. The results demonstrate that a scaling factor of 4.5 produces specimens with optimal comprehensive performance. Subsequently, high-precision fabrication of heterogeneous TPMS structures was successfully achieved using this scaling factor.
