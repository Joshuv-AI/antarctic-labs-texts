Involving consumers in health research through the use of consumer panels: a scoping review
Authors: Fiona Dangerfield, Virginia Dickson-Swift, Anne E.M. Brabers, Judith D. de Jong, Joanne Adams, Sally Fraser, Dianne Lowe, Leigh Kinsman, Evelien Spelten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe importance of involving the lived experience of consumers in health research is increasingly well recognised. There are multiple models of consumer involvement, with consumer panels one way of efficiently organising the involvement of people in health research. Consumer panels are a way of supporting ongoing or longer-term meaningful consumer involvement rather than one-off involvement in discrete research activities. The aim of this review was to map the depth and breadth of studies reporting the use of consumer panels in health research internationally and to synthesize this evidence to provide useful guidance for researchers interested in the development of consumer panels for research.MethodsA scoping review was conducted. Four databases were searched for English language studies from high income countries reporting on consumer panel member involvement in health research. Seven reviewers undertook title and abstract screening, and five reviewers undertook full text screening. Results were summarised and content analysis was undertaken to synthesise the findings. One rural health consumer contributed to screening, data interpretation and manuscript writing.ResultsA total of 71 papers were included in this review. Most papers were from the Netherlands (n = 28) and the United States (n = 21). The size of panels was either small (< 50 members) (n = 26) or large (> 200 members) (n = 34). There was great variation in panel composition and level of panel member involvement in research. Involvement was often limited to discrete stages of the research cycle, with details about sustained, meaningful engagement often lacking. Benefits of panel involvement were documented for both researchers and consumers; however, benefits to consumers were often less explicit, particularly in terms of long-term impact.ConclusionConsumer panels can offer valuable insights grounded in lived experience, which have the potential to improve the quality, relevance and equity of health research. However, the development and involvement of consumer panels in health research remain inconsistently reported and evaluated. Co-developing structures that support meaningful, ongoing participation will provide greater clarity around roles, evaluation and impact. This is essential for embedding consumer engagement meaningfully in research and translating it into health policy and practice change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00785-1.

Introduction

Globally, there has been an increasing recognition of the importance of involving consumers of health services and the broader public in health research [1–5]. Consumer involvement, sometimes referred to as patient and public involvement (PPI) or consumer and community engagement (CCE) is reflected in health research policies and recommendations in high income countries such as the United Kingdom [4], the United States [6], New Zealand [7], Canada [8], Sweden [9] and the Netherlands [10]. In Australia, involving consumers in designing, implementing and evaluating health services is mandated in the Australian National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards [11] and involving consumers in health research is increasingly required in research funding submissions [3, 12].

Models of CCE or PPI in health research have been widely utilised but are often criticised as being ‘tokenistic’ by offering opportunities for consultation rather than active and meaningful participation [13, 14]. Consumer panels can be used to efficiently organize the sustained or ongoing involvement of people in health research, thus supporting meaningful, longer-term consumer involvement rather than one-off involvement in discrete research activities. Panels can include patients, citizens, families, carers, community members or consumer representatives who represent the views and interests of a community, or a group of people and are sometimes referred to as ‘consumer advocates’ [2, 3, 15–17]. Initially designed to collect continuous marketing data through surveys, consumer health panels (CHPs) are growing in popularity as a way of involving the lived experience of consumers in all stages of research, from shaping the direction of future research, improving the research process and dissemination and translation of research findings [2, 3, 18].

While the development of CHPs is growing and their usefulness is reported, there still remains a gap in our understanding of the role panels play in health research and how panels are developed. Examples of CHPs include Australia’s Health Panel developed by the Consumers Health Forum of Australia (CHF) [18] and the Dutch Health Care Consumer Panel (DHCCP) developed by the Netherlands Institute for Health Services Research [10, 19]. Australia’s Health Panel is designed to collect the views of Australians about the state of the nation’s healthcare system with findings used to influence government policy in the community’s interest and shape health system change for better health outcomes. The DHCCP is one of the longest CHPs in the world. In 2025, this panel consisted of around 10,500 people who have agreed to participate in health and wellbeing surveys at regular intervals. Panel members can only join the panel by invitation. At the start of the membership a range of sociodemographic characteristics are collected (for example age, gender, level of education, income, self-reported general health). The panel is renewed on a regular basis. Renewal is necessary to make sure that members do not develop specific knowledge of, and attention for, healthcare issues, and that no ‘questionnaire fatigue’ occurs. Panel renewal counteracts the turnover of panel members who, for example, have died or moved without providing updated contact details. The results of the surveys are used to evaluate policy, to generate input for policy, and to explore panel member expectations of and experience with health care.

CHPs such as the Dutch and Australian panels have the capacity to systematically involve members of the public who have lived experience of health services in all aspects of health care research, service design and evaluation. Several reviews have examined the use of consumers in health research [20–22] with these works primarily focused on factors supporting consumer engagement practices in research governance and priority setting. Whilst these reviews provide some information about how consumers have been involved in health research they do not specifically focus on the development and application of consumer panels in health research internationally. To address this gap, the aim of this review was to map the depth and breadth of studies reporting the use of consumer panels in health research internationally and to synthesize this evidence. The results of this review provide a comprehensive examination of current evidence in relation to the use of CHPs in health research for researchers interested in the development of consumer panels for health research.

Methods

Design

Scoping reviews are a valuable method for assessing the breadth and depth of existing literature on a specific topic. They provide a clear indication of the volume of available studies and offer a detailed overview of previous studies [23]. This review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews [24] and the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [25] was used for reporting. The review objectives for this scoping review were developed collectively by the research team (including a health consumer) using the population-concept-context framework [24]. This review addresses literature on consumers/participants (population), as members of CHPs (concept), participating in health research (context). The following objectives guided the review: [1] To explore the purpose of consumer panels in health research; [2] To explore who participates in CHPs and how members are recruited; [3] To outline the role of panel members in health research; and [4] To document the key outcomes of using consumer panels in health research. A formal scoping review protocol was not published.

Information sources and search strategy

An electronic database search was conducted in January 2024 for articles published from January 2012 to capture contemporary research outlining the use of CHPs in research. The following databases were searched - Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE - OVID), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL - EBSCO), PsychINFO (ProQuest) and Web of Science. As the concept of consumer health panels does not have consistent terminology, a preliminary search strategy was developed in consultation with a specialist health librarian and tested in MEDLINE. The search terms were then searched as both keywords and subject headings (e.g., MeSH) in the titles and abstracts and Boolean operators employed. A full MEDLINE search was then carried out by the librarian (Table 1). This search strategy was then adapted for use in CINAHL, PsychINFO and Web of Science.

The resulting articles were imported into Covidence (Vertias Health Innovation, Aus) – Cochrane’s systematic review management software [26] and duplicates were removed. Prior to title and abstract screening, a sample of 50 articles were independently assessed by 4 reviewers (VDS, FD, LK, ES) to ensure reliability in the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 2).

Data extraction and analysis

A data extraction form was created in Excel and pilot tested by 4 research team members (VDS, FD, JA, ES) who used it to extract data from 5 articles to ensure consistency of application. Following the pilot extraction, 6 team members (VDS, FD, JA, AB, JdeJ, ES) extracted the data into the data extraction form. Data extracted aligned with the review objectives and categories included title, authors, publication year, country where the panel was located, purpose of the panel, how panel members were recruited, key panel member characteristics, remuneration of panel members, method of panel operation, specific activities of panel members, duration of panel member involvement, health research area and key outcomes. Extracted data and definitions of categories can be found in Supplementary Datafile 1. Consistent with the guidelines for the effective reporting of scoping reviews [27] and the JBI framework [28] data were summarised based on the categories that were extracted from the included studies.

Broad purpose of consumer panels in health research

The purpose of establishing consumer panels can broadly be described to support and enhance health research. Many of the panels were established to draw upon the lived experience of panel members to inform the research process or particular stages of the research cycle [37–61]. Consumer panels were a way of creating meaningful engagement and shared decision making with people as research partners [54, 62–67], connecting with communities to address inequities and eliminate barriers for participation in research [35, 68–71], and a way of developing ethically and culturally safe research [36, 72]. The purpose of some panels was to produce relevant and influential research for health care policy and practice [66, 73, 74]. For example, the Dutch Health Care Consumer Panel (DHCCP) aims to measure panel members knowledge, expectations and experiences of healthcare to influence health care policy [29–34, 75–96]. Only a few panels were created to provide a source or ‘pool’ of participants for research [97–99].

Some panels had a clear focus on a specific health condition, while many panels focused on broader health topics or issues. Specific health conditions of focus included Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias [65, 97]; cancer [54, 60, 74, 87]; cardiovascular disease [57, 86]; chronic conditions [49]; diabetes [41, 43, 90, 98]; obesity [38, 41, 91, 98]; asthma [48]; tinnitus [32]; musculoskeletal conditions [46, 56, 59]; mental health [53, 61, 66, 73]; and liver disease [50]. Many panels focused on broader health issues such as community health [35, 42, 68, 84, 96]; clinical trials [37]; clinical [44, 62, 99] and non-clinical [58, 64, 71] health research; data linkage [51]; genetic testing [78]; medical decision-making [30, 31, 88, 95]; medication use [75, 77, 89, 92]; health literacy [69, 76, 94]; use of health insurance [29, 33, 80–82, 93]; health care communication [34, 83]; e-health [85]; health and the environment [52]; and arts-based health care interventions [45].

Who participates in consumer health panels and how members are recruited

Only 40 of the 71 studies provided demographic details of panel members. Reported demographics varied depending on the purpose of the panel and the research question being considered. When reported, many panels attempted to keep an even balance of gender, with average age of participants tending to be over 50 years [29–34, 76, 79, 81, 82, 85, 87–89, 92, 94], however some panels reported a higher percentage of females [51, 56, 62, 65, 69, 86]. Some panels included specific population groups, e.g., older adults [39, 67]; culturally and ethnically diverse communities [35, 36, 68, 70, 94, 97]; underserved communities [40, 63]; Indigenous communities [72]; and parents and carers [47, 55, 66, 79].

A range of direct and indirect methods were used to recruit people as panel members. Direct recruitment methods included the distribution of invitations via mail, telephone or email from existing distributions lists, panels or patient registries [29, 32–34, 46, 75, 76, 79–82, 87–89, 92, 93, 95, 99]; personally approaching people while attending events at community-based organisations [36, 39, 69, 97], including places of worship [52] and tribal councils [72]; and accessing people through patient health records [37] and health clinics [30, 41, 42, 57, 76, 77, 88]. Indirect methods included the use of media [52, 58, 67]; flyers in public locations [68]; community newsletters [55], websites [61] and online crowd sourcing platforms [46]. Recruiting via existing community partners, champions, connectors, networks [35, 40, 43, 44, 47, 49–52, 63, 68, 73], research centres [58, 71]; advisory councils and committees [60, 70] and community-based organisations [55, 65–67] were also used as a way of promoting panels. Nearly a quarter of studies did not provide information on how members were recruited [31, 45, 48, 53, 54, 59, 62, 64, 74, 84–86, 90, 91, 94, 96].

Role of panel members in health research

Panel members were involved in a variety of research activities with differing levels of involvement. For example, panel members provided their lived experience or a community perspective on specific research topics by completing surveys [29–34, 46, 69, 75–96, 99] or participating in focus groups [40, 62, 69, 70]. Some panels supported panel members in research governance roles such as priority setting and/or development of research questions [36, 41, 43, 47, 49, 52, 54–58, 62, 65–67]; co-investigators [54, 62]; co-chairs on research boards or committees [35, 48, 51]; and advising or developing funding grants [38, 44, 48, 49, 56, 63]. Other panels involved members while undertaking research such as co-developing ethics documents [53, 54, 67]; ensuring research study designs were patient-friendly and culturally safe [36, 37, 39, 42, 45, 56, 63]; reviewing research participant materials (brochures, information sheets, plain language summaries) [48, 50, 52, 53, 66]; assisting with participant recruitment and enrolment [37, 38, 54, 61, 63, 66, 71, 98]; and informing and undertaking data collection and analysis [49, 57, 72]. Some panels involved members in research translation, including the co-authorship and dissemination of research findings [42, 47–49, 52, 54, 56, 61, 65, 67, 72, 98]; implementation and evaluation [38, 73]; and policy development [62].

Key outcomes of using consumer panels in health research

Some authors of the selected studies reported the use of panels improved the overall quality of the research process [50, 54, 55, 58, 62, 73], while others highlighted specific aspects of the research process that improved due to panel members’ involvement and engagement. For example, clearer participant study materials and research documents [47, 53]; effective dissemination and knowledge translation [72]; research funding prioritisation and success [38, 44]; and the importance of including consumer engagement in future research designs [41, 47, 57] were described. Importantly, using panels led to the development of strategic partnerships, inclusive governance structures and stronger links with specific communities, leading to greater diversity in research [35–40, 43, 63, 71, 73, 97].

The use of panels brought benefits for both panel members and researchers. Panels increased the confidence and capacity of panel members to participate as partners in health research [59, 65, 68, 69]. Panels facilitated growth in understanding of the research process by developing trusting relationships between panel members and researchers [56, 59, 63, 66, 70], and researchers gained a better understanding of research topics from the perspective of consumers [29–34, 41, 46, 51, 61, 65, 75–96].

Some authors described beneficial practice and policy impacts resulting from the use of consumer research panels. Panels improved health care practice by delivering meaningful community-led solutions [42, 72, 98]; and developing collaborative relationships between clinicians, researchers and consumers [57, 66]. The use of panels in research can connect the public with policymakers and researchers [52] and lead to the development of stakeholder-informed policies [29, 62, 65, 80–82].

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to synthesise evidence in relation to the use of consumer panels in health research internationally. Consumer panels can play a valuable role in health research by providing insights, perspectives, and feedback directly from individuals with lived experience of specific health conditions, or who are impacted by a broad range of health issues. Analysis of included studies and broader literature is discussed in the following areas.

Diversity in consumer health panel purpose and membership

A wide range of studies were included in this scoping review. There was great variation in panel composition, size and consistency of panel member involvement. Studies also described different points in time in the development of panels and some referred to singular research projects that panel members were involved in. How panels were named was also variable with some described as panels and others identified as advisory boards, registers, committees, councils, forums, networks, partners, and garden cafes. In some cases, the name was indicative of function and level of consumer involvement. The variable terminology used to name consumer panels may reflect wider inconsistences in definitions for involving people in research [100]. Few studies provided a description of how the panel was established and maintained. This may have been to reduce repetition when the same panel was used across multiple studies e.g., Dutch Healthcare Consumer Panel, or because documenting these processes is complex. Describing how consumer panels are developed and maintained is important to further our understanding of successful ways of working with consumers when incorporating their lived experiences in the design and conduct of health research.

Diversity also extended to the breadth of health-related issues the panels focused on. While many panels involved members in research on specific health issues, others focused on addressing broader health areas reflecting the experiences of diverse population groups. Valuing diverse voices and experiences in health research, fosters inclusivity and enhances overall effectiveness, ensuring that the real-world values and needs of diverse communities are met [101].

This review highlights the growing use of consumer panels in health research as a structured mechanism for involving members of the public in research processes. While panels can offer continuity, accountability and clearly defined opportunities for engagement, their use also raises important considerations for evolving models of participatory research. Participatory approaches emphasise flexibility, mutual learning and power sharing which may be constrained by fixed panel structures if not carefully implemented. The notion that “one size does not fit all” is an important consideration as panel size, composition and the depth of involvement must be tailored to the specific research context and goals. Smaller panels may support deeper engagement but limit representativeness while larger panels might increase diversity but reduce opportunities for meaningful interaction. Future work should explore how panel approaches can evolve to support dynamic and equitable partnerships with consumers.

Involvement of panel members in health research

The involvement of panel members in research varied widely across the included studies. Whilst the focus of this review was on sustained involvement of panel members, many of the included studies did not report on duration of panel involvement, making it difficult to ascertain if panel members’ input was sustained over time. Panel member involvement was documented in individual stages of the research cycle, such as developing research questions and topics, data collection, analysis and dissemination, however very few studies described panel member involvement from study conception to dissemination [100]. For many studies, panel member involvement focused on final stages of research, such as dissemination rather than establishing and conducting the research. However, in many studies, the level of involvement was unclear.

While there are increasing requirements for researchers to demonstrate consumer and community involvement in research [3, 4, 6, 8, 12], ensuring meaningful and broad involvement of panel members within research can be challenging. While panel member involvement often depended on the type and size of the research project, it appears that involvement was more extensive in projects where researchers shared leadership with panel members, and the expertise being considered was demonstrably held within the community. Addressing power imbalances helps to avoid the tokenistic involvement of panel members in research [102]. Consumer and community involvement takes considerable time and may require extensive training and skill development for both researchers and consumers [101, 103]. Several studies described efforts to upskill consumer panel members; however, these often require panel members to engage on the researchers’ terms, rather than the researchers adapting to the needs or contexts of the panel members.

Outcomes and evaluation of panel member involvement

Involving panel members in health research can lead to benefits for researchers, panel members, health care providers and policymakers [104]. Interestingly, panels with a greater number of members, a clear established platform for membership and governance, and a broader health focus were more likely to report benefits and outcomes that also had a broader focus [76, 80]. Many of the outcomes of including panels lead to long-term solutions for problems faced by populations that are often hardly reached. This is possible because of an emphasis on capacity building of those with lived experience of complex conditions and circumstances [40, 63]. In some cases, access to a panel allowed for ongoing development [48, 66] and offered self-determined outcomes [72]. Consultation with and access to a panel facilitated development of meaningful social resources and sustainable solutions [39, 54].

Consumer panels provide valuable insights in health research by incorporating the perspectives of individuals with lived experience. This inclusion enhances the relevance and quality of studies, ensuring outcomes are meaningful and applicable. Engaging consumers fosters trust and credibility among communities, funding bodies, and ethics committees, as research becomes more inclusive and grounded in real-world experiences [101]. Researchers gain a deeper understanding of the lived experience of consumers and collaborative engagement can lead to shared learning sparking innovation by challenging assumptions and encouraging diverse perspectives [105]. Beyond research, consumer panels can play a pivotal role in shaping health policy and practice. The involvement of consumer panels in health research can result in the development of interventions that are acceptable, feasible, and more readily adopted in practice resulting in socially accountable research that drives meaningful change [106].

However, it is unclear who stands to benefit most from consumer involvement in health research. In this review the benefits for panel members were not often clearly described. Some studies outlined consumer benefits pertaining to panel member receipt of training and skill development. While capacity building undoubtedly holds benefits for panel members, it is also inherently beneficial to researchers in the conduct of their work [101]. Very few studies described panel member remuneration. Remuneration of panel members is a direct benefit to consumers and acknowledges the value attributed to their time and lived experiences. Providing remuneration has previously been considered coercive [107], but more recently it has been viewed as fair and equitable compensation [108].

The benefits to the research community of involving consumers in research are well documented however benefits to consumers are less well known. Results of the studies in this review also reported benefits for consumers including being heard, building social relationships, increased awareness of the benefits of diversity, increased knowledge of research benefits and readiness to participate as a partner in health research, and self-determination. A recent review by Tobiano and colleagues exploring the roles of consumers in research teams found that shared decision making was vital to ensure meaningful engagement of consumers in research activities [105].

It is likely that maintaining and clearly documenting benefits for consumers is a key element for the ongoing success of research informed by consumer involvement. Quantifying benefits to panel members is challenging, because many benefits may be experienced in the longer term, well beyond the completion of the project it might be attributed to. This might be evidenced in an increased capacity to advocate for themselves and others in a range of matters unrelated to their initial involvement in the panel. This represents a considerable challenge in evaluating the meaning and value of panel member involvement in research.

It can be seen from the above discussion that making a determination on the value and meaning attributed to panel member involvement is complex. It relies upon open acknowledgement of the purpose of research and what each party brings to the table in advancing knowledge on respective topics. Evaluation of panel member involvement must also be cognisant of both short- and longer-term outcomes as well as a range of mutually beneficial outcomes [101]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of evaluation of panel member involvement [105, 106], however few studies in this review included formal evaluation of panel member involvement. This is a key area for future research.

Whilst there are a number of frameworks to support the development of consumer involvement in research [16, 109] many have limitations. To address these limitations Greenhalgh and colleagues suggest that the development of a menu of evidence-based resources which stakeholders can use to co‐design their own frameworks would be appropriate [16]. Development of resources that describe effective, equitable and timely representation of consumer involvement in research would be highly beneficial for all parties involved in research and policy development. This must be inclusive of funding bodies who need to provide clear directives within grant applications for research and policy translation. It is perhaps beyond the scope of many Human Research Ethics Committees to consider the many variables which influence the impact of consumer involvement in research. It is clear that most models describing consumer involvement in research are initiated and led by researchers. This leads to inevitable bias in perspectives in consideration of benefits, costs and potential harms.

Future research regarding consumer panels should consider the development of a range of resources that can be used to guide development, maintenance and progression of consumer panels. This will be an increasingly important consideration given the ongoing emphasis of engagement with consumers in all forms of research. Future research might also consider panel member benefits more broadly. This would include considering longer term perspectives of benefits which may be experienced beyond the scope of initial panel involvement.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this scoping review is that it is the first international review documenting the use of consumer panels in health research. This has enabled a comprehensive overview of key aspects of consumer panel development and application in health research. There are, however, some noteworthy limitations. A large number of the included studies were carried out in the Netherlands drawing on the Dutch Healthcare Consumer Panel. Given that the Netherlands Institute for Health Services Research (Nivel) developed the Dutch Healthcare Consumer Panel in 1991 this is not surprising. Nivel is a world leader in consumer panels for healthcare with several health consumer panels, each with a different focus, for (inter)national use. Whilst we aimed to extract information about how the panels were developed not all included studies provided that level of detail. The data that was extracted provides us with a snapshot of key aspects of panel use. A consultation phase with stakeholders or consumers was not included, as the scope of this review was to synthesise documented evidence rather than explore perspectives or experiences. A more comprehensive understanding of the use of panels could be gained by consulting with panel owners and consumers directly. This would be a useful recommendation for future work. This review only included peer-reviewed research articles including reviews and protocols. Peer or non-peer reviewed literature such as editorials, commentaries, opinion pieces or conference abstracts, and grey literature were excluded as it would have significantly broadened the scope and scale of the review beyond what was feasible. We acknowledge that this may have led to the omission of valuable insights or emerging practices in the use of consumer panels not published as peer-reviewed research articles. For example, Wale et al. [104] published a peer-reviewed commentary on the use of a Community Advisory Group in a research - health service alliance that was not included in this review. The literature search was limited to include studies published in English which may mean that studies outlining the use of consumer panels in other languages may have been missed. While a comprehensive electronic literature search was conducted with the support of an expert librarian and extensive hand searching of reference lists undertaken, there is a possibility that some studies outlining the use of consumer panels may not have been located.

Conclusions

This scoping review synthesised evidence on the use of consumer panels in health research in high income countries, highlighting the diversity in their composition, function, and terminology. The results of the review highlight that while consumer panels can offer valuable insights grounded in lived experience, their development and involvement in research remain inconsistently reported and evaluated. Involvement was often limited to discrete stages of the research cycle, with details about sustained, meaningful engagement often lacking. While researchers increasingly recognise the importance of consumer input, challenges such as power imbalances, unclear roles, and a lack of shared leadership persist. Benefits of panel involvement were documented for both researchers and consumers; however, benefits to consumers were often less explicit, particularly in terms of long-term impact. Remuneration and skill development were infrequently addressed, though both are critical to equitable participation. Importantly, few studies conducted formal evaluations of consumer panel involvement, representing a key area for future research. To support more effective and inclusive practice, future efforts should focus on the co-design of adaptable frameworks and resources, underpinned by evidence and stakeholder input. Greater clarity around benefits, structures, and evaluation will be essential for embedding consumer engagement meaningfully in research and translating it into health policy and practice change.
