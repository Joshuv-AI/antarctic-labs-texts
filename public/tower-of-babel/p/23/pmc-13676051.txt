HeatRisk and air quality risk recommendations: A narrative review and action algorithm for the care of people at risk for and living with cardiovascular disease
Authors: Elizabeth Beveridge, Jose Ramon Medina Inojosa, Riya Goel, Rebecca Philipsborn, Zachary T Martin, Kristen Harris, Noah Newman, Sanjay Rajagopalan, Laurence Sperling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide and is influenced by climate change-related exposures. Non-optimal temperature and poor air quality exposure worsens cardiovascular outcomes, particularly for individuals with preexisting cardiovascular disease. While the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Environmental Protection Agency recommend tools like HeatRisk and Air Quality Index, there is limited guidance for implementation in those with preexisting cardiovascular disease. This narrative review addresses this gap.MethodsA literature review was conducted using PubMed, supplemented by snowballing to identify studies published between 2000 and 2025 related to the cardiovascular effects of temperature extremes and poor air quality. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were prioritized, particularly those that addressed risk factors such as pregnancy, obesity, geography, and socioeconomic status. Additional context was obtained from public health agencies including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Environmental Protection Agency, and National Weather Service. The pathophysiology and risk factors were then applied to the existing guidance, recommendations, and algorithms of these agencies.ResultsNon-optimal temperature and poor air quality are associated with an increased mortality and morbidity in patients with heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke, and myocardial infarction. These exposures also worsen cardiometabolic risk factors such as hypertension, insulin resistance, and atherosclerosis. High-risk subgroups included women, older adults, residents of temperate climates, individuals taking certain medications, pregnant individuals, and those with socioeconomic or occupational exposure risk.ConclusionsPatients with cardiovascular disease should be counseled on the risks associated with non-optimal temperature and poor air quality. It is recommended that high risk individuals (risk score greater than or equal to three) should take precautions at HeatRisk Level 1 (Yellow) and/or the AQI ≥ 50, while moderate risk individuals (score less than two) should consider precautions at HeatRisk Level 2 (Orange) and/or AQI ≥ 100 (Central Illustration).Keywords: Non-optimal temperature, Cardiovascular disease, Air pollution, Climate change, Risk factors, Primary prevention, Secondary prevention

Introduction

Increased greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution due to human activity are adversely contributing to the global environmental phenomenon known as climate change. In turn, climate change is increasing the frequency of extreme temperature events and worsening air quality worldwide [1,2]. Poor air quality and non-optimal temperature (NOT), which includes both hot and cold temperature extremes relative to expected seasonal and geographic norms, are independently associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [3,4]. There is also a synergistic effect: Heat and poor air quality exposure together are associated with a greater cardiovascular risk than either factor alone [3,4]. This risk is even greater amongst those with pre-existing cardiovascular disease [[5], [6], [7]].

Although climate-related cardiovascular risk is well established, its translation into evidence-based approaches to prevention and clinical decision-making remains limited. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) recommend using HeatRisk and Air Quality Index (AQI), both of which are metrics used to categorize environmental risk for heat and pollution respectively [8,9]. These approaches however have limitations: they are generalized and do not address the increased risk of vulnerable populations, including those with cardiovascular disease (CVD). There is also currently no adjacent standardized metric for cold exposure-related cardiovascular risk stratification, despite exposure to cold temperature posing a significant cardiovascular risk [10].

Given the prevalence of CVD combined with an accelerated rate of climate change [11], it is important that the current evidence be translated into specific and clinically relevant guidance for at-risk and vulnerable populations (Table 1). The purpose of this review is multifold: 1) Summarize the impact of NOT and air quality on CVD, 2) describe the physiologic mechanism of NOT and air quality on CVD, 3) highlight populations at risk, 4) review current tools including HeatRisk and AQI, 5) explain current clinical practice, and 6) provide recommendations for how HeatRisk and AQI can be applied clinically to patients with CVD (Central Illustration).

There are several important distinctions related to this review. While the association between NOT, air quality, and CVD outcomes is supported by well-established epidemiologic and mechanistic evidence, the AQI and HeatRisk tools were created to communicate population-level risk. Therefore, the application of these metrics may benefit from careful, expert-based interpretation and an understanding that the proposed recommendations are adaptable. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the proposed recommendations.

The shared burden of non-optimal temperature and air pollution on cardiovascular disease

CVD remains the leading cause of health-related burden worldwide due to its high prevalence, severity, and accelerating trend [12,13]. In 2022, the prevalence of CVD has increased to 825 million cases of CVD globally, compared to 523 million in 2019 with 19.7 million global deaths in 2022 [12,13]. In the United States, CVD has been the leading cause of death since 1921 [14]. Currently, 48.6% of Americans aged 20 years and older - roughly 127.9 million people- are living with CVD, with the most common diagnoses being coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke or hypertension [15]. Specifically, minority groups often experience the greatest prevalence [15].

Environmental factors like temperature extremes, poor air quality, and natural disasters like wildfires increasingly shape the burden of CVD, despite advances in prevention and treatment [11]. Scientists agree that failure to mitigate emissions is resulting in significant acceleration of climate change [2], with global temperatures in 2024 reaching unprecedented highs [16]. Of the past 140 years that temperatures have been monitored, the warmest years have occurred since 2010 [17]. Projection models predict that based on current trends, many cities in the United States may experience three times their current average number of days hotter than 32 °C (90°F) by 2050 [1].

The same human activities driving climate change, such as the burning of fossil fuels, also contribute to poor air quality through the emission of greenhouse gases and production of particulate matter (PM) [18]. PM is separated into sizes that include PM10, PM2.5, and ultrafine PM. PM10 is PM <10 µm in diameter, and includes dust, pollen, and mold while PM2.5, <2.5 µm in diameter, includes combustion particles, organic compounds, and metals and ultrafine PM are <0.1 µm in diameter and are also sourced from combustion and industrial emissions [18]. PM can be inhaled into the lungs and absorbed in the blood, with PM2.5 and ultrafine PM posing the greatest health risks- both for the development of CVD disease, and for worsening existing CVD [18].

Air pollution has become the leading environmental risk factor for mortality and morbidity worldwide [19]. A 2019 study found that 86% of urban populations globally between 2000 and 2019, lived in areas with annual PM2.5 levels above the 2005 World Health Organization standards [20]. In an analysis of the global burden of disease attributable to air pollution, researchers estimated that ambient air pollution caused 7.6% of total global mortality in 2015, or roughly 4.2 million deaths [19]. Specifically, air pollution is associated with adverse respiratory diseases, decreased lung function, cancer, obesity, diabetes, and CVD [21,22]. According to an analysis of the global burden of disease data from 2015, 19% of all cardiovascular deaths were due to air pollutants [19].

Climate change is also increasing the number of climate-driven disasters such as wildfires, a significant contributor of PM2.5 [23]. Of note, the toxicity of PM2.5 from wildfires is much higher and more carcinogenic than PM2.5 from other sources [24]. Specifically, 0.55% of cardiovascular deaths are attributable to wildfire-related PM2.5 [25]. This is only increasing as the number of wildfires increases [23]. Emerging evidence suggests that co-exposure to heat and air pollution may have synergistic effects, amplifying cardiovascular risks beyond the additive effects of each exposure alone, even when one of the exposures is moderate [26,27].

Physiologic mechanism of NOT and poor air quality on cardiovascular system

Physiologic effect of heat on the cardiovascular system

Heat-related illnesses occur when thermoregulatory mechanisms are insufficient to maintain homeostasis, leading to a spectrum of illnesses ranging from mild to life-threatening (Table 2) [28]. Milder forms include heat associated edema, exercise-associated muscle cramps, and heat rash, while moderate severity conditions include heat syncope and heat exhaustion (Table 2) [28]. The most severe conditions are rhabdomyolysis and heat stroke, the latter defined by a core temp above 40.5 °C (Table 2) [28].

Effective thermoregulation relies heavily on the cardiovascular system [28]. When the hypothalamus detects elevated body temperature, it activates heat-loss mechanisms primarily by increasing skin blood flow and promoting sweating (Fig. 1) [28]. Increased skin blood flow enhances dry heat loss, mainly through radiation and convection, while sweating enables evaporative cooling (Fig. 1).

Heat transfer mechanisms.

The four types of heat transfer between humans and the environment (radiation, convection, evaporation, conduction). Conduction is the heat transfer through direct contact with an object. Radiation is heat transfer from electromagnetic waves, primarily from the sun. Convection is heat transfer through movement of fluids (liquids or gases). Evaporation is heat transfer as a liquid (such as sweat) that absorbs heat from the body and becomes vapor to cool the surface of the skin.

Without proper hydration or external cooling, cutaneous vasodilation decreases systemic vascular resistance, while sweating reduces plasma volume and lead to hypovolemia. Together, these changes threaten arterial pressure and require an increased cardiac output, primarily through elevated heart rate, to maintain blood pressure and perfusion [28]. This demand may exceed the compensatory capacity in individuals with preexisting CVD, increasing the risk for localized ischemia which can lead to myocardial infarction or in the case of decreased cerebral perfusion, syncope [28].

Additional risks from hypovolemia and sweating include electrolyte abnormalities, hemoconcentration, increased inflammatory signaling, and hypotension (Fig. 2) [16]. Losses of sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium through sweat can disrupt the balance required for normal cardiac electrophysiology [16]. These electrolyte abnormalities may increase the risk of arrhythmias [16]. Also, a decrease in plasma volume leads to hemoconcentration, increasing blood viscosity, and the risk of thrombotic events such as MI, stroke, peripheral occlusion, and claudication, among others. (Fig. 2) [11,26]. This places individuals who already have an increased risk of clotting at an even higher risk when exposed to extreme heat [26]. This could include genetic predispositions that involve primary and secondary hemostasis[26] It could also include patients with endothelial dysfunction such as but not limited to atherosclerosis, postmenopausal women, and diabetes (Table 2) [1,16,27]. Additionally, any patient experiencing stasis due to long travel (i.e. truck driver), recovery (i.e. post-surgical), or malignancy is at an increased risk of hypercoagulation [1,26].

Dehydration and hypovolemia can also cause decreased tissue perfusion, causing an increased stress response from hormones like cortisol and adrenaline (Fig. 2) [29]. This leads to an increase in proinflammatory cytokines [30]. The reduced blood volume causes reduced clearance of these inflammatory mediators (Fig. 2) [31]. Similarly, other studies suggest that the proinflammatory state is multifactorial. In addition to a sympathetic and hormonal stress response, heat can cause an increase in reactive oxygen species that can damage endothelial lining [27]. Dysfunctional endothelial lining can impair the vasodilation response from nitric oxide, thus predisposing to CVD conditions like atherosclerosis, hypertension, or metabolic disorders like hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia [32]. Patients that may be at an increased risk related to these effects due to an inflammatory state at baseline include those with infection, hypertension, history of smoking, etc. (Table 5) [33].

Physiologic effect of cold temperatures on the cardiovascular system

Climate change will increase the frequency of both extreme hot and cold days, particular in regions unaccustomed to large temperature variations [11]. Hypothermia, when the body temperature is below 35 °C or 95°F [34], can also cause poor cardiovascular outcomes, especially for those with preexisting CVD [11]. Historically, cold temperatures have accounted for a larger proportion of cardiovascular deaths than heat. In a global analysis of 32 countries, cold temperatures contributed to 8.2% of cardiovascular deaths, compared to 0.66% for heat [10]. This is likely a reflection of greater frequency of cooler days relative to the frequency of hotter days, which is likely to change with the continued evolution of climate change [10].

The physiological effects of colder temperatures are also multifactorial (Fig. 2). Hypothermia activates the sympathetic nervous system, producing systemic vasoconstriction and reduced peripheral blood flow. The resulting increases in blood pressure and myocardial oxygen demand can precipitate demand ischemia, particularly in individuals with preexisting CVD (Fig. 2) [11]. Hypothermia can also induce bradycardia by slowing cardiac conduction velocity, increasing susceptibility to arrythmias (Fig. 2) [11].

Colder temperatures can also trigger a hypercoagulable state by activating coagulation factors and promoting thrombus formation (Fig. 2) [11]. Hypothermia may also promote cholesterol crystallization, increasing the risk for atherosclerotic progression and subsequent plaque rupture and myocardial infarction [11]. These risks are particularly concerning for individuals with preexisting ischemic heart disease, previous MI, and heart failure. Additionally, it should be noted that extreme cold can be exacerbated by other factors such as high winds, rain, and snow as well as a lack of appropriate physiologic adaptive capacity and lack of shelter or heat [34].

Physiologic effects of poor air quality on the cardiovascular system

Poor air quality can adversely affect the cardiovascular system through a variety of mechanisms (Fig. 2) [22,[35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Initiation of fine PM2.5 initiates a cascade of cardiovascular effects beginning with pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation, which triggers systemic cytokine release, autonomic nervous system imbalance, and neuro-humoral activation [22]. These responses can elevate blood pressure and heart rate, impair endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, and promote vasoconstriction. Over time, this sets the stage for endothelial dysfunction, a prothrombotic milieu (enhanced platelet activation, coagulation, reduced fibrinolysis), and heightened susceptibility to arrhythmias and acute coronary events. The acute effects can manifest within hours to days of exposure while chronic exposure can accelerate atherogenesis, lipid oxidation, macrophage activation, and plaque progression.

Long-term exposure to PM2.5 emphasizes the effects of cumulative particulate burden, contributing to cardiometabolic derangements- insulin resistance, hypertension, diabetes- that magnify cardiovascular risk (Table 5) [[43], [44], [45]]. Additionally, ultrafine particulate constituents may translocate or trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic changes, maintaining a state of low-grade systemic inflammation [[46], [47], [48]]. Collectively, these mechanisms underpin the robust epidemiological associations between PM2.5 exposure and increased incidence of myocardial infarction, heart failure, arrhythmias, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 2) (Table 4).

Predictors of vulnerability to heat and poor air quality as related to poor CVD outcomes

Certain populations are more vulnerable to adverse cardiovascular outcomes from NOT and poor air quality [3,5,22,[49], [50], [51], [52]]. These fall into three domains: biologic susceptibility, pharmacotherapy-associated vulnerability, and environmental/structural vulnerability (Table 1).

Biologic susceptibility

Those with biologic susceptibility to extreme temperature and air pollution include individuals with chronic conditions, females, older adults, pregnant individuals, and obese individuals. Individuals with preexisting CVD may exhibit impaired thermoregulation capacity and pathologic changes that impair typical physiological response to air quality or changes in temperature (Table 4) [5]. While CVD is the focus of this review, those with chronic conditions of other organ systems (e.g., lung disease, chronic kidney disease) are also more vulnerable to the effects of extreme temperature and air pollution.

Females, particularly postmenopausal women, may have greater vulnerability to temperature changes and air pollution, partly due to reduced estradiol-mediated thermoregulatory vasodilation [49,53,54].

Older adults are the most susceptible to heat and air pollution due to physiologic changes with age including increased vasoconstriction, reduced sweat gland density and dermal capillaries, arterial stiffness, and decreased cellular repair. These can impair thermoregulation and exacerbate cardiovascular risk effects of temperature variation and air pollution [22,53,55].

Pregnancy may increase sensitivity to temperature changes and poor air quality, with studies highlighting the impact of temperature extremes to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, stillbirth, low birth weights, and congenital heart defects [52,56]. Temperature variations and air pollution increase metabolic demand and trigger inflammatory pathways that may affect placental blood flow and fetal development [52,56].

Obesity reduces heat dissipation due to an increased body surface area-to-mass ratio and insulating adipose tissue [57]. Both temperature variations and air pollution amplify inflammatory responses [58]. Specifically, higher blood pressure and ventricular arrhythmias have been observed in obese individuals after PM2.5 exposure [59].

Pharmacotherapy-associated vulnerability

Pharmacotherapy-associated vulnerability is two-fold as common cardiovascular medications may both increase sensitivity to extreme temperature exposure and also be affected by the environmental conditions (Fig. 2) [50]. First, severally commonly used medication can heighten heat sensitivity, including antiplatelet agents which may blunt vasodilation, limiting the body's ability to eliminate heat (Table 1) [50]. Similarly, many non-cardiovascular medications such as anticholinergics can affect central thermoregulation [50].

Second, several medications can be affected by environmental conditions such as nonextreme temperature and adequate hydration. For example, insulin and inhalers may have reduced efficacy with exposure to heat [50]. Other medications such as antihypertensives may increase the risk of hypotension and syncope in the setting of dehydration. Diuretics can further worsen volume and electrolyte loss, increasing the risk of arrhythmias and hypotension. Dehydration and hemoconcentration may increase the relative concentration and toxicity of certain drugs with narrow therapeutic windows such as warfarin and lithium (Table 1) [50].

Environmental and structural vulnerability

Racial and ethnic disparities in temperature and pollution related cardiovascular risks reflect structural and systemic inequalities as opposed to biologic differences [11]. Historical practices like redlining predisposed many Black and low-income communities to pollution sources and urban heat islands with less green space [60]. These risks extend to occupational and activity-related exposures [61].

HeatRisk and AQI

Alert systems have proven to be a useful public health tool [62]. As such, the CDC and National Weather Service collaborated to create HeatRisk and the EPA created AQI such that the public can quickly and easily know when the temperature or air quality has reached dangerous levels. Both HeatRisk and AQI use the zip code of the user to in turn provide health-related guidance [8,9]. This type of locally scaled data is extremely useful to provide specific public health guidance. As stated, no similar tool exists for colder temperatures.

HeatRisk

HeatRisk reports the level of risk of heat-related impacts using a numeric color-coded spectrum of green, yellow, orange, red, and magenta, each indicating a higher risk (Table 3). As depicted in Table 3, there is little to no risk for areas with heat in the green category [8]. Temperatures in the yellow and orange category can affect individuals who may be more sensitive to heat (Table 3) [8]. While temperatures in the red and magenta category are risky for most individuals (Table 3) [8].

HeatRisk uses an algorithm that determines risk by calculating heat index, a common way to measure heat that looks at a combination of temperature and relative humidity [63]. Many studies have found a significant interaction between heat index and CVD mortality [[63], [64], [65]]. Specifically, the algorithm looks at four factors including: (1) how abnormal the temperatures are for that time of year, (2) the duration of the heat, (3) the potential risk of high temperatures based on CDC data, and (4) a proxy for humidity [8]. Humidity is approximated by looking at two additional factors: (1) how abnormally high the nighttime temperatures are (since more humidity leads to higher nighttime temperatures) and (2), the difference between daytime highs and overnight lows (the more humid, the smaller the difference) [8].

It should be noted that current cardiovascular research varies widely in how heat is measured. Researchers emphasize that factors beyond just temperature such as humidity, wind speed, and air pollution should be accounted for in heat exposure, rather than as confounders [16,31,66]. Besides heat index, these metrics include mean temperature, maximum and minimum temperature, wet bulb globe temperature (which includes humidity, air temperature, wind speed, and radiant heat via the sun angle, and cloud cover), apparent temperature (which in addition to heat index includes wind speed), and heatwaves [16,31]. Despite differences in heat metrics, one review that included various heat metrics found that a 1 °C increase in temperature was positively associated with CVD mortality of all causes [66]. These findings highlight that a tool like HeatRisk, which uses heat index, is useful for understanding how to prevent adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

Air quality index

AQI is a numeric color-coded index that reports daily air quality (Table 3). The scale is from 0–500, with the higher the AQI value, the greater the level of pollution. The air quality numerical value is determined by the concentration of five major air pollutants including ground level ozone, particle pollution (i.e. PM10, PM2.5, ultrafine PM), carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, and sulfur dioxide [62]. Air quality indexes (AQIs) simplify complex air pollution data into color-coded, unitless scales for public communication, but their design is inherently limited. AQIs are typically based on short-term exposure thresholds derived from national regulatory standards, which vary widely. They identify the single pollutant most exceeding its standard, although not accounting for cumulative or synergistic effects of multi-pollutant exposures. Moreover, averaging periods differ by pollutant (e.g., 8 h for ozone, 24 h for PM2.5), creating reporting delays and potentially reducing responsiveness during acute pollution episodes.

Limitations

The breakpoints between AQI categories- labeled “good”, “moderate”, or “unhealthy”- are not biologically meaningful; epidemiologic data demonstrates no safe threshold for most pollutants, meaning health risks may persist even at “good” AQI levels. Consequently, AQI may misrepresent actual health risks, particularly chronic low-level exposure, and reinforce misperceptions of safety. Their categorical and color-coded nature can focus public attention on arbitrary regulatory boundaries rather than continuous health risk gradients. Air Quality Health Indexes (AQHIs) such as the Canadian AQHI, address some of these shortcomings by combining health risk contributions from multiple pollutants based on local epidemiological data, producing scales that more accurately reflect real-world, risk-based exposure relationships. However, even AQHIs remain imperfect, as they typically model only one health endpoint (e.g., mortality), and their categorical thresholds are still subjectively defined.

Additionally, index-based frameworks like AQI and HeatRisk do not account for the amplifying effect of concurrent heat exposure and poor air quality. For example, staying outdoors in the shade may pose limited risk on an extremely hot day with good air quality but substantially greater risk on a moderately hot day with poor air quality, a distinction not captured by either index alone. This limitation highlights the need for a joint, health-impact-based guidance that incorporates simultaneous temperature and pollution data using multiple pollutants. Nevertheless, AQI and HeatRisk remain valuable public health tools due to their feasibility and accessibility. When interpreted as approximations of risk along a gradient rather than indicators of safety, these indices can promote progressive protective actions as combined heat and air quality risks increase.

Current clinician and patient recommendations using heatrisk and AQI

Currently, the CDC recommends three general steps for healthcare professionals when creating a plan to mitigate environmental risks: (1) use the CHILL’d-OUT questionnaire to assess a patient’s risk factors for exposure to heat and pollution [61], (2) teach patients how to use HeatRisk and AQI to monitor their risk, and (3) create a personalized action plan for each patient (Table 6).

The CDC recommends that all patients take precautions to prevent overheating when the HeatRisk level is orange or higher, and when AQI is above 100 [67]. For some patients with CVD, the CDC suggests patients take precautions at lower levels. While the recommendations are not specific, some patients may need to take precautions at the yellow level of HeatRisk and/or an AQI of 50 [67].

The CDC’s recommendations for overheating are not tailored to one population. In addition, the recommendations do not consider concurrent pollution levels. Suggestions include staying in the shade, taking breaks during outdoor activities, and rescheduling activities to cooler times [67]. The CDC also encourages the use of air conditioning when available and suggests that fans should only be used when indoor temperatures are <90°F, as using a fan above this temperature can increase body heat [67]. Patients are encouraged to stay hydrated with water, limiting drinks with sugar, alcohol, caffeine, or sodium [67]. Patients are also taught the warning signs of heat-related symptoms such as heavy sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, headache, general fatigue, weakness, and nausea [67]. Additionally, the importance of regular exercise should be emphasized as this can help to improve thermoregulatory capacity and decrease the strain on the heart in times of extreme heat [67].

As for air quality, CDC recommendations are also intuitive and focus on decreasing exposure. Specific recommendations include staying indoors, using an air purifier[67] or face mask [62], and planning outdoor activities away from roads with heavy traffic [62]. Some healthcare professionals are calling for a more personalized risk assessment approach that could include low-cost air pollution sensors [40]. Patients are also educated to look for unusual symptoms from poor air quality such as changes in breathing (i.e. wheezing, shortness of breath, inability to breathe as deep or vigorous), chest discomfort (i.e. pain, tightness, palpitations), and fatigue [62].

Despite the heightened vulnerability amongst patients with preexisting CVD, the current CDC guidelines are insufficiently tailored to this high-risk group (i.e. yellow vs orange HeatRisk and 50 or 100 AQI). Additionally, unique manifestations of CVD, as well as the presence of additional factors such as medications, gender, metabolic state (i.e. obesity, pregnancy) or societal factors such as redlining, socioeconomic status, and race are not accounted for.

Specific recommendations can be beneficial for busy clinicians to streamline and standardize care across populations. Importantly, specific recommendations on how to better tailor focus on environmental prevention to patients with preexisting CVD or other risk factors for outcomes due to heat or poor air quality are needed. Guidance is more important than ever as climate change continues to worsen, exacerbating weather extremes and natural disasters such as wildfires.

Recommendations for patients with CVD as related to heat and poor air quality

From a patient care perspective, HeatRisk and AQI are important tools that healthcare professionals and patients can use to curate a plan in the case of extreme temperatures/poor air quality. However, the current recommendations do not account for vulnerable populations such as those with CVD. Additionally, the guidelines do not account for cooler temperatures as well as both temperature and air quality simultaneously and cumulatively.

Current evidence supports an incremental concern and an amplifying effect regarding NOT and air quality on poor cardiovascular outcomes and risk factors, suggesting that patients with ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or history of past stroke or myocardial infarction may be at an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Using the new suggested recommendations in Central Illustration, individuals with a history of known CVD, or a calculated risk score greater than three may take precautions at lower exposure levels (i.e., when the HeatRisk reaches Level 1 (Yellow) and/or the AQI is ≥ 50). Individuals with a calculated risk score (Central Illustration) of one or two should consider precautions when HeatRisk reaches Level 2 (Orange) and/or AQI is ≥ 100. The cut offs of 50 and 100 were chosen to maintain the simplicity and existing categorization of HeatRisk and AQI. Similarly, variables including certain conditions, medications, age, and BMI were given various weights. Age and lack of air conditioning access were given a 2 while preexisting conditions were given a 1. Medications were given a 0.5 wt. These weights are expert-consensus-derived based on the existing evidence. Certainly, future prospective studies are needed to validate the accuracy of these weights and algorithm. Additionally, given the limitations of HeatRisk and AQI, these suggestions are just that and individuals should use clinician guidance. Similarly, the weighted variables.

Future directions

This review has centered around environmental risk algorithms including EPA and HeatRisk. Future research is needed to validate these algorithms. Likewise, there is a need for a validated risk algorithm for colder temperatures, as well as an algorithm that addresses simultaneous NOT (both extremes of temperature) and poor air quality concurrently and cumulatively.

This review has primarily focused on individuals living with CVD risk factors that have been studied; however, other populations likely fall into higher risk groups. These may include pregnant women without any CV factors, children, people living with lung disease, renal disease, etc. Further studies are needed to better understand how environmental risk recommendations apply to these populations. As well, further studies are needed to better understand the effects of heat and air pollution on other groups with CVD including peripheral artery disease and valvular disease.

Lastly, future studies should address implementation challenges of these proposed recommendations. Although these suggestions can be discussed in individual clinical settings, there may be more personalized and adaptable prevention tools. Future avenues could explore a downloadable mobile application or automated reminder through the electronic health record.
