Analysis of Salivary pH in Healthy and Diseased Periodontium among Young and Elderly Adults
Authors: Narenthiran Narasimman, Sayeeganesh Nagarajan, Vignesh Krishnaswamy, Vanathy Desingu, V. M. Deepa, Gayathri Meikandan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Salivary pH plays a vital role in oral health. Variations in salivary pH influence the progression of periodontal diseases, dental caries, and systemic conditions. This study aimed to compare salivary pH levels between individuals with healthy and diseased periodontium across different age groups and genders.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted on 80 subjects, categorised into eight groups (n = 10 each) based on age (young/elderly), gender, and periodontal status (healthy/diseased). Unstimulated saliva was collected and analyzed using an electrometric pH meter. Statistical analysis was performed using ANOVA and post hoc tests.Results:Significant differences were observed in salivary pH between young women with diseased periodontium (pH, 7.090) and those with healthy periodontium (pH, 6.704) (P = 0.001). No significant differences were found among other groups (P > 0.05).Conclusion:Salivary pH varies with periodontal health status and gender, highlighting its potential as a noninvasive diagnostic marker for periodontal disease.

BACKGROUND

Saliva plays a crucial role in maintaining oral health. Its pH is a crucial factor in oral homeostasis.[1] The normal unstimulated salivary pH ranges between 6.8 and 7.4.[2] Salivary pH increases on stimulation, aiding in acid neutralization and enamel remineralization.[3] Conversely, low pH levels promote the growth of aciduric bacteria, leading to tooth decay.[4] In addition, periodontal pathogens thrive in specific pH ranges, influencing disease severity.[5] Saliva has emerged as a valuable diagnostic medium due to its noninvasive collection, cost-effectiveness, and correlation with systemic health.[6] Elevated levels of inflammatory markers in periodontitis patients further support its diagnostic utility.[7] The objective of this study was to compare salivary pH levels between individuals with healthy and diseased periodontium across different age groups and genders.

METHODOLOGY

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Periodontology, Vinayaka Mission Shankaracharya Dental College. As per Helsinki guidelines, 80 subjects were enrolled after the institutional ethical review committee approved the study, IEC no: VMSDC/IEC/431, after verbally explaining the nature of the study and obtaining written informed consent.

For each patient, gingival and periodontal assessments were performed using the NIDR gingival inflammation index. The control group included patients with clinically healthy gingiva having probing depth of up to 3 mm, and the test group included patients with diseased periodontium having pocket depth of ≥5 mm in at least 30% of sites with loss of attachment and bleeding on probing. Participants with systemic diseases, smokers or tobacco users, or recently treated with antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs (within 6 months) were excluded.

Eight different groups with ten subjects each were formed among the patients, having group 1:- young adult men with diseased periodontium, group 2: young adult women with diseased periodontium, group 3: middle-aged adult men with diseased periodontium, group 4: middle-aged adult women with diseased periodontium, group 5: young adult men with healthy periodontium, group 6: young adults women with healthy periodontium, group 7: middle-aged adult men with healthy periodontium, and group 8: middle-aged women with healthy periodontium.

Saliva collection and pH measurement

For sample collection, subjects were instructed not to eat/drink before the test and to swallow any remaining saliva in their mouths. Afterward, unstimulated saliva is collected 2 hours postprandial by drooling method. Salivary pH was measured using a calibrated electrometric pH meter (glass electrode).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v26.0. ANOVA and post hoc Turkey tests compared mean pH across groups (P < 0.05 considered significant).

RESULTS

Based on demographic data, 80 participants were found to represent 50% women and 50% men. Fifty percent of respondents were young, while 50% were middle-aged or elderly. Salivary sample analysis revealed that all groups had different pH values (average ANNOVA value was 3.067; P-value was 0.007 – not statistically significant) [Table 1 and Figure 1].

DISCUSSION

In this study, salivary pH varied significantly between individuals, the elevated pH observed in young women with periodontitis compared with their healthy counterparts. Table 2 suggests a compensatory mechanism in response to periodontal inflammation. This aligns with previous research indicating that periodontal pathogens thrive in a near-neutral pH environment.[8] The higher pH reflects an adaptive response to buffer acidic byproducts of bacterial metabolism, thereby modulating the oral microenvironment to favor microbial homeostasis.[9]

In addition, the results showed no significant pH differences in male subjects, which could be related to hormonal influences on salivary composition as estrogen enhances salivary flow and bicarbonate secretion, whereas testosterone does not exhibit a similar effect[10] No significant pH variation was seen in the elderly patients regardless of periodontal status, due to age-related hyposalivation and reduced buffering capacity of the salivary gland.[1011] Studies have documented that salivary gland dysfunction in ageing population leads to decreased flow rates and altered pH dynamics, which may mask disease-associated pH changes.[11]

The observed pH differences suggest that saliva serves as a valuable diagnostic fluid for periodontal disease. Elevated pH in diseased periodontium increases ammonia production from bacterial urease activity, neutralizing acidic conditions.[12] This phenomenon has been previously reported in patients with chronic periodontitis, where higher salivary pH correlated with increased periodontal pocket depth. Hence, the noninvasive nature of saliva testing makes it an ideal diagnostic method.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the potential of salivary pH as a gender-sensitive diagnostic marker for periodontal disease, particularly in young women. The findings underscore the need for personalized oral health strategies that account for demographic variations. Further research is needed to evaluate the dynamic interplay between salivary pH, microbial shifts, and disease progression.
