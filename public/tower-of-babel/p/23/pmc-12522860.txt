The Chinese version of the Self-Injurious thoughts and behaviors Interview-Revised (C-SITBI-R): assessing NSSI and suicidal behaviors among adolescents in clinical settings
Authors: Yi-Hui Liu, Jia-Rong Liang, Jia-Hui Hu, Ting-Ting Zhang, Shi-Bin Wang, Fu-Jun Jia, Cai-Lan Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) increasingly prevalent among youth, with particularly high rates in China. Existing assessment tools often lack comprehensiveness or cultural adaptation. This study aimed to translate and validate the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview-Revised (SITBI-R) for Chinese adolescents in clinical settings and evaluate its diagnostic accuracy against DSM-5 criteria and suicide risk assessment protocols.MethodsA total of 170 adolescents aged 12–19 years were recruited from two psychiatric hospitals. The Chinese version of SITBI-R (C-SITBI-R) was compared with established instruments including the M.I.N.I., self-report scales for non-suicidal self-injury assessment, and DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Content validity, construct validity, test-retest reliability, interrater reliability, and diagnostic agreement were evaluated.ResultsThe C-SITBI-R exhibited excellent content validity (I-CVI = 1.00, S-CVI/UA = 1.00) and significant construct validity correlations with validation instruments. Perfect interrater agreement was achieved (all κ and ICC values = 1.00, p < 0.001), while test-retest reliability showed excellent consistency for lifetime presence and timing variables (κs = 0.78-1.00, ICCs = 0.93-1.00, p < 0.001), and moderate to strong agreement for past month frequencies of major SITBs (ICCs = 0.64–0.75, p < 0.001).High diagnostic consistency was observed with DSM-5 criteria for NSSI (κ = 1.00) and SBD (κ = 0.86), and M.I.N.I. suicide risk assessment (κ = 0.94).ConclusionsThe C-SITBI-R demonstrates strong psychometric properties and cultural adaptation for systematic assessment of SITBs among Chinese adolescents. Its strong diagnostic accuracy and clinical feasibility support its implementation in routine psychiatric practice for enhanced identification and management of at-risk youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07443-6.Keywords: Self-injurious thoughts and behaviors, Non-suicidal self-injury, Suicidal behavior disorder, Adolescents, Psychometric properties

Background

Self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) encompass a range of phenomena including non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), suicidal ideation, plans, preparations, gestures, and attempts. SITBs among youth have been rising globally, characterized by complexity, harmfulness, and high recurrence rates [1]. Research has demonstrated that suicide ranks as the third leading cause of death among male adolescents and the second among female adolescents worldwide [2, 3]. In 2020, global NSSI prevalence among children and adolescents reached approximately 19.5%, with rates exceeding 27% among Chinese middle school students following the COVID-19 pandemic onset [4, 5]. Among Chinese adolescents with psychiatric disorders such as depression, suicidal ideation prevalence approaches 38.2% [6], underscoring the urgent need for accurate assessment in this vulnerable clinical population.

While different types of SITBs have distinct definitions, evidence demonstrates strong interconnections among them. Research has demonstrated that NSSI is one of the strongest predictors of suicidal behavior, with these phenomena frequently co-occurring due to shared underlying mechanisms including emotional regulation deficits, behavioral disinhibition, and neurobiological alterations such as impaired prefrontal-amygdala functional connectivity [7, 8]. Given this overlap, assessing these phenomena in isolation cannot provide a complete clinical picture and risks underestimating suicide risk. Comprehensive assessment of the full spectrum of SITBs is therefore essential for appropriate care planning and effective therapeutic intervention.

Previous studies have developed scales that assess only specific aspects of SITBs, such as the Functional Assessment of Self-Mutilation (FASM) [9], the Revised Version of Adolescents Self-Harm Scale [10], the Objective Scale for Suicidal Intent [11], and the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation-Chinese Version (BSI-CV) [12]. However, existing self-report formats may compromise accuracy due to SITB conceptual ambiguities without clinical guidance, which limits their clinical adoption and hinders research comparability among Chinese adolescents. The original Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview (SITBI) was developed by Nock et al. in 2007 [13]. This structured interview tool comprises 169 items across five sections (i.e., suicidal ideation, suicide plans, suicide gestures, suicide attempts, and NSSI), assessing various dimensions including presence, frequency, age of onset, severity, function, precipitants, characteristics, and future likelihood. Its psychometric properties have been validated across inpatient, outpatient and community samples [14, 15]. This instrument has been translated into Spanish [16], German [17] and Korean [18]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in diagnosing NSSI and suicidal behavior disorder (SBD) according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) criteria [19, 20]. In 2020, Fox et al. developed a revised version (SITBI-R) to address limitations of the original version, including enhanced assessment of dangerous behaviors, NSSI severity, suicidal behavior intent and urges, expanded evaluation of suicidal ideation, more detailed assessment of suicide plans, and improved distinctions between suicide threats and gestures [21]. These enhancements are crucial for Chinese adolescents in clinical settings. For instance, suicide threats/gestures represent distinct non-suicidal phenomena that serve to communicate distress or elicit responses from others [22], yet their potentially socially unacceptable functions may hinder adolescent recognition or disclosure—challenges that underscore the clinical value of improved assessment precision. The SITBI-R has been increasingly utilized in research [23–25], with studies demonstrating its effectiveness among adolescents [26]. Most recently, a study validated the SITBI among Taiwanese outpatient adolescents [27], yet this validation was confined to outpatient populations, lacked comprehensive cultural adaptation for mainland Chinese contexts, did not include content validity assessment, and failed to establish diagnostic agreement with clinical criteria. These unresolved issues highlight the need for comprehensive SITBI-R validation, providing the foundation for the current work.

Therefore, this study aimed to translate and adapt the SITBI-R for the Chinese cultural context and evaluate its validity, reliability, and applicability in diagnosing NSSI and SBD, as well as assessing suicide risk among Chinese adolescents in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Registration No. KY-Q-2022-455-01, approved on December 14, 2022) and Guangdong 999 Brain Hospital (Registration No. 2022-020-036), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited from the inpatient and outpatient departments of these two hospitals between November 2022 and August 2023. Inclusion criteria included age between 12 and 19 years and documented presence of one or more types of SITBs in medical records. Exclusion criteria included severe acute psychosis, high risk of self-harm requiring intervention, organic brain diseases, unstable physical illnesses, and intellectual disability.

Sample size was determined based on previous SITBI development and translation studies, with a total of 170 eligible participants recruited. All participants and their guardians provided written informed consent and were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time for any reason.

Translation and cultural adaptation

This study followed the Brislin translation model. Two psychiatrists conducted the initial translation, followed by back-translation by two psychology experts. After cultural adaptation, three independent psychiatric professors were consulted for expert review and modifications, resulting in a preliminary Chinese version. Pilot testing was then conducted with 12 participants (female/male = 10/2), who were interviewed in detail about their understanding of each item and their overall interview experience. Based on their feedback and suggestions, the instrument was further refined to produce the final Chinese version (C-SITBI-R).

Data collection and assessment

The remaining 158 participants underwent formal interview data collection. Three psychiatrists who received consistency training conducted all participant interviews, including the two psychiatrists involved in the initial translation process plus one additional psychiatrist. Participants completed corresponding self-reported scales after the interview for construct validity analysis. Test-retest reliability was assessed by re-interviewing participants after 1–2 weeks using selected items from the C-SITBI-R. For interrater reliability evaluation, interviews were audio-recorded when permitted, and all three interviewers participated in the assessment process by independently completing blank C-SITBI-R forms while listening to the recorded interviews. Additionally, two independent psychiatrists with more than 20 years of clinical experience, who were not involved in the interview process, used DSM-5 criteria to diagnose NSSI or SBD and administered the M.I.N.I. section C to assess current suicide risk.

Measures

The Chinese version of the Self-Injurious thoughts and behaviors Interview-Revised (C-SITBI-R)

The C-SITBI-R comprises 85 items across 8 sections: NSSI (21 items), suicidal ideation (16 items), suicide plans (13 items), suicide preparation (2 items), suicide threats/gestures (7 items), aborted/interrupted attempts (8 items), suicide attempts (14 items), and nearby suicides (4 items). The interview typically takes about 10–30 min. Each type of SITB is initially screened for presence; if present, additional items such as age of onset, frequency, and severity are assessed. If absent, the interview skips to the next section. A 5-point Likert-type response options is used to assess urge/intensity, confidence, future likelihood, and other parameters. NSSI severity is evaluated through graduated response options. Open-ended questions collect detailed information on frequency, age of onset, and specific aspects including methods, location, timing of suicide plans, suicide preparation details, precipitating factors, methods, and consequences of suicide attempts.

The Chinese version of functional assessment of Self-Mutilation (C-FASM)

The C-FASM, developed by Qu et al. in 2020, is a 31-item instrument assessing various dimensions of NSSI [9]. Section A comprises a 10-item checklist evaluating past-year NSSI presence, frequency, and medical treatment. Item B assesses lifetime NSSI presence. Items C-G evaluate specific aspects for participants reporting past-year NSSI, including suicidal intent, duration of contemplation prior to self-injury, substance use during episodes, and pain experience. Section H utilizes a 15-item scale with 4-point Likert scaling (0–3) to assess NSSI functions across three domains: emotion regulation (items 2, 4, 9, 11, 15), attention seeking (items 3, 6, 7, 12, 13, 14), and social avoidance (items 1, 5, 8, 10).

The revised version of adolescents Self-Harm scale (Self-Harm scale)

The Adolescents Self-Harm Scale, originally developed by Zheng et al. in 2006 and subsequently revised by Feng et al. in 2008, comprises 18 items assessing lifetime NSSI methods in two dimensions [10]. NSSI frequency is evaluated using a 4-level scale (never, once, twice to four times, at least five times), and the severity is assessed using a 5-level scale (none, mild, moderate, severe, extremely severe). Each item’s total score is calculated as the product of frequency and severity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.85. In this study, self-harm was classified as “yes” (lifetime frequency ≥ 1) and “no” (lifetime frequency = 0) to determine the prevalence of each NSSI method.

Beck scale for suicide Ideation-Chinese version (BSI-CV)

The BSI-CV is a 19-item instrument adapted from the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSI), Scale for Suicide Ideation-Current (SSI-C), and Scale for Suicide Ideation-Worst (SSI-W) [12]. Each item is rated on a three-point scale (0–2) to assess suicidal thoughts over the past week and at their worst point. The instrument generates two subscale scores—suicidal ideation (items 1–5) and suicide risk (items 6–19)—and a total score. Higher scores are indicative of greater severity.

The Chinese version of Mini-International neuropsychiatric interview (M.I.N.I.)

The Chinese version of the M.I.N.I., translated in 2009 with the authors’ permission, includes a 6-item suicidality module (Section C). Five questions (C1-C5) assess past-month suicidality, covering passive suicidal ideation (i.e., wishing to be dead), active suicidal ideation, self-harm intentions, suicide plans, and attempts. The final question (C6) assesses lifetime suicide attempts. Current suicide risk is categorized based on the sum of points for “yes” responses as low risk (1–5 points), moderate risk (6–9 points), or high risk (≥ 10 points). Its internal consistency coefficient is 0.94 [28].

Data analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For the severity of various types of SITBs, consistent with previous studies [13, 17, 21], means and standard deviations were used for presentation. Characteristics between participants with and without suicide attempts were examined using Mann-Whitney U tests.

Regarding validity and reliability assessment, according to Nock et al., the SITBI-R is an in-person interview tool that assesses a wide range of thoughts and behaviors through multiple item forms, rather than a self-report tool measuring a single concept. Therefore, factor analysis and internal consistency reliability coefficients were not computed, as previous studies provided no theoretical or empirical support for such analyses. Content validity was evaluated using the Item-Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and Universal Agreement Scale-Content Validity Index (S-CVI/UA) with a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not relevant” to “highly relevant”, with I-CVI of 1.00 and an S-CVI/UA of ≥0.80 considered highly acceptable [29]. Construct validity was examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients, with ρ values above 0.70 indicating high consistency. For reliability assessment, two-way random effects absolute intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used for quantitative data in both interrater and test-retest reliability analyses. Fleiss’ κ was used for qualitative data when assessing interrater agreement among the three evaluators, while Cohen’s κ was used for pairwise comparisons in test-retest reliability. Values for both Cohen’s κ and ICCs below 0.40 indicated weak consistency, values above 0.75 indicated excellent consistency, and values between 0.40 and 0.75 indicated fair consistency. Fleiss’ κ values below 0.2 indicated low consistency, 0.2–0.4 indicated average consistency, 0.4–0.6 indicated moderate consistency, 0.6–0.8 indicated high consistency, and 0.8-1.0 indicated very high consistency [29].

The consistency between C-SITBI-R and DSM-5 criteria for NSSI and SBD diagnosis, and suicide risk assessment in M.I.N.I. Section C, was evaluated using Cohen’s κ.

No missing data occurred in this study. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cultural adaptation and content validity

Based on the feedback and suggestions from psychiatric professors and a pilot sample of 12 inpatient adolescents, several modifications were made to better align with the Chinese cultural context. The major revisions were as follows.

Substantial modifications were made to the assessment of NSSI methods, frequency, and purposes. First, “pulling hair” was added with 6-point severity levels (item 11) matching the grading format of other NSSI methods, ranging from “no hair loss” to “severe scalp avulsion requiring medical intervention (e.g., suturing, skin grafting)”. Second, the severity of “scraping or scratching skin” was modified to be based on wound area rather than bleeding amount, as pilot participants indicated that scratching injuries were typically more superficial and less severe compared to cutting injuries (item 5), making wound area a more appropriate and targeted assessment criterion. Third, “Hit self” was expanded to include beating oneself with hands, head-butting, and punching or kicking hard objects. Fourth, in addition to assessing the frequency, the newly added item 16 evaluates the number of days NSSI occurred in the past year, using five days as the cutoff, which aligns with the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for NSSI. Fifth, the questions about NSSI purposes were converted into three structured options based on C-FASM (i.e., emotion regulation, attention seeking and social avoidance) in item 19, replacing the original open-ended format.

Then, items assessing the duration of thoughts or considerations prior to engaging in SITBs were revised across multiple sections (e.g., item 18 in NSSI, items 8 and 15 in suicidal ideation, item 8 in suicide plans, and item 11 in suicide attempts). The original response options were “0 seconds, 1–59 seconds, 1–15 minutes, 16–60 minutes,” etc., totaling nine options. Pilot participants indicated that the time spans in these options were too small, making accurate selection difficult. Therefore, in the Chinese version, these items were revised using a 6-point response format referenced from the C-FASM, namely “fleeting, a few minutes, less than an hour, more than an hour but less than a day, more than a day but less than a week, more than a week”, and an additional option was added to indicate “too variable to summarize simply”.

Further modifications were made to other sections. In suicide plans and suicide attempts, culturally inappropriate methods such as “auto exhaust”, “other gases” and “firearms” were replaced with “charcoal burning or gas poisoning” to reflect common practices in the Chinese context. Items were also integrated and rearranged to better align with Chinese recall patterns, allowing researchers to establish customized interview sequences based on specific needs and circumstances. Besides, clearer distinctions were provided between aborted attempts and interrupted attempts, both of which involve stopping before taking any actual suicide action. Aborted attempts refer to self-abandoned plans due to incidental external events, while interrupted attempts involve termination by deliberate intervention. Examples of “incidental events” (e.g., unrelated phone call or text) and “deliberate intervention” (e.g., physical restraint, removal of sharp instruments, or persuading someone down from a roof-jump attempt) were added to the instructions to emphasize these differences, as suggested by the original author.

Additional content was removed or shortened. The dangerous behaviors section (i.e., tempting fate) was eliminated due to its rarity in pilot testing. Items asking about frequency “in the past year” and “in your life” of SITBs were also deleted due to difficulty in accurate recall and estimation.

The pilot testing demonstrated good safety and validity. No significant emotional downturns, irritability, or increased suicidal thoughts were reported during or after interviews. Both I-CVI and S-CVI/UA achieved 1.00 in this study.

For NSSI, cutting skin (n = 123) was the most frequently reported method and was rated as the most severe in terms of injury, with emotion regulation (n = 143) being the primary endorsed function. The common forms of suicidal ideation were “I wish I were never born” (26.1%), “I wish I could disappear or not exist” (19.7%), and “I wish I would go to sleep and never wake up again” (18.5%). When it came to actual attempts, the most frequent methods were overdose (57.3%), cutting (51.2%), and strangulation (26.8%). Individuals with a history of suicide attempts presented with a more severe clinical profile compared to those without. This was characterized by a greater diversity of NSSI methods (Z = − 3.00, p = 0.003), more time spent thinking about plans (Z = − 2.01, p = 0.044), more serious consideration of those plans (Z = − 3.01, p = 0.003), greater confidence in completing suicide (Z = − 2.330, p = 0.020), and more extensive suicide preparation (Z = − 5.42, p < 0.001).

SITB frequencies in the past week and past month showed significant positive correlations with BSI-CV suicidal ideation (ρ = 0.22–0.54, p < 0.05), BSI-CV total scores (ρ = 0.17–0.49, p < 0.05), Self-Harm Scale total scores (ρ = 0.17–0.35, p < 0.05), and M.I.N.I. Section C total scores (ρ = 0.22–0.71, p < 0.05). Regarding BSI-CV suicide risk, significant correlations were observed for aborted attempts, interrupted attempts, and suicide attempts in the past month (ρ = 0.22–0.45, p < 0.05). Notably, which involve deliberate negative statements and behaviors without genuine suicidal intent, demonstrated non-significant but negative correlations with the BSI-CV and M.I.N.I., supporting the conceptual validity of this distinction in the C-SITBI-R.

Individual items comparisons revealed strong consistencies. Past month and lifetime presence of NSSI, suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts showed excellent correlations with corresponding M.I.N.I. Section C items (ρ = 0.79-1.00, p < 0.001). NSSI methods demonstrated moderate to strong correlations with the Self-Harm Scale (ρ = 0.53–0.87, p < 0.001). Regarding NSSI functions, emotion regulation showed high consistency with the C-FASM (ρ = 0.68, p < 0.001), while attention seeking and social avoidance showed moderate consistencies (ρ = 0.46 and 0.38, respectively, p < 0.001). Lower correlations were observed for frequency and duration of worst-point suicidal ideation, and seriousness of worst-point suicide plans (ρ = 0.22–0.30, p < 0.05), likely due to differences in coding systems between instruments.

Interrater reliability

Sixty randomly selected recordings (20 from each interviewer) were analyzed for interrater reliability assessment. The analyses focused on the lifetime presence and frequency in the past week and past month across all types of SITBs. Perfect agreement was achieved, with all κ values and ICC values reaching 1.00 (p < 0.001).

Test-retest reliability

Thirty inpatient participants were selected for the retest assessment with a mean interval of 10 days (SD 3.60). Lifetime presence, age of onset, and the last occurrence of SITBs showed excellent consistencies with κs = 0.78-1.00 and ICCs = 0.93-1.00 (p < 0.001) (Table 5). Agreement for nearby completed suicide and nearby attempted suicide was also excellent, with κ values of 0.76 and 0.86, respectively, both p < 0.001. Past month frequencies of NSSI, suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts showed moderate to excellent agreements (ICCs = 0.64–0.75, p < 0.001), while frequencies of suicide threats/gestures, aborted attempts, and interrupted attempts were not significant (ICC = 0.60, p = 0.104;ICC = 0.49, p = 0.064; ICC = 0.55, p = 0.151, respectively), likely due to the low incidence rates and varying time frame interpretations. Further examination of specific NSSI methods revealed excellent consistencies for cutting skin (κ = 1.00, p < 0.001), burning skin (κ = 0.89, p < 0.001), biting self (κ = 0.92, p < 0.001), and pulling hair (κ = 0.85, p < 0.001), with good consistencies observed for inserting sharp objects (κ = 0.72, p < 0.001), scratching skin (κ = 0.70, p < 0.001), and hitting self (κ = 0.59, p < 0.01).

Diagnostic and risk assessment applicability

A diagnostic format was developed using specific C-SITBI-R items that align with DSM-5 criteria for NSSI and SBD, as well as the six M.I.N.I. Section C items for current suicide risk assessment (Table S1). Using this format, interviewers diagnosed NSSI and SBD and assessed current suicide risk, while independent psychiatrists conducted parallel evaluations based strictly on DSM-5 and M.I.N.I. criteria. High agreement was demonstrated across all 158 participants, with κ values of 1.00 for NSSI, 0.86 for SBD, and 0.94 for current suicide risk, all p < 0.001.

Discussion

This study adapted a comprehensive and structured clinical interview for assessing SITB among Chinese adolescent patients, demonstrating excellent psychometric properties and high applicability in matching DSM-5 diagnoses for NSSI and SBD.

The characteristics of NSSI observed in this sample offer clinical insights. The early age of onset (approximately 10–15 years), consistent with previous findings [13, 17], is particularly concerning given its established link to higher levels of bodily harm and greater suicide risk [30]. While skin cutting was the predominant method, it is crucial to recognize that NSSI methods vary significantly across cultural backgrounds, regions, and settings [31, 32], and are influenced by factors such as sexual orientation and gender identity [33, 34]. Furthermore, the primary function of emotion regulation in this study suggests that adolescents primarily engage in NSSI as a maladaptive coping strategy for overwhelming emotions when lacking effective emotional management skills., pointing to a clear target for therapeutic intervention.

Suicide threats/gestures demonstrated low prevalence and non-significant test-retest reliability over the past month, align with findings from the original and German versions of SITBI-R. This pattern suggests that suicide threats/gestures may function more as transient communicative behaviors rather than stable risk indicators, requiring different clinical interpretation compared to other SITB types. Their relatively minor consequences making them easier to forget, and their potentially socially unacceptable functions (i.e., manipulating others’ emotions and behaviors) may hinder adolescents from recognizing or disclosing their presence [13]. Similarly, low social acceptance may explain the weak consistency of “attention seeking” and “social avoidance” functions in NSSI. These findings highlight the importance of contextualizing reliability results within the specific behavioral characteristics of different SITB subtypes.

Significant gender imbalance was observed among study participants, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1, aligning with previous researches indicating female gender as a risk factor for NSSI and suicide attempts [35, 36]. Female adolescents are more susceptible to sex hormones fluctuations and brain development changes, exhibit poorer adaptation to negative emotions and weaker impulse control abilities. Additionally, they may experience more childhood trauma and increased exposure to negative influences of social media and peers, all contributing to this disparity [2, 37].

Another important finding concerns the increased behavioral complexity observed among adolescents with suicide attempts. Traditional perspectives viewed suicidal symptomatology as a continuum from suicidal ideation to action [38, 39]. However, many individuals attempt suicide without active ideation or prior planning, with 75–90% making final decisions within one hour of the act [40], challenging traditional suicide models. Recently, suicidal thoughts and behaviors have been shown to be highly unstable and fluctuating, and the suicide process is increasingly conceptualized as a dynamic system rather than a linear progression. Ecological momentary assessment studies demonstrate that the intensity of suicidal ideation can fluctuate dramatically within mere hours, and that while classic risk factors like hopelessness, burdensomeness, and loneliness correlate with these momentary thoughts, they have limited utility in predicting the next near-term spike [41]. For suicide, emotional impulsivity, mental disorders presence, sudden life events, and lack of social support can lead to the sudden onset of suicidal behavior or rapid fluctuations within short periods [40, 42]. This perspective provides new insights for understanding the complexity, individual variability, and temporal dynamics of suicidal behavior, offering important implications for suicide prevention research and practice. Furthermore, the prevalence of aborted/interrupted attempts in the current sample aligned with previous research [43, 44]. Studies on aborted/interrupted attempts show inconsistent consensus regarding their relationship to suicide attempts, with some indicating these attempts as precursors but with milder clinical symptoms than actual attempts [45], while others find minimal clinical differences between completed and aborted/interrupted attempts [44]. The non-significant test reliability of the frequency of these behaviors may reflect not only their low occurrence rates but also the characteristic instability of adolescent symptom reporting over brief intervals, as adolescents may genuinely experience varying levels of distress or behavioral intentions across assessment periods.

Moreover, the C-SITBI-R demonstrated high diagnostic applicability, enabled accurate diagnosis, promoted communication between clinicians and researchers, and enhanced the quality and comparability of different studies [46]. A high proportion (121 out of 145) of adolescents participated met the DSM-5 criteria for NSSI in this study. However, the five-day annual threshold remains controversial, with opinions ranging from being too low for effective threshold discrimination to serving as an appropriate early intervention warning line [47]. Similarly, the promotion and inclusion of SBD, along with current suicide risk assessment, facilitate timely identification and service of high-risk suicide groups [48].

The C-SITBI-R features concise, focused questions posed in a non-judgmental manner using developmentally appropriate language, directly addressing suicidal thoughts, intentions, and plans, including methods, timing, and locations. No significant emotional deterioration was observed among interviewees during the interviews. Evidences show that adolescents undergoing one-on-one suicide-related interviews experience less emotional distress, reduced suicidal ideation, and lower suicide risk compared to those completed self-report questionnaires [39]. Therefore, clinicians should not fear that such discussions might trigger additional SITBs, and should actively identify and explore potential protective factors.

Future directions should integrate assessment practices within suicidology paradigms that emphasize understanding individual suffering within biographical and sociocultural contexts [49] (e.g., by considering the influence of ecological-relational systems [50] and specific stressful life events [51]). Contemporary frameworks advocate moving beyond narrow risk focus to actively explore protective factors, including reasons for living [52]. For adolescent populations specifically, implementing such comprehensive approaches requires particular attention to educational settings, which represent primary environments where youth spend substantial time and where early intervention opportunities naturally arise [53]. The ongoing development of empirically supported, school-based universal prevention programs underscores the critical need for robust assessment tools in these contexts [54, 55]. The validated C-SITBI-R could provide foundations for these efforts through comprehensive evaluation, which is recognized as a core component of effective suicide interventions [56]. Its role is twofold: enabling standardized identification of at-risk adolescents for targeted interventions, and serving as a rigorous outcome measure to evaluate program efficacy across both clinical and educational settings.

This study has limitations. First, the treatments received by both inpatients and outpatients likely reduced SITBs, which may have weakened the test-retest reliability to some extent. Recall bias could also be a factor, given potential memory loss and attention deficits resulting from mental illness and MECT side effects. Moreover, despite initial clarifications by researchers, it is possible that some patients might have concealed the true extent of their SITBs to avoid extended hospitalization. Finally, as the instrument was culturally adapted for adolescents, its direct application to adults warrants caution. The different risk profiles of this older group may necessitate re-evaluating items from the original “dangerous behaviors” section (e.g., dangerous driving, volunteering for dangerous missions, or misuse of medications).

Overall, the revised interview instrument is suitable for Chinese adolescent patients in clinical settings, providing an interview framework and content for thorough assessment of self-injury and suicide-related situations, thereby assisting clinicians in systematic evaluation and risk management.

Conclusions

The C-SITBI-R demonstrated excellent psychometric properties and high clinical applicability among Chinese adolescents. This validated instrument provides clinicians with a standardized, reliable tool for comprehensive assessment of SITBs, accurate diagnosis of NSSI and SBD, and effective evaluation of current suicide risk in Chinese clinical settings.
