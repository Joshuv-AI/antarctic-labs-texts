Legal status, sex work engagement and inequities in the HIV pre‐exposure prophylaxis cascade among migrant women in 11 European countries
Authors: Haoyi Wang, Anne Flaherty‐Gupta, Hala Majdoub, Edwin Sesange, Kim Isip, Justine Lubnow, Pedro Barrios Sanchez, Linda Pavanello, Etinosa Erevbenagie‐Johnbull, Lillian Petry, Emma Gooding, Magdalena Ankiersztejn‐Bartczak, Stavroula Triantafyllidou, Ann Piercy, Kai J. Jonas, Denis Onyango, Hanne M. L. Zimmermann, the MiHealth HIV Partnership
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigrant women account for a substantial proportion of new HIV diagnoses in Europe, yet remain underrepresented in HIV prevention research. Little is known about how legal status and national policy context shape engagement with PrEP among migrant women, particularly those without secure documentation. We assessed the PrEP cascade and HIV prevention preferences among migrant women, examining how legal status, sex work engagement and national reimbursement policies drive inequities in access.MethodsWe conducted a community‐led, cross‐sectional survey (June–July 2025) of 521 HIV‐negative migrant women recruited offline via community‐based organisations. Multivariable logistic regression examined associations between legal status, sex work, country‐level PrEP reimbursement policies and PrEP cascade outcomes.ResultsAmong 521 participants, 259 (49.7%) lacked secure legal status (119 asylum seekers, 89 refugees and 51 undocumented migrants) and 90 (17.3%) were engaged in sex work. Overall, 40.1% (95% CI 35.9–44.5) were aware of PrEP and 24.6% (20.9–28.6) intended to use it, yet only 6.0% (4.1–8.4) were current users; 36.7% (23.8–51.7) of PrEP‐experienced women had discontinued use. Compared with naturalised citizens or documented residents, undocumented migrants (aOR = 2.57, 95% CI = 1.19–5.64), asylum seekers (3.67, 1.98–6.93) and refugees (2.28, 1.22–4.31) had higher odds of PrEP awareness. Sex workers had higher odds of current PrEP use (5.50, 1.61–20.42). Residing in countries with full PrEP reimbursement was independently associated with higher uptake (4.96, 1.30–26.11). Although condoms were the most preferred method (60.3%), interest in daily oral and long‐acting injectable PrEP was evident, particularly among undocumented women and sex workers.ConclusionA four‐fold gap between PrEP intention and actual uptake among migrant women reflects a failure of universal health coverage, driven by restrictive migration governance and health financing. Achieving HIV equity requires decoupling PrEP eligibility from documentation status and expanding community‐led delivery.

INTRODUCTION

Despite substantial advances in HIV prevention, migrants in low‐prevalence destination countries remain disproportionately affected by HIV [1]. In 2022–2023, nearly half of all new HIV diagnoses in the EU/EEA occurred among migrants [2]. Women account for approximately one‐quarter of new infections in the region [3, 4], and available data suggest that migrant women, particularly those without secure legal status, experience heightened vulnerability to HIV acquisition after arrival in Europe [2, 5]. Although surveillance systems rarely disaggregate by both gender and migration status, evidence from several countries, such as France, indicates that post‐migration exposures, including economic precarity, housing instability and constrained access to healthcare, contribute substantially to HIV vulnerability among migrant women [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Legal status is a critical but underexamined structural determinant of HIV prevention access. Across Europe, healthcare entitlements, insurance coverage and reimbursement policies vary widely by migration category, often restricting undocumented migrants and asylum seekers to emergency care or creating administrative and financial barriers to preventive services [2]. For women engaged in sex work, criminalisation, stigma and occupational marginalisation can further limit access to confidential and culturally competent care [11, 12, 13]. Together, these intersecting systems of migration governance, social exclusion and health financing may shape not only who can physically access biomedical prevention but also individual risk perception and the overall acceptability of these interventions [14].

Oral HIV pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) demonstrates high effectiveness [15, 16] when used consistently [17]. Daily oral and on‐demand PrEP have been available in many European countries for nearly a decade, and long‐acting injectable (LAI) cabotegravir has recently received European authorisation, expanding the prevention toolkit [18]. Yet, uptake and retention among women remain limited and uneven across populations [19], and data on migrant women are virtually absent. A recent systematic review estimated that although nearly half of heterosexual women in Europe were aware of PrEP, only a minority intended to use it, and actual uptake was extremely low, largely limited to clinical cohorts [19]. Notably, migrant women and female sex workers (FSW) were largely absent from these analyses. Consequently, little is known about how legal precarity, sex work engagement and national reimbursement policies shape the PrEP cascade—awareness, intention, uptake and discontinuation—among migrant women.

Migrant women and those engaged in sex work are frequently excluded from traditional clinical cohorts and national surveillance. Community‐based engagement has been recognised as central to equitable HIV responses, particularly for populations experiencing distrust of formal health systems or exclusion from statutory services [20]. Partnering with community‐based organisations (CBOs) fosters trust, facilitates access and generates data otherwise inaccessible through formal health systems [21]. Yet, few studies have meaningfully engaged migrant women, especially undocumented migrants and those engaged in sex work, in HIV prevention and PrEP research.

In this study, we conducted a community‐led, multi‐country survey across 11 European countries to provide a contemporary assessment of the PrEP cascade among migrant women originating predominantly from the Global South. We examined patterns of awareness, intention, uptake and discontinuation; explored HIV prevention preferences, including oral and LAI‐PrEP; and assessed how migration governance (legal status), sex work engagement and national health financing arrangements (PrEP reimbursement policy) shape inequities in access to prevention. By centring populations often excluded from research and policy discourse, this analysis aims to inform more equitable HIV prevention strategies across Europe.

METHODS

Study participants and recruitment

We conducted a cross‐sectional survey between June and July 2025 across 11 European countries: France, Malta, Spain, Italy, Cyprus, Germany, Belgium, Poland, Greece, the United Kingdom (UK) and Ireland. The survey was developed by the communities and the CBOs working with migrants and people affected by HIV in these countries (Mi‐Health Partnership, https://mihealtheurope.org/), and was co‐designed and reviewed by sexual health and HIV experts from Maastricht University. Recruitment occurred primarily offline through outreach activities organised by partner CBOs, including events in migrant community centres, women's centres, refugee centres and community HIV testing events. A list of collaborating CBOs is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The survey was available in English, French, Spanish, Russian, Urdu, Ukrainian, Italian and Portuguese.

Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older; self‐identified as female (including cisgender, transgender and non‐binary individuals); reported being HIV‐negative; were migrants (born outside their current country of residence); and were residing in Europe. Participants who did not meet these criteria were excluded. Individuals residing outside the countries where outreach activities occurred were not excluded, recognising the mobility of migrant populations who may access community events transnationally [22]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before survey completion. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Review Committee Psychology and Neuroscience at Maastricht University (OZL_262_08_01_2023_S36).

Measures

All measures in this analysis were self‐reported. Legal status was categorised as: (1) naturalised citizen/documented resident (non‐temporary residents); (2) refugee (formally recognise as needing international protection and granted refugee status); (3) asylum seeker (applied for protection, awaiting decision); and (4) undocumented migrant (without the required legal authorisation or documentation). In this analysis, we considered migrants with a naturalised citizen/documented resident (non‐temporary residents) as having a secure legal status, while migrants being asylum seekers, undocumented and refugees (given that refugee status is often temporary, revocable and associated with precarious living conditions [e.g., reception centres]) as having an insecure status. Participants also self‐reported their current sex work engagement (female sex worker vs. non–sex worker).

PrEP cascade indicators included PrEP awareness, intention to use PrEP, current oral PrEP use and PrEP discontinuation (previously used but no longer using, only available among those migrant women who were PrEP‐experienced). Each indicator was measured as binary (‘yes’ or ‘no’). To assess HIV prevention preferences, participants ranked their preferred prevention methods among condom, female condom, daily oral PrEP, on‐demand oral PrEP, LAI‐PrEP and avoiding the sharing of injection drug equipment.

Sociodemographic variables included age, education, perceived financial status, ethnic background, housing status and region of origin (classified by WHO region). To adjust for PrEP access context, we categorised countries according to their PrEP reimbursement status as of 1 June 2025: fully reimbursed (PrEP covered by health insurance), partially reimbursed (copayment required) and non‐reimbursed (out‐of‐pocket payment only) as a policy proxy for the country of residence. (Sexual‐)Behavioural variables included relationship status, experience of partner infidelity, perceived difficulty discussing sexual health with partners and experience of (sexual) violence. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterise participants' sociodemographic, (sexual‐)behavioural and outcome variables across the four legal status categories. Between‐group differences were assessed using the χ 2 test.

We then fitted univariable logistic regression models to examine associations between each explanatory variable and the following indicators: PrEP awareness, intention to use PrEP and current PrEP uptake. PrEP discontinuation was excluded from modelling due to limited statistical power, as only a small number of participants had prior PrEP experience. For each endpoint, stepwise selection was applied to identify the best‐fitting multivariable model based on the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC). Legal status and sex work engagement were retained in all models regardless of statistical significance, given their conceptual importance to the study. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Regression diagnostics showed no evidence of collinearity, outliers, or violations of model assumptions. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two‐sided).

RESULTS

Study population characteristics

Of 525 HIV‐negative migrant women living in Europe who participated in the survey, four were excluded for not reporting their migrant status, leaving 521 participants in the final analysis.

Almost half of the participants (n = 259, 49.7%) did not have a secure legal status: 51 (9.8%) were undocumented migrants, 119 (22.8%) asylum seekers and 89 (17.1%) refugees, while 262 (50.3%) were naturalised citizens or documented residents. Ninety (17.3%) participants self‐identified as FSWs. Almost all participants (n = 511) resided in countries where the community outreach occurred, although 10 were living elsewhere in Europe (i.e., Austria, Bulgaria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, the Netherlands and Norway; Figure 1).

Overall, the median age was 36 years (interquartile range [IQR] 29–44). By legal status, median age was 32 years (IQR 28–38) among undocumented migrants, 33 (26–40) among asylum seekers, 37 (32–45) among refugees and 38 (30–49) among naturalised citizens/documented residents. Perceived financial status differed significantly across migrant groups (χ 2 test, p < 0.001), with undocumented migrants and asylum seekers reporting higher levels of financial hardship and unstable housing compared to the overall sample. Over half of undocumented migrants (54.9%) were sex workers, compared with 3.4% of asylum seekers and 6.7% of refugees (p < 0.001). Undocumented migrants were also more likely to report partner infidelity and difficulty discussing sexual health with partners, despite being statistically insignificant. PrEP cascade

Figure 2 summarises PrEP awareness, intention to use, current oral PrEP use and discontinuation by legal status and sex work engagement. Overall, 40.1% (95% CI = 35.9–44.5) of participants were aware of PrEP, 24.6% (20.9–28.6) intended to use PrEP and 6.0% (4.1–8.4) were current users; among those with PrEP experience, 36.7% (23.8–51.7) reported discontinuation.

PrEP cascade among undocumented migrant women, asylum‐seeking women, refugee women, naturalised citizen/documented resident women and their sex work engagement status in Europe, June 2025. Error bars show 95% confidence intervals using continuity correction methods.

By legal status, undocumented migrants had the highest PrEP awareness (64.7%, 47.0–75.0), intention (39.2%, 26.3–53.5) and uptake (23.5%, 13.2–37.8) and the lowest discontinuation (14.3%, 2.5–43.8). Asylum seekers also reported higher awareness (47.9%, 38.7–57.2) and intention (31.9%, 23.9–41.2), but low uptake (2.5%, 0.7–7.7). Refugees had the second lowest awareness (34.8%, 25.2–45.7) and intention (18.0%, 10.9–27.8), and the highest discontinuation (75.0%, 35.6–95.5). Naturalised/documented residents had the lowest overall engagement across the cascade.

By sex work engagement, female migrant sex workers had higher PrEP awareness (60.0%, 49.1–70.0), intention (30.0%, 21.0–40.7) and uptake (18.9%, 11.7–28.8) than non‐sex workers (awareness [36.0%, 31.5–40.7]; intention [23.4%, 19.6–27.8]; uptake [3.2%, 1.9–5.5]). Discontinuation was lower among sex workers (29.1%, 13.4–51.2) than non–sex workers (44.0%, 35.0–64.7).

For current PrEP uptake, higher odds were observed among sex workers (aOR = 5.50, 1.61–20.42), those from the Western Pacific region (aOR = 14.3, 2.50–79.83), and those who found it easy to discuss sexual health with partners (aOR = 5.82, 1.66–26.01). HIV prevention preferences

Figure 3 shows the three most frequently ranked HIV prevention preferences. Overall, condoms were most preferred (60.3%, 55.6–64.8), followed by female condoms (10.4%, 7.8–13.7) and daily oral PrEP (9.3%, 6.9–12.4).

Top 3 preferred HIV prevention methods among undocumented migrant women, asylum‐seeking women, refugee women, naturalised citizen/documented resident women and their sex work engagement status in Europe, June 2025. Error bars show 95% confidence intervals using continuity correction methods.

Similar patterns were seen across legal and sex work status. However, undocumented migrants and asylum seekers preferred daily oral PrEP over female condoms (undocumented migrants: 16.7% vs. 7.1%; asylum seekers: 15.7% vs. 13.0%). Refugees showed a similar preference for daily oral PrEP and avoiding shared injection equipment (both 5.3%). Meanwhile, female migrant sex workers reported a higher preference for daily oral PrEP (11.8%, 5.9–21.8) than for female condoms; they additionally preferred on‐demand oral PrEP and LAI‐PrEP (3.9%, 1.1–11.9).

DISCUSSION

In this multi‐country, community‐led survey of migrant women across 11 European countries, we identified a substantial gap between PrEP awareness and intention, and sustained uptake. Although nearly one quarter of participants reported intention to use PrEP, only 6% were current users, and more than one third of women with prior PrEP experience had discontinued. These findings indicate that demand for biomedical prevention exists among migrant women living in Europe, but structural barriers impede translation of intent into sustained use [19]. Three patterns merit particular attention: the differentiated role of legal status and sex work across cascade stages, the role of reimbursement status of PrEP in the resident countries in PrEP uptake, and the high rate of discontinuation among PrEP‐experienced women.

Legal status was consistently associated with engagement across the PrEP cascade. Undocumented migrants, asylum seekers and refugees had higher odds of PrEP awareness compared with naturalised citizens or documented residents. This pattern may reflect the higher perception of HIV vulnerability among undocumented migrants, asylum seekers or refugees [22, 23], or their greater exposure in settings where HIV prevention messaging is more visible [24]. Conversely, these data might also indicate that migrants with more secure legal statuses (e.g., naturalised citizens and documented residents) may perceive themselves to be at lower risk of HIV, despite evidence that HIV acquisition frequently occurs post‐migration; up to 51% of new infections among migrant women in Europe are estimated to be acquired after arrival [8]. However, higher awareness did not correspond to higher uptake among the migrant women included in this analysis, indicating that informational access alone may be insufficient to ensure continuity of prevention. Specifically, asylum seekers and refugees, in addition to facing socioeconomic instability, showed lower PrEP use, possibly reflecting limited access to healthcare systems and fear of disclosure within formal medical settings [25, 26]. These findings suggest that healthcare access barriers, rather than lack of motivation, are the primary obstacle for newly arrived migrants, and underscore the need for PrEP delivery models that do not require stable documentation or insurance status [27, 28].

Health financing, as the structural context, appeared to play an important role. Residing in countries where PrEP is fully reimbursed was significantly associated with higher uptake among migrant women, suggesting that financing arrangements and coverage policies can influence access [29, 30, 31]. Reimbursement policy intersected with legal status: even in countries with full reimbursement, migrant women without secure documentation may face administrative requirements, such as proof of residence, national insurance registration, or identity documentation, that effectively exclude them from coverage [32]. Harmonising eligibility criteria to decouple PrEP access from legal status, as several countries have begun to do for emergency care [33], is a necessary policy step if reimbursement gains are to benefit the most marginalised.

Undocumented migrant women, in particular, were disproportionately represented among those with unstable housing and financial insecurity, and over half reported engaging in sex work. They were also more likely to experience relationship infidelity and difficulty discussing sexual health with partners, as highlighted in our data. These compounded vulnerabilities may increase their risk of HIV exposure, yet they also appear to show a higher intention to use PrEP [34, 35]. This pattern is consistent with previous studies among migrant and FSW populations [5, 36, 37], where social marginalisation is associated with both higher (perceived) HIV risk and proactive health‐seeking behaviours when services are made available [38, 39]. In addition, as revealed in our analysis, sex work engagement was associated with higher current PrEP use. This finding may reflect occupational risk awareness and successful engagement through sex worker–focused outreach networks [5, 40]. The higher uptake among sex workers, despite facing substantial stigma and barriers to routine care, underscores the effectiveness of targeted, community‐linked delivery [41] and suggests that similar models could benefit other high‐vulnerability subgroups if systematically implemented.

Beyond initial uptake, the high rate of discontinuation observed among PrEP‐experienced women warrants further attention. Reasons for PrEP discontinuation were not systematically assessed in this study, limiting our ability to identify individual‐level drivers of discontinuation. However, discontinuation may reflect a combination of structural barriers and individual‐level factors, including challenges accessing follow‐up care, financial constraints, concerns about side effects, stigma, partner dynamics and changes in perceived HIV risk. Given the socioeconomic instability experienced by many migrant women in this study, continuity of PrEP care may be particularly difficult to sustain in the context of unstable housing, mobility across regions or countries, and variable healthcare entitlements [34, 35]. The particularly high discontinuation among refugees, many of whom were housed in reception centres with limited transport access and precarious living circumstances, may suggest that continuity of care represents an important challenge, rather than initial PrEP initiation. As LAI‐PrEP becomes more widely available in Europe, differentiated delivery models that reduce visit frequency and simplify follow‐up requirements may support persistence among women facing unstable living conditions or competing social pressures [42, 43]. Community‐based dispensing and mobile health delivery have shown promise in South African contexts [44] and merit prospective evaluation within European migrant health programmes.

Condoms remained the most preferred HIV prevention method across all subgroups (60.3%), consistent with global evidence of sustained condom preference in diverse populations [45, 46]. A notable gap existed between intention to use PrEP (24.6%) and ranking PrEP as a preferred method (9.3%). This likely reflects the practical reality that condoms are more familiar [47], immediately accessible and require no medical appointment [19], rather than a preference over biomedical prevention. Furthermore, preference for condoms could encompass a dual need for HIV and STI prevention, as well as contraception. Concerns regarding the side effects of medications, mistrust of biomedical interventions, or fears of stigma and partner violence if ‘HIV pills’ are discovered [48] may also strongly influence the preference for condoms among migrant women. However, unfortunately, condoms are not always consistently available or accessible to migrant women [49], which further highlights the importance of ensuring a reliable supply alongside PrEP expansion to the migrant women population. More importantly, female migrant sex workers showed an even broader preference profile, including on‐demand oral and LAI‐PrEP, suggesting awareness of occupational exposure and willingness to adopt prevention methods that enhance adherence and autonomy [50], particularly in the context of reported sexual violence, as observed in our analysis. These preference patterns reinforce the case for diversified, person‐centred prevention options that reflect the varied circumstances of migrant women.

These findings have three direct policy implications. First, migrant women—irrespective of legal status—should be explicitly recognised as a priority population in national and European HIV prevention frameworks, with PrEP eligibility and reimbursement decoupled from documentation requirements. Second, prevention programmes must offer multiple modalities (condoms, daily oral PrEP and LAI‐PrEP) to accommodate the heterogeneous needs and access realities of migrant women, with female migrant sex workers specifically prioritised for long‐acting formulations given their elevated uptake and risk profile, particularly where adherence to daily oral PrEP or other HIV prevention methods can be challenging due to high occupational exposure or common partner/client sexual violence [51]. Third, our findings highlight the importance of considering intersectionalities among key populations for HIV, especially migrant status and their engagement in sex work. HIV services, interventions and communications must be aware of the potential intersectionalities to fully engage these populations [52].

The community‐led design of this study was essential to its reach [20]. Nearly half of the participants did not have a secure legal status, and one in five reported engaging in sex work, groups often underrepresented in epidemiological research or surveillance networks [2]. This was achieved through partnerships with trusted CBOs who conducted offline outreach in community centres, women's shelters and HIV testing events, rather than digital recruitment that tends to exclude the most marginalised [21]. These findings demonstrate that meaningful epidemiological data on structurally excluded populations can be generated when research is designed with and by affected communities, and that similar community‐embedded models are required for equitable PrEP delivery.

This study has several limitations. The cross‐sectional design limits causal inference, and the use of self‐reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive outcomes such as sex work engagement and HIV risk behaviours. Recruitment through community networks may limit generalisability to migrant women not connected to outreach organisations. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the higher PrEP awareness and uptake observed among certain subgroups, such as sex workers and undocumented migrants, may partly reflect their prior exposure to targeted prevention or outreach activities by CBOs, rather than their legal or occupational status alone. Consequently, our findings might overestimate awareness levels compared to the broader migrant population not connected to these networks. In addition, reasons for PrEP discontinuation were not systematically collected, limiting our ability to determine whether discontinuation reflected changing prevention needs, individual preferences, or barriers related to healthcare access and continuity of care. Further, the 11 countries included exhibit substantial heterogeneity in both migration policy environments and healthcare financing structures; country‐level variation could not be fully modelled given sample sizes, which limits the generalisability of our policy findings to specific national contexts. Importantly, while the overall sample was adequately powered for PrEP awareness analyses, the absolute number of women currently using PrEP was small, resulting in sparse data in the uptake regression model. Adjusted odds ratios with wide confidence intervals should be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory rather than definitive estimates. Replication in larger samples with greater representation of current PrEP users is needed to confirm these associations in future studies. Nevertheless, the multi‐country scope, multilingual implementation and inclusion of structurally marginalised subgroups strengthen the contribution of this analysis to understanding prevention access in contemporary Europe. Lastly, our findings are specific to migrant women in Europe and may not be generalisable to other migrant groups, such as MSM or trans* populations, or to regions with different migration dynamics and healthcare systems/frameworks, such as North American or Asia‐Pacific countries.

In conclusion, this pan‐European, community‐led study highlights a pronounced gap between PrEP intention and uptake among migrant women in Europe. While inequities in HIV prevention access among migrant populations have been previously described, this study provides multi‐country empirical evidence across 11 European settings, with a specific focus on migrant women and the role of legal status, sex work engagement and national reimbursement context. Although the relatively small number of current PrEP users warrants cautious interpretation of some subgroup findings, the results suggest that differences in PrEP uptake may reflect a combination of structural and individual factors, rather than lack of demand alone, and are consistent with broader evidence on gaps in universal health coverage for migrant populations across Europe [9, 10, 53]. Legal insecurity and inadequate reimbursement policies may contribute to differences in access to PrEP, alongside other individual and structural factors. Closing this gap requires decoupling PrEP eligibility from migration status, expanding long‐acting formulations with differentiated delivery, and embedding HIV prevention within trusted community‐led programmes that can reach women that conventional health systems cannot.
