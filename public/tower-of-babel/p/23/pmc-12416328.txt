High-grade astrocytoma with piloid features (HGAP) is a newly described entity that is characterized by its unique DNA methylation pattern and functional alterations in CDKN2A/B, ATRX, and the MAPK pathway.1 HGAP was recently added to the WHO 2021 CNS classification system among the group of circumscribed astrocytic gliomas and present with high-grade piloid histology with similarities to glioblastoma.2 Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas include 6 types: pilocytic astrocytoma, high-grade astrocytoma with piloid features, pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, chordoid glioma, and astroblastoma.3 These tumors are most frequently located along the midline, such as in the posterior fossa and the diencephalic region. They typically present at a median patient age of 41 years, with no preference for either sex.1,4 There is an association between HGAP and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; around 9% of the reported cases),2,5–9 and some authors have suggested that this etiology should be a suspected cause of obstructive hydrocephalus in patients with NF1.6

Prognostic data for HGAP are somewhat limited, but one study reported a 5-year overall survival rate of 50%, with long-term survival exceeding 25 years in some cases.6 While a definitive CNS WHO grade is not currently assigned, the clinical behavior of HGAP is thought to roughly correspond to CNS WHO grade 3 tumors based on the 2021 classification, with the mainstay of treatment consisting of resection plus combined chemoradiation therapy.10 When HGAP tumors are located in the pineal region, resection becomes particularly challenging due to their deep location and proximity to critical structures such as deep venous structures, the midbrain, and the thalamus. However, the introduction of the exoscope in the operating room has improved ergonomics compared with the microscope, offering enhanced visualization while maintaining more space in the surgical field compared with the endoscope. This technological advancement facilitates safer and more effective resection in this complex anatomical area.11

Herein, we present the case of a 69-year-old man with an HGAP located in the pineal region. The tumor was near-totally resected using an exoscopic supracerebellar/infratentorial approach, which allowed for enhanced visualization and improved surgical precision.

Illustrative Case

History and Presentation

A 69-year-old, right-handed man presented to the emergency department with a 10-day history of lightheadedness, blurry vision, memory loss, and urinary incontinence. The patient had a medical history of hypertension but was otherwise healthy. Because of his symptoms and the suggestive diagnosis of normal pressure hydrocephalus, the patient underwent CT of the brain. CT showed a pineal gland lesion with mass effect on the midbrain and tectum and obstructive hydrocephalus. Confirmatory MRI of the brain (Fig. 1A–C) showed a 22 × 24 × 24–mm heterogeneously enhancing, partly cystic/necrotic mass in the pineal cistern with mass effect on the tectum, with obstructive hydrocephalus. The lesion demonstrated a significant diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). The differential diagnosis for the lesion included pineocytoma, pineoblastoma, germ cell tumor, brain metastasis, and tectal plate glioma. A lumbar puncture was contraindicated due to the risk of brain herniation. Thus, the patient underwent endoscopic third ventriculostomy with biopsy of the lesion. The pathology was initially reported to be a pineal tumor with intermediate differentiation. CSF samples were negative for alfa-fetoprotein and beta–human chorionic gonadotropin. Operative Details

A left paramedian supracerebellar/infratentorial approach was chosen with the patient in a semi-sitting position (Video 1). Precordial Doppler was used for detection of an air embolism. The patient’s head was fixed in a Mayfield head holder, and the patient’s anatomy was registered with stereotactic navigation. Augmented reality was used through the microscope to map the surgical template to the scalp. The left paramedian suboccipital region was prepped. An L-shaped incision was made with the short limb to the left and long one midline. Soft tissue was mobilized inferolaterally. A suboccipital paramedian keyhole of 3 × 2 cm was made with the drill. The dura was opened and hinged superiorly at the traverse sinus, and tack up sutures were placed.

At this point, the exoscope was used for visualization. In this case, we used the ORBEYE Exoscope (Olympus America Inc.). The main arm of the exoscope was placed over the left nondominant shoulder of surgeon with the camera pointed upward and the patient in a semi-sitting position (Fig. 1D). The space between the tentorium and the cerebellum was dissected all the way to the pineal region. The midline veins were carefully preserved. The arachnoid of the cerebellum-mesencephalic fissure was opened to expose the tumor. Special attention was paid to the superior cerebellar artery and the fourth nerve. Once the arachnoid adhesions were taken down, the tumor was identified at the level of the TIGR triangle (i.e., the region bordered by the tentorial surface of the cerebellum, the internal cerebral vein and vein of Galen complex, and vein of Rosenthal).12 We coagulated the posterior wall, and we performed a central debulking using an ultrasonic aspirator. The tumor was soft and moderately vascularized. The right portion of the tumor capsule was detached from the posterior thalamus using ring curettes. Using small cottonoids, we dissected the tumor along normal brainstem interface, as well as the superior attachment at the vein of Galen. With stepwise debulking and dissection, much of the tumor was removed. Subsequently, an angled endoscope was placed into the third ventricle for inspection, and we could observe more tumor tissue on the left thalamus. The exoscope was readjusted to see this area, and a large portion of the tumor was dissected and removed from the posterior left thalamic wall.

At the end, a tiny tumor attachment was left adherent to the top of the brainstem. The third ventricle was wide open with clear visualization of the foramen of Monro on both sides. After hemostasis, the dura was reapproximated using a primary closure and augmented with dural sealant. The soft tissue and skin were closed per routine.

Histopathology showed a vascular and vaguely nested tumor with focal microvascular proliferation and necrosis (Fig. 2). Multiple mitotic figures were noted, but there was no rosette formation. The tumor cells were strongly and diffusely positive for synaptophysin and Olig-2, and negative for GFAP and neurofilament. The Ki-67 proliferation index was focally increased up to 30%. The tumor cells were negative for IDH1 R132H mutant antibody. The vast majority of tumor nuclei showed loss of expression of ATRX and P53 immunostaining in a wildtype pattern. Postoperative Course

On the 1st postoperative day, the patient was stable and extubated. Postoperative MRI showed no acute bleeding or ischemia and a macroscopically near-total resection (Fig. 3). The patient’s course was complicated by posterior fossa syndrome with akinetic mutism, which resolved after approximately 1 week. He received 2 cycles of temozolomide with concomitant radiation therapy up to 6000 cGy. His most recent MRI study performed 6 months after surgery showed that the postoperative enhancement had resolved, and he had no evidence of disease. Discussion

We have described the resection of an infrequent pineal region tumor. The resection performed via an exoscopic supracerebellar/infratentorial approach was successful, but the patient’s postoperative course was briefly complicated by akinetic mutism.

Observations

High-Grade Astrocytoma With Piloid Features

HGAP was identified as a unique subtype of circumscribed glioma in 2021.2 The histopathology of HGAPs shows a range of features, including both lower-grade and higher-grade characteristics, with some overlapping traits seen in pilocytic astrocytoma and glioblastoma. Due to these overlapping features, diagnosing HGAP depends on its epigenetic profile. Specifically, DNA methylation profiling is essential for making a definitive diagnosis.13

Histologically, the majority of these tumors manifest as moderately pleomorphic astrocytic gliomas, resembling glioblastoma or pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma. The piloid processes are fine, hairlike structures, which can mimic pilocytic astrocytoma or anaplastic pilocytic astrocytoma. However, glioblastoma-like features are more common, with Rosenthal fibers or eosinophilic granular bodies in approximately one-third of cases and microvascular proliferation with or without necrosis in two-thirds of cases.6 Immunohistochemical staining is helpful to differentiate from other midline diffuse gliomas, as HGAP tumors are IDH wildtype and overwhelmingly negative for H3K27m immunostaining.3 Molecular testing commonly reveals deletions of CDKN2A/B, alterations in the MAPK pathway genes, and mutations in ATRX or loss of ATRX expression.1 However, due to a paucity of reported cases, the prognosis for these tumors varies between WHO grade 1 and grade 4.

While pathology-based identifiers are well established, radiological features remain largely inconclusive, as HGAP is a relatively rare tumor. T2-weighted imaging shows a well-circumscribed mass that aligns with the glial tumor cells being thickened and hyalinized in nature.

FLAIR imaging revealed suppression of internal hyperintense foci, indicating a T2-FLAIR mismatch. This finding is highly specific for IDH-mutant astrocytoma in adults, distinguishing it from other diffuse gliomas like oligodendroglioma.14 Post-gadolinium images demonstrated uneven enhancement in winding, serpentine areas, corresponding to regions with increased microvascular density and vessels showing thickening and hyaline degeneration. Due to the varied cellularity and proliferative index, DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient mapping showed no significant diffusion restriction.15 As mentioned above, these tumors were described in the midline, even in the spinal cord,16 and as far as we know, this represents the second HGAP in the pineal region.17

The standard treatment for HGAP following resection remains undefined. Due to the similarities with high-grade astrocytomas, some researchers have recommended considering adjuvant chemoradiation therapy. Temozolomide has been used by some as an adjuvant after surgery and radiotherapy, but its effectiveness varies, and its exact role is still being studied, especially due to the presence of multiple molecular mutations.2 One case report described the use of chemotherapy with carboplatin and vincristine in a young female with cerebellar HGAP, which resulted in transient stability for 4 months before the regimen was switched to selumetinib due to tumor progression.18 Given the diverse clinical experiences and the molecular variations observed in HGAP, it is recommended that treatment be tailored to each individual case. DNA methylation profiling or gene panel sequencing are recommended for any pilocytic astrocytoma that exhibits features of a high-grade glioma. This diagnostic tool can help clarify the tumor’s molecular characteristics, aiding in the accurate classification and informing treatment decisions.1

Based on the small series reported, the prognosis for HGAP is poor, with a 5-year survival rate of around 50%. While this is somewhat better than the survival rate for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, it is still lower than that of IDH-mutant gliomas.1,2

Utilization of the Exoscope for Paramedian Supracerebellar Infratentorial Approach

The supracerebellar infratentorial (SCIT) approach, first described by Victor Horsley more than 100 years ago, has undergone significant evolution in its surgical technique.19 Although Horsley initially introduced the concept, it was not until 1971 that Stein formally documented the surgical approach.20 The original technique involved a midline craniotomy with bilateral opening of the posterior fossa dura, performed in a sitting position. However, it was not until 1974 that a paramedian variation of the approach was introduced, marking an important refinement in the procedure.21 The paramedian variation offers better access to certain lateral regions, such as the posterior incisural space and hippocampal gyrus. Additionally, minimizing the opening with the paramedian approach reduces the risk of injury and thrombosis of the torcula,22 and the cerebellar bridging veins are less prominent when moving laterally, making their preservation more feasible.

Although the sitting position can provide advantages like facilitating gravity-assisted drainage of blood and CSF, minimizing bleeding, ensuring a clearer surgical field, and enabling continuous bimanual dissection, it also poses certain risks, such as venous air embolism, tension pneumocephalus, and peripheral nerve damage.23 Despite these risks, many neurosurgeons still favor the sitting position for posterior fossa surgeries, supported by various studies demonstrating that the operative risks associated with body positioning are minimal, provided there is thorough preoperative preparation, effective collaboration with anesthesiologists during the procedure, and vigilant monitoring.24,25

In addition to the technical aspects of the surgical approach, the sitting position might not always offer the most comfort for the surgeon. Working with elevated and outstretched arms can impose significant physical strain, especially during long or complex procedures and even when an armrest supports the lower arm or wrist. The degree of arm extension required depends on the ocular-corpus length of the neurosurgical microscope.26 To reduce surgeon fatigue during this approach, other authors have introduced the use of the endoscope and exoscope. When using the endoscope, the differences in surgical freedom and angle of attack are minimal in practical use, particularly in paramedian approaches. As the cranial opening shifts laterally, the natural slope of the cerebellum and tentorium directs the endoscope more inferiorly toward the posterior incisura, granting access from the splenium to the superior colliculi.27 While it enhances surgical comfort and provides clear visualization, the choice of method should ultimately depend on the surgeon’s familiarity with and experience using the equipment.

On the other hand, the first SCIT approach using an exoscope was reported in 2012.28 The advent of the 3D exoscope system has created new opportunities for enhanced visualization and improved ergonomics in neurosurgery. In comparison with the traditional operating microscope, the 3D exoscope offers greater magnification, superior depth perception, and similar stereoscopic effects. Moreover, the 3D exoscope offers significant ergonomic advantages, enabling the surgeon to adjust the view to nearly any position using a hydraulic counterbalance system. Additionally, the ocular-corpus length is reduced, which in turn minimizes the degree of arm extension. In contrast with the endoscope, the surgical corridor is still long and narrow, making it more challenging to dissect the inferior aspect of a pineal or cerebellar tumor, particularly if it is covered by the apex of the culmen. However, the TIGR12 triangle is easily visualized, which defines the window, or deep keyhole, to access the pineal region in paramedian approaches. In our experience, the exoscope is an effective tool for pineal region tumor resection and hydrocephalus relief, especially in cases involving posterior third ventricle invasion. It allows for total resection to be achieved with minimal complications, demonstrating its value in these complex procedures.

Lessons

HGAP was identified as a distinct subtype of glioma in 2021 and, as far we know, here we present the second case in the pineal region. Histopathologically, HGAP shows both lower- and higher-grade features, with overlapping traits of pilocytic astrocytoma and glioblastoma, making diagnosis dependent on DNA methylation profiling. Histologically, these tumors often resemble glioblastoma or pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma, with Rosenthal fibers, eosinophilic granular bodies, and microvascular proliferation present. HGAP tumors are IDH wildtype and negative for H3K27m staining. Radiologically, HGAP presents as a well-circumscribed mass with T2-FLAIR mismatch and uneven post-gadolinium enhancement. Treatment remains undefined, but adjuvant chemoradiation therapy, including temozolomide, may be considered. Prognosis is poor, with a 5-year survival rate around 50%.

To mitigate surgeon fatigue while performing an SCIT approach, alternatives like the endoscope and exoscope have been introduced. While the endoscope offers minimal differences in surgical freedom and angle of attack, especially in paramedian approaches, the exoscope presents notable advantages. The 3D exoscope allows for enhanced magnification, superior depth perception, and ergonomic benefits, enabling the surgeon to adjust the view for optimal positioning. It reduces arm extension, improving comfort during long surgeries. The exoscope has been particularly effective in pineal region tumor resections and hydrocephalus relief, particularly when dealing with posterior third ventricle involvement. The reduced ocular-corpus length and ergonomic design support greater surgical precision with minimal complications, making it a valuable tool in complex neurosurgical procedures.
