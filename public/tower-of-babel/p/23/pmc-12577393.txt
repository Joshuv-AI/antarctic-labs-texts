Sexual function and its association with psychosocial factors after penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts: a retrospective study using the oMtFSFI
Authors: Hanna Hagen, Cosima-Pauline Schmidbauer, Philip Klumpen, Kurt Angel, Stephan Madersbacher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPenile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty is the most commonly performed genital gender-affirming surgery for transfeminine individuals. Sexual function following vulvo-vaginoplasty has been primarily assessed using instruments developed and validated for cis women. This study aimed to evaluate postoperative sexual function outcomes using the only validated tool specifically designed for trans women. Additionally, potential associations with psychosocial factors were explored.MethodsData from trans women who underwent penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts at Klinik Landstraße Vienna were retrospectively analyzed. Sexual function was assessed using the Operated Male-to-Female Sexual Function Index (oMtFSFI), while psychosocial factors were explored through selected items from the Essener Transgender Lebensqualitäts-Inventar (ETLI). Peri- and postoperative complications were extracted from digital medical records and classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Spearman’s r was used to analyze correlations between sexual function and both clinical and psychosocial outcomes.ResultsThe study included 42 participants (Mage = 41 years). While general satisfaction was high, sexual function was suboptimal in several domains. Mild to moderate sexual dysfunction (M = 40.6, SD = 8.5) was reported, with the highest scores observed for sexual pain (M = 9.8, SD = 4.0). The observed complications were generally minor and managed conservatively. Significant correlations were found between sexual dysfunction and family acceptance (r = − .48, p ≤ .05), as well as between sexual dysfunction and the ability to openly express one's trans identity (r = − .57, p ≤ .01).ConclusionPenile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts is a generally safe procedure, with satisfactory aesthetic outcomes. However, postoperative sexual function remains suboptimal, particularly in terms of sexual pain and genital self-image. These findings suggest that psychosocial factors play a crucial role in postoperative sexual function. Future studies should investigate the impact of surgical techniques, postoperative care, and psychosocial support on sexual health outcomes following gender-affirming vulvo-vaginoplasty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01917-1.

Introduction

Gender-affirming surgery (GAS) plays a fundamental role in the medical care of trans and genderdiverse (TGD) individuals, significantly improving mental health and quality of life [1]. Penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty is the most commonly performed genital gender-affirming surgery among transfeminine individuals [2]. This technique typically involves orchiectomy, resection of the corpora cavernosa, and creation of a neovaginal canal between the rectum and bladder, lined with inverted penile shaft skin [3]. It is generally considered a relatively safe operation, with most postoperative complications being mild to moderate and manageable [4, 5]. For greater vaginal depth, scrotal skin grafts can be used to extend the neovaginal canal. While this approach may enhance the experience of penetrative intercourse, it also carries a greater risk of complications, such as injury to adjacent organs and prolonged operative times. Given the variability in surgical techniques and their potential impact on postoperative outcomes, further research is needed to better understand their effects on patient satisfaction, functional outcomes, and complication rates following genital GAS.

Sexual function, a key outcome of genital gender-affirming surgery and an essential component of overall well-being, has received relatively little attention in the literature [6]. While studies consistently show that GAS improves body image and increases sexual satisfaction, research on sexual function after vulvo-vaginoplasty is methodologically heterogeneous [7]. The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), initially designed for cis women, is the most commonly used tool for assessing sexual function, but its applicability to TGD individuals remains limited [6, 8]. This underscores the need for instruments specifically designed to capture the unique sexual experiences and needs of TGD individuals, as emphasized in the 2022 position paper by the European Society of Sexual Medicine [9]. Currently, the operated Male-to-Female Sexual Function Index (oMtFSFI) is the only tool specifically developed for evaluating sexual function in trans women after vulvo-vaginoplasty [10]. Although validation studies have shown a strong correlation between the oMtFSFI and FSFI, its use in current research remains limited [10].

Stigma and exclusion have been shown to negatively affect mental health and overall quality of life in TGD individuals [11, 12]. Research suggests that exposure to trans-related stigma is associated with increased levels of depression and anxiety, which may also influence overall well-being [11, 12]. This raises important questions about how these factors might influence postoperative outcomes in this population. However, much of the existing research on sexual function has focused predominantly on physiological outcomes, often neglecting the broader psychosocial context [9]. Factors such as societal acceptance, family support, and experiences of discrimination may play a pivotal role in shaping both postoperative satisfaction and sexual functionality, but remain underexplored in the current literature.

The aim of this study was to describe sexual function following penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without the use of scrotal skin grafts, employing the oMtFSFI, a validated tool specifically designed for trans women after genital GAS [10]. Additionally, this study examined peri- and postoperative complications and explored potential associations between sexual function and key psychosocial factors, such as societal and family acceptance, as well as experiences of discrimination. These will be assessed using selected items from the Essener Transidentität- Lebensqualitäts-Inventar (ETLI), a quality of life inventory specifically developed for trans individuals [13]. This exploratory approach aims to uncover new insights into the complex interplay between surgical outcomes and psychosocial factors in TGD individuals following genital GAS, contributing to a more holistic understanding of their postoperative sexual function and experiences.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cross-sectional questionnaire study. All participants were surveyed using a paper-pencil questionnaire. Clinical data were extracted from digital medical records. Since all participants were operated by the same surgeon, this study is considered a single-surgeon study.

Sample and study procedure

All individuals who underwent penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty at Klinik Landstraße Vienna between 2009 and 2020 (N = 200) were contacted by telephone and subsequently received the study questionnaire by mail. Current contact information was unavailable for 24% (48 persons) and 0.5% (one person) had already died. Participation in the study was voluntary and not remunerated. The selection criteria included being of legal age, providing written informed consent as approved by the institutional review board and returning the completed questionnaire by the deadline (July 2023). Ultimately, the study sample consisted of 42 participants. All collected data were used exclusively for study purposes and were managed and stored pseudonymously in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation.

Survey instruments

An English translation of the questionnaire used in this study is available in the supplementary materials. Sexual function was assessed using the operated Male-to-Female Sexual Function Index. [10] The oMtFSFI, originally developed and validated in Italian, is a questionnaire designed to assess sexual function in trans women following genital GAS. The questionnaire consists of eighteen questions answered on a four-point Likert scale (1 = almost always to always; 4 = almost never to never) and covers three domains of sexual dysfunction: sexual dissatisfaction (10 items), genital self-image (4 items) and sexual pain (4 items). Each domain is categorized into four levels: normal, mild to moderate, borderline critical and critical. In this study, a German translation of the oMtFSFI was used. An additional item was included in this study to assess overall satisfaction with the neogenitalia, using a four-point Likert scale (1 = very satisfied; 4 = dissatisfied).

The items evaluating psychosocial factors were derived from a validated German-language quality of life questionnaire specifically designed for trans individuals, the Essener Transidentität Lebensqualitäts-Inventar (ETLI). [13] Only selected items from the ETLI were used, focusing specifically on social acceptance and exclusion. The questions assessed: perceived ability to openly express one’s trans identity, feeling ostracized by society, frequency of experiencing insults in public spaces, and acceptance of one’s trans identity by family members. The questions were answered on a four-point Likert scale (ranging from never to always). In line with international standards, a pretest was conducted with 13 randomly selected respondents to check for the comprehensibility and acceptability of the questionnaire [14].

Peri- and postoperative complications

Digital medical records were used to evaluate the complication rates associated with penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty (impuls.kis and webokra software). The frequency of each complication was recorded and severity was graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system [15]. This system grades complications based on the type of intervention required for treatment: the more severe the complication, the higher the Clavien-Dindo grade (I-V).

Statistical analysis

The responses were tabulated in Microsoft Excel (Version 2021, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), and the dataset was exported to IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including mean values (M), standard deviations (SD), and ranges (minimum to maximum values), were calculated. Additionally, exploratory Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between relevant variables. As a non-parametric method, Spearman’s correlation is particularly suitable for skewed or non-normally distributed data. The strength of the correlation was interpreted according to common conventions, with a correlation of r <0.3 considered weak, 0.3 ≤ r <0.5 considered moderate, and r ≥0.5 considered strong. The significance level was set at p ≤.05.

The study sample consisted of 42 trans women with a mean age of 49 years at the time of the survey (SD = 14.5, range: 24–83). The average age at the time of the surgery was 41 years. Sexual function

The sexual function of the present sample was assessed using the three subscales and the total score of the oMtFSFI. In line with established conventions, a Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70 was indicative of sufficient reliability [16]. In this sample, the oMtFSFI generally demonstrated good reliability, except for the sexual pain subscale, which showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.68 (see Table 3).

Based on the sexual dysfunction scale (total score), the sample exhibited mild to moderate sexual dysfunction (M = 40.6, SD = 8.5). With regard to sexual dissatisfaction, the participants fell within the range of normal sexual dissatisfaction (M = 22.1, SD = 7.2). High scores were observed on the sexual pain subscale, with 36% of participants reporting critical pain during sexual activity. The mean score indicated mild to moderate sexual pain (M = 9.8, SD = 4.0). The mean genital self-image score was classified as mild to moderate (M = 7.7, SD = 3.6), with 10% of participants reporting a critically negative genital self-image. Forty-three percent of patients reported being very satisfied with the appearance of their neogenital, 33% were satisfied, 12% were moderately satisfied, and 12% were dissatisfied with the results. General satisfaction with neogenitalia was significantly correlated with all oMtFSFI subscales as well as the total score. Dilatation regimen

Only 26% of the participants reported continuing regular dilation. However, whether patients engaged in dilation was not significantly correlated with any of the oMtFSFI subscales.

Societal acceptance

A total of 12% of the participants reported that they were never able to openly express their trans identity, and 19% reported feeling either rarely or never accepted by their family. Furthermore, 45% felt marginalized by society at least some of the time. 38% had experienced insults or discrimination on the street due to their trans identity. The ability to openly express one’s trans identity was highly correlated with the oMtFSFI total score (r = −.57, p ≤.01), sexual dissatisfaction (r = −.52, p ≤.01), and genital self-image (r =.58, p ≤.01). Additionally, family acceptance was moderately correlated with the oMtFSFI total score (r = −.48, p ≤.05), sexual dissatisfaction (r = −.40, p ≤.05), and genital self-image (r = −.43, p ≤.05). The perception of societal ostracism was significantly correlated with the genital self-image subscale (r =.39, p ≤.05). No significant associations were found for insults in public.

Complication rates and graduation

The most frequently recorded peri- and postoperative complications after penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal grafts in our sample were the need for blood transfusions (9.5%) and meatal stenosis (9.5%). No cases of necrosis of the neoclitoris or labia, loss of sensitivity of the neoclitoris, injury to the vascular-nerve bundle, or injury to the bladder, ureters, or bowel were reported. All complications were classified as grade II or IIIb at most according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. An analysis of complication rates revealed that 59% of patients had no recorded complications, 29% experienced a single complication, and 12% had two complications. Notably, no patient experienced more than two complications. No significant correlation was found between the number of complications and the oMtFSFI total score or its subscales. Discussion

This study aimed to assess postoperative sexual function and explore potential psychosocial associations in individuals who underwent gender-affirming penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts. The results indicate that while satisfaction with the neogenitalia postoperatively is generally high, sexual function remains suboptimal in certain areas, particularly regarding sexual pain and genital self-image. Furthermore, societal acceptance, family support, and the ability to express one’s trans identity were found to be significantly correlated with different aspects of sexual dysfunction, suggesting that psychosocial factors play a critical role in shaping the postoperative experience of trans women.

A key factor contributing to the observed mild to moderate sexual dysfunction in this sample might be the high prevalence of sexual pain. In both our study and a comparable French study, trans women who underwent vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts reported moderate levels of sexual pain on the oMtFSFI [17]. This finding aligns with previous research from the developers of the oMtFSFI [10, 18]. Our results on sexual pain (M = 9.8) indicated even higher levels compared to the French study (M = 8.4) and the validation studies of the oMtFSFI (M = 8.2, see Table 3). While the latter describe their surgical approach as skin flap vaginoplasty, they do not provide specific technical details [10, 18]. Since insufficient neovaginal depth or width may contribute to pain during penetrative intercourse, the exclusive use of penile skin for neovaginal construction could partially explain the higher rates of sexual pain in our sample. Alternative techniques, such as incorporating scrotal skin grafts or using a spatulated urethra, might help mitigate these structural limitations and improve sexual function [19, 20]. This underscores the need for future studies to carefully evaluate surgical techniques in relation to postoperative sexual outcomes, ensuring that clinical decisions are guided by a comprehensive assessment of all relevant factors.

While the surgical approach likely plays an important role in influencing postoperative sexual outcomes, peri- and postoperative complications must also be considered potential influencing factors. In this study, the number of complications was not significantly associated with sexual function. Furthermore, we did not observe severe complications, such as necrosis or rectal injuries, which could have significantly impacted sexual function. Most recorded complications were minor and managed either conservatively or with minor surgical interventions, supporting the safety and reliability of the penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty technique used in our study [4]. This suggests that the observed impairments in sexual function are unlikely to be solely attributed to surgical complications. However, causal inferences cannot be drawn from correlation analyses alone and further research is needed to determine how different vaginoplasty techniques and their associated complications may impact sexual function outcomes.

Beyond surgical techniques, postoperative care practices, including dilation protocols, play a crucial role in maintaining neovaginal dimensions and potentially reducing the risk of sexual pain [21]. In our cohort, only a small proportion of participants adhered to a regular dilation regimen, which is essential for preventing neovaginal contraction and maintaining long-term vaginal depth and elasticity [22]. Establishing a well-structured and patient-centered dilation protocol could help improve sexual function outcomes. Furthermore, pelvic floor rehabilitation should be integrated into postoperative care, as muscle strengthening may aid in enhancing sexual function and overall recovery [23, 24]. Prospective studies should evaluate the benefits of both pre- and postoperative pelvic floor training, as well as other potential rehabilitative interventions, to enhance recovery outcomes and improve sexual function. Developing a standardized pre- and postoperative care protocol should be a key objective for the future.

Finally, emotional and relational factors, particularly the experience of transphobia and discriminatory behavior, might also impact sexual function. It has been found that TGD individuals often face disrespectful behavior from sexual partners, which is likely to negatively affect both their sexual well-being and sexual function [25]. Additionally, experiences of discrimination are known to adversely affect quality of life in trans individuals, which may, in turn, influence postoperative recovery outcomes as well. Results from the 2012 EU-LGBT-survey highlight the urgent need to reform in discriminatory legislation and tackle negative societal attitudes to improve life satisfaction among trans individuals [26]. However, our results indicate that trans women continue to experience discriminatory behavior and remarks related to their gender identity in everyday life. Furthermore, several psychosocial factors in our sample were linked to sexual dysfunction, highlighting their potential influence on shaping postoperative sexual function. Family support seemed to be negatively associated with sexual dysfunction, emphasizing the importance of a strong social support network in the postoperative period. Interestingly, a negative correlation was found between the genital self-image scale and the ability to openly express one’s trans identity. This may indicate that for individuals who are more open about their identity, external pressures such as social stigma and discrimination may adversely affect their genital self-image. These interpretations align with previous research demonstrating that greater openness regarding one’s trans identity is linked to higher levels of discrimination and lower overall life satisfaction [26].

This study underscores the need for comprehensive medical care that addresses not only the physical aspects of recovery but also the psychosocial well-being of trans women to enhance postoperative sexual function. Targeted psychosocial support, such as peer support groups or resilience training, could help trans women cope with experiences of discrimination or societal rejection [27]. Integrating psychosocial counseling into postsurgical care could provide a safe space for patients to openly discuss their concerns and challenges. However, addressing the structural factors that contribute to stress and discrimination is equally crucial. Given that TGD individuals often avoid healthcare services due to fears of violence and discrimination - leading to severe consequences - enhancing gender diversity training for medical staff could help build trust and reduce transphobic experiences in healthcare settings [28, 29]. This would not only promote physical recovery but also support mental well-being, ultimately improving the overall postoperative experience.

In order to advance medical support for trans women, it is essential to use reliable tools to assess postoperative outcomes. Based on established guidelines, the oMtFSFI has demonstrated good reliability in previous studies [10, 18]. Our analysis confirmed its overall reliability within this sample, suggesting that it provides stable and consistent measurements of sexual function in trans women following genital GAS. This supports its potential use in future research assessing sexual function in this population. However, its primary focus on heterosexual penetrative intercourse does not fully reflect the diversity of sexual experiences within the TGD community. Many trans women engage in a variety of sexual activities beyond traditional heterosexual norms, which may influence sexual satisfaction differently [30]. Future studies should explicitly consider the influence of sexual orientation, partner constellations, and diverse sexual practices when assessing sexual function, in order to provide a more inclusive and accurate understanding of trans individuals’ postoperative experiences [30].

Alongside the assessment of sexual function, it is equally important to examine potential complications arising from genital GAS. Our findings align with previous studies, suggesting that penile inversion vulvo-vaginoplasty without scrotal skin grafts is generally a safe procedure. Our complication rates are roughly consistent with those reported in the literature [4, 31]. However, comparing complication rates across studies is challenging because of the lack of standardized reporting methods. Implementing a uniform follow-up protocol after vulvo-vaginoplasty could enhance study comparability and reduce the risk of underreporting postoperative complications, as is currently being attempted by the GenderCOS project [32]. Furthermore, the applicability of the Clavien-Dindo classification system in GAS research warrants further critical evaluation. While certain complications of vulvo-vaginoplasty may not be life-threatening, they may not always be resolved pharmacologically or surgically, yet they can significantly impact quality of life, such as the loss of sensitivity or hypersensitivity of the neoclitoris.

Limitations and strengths

The relatively low response rate of 28% may introduce a selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the lack of a standardized follow-up protocol increases the potential for underreporting of complications. Another important limitation is the absence of preoperative assessments of sexual function, which are crucial for interpreting postoperative outcomes [33]. As many trans women experience genital dysphoria prior to surgery, the process of restoring sexual function after genital GAS can often be complex [7, 30, 33]. Moreover, factors such as hormone levels and hormone supplementation can significantly influence sexual function in both cis and trans individuals [7]. Although many trans women report a decrease in sexual desire after starting hormone therapy, this does not necessarily correspond to reduced sexual satisfaction but should be considered a mediating factor affecting postoperative sexual function [33]. The study primarily focused on heterosexual penetrative intercourse, which may not fully capture the diversity of sexual experiences among TGD individuals [30]. Future research should therefore include broader assessments of sexual activity and satisfaction to better reflect this diversity. Furthermore, psychosocial factors were measured using selected items rather than comprehensive scales, which limits data depth and may underestimate their impact on postoperative outcomes. Finally, sexual function was assessed using a formally translated German version of the oMtFSFI, which has not yet been validated. Nevertheless, preliminary reliability analyses indicate good internal consistency, and a formal validation study of the German adaptation is currently underway.

Despite these limitations, the study‘s strengths include the use of a validated instrument specifically designed to assess sexual function in transfeminine individuals following genital GAS. Moreover, this is among the first studies to explore the relationship between sexual function after vulvo-vaginoplasty and psychosocial factors, providing valuable insights into the significant role these factors play in postoperative outcomes and offering potential guidance for future interventions aimed at improving well-being after surgery. Another strength lies in the equitable access to surgery ensured by Austria’s publicly funded healthcare system, which helped minimize potential socioeconomic bias in the sample. Overall, this study contributes important knowledge on how psychosocial factors may influence outcomes following vulvo-vaginoplasty.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the need for continuous advancements in surgical techniques and postoperative care to address persistent challenges in sexual function following gender-affirming vulvo-vaginoplasty. Additionally, our study underscores the ongoing difficulty in standardizing outcome measurements for gender-affirming treatments, emphasizing the importance of prospective studies that utilize validated and inclusive metrics. Future research should focus on refining surgical approaches and integrating psychosocial support into postoperative care to enhance both sexual and emotional well-being. A comprehensive participatory approach, incorporating input from the TGD community, is essential for developing effective assessment tools that accurately capture sexual function and satisfaction, ensuring research aligns with the lived experiences of trans individuals.
