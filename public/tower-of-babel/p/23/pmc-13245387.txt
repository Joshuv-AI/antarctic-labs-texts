Epidemiological characterisation of dengue fever across Saudi Arabia
Authors: Reem A Bin Suaydan, Waleed S Al Salem, Eitezaz A Zaki, Zenaida S Stead, Lama Alrasheed, Roua A Alsubki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue fever is an emerging public health threat in Saudi Arabia. To guide evidence-based vector control, this study aimed to characterise the epidemiological and clinical patterns of infections across key regions in the Kingdom between 2022 and 2024.MethodsA total of 2850 serum samples from suspected dengue patients were collected from hospitals in seven regions in Saudi Arabia. RNA extraction and dengue virus detection were conducted using standardised reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) protocols. Statistical analysis was applied to identify the sociodemographic predictors of PCR-confirmed dengue.ResultsAmong the 2850 individuals tested, 1447 (50.8%) were PCR positive. Most cases (n=1269) occurred in 2023, with Makkah alone recording 801 cases. Males represented 73.5% of PCR-positive cases, while non-Saudi nationals accounted for 47.7%. Multivariable regression showed significantly lower odds of positivity among females (odds ratio [OR] 0.65 [95% confidence interval {CI} 0.55 to 0.76], p<0.001) and higher odds among non-Saudi nationals (OR 1.17 [95% CI 1.00 to 1.38], p=0.050). Despite the relatively low caseloads, Jazan and Asir had higher adjusted odds compared with Madinah.ConclusionsDengue transmission shows strong geographic, demographic and seasonal variation, with evidence of epidemic activity in 2023. These findings underscore the need for targeted vector control and enhanced surveillance in high-risk regions.

Introduction

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral infection caused by four closely related serotypes of the dengue virus (DENV-1–DENV-4), primarily transmitted by the Aedes aegypti mosquito. The World Health Organization (WHO) recognises the disease as one of the main global health threats due to its increasing incidence and expanding geographical distribution.1 The global burden has increased by >400% between 2000 (505 430 cases) and 2013 (>2.4 million), faster than any other communicable disease.2–5 In 2024, dengue infections reached a historic high of 14.6 million cases, with >12 000 dengue-related deaths.1 This surge is attributed to rapid urbanisation, climate change, global travel and the adaptive expansion of Aedes vectors into new territories.2 Dengue not only poses a significant clinical burden but also imposes substantial socio-economic costs through healthcare expenditures, lost productivity and strain on health systems.3 Thus, effective surveillance and control strategies are essential to mitigate outbreaks and reduce disease transmission.

Although historically underreported compared with other regions such as Southeast Asia and Latin America, dengue has become a critical health concern across the Middle East, the Arabian Peninsula and the greater Red Sea region. The last two decades have witnessed a notable increase in localised outbreaks, particularly in coastal and urbanised zones. The Red Sea State of Sudan and Egypt’s Red Sea coast have experienced repeated dengue outbreaks, resulting in significant disease exportation to the Middle East and the Arabian Peninsula.6,7 Besides the regional transmission, other factors contribute to the increasing dengue incidence in the Middle East, including increasing population density, inadequate water storage practices and climatic conditions characterised by high temperatures and seasonal humidity. These factors collectively create a favourable environment for A. aegypti proliferation and DENV transmission.

Saudi Arabia has experienced a steady increase in dengue incidence since the first major outbreak was documented in Jeddah in 1993.8 Between 1995 and 2003, annual cases ranged from 0 to 36.9 The numbers then rose sharply, with 291 cases in 2004, 305 in 2005 and 1308 in 2006, showing a clear upward trend.9 More than 4000 cases were recorded in the first half of 2023 alone, highlighting the worsening disease burden.8 The western coastal belt has historically been most vulnerable due to its climate, population density and urban development (Figure 1). Previous studies have reported the presence of all DENV serotypes across major regions, including Jazan, Aseer, Madinah, Makkah and Jeddah.10,11 However, the dominant serotypes are DENV-1, DENV-2 and DENV-3.10,11

The high burden of dengue in Saudi Arabia has been linked to both demographic and environmental factors, particularly climate and population density. Seasonal climatic patterns, notably a primary wet season during spring (March–May) and a secondary rainfall period in late autumn (November–December), are believed to facilitate the proliferation of A. aegypti vectors.12 Furthermore, approximately one-third of the Saudi population consists of foreign workers and the country receives more than eight million visiting Hajj and Umrah pilgrims annually.12 These patterns of large-scale human movement contribute to increased population mobility across different parts of the country, which may increase the risk of dengue virus importation and facilitate its wider geographic spread. Pilgrimage sites, such as Makkah, also exhibit climatic conditions favourable to the introduction and sustained transmission of DENV. In addition, mass gatherings during Hajj and Umrah facilitate the co-circulation of multiple DENV serotypes and disease transmission, thus increasing the risk of new infections.9

Despite the increasing burden of dengue in Saudi Arabia, systematic data comparing clinical and epidemiological characteristics across regions and different time periods remain limited. Existing regional studies often focus on single outbreaks or narrow populations, limiting their generalisability. Studies by Alyahya8 and Hashem et al.12 were limited to single-city analyses based solely on data from Jeddah, restricting their generalisability across regions. Molecular investigations by Dafalla et al.10 and Bello et al.11 characterised viral lineages but do not include demographic or temporal analyses. Likewise, modelling studies by Altassan et al.9 relied on aggregated surveillance data without individual-level polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmation. Together these limitations highlight the need for multisite, multiyear, PCR-confirmed datasets that can identify the most affected demographic groups, assess regional disparities and characterise seasonality and outbreak patterns with greater accuracy.

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by providing a multiyear, multiregional epidemiological characterisation of dengue fever in Saudi Arabia based on PCR-confirmed cases. Specifically, it seeks to describe the sociodemographic profile of individuals testing positive for dengue, examine the regional and temporal distribution of confirmed dengue cases and identify independent predictors of dengue infection using multivariable analysis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study based on hospital records of patients evaluated for suspected dengue fever. Data were collected from seven Saudi regions with endemic dengue transmission—Makkah, Madinah, Jazan, Najran, Asir, Bahah and Riyadh—selected in consultation with the Ministry of Health to ensure coverage of high-burden areas. Hospitals were chosen purposively and included large tertiary centres and private hospitals that routinely perform diagnostic testing for dengue and other viral haemorrhagic fevers. These institutions serve as regional reference centres with established laboratory and reporting systems, ensuring reliable and complete case documentation.

Within each hospital, all patients presenting to outpatient or inpatient departments between January 2022 and March 2024 with clinical features consistent with suspected dengue infection were eligible. For patients with multiple dengue PCR tests during the study period, only the first test was considered, treating each patient as a single cross-sectional observation within the multiyear dataset. The sampling frame comprised all febrile patients tested for DENV by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) during this period, with no restrictions on age, nationality or socio-economic status.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The clinical definition of suspected dengue followed the WHO 2009 case classification (updated in 2023). A suspected case was defined as any patient presenting with acute febrile illness (axillary temperature ≥38°C) and at least two of the following clinical features: headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, haemorrhagic manifestations and leucopenia.13 Supportive laboratory findings, such as leucopenia (<4×109/l) or thrombocytopenia (<100×109/l), strengthened the clinical suspicion but were not required for inclusion. Eligible participants included Saudi nationals, expatriate residents and international travellers who presented to participating hospitals between January 2022 and March 2024 and whose attending clinicians requested dengue RT-PCR testing. Travel history during the 14-d incubation window was reviewed from medical records to verify exposure within dengue-endemic areas of the Kingdom.

Exclusion criteria included patients with confirmed alternative diagnoses (malaria, influenza or typhoid) and duplicate laboratory records. Notably, no gender-based factors, such as pregnancy, were used for exclusion since the study aimed to report unbiased gender differences in dengue infections. No other notable groups were excluded from the study.

Sample collection and data sources

A total of 2850 venous blood samples were collected between 2022 and 2024 from suspected cases by trained phlebotomists at participating hospitals (Supplementary Table 1). A consecutive sampling approach was used. All individuals presenting to participating hospitals with symptoms compatible with dengue fever and meeting the inclusion criteria were approached for PCR testing at the time of clinical evaluation. Samples were obtained from 119 hospitals across seven regions of Saudi Arabia. These facilities included major tertiary-care centres, secondary public hospitals, Ministry of Health general hospitals and private medical centres. The distribution comprised approximately 24 tertiary hospitals, 67 general hospitals and 28 private institutions. Serum or plasma was separated and aliquoted into sterile microtubes. A standardised case report form (CRF) was utilised across all sites to extract demographic and clinical information. The dataset included variables such as age (continuous, in years), gender (male, female), nationality (Saudi, non-Saudi), region of presentation, year and month of sample collection. Mortality status was also recorded. Laboratory technicians and data abstractors at each site received a brief orientation on CRF completion and specimen handling, including instructions on the timing of blood draws relative to symptom onset and standard labelling procedures. Samples were transported in refrigerated containers (4°C) to the Jeddah Regional Laboratory, the central reference facility for dengue diagnostics in Jeddah. Upon arrival, samples were stored at −70°C until processing. All data were de-identified before analysis to maintain confidentiality.

Viral RNA extraction

The Viral RNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Munpyeongseo-ro, Daejeon, Korea) was used for RNA extraction, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The ExiPrep Plus Viral DNA/RNA Kit (Bioneer) and the extraction platform (Bioneer) were used to automate the extraction process. Patient samples were lysed with a chaotropic binding buffer to promote the adherence of viral RNA to magnetic beads. After placement on a magnetic stand, the supernatant and debris were discarded and the beads were washed multiple times to eliminate residual proteins, inhibitors and contaminants. The purified RNA was eluted using RNase-free buffer into sterile microtubes for immediate downstream analysis.

DENV detection using RT-PCR

RT-PCR was performed on all the samples using the RealStar Dengue RT-PCR Kit 2.0 (Altona Diagnostics, Hamburg, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reactions were performed on a LightCycler 480 II thermal cycler (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Each reaction well contained 20 µl of RT-PCR master mix and 10 µl of extracted RNA or controls. FAM-labelled fluorescent probes were used to detect dengue virus RNA while JOE-labelled probes provided internal controls. According to the manufacturer’s interpretation criteria, a sample was considered positive when a characteristic amplification curve was observed in the FAM channel. Although the kit does not prescribe a fixed cycle threshold (Ct) value, the laboratory classified reactions with a Ct <40 as positive and those with a Ct ≥40 or without an amplification curve as negative, consistent with standard practice and the analytical sensitivity reported by Altona Diagnostics. Notably, the RealStar Dengue RT-PCR Kit 2.0 qualitatively detects all four dengue virus serotypes (DENV-1–DENV-4) but does not differentiate between them.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were compiled, cleaned and de-identified using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Data quality was checked through double-entry verification, range and logic checks and reconciliation of inconsistencies against the original laboratory logs. Implausible and duplicate records were flagged and corrected prior to analysis. The cleaned dataset was evaluated before modelling. Missing data were minimal: one case lacked age (0.04%) and two cases had unspecified gender (0.07%), while nationality, region and PCR status were complete after standardisation. As missingness was negligible and appeared random, no imputation was performed.

Two master datasets, comprising all relevant variables, were developed: one including all PCR-positive and negative cases and the other including only PCR-positive cases. The data were extracted for analysis using SPSS version 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise demographic variables (age, gender, nationality and region) and their relationship with PCR-confirmed dengue cases. χ2 tests were used to assess the associations between PCR positivity and categorical variables, including gender, nationality, region and month of sample collection. Additional χ2 tests were conducted for subgroup analysis to explore differences within year and region combinations. The possibility of inflated type I error was recognised due to the use of multiple χ2 tests to compare PCR-positive and PCR-negative groups across several categorical variables. Given the exploratory nature of these subgroup comparisons, p-values were interpreted cautiously and were not formally adjusted for multiple testing.

For multivariable analysis, a binary logistic regression model was fitted to identify the independent sociodemographic predictors of PCR-confirmed dengue. Age was assumed to be continuous in the regression model. Examining the regression coefficients and residual patterns did not suggest any violation of the linearity-in-the-logit assumption, and age demonstrated a stable, non-significant effect (p=0.885). Thus it was retained as a continuous variable since categorisation was unlikely to improve model performance or interpretability. Nationality was coded as a binary variable (Saudi vs non-Saudi) to ensure cell stability, whereas detailed nationality categories were used only for descriptive analyses presented in the supplementary tables.

The main variables for the logistic regression were selected in advance based on their established relevance in previous epidemiological studies and their completeness in the dataset. Clinical and environmental factors such as presenting symptoms, travel history and rainfall were not consistently available across hospitals. Thus they were excluded to preserve model stability and avoid excessive case-wise deletion. Cases with incomplete or unstable categorical combinations that caused perfect separation were also excluded to ensure model convergence, yielding 1649 complete observations for regression. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and condition indices in SPSS. All predictors demonstrated VIF values <2.0, and the highest condition index was 5.6, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables. Correlation checks between categorical predictors also confirmed that no categories exhibited perfect overlap. These results support the independence and stability of the regression model.

Model diagnostics and goodness-of-fit were assessed to evaluate the stability and reliability of the logistic regression model. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test revealed a significant difference between the observed and predicted outcomes (χ2(8)=47.10, p<0.001), indicating suboptimal calibration. The −2 log likelihood value was 3703.94 and the Nagelkerke R2 was 0.054, reflecting modest explanatory power consistent with epidemiological datasets. Multicollinearity was not a concern, as all VIFs were <2.0 and the highest condition index was 5.6. However, SPSS presented convergence warnings. This was attributed to low counts in certain regional categories rather than model instability. Potential sparse-data effects were investigated to ensure model robustness. Small cell counts were identified, primarily in the Bahah region, where limited dengue cases resulted in quasi-separation for specific gender subgroups. Sensitivity checks were performed by combining low-frequency regions into broader geographic categories. The resulting coefficients and significance patterns remained unchanged. Because the model coefficients were stable and the overall direction of effects was consistent, alternative penalised and exact logistic regression was deemed unnecessary. Therefore, the final model was retained for interpretability and comparability with prior Saudi epidemiological studies.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health, Saudi Arabia (reference no. A01650). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to the collection of samples. All data were de-identified before analysis to ensure participant confidentiality.

Results

Sociodemographic data

Of the 2850 suspected dengue cases analysed, 1447 (50.8%) tested PCR positive and 1401 (49.2%) tested PCR negative. The mean age was 37.5±15.7 y for the PCR-positive patients and 37.3±21.3 y for the PCR-negative patients. This difference was not statistically significant (p=0.843; Table 1). Age distributions were further summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) to account for skewness. Among PCR-positive individuals, the median age was 36 y (IQR 27–46), compared with 35 y (IQR 23–52) in PCR-negative patients. Although the mean ages were similar (37.5 vs 37.3 y), the larger standard deviation (SD) in the PCR-negative group (21.3 vs 15.7 y) indicated greater variability and mild right skewness. Therefore, a Mann–Whitney U test was conducted in addition to the t-test and showed no statistically significant difference in age distributions between the two groups (p=0.812). These findings confirm that age was not a meaningful discriminator of PCR positivity in this dataset.

Males accounted for 1064 (73.5%) of PCR-positive cases compared with 907 (64.8%) in the PCR-negative group. This difference was statistically significant (χ2=37.4, p<0.001), suggesting a higher proportion of males among confirmed cases. However, the proportion of males did not significantly vary across years (χ2=1.594, df=2, p=0.451; Supplementary Table 2), indicating a stable gender distribution over time.

Nationality was also associated with PCR status (Figure 2). Saudis represented 756 (52.3%) of PCR-positive individuals, while non-Saudi patients collectively accounted for 691 (47.7%). Among non-Saudis, the most affected nationalities were Egyptians (n=141 [10.0% of PCR positives]), Yemenis (n=124 [8.8%]), Bangladeshis (n=66 [4.7%]) and Pakistanis (n=77 [5.5%]) (Supplementary Table 3). This distribution differed significantly from that of the PCR-negative group, in which Saudis comprised 62.8% (n=804) and non-Saudis 37.2% (n=476) (Supplementary Table 3). The observed differences were statistically significant (χ2=106.1, df=9, p<0.001). However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as denominator data on the total number of individuals from each nationality in the source population were not available. Thus these figures reflect proportions among tested individuals rather than the population-level risk.

The variation across years was statistically significant (p<0.001), although differences in sample collection volume by year should be considered when interpreting these patterns.

Geographic distribution of dengue in Saudi Arabia

The geographic distribution showed considerable variation across regions and years (Figure 3, Table 2). Makkah accounted for the largest share of PCR-positive cases at 849 (58.6%), followed by Najran at 290 (20.1%) and Madinah at 257 (17.8%). In contrast, the reported PCR-positive cases were 48 (3.3%) for Jazan, 75 (5.2%) for Riyadh and 51 (3.5%) for Asir. The differences in regional distribution between PCR-positive and PCR-negative groups were statistically significant (χ2=98.12, p<0.001).

Few cases were confirmed in 2022, with Jazan reporting 29 PCR-positive cases, followed by Makkah (n=10) and Najran (n=2). No positive cases were recorded in Madinah, Asir, Bahah and Riyadh during that year. The year 2023 represented an epidemic,8 with the highest number of PCR-confirmed cases reported in Makkah (n=801), followed by Najran (n=290), Madinah (n=254), Riyadh (n=75) and Asir (n=44). Smaller clusters were observed in Jazan (n=19) and Bahah (n=4). The case numbers sharply declined in 2024. Only Madinah (n=15) and Makkah (n=38) recorded new PCR-positive cases, with all other regions reporting zero confirmed cases.

Several regions recorded zero PCR-positive cases in certain years. Rather than indicating an absence of dengue transmission, these zeros reflect the operational timeline of the study. Sample collection commenced only after formal approval of the research protocol by the Research Ethics Committee at the end of 2022 and ceased in early 2024. Participating hospitals were not authorised to provide blood samples for this research before approval. Consequently, the absence of cases in some regions during 2022 and 2024 is attributable to the absence of sampling rather than the absence of infection.

Temporal trends of dengue

The temporal analysis of dengue incidence revealed a pronounced surge in PCR-confirmed cases during mid-2023 (Figure 4). Most of the cases were recorded between March and August, with peaks in June (n=369) and July (n=305). These two months alone accounted for 45% of all PCR-positive cases that year, followed by March (n=135) and April (n=114). Fewer cases were reported in the latter part of the year, with August (n=137), September (n=121) and October–December showing a continued decline. The χ2 analysis confirmed statistically significant differences in monthly case distribution (χ2=229.7, df=11, p<0.0001; Supplementary Table 4), indicating non-random temporal clustering of cases. However, the χ2 result may have reflected the contrast between the 2023 epidemic peak and the near-zero case counts in the partial years of 2022 and 2024 rather than true month-to-month variability across all three years. Thus a supplementary χ2 test was conducted, restricted to 2023 alone. The distribution of cases remained highly heterogeneous (χ2=158.51, df=11, p<0.0001). This confirms that the observed seasonality reflects genuine temporal fluctuations.

Temporal trends of PCR-confirmed dengue cases in Saudi Arabia during the 2023 epidemic year.

Conversely, 2022 (n=41) and 2024 (n=53) showed very low PCR-positive case counts. In 2024, a spike was observed in January (n=45) and February (n=8), with no PCR-positive cases detected in subsequent months (Supplementary Table 4). These patterns largely reflect the study’s operational period. Sampling in 2022 began only after ethical approval was obtained late in the year, while sample collection in 2024 ceased in March. Therefore, case numbers in those years reflect sampling availability rather than true incidence. The 2023 data represent the only full year of surveillance.

Factors predicting PCR positivity

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify demographic and geographic predictors of PCR-confirmed dengue among suspected cases (Table 3). The model included age, gender, nationality and region. The reference categories were male for gender, Saudi for nationality and Madinah for region. Notably, Madinah was selected because of its consistent case volume and epidemiological importance, allowing stable comparisons across regions with very low or very high positivity rates. A total of 1649 complete cases were included in the analysis after excluding missing and ambiguous data.

After adjusting for other variables, females had significantly lower odds of testing PCR positive compared with males (adjusted OR [aOR] 0.65 [95% confidence interval {CI} 0.55 to 0.76], p<0.001). This finding is consistent with the descriptive analyses presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2.

Substantial regional variation was observed after adjustment. Compared with Madinah, residents of Asir (aOR 1.79 [95% CI 1.05 to 3.04], p=0.032) and Jazan (aOR 2.11 [95% CI 1.13 to 3.94], p=0.020) had significantly higher odds of PCR-confirmed dengue, suggesting localised outbreaks or underdiagnosed transmission in these areas. In contrast, odds were significantly lower in regions such as Makkah (aOR 0.48 [95% CI 0.39 to 0.60], p=0.001) and Bahah (aOR 0.53 [95% CI 0.41 to 0.70], p=0.001), despite these areas reporting high absolute case counts. Furthermore, compared with Saudi nationals, individuals of several non-Saudi nationalities, including Egyptian, Yemeni, Pakistani and Bangladeshi, had higher odds of PCR-confirmed dengue (aOR 1.17 [95% CI 1.00 to 1.38], p=0.050).

Age was not a statistically significant predictor of PCR positivity in the adjusted model (aOR 1.00 [95% CI 0.99 to 1.01], p=0.885) and some coefficients displayed unstable estimates with wide CIs. This outcome suggests that, within the hospital-tested population, age did not significantly influence the probability of dengue diagnosis.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study identified distinct sociodemographic, geographic and temporal patterns in PCR-confirmed dengue cases across seven high-burden regions in Saudi Arabia between 2022 and 2024. Most cases were concentrated in 2023, particularly in the summer months. Males and non-Saudi nationals had significantly higher odds of PCR-confirmed infection. Regional clustering was seen, especially in Makkah, Najran and Madinah, although multivariable analysis revealed lower adjusted odds in Makkah and Madinah compared with other regions. Age was not a statistically significant predictor of PCR positivity.

Gender disparities

A consistently higher proportion of PCR-confirmed cases were male (73.5%). Females had 35% lower odds of PCR positivity compared with males (aOR 0.65 [95% CI 0.55 to 0.76]), a pattern consistent with earlier research from Saudi Arabia and other endemic countries.14–17 Potential explanations include occupational exposure, mobility patterns and healthcare access differences between genders. Studies by Asebe et al.14 suggest that men may be more exposed to Aedes mosquitoes due to outdoor work and public commuting. Specifically, outdoor workers and commuters are active during peak Aedes biting hours (early morning/late afternoon), which may create temporal overlap with peak vector activity. Outdoor work areas, such as construction sites, roadsides, agricultural fields and transit hubs, also frequently contain water containers that collect stagnant water. These containers create potential breeding grounds for Aedes mosquitoes. Quadir et al.15 similarly noted that rural residency is more common among males, potentially increasing their exposure risk. However, other studies have found a higher prevalence of severe dengue in females,18 highlighting the influence of sociocultural and temporal factors. It is also important to consider that the higher proportion of males among the confirmed cases in the present study may partly reflect differences in testing patterns. Specifically, males might have been more likely to present to healthcare facilities or be sampled for PCR testing.

Nationality and social vulnerability

Saudi citizens represented 52.3% of PCR-positive cases, while non-Saudi nationals represented 47.7%. Individuals from Egypt (10.0%), Yemen (8.8%) and Pakistan (5.5%) were particularly affected. Non-Saudi nationals had slightly higher odds of PCR positivity compared with Saudis (aOR 1.17 [95% CI 1.00 to 1.38]). Although the association reached borderline statistical significance (p=0.050), this suggests a modest elevation in dengue positivity among non-Saudis within the hospital-tested population. Migrant workers comprise a significant proportion of the Saudi population and may reside in high-density housing with limited access to preventive healthcare. Several studies suggest that these conditions may increase exposure to dengue vectors and delay diagnosis.19–23 Moreover, many migrants originate from dengue-endemic regions, raising the possibility of secondary strain importation.19 However, the present findings do not establish causality. Nationality here reflects associations observed among hospital-tested patients, not population-level risk. Furthermore, the country of origin does not imply the source of infection.

Geographic and environmental patterns

Geographically, the dengue cases were concentrated in Makkah (52.9%), Madinah (17.8%) and Najran (20.1%), aligning with previous surveillance data that confirms these regions as consistent hotspots, especially due to pilgrimage patterns.19,24–27 Makkah and Madinah were disproportionally affected in 2023, accounting for 801 and 254 PCR-positive cases, respectively. These cities are home to holy mosques that receive millions of visitors annually for Hajj and Umrah pilgrimages. Hajj is held yearly and attracts about two million Muslim visitors, while Umrah adds up to six million more annual visitors.19 Most of these visitors are from dengue-endemic regions across the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia and eastern Africa, which may result in continuous strain importation.19 Notably, the 2023 Hajj was held between 26 June and 1 July, coinciding with the largest spike in PCR-positive cases (369 in June and 305 in July). This notwithstanding, it is worth noting that the high number of cases in Makkah and Madinah may be partially attributable to the higher testing volume in that region.

However, residents of Jazan had more than twice the odds of PCR-confirmed dengue compared with Madinah (aOR 2.11 [95% CI 1.13 to 3.94]). Makkah showed significantly lower odds (aOR 0.48 [95% CI 0.39 to 0.60]) despite reporting the largest number of PCR-positive cases. This contrast likely reflects differences in testing volume rather than true reductions in transmission risk. Makkah is a major referral hub and may receive a large number of febrile patients during routine seasons and mass gatherings. The large testing volume may have diluted the positivity rates. This notwithstanding, the significant ORs for Jazan in the regression analysis suggest that additional factors may contribute to the local dengue risk, such as weather and topography. Jazan is relatively flat and located at sea level, making it susceptible to frequent rainfall-induced flooding, especially during seasonal storms.19 These conditions can facilitate water stagnation in urban and peri-urban areas, creating potential breeding sites for A. aegypti mosquitoes. Previous studies have demonstrated that such geomorphological features are strongly associated with vector proliferation and persistent transmission cycles in low-lying coastal zones.19 Moreover, the limited drainage infrastructure in Jazan likely contributes to the accumulation of standing water following heavy rainfall, thereby enhancing ideal mosquito breeding habitats and increasing the potential for local disease transmission.

Temporal trends

The increase in cases observed in mid-2023, especially in June and July, coincided with periods of seasonal rainfall and mass gatherings, suggesting that both environmental and social factors may have contributed to increased disease transmission. For example, neighbourhoods in Makkah recorded up to 45 mm of rain within 24 h on multiple occasions between July and August 2023.28 This exceeded the typical dry-season monthly average of <10 mm, representing uncharacteristic weather events for the region. However, no time-series data, case-cluster mapping or entomological surveillance were available in this study to confirm a causal link between rainfall and the mid-year spike in cases. Heavy rainfall may have created temporary stagnant water pools, facilitating Aedes breeding in both public and residential areas. Rainfall coincided with the Hajj pilgrimage (June 26–July 1), which brought nearly two million visitors to Makkah. This temporal overlap may have increased opportunities for transmission, but no causal link can be established with the available data. Earlier spikes in March and April also aligned with the Ramadan period (March 23–April 21), further suggesting that dengue infections may be related to social factors. Specifically, the behavioural changes during Ramadan, including reduced working hours, visits to friends and neighbours and activity at different hours related to breaking the fast, may have contributed to dengue transmission. The Ramadan period also coincided with high rainfall, which may have contributed to Aedes proliferation.29

This simultaneous occurrence may have amplified exposure and viral circulation, but the association must be interpreted with caution. Without accounting for variables such as population density, sanitation infrastructure and the timing of vector control campaigns, the surge in cases cannot be attributed solely to climatic and social factors. Historical evidence suggests a relationship between unusual weather and increased dengue activity.30–32,12 Melebari et al.30 documented a notable increase in dengue incidence in Makkah during 2019, attributed to heavier-than-normal rainfall that year, while 2024 saw a dramatic decline in reported cases. This was largely due to the cessation of sample collection in March rather than a definitive reduction in transmission. A brief spike observed in January 2024 may be associated with atypical winter rainfall or delays in vector control, although no environmental data were available to verify this observation.

Non-significant findings

Age was found to be a non-significant predictor of PCR-confirmed dengue (p=0.885), a finding that contrasts with previous studies identifying a higher risk in younger adults ages 15–30 y.19 The lack of association may be due to sample homogeneity, selection bias (hospital-based testing) or the exclusion of mild and asymptomatic cases, which are more common in younger individuals. Sparse data in age categories led to less reliable regression estimates. For instance, relatively few patients were <15 y or >70 y of age, resulting in wide CIs and unstable OR estimates for these groups.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the research was based on retrospective, hospital-derived data, which likely captures only a subset of all dengue infections and underrepresents asymptomatic or community-level cases. As a result, the true burden of dengue in the wider population is likely higher than reflected here, underscoring the need for additional research with broader and more inclusive sampling. Moreover, the study population was primarily drawn from tertiary and private hospitals that routinely conduct PCR testing, which may not fully represent patients attending primary-care facilities or those who do not seek care. This may introduce severity-related and access-related selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings to the broader community.

Second, vector density, rainfall volume and other ecological variables were not monitored and could not be included, limiting the interpretation of environmental drivers. RT-PCR testing was used only for qualitative detection of DENV, without serotyping or genomic characterisation. The absence of serotype or strain identification restricted interpretation of transmission patterns, potential viral introductions and the epidemiological basis of the 2023 outbreak.

Third, the regression model encountered convergence warnings in some strata due to sparse data. Additionally, population denominators were unavailable, meaning the positivity rates and odds reflect only those tested, not the general population. PCR-based misclassification was also possible. While the RealStar Dengue RT-PCR assay has high analytical sensitivity, false negatives may occur due to low viral loads, delayed sample collection or sample degradation, potentially leading to underestimation of true infections. Conversely, false positives could have resulted in overestimation.

Fourth, interactions between region, year and other contextual factors were not explored in this model. Finally, the use of multiple χ2 tests across subgroups may have introduced a risk of type I error. Thus the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Public health implications

Existing public health efforts in Saudi Arabia focus on routine vector control activities, hospital-based case reporting and seasonal health advisories in high-risk regions. The presented findings highlight the need for more targeted surveillance and response strategies, particularly in regions with a higher risk of dengue and during the months before the rainy season and mass gatherings. Vector control efforts should be intensified in areas like Jazan and Asir, where transmission appears locally concentrated. Potential interventions include larval source management in flood-prone districts,33 routine household-level inspections for standing water34 and prioritised deployment of ultra-low-volume fogging in areas with documented clustering of cases.35 These regions would also benefit from seasonal pre-rainfall clean-up campaigns to eliminate containers that accumulate water during monsoon periods.

Several critical components are not yet fully established. Most notably, testing strategies are presently restricted to hospital-based surveillance of symptomatic patients. These strategies should be expanded to cover seasonal peaks and include migrant populations, who may be relatively more susceptible due to housing and occupational conditions. Integrating climatic data, vector indices and mobile population surveillance would also significantly improve epidemic forecasting and response. Introducing such systems would enhance epidemic forecasting and provide an early warning framework to support timely vector control and public health response.

Future research

Future studies should prioritise community-based surveillance to capture subclinical and asymptomatic infections, especially in high-risk zones. Entomological assessments and geospatial mapping of vector density are also needed to understand the ecological basis of dengue transmission. Finally, genomic surveillance of circulating dengue virus strains is required to model serotype dynamics, immune interactions and outbreak potential in Saudi Arabia.

Conclusions

This study offers a comprehensive epidemiological characterisation of dengue fever in Saudi Arabia from 2022 to 2024. The findings highlight pronounced spatiotemporal variation in incidence, with a mid-year surge in 2023 and geographic clustering in Makkah, Madinah and Najran. Demographic analysis showed higher odds of infection among males and non-Saudi nationals, while age was not a statistically significant predictor. Multivariable regression further revealed elevated adjusted odds in regions such as Jazan and Asir, suggesting the presence of localised transmission drivers.

These findings underscore the need for geographically targeted and seasonally timed vector control interventions. Public health authorities should prioritise enhanced surveillance during peak months, particularly between March and August, and in regions with consistently high positivity rates. Integrating climate monitoring, entomological data and mass gathering surveillance into routine dengue response systems would significantly improve early warning and outbreak preparedness. Given the disproportionate representation of non-Saudi nationals among PCR-positive cases, migrant-inclusive public health strategies are also warranted. These interventions could include tailored risk communication, expanded access to testing and proactive vector control in high-density residential and labour housing environments.
