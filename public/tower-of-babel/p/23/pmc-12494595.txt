Introduction

Acquired cystic kidney disease (ACKD) affects around 40–45 % of subjects with chronic hemodialysis [1]. The longer the patient is on hemodialysis, the more he will be at risk of an acquired cystic disease [2]. Renal tumors can complicate ACKD with increased frequency [3,4]. Acquired cystic disease-associated renal cell carcinoma (ACD-RCC) is a common subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) occurring exclusively in patients with ACKD in the setting of end-stage renal disease [5]. ACD-RCC was first introduced as a distinct entity in the 2016 WHO classification of renal tumors and has been retained in the 2022 edition [3,6]. However, it remains underdiagnosed due to histologic overlap with other subtypes. Our study aimed to study the clinicopathological features of this under-recognized entity.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old patient with a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic renal failure at the hemodialysis stage over 10 years, was hospitalized in the urology department after stabilization of a hemorrhagic shock of suspected renal origin at the intensive care unit.

The patient was apyretic, hemodynamically stable, and respiratory stable on physical examination, with a Glasgow score of 15 and a tender right lumbar fossa.

An abdominal ultrasound showed dilation of the right pyelocaliceal cavities with no detectable obstruction. The CT scan of the urinary tract revealed an enlarged globular kidney, distorted by multiple pan renal cystic formations, with doubt about a medio-renal intra-parenchymal collection and a discrete pyloric dilatation. There was no detectable lithiasis obstruction. During his stay in the urology department, the patient presented with a second episode of hemorrhagic shock. The decision of a hemostatic nephrectomy was ineluctable. The nephrectomy specimen was forwarded to the pathology department for detailed histopathological analysis.

On gross examination, the kidney measured 11*7*5.5 cm, with a 3 cm-long ureter. A mediorenal, subcapsular nodule measuring 2 cm on the long axis was identified. On cross-section, this nodule was yellowish-white, poorly defined, and softly consistent.

Discussion

ACKD is defined by the presence ≥ three cysts per kidney, or cysts accounting for >25 % of the renal parenchyma in a patient without polycystic kidney disease [8]. These cysts are usually numerous, bilateral, small (less than 2 cm), and located in the cortex and renal medulla. They are easily detected on CT. The kidneys are small, with marked parenchymal atrophy [1]. This multi-cystic aspect was noted in our case. Cysts may be complicated by intracystic bleeding, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, or superinfection, which would explain our patient's clinical presentation [1].

Renal tumors can complicate ACKD with increased frequency. The risk of renal tumors in patients with ACKD is more significant than 100 times that of the general population [4]. Almost all histologic variant of RCC can be present in the background of ACKD. ACD-RCC is regarded as the predominant subtype of RCC occurring in patients with ACKD [5].

ACD-RCC occurs about 20 years earlier than the general population, mainly in men [3,9]. According to a meta-analysis published in 2024, 80.2 % of patients were male [10]. The main risk factors include long duration of dialysis, young age and male gender [11]. This entity is characterized by a more extended dialysis period than those in patients with clear cell carcinoma or papillary carcinoma. The mean dialysis period was 148.2 months, more than 12 years, compared with 10 years for the other subtypes [10].

ACD-RCC is often asymptomatic, discovered incidentally by radiological examinations, but may be associated with microscopic hematuria or flank pain [5]. Multifocality and bilaterality are seen in >50 % and > 20 % of the cases, respectively [12]. However, in our case, the tumor was unilateral. This entity is still poorly understood by radiologists, and few data exist on its radiological aspects [13]. In our case, the initial CT scan did not reveal the tumor. This may be explained by the small size of the tumor, the emergency setting, and the poor knowledge of this entity's radiological aspects. Following the histological diagnosis, a review of the imaging was undertaken. The radiologists were provided with information regarding the precise localization of the tumor. This review confirmed the presence of the tumor and excluded any involvement of the contralateral kidney.

Pathological examination is the gold standard for the diagnosis. Macroscopically, tumors often appear as nodules arising within cysts or as solid masses separate from the cysts. On cut, tumors present a brown to yellow-tan surface. Areas of hemorrhage or necrosis are common [14]. The non-cystic tumors are well-circumscribed and may be surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule. The average size of these tumors is 2.7 cm [0.2–8.5] [11]. The renal parenchyma is generally atrophic and remodeled by multiple cysts. Histological examination shows a carcinomatous proliferation with architectural heterogeneity, including papillary, tubular, cribriform, microcystic, and solid patterns. Tumor cells often have abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and large nuclei with prominent nucleoli. The majority of ACD-RCCs exhibit WHO/ISUP grade 3 or 4 features [11]. In our case, the tumor was classified grade 3 ISUP. One characteristic feature of these tumors is the presence of intracytoplasmic or intercytoplasmic vacuoles, imparting a cribriform or sieve-like appearance. The presence of oxalate crystals in the tumor is characteristic, but not necessary for diagnosis. They are found in most, but not all tumors [3,14]. Oxalate crystals are also observed in renal tubules in the non-neoplastic renal parenchyma of ACKD. In the context of terminal renal failure, they promote cyst and tumor formation through mechanical obstruction of renal tubules and regulation of tubular cell cycles [15]. Sarcomatoid differentiation is rarely found in these tumors. In a series of 85 cases, it was observed in 1.4 % [8]. In the case we present, there was no sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation. Foci of clear cells hemorrhage, intracytoplasmic hemosiderin, and psammoma bodies may be observed [11]. The surrounding renal parenchyma is the site of numerous cysts lined by large cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and nucleolated nuclei similar to tumor cells [14].

Immunohistochemically, tumor cells express PAX8 and Racemase. CD10's positivity is variable. CK7 is often negative or rarely focally positive. GATA 3 and C-kit are negative. However, a specific immunohistochemical profile is not required to make this diagnosis [3,14].

ACD-RCC is characterized by genetic abnormalities distinct from papillary and clear cell carcinoma. Molecular studies have revealed gains and losses of multiple chromosomes [3,14]. The most common abnormalities include gains in chromosomes 3, 7, and 16 [5].

This entity poses a problem of differential diagnosis, mainly with papillary carcinoma, due to the frequent presence of papillary architecture, the eosinophilic appearance of the cells, and the intense and diffuse expression of Racemase in this tumor. However, architectural heterogeneity, cribriform and sieve-like architecture, intra-tumor oxalate crystals, and CK7 labeling, often negative or focally positive, support the diagnosis of ACD-RCC. Besides, this entity's molecular profile differs from that of papillary carcinomas. Clear cell carcinoma may also pose a differential diagnosis with this entity if clear cells and acinar architecture are present. Morphological aspects and immunohistochemical studies, particularly with carbonic anhydrase, help correct the diagnosis.

Most ACD-RCCs have a good prognosis. This can be explained by the early discovery of these tumors due to the clinical surveillance and regular radiological assessment of hemodialysis patients. Based on a cohort of 112 tumors, ACKD-RCC demonstrated cancer-specific and recurrence-free survival outcomes comparable to clear cell RCC and more favorable than those observed in papillary RCC [8]. However, in rare cases, the tumors are aggressive, with a risk of recurrence and metastasis. The aggressive potential is noted essentially in tumors presenting with sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation, large size, high pathological stage and grade, and lymphovasular invasion [3,8,14]. In our case, the outcome was fatal. The patient died after 3 days of nephrectomy. Further studies are needed to better assess the evolutionary profile of this entity.

Conclusion

Acquired cystic disease-related renal cell carcinoma is a common but unrecognized entity. It is characterized by clinical, histopathological, and evolutionary characteristics that differ from other RCC subtypes. Enhanced recognition of these features by pathologists is mandatory to ensure accurate histopathological diagnosis and appropriate clinical management.
