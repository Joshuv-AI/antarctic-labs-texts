Comparison of HALP and DECAF in predicting 30 day in hospital mortality in a retrospective cohort study of acute exacerbation of COPD
Authors: Ridvan Gode, Behcet Demir, Ertan Sonmez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) are associated with substantial short term mortality and healthcare utilization, yet the prognostic utility of the immunonutrition based hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score in this setting remains unclear, particularly in comparison with the well validated dyspnoea, eosinopenia, consolidation, acidaemia, atrial fibrillation (DECAF) score.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single centre observational cohort study of adults presenting to the emergency department (ED) with AECOPD between June 2022 and June 2025. DECAF and HALP scores were calculated at admission using routinely collected clinical and laboratory data, and the primary outcome was 30 day in hospital mortality. Discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with Youden-derived, data-driven cut-off values, and we further evaluated previously validated DECAF thresholds and a categorical DECAF model. Associations with 30 day mortality were examined using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for baseline clinical variables. Model calibration was evaluated using Brier scores and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and 30 day survival was analysed with Kaplan–Meier.ResultsA total of 499 patients were included in the study, 63 (12.6%) died within 30 days, and 76% of the cohort were male. The HALP score showed acceptable predictive performance (area under the curve (AUC) 0.724), at a cut-off of 16.84, sensitivity was 57.1%, specificity 75.0% and negative predictive value (NPV) 92.4% for 30 day in hospital mortality. The DECAF score demonstrated excellent discrimination for 30 day in hospital mortality (AUC 0.932), and showed significantly better discrimination than HALP (DeLong p < 0.001), using a cut-off of ≥ 4, sensitivity was 76.2%, specificity 95.6% and NPV 96.5%.ConclusionIn ED patients with AECOPD, the HALP score provides only limited prognostic information for 30 day in hospital mortality, whereas the DECAF score exhibits excellent discriminative performance and retains its value as a robust, clinically applicable predictor of short term outcomes in acute care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04532-4.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global health problem and one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, ranking among the top three causes of death, with most COPD‑related deaths occurring in low‑ and middle‑income countries [1–3].

More recently, the hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score, a composite index integrating hematological and nutritional parameters, has been proposed as a prognostic marker across various clinical conditions [4]. From a pathophysiological standpoint, the HALP score reflects the combined impact of reduced oxygen‑carrying capacity (anemia), depletion of protein reserves (hypoalbuminaemia), impaired cellular immunity (lymphopenia) and platelet‑driven pro‑inflammatory, prothrombotic activity, and thus capturing both systemic inflammatory burden and nutritional compromise [5]. The HALP score was originally developed by Chen et al. in 2015 as a simple, laboratory based index derived from routine blood tests [6]. Higher HALP scores have been associated with reduced mortality in diseases such as solid malignancies, acute ischemic stroke, and heart failure [7]. HALP has been preliminarily studied in acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD), but no head‑to‑head comparison with DECAF has been reported [8, 9]. AECOPD represent clinically significant events associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization, this makes reliable risk stratification tools essential in acute care. Dyspnoea, eosinopenia, consolidation, acidaemia, atrial fibrillation (DECAF) score was first described by Steer et al. in 2012 as a clinical prediction tool for in hospital mortality in patients with AECOPD [10]. The DECAF score, a well validated prognostic model demonstrating strong discriminative capacity for short term mortality [11–14], serves as an appropriate reference for comparison with the HALP score in predicting clinical outcomes.

Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the prognostic performance of the HALP score for predicting 30 day in hospital mortality in patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) with AECOPD, and to compare this performance with that of the DECAF score, whose validity has been established in multiple previous studies.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective observational cohort study evaluating the prognostic value of the DECAF and HALP scores for 30 day in hospital mortality in patients presenting with AECOPD.

Settings

This single center study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital that serves as the main referral center for chest diseases in the city. The study included patients who presented to the ED between June 2022 and June 2025. The ED manages approximately 250,000 patient visits annually, reflecting a high volume acute care setting.

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [15].

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they presented to the ED and were diagnosed with AECOPD. To ensure diagnostic reliability, only patients with at least one documented pulmonary function test supporting a diagnosis of COPD in the hospital records were included. An acute exacerbation was defined in accordance with the 2024 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease guidelines as a worsening of dyspnea and/or cough accompanied by an increase in sputum production occurring within a period of less than 14 days. Patients with chronic conditions known to influence HALP score components were excluded a priori, including chronic kidney disease, hematological malignancies, other active malignancies, and liver dysfunction evidenced by abnormal liver function tests and the use of immunosuppressive therapy within the preceding six months. During data processing, patients were further excluded if essential baseline data or information on 30 day mortality were missing.

Variables

Demographic characteristics, including age and sex, smoking history, comorbid conditions, and vital signs at presentation were considered potential confounders. In addition, selected hematological, biochemical, and arterial blood gas parameters obtained on the day of ED admission were included. These parameters included hemoglobin (g/dL), leukocyte count (10⁹/L), eosinophil count (10⁹/L), platelet count (10⁹/L), red cell distribution width, serum glucose (mg/dL), blood urea nitrogen (mg/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), C-reactive protein (mg/L), serum albumin (g/L), lactate (mmol/L), and arterial pH values. The main exposures of interest were the DECAF and HALP scores assessed at hospital admission. The primary outcome was 30 day in hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included the need for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), either at the time of initial hospitalization or at any point during the index stay.

Data sources and scores

All study data were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system and the national Ministry of Health electronic health database (e-Nabız, Republic of Turkey Ministry of Health). In cases where mortality data could not be confirmed through these systems, patients and/or their relatives were contacted via telephone using the contact information registered in the electronic records. DECAF was calculated at admission using the original scoring scheme [16]. Dyspnoea severity was assessed using the extended Medical Research Council Dyspnoea Scale, with 1 point assigned for grade 5a (housebound but independent in personal care) and 2 points for grade 5b (housebound and dependent in personal care). One point was assigned for eosinopenia (absolute eosinophil count < 0.05 × 10⁹/L), radiographic pulmonary consolidation, arterial acidemia (pH < 7.30), and the presence of atrial fibrillation identified by electrocardiography or documented medical history. Clinical variables required for DECAF score calculation, including radiographic consolidation, atrial fibrillation, and dyspnoea severity, were retrospectively extracted from electronic records. Pulmonary consolidation was determined by direct review of chest imaging performed at presentation using the hospital imaging system. Atrial fibrillation was identified either from a documented previous diagnosis in the medical record or from the electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained at the index presentation. Dyspnoea severity was determined based on documentation recorded by the examining physician at presentation. The HALP score was calculated using the formula: hemoglobin (g/L) × albumin (g/L) × lymphocyte count (10⁹/L) / platelet count (10⁹/L). HALP scores were calculated using laboratory values obtained at the time of ED presentation.

Bias

Several steps were taken to minimise potential sources of bias inherent in this retrospective, single centre cohort. Information bias was addressed by extracting data from a unified electronic medical record and by prespecifying operational definitions for all variables, including the DECAF and HALP components. Radiographic consolidation and atrial fibrillation were independently reviewed by two emergency medicine physicians who were blinded to patient outcomes, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, although some residual misclassification cannot be entirely excluded. All evaluations were based on contemporaneously recorded clinical information, without retrospective reinterpretation of symptoms, and both investigators responsible for data collection were blinded to patient outcomes at the time of abstraction.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were summarised using median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‑normally distributed data and mean ± standard deviation for approximately normally distributed data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For between group comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non normally distributed data and the independent samples t test for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

The primary outcome was 30-day in-hospital mortality. IMV and ICU admission were evaluated as secondary clinical outcomes. ROC analyses were performed to assess the discrimination performance of DECAF and HALP for the primary and secondary outcomes. AUCs were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and the AUCs of DECAF and HALP were compared using DeLong’s test for each outcome. Youden‑derived, data driven cut‑off values were identified in this cohort. Internal validation of the score-based ROC analyses was performed using 10,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected AUC values for DECAF and HALP. For DECAF, the Youden-derived cut-off from the present cohort was > 3, which corresponds clinically to a DECAF score of ≥ 4. In addition, we examined the operating characteristics of prespecified DECAF thresholds (≥ 2, ≥ 3, ≥4, and ≥ 5) by constructing 2 × 2 contingency tables and calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), NPV, and the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV are reported with 95% CI. Associations of DECAF and HALP scores with 30 day in hospital mortality were first examined in univariable logistic regression analyses. Covariates for the adjusted models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance in AECOPD, availability at the time of ED presentation, avoidance of overadjustment, fulfilment of statistical assumptions, and events-per-variable constraints. A stepwise or purely significance-based variable selection strategy was not used. To evaluate the independent prognostic value of each score, two separate multivariable logistic regression models were then constructed. Model A was specified as the DECAF-based model and included DECAF score, age, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and lactate. Model B was specified as the HALP-based model and included HALP score together with the same baseline covariates. Variables that were components of the DECAF or HALP scores were not entered separately into the corresponding multivariable models to avoid overadjustment and mathematical coupling. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a threshold of 5 predefined as indicating acceptable collinearity. The linearity-in-the-logit assumption for all continuous covariates was evaluated using the Box–Tidwell procedure. The events-per-variable ratio was calculated to assess model adequacy. To further explore the prognostic value of DECAF, we also modelled DECAF as a categorical predictor with prespecified score strata (≤ 2, 3, and ≥ 4) and estimated category specific OR for 30-day in-hospital mortality. Because sparse events were observed in the lower DECAF categories, category-specific OR were estimated using Firth’s penalized maximum likelihood logistic regression. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test, and Brier scores for the two adjusted logistic regression models (Model A, DECAF-based; Model B, HALP-based). Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier methods. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 790 patients were initially screened for eligibility, and 499 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Overall, 76% of the cohort were male and 24% were female. Of the 499 patients in our cohort, 169 (33.9%) were discharged home, and none of these discharged patients died within 30 days. Since no patient discharged within 30 days died post-discharge, 30-day mortality was entirely equivalent to 30-day in-hospital mortality in this cohort. Thus, in this dataset, 30-day all-cause mortality and 30-day in-hospital mortality were identical, because all deaths occurred during the index hospitalisation and none occurred after discharge. The median HALP score in the overall cohort was 26.8 (IQR 15.3–41.8), with higher values in survivors (28.8 [16.7–43.4]) and lower values in non-survivors (14.4 [9.3–24.3]). The median DECAF score was 2 (1–3) in the total cohort, 2 (1–3) in survivors, and 4 (4–4) in non survivors, indicating a clear gradient of increasing mortality risk with higher DECAF values. The associations of DECAF and HALP scores with 30 day in hospital mortality are presented in Table 2. In univariable logistic regression analysis, each one point increase in the DECAF score was associated with higher odds of 30 day in hospital mortality (OR 13.640; 95% CI 7.560–24.610), whereas higher HALP scores were associated with lower odds of mortality (OR 0.960; 95% CI 0.941–0.979). In the adjusted analyses, two separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Each adjusted model included 6 predictors, yielding an events-per-variable ratio of 10.5. No relevant multicollinearity was observed, with all VIF values below 2.0. In Model A, which included DECAF together with age, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and lactate, DECAF remained independently associated with 30 day in hospital mortality (adjusted OR 11.970; 95% CI 6.359–22.531). In Model B, which included HALP and the same baseline covariates, HALP also remained independently associated with 30 day in hospital mortality (adjusted OR 0.974; 95% CI 0.956–0.994), indicating that lower HALP scores were associated with higher mortality risk. To better understand the magnitude of the per point DECAF OR and to explore potential sparse data effects, we evaluated the distribution of 30 day in hospital mortality across DECAF score categories (Supplementary Table 1). Mortality was extremely rare among patients with DECAF scores ≤ 2, increased at a DECAF score of 3, and rose steeply at DECAF ≥ 4. Using Firth’s penalized logistic regression with DECAF ≤ 2 as the reference group, DECAF 3 and DECAF ≥ 4 remained strongly associated with higher 30-day mortality. The Firth-corrected OR were 19.94 for DECAF 3 (95% CI 3.65–108.77, p = 0.001) and 466.76 for DECAF ≥ 4 (95% CI 85.92–2535.65, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Calibration metrics for the two adjusted models are summarised in Table 4. The DECAF based adjusted model (Model A) showed good apparent calibration, with a lower Brier score (0.051) and a non significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 3.709, p = 0.882). In contrast, the HALP based adjusted model (Model B) had a higher Brier score (0.084), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 16.903, p = 0.031) suggested some residual miscalibration. Across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, particularly between approximately 10% and 30%, the DECAF-based adjusted model provided a higher net benefit than the HALP-based adjusted model and the treat-all or treat-none strategies. These findings suggest that the DECAF-based model had better apparent clinical utility in this cohort.

We further examined the diagnostic performance of DECAF at several clinically relevant thresholds (Table 5). The Youden derived cut-off of DECAF > 3 (corresponding to a score of ≥ 4) achieved the highest Youden index in this cohort (0.718), reflecting a favorable balance between sensitivity (76.2%) and specificity (95.6%). Lower thresholds, particularly DECAF ≥ 2 and ≥ 3, provided higher sensitivity and NPV but at the cost of substantially lower specificity and PPV, whereas higher thresholds such as DECAF ≥ 5 yielded very high specificity with markedly reduced sensitivity. The discriminative performance of the DECAF and HALP scores for predicting 30 day in hospital mortality is presented in Table 6. The DECAF score showed excellent ability to predict 30 day in hospital mortality (AUC 0.932, 95% CI 0.903–0.961; p < 0.05) whereas HALP demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance (AUC 0.724, 95% CI 0.655–0.794; p < 0.05). DeLong’s test confirmed that the AUC of DECAF was significantly higher than that of HALP (p < 0.001). Similar findings were observed for IMV (AUC 0.855 vs. 0.619; DeLong p < 0.001) and ICU admission (AUC 0.887 vs. 0.652; DeLong p < 0.001), indicating consistently better discrimination performance of DECAF across the primary and secondary clinical outcomes. The HALP score at the cut-off of 16.84, the test showed modest sensitivity (57.1%), specificity (75.0%) and an NPV of 92.4% for 30 day in hospital mortality. Bootstrap-based internal validation showed negligible optimism. The optimism-corrected AUCs were 0.932 for DECAF and 0.725 for HALP, supporting the stability of the primary ROC findings (Supplementary Table 2). The predictive performance of the DECAF and HALP scores for 30 day in hospital mortality was further evaluated using ROC curve analysis (Fig. 2). In visual comparison, the ROC curve for the DECAF score lay consistently above that of the HALP score, in line with its higher AUC and stronger overall discrimination. In the Kaplan–Meier analysis, DECAF ≥ 4 was associated with markedly lower 30-day survival than DECAF < 4 (28.4% vs. 96.5%; log-rank p < 0.001). HALP < 16.84 was also associated with lower 30-day survival than HALP ≥ 16.84 (75.2% vs. 92.4%; log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Patients with higher DECAF scores and lower HALP scores showed significantly worse short term survival compared with their respective reference groups, consistent with the regression and ROC findings.

Discussion

Our results suggest that, in patients presenting to the ED with AECOPD, the immunonutrition based HALP score is not superior to the DECAF score, whose validity has been established in multiple previous studies for predicting 1 month in hospital mortality. In our cohort, HALP showed only modest discriminative ability (AUC 0.724; NPV 92.4%), whereas DECAF demonstrated excellent prognostic performance for 30 day in hospital mortality (AUC 0.932; NPV 96.5%) in this setting. Although data on the use of HALP in COPD, particularly during acute exacerbations, remain limited, our study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to directly compare HALP with DECAF in an ED based AECOPD cohort, and thus providing novel evidence on the relative value of an immunonutrition based index versus an established clinical prediction tool.

In the study by Akbay et al., which specifically aimed to predict short term (in hospital) mortality in AECOPD, the cohort consisted exclusively of hospitalized patients, representing a more severe and clinically selected population [9]. In contrast, our cohort included patients diagnosed with AECOPD at the time of ED presentation. Despite this difference in case mix, the AUC of the HALP score for 1 month in hospital mortality in our study was slightly higher, suggesting that HALP preserves an acceptable level of short term prognostic discrimination even when applied earlier in the care pathway.

Compared with the studies by Cao, Zhang, and Yildiz et al., which evaluated long term mortality in AECOPD, HALP values in our cohort were higher, a difference that is likely related to the shorter follow up in our study, during which baseline immunonutritional status may exert a relatively greater influence on outcomes [17–19].

Unlike the ICU based cohort reported by Zheng et al., in which the HALP score showed near perfect discrimination for 30 day rehospitalization (AUC 0.963), the AUC of HALP for 30 day in hospital mortality in our ED based AECOPD cohort was more modest (0.724) [20]. This difference can reasonably be attributed to disparities in outcome (readmission vs. death), clinical setting (ICU vs. ED), and the inherently multifactorial nature of mortality. Consistent with this, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in our cohort showed a more gradual separation of curves over 30 days than in the ICU cohort of Han et al., indicating that HALP provides clinically relevant, but not dominant, prognostic information for short term in hospital mortality in the ED setting [8].

Because AECOPD are associated with substantial increases in morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization, the search for reliable prognostic tools to support risk stratification has been ongoing for many years. In this context, a randomized controlled trial by Dismore et al. demonstrated that using the DECAF score to select patients for a hospital at home model allowed inclusion of a larger proportion of patients compared with existing pathways, was generally preferred over inpatient care by both patients and families, and was considered safe, and thus highlighted the importance of accurately identifying low risk candidates for out of hospital management [21]. To this end, meta analyses by Ji et al., Huang et al., and Shen et al. have shown that the DECAF score provides good discrimination for 30 day mortality, with reported AUC values generally ranging between 0.73 and 0.79 across heterogeneous AECOPD cohorts [11, 12, 14]. In our study, the markedly higher AUC of 0.932 observed for 30 day in hospital mortality suggests very strong discriminative performance of DECAF in this ED based cohort. Although this estimate lies above AUC reported in previous meta analyses, the direction of effect is consistent with the existing literature, and the difference is more likely to reflect variations in case mix, outcome definition, and clinical setting, as well as the possibility that the applied exclusion criteria yielded a more acutely unwell and clinically homogeneous cohort, rather than a fundamentally different behaviour of the score itself.

In the present study, the HALP score yielded AUCs of 0.652 for ICU admission and 0.619 for IMV, values that are broadly concordant with those reported by Wang et al. in a large AECOPD cohort (AUC 0.672 for ICU and 0.657 for IMV), with the slightly lower IMV discrimination in our cohort plausibly related to differences in sample size and the exclusion of in-hospital mortality from their analysis [22]. By contrast, Zhao and Bao reported a considerably higher AUC of 0.804 for a composite endpoint of ICU admission and/or IMV in pneumonia patients, a result that is not directly comparable to ours because their population differed and the HALP score was incorporated as one component of a multivariable composite model rather than assessed as an isolated predictor [23]. In our cohort, the DECAF score demonstrated excellent discriminative ability for both IMV (AUC 0.855) and ICU admission (AUC 0.887). The ICU result is broadly consistent with the AUC of 0.90 reported by Son et al. [24] and higher than the 0.810 observed by Lee et al. [25]. For IMV prediction, our DECAF AUC exceeded those reported by Singh et al. [26] and Lee et al. [25] (0.80 and 0.801, respectively), but was lower than the AUC of 0.881 reported by Malik and Sangwan [27]. Overall, these between-study differences are most likely driven by heterogeneity in study populations and by variations in outcome definitions, including the combined evaluation of IMV with NIMV and the joint assessment of IMV with short term mortality in some reports.

Beyond their discriminative performance, the two scores differed markedly in calibration. The DECAF-based adjusted model showed good apparent calibration, supporting the reliability of its predicted probabilities in this cohort, whereas HALP exhibited clear signs of miscalibration, with substantially greater prediction error and poorer agreement between predicted and observed risks. Although HALP was able to rank patients by risk with moderate accuracy, its absolute probability estimates were less trustworthy. Albumin, a key component of HALP, behaves as a negative acute-phase reactant and may fall during systemic inflammation independently of underlying nutritional status, while lymphocyte counts are prone to rapid redistribution under physiological stress. We believe that DECAF’s superior performance can be attributed to its ability to directly index acute physiological failure and organ strain acidaemia, consolidation, atrial fibrillation, and severe dyspnoea whereas HALP primarily reflects chronic systemic inflammatory nutritional depletion, a slower and less specific driver of imminent 30-day mortality. Overall, these features suggest that HALP may be useful as a complementary risk-ranking tool but should not be relied upon as a stand-alone source of calibrated mortality probabilities in AECOPD without further recalibration or integration into a broader prognostic model. We also acknowledge that the apparent calibration of the HALP-based model, including the Hosmer–Lemeshow result, should be interpreted with caution and that more extensive internal validation will be an important focus of future work.

The per point OR for DECAF was, however, notably large in both univariable and multivariable analyses. This finding warrants cautious interpretation and is most plausibly explained by the steep underlying risk gradient across DECAF score categories rather than a coding or scaling artefact. Specifically, 30‑day in‑hospital mortality was almost absent among patients with DECAF scores ≤ 2, rose at a score of 3, and increased sharply at scores ≥ 4, a pattern that is consistent with sparse events in the lower score categories. To address potential sparse‑data bias, we re‑estimated category‑specific OR using Firth’s penalized maximum likelihood logistic regression; the corrected estimates remained very large and statistically significant, reinforcing that these OR reflect a steep mortality gradient across DECAF categories rather than an artefactual effect of model specification. In line with this risk distribution, the Youden-derived cut-off of DECAF ≥ 4 achieved the most favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity among the prespecified thresholds examined. Lower thresholds, such as ≥ 2 and ≥ 3, offered higher sensitivity and NPV and may therefore be useful for ruling out high-risk patients, whereas ≥ 4 provided substantially greater specificity, making it more appropriate for identifying patients at the highest risk of early death. Although our primary aim was to compare HALP with DECAF, future studies should evaluate whether combining these scores (DECAF+HALP), or integrating HALP into DECAF based models, offers incremental prognostic value beyond either score alone.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its single centre design inherently limits causal inference and may restrict the generalisability of our findings to other settings, and the retrospective extraction of DECAF components from clinical records may have introduced information bias. Second, the classification of radiographic consolidation and atrial fibrillation may be subject to information bias. Radiographic consolidation was determined from chest imaging performed at presentation, and the presence of atrial fibrillation was based on admission ECGs and documented medical history; both were jointly reviewed and adjudicated by two emergency medicine specialists who reached a consensus decision. However, some residual misclassification cannot be completely excluded, particularly because chest radiographs were not systematically reported by a radiologist and the presence of atrial fibrillation on the ECG obtained at presentation was not independently verified by a cardiologist.

Third, we were unable to systematically account for potentially important confounders, including prior or concomitant treatments such as systemic corticosteroids, non invasive ventilation, antibiotics, and nutritional support, as well as subclinical disturbances in nutritional or inflammatory status, all of which could influence both HALP values and survival. Fourth, we were unable to perform sensitivity analyses in a cohort that included the excluded patients, which represents an important limitation of our study. In total, 172 patients were excluded at baseline because they met at least one of the predefined exclusion criteria. This extent of exclusion may limit the generalisability of our findings to broader, unselected AECOPD populations and may have resulted in a more clinically homogeneous cohort, potentially influencing the observed performance of both DECAF and HALP. Additionally, the exclusion of patients with active malignancies, chronic kidney disease, and hepatic dysfunction may have artificially inflated the discriminative performance of both scores by removing complex cases in whom death was primarily driven by non-COPD causes. A further limitation concerns the handling of missing baseline data. Of the 790 patients initially screened, 115 were excluded owing to incomplete baseline variables. The most frequently absent data elements were full blood count or serum albumin results, which are essential for HALP score calculation, electrocardiographic data required for the atrial fibrillation component of the DECAF score, and chest imaging needed to determine radiographic consolidation within the DECAF scoring framework. These gaps reflect the retrospective, real-world nature of the study, as not all investigations are uniformly obtained for every patient in routine ED practice. We cannot entirely exclude the possibility that patients with incomplete data had systematically less severe presentations, given that less critically ill patients are less likely to undergo full laboratory and radiographic evaluation. If so, restriction to complete cases may have introduced a degree of selection bias towards a more acutely unwell and comprehensively assessed population. Because the calculation of both DECAF and HALP requires all constituent components to be present, multiple imputation of individual variables would have yielded scores derived from statistically modelled rather than directly observed clinical values, which we considered methodologically inappropriate for a score comparison study; complete-case analysis was therefore retained. We did not apply more complex non-linear modelling approaches, and this should be regarded as an additional methodological limitation. We also note that future studies should include these excluded patient groups and compare their outcomes in adjusted sensitivity analyses, as this would provide a more complete assessment of the prognostic performance of both scores. Finally, our cohort comprised patients managed in the ED, and the results may not be directly applicable to ICU only populations or to patients with milder exacerbations treated exclusively in general wards or outpatient settings.

Conclusion

In patients presenting to the ED with AECOPD, the HALP score showed only limited ability to predict 30 day in hospital mortality. However, the DECAF score demonstrated excellent discriminative performance for 30 day in hospital mortality, reinforcing its role as a robust and clinically applicable risk stratification instrument in acute care.
