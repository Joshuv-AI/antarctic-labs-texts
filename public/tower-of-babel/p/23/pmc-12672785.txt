Integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptome to identify novel gene markers for germinal center B cells (GCB) subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Huxing Chen, Saijun Ma, Weixin Zhou, Duane Weng, Yuqing Sun, Dan Lu, Jing Li, Lu Ao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The gold standard cell-of-origin (COO) classification based on the gene expression profiling (GEP) is robust in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL). However, its clinical application remains limited. The qualitative transcriptional characteristic on account of the within-sample relative expression orderings (REO) of genes, which is highly stable against batch effects and variations of specimen quality, presents great potential for clinical use. In this study, we developed a qualitative signature to distinguish the germinal center B cells (GCB) subtype of DLBCL from non-GCB subtypes. The signature consisting of 19 gene pairs (19-GPS), was identified from gene pairs with reversal REO patterns between the GCB and non-GCB subtypes. It was validated across four independent datasets with accuracies of 89.37%, 84.34%, 88.29%, and 92.72%, respectively. Patients classified as the GCB subtype by the 19-GPS had significantly higher overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) rates compared to the non-GCB patients. In the validation datasets, the 19-GPS based COO classification demonstrated enhanced performance in prognostic stratification compared to the gold standard COO classification. Three marker genes in 19-GPS, namely HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4, were identified with prognostic value for DLBCL. The expression of NLRP4 in B cells may be related to the transformation of follicular lymphoma (FL), a lymphoma derived from GCB, into DLBCL. All three aforementioned genes may be associated with the responsiveness of malignant human B-cell Lines to R-CHOP therapeutic agents. These results demonstrated that 19-GPS signature was effective and might potentially assist in guiding the prognosis and treatment options for DLBCL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06517-5.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs) [1], is a clinically aggressive and heterogenous disease [2, 3]. Through gene expression profiling (GEP) analysis, DLBCL is divided into three cell of origin (COO) molecular subtypes: germinal center B cell-like (GCB), activated B cell-like (ABC), and unclassified subtypes (UC) [4, 5]. It holds great significance for prognosis and a possible treatment guidance, particularly with the most frequently utilized regimen of rituximab combined with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP), where patients with the GCB subtype exhibit a significantly superior prognosis compared to those with the non-GCB subtype [6, 7]. Therefore, in accordance with the classification guidelines for mature lymphoid neoplasms established by the World Health Organization (WHO) [8] and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) [9], it is advisable to distinguish between germinal center B-cell (GCB) and non-germinal center B-cell (non-GCB) subtypes when diagnosing DLBCL. Currently, GEP using fresh frozen tissue for COO assignment, employing algorithms such as the Wright method [3], is widely acknowledged as the “gold standard” [10, 11]. Since Wright’s algorithm necessitates the normalization of GEP data, its effective application presupposes a target dataset comprising a substantial number of tumors, exhibiting a comparable ratio of ABC to GCB as in the original cohort utilized to establish the algorithm [10], which limits its clinical practicality. Notably, the Lymph2Cx digital targeted GEP assay (a 20-gene test within the NanoString nCounter system) has offered reliable COO subtype classification for DLBCL [10, 12, 13]. Nevertheless, Lymph2Cx still necessitates the normalization of gene expression data. It implements RNA quality assessment through geometric mean quantification of five conserved housekeeping genes [14], while technical reliability studies reveale operational thresholds with failure rates ranging from 5.4 to 12% [13, 14], coupled with an unclassified specimen rate of approximately 11% [10]. Consequently, it’s urgent to establish clinically applicable tests or signatures for more accurate classification of DLBCL.

In addition, numerous transcriptional signatures have been established to predict the cell of origin (COO) subtypes or survival outcomes for DLBCL patients [15–17]. These signatures typically rely on absolute expression values of signature genes. However, the quantitative information of gene expression measurements is easily vulnerable to batch effects introduced by different laboratory conditions [18]. Fortunately, the qualitative transcriptional signature based on the within-sample relative expression orderings (REO) of genes has been demonstrated to be robust against experimental batch effects and remains invariant under monotonic data transformations [19, 20], which is suitable for personalized analysis in clinical settings. Our previous research has successfully developed several REO-based signatures for cancer molecular classification, including gastric cancer [21], colorectal cancer [22], gliomas [23], and hepatocellular carcinoma [24, 25]. Therefore, it is worthwhile to develop a qualitative transcriptional signature to determine the COO subtypes of DLBCL.

In this study, a qualitative signature based on REO was established for the COO classification of DLBCL, comprising 19 gene pairs denoted as 19-GPS. This signature was validated across four independent datasets measured on three platforms, encompassing 1006 fresh frozen (FF) biopsy samples and 498 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples. Subsequently, three GCB subtype-specific genes in 19-GPS, the 5-hydroxymethylcytosine binding, ES cell specific gene (HMCES), the Zinc finger AN1-type containing 4 gene (ZFAND4), and the NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 4 gene (NLRP4) were identified by expression analysis of bulk and single-cell transcriptome data. These findings may contribute to understanding the potential prognostic significance of the 19-GPS and guiding the risk stratification for DLBCL patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection and data preprocessing

The gene expression profiles of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) were collected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, http://cancergenome.nih.gov/).As detailed in Table 1, the datasets were categorized into three parts: the training set comprised five datasets with a total of 381 DLBCL samples, which were utilized to develop the REO-based signature; the validation set included four datasets encompassing 1,504 DLBCL samples that were employed to assess the predictive power of the signature. The cell-of-origin (COO) subtypes for DLBCL patients within these datasets were determined through GEP. In accordance with the original dataset annotations, UC and ABC cases were categorized into the non-GCB subgroup.

Additionally, four datasets derived from malignant human B-cell lines were used for drug correlation analysis. In dataset GSE53798, gene expression profiles of 26 drug-naïve cell Lines within a human B-cell cancer cell Line panel were obtained, including 13 DLBCL cell Lines, 11 multiple myeloma (MM) cell lines, one plasmacytoma cell line, and one undifferentiated lymphoma cell line. Dose response experiments were conducted on HBCCL using drugs cyclophosphamide (C), doxorubicin (H), and vincristine (O) from R-CHOP immunotherapy. The positive area under the dose-response curve (AUC0) for each drug was used to indicate the level of chemical resistance exhibited by each cell line. In dataset GSE224867, gene expression profiles from 18 DLBCL cell lines treated with or without doxorubicin were obtained. These cell lines were classified into three groups based on their response levels to doxorubicin: senescence-competent, senescence-intermediate, and senescence-incompetent. In dataset GSE89231, gene expression profiles of 6 DLBCL cell Lines exposed to 2.5 µg/mL doxorubicin at four critical time points: 0 h (baseline), 2 h, 12 h, and 48 h post-treatment were obtained. The dataset GSE206130 provides gene expression profile data for two DLBCL cell lines, U-2940 and Karpas-1106P, after 48 h of treatment with rituximab (R) or prednisone (P). Finally, three datasets were used to identify GCB subtype-specific marker genes as follows. Dataset GSE182434 contains single-cell RNA sequencing data from 3 ABC-DLBCL patients, 1 GCB-DLBCL patient, and 3 patients with follicular lymphoma (FL). Dataset GSE86613 includes microarray data from 41 cases of DLBCL, 7 cases of FL, and 2 cases of transformed FL. Dataset GSE272403 comprises bulk RNA sequencing data from 60 cases of FL, 4 cases of transformed FL, and 12 tonsil cells from healthy donors (4 GCB cell samples and 8 non-GCB cell samples).

For the microarray data, raw mRNA expression data (.CEL files) were processed using the robust multichip average algorithm (RMA). The Entrez IDs were utilized to Map genes based on the corresponding platform annotation files. For NGS data in TCGA, the level 3 fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were directly downloaded. For bulk NGS data in GEO, raw read counts data or normalized read counts data for each gene were obtained. For the scRNA-seq data in GEO, the cell annotation information and raw read count data were downloaded, and the R-package Seurat 4.4.0 scRNA (https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was used for data read, quality control, and pre-processing. During the quality control process, the following criteria were used to define non-compliant cells: (1) cells with fewer than 200 detected genes; (2) cells with over 6000 detected genes; (3) cells with over 15% of mitochondrial-specific genes detected. Subsequently, gene expression levels were normalized using the “NormalizedData” function. The Ensembl gene ID, which corresponds to the unique Entrez gene ID, was adopted. All gene expression data underwent log2 transformation.

Development of the REO-based transcriptional signature for determining GCB and non-GCB subtypes

For a given gene pair (a, b), Ga and Gb, represented the expression values of gene a and b, respectively. In a particular DLBCL subtype (the GCB or non-GCB subtype), if the REO pattern (Ga > Gb or Ga < Gb) was kept in significantly more samples than expected by random chance, then the given gene pair was defined as a stable gene pair for that DLBCL subtype. The significance of stable REO pattern of a gene pair in GCB samples or non-GCB samples was determined by the binomial test as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P=1-{\sum\:}_{i=0}^{s-1}{(n}_{i})\left({p}_{0}{)}^{i}\right(1-{p}_{0}{)}^{n-1}$$\end{document}

where n was the total number of samples and s was the number of samples which exhibit the REO pattern (Ga > Gb or Ga < Gb), and p0 (here, p0 = 0.5) was the probability of observing a certain REO pattern in a sample by chance. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to estimate the false discovery rate (FDR). Gene pairs with stable REO pattern in GCB or non-GCB sample but with reversal REO pattern between GCB and non-GCB samples in the training set were extracted as the reversal gene pairs.

Secondly, gene pair with a F-score > 0.75 was filtered out as the candidate gene pair. The F-score was defined as the harmonic mean of sensitivity and specificity, which was calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F-score=2\times\frac{sensitivity\times specify}{sensitivity+specify}$$\end{document}

where sensitivity and specificity were the proportion of correctly identified GCB and non-GCB samples by each candidate gene pair, respectively.

Thirdly, all candidate gene pairs were sorted in a descending order according to their reversal degree, where the reversal degree for each gene pair was calculated as follow:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:avg{R}_{ab}=\sqrt{\left|\right(mean\left[\:{R}_{ab}\left(GCB\right)\right]\left|*\right|mean\left[{R}_{ab}\left(non\_GCB\right)\right]|}$$\end{document}

where mean [Rab (GCB)] and mean [Rab (non-GCB)] represented the absolute mean of rank differences of the candidate gene pair (a, b) in GCB samples and non-GCB samples, respectively. The rank difference of gene pair (a, b) was calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{ab}=R_a-R_b$$\end{document}

where Ra, Rb represented the rank of Ga and Gb in a sample, respectively, and Rab was the rank difference between the two genes.

Finally, top 414 candidate gene pairs were selected as seeds and a forward stepwise selection algorithm was performed to search for the optimal subset of gene pairs that reached the highest F-score in the microarray datasets.

Performance evaluation

Accuracy was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the signature, which are calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{accuracy=}\frac{\text{TG+TN}}{N}$$\end{document}

where TG denoted the number of actual GCB which were correctly classified as GCB, TN denoted the number of actual non-GCB which were correctly classified as non-GCB, and N denoted the size of DLBCL samples.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals for AUCs were calculated with the nonparametric Delong’s test.

Survival analysis

The overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) served as the prognosis endpoint for DLBCL patients. Kaplan-Meier survival plots and log-rank test were used to assess the differences in OS and PFS of different groups. The univariate Cox proportional-hazards regression model was used to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [26]. Clinical data used for survival analysis in this study were provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Clustering and correlation analysis

Hierarchical analysis for bulk RNA-seq data was performed through Ward’s method and the pheatmap package was applied for visualization. When subsequently analyzing scRNA-seq data using the R package Seurat version 4.4.0, unqualified cells and genes detected in less than 3 cells were deleted. To remove the batch effect, the R package Harmony was used to de-batch the sample sources. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [27] was used for dimensionality reduction of scRNA-seq data. Cell annotation was conducted based on the information provided by the GEO database. Pseudo-time analysis for B cells was carried out with the Monocle 2.24 (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/3.15/bioc/src/contrib/Archive/monocle/). Correlation analysis was applied using Spearman’s correlation. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, specifically version 4.1.2 for bulk transcriptome data analysis and version 4.4.0 for single-cell transcriptome data analysis.

PPI network analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks of the candidate gene were performed in the STRING database version 12 (https://string-db.org/) [28]. The confidence score was set as > 0.4 and four interaction sources: Text-mining, Experiments, Databases, Co‑expression were selected.

Results

Development and validation of the REO-based transcriptional signature

Figure 1 briefly described the flowchart of this study. Firstly, 203,125,451 and 202,978,082 gene pairs with significantly stable REO were identified between 170 GCB and 171 non-GCB samples from the microarray datasets GSE19246, GSE23501, GSE56313 and GSE64555 (FDR < 0.2). Then 1,977,878 stable gene pairs with reversal REO between the two groups were obtained. Similarly, 1,751,289 stable gene pairs with reversal REO were obtained between the two groups in the RNA-seq dataset TCGA-DLBC. Secondly, 827 gene pairs with F-score > 0.75 were extracted from the intersection of the above two gene pair lists as the candidate gene pairs (Supplementary Table 2) and sorted in a descending order according to their reversal degree in the microarray datasets. Finally, 19 gene pairs with the highest F-score (0.9179) in the microarray datasets, denoted as 19-GPS (Table 2), were selected to distinguish the GCB subtype from non-GCB subtypes according to the classification rule as follows: a sample was classified into the GCB subtype when at last 10 gene pairs voted for GCB subtype; otherwise, the non-GCB subtypes.

As depicted in Fig. 2A, the accuracies of 19-GPS in the training data were 91.79% for 341 microarray samples, and 90% for 40 RNA-seq samples, respectively. Subsequently, 19-GPS was validated in independent datasets measured by microarray and RNA-seq. In the microarray datasets, 19-GPS achieved an accuracy of 89.37% in GSE10846 with 414 FF samples and 84.34% in GSE31312 with 498 FFPE samples, respectively. In two datasets measured by RNA-seq, GSE147986 and NCICCR-DLBCL, the accuracies of 19-GPS were 88.29% and 92.72%, respectively.

In the combined training data, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 19-GPS reached 0.9753 (95% CI: 0.9634–0.9872, Fig. 2B), which was higher than those of individual gene pairs (Table 2). In validation datasets, the 19-GPS had AUC values ranging from 0.9357 to 0.9753 (Fig. 2C-F). Compared to the gold standard, 19-GPS reclassified 9–30% of GCB samples as non-GCB, 11.11–40% of UC samples as GCB, and 0–6% of ABC samples as GCB (Fig. 2G-J). Remarkably, affymetrix microarray data (.CEL files) for above datasets were just processed by background adjustment without quantile normalization, and the RNA-seq dataset GSE147986 was not processed with FPKM normalization. The results revealed that the 19-GPS was invariant to monotone data normalization definitely and well performed in independent datasets measured by different technologies.

Comparison of prognostic significance of 19-GPS with the gold standard COO classification based on Wright algorithm or the DGE model

In training datasets, only dataset GSE23501 with the survival information was used for survival analysis. The GCB and non-GCB cases classified by Wright algorithm had significantly different OS (log-rank P = 2.83 × 10−2; HR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.06–11.23; Fig. 3B) and PFS (log-rank P = 5.25 × 10−3; HR = 3.97, 95% CI: 1.4–11.31; Fig. 3E). Similarly, the GCB and non-GCB cases classified by 19-GPS also had significantly different OS (log-rank P = 1.34 × 10−3; HR = 3.96, 95% CI: 1.22–12.86; Fig. 3 A) and PFS (log-rank P = 9.9 × 10−3; HR = 3.44, 95% CI: 1.27–9.32; Fig. 3D). Compared with the Wright algorithm, the 19-GPS could give more superior OS curves and very similar PFS curves.

In validation datasets GSE10486, GSE31312 and NCICCR-DLBCL, patients in the GCB subtype classified by 19-GPS had significantly higher OS rate and PFS rate than those in the non-GCB subtype (Fig. 3 C, F). Intriguing, 19-GPS showed stronger significance levels than the Wright algorithm in GSE10846 (OS in CHOP group: log-rank P = 1.71 × 10−4 VS 3.53 × 10−4; OS in R-CHOP group: log-rank P = 9.3 × 10−6 VS 1.47 × 10−3; PFS in R-CHOP group: log-rank P = 1.56 × 10−5 VS 2.29 × 10−3). Similar results were noted for cases categorized by the DGE model [29] in NCICCR-DLBCL (OS: log-rank P = 2.47 × 10−5 VS 2.77 × 10−5; PFS: log-rank P = 1.57 × 10−7 VS 9.26 × 10−7). While for dataset GSE31312, 19-GPS showed stronger significance level than the Wright algorithm in PFS (log-rank P = 4.26 × 10−4 VS 1.57 × 10−3).

To further investigate the prognostic value of 19-GPS, cases treated with R-CHOP immunochemotherapy were collected from aforementioned datasets, including 1006 cases with OS information and 988 cases with PFS information. Among 483 gold standard-defined GCB cases, 60 were reclassified as non-GCB by 19-GPS. These reclassified patients exhibited significantly inferior OS compared to the remaining 423 GCB cases (log-rank P = 4.92 × 10−2; HR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1–2.56; Fig. 3G). Similarly, PFS for 56 reclassified non-GCB cases was markedly reduced versus 418 GCB patients (log-rank P = 2.79 × 10−2; HR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.05–2.75; Fig. 3I). In 523 gold standard-defined non-GCB cases, 62 were reclassified as GCB by 19-GPS. These patients demonstrated significantly higher OS compared to 461 non-GCB cases (log-rank P = 1.98 × 10−2; HR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.09–2.85; Fig. 3H). Likewise, 61 reclassified GCB cases showed superior PFS relative to 453 non-GCB patients (log-rank P = 9.02 × 10−4; HR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.39–3.82; Fig. 3 J). Overall, COO classification utilizing 19-GPS demonstrated a tendency to offer enhanced prognostic significance when compared to the gold standard of GEP.

Identification of the Marker genes of GCB subtype in 19-GPS

The hierarchical clustering of 27 genes in 19-GPS was performed in dataset NCICCR-DLBCL. Clustering based on expression patterns of above signature genes segregated GCB and non-GCB types, which showed high concordance with the DGE model or 19-GPS (Fig. 4 A). In cluster 1, 10 genes AFF2, MME, ZFAND4, LMO2, ASB13, SERPINA9, NLRP4, SSBP2, MYBL1 and HMCES tended to be highly expressed in the GCB subtype, and they were all at the first column of 19-GPS in Table 2. Obviously, the high expression of these genes would contribute to classifying the sample as GCB subtype, so we defined them as GCB subtype related genes. Among them, four genes, LMO2 [15, 30, 31], MYBL1 [31], SERPINA9 [32], and MME [32], were canonical marker genes associated with GCB-DLBCL, while other three genes HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4 were rarely researched in DLBCL before.

After preprocessing the single-cell data from GSE182434, a total of 24,587 cells were retained, and 2,000 highly variable genes were identified. As illustrated in Fig. 4B-C, NLRP4 exhibited specific expression in B lymphocytes. In comparison to ABC-DLBCL cells, ZFAND4 showed elevated expression levels in both B lymphocytes and Tfh cells of GCB-DLBCL; HMCES was predominantly expressed in natural killer (NK) cells and CD4 regulatory T cells within GCB-DLBCL. Then, the average gene expression levels across all cells in individual samples were calculated. Intriguing, the expressions of all three genes were significantly higher in follicular lymphoma (FL) than in ABC-DLBCL (Fig. 4D). It was noteworthy that the 19-GPS consistently classified three FL samples and one GCB-DLBCL into the germinal center B-cell-like (GCB) subtype, suggesting a potential association between cell-of-origin (COO) and the observed differential gene expression patterns.

Indeed, similar to GCB-DLBCL, FL also originates from the germinal center [33, 34], and has been classified as a GCB subtype of lymphoma [35]. Bulk transcriptome data demonstrated that the expression levels of the three genes were significantly higher in both follicular lymphoma (FL) and GCB-DLBCL samples compared to ABC-DLBCL samples (Fig. 4H). Notably, three aforementioned genes were also significantly upregulated in normal tonsillar GCB cells versus non-GCB cells (Fig. 4I). The pseudo-time series analysis of 8532 B cells across all samples indicated that B cells from FL samples displayed a trend toward differentiation into DLBCL (Fig. 4E-F), accompanied by a noticeable decrease in NLRP4 gene expression during this process (Fig. 4G). In addition, NLRP4 expression also tended to be lowly expressed in transformed FL than FL samples based on both bulk RNA-seq and microarray data (Fig. 4 J). Therefore, these findings validated that HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4 as signature genes for the GCB subtype lymphoma.

The prognostic value of GCB marker genes HMCES, ZFAND4 and NLRP4 in patients treated with R-CHOP

Next, patients treated with R-CHOP in the validation dataset NCICCR-DLBCL were categorized into the high and low expression groups based on the median values of three GCB marker genes: HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4. In cases where the number of samples was odd, the sample corresponding to the median value was excluded from analysis. Notably, all three genes demonstrated prognostic significance. Specifically, both OS rates and PFS rates in the high expression group for these genes were observed to be higher than those in their respective low expression counterparts (Fig. 5A-F). Though grouping by the mean expression of HMCES failed to achieve statistical significance, yet a significant survival difference emerged between the two groups after increasing the proportion of high-expression samples to two-thirds of the total cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1). Importantly, among GCB samples classified by 19-GPS, the PFS rate for NLRP4 in the high expression group remained significantly elevated compared to that in the low expression group (Fig. 5G). Similar findings were observed in datasets GSE10486 (Fig. 5H) and GSE31312 (Fig. 5I), suggesting that NLRP4 holds prognostic value in patients with the GCB subtype undergoing R-CHOP treatment. Correlation analysis of HMCES, ZFAND4 and NLRP4 with drug response in malignant human B-cell cell lines

In the drug-naïve HBCCL panel from dataset GSE53798, the expression of HMCES exhibited a negative correlation with the AUC0 values of cyclophosphamide and doxorubicin. Additionally, the expression levels of ZFAND4 and NLRP4 were negatively correlated with the AUC0 values for all three drugs, respectively (Fig. 6 A). In dataset GSE224867, only the expression of NLRP4 was found to be negatively correlated with response levels to doxorubicin in both drug-naïve and drug treated cell lines (Fig. 6B-C). In drug-naïve cell lines, the expression levels of the aforementioned three genes tended to be higher in the good response group compared to those in the poor response group (Fig. 6D). Then, following treatment with doxorubicin, the expression level of NLRP4 in the good response group remained higher than that in the poor response group, reaching a marginally significant level (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, in dataset GSE89231, NLRP4 expression increased in U2932 cells (classified as good responders by GSE224867) following doxorubicin treatment, while it exhibited time-dependent decreases in the remaining five cell lines; Notably, three of these cell lines (NUDHL1, SUDHL5, OCILY7), classified as poor responders in GSE224867, showed similar declining trends (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In the two DLBCL cell lines of the GSE206130 dataset, significant differences in the expression of HMCES were observed among the three groups: untreated, rituximab-treated, and prednisone-treated (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, drug response patterns observed for ZFAND4 exhibited a striking similarity to those of HMCES (Fig. 6H), while the drug response pattern of MS4A1, which served as the target gene for rituximab, demonstrated the same similarity (Fig. 6I). Given that a consistent positive correlation between HMCES and ZFAND4 was identified in GSE53798 (Fig. 6 A) and GSE224867 (Fig. 6B-C), we speculated that they may be both related to MS4A1. Subsequently, a significant positive correlation was indeed observed between the expression levels of the HMCES, ZFAND4, and MS4A1 genes in pre-therapeutic tissue samples from GSE10846 (Fig. 6J-L).

Notably, the NLRP4 gene exhibited different drug response patterns compared to HMCES or ZFAND4. Specifically, in both cell lines, NLRP4 tended to be down-regulated following prednisone treatment, while it was significantly up-regulated after rituximab treatment in Karpas-1106P (Fig. 6 F). These findings suggested that all three aforementioned genes were associated with the responsiveness of malignant human B-cell lines to R-CHOP therapeutic agents to some extent.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a qualitative transcriptional signature comprising 19 gene pairs, termed the 19-GPS, for the cell-of-origin (COO) subtypes classification of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). This signature was validated across four independent datasets from three platforms, including 1006 fresh-frozen (FF) and 498 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens. Survival analyses indicated that the 19-GPS tends to provide superior prognostic relevance for COO subtypes compared to the gold standard. Cluster analysis of the expression profiles of 27 characteristic genes in 19-GPS revealed that DLBCL could still be divided into GCB and non-GCB types, and it’s highly consistent with the Wright algorithm, thereby explaining the effectiveness of the 19-GPS.

Furthermore, three genes HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4 from 19-GPS, which had been infrequently studied in DLBCL, were identified to be GCB marker genes with prognostic value. HMCES involved in DNA double-strand break repair [36, 37], has been recognized as a novel shield of basic sites in single-stranded DNA [38], consistent with our PPI network analysis. Single-cell transcriptome data revealed that HMCES is highly expressed in natural killer (NK) cells from CGB-DLBCL compared to those with ABC-DLBCL. Notably, NK cell-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) has been reported as a crucial mechanism underlying rituximab’s action in DLBCL [39]. ZFAND4 also known as ANUBL1 [40], is expressed in normal germinal-center B cells [41]. In tonsil-derived samples, we found that ZFAND4 expression levels were indeed significantly higher in normal GCB cells than that in non-GCB cells. In addition, ZFAND4 showed elevated expression levels in both B lymphocytes and Tfh cells of GCB-DLBCL. Studies have shown that CD4 + T follicular helper (Tfh) cells directly participate in the formation and maintenance of germinal centers (GCs) through co-stimulatory signals such as CD40-CDlg [42] and ICOS-ICOSL [43]. Targeting the regulation of Tfh-GCB interactions may represent a critical approach for controlling the progression of GCB-subtype lymphomas [43]. The NLRP4 functions as a negative regulator of the type I interferon signaling pathway by promoting DTX4-mediated ubiquitination of activated TBK1 [44]. Interestingly, targeting the type I interferon signaling pathway has emerged as an effective therapeutic strategy in tumor immunotherapy [45–47]. In this study, we found that NLRP4 exhibits prognostic significance in patients with the GCB subtype undergoing R-CHOP treatment, and it may be linked to the transformation of follicular lymphoma (FL) to DLBCL. In fact, over 90% of transformed FLs (tFLs) exhibit the morphology of DLBCL, predominantly belonging to the germinal center B-cell (GCB) subtype [48], which carries a notably worse outcome [49]. Subsequently, cell line experimental data indicated that all three aforementioned genes were associated with the responsiveness of malignant human B-cell lines to R-CHOP therapeutic agents to some extent. Therefore, we speculate that HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4 may serve as potential antitumor targets in DLBCL, warranting further investigation.

We acknowledge certain Limitations to this study. Firstly, due to the Limitation of public datasets, the classification performance of 19-GPS and the prognostic value of NLRP4 gene are needed to be evaluated in a larger prospective data, including data generated by the NanoString nCounter system or PCR platform. Secondly, further experiments are required to elucidate the drug response mechanism of the three GCB genes HMCES, ZFAND4, and NLRP4 during R-CHOP immunotherapy.

To conclude, using large collections of DLBCL samples from public database, we identified and validated a qualitative transcriptional signature (19-GPS) for determining the COO subtype of DLBCL, which would be useful in COO classification and guiding prognosis for DLBCL patients.
