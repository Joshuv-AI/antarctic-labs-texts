Effect of inspiratory muscle training in patients with chronic heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Mengxuan Yang, Wei Huang, Zeruxin Luo, Xiu Zhang, Jianhua Su, Pengming Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effect of inspiratory muscle training (IMT) in chronic heart failure (CHF) patients remains unclear now. This study aimed to comprehensively identify the therapeutic effects of IMT among CHF patients based on current evidence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs).MethodsSeveral databases were searched up to January 2, 2024 for RCTs investigating the clinical application of IMT in CHF patients. Primary outcomes were maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and pulmonary function. Secondary outcomes were exercise performance, including the six-minute walk test (6MWT) and Borg dyspnea index, quality of life evaluated by the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLWHF) and N terminal-pro brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). Statistical analyses were conducted by the RevMan 5.3 software.ResultsFifteen RCTs with 494 cases were included in this meta-analysis. Pooled results demonstrated that IMT significantly increased the MIP [mean difference (MD) =16.36 cmH2O, 95% confidence interval (CI): 12.26 to 20.46, P<0.001] and VO2peak (MD =1.66 mL/kg/min, 95% CI: 0.27 to 3.05, P=0.02). Besides, patients receiving the IMT showed increased 6MWT (MD =37.40 m, 95% CI: 16.46 to 58.35, P<0.001) and decreased Borg dyspnea index (MD =−0.63, 95% CI: −0.83 to −0.44, P<0.001), MLWHF (MD =−8.51, 95% CI: −13.60 to −3.42, P=0.001) and NT-proBNP (MD =−81.67 pg/mL, 95% CI: −124.88 to −38.45, P<0.001).ConclusionsIMT plays a role in improving the clinical outcomes including the inspiratory muscle function, exercise performance, quality of life and NT-proBNP among CHF patients.

Key findings

• Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) could improve clinical outcomes including the inspiratory muscle function, exercise performance, quality of life and N terminal-pro brain natriuretic peptide in chronic heart failure (CHF) patients.

What is known and what is new?

• IMT has been reported to play a role in improving clinical outcomes for patients with various diseases such as the obstructive sleep apnea and chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases.

• Based on available evidence, IMT could significantly increase the maximal inspiratory pressure and VO2peak. Besides, patients receiving the IMT showed increased six-minute walk test (6MWT) and decreased Borg dyspnea index, quality of life and N terminal-pro brain natriuretic peptide.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study highlights the therapeutic effect of IMT for CHF patients. Thus, IMT should be considered as the adjuvant therapy for CHF patients to improve their clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a progressive, multifactorial disease with a high incidence, mortality rate, and readmission rate, imposing significant economic and psychological burdens on society and families (1,2). Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data from 2019 shows that there were approximately 37 million people worldwide in need of rehabilitation due to cardiovascular diseases (3). Among these, the largest proportion was attributed to heart failure (HF), affecting approximately 35 million people, followed by acute myocardial infarction, which affected about 1.3 million people (4). Currently, in China, the incidence of cardiovascular diseases is on a significant rise, with nearly 330 million individuals affected. Among Chinese adults aged 35 and above, the prevalence of HF is approximately 1.3%, equating to around 8.9 million HF patients. Between 2000 and 2015, the incidence of cardiovascular diseases increased by 44% (5). CHF has a higher overall incidence and mortality rate compared to both healthy individuals and other cardiovascular disease patients. Important risk factors contributing to CHF include diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and atherosclerosis (6,7).

Exercise intolerance and difficulty in breathing are the primary symptoms in patients with CHF, and they are also the major factors leading to a decreased quality of life in these patients. The mechanisms underlying these symptoms in HF patients include not only hemodynamic abnormalities but also changes in peripheral skeletal muscles (8,9). A significant body of research has confirmed that aerobic exercise can reverse this alteration and improve the exercise capacity of CHF patients (10,11). However, Nakagawa et al. have found that muscle weakness in CHF patients initially occurs in the respiratory muscles and then appears in the limbs (12). In CHF patients with respiratory muscle fatigue, symptoms of breathlessness become more pronounced, and exercise endurance further decreases. Therefore, effectively alleviating the weakness or fatigue of respiratory muscles is crucial for improving the clinical outcomes of CHF patients.

The role of inspiratory muscle training (IMT) in improving clinical outcomes for patients with various diseases such as the obstructive sleep apnea and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has been widely confirmed (13,14). Previous literature indicated that IMT may improve the clinical outcomes in patients with HF (15,16). However, these studies were published quite some time ago, and there is insufficient evidence regarding whether IMT could effectively improve outcomes for patients with CHF.

Therefore, we aimed to further determine the effect of IMT on CHF patients based on current evidence provided by randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which may contribute to the clinical application of IMT among CHF patients. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-519/rc) (17).

Methods

This work has been registered in INPLASY platform with the registration number of INPLASY202550062 (DOI: 10.37766/inplasy2025.5.0062; https://inplasy.com/?s=+INPLASY202550062).

Search strategy

The Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro), PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases were searched from inception to January 2, 2024 for available RCTs. Following terms were used during the search: inspiratory muscle training, IMT, chronic heart failure, CHF, randomized and randomly. Detailed search strategy was as follows: (inspiratory muscle training OR IMT) AND (chronic heart failure OR CHF) AND (randomized OR randomly). Meanwhile, MeSH terms and free texts were applied, with search fields restricted to tiles, keywords and abstracts. References in included studies were also reviewed for eligibility.

Inclusion criteria

Studies met following criteria were included: (I) patients were diagnosed with CHF with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or III and over 18 years old; (II) patients were randomly allocated to the IMT group or control group; (III) patients in the intervention group received IMT for at least 4 weeks and patients in the control group received sham IMT, traditional training or no intervention; (IV) primary outcomes were inspiratory muscle function evaluated by the maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and pulmonary function including the VO2peak, forced expiratory volume in one second % (FEV1%), forced vital capacity % (FVC%), FVC, FEV1/FVC, and maximal ventilator volume (MVV) and secondary outcomes were exercise performance including the six-minute walk test (6MWT) and Borg dyspnea index, quality of life evaluated by the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLWHFQ) and N-terminal pro brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP); (V) above outcomes were compared between the IMT and control groups providing specific data about the baseline and post-IMT values; (VI) articles were published in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria

Studies met following criteria were excluded: (I) other interventions were mixed; (II) duplicated, overlapped or insufficient data; (III) reviews, case reports, animal trials or non-RCTs; (IV) full texts were unavailable.

Duplicated publications were identified and excluded using the EndNote software. Two authors independently reviewed the literature according to above mentioned inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancy was resolved by team discussion. We screened titles and abstracts to exclude studies which were clearly irrelevant. Then we reviewed the full texts for detailed eligibility evaluation.

Methodological quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed according to the PEDro scale. Studies with a PEDro score of 6 or higher, 4 or 5 and 3 or lower were defined as high-, fair- and low-quality studies (18,19).

Data collection

Following information was extracted from included studies: the name of first author, publication year, country, number of cases, NYHA class level, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), age, parameters about IMT including the initial training pressure, training time, sessions and duration, control care and endpoints with corresponding detailed data.

The quality assessment and data extraction were also conducted by two investigators independently and all disagreements were resolved by team discussion.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted by RevMan 5.3 version software. Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated by the I2 statistic and Q test. When significant heterogeneity was observed representing as I2>50% and/or P<0.10, the random-effect mode was applied; or the fixed-effect model was used (20,21). Continuous data were compared and analyzed as the changes from pre-IMT values to post-IMT values. Mean differences (MDs) with standard deviations (SDs) were combined to calculate the MDs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) between the IMT and control groups. If data were presented as means and range values, then they would be converted to means and SDs according to the formula reported by Weiner et al. (22). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of IMT on primary outcomes among CHF patients

Primary outcomes included the MIP and pulmonary function. Night RCTs explored the impact of IMT on MIP in CHF patients. Pooled results indicated that the MIP of patients receiving IMT was significantly higher than that of patients in the control group (MD =16.36 cmH2O, 95% CI: 12.26 to 20.46, P<0.001; I2=46%, P=0.06) (Figure 2).

As for the pulmonary function, only the VO2peak was significantly increased in the IMT group compared with the control group (MD =1.66 mL/kg/min, 95% CI: 0.27 to 3.05, P=0.02; I2=96%, P<0.001) (Figure 3). No significant improvement in the FEV1% (MD =0.22%, 95% CI: −0.57% to 1.01%, P=0.59), FVC% (MD =0.65%, 95% CI: −1.34% to 2.65%, P=0.52), FEV1/FVC (MD =−0.75%, 95% CI: −5.90% to 4.40%, P=0.78), FVC (MD =0.12 L, 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.25, P=0.06) or MVV (MD =4.34 L, 95% CI: −5.50 to 14.19, P=0.39) (Table 3).

Effects of IMT on secondary outcomes among CHF patients

Secondary outcomes included the exercise performance, quality of life and NT-proBNP. According to the pooled results, IMT could significantly improve the exercise performance among CHF patients, representing as increased 6MWT (MD =37.40 m, 95% CI: 16.46 to 58.35, P<0.001; I2=78%, P<0.001) (Figure 4A) and decreased Borg dyspnea index (MD =−0.63, 95% CI: −0.83 to −0.44, P<0.001; I2=5%, P=0.39) (Figure 4B).

Discussion

This meta-analysis included 15 RCTs with 494 participants and demonstrated that IMT could improve clinical outcomes of CHF patients. In detail, patients who received IMT showed significantly improved respiratory muscle function, VO2peak, exercise performance, quality of life and NT-proBNP. Therefore, IMT is an effective measure for improving the clinical outcomes of CHF patients and is worthy of clinical promotion.

As CHF progresses, the main cause of cardiac dysfunction in patients is due to genetic variations in myocardial cells leading to changes in protein phenotype, resulting in ventricular remodeling and subsequent changes in neurohumoral factors. These changes further exacerbate myocardial remodeling, leading to a worsening of heart function. Additionally, due to autonomic nervous system dysfunction in CHF patients, sympathetic nerve activity reflexively increases, resulting in decreased heart rate variability (HRV). Both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves are damaged, leading to secondary delayed function of pressure receptors. As heart function deteriorates, parasympathetic nerve damage becomes more severe, and excessive activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) by an overactive sympathetic nervous system causes vasoconstriction and increases the afterload of the left and right ventricles, raising pulmonary artery pressure (38-40).

Studies by Mello and Tumminello and others have shown that IMT reduces resting muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) by approximately 15%. This can comprehensively improve the regulation of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems in the cardiovascular system of patients with CHF, increase cardiac output, and enhance heart function (28,41). IMT can improve the intrinsic efficiency of the heart, reducing myocardial oxygen consumption at a given metabolic rate, increasing venous return, left and right ventricular volumes, and consequently increasing end-diastolic volume (EDV) of the heart and improving ejection fraction (42). At the same time, the increase in venous return stimulates ventricular contraction, enhances sympathetic nerve drive, and the influence of catecholamines on the myocardium. Myocardial contractility increases, myocytes hypertrophy, stroke volume increases, and end-systolic volume (ESV) decreases. Therefore, IMT can reduce the impact of metabolic reflexes during rest and exercise, thereby reducing the effect of respiratory fatigue on peripheral blood flow (43,44). By improving the function of the heart and circulatory system, it can increase oxygen delivery to the working muscles.

Existing research still lacks sufficient evidence to confirm that IMT is an effective intervention that can significantly improve lung function. Not all CHF patients show a significant increase in lung function indicators after IMT. Our results have shown that IMT does not improve FEV1%, FVC%, FEV1/FVC or MVV in CHF patients. This may be related to factors such as the baseline lung function of the patients, medications, the method and intensity of IMT, and the sensitivity of different populations to the training, which can affect the stability and accuracy of the results. Therefore, further large-scale RCTs are needed in the future to investigate the impact of IMT on lung function in CHF patients. However, our study indicated that IMT could significantly increase the VO2peak of CHF patients. Mapelli et al. have demonstrated that in patients with HF, VO2peak is a key indicator for assessing exercise capacity and prognosis and low VO2peak is associated with worse outcomes, while increases in VO2peak may improve survival and quality of life (45). For example, HF patients with a VO2peak below 12.2 mL/kg/min had a 66% 1-year cardiac-related mortality rate, compared to 34% in those with higher values. Another study found that each 1 mL/kg/min increase in VO2peak was associated with a 12% improvement in prognosis (46,47). Above finding also indicated that IMT could improve the prognosis of CHF patients.

Actually, several meta-analyses have explored the clinical value of IMT in HF patients. Li et al. included 17 studies and demonstrated that IMT played a role in increasing VO2peak (MD =2.53 mL/kg/min, P<0.001) and MIP (MD =17.25 cmH2O, P<0.001) and improving the quality of life (MD =−13.34, P<0.001), but IMT did not significantly increase the 6MWT (P=0.09) or lung function (15). However, they did not conduct subgroup analysis based on the type of HF [acute HF (AHF) or CHF]. Sadek, Azambuja and Baral et al. performed similar meta-analyses as Li et al. did (48-50). Notably, Wu et al. included eight studies and showed that IMT improved MIP (MD =16.52 cmH2O, P<0.01), dyspnea (MD =−0.95, P<0.01), quality of life and 6MWT in 2018 (51). Our meta-analysis included 15 RCTs and provided higher-level evidence that IMT could effectively improve clinical outcomes in CHF patients.

There are several limitations in our meta-analysis. First, the overall sample size is relatively small and some studies included a limited number of subjects. Second, we were unable to perform more specific analysis based on some important parameters such as the age and LVEF due to the lack of original data. Third, the pooled analysis for the VO2peak and MLWHF showed significant heterogeneity, which might cause some bias. Four, the detailed strategies for IMT and control care differed in these RCTs.

Conclusions

In overall, IMT plays a role in improving the clinical outcomes including the inspiratory muscle function, exercise performance, quality of life and NT-proBNP in CHF patients.
