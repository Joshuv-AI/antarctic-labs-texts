Pathogenicity and vaccine protection of circulating pertactin- and filamentous hemagglutinin-deficient Bordetella pertussis strains
Authors: Hanying Dai, Xiuwen Sui, Jie Che, Chunying Zhang, Jiayu Zhao, Jie Li, Bohan Chen, Luyao Guo, Dongshan Yan, Hairui Wang, Xueping Liu, Li Xu, Yuan Gao, Aiping Qin, Tao Zhu, Maojun Zhang, Zhujun Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe increasing emergence of vaccine antigen-deficient Bordetella pertussis (Bp) variants has raised concerns regarding disease control and prevention. Here, we characterized the pathogenicity and vaccine protection of circulating pertactin-deficient (PRN−), filamentous hemagglutinin-deficient (FHA−), and double-deficient (FHA−PRN−) Bp isolates. In vitro assays assessed bacterial proliferation, autoaggregation, adhesion to and invasion of A549 cells, associated cytokine responses in THP-1 cells, and cell death induction in both A549 and THP-1 cells. In vivo, the lung colonization, pulmonary immune responses, and the protection of three pertussis-containing vaccines against antigen-deficient isolates were investigated in a murine model. The PRN– isolate exhibited WT-like proliferation, adhesion, invasion, and lung colonization, and induced markedly enhanced pro-inflammatory responses and increased A549 cell death. The FHA− and FHA−PRN− isolates showed faster in vitro growth, reduced autoaggregation, adhesion, invasion, and THP-1 cell death levels, similar lung colonization, alongside a slight increase in inflammatory cytokine induction compared with the WT strain. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the PRN− isolate induced a WT-like lung immune cell profile, whereas the FHA− and FHA−PRN− isolates induced a higher proportion of neutrophils, and FHA− infection was associated with reduced alveolar macrophage and dendritic cells. DTcP and DTaP vaccines provided distinct levels of protection against antigen-deficient isolates. Breakthrough infections were observed in 69.57% (16/23) vaccinated mice challenged with the FHA−PRN− strain. In conclusion, PRN and FHA deficiencies could alter some pertussis virulence-associated phenotypes and modulate host immune responses, thereby contributing to changes in vaccine protection.KEYWORDS: Bordetella pertussis, pertactin deficiency, filamentous hemagglutinin deficiency, pathogenicity, vaccine protection

Introduction

Pertussis, or whooping cough, is caused primarily by Bordetella pertussis (Bp) and remains a significant public health concern despite widespread vaccination [1]. Current trends indicate a global resurgence of pertussis, including in countries with high coverage of acellular pertussis vaccines (ACVs) [2–5]. In China, the reported incidence increased from 0.48 per 100,000 in 2015 to 33.59 per 100,000 in 2024, representing an approximately 71-fold increase over the past decade [1]. The resurgence of pertussis is multifactorial, with the waning of vaccine-induced immunity and genetic changes in Bp representing two of the major contributing factors [6]. In recent years, Bp isolates with mutations or deletions in one or more vaccine-related antigens have emerged worldwide [7–11]. The loss of the adhesin protein pertactin (PRN) is the most common manifestation in these isolates [12–14]. Clinical isolates lacking pertussis toxin (PT) or filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA) have been identified sporadically [11,15,16]. In China, FHA-deficient isolates were first reported in 2024 [15].

Accumulating evidence indicates that the vaccine-related antigenic profiles of circulating Bp isolates have diverged from those of the vaccine strains, including the international reference strain Tohama I and the China-licensed vaccine strain CS, both of which are characterized by the allelic profile ptxA2/ptxP1/prn1/fim3-1/fim2-1/fhaB1 [17–19]. In China, Bp isolates carrying the genotypes ptxA1/ptxP3/prn150/fim3-1/fim2-1/fhaB1 and ptxA1/ptxP3/prn150::IS481/fim3-1/fim2-1/fhaB1 have become predominant among circulating strains [15,20–24], indicating a shift away from the antigenic profiles of the vaccine strains. Recent findings suggest that these antigenic changes in Bp may lead to vaccine-induced immunity evasion [23,25], rendering disease control and prevention more challenging.

The widespread prevalence of PRN− strains and the emergence of FHA− Bp strains have increasingly raised public health concerns, as PRN and FHA are key antigenic components of ACVs [26]. However, limited information is available on their pathogenicity, immune responses, and vaccine protection. To address this knowledge gap, a comprehensive evaluation of circulating PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp strains was conducted, including their in vitro growth and autoaggregation; ability to adhere to, invade, and replicate in A549 cells; inflammatory cytokine responses in THP-1 cells; and apoptosis-inducing capacity in A549 and THP-1 cells. In addition, the in vivo lung colonization, pulmonary immune responses, and the protection of three pertussis-containing vaccines against PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp strains were evaluated in a mouse model. This study aims to elucidate how the loss of PRN and FHA influences bacterial virulence and vaccine protection, thereby providing critical insights into the evolution of circulating strains and supporting efforts to develop more effective disease control strategies and next-generation pertussis vaccines.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The strains used in this study, wild-type (WT, FHA+/PRN+), PRN-deficient (PRN−), FHA-deficient (FHA−), and FHA/PRN double-deficient (FHA−PRN−), were maintained in the National Institute for Communicable Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Genome-based typing (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/bordetella/) showed that all isolates shared an antigenic profile of ptxA1, ptxP3, fim3-1, and fim2-1. The WT strain carried fhaB1 and prn150, whereas the antigen-deficient strains exhibited deletions or disruptions in fhaB, prn150, or both (Supplementary Table 1). Characterization of these deficiencies has been described previously by our group and others [15,27].

A modified mouse intracerebral challenge assay reference strain (MICA) was provided by the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control (China), and the China-licensed vaccine strain (CS) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Supplementary Table 2). Bacteria were inoculated onto charcoal agar plates (CM119B, Oxoid, England) supplemented with 10% (v/v) defibrinated sheep blood (Y099, Sanmu Animal Husbandry, China) at 37°C for 3–5 days.

Verification of prn and fhaB transcription

Total RNA was extracted from WT, PRN−, FHA−, and FHA−PRN− Bp isolates using the Eastep® Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; LS1040), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcription levels of prn and fhaB were quantified by SYBR Green-based quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan; RR820) with gene-specific primer sets, with rpoB serving as an internal reference gene.

In vitro growth curve determination and autoaggregation assay

The growth curve determination was performed based on a published study [28]. Prior to the experiments, the relationship between OD600 and viable counts (CFU) was validated for all tested strains. A strong linear correlation was observed between OD600 and CFU counts, confirming that OD600 measurements reliably reflect bacterial density. 200 μL of fresh bacterial suspension with an optical density (OD) of 1.0 at 600 nm was inoculated into 4 mL of supplemented Stainer–Scholte (SS) broth (M2343; Tuopu Bioengineering, Shandong, China) and incubated at 37°C with 200-rpm orbital agitation for 96 h. OD600 was measured every 12 h from 100-μL culture aliquots.

Bacterial autoaggregation was assessed by incubating bacterial suspensions (OD600 = 1.0, OD0) at 37°C under static conditions and measuring OD600 from the upper phase at defined time points (ODt) [29]. Autoaggregation was calculated using the following equation: autoaggregation (%) = [1−(ODt/OD0)] × 100.

Bacterial adhesion, invasion, and intracellular replication assays

A549 cells are well-established as an in vitro model of human respiratory epithelial cells for evaluating bacterial adhesion, invasion, intracellular replication, and epithelial cell responses to Bp infection [30–33]. Based on established models, bacterial adhesion and invasion were quantified using a gentamicin protection assay as previously described [33], with minor modifications. 3.0 × 105 A549 cells (ATCC CCL-185, Supplementary Table 3) were seeded in 12-well culture plates (3524; Corning, USA) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (FSP500, ExCell Bio, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA) for 36 h. Cells were then washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10010023, Gibco, USA) and stimulated with bacteria at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. Infections were performed in antibiotic-free and serum-free medium to avoid any potential effects on bacterial viability. Plates were centrifuged at 300 ×g for 10 min, followed by 4 h of incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2.

For the adhesion assay, unattached bacteria were removed by washing cells twice with PBS after 4 h of infection. Cells were then lysed with sterile water at 37°C, and adherent bacteria were enumerated by the drop plate method [34] on supplemented charcoal agar plates for 3–7 days. The adhesion rate was calculated as the ratio of adherent bacteria to the initial bacterial count.

For the invasion assay, following the removal of unattached bacteria by washing, cells were incubated in fresh medium containing 50 µg/mL gentamicin (17581820; INALCO, France) for 1 h to kill non-invaded bacteria. Then, cells were washed and lysed for CFU counting as described above. The invasion rate was calculated as the ratio of intracellular bacteria to adherent bacteria.

For the intracellular replication assay, after an initial 4-h infection and subsequent 1-hour gentamicin treatment, cells were washed and incubated in fresh medium for the indicated times. CFU counting was performed as described above.

Cytokine levels determined by ELISA

THP-1 cells are widely used as a model of human monocytes/macrophages to assess immune responses and cytotoxicity in Bp infection [35–37]. THP-1 cells (ATCC TIB 202; Supplementary Table 3) were seeded in 12-well (8.0 × 105 cells/well) culture plates (712012; NEST, China) and incubated overnight in RPMI 1640 medium (C11875500BT; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 2% FBS without antibiotics. Cells were maintained in an undifferentiated state, without the use of PMA or other differentiation agents, before infection. Cells were then infected with Bp (MOI of 10) and co-cultured for the indicated periods. Culture supernatants were collected and analysed for cytokines and chemokines using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China), including IL-1β (E-EL-H0149), IL-6 (E-EL-H6156), IL-12 (E-EL-H0150), MCP-1 (E-EL-H6005), CXCL2 (E-EL-H1904), and G-CSF (E-EL-H0079).

Cell death detection assay

During bacterial infection, cell death was assessed as previously described [38]. Briefly, A549 cells (3.0 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well culture plates (Corning) for 36 h. THP-1 cells (8.0 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well culture plates (NEST) and incubated overnight in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2% FBS (Gibco) without antibiotics. Cells were then infected with Bp at an MOI of 50 for A549 cells and 10 for THP-1 cells. Then, cells were harvested at indicated times and evaluated for apoptosis/death using a FITC annexin V apoptosis detection kit (556547; BD Pharmingen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bacterial colonization of murine lungs

BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks, n = 4–6/group) were anaesthetized with isoflurane and intranasally inoculated with 1 × 106 CFU Bp in 100 μL sterile physiological saline as described previously [39]. Five days post-infection, murine lungs were collected, homogenized in PBS, and centrifuged. The supernatant was serially diluted for CFU counting. All procedures were compliant with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Communicable Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (2025-053).

Flow cytometric analysis of lung immune cell populations

BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks, n = 6/group) were infected with WT and antigen-deficient Bp as described above. After five days post-infection, the lung tissues were enzymatically digested, filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, and erythrocytes were lysed before staining to create single-cell suspensions. Cells were incubated with Zombie Aqua™ Fixable Viability Dye, followed by surface staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table 4).

Leukocytes were identified as CD45+ cells; neutrophils were defined as CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+ cells, Siglec-F+ neutrophils as CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+Siglec-F+ cells, alveolar macrophages (AM) were identified as CD45+CD11blowSiglec-F+F4/80+ cells, interstitial macrophages (IM) were defined as CD45+CD11bhighF4/80+ cells, dendritic cells (DCs) were identified as CD45+CD11c+MHCII+ cells.

Vaccination and B. pertussis challenge

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks, n = 6–8/group) were subcutaneously immunized with three acellular pertussis vaccines (DTcP1, DTcP2, and DTaP) on days 0 and 21 with test vaccines at one-tenth of the human dose (50 µL). Mice were then challenged with 1 × 106 CFU of Bp (100 µL) intranasally under light isoflurane anaesthesia on day 35. Five days later, murine lungs were collected, homogenized in PBS, and centrifuged to obtain supernatants for CFU counting. CFU > 0 in vaccinated murine lungs post-challenge was defined as a breakthrough infection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical calculations and visualizations were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.3.0). The significance of the differences between data value means was evaluated by ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001; ns (nonsignificant) indicates p ≥ 0.05.

Results

FHA deficiency promotes bacterial growth and impairs autoaggregation

Verification of prn and fhaB transcription was performed through quantitative real-time PCR analysis (Supplementary Figure 1). To investigate the phenotypic characteristics of circulating PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp strains, bacteria were inoculated into SS liquid medium. The FHA− and FHA−PRN− isolates displayed substantially accelerated growth compared with the WT strain, whereas the PRN− isolate demonstrated a modest reduction in growth (Figure 1(A)).

Autoaggregation and microcolony formation are thought to contribute to bacterial persistence and protection from the host immune system [29]. Therefore, the differences in autoaggregation between antigen-deficient strains and the WT strain were investigated subsequently. Autoaggregation of the PRN− strain remained comparable to the WT strain across 2–24 h. The FHA− strain showed similar autoaggregation to the WT strain at early time points (2 and 4 h), but a pronounced reduction emerged from 8 to 24 h. The FHA−PRN− isolate showed enhanced autoaggregation at 2 and 4 h, whereas a decline was observed at 12 and 24 h compared with the WT strain (Figure 1(B,C)).

FHA deficiency reduces adhesion, invasion, and intracellular replication in A549 cells

Successful bacterial infection relies on effective engagement with host epithelial cells, including adhesion and, where applicable, invasion, which represent critical early determinants of colonization and host immune activation [40]. Given the established roles of FHA and PRN in mediating bacterial-host interactions, we performed in vitro adhesion and invasion assays to evaluate how the loss of these antigens affects the early host cell interaction capacity of PRN- and FHA-deficient isolates in epithelial cells, A549 cells were used as the host (Figure 2). The results demonstrated that the FHA− and PRN−FHA− strains exhibited markedly reduced adhesion and invasion compared with the WT strain, whereas the PRN− strain showed levels comparable to those of the WT strain (Figure 2(A,B)). Notably, all strains were capable of intracellular colonization. The PRN− strain exhibited slightly enhanced intracellular replication compared with the WT strain, whereas replication of the FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains was markedly attenuated (Figure 2(C)). A further analysis showed that, relative to their initial intracellular CFU, PRN−, FHA−, and PRN−FHA− strains all exhibited markedly lower fold-increases in intracellular bacterial loads than the WT strain, particularly at the early stage of infection (24 h) (Figure 2(D)).

Deficiency of PRN and FHA differentially modulates cytokine and chemokine production in THP-1 cells

To further elucidate the host–pathogen interactions associated with the cellular inflammatory responses, cytokines and chemokine production were assessed from 24 to 72 h in THP-1 cells stimulated with PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp isolates and a WT isolate (Figure 3). The PRN− isolate elicited a markedly stronger pro-inflammatory response than the WT strain, with significantly elevated IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, MCP-1, CXCL2 and G-CSF from 24/48 to 72 h (Figure 3(A–F)). The FHA− isolate and FHA−PRN− isolate induced slightly stronger pro-inflammatory responses than the WT strain, which was characterized by slightly elevated IL-1β at 48 h (Figure 3(A)), moderately increased IL-6 and IL-12 from 48 to 72 h (Figure 3(B,C)), alongside decreased IL-1β at 72 h (Figure 3(A)) and G-CSF from 24 to 72 h (Figure 3(F)).

Antigen-deficient Bp isolates exhibit cell-type-specific modulation of host cell death

Because bacteria can modulate host cell apoptosis to increase bacterial persistence and immune evasion [41–43], we used flow cytometry to assess whether antigen deficiency altered the apoptotic responses of A549 and THP-1 cells to Bp infection (Figures 4 and 5). In A549 cells, the PRN− and FHA− strain induced significantly higher levels of cell apoptosis/death than the WT strain at 48 and 72 h (Figure 4(D)), including higher levels of early apoptosis (Figure 4(B)) and late apoptosis/death (Figure 4(C)). The circulating PRN−, FHA−, and FHA−PRN− strains exhibited proliferation in murine lungs comparable to that of the WT strain (Figure 6(A)), corresponding to a comparable recovery rate to that of the WT strain (Figure 6(B)). Flow cytometric analysis of lung immune cells demonstrated distinct antigen-specific immune cell profiles following infection (Figure 6(C–H)). Compared with WT infection, PRN− isolate induced a comparable proportion of leukocytes, AM, IM, and DCs in the lungs of infected mice (Figure 6(C–H)). In contrast, neutrophil infiltration was significantly elevated in lungs infected with FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains (Figure 6(D)), and a marked reduction in AM and DCs was observed exclusively in FHA−-infected lungs, while these populations were preserved in both PRN− and FHA−PRN− infections (Figure 6(F,H)).

FHA-deficient strains compromise vaccine protection and exhibit high rates of breakthrough infection

To investigate the vaccine protection of PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp strains, groups of mice were immunized twice by subcutaneous injection with a co-purified acellular pertussis vaccine DTaP (PT and FHA), component-purified acellular pertussis vaccine DTcP1 (PT, FHA, and PRN), and component-purified acellular pertussis vaccine DTcP2 (PT and FHA) before bacterial challenge. The experimental procedure is shown in Figure 7(A). Two weeks after the second immunization, mice were challenged intranasally with 1 × 106 CFU (100 µL) of MICA, CS, WT, PRN−, FHA−, and FHA−PRN− strains. Five days post-challenge, the bacterial burden of the challenged murine lungs was quantified by CFU counting (Figure 7(B–G)). The results showed that the PRN− strain exhibited slightly compromised vaccine protection compared to the WT strain (Figure 7(D,E)), and the FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains displayed significantly impaired vaccine protection relative to the WT strain (Figure 7(D,F,G)). Notably, breakthrough infections occurred in 69.57% (16/23) mice challenged with the FHA−PRN− strain (Figure 7(G) and Table 1).

In addition, the results showed that DTcP1 and DTcP2 conferred superior protection against the PRN− strains compared with DTaP (Figure 7(E)). DTcP1 and DTaP provided significantly higher levels of protection against the MICA, WT, and FHA− strains relative to DTcP2 (Figure 7(B–F)). Overall, DTcP1 showed a higher level of protection against newly emerging B. pertussis variants than DTcP2 and DTaP.

Discussion

The rising prevalence of Bp isolates carrying mutations or deletions in vaccine-related antigens [35,48–50] has raised increasing concerns for disease control and prevention. Studies characterizing the pathogenicity and vaccine protection of antigen-deficient isolates are limited. Here, the pathogenicity and vaccine protection of PRN- and FHA-deficient isolates were investigated through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assays. The results indicated that FHA-deficient isolates exhibited enhanced proliferation but reduced autoaggregation, epithelial adhesion, invasion, and intracellular replication. Antigen-deficient strains also elicited distinct cytokine responses and apoptosis patterns in epithelial and immune cells. In vivo, PRN- and FHA-deficient strains displayed similar lung colonization, distinct lung immune cell responses, and different vaccine protection compared to WT. These findings highlight the impact of antigen loss on host–pathogen interactions and vaccine responses, underscoring the need for continuous surveillance and optimization of acellular pertussis vaccines.

Consistent with previous reports identifying FHA as a key mediator of Bp autoaggregation [44], both FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains exhibited significantly reduced autoaggregation compared with the WT strain. Bacterial autoaggregation is often regarded as a crucial initial step in the establishment of structured biofilms [29], and the formation of surface-adherent biofilm communities in the respiratory tract plays a significant role in ensuring long-term tissue adherence [45,46]. FHA is essential for the formation of stable biofilms on ciliated surfaces [45,47] and facilitates Bp attachment to lung epithelial cells [48–50]. In line with these observations, loss of FHA in our experiments was associated with reduced adhesion to A549 cells and diminished invasion efficiency. Although PRN has been reported to facilitate host cell adhesion in earlier studies [51,52], PRN deficiency alone did not significantly affect adhesion and invasion in our assay. While PRN has been implicated in Bp adherence to ciliated respiratory epithelium based on rabbit tracheal explant studies, subsequent murine in vivo experiments failed to demonstrate a significant role for PRN in bacterial colonization [52], highlighting model-dependent differences. Moreover, the FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains displayed comparable adhesion and invasion phenotypes in our assay. Together, these findings indicate that FHA plays a predominant role in mediating Bp attachment to lung epithelial cells, whereas the contribution of PRN appears to be accessory and potentially context dependent.

Interestingly, while the FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains exhibited accelerated growth in SS medium compared with the WT and PRN− strains, their proliferation in A549 cells was significantly decreased. The discrepancy between extracellular and intracellular proliferation of FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains may reflect the dual roles of FHA and PRN. The biosynthesis of these large surface-associated proteins is metabolically costly, and their absence may confer a growth advantage in nutrient-rich broth. Conversely, FHA and PRN function as major adhesins that mediate attachment to respiratory epithelial cells. The loss of these factors not only diminished bacterial adhesion and invasion but also impaired intracellular proliferation. Together, these findings suggest that the structural and functional integrity of surface adhesins is critical for the pathogenicity of Bp. Accordingly, FHA remains a promising target for novel therapeutics, including anti-adhesion agents or next-generation vaccines that disrupt early colonization [48].

In addition, we assessed the cytokine responses of THP-1 cells following infection with WT and antigen-deficient Bp strains. The PRN− isolate triggered stronger pro-inflammatory responses than the WT strain. Our findings are consistent with those of a recently published study, which suggested that PRN may contribute to pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression [53]. The PRN− strain was shown to induce elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, supporting the concept that PRN deficiency may produce exaggerated pro-inflammatory signalling in host cells [53]. The FHA− and FHA−PRN− isolates also induced slightly stronger pro-inflammatory responses than the WT strain, which was marked by moderately increased IL-6 and IL-12, and reduced G-CSF. The decrease in G-CSF suggested impaired neutrophil recruitment, potentially promoting bacterial persistence within the host. These findings suggest that PRN and FHA critically mediate host–pathogen interactions and shape the host immune response. The results indicated that while adhesin deficiency reduced direct bacterial interactions with epithelial cells, it simultaneously altered host innate immune responses, identifying a potential trade-off between colonization efficiency and host immunity modulation. Antigen-deficient strains may enhance their adaptability by altering inflammatory patterns and immune regulation, thus achieving persistent circulation and immune evasion.

Previous studies have shown that many pathogens, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and pathogenic Escherichia coli, can induce host cell apoptosis to avoid host defenses [54–56]. Bp toxins, including pertussis toxin and adenylate cyclase toxin, have also been implicated in this process [57–59]. Our findings extend this concept by demonstrating that deficiency of surface adhesins, specifically PRN and FHA, is also associated with marked and cell-type-specific alterations in host cell apoptosis. In respiratory epithelial A549 cells, PRN− and FHA− Bp isolates induced markedly higher apoptosis compared with the WT strain. In line with our in vitro observations, transcriptomic analysis of lung tissue from mice infected with a Bp Prn knockout strain revealed upregulation of genes involved in cell death pathways [53], supporting the physiological relevance of enhanced epithelial death during infection. Together, these findings suggest that PRN and FHA deficiencies may indirectly aggravate airway pathology by promoting epithelial damage. Accumulating evidence from both pulmonary and intestinal infection models indicates that extensive epithelial cell death can disrupt barrier integrity while simultaneously releasing soluble intracellular nutrients, thereby generating a permissive tissue microenvironment that bacteria can metabolically exploit to support persistence, even in the absence of increased pathogen burden [60,61]. Therefore, such extensive epithelial cell apoptosis induced by Bp infection could trigger the disruption of the airway barrier, potentially reducing mechanical clearance of the bacteria because of ciliary damage, and facilitating the release of intracellular nutrients into the local environment to fuel bacterial persistence and growth [46].

In contrast, PRN−, FHA−, and FHA−PRN− isolates induced significantly lower levels of cell death in THP-1 macrophage-like cells compared with the WT strain. Macrophages play a central role in bacterial clearance and orchestration of innate and adaptive immune responses. Suppression of macrophage apoptosis may therefore represent an immune evasion strategy that allows Bp to prolong host cell survival, thereby avoiding immune detection or creating a permissive intracellular niche for bacterial persistence. This observation was consistent with previous reports showing that FHA alone induced apoptosis of activated T lymphocytes, particularly Th1 cells [62], suggesting that FHA-dependent apoptosis contributes to immune regulation. Additionally, recent in vitro evidence demonstrates that avirulent or antigen-altered Bp phenotypes exhibit enhanced survival and metabolic adaptation within macrophages, highlighting reduced host cell cytotoxicity as a key mechanism facilitating intramacrophage persistence and immune evasion [36]. Together, deficiencies of PRN and FHA may thus attenuate apoptosis of immune cells, reshaping the local immune microenvironment toward reduced inflammatory damage while allowing sustained bacterial presence.

In the mouse infection model, the PRN− isolate demonstrated colonization comparable to that of the WT strain. This finding was consistent with a previous report demonstrating that the bacterial burden in the lungs of unvaccinated mice was similar when infected with either PRN+ or PRN− Bp strains [63]. Although the FHA− and FHA−PRN− isolates exhibited markedly reduced adhesion and invasion capacities in vitro, the in vivo lung colonization efficiency was comparable to that of the WT strain. Given that the FHA− isolate induced stronger epithelial cell apoptosis than the WT isolate, the FHA−PRN− isolate elicited a response similar to that of the WT strain, indicating that the epithelial-damaging potential of the FHA-deficient strains appeared largely unaffected and thus exhibited similar lung colonization ability with the WT isolate. Therefore, although the absence of PRN and FHA attenuated infectivity, particularly bacterial adherence and invasion, it may not substantially compromise the overall virulence of Bp during its interactions with host cells.

The in vivo lung immune cell profiles provide a coherent cellular framework that integrates the distinct host responses elicited by antigen-deficient Bp isolates observed in vitro. The PRN− isolate induced a comparable immune cell profile to that of the WT strain, with similar proportions of total leukocytes, neutrophils, AM, IM, and DCs to the WT isolate. Notably, this apparently unaltered cellular recruitment contrasted with the pronounced pro-inflammatory response observed in vitro, where PRN− infection of THP-1 cells resulted in markedly elevated production of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, MCP-1, CXCL2, and G-CSF. Taken together, these findings suggest that effective control of PRN− bacteria in vivo is achieved not through increased immune cell recruitment, but rather through enhanced inflammatory activation of resident immune cells within the pulmonary environment. While studies regarding Prn-deficient Bp strains induced immune responses are limited, our finding is consistent with a previous study showing that the loss of PRN resulted in stronger pro-inflammatory responses [53]. In contrast, FHA− and FHA−PRN− infections were characterized by significantly increased neutrophil accumulation in the lungs of infected mice, indicating engagement of alternative or compensatory inflammatory pathways in vivo that are independent of robust IL-1β or G-CSF signalling. Our results are in line with an early study that suggests FHA-deficient B. bronchiseptica strains upregulated the recruitment of neutrophils to the lungs during the first four days post-inoculation [64]. Notably, a significant reduction in alveolar macrophages and dendritic cells was observed exclusively in FHA−-infected lungs, consistent with previous reports showing that Bp actively suppresses host immunity by impairing alveolar macrophage antimicrobial activity and limiting dendritic cell migration to lymphoid tissues [65]. Moreover, this phenotype aligns with emerging evidence that Bp can adopt an avirulent, low-cytotoxic state to facilitate adaptation to the intramacrophage environment, thereby promoting persistence rather than overt inflammatory clearance [36]. Together, these findings indicate that PRN and FHA deficiencies differentially modulate the pulmonary immune landscape in a manner that favours bacterial persistence in hosts.

Challenge experiments in vaccinated mice demonstrated that the FHA-deficient strains exhibited significantly compromised vaccine protection compared with the WT strain, as evidenced by the number of breakthrough infections in mice challenged with the FHA− and FHA−PRN− strains. The breakthrough infections observed with the FHA−PRN− strains may be attributed to the combined loss of prn and fha, which results in a complete abrogation of bacterial surface binding by acellular pertussis vaccine-induced serum antibodies [63]. Besides, our findings indicate that DTcP and DTaP vaccines provided distinct levels of protection against these antigen-deficient isolates. DTcP2 does not contain the PRN component, and the antibodies that exert specific protective functions are limited to PT- and FHA-specific antibodies [66,67]. Consequently, when mice were challenged with FHA− strain, FHA-specific antibodies were potentially unable to effectively bind to the bacteria, and protection mediated solely by PT-specific antibodies was insufficient. In contrast, PRN-specific antibodies induced by DTcP1 likely compensated for the function of FHA-specific antibodies, resulting in fewer breakthrough infections and indirectly supporting the protective role of PRN-specific antibodies. In mice challenged with PRN− strain, both DTcP1 and DTcP2 provided complete protection, indicating that FHA- and PT-specific antibodies offer strong protective efficacy even when PRN-specific antibodies are not functional. A notable degree of individual heterogeneity was observed in mice vaccinated with DTaP, potentially arising from uneven antigenic epitope exposure associated with the co-purification process used in DTaP1 manufacturing. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that antigen-deficient strains can partially evade host immunity and that vaccine-induced protection depends on the presence of key antigens [63].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, we analysed a limited number of representative strains, which may not fully capture the genetic and phenotypic diversity of circulating Bp. Secondly, the antigen-deficient isolates used in this study were naturally occurring clinical strains and not genetically engineered mutants. Although aside from the target prn and fhaB loci, the four strains share identical allelic profiles for both housekeeping genes and key virulence determinants, further gene complementation assays to restore prn and fhaB in the deficient strains could rigorously confirm the direct link between the loss of these antigens and the observed phenotypic changes. Thirdly, the bacterial colonization was evaluated only in the lungs, whereas Bp initially colonizes the upper respiratory tract. Nevertheless, lung bacterial burden is a well-validated and clinically relevant endpoint for assessing vaccine efficacy in mouse models, reflecting protection against lower respiratory tract infection and severe disease. Future studies incorporating upper airway colonization will further clarify vaccine effects on mucosal immunity and transmission. Moreover, as the assessment of vaccine fitness was conducted in a murine model, further in-depth researches are warranted to comprehensively elucidate the vaccine protection of circulating PRN- and FHA-deficient Bp isolates in humans.

From a biological and clinical perspective, our findings suggest that the emergence of FHA- and/or PRN-deficient Bp strains may influence both pathogenic behaviour and vaccine-mediated control of infection. This study provides valuable insights for understanding pertussis resurgence and for optimizing vaccine antigen composition. Future studies employing targeted gene deletion and complementation, expanded strain panels, and models addressing upper respiratory tract colonization and transmission will be essential to fully elucidate the roles of FHA and PRN in pertussis pathogenesis and to guide rational vaccine design. In summary, PRN and FHA deficiencies could alter some pertussis virulence-associated phenotypes and modulate host immune responses, thereby contributing to changes in vaccine protection, underscoring the importance of continuous genomic surveillance and rational optimization of next-generation pertussis vaccines.
