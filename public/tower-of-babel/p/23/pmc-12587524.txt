Association between the blood urea nitrogen-albumin ratio and mortality in critically ill patients with gastrointestinal bleeding: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xingyi Yang, Mengyuan Chen, Mengyin Zhu, Yiling Ying, Lang Gao, Lihong Lv, Xiaoxiao Wang, Xiangshu Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) represents a life-threatening medical emergency. This study investigates the prognostic utility of the Blood Urea Nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR) in patients with GIB.MethodsPatients were stratified according to BAR levels. The association between BAR and 28-day and 1-year mortality was examined using Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS), and Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis. Comprehensive subgroup analyses were further conducted to evaluate consistency across clinically relevant strata.ResultsIn this study, a total of 1624 patients were included. Elevated BAR was an independent predictor of higher mortality at 28 days (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04) and 1 year (HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03; both P < 0.001). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis indicated moderate discrimination for mortality prediction. Restricted cubic splines suggested a linear dose-response relationship between BAR and mortality. Significant interactions were observed with acute kidney injury (AKI) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). A nomogram incorporating BAR and other predictors achieved AUCs of 0.805 (0.779–0.832) for 28-day and 0.789 (0.765–0.814) for 1-year mortality.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that an elevated BAR independently predicts mortality in patients with GIB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04385-x.Keywords: Gastrointestinal bleeding, Blood urea nitrogen, MIMIC-IV database, Mortality

Introduction

Gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) is a common, high-risk emergency with heterogeneous etiologies [1]. Clinical presentations—including hematemesis, melena, or fecal occult blood—vary depending on the bleeding site and severity [2]. Upper gastrointestinal bleeding has an annual incidence of approximately 67 per 100,000 individuals, significantly higher than lower gastrointestinal bleeding (36 per 100,000) [3]. The mortality rate of GIB ranges from 5% to 10% [4]. Advanced age, comorbidities (e.g., liver cirrhosis or renal dysfunction), and physiological derangements such as hypotension or hyperlactatemia are independent predictors of mortality [5–7].

Massive hemorrhage carries a significant risk of life-threatening complications, including hypovolemic shock, acute kidney injury (AKI), myocardial ischemia, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [8–11], all requiring prompt resuscitation and intensive care. The management of severe bleeding typically involves a multimodal approach incorporating blood product transfusions, endoscopic hemostasis, vasoactive support, and often prolonged hospitalization, which significantly strains healthcare systems [12, 13]. Given the unpredictable disease progression, early and precise risk stratification is crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes.

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), a routinely assessed parameter of renal function, reflects not only glomerular filtration but also extrarenal influences such as dehydration, protein catabolism, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage [14]. In GIB patients, BUN elevation primarily results from intestinal absorption of nitrogenous blood breakdown products [15]and diminished renal perfusion secondary to hypovolemia [16]. Notably, marked BUN increases may predispose patients to AKI [17]. Concurrent hypoalbuminemia exacerbates physiological derangements by reducing intravascular oncotic pressure while activating anti-inflammatory responses and oxidative stress during ischemia-reperfusion injury [18, 19]. These pathological cascades subsequently impair cellular metabolism and accelerate multiple organ dysfunction progression [20].

The Blood Urea Nitrogen-to-albumin ratio (BAR), integrating both BUN and Albumin (ALB) measurements, has emerged as a reliable prognostic marker across multiple clinical contexts, including acute pancreatitis, sepsis, and pneumonia [21–23]. Elevated BAR values consistently correlate with worse clinical outcomes, and higher mortality rates in various systemic disorders. Nevertheless, its prognostic utility specifically for GIB patients remains inadequately characterized. This study seeks to (1) assess the prognostic value of BAR in GIB patients, and (2) establish the optimal BAR threshold for early detection and risk stratification of high-risk cases in clinical practice.

Database and methods

Database introduction

We conducted our analysis using the MIMIC-IV database, an openly available source of de-identified ICU patient records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, USA. The dataset comprises de-identified clinical records, spanning 2008–2022 and encompassing over 90,000 intensive care unit (ICU) admissions. Institutional review board approval was exempted due to the anonymized nature of the data. The lead investigator (Xingyi Yang, Record ID: 67058598) completed all required data use certification procedures prior to database access and analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study cohort was sourced from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database. Using the International Classification of Diseases codes, we initially identified 4576 patients with gastrointestinal bleeding. During the screening phase, we excluded patients who met any of the following criteria: not admitted to the ICU for the first time, with an ICU stay shorter than 24 h, and with missing data on blood urea nitrogen or albumin. Following these exclusions, a total of 1624 patients were ultimately included in the analysis (see Fig. 1 for details).

A flow diagram of study participants

Data collection and monitoring

This study collected comprehensive clinical and demographic data, including patient characteristics (age, sex, race), vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature), and documented comorbidities, specifically heart failure (HF), myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic heart disease (IHD), cirrhosis, diabetes, AKI, and sepsis. Laboratory parameters comprised white blood cell (WBC) count, Red boold cell distribution width (RDW), platelets, hemoglobin, albumin, BUN, anion gap, bilirubin, creatinine, glucose, potassium, and sodium, with values averaged over the first 24 h of ICU admission and emphasis on the BAR. Disease severity was quantified using Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) scores, while key interventions such as mechanical ventilation (MV) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) were systematically recorded. The primary endpoints were 28-day and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with gastrointestinal bleeding.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data normality was verified by Shapiro-Wilk testing, with normally distributed variables expressed as mean ± SD (analyzed by t-test) and non-normal variables as median [Interquartile Range (IQR)] (Mann-Whitney test). Categorical data were tabulated by count (percentage).

For missing data management, variables with over 10% incomplete values were omitted, whereas those with 10% or fewer missing entries were processed through multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE).

To systematically assess the potential threshold effect of the BAR on clinical outcomes, patients were stratified into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on BAR levels using R software (version 4.3.0): Q1 (BAR < 6.73, n = 405), Q2 (6.73 ≤ BAR < 11.2, n = 412), Q3 (11.2 ≤ BAR < 18.6, n = 397), and Q4 (BAR ≥ 18.6, n = 410).

Covariate selection followed a two-step procedure: variables associated with 28-day mortality in univariate Cox analyses (P < 0.05) were entered into the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, and only predictors that remained statistically significant after adjustment (P < 0.05) were retained in the final model. In addition, clinically important covariates, including MBP/INR, cirrhosis, use of mechanical ventilation, and basic demographic characteristics, were also incorporated irrespective of their statistical significance. BAR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and by quartiles (Q1 as the reference).

To further explore the impact of BAR on survival outcomes, K–M curves and log-rank tests were used to assess survival differences across BAR thresholds. Additionally, ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of BAR, BUN, albumin, SOFA, AIMS65, as well as combined indices including SOFA + BAR and AIMS65 + BAR. RCS were employed to investigate potential nonlinear dose-response relationships between BAR and mortality outcomes. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, race, sepsis, AKI, cirrhosis, diabetes, HF, MI, IHD, CRRT, and MV were conducted to examine potential interaction effects.

Finally, the Boruta algorithm identified the five most robust predictors of 28-day mortality, which were integrated into a prognostic nomogram. To address the issue of multiple comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni adjustment, setting the significance threshold at P < 0.004 (0.05/12) for both Cox regression models and subgroup analyses. All statistical procedures were carried out using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Significant variations were observed in vital signs: systolic blood pressure decreased from 124 mmHg (IQR 108–138) in Q1 to 114 mmHg (IQR 100–132) in Q4, whereas no statistically significant difference in heart rate was observed across quartiles (P = 0.057). Laboratory parameters followed distinct trends, including declining levels of hemoglobin (9.73 g/dL in Q1 vs. 8.70 g/dL in Q4), albumin (3.20 g/dL in Q1 vs. 2.70 g/dL in Q4), and rising creatinine levels (0.80 mg/dL in Q1 vs. 2.60 mg/dL in Q4).

The prevalence of comorbidities increased with higher BAR quartiles, particularly for diabetes (20.2% in Q1 to 38.3% in Q4), heart failure (16.5% in Q1 to 43.4% in Q4), and ischemic heart disease (16.5% in Q1 to 42.7% in Q4). Critical care interventions such as mechanical ventilation (71.9% in Q1 to 82.2% in Q4) and CRRT (5.2% in Q1 to 20.0% in Q4) were more frequently utilized in higher quartiles.

Mortality outcomes worsened progressively: 28-day mortality increased from 8.6% in Q1 to 27.8% in Q4 (P < 0.001), while 1-year mortality rose from 26.7% to 37.3%. Additionally, both ICU length of stay and hospital stay increased with increasing BAR quartiles.

Association between BAR and all-cause mortality

To rigorously examine the association between the BAR and clinical prognosis, we developed three progressively adjusted Cox proportional hazards models.

28-day mortality

Cox regression analyses demonstrated that each unit increase in continuous BAR was consistently associated with an elevated mortality risk across all models (Model 1, adjusted for age and gender: HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04, P < 0.001; Model 2, adjusted for age, gender, race, MBP, RDW, and SIRS: HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.03, P < 0.001; Model 3, adjusted for age, gender, race, MBP, RDW, WBC, INR, SIRS, sepsis, cirrhosis, and MV: HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03, P < 0.001). When analyzed by quartiles, progressively higher mortality risks were observed with ascending BAR quartiles compared with Q1 (reference). In Model 1, significantly increased hazards were observed for Q2 (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.22–2.82, P = 0.004), Q3 (HR 3.10, 95% CI 2.09–4.60, P < 0.001), and Q4 (HR 3.89, 95% CI 2.63–5.76, P < 0.001). These associations remained significant in Model 2 (Q2: HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.10–2.55, P = 0.016; Q3: HR 2.70, 95% CI 1.81–4.02, P < 0.001; Q4: HR 3.14, 95% CI 2.12–4.67, P < 0.001) and in Model 3, significant associations were observed for Q3 (HR 2.02, 95% CI 1.36–3.02, P < 0.001) and Q4 (HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.39–3.09, P < 0.001), whereas Q2 did not reach statistical significance. All models exhibited significant dose-response trends between BAR quartiles and mortality risk (P for trend < 0.001).

365-day mortality

In the Cox proportional hazards regression analyses, BAR showed consistent associations with mortality. When analyzed as a continuous variable, BAR exhibited significant associations with an increased mortality risk across all models. Specifically, the HR values were 1.03 (95% CI 1.02–1.04, P < 0.001) in Model 1 (minimally adjusted), 1.02 (95% CI 1.02–1.03, P < 0.001) in Model 2 (partially adjusted), and 1.02 (95% CI 1.01–1.02, P < 0.001) in Model 3 (fully adjusted). When categorized into quartiles, higher BAR levels demonstrated a clear dose-dependent relationship with mortality (all models, P for trend < 0.001). Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the second quartile (Q2) showed an elevated mortality risk in Model 1 (HR 1.66, 95% CI 1.20–2.30, P = 0.002) and Model 2 (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.10–2.12, P = 0.011), but the association for Q2 was not statistically significant in Model 3. The highest quartiles (Q3 and Q4) maintained strong associations even after full adjustment, with HR values of 1.75 (95% CI 1.28–2.40, P < 0.001) and 1.83 (95% CI 1.34–2.51, P < 0.001), respectively. However, the effect sizes showed a progressive attenuation with additional covariate adjustments. Details are shown in Table 3.

Analysis of Kaplan-Meier and ROC curves

K-M analysis

K-M analysis demonstrated a significant inverse association between the BAR and survival probability (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Survival rates exhibited a graded reduction with increasing BAR quartiles, with Q1 (lowest BAR) showing the highest survival probabilities and Q4 (highest BAR) demonstrating the poorest outcomes at both short- and long-term follow-up periods.

ROC Curves

ROC analysis demonstrated that the BAR had moderate prognostic discrimination, with an AUC of 0.649. Similarly, for 1-year mortality, BAR remained predictive (AUC = 0.635). The combination of BAR with established scoring systems further improved predictive performance, yielding the highest AUC values for 28-day mortality (SOFA + BAR: 0.774; AIMS65 + BAR: 0.684) and 1-year mortality (SOFA + BAR: 0.742; AIMS65 + BAR: 0.671) (Fig. 3; Table 4).

Restricted cubic spline analysis

RCS regression analyses demonstrated a linear dose-response relationship between BAR levels and mortality risk (both 28-day and 1-year mortality). Risk of death increased progressively with higher BAR values (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linearity > 0.05; Fig. 4).

Subgroup analyses of BAR on clinical outcomes in GIB patients

Subgroup analysis identified significant interactions between the BAR and mortality at both 28 days and 1 year among patients with acute kidney failure (P < 0.001) and those undergoing CRRT (P = 0.004). However, no significant effect modification was observed in other subgroups, including age, sex, race, sepsis, cirrhosis, diabetes, HF, MI, IHD, or MV (Fig. 5).

Predictor screening and nomogram model construction

To develop a clinically applicable prediction model, we utilized the Boruta algorithm to identify the five most influential predictors: (1) SOFA score, (2) sepsis status (present/absent), (3) total bilirubin level, (4) anion gap, and (5) BAR value (Figure S1). Based on these variables, we constructed a point-based nomogram to estimate individualized 28-day and 1-year survival probabilities (Fig. 6). To use the nomogram, clinicians locate a patient’s value for each predictor, assign the corresponding points, sum the points across all predictors, and then map the total points to the predicted probabilities for 28-day and 1-year mortality on the nomogram’s risk scales. For bedside use, we also deployed an online calculator that mirrors the nomogram and allows users to input the five variables to obtain individualized risk estimates: https://prediction-risk.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/. The calculator displays predicted probabilities and facilitates clinical communication and documentation. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curve analysis and showed good discriminative ability, with AUC values of 0.805 (95% CI: 0.779–0.832) for 28-day mortality and 0.789 (95% CI: 0.765–0.814) for 1-year mortality (Figure S2).

Discussion

In recent years, increasing interest has focused on new biomarkers for risk stratification in GIB. A higher hemoglobin-to-RDW ratio has been reported to correlate strongly with greater mortality risk in patients with GIB [24]. Moreover, inflammatory indicators, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and various platelet-based measures, have demonstrated value in assessing disease severity among critically ill individuals with GIB [25, 26].Despite these advances, there remains a clear clinical need for simple, rapid, and reliable assessment tools to support disease monitoring and guide therapeutic decision making.

Our study demonstrates that the serum BAR is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with gastrointestinal bleeding (P < 0.001). Cox regression analyses stratified by BAR quartiles revealed that higher BAR values were significantly associated with increased mortality. Consistent with this finding, K-M survival curves confirmed a progressively worse prognosis with rising BAR levels (log-rank P < 0.001). Furthermore, temporal assessment of BAR demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy for mortality, complementing conventional clinical scoring systems such as SOFA and SIRS. RCS analysis indicated a linear dose-response relationship between BAR and mortality risk (P for nonlinearity >0.05). To enhance the clinical applicability of BAR, we implemented the Boruta algorithm for feature selection and developed a prognostic model integrating BAR with four other important clinical predictors. This model exhibited good predictive performance (AUC: 0.805). Subgroup analyses suggested that BAR’s prognostic value remained consistent across most subgroups, though effect modification was observed in patients with AKI and those undergoing CRRT. The observed interactions may stem from (1) reduced BUN excretion in AKI, leading to elevated serum BUN levels, coupled with inflammation-induced hypoalbuminemia due to altered renal filtration [27, 28]; and (2) increased BUN clearance during CRRT, potentially accompanied by albumin loss, affecting BAR interpretation [29].

In comparison with gastrointestinal bleeding–specific risk scores, our analysis demonstrated that BAR had discriminative performance comparable to AIMS65 [30], with similar AUC values for 28-day and 1-year mortality and no significant difference by DeLong’s test. This suggests that BAR, despite being derived from only two routine laboratory parameters, may provide prognostic information of similar magnitude. However, given the single-center retrospective nature of our study, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and we cannot conclude that BAR is equivalent to or can replace AIMS65. Other established scores, such as the Rockall score [31], the Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS) [32], and AIMS65, are guideline-recommended, as they incorporate comorbidities, hemodynamic status, and endoscopic findings, thus providing a more comprehensive assessment of bleeding severity and rebleeding risk. Comparative studies have shown that AIMS65 performs well for mortality prediction, while GBS is particularly effective in identifying patients who require urgent intervention or transfusion, and Rockall adds prognostic value when endoscopic data are available. In the ICU setting, however, such information is often unavailable during the early phase, and in our cohort the absence of systematic endoscopic data precluded direct calculation of Rockall or GBS. Taken together, BAR should be considered a complementary rather than a replacement tool: it can provide rapid and objective risk signals when information is incomplete, and may enhance prognostic accuracy when used alongside AIMS65, Rockall, or GBS once full clinical information becomes available. These outputs are intended to complement—rather than replace—clinical judgment; higher predicted probabilities indicate higher mortality risk and can support early risk stratification and management planning.

The BAR has recently been recognized as a valuable prognostic marker across various clinical conditions. Among ICU patients with acute pancreatitis, retrospective evidence indicated that a BAR value greater than 16.92 was strongly linked to higher rates of all-cause mortality (ACM). The AUC for BAR on day 14 was 73.23%, notably higher than that of BUN (69.73%) or albumin alone (64.29%) [33].In 137 patients with community acquired pneumonia (CAP), Asano et al. found a positive correlation between BAR and 28-day in-hospital mortality, with an AUC of 0.83 [34].Similarly, Cao et al. identified BAR as an independent predictor of mortality in sepsis (HR = 1.032 95% CI: 1.010–1.055) [23].Collectively, these findings across infectious, pancreatitis, and pneumonia highlight BAR as a robust and broadly applicable biochemical marker. Its integrative nature and consistent predictive performance continue to attract increasing clinical attention.

In patients with GIB, an elevated BAR arises from multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Gastrointestinal hemorrhage leads to increased intestinal absorption of protein degradation products, such as amino acids. Within the liver, these amino acids undergo transamination, generating ammonia that is further metabolized into urea, contributing to elevated BUN levels [35, 36]. Additionally, upper GIB frequently induces hemorrhagic hypovolemia, predisposing patients to prerenal azotemia [37]. Elevated BUN levels have been strongly associated with mortality in GIB [38]. Under hypovolemic conditions, diminished renal perfusion triggers neurohormonal activation—including sympathetic nervous system stimulation and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system upregulation—enhancing proximal tubular urea reabsorption and further increasing BUN levels [39]. Consequently, in hemodynamically unstable patients, BUN not only reflects renal perfusion but also serves as a proxy for neuroendocrine activation.

A decreased serum albumin level is a well-recognized marker of physiological stress, malnutrition, or chronic inflammation [40, 41]. In acute hemorrhage, sympathetic nervous system activation and the release of inflammatory mediators markedly increase capillary permeability, facilitating albumin extravasation and a consequent rapid decline in circulating albumin levels [42, 43]. Furthermore, systemic inflammation—driven by cytokines such as interleukin-6—suppresses hepatic albumin synthesis [44].

The BAR combines two essential biomarkers— BUN and albumin—into a single integrative metric. Unlike isolated BUN or ALB measurements, BAR more comprehensively reflects systemic perturbations, including acute hemorrhage, metabolic dysregulation, and inflammatory stress. Given its sensitivity to these pathophysiological derangements, an elevated BAR serves as an efficient, cost-effective tool for early risk stratification and disease severity assessment, as it is readily calculable from routine laboratory data. Clinically, a high BAR value may signal increased risk, prompting closer monitoring and timely intervention, while a low BAR may indicate stability, potentially reducing unnecessary interventions. Due to its simplicity and accessibility, BAR has significant potential to improve risk assessment and optimize resource allocation in clinical practice.

While this large-scale, population-based study provides valuable clinical insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center design, despite utilizing comprehensive real-world data, may limit the generalizability of findings to diverse geographic populations. Second, the lack of systematic assessment of disease severity and etiologic factors could introduce heterogeneity in subgroup analyses. Additionally, by focusing exclusively on all-cause mortality without causal attribution, the study does not account for the multifactorial nature of critical illness mortality. The restriction to ICU patients may further compound selection bias. Future multicenter studies incorporating standardized severity assessments and refined analytical approaches would help address these limitations.

Conclusion

The BAR demonstrates a significant association with clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with GIB and may serve as a valuable prognostic indicator. However, large-scale, prospective multicenter studies are required to further validate its clinical utility.
