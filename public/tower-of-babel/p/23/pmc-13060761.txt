Association Between Obesity Status and Hypertension Phenotypes: Are Inflammatory Indicators the Missing Link? Evidence From a Large Population Study
Authors: Xue Li, Huanhuan Miao, Zhanyang Zhou, Zheng Yin, Yuqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypertension exhibits variability in diagnosis and treatment across phenotypes. Obesity and metabolic disorders are key risk factors, interacting with inflammatory states. This study explores their associations with hypertension phenotypes and the mediating role of inflammation. This study analyzed 17 158 participants from NHANES (2007–2018). Hypertension phenotypes were diagnosed per guidelines. Associations were evaluated using weighted generalized linear models, with the receiver operating characteristic(ROC) curves identifying optimal obesity indices. Stratified analyses were conducted by gender, lifestyle, and diet. Mediation analysis assessed inflammation's role. Among six obesity indicators, Weight‐Waist Index (WWI) was better for Isolated Systolic Hypertension (ISH) (area under curve[AUC]:0.64, 95%confidence interval[CI]:0.62–0.67), Waist Circumference (WC) was better for Isolated Diastolic Hypertension (IDH) (AUC:0.68, 95%CI:0.67–0.69). High obesity indicators (WC for IDH, WWI for ISH and WC for SDH) without metabolic disorders linked to IDH (odds ratio[OR] 3.02, 95% CI 1.57–5.82), not ISH or Systolic‐Diastolic Hypertension (SDH) (p > 0.05). High obesity indicators (WC for IDH, WWI for ISH and WC for SDH) with metabolic disorders associated with all phenotypes (IDH: OR 3.62, 95% CI 2.19–5.97; ISH: OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09–2.84; SDH: OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.25–3.34). Inflammation mediated partially: 6.44% via RBC in IDH (non‐obesity metabolic disorders, P < 0.001), 18.4% via Monocyte in ISH (obesity without metabolic disorders, P < 0.05). Stratified analyses showed phenotype‐specific differences: IDH nonsignificant, ISH by smoking, SDH by age. High WC without metabolic disorders is linked to IDH, while metabolically disordered obesity correlates with all phenotypes. These effects are mediated by distinct variables, aiding phenotype‐specific diagnosis and treatment.Keywords: hypertension phenotypes, inflammation, mediation analysis, metabolic disorders, NHANES, obesity

Introduction

Hypertension affects over 1.2 billion people globally, contributing to a 56.1% rise in premature deaths over the past decade [1]. Rates of awareness, treatment, and control remain stagnant from 2017–2020 to 2021–2023 [2]. Recent guidelines emphasize phenotype‐specific management, categorizing hypertension into Isolated Systolic Hypertension (ISH), Isolated Diastolic Hypertension (IDH), and Systolic‐Diastolic Hypertension (SDH). These phenotypes vary in age distribution, risk factors, clinical management, and long‐term impacts, requiring tailored approaches [3]. Enhanced understanding and prevention strategies for different hypertension phenotypes are crucial to address this global health challenge.

Obesity, affecting over 600 million globally, significantly raises hypertension risk via sympathetic nervous system, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS), and immune‐inflammatory activation, with insulin resistance playing a key role [4]. Indicators like Body Mass Index(BMI), Waist Circumference(WC), waist‐to‐height ratio(WHtR), Weight‐Waist Index(WWI), Body Roundness Index (BRI), and Relative Fat Mass Index (RFM) assess obesity [5]. Hypertension often involves metabolic disturbances, including dysregulated glucose, lipids, and uric acid [6]. Obesity and metabolic status must be jointly considered in hypertensive patients. Obesity drives chronic low‐grade inflammation, releasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines like TNF‐α and IL‐6, while metabolic disturbances exacerbate hypertension through sympathetic over‐activation, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation [7].

Current research highlights obesity and metabolic disturbances as key hypertension risk factors, with metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO) individuals showing the highest hypertension risk, followed by metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) and metabolically unhealthy non‐obesity (MUNO) phenotypes [8, 9]. However, their impact on different hypertension phenotypes remains unclear. This study emphasizes phenotype‐specific diagnosis, aiming to identify optimal obesity indicators for predicting hypertension phenotypes, assess obesity and metabolic disturbances’ distinct effects, and explore blood cell indices’ mediating roles across obesity and metabolic states.

Methods

Research Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018, employing a stratified, multistage random sampling design to ensure national representativeness [10]. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained. Exclusion criteria included age <18, pregnancy, missing data on blood pressure, complete blood count, body measurements, lipid profile, blood glucose, uric acid, covariates, and antihypertensive drug use. After exclusions, 17 158 participants were included for comprehensive statistical analysis (Figure 1).

Definition of Obesity Indices

Following standardized procedures, skilled healthcare professionals at mobile examination centers (MEC) measured body height (BH), body weight (BW), and WC using rigorous techniques and state‐of‐the‐art equipment. Indicators were calculated using the following formulas [5, 11]:

BMI = BW (kg) / BH2 (m)

BMI = BW (kg) / BH2 (m)

WHtR = WC (cm) / BH (cm)

WHtR = WC (cm) / BH (cm)

WWI = WC (cm) / BW^(1/2) (kg)

WWI = WC (cm) / BW^(1/2) (kg)

BRI = 364.2−365.5 * sqrt (1−(WC / (2 * π * BH / 2))^2) (cm)

BRI = 364.2−365.5 * sqrt (1−(WC / (2 * π * BH / 2))^2) (cm)

RFM = 64−(20*BH/WC) + (12*gender), where gender = 1 for female and 0 for male.

RFM = 64−(20*BH/WC) + (12*gender), where gender = 1 for female and 0 for male.

Definition of Metabolic Disorders

Any abnormality in blood lipid, blood glucose, or blood uric acid was considered indicative of a metabolic disorder.

We adhered to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (2002) guidelines to identify participants with hyperlipidemia based on the following criteria: ① total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 200 mg/dL, ② triglycerides (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dL, ③ low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) ≥ 130 mg/dL, or ④ high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) ≤ 40 mg/dL (for males) or ≤ 50 mg/dL (for females). The specific procedures for measuring these indices are outlined on the NHANES Laboratory Methods webpage.

Glucose dysregulation [12] was defined as impaired fasting glucose (IFG) in the range of 100–125 mg/dL, abnormal HbA1c (5.7%–6.4%), or elevated 2‐h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) levels in the range of 7.8–11.0 mmol/L.

After examining serum uric acid levels at MEC, NHANES researchers defined hyperuricemia [13] as serum uric acid levels ≥ 7 mg/dL for males and ≥ 6 mg/dL for females.

Assessment of Mediators

Mediating variables were selected based on routine blood test results related to inflammatory status, including red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), platelets (PLT), neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, the Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI), and C‐reactive protein (CRP). SIRI was calculated as (monocyte count × neutrophil count) ÷ lymphocyte count [14]. CRP was measured using latex‐enhanced turbidimetry or particle‐enhanced immunoassay methods.

Covariates

The following covariates were included: age, gender, race, education level, income status based on the poverty‐to‐income ratio (PIR) (≤1.3, 1.31–3.5, >3.5), physical activity (PA) (<600 MET‐min/week, 600–3,000 MET‐min/week, ≥3000 MET‐min/week), smoking status, alcohol consumption, energy, sodium, and potassium intake collected during the initial 24‐h dietary recall interview at the MEC, serum albumin (g/dL), serum creatinine (mg/dL), and cardiovascular disease (CVD). CVD was determined based on self‐reported questionnaire results for heart failure (MCQ 160b), coronary heart disease (CHD) (MCQ 160c), angina (MCQ 160d), heart attack (MCQ 160e), and stroke (MCQ 160f). If any of these conditions were reported as “yes,” CVD was classified as “yes”; otherwise, it was “no.”

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and weighted percentages; continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as weighted means and weighted standard deviations, while non‐normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as weighted medians and weighted interquartile ranges. Differences between groups were analyzed using the chi‐square (x 2) test or Kruskal‐Wallis H test. A statistically significant result was determined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05.

Weighted generalized linear models were used to analyze the relationship between different obesity indicators, obesity status, and hypertension phenotypes. Weighted univariate generalized linear models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to identify the optimal obesity indicators for different hypertension phenotypes. Weighted multivariate generalized linear models were used to assess the relationship between different obesity statuses WCD and hypertension phenotypes. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, and income status; Model 3 further adjusted for PA, energy, sodium, and potassium intake, serum albumin, serum creatinine, smoking, alcohol consumption, and CVD. Stratified analyses were conducted using fully adjusted weighted generalized linear models to examine differences across strata, stratified by gender, PA, smoking status, alcohol consumption, age (weighted median), energy intake (weighted median), sodium intake (weighted median), and potassium intake (weighted median).

Mediation analysis models were employed to explore whether inflammatory status acts as a mediating factor in the relationship between different obesity statuses WCD and hypertension phenotypes. Traditional mediation analysis is based on parametric regression assumptions and typically estimates three types of effects: (I) the total effect (the overall impact of the independent variable X on the dependent variable Y, including both direct and indirect effects); (II) the direct effect (the residual effect of the exposure on the outcome, operating through pathways other than the mediator); and (III) the indirect effect (the pathway from exposure to outcome through the mediating variable). In all mediation analyses, adjustments were made for the following covariates: age, gender, race, education level, PIR, PA, energy intake, sodium intake, potassium intake, serum albumin, serum creatinine, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and history of CVD. When the total, direct, and indirect effects are in the same direction, it is feasible to calculate the “mediation ratio,” which quantifies the proportion of the total effect attributable to the mediating variable. The significance of the mediation effect was tested using the Sobel test. At the same time, the hypothesis concerning the significance of the mediating effect was tested using a bootstrap sample (n = 1000) [15]

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

This study recruited 17 158 participants, including 503 With IDH, 1556 With ISH, and 733 With SDH. Compared to other groups, IDH patients were younger, had higher sodium intake, larger BMI and WC, higher counts of WBC, RBC, PLT, and neutrophils, and a higher proportion of males, alcohol consumers, and never‐smokers. ISH patients were relatively older, had higher WWI, BRI, and RFM, higher counts of WBC and neutrophils, lower education levels, lower PA, a higher proportion of smokers, and lower PIR. All hypertension groups exhibited a higher prevalence of metabolic disorders (Table 1).

Optimal Obesity Indicators for Different Hypertension Phenotypes

Among the six obesity indicators examined (BMI, WC, WHtR, WWI, BRI, and RFM), the optimal indicator for IDH was WC (AUC = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.62–0.67), for ISH was WWI (AUC = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.67–0.69), and for SDH was WC (AUC = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.61–0.65) (Supplementary 1).

This study included 503 IDH participants. Since ROC analysis indicated that WC was the more suitable obesity index for differentiating IDH subjects from normotensive controls, we stratified the population using the weighted median of WC (95.1 cm) to ensure group balance. The final sample sizes for these groups were 40, 104, 38, and 321, respectively. Compared to D1, the D4 group was older, had higher energy intake, larger WC, higher RBC counts, and higher mean SBP. Characteristics of the ISH With Various WCD Status

This study included 1556 ISH participants. Since ROC analysis indicated that WWI was the more suitable obesity index for differentiating ISH subjects from normotensive controls, we stratified the population using the weighted median of WWI (10.78 cm/kg^1/2) to ensure group balance. This resulted in low and high WC groups, which were then cross‐classified with metabolic status into four categories (D1, D2, D3, and D4). The final sample sizes for these groups were 67, 296, 106, and 1087, respectively. Mediation Analysis

In this study, both obesity and metabolic disorders influenced inflammatory status (Supplementary 4). However, in IDH, RBC and monocytes played a primary mediating role. The proportion of the mediation effect through monocytes in D4 was 3.24%, while the proportion mediated by RBC across WCD states ranged from 2.74% to 6.44% (with D2 showing the highest mediation proportion through RBC). In SDH, RBC, WBC, neutrophils, and monocytes were the primary mediators. The mediation proportions through RBC in D2 and D4 were 10.36% and 6.72%, respectively; through WBC in D2 and D4 were 1.1% and 1.62%, respectively; through neutrophils in D4 was 2.42%; and through monocytes in D4 was 7.54% (Figure 5). In ISH, WBC, neutrophils, and monocytes were the primary mediators. The proportion of the mediation effect through WBC across WCD states ranged from 1.48% to 3.71% (with D3 showing the highest proportion), and the proportion mediated by neutrophils ranged from 1.73% to 6.68% (with D3 showing the highest proportion). The mediation proportions through monocytes in D3 and D4 were 18.4% and 10.08%, respectively (Figure 6). Additionally, CRP and SIRI also played mediating roles in ISH and SDH (Supplementary 5, 6). On the other hand, the indirect effects of inflammatory mediator variables calculated using the Bootstrap method were consistent with those obtained from the Sobel test across IDH (Supplementary 9 and Supplementary 8), ISH (Supplementary 11 and Supplementary 10), and SDH (Supplementary 13 and Supplementary 12). Additionally, the proportion of the mediating effect of inflammatory variables calculated via the Bootstrap method (Supplementary 14) was similar to the results derived from the Sobel test, indicating that the mediating effect of inflammatory variables in this study is relatively stable.

To ensure the robustness of the study findings, the weighted average values of obesity indicators (WC for IDH, WWI for ISH and WC for SDH) were used as grouping thresholds to redefine WCD status and reanalyze its association with hypertension phenotypes. The results remained consistent (Supplementary 7).

Discussion

This study analyzed data from 17 158 participants in the NHANES 2007–2018 dataset, including 503 cases of IDH, 1556 cases of ISH, and 733 cases of SDH. The findings highlight that the WWI is a superior indicator for ISH, while WC is more effective for IDH. High obesity indicators (WC for IDH, WWI for ISH and WC for SDH) without metabolic disorders positively correlates with IDH but shows no significant relationship with ISH and SDH. Conversely, high obesity indicators (WC for IDH, WWI for ISH and WC for SDH) with metabolic disorders exhibits a significant positive correlation with all hypertensive phenotypes, partially mediated by inflammatory status.

Among the six obesity indicators examined, WWI emerged as superior for ISH, while WC was more effective for IDH. This is attributed to WC reflecting central obesity, whereas WWI considers both central and overall body shape [5]. A previous study [16] showed that obesity alters hemodynamics, with peripheral obesity linked to higher cardiac output (CO) and lower systemic vascular resistance (SVR), while central obesity is associated with lower CO and higher SVR.

In our study, WC proved superior to WHtR for IDH, likely because WC directly indicates abdominal and visceral fat accumulation, a strong hypertension risk factor [17]. WHtR, though accounting for height, may be less intuitive. Additionally, this study did not explore gender‐stratified optimal obesity indicators [18].

WWI outperformed WHtR for ISH, as WWI better predicts hypertension risk and is strongly linked to metabolic syndrome and CVD, especially in older populations [5]. WHtR has limitations in older individuals due to reduced height or muscle mass [19].

Previous studies challenge the notion that MHO is benign, showing its association with elevated hypertension risk. A meta‐analysis [20] found the highest hypertension risk in MUO, with MHO also significantly elevated. This underscores the need for clinicians to evaluate both metabolic and weight indicators. A Chinese study [21] found MHO more detrimental to prehypertension and hypertension progression, particularly in younger individuals. Our study further stratified hypertension phenotypes, identifying MHO primarily influences IDH, increasing its risk. A cross‐sectional analysis [22] indicated obesity was significantly associated with IDH. A U.S. study [23] found increased BMI a significant predictor of IDH or SDH, aligning with our findings that MHO increases IDH risk, reinforcing the obesity‐IDH association.

Compared to MHO, metabolic disturbances, advanced age, and smoking are more pronounced in ISH and SDH populations. ISH is common in older adults with higher arterial stiffness, leading to increased systolic blood pressure. SDH reflects increased SVR and arterial stiffness. Research indicates MHO individuals are less prevalent in older populations and have lower cardiovascular risk than MUO individuals, but higher than normal‐weight individuals, with weaker associations with arterial stiffness [24]. MHO individuals, due to normal metabolic markers and better insulin sensitivity, may not significantly impact vascular function and blood pressure regulation, showing no significant correlation with ISH or SDH.

Our study found obesity with metabolic disorders increases various hypertension phenotype risks. Specifically, for IDH, the risk ratio was higher for obesity with metabolic disorders than MHO. A Chinese study [9] showed MUO had the strongest hypertension association, indicating both metabolic dysfunction and obesity are significant hypertension risk factors. A prospective cohort study [25] found both metabolically healthy and unhealthy obesity elevate hypertension risk, with the latter notably higher. Our research confirmed this through hypertension classification analysis.

Metabolic disorders without obesity showed no statistically significant association with ISH, IDH, or SDH risks. A prospective cohort study [8] of Chinese children and adolescents found hypertension risk associated with MHO rather than MUNO, supporting those metabolic disorders without obesity do not significantly increase hypertension risk. However, a meta‐analysis [20] identified metabolic abnormalities as an independent hypertension risk factor. A Chinese prospective cohort study [26] found individuals with metabolic abnormalities, regardless of weight status, had a significantly higher hypertension risk than the metabolically healthy non‐overweight/obesity group. The discrepancy in our findings may stem from several factors, including the focus on specific hypertension phenotypes, smaller sample sizes, and different definitions of metabolic disorders. Notably, the aforementioned articles adopted the International Diabetes Federation criteria [26] for metabolic syndrome, which excludes serum uric acid. Additionally, the population of our study was not further subdivided based on underlying mechanisms of metabolic disorders. Different mechanisms underlie metabolic disorders, which may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. For instance, lipid and glucose dysregulation contribute to hypertension primarily through insulin resistance [27], whereas uric acid influences blood pressure via inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to endothelial dysfunction [28]. This heterogeneity in mechanisms may lead to the dilution of individual effects.

In IDH patients, RBCs were a significant mediating variable, with metabolic disorders without obesity exerting a substantial proportion of their mediating effect through RBCs. In ISH patients, the WBC system was more prominent, with MHO exerting a larger proportion of its mediating effect through the WBC system.

A review [29] showed pro‐oxidants and adipocytokines in metabolic syndrome (MetS) alter RBC morphology, reduce RBC deformability, and increase whole blood viscosity (WBV). Elevated WBV, attributed to RBC aggregation, is associated with insulin resistance, central obesity, and hypertension. A study [30] of middle‐aged Japanese found erythrocytosis significantly associated with MetS, and a cohort study [31] reported higher RBC counts in the hypertension group than the control group, explaining RBCs’ mediating role in IDH patients with metabolic disorders.

A review [32] highlights adipose tissue promotes pro‐inflammatory factors secretion, attracting inflammatory cell infiltration. Leptin binds to receptors on monocytes and macrophages, promoting their proliferation and inflammatory cytokine expression. Neutrophils, expressing short‐form receptors, have delayed apoptosis and enhanced chemotaxis. Leptin influences other immune cells, broadly regulating immune responses. Immune cells and cytokines contribute to immune activation in hypertension [33], elevating blood pressure and inducing vascular remodeling. A study [34] found higher neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) levels in ISH and SDH patients than the normotensive control group, with NLR an effective indicator of brachial‐ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), suggesting more pronounced early arterial stiffness in ISH and SDH patients, elucidating obesity's mediation of ISH through immune status.

In our stratified analysis, smoking status (both past and current) impacted ISH more than obesity combined with metabolic disorders. In SDH patients, advanced age exerted a greater influence than obesity combined with metabolic disorders. Tobacco [35] and age [36] primarily contribute to vascular damage, leading to arterial stiffness, which dominates blood pressure changes.

This study has several strengths. First, we explored associations between obesity, metabolic disorders, their combined effects, and hypertension phenotypes using a nationally representative large‐sample dataset. Second, among numerous obesity indicators, we identified effective ones for distinct hypertension phenotypes. Additionally, we analyzed the significant impact of obesity and metabolic disorders on different hypertension phenotypes, providing a basis for phenotype‐specific diagnosis and treatment. Finally, we investigated the mediating role of inflammatory status, offering new insights into underlying mechanisms.

However, our study has limitations. First, the observational design precludes definitive causal relationships. Second, findings from U.S. adults may not fully represent other populations. Third, although we adjusted for many potential confounders, unmeasured confounders cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, future research is needed to validate the pathophysiological explanations provided and conduct intervention experiments to verify their effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study found that MHO is only positively associated With IDH, while MUO is positively associated With IDH, ISH, and SDH. In IDH patients, metabolic disturbances Without obesity exert a larger mediating effect Through RBC; whereas in ISH patients, MHO has a larger mediating effect Through the white blood cell lineage, which indicates that different obesity states involve distinct mediating variables in their impact on hypertension phenotypes. These findings suggest that different hypertension phenotypes require attention to different risk factors, providing a basis for phenotype‐specific diagnosis and treatment.
