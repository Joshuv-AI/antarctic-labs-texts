Higher drug overdose mortality among non-hispanic black adults aged 55 and older in the U.S.: Analysis of national death records from CDC's WONDER database (1999–2023)
Authors: Zongbo Li, Yanwei Jin, M.Kumi Smith, Bruce R. Schackman, Erika L. Crable, Czarina N. Behrends, Karla Wagner, Alexander Y. Walley, Brandon D.L. Marshall, Xiao Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older experienced the sharpest increases in drug overdose mortality from the mid-2010s onwards. We aimed to elucidate longitudinal patterns of rising overdose mortality among this population and identify key substances driving this trend.MethodsWe analyzed national death records from CDC's WONDER database (1999–2023) to examine unintentional drug overdose death rates across racial/ethnic and age groups, focusing on non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older. We examined trends by substance type and census region (i.e., Northeast, Midwest, South and West), and introduced a metric named contribution ratio (CR)—defined as the ratio of the change in overdose mortality involving a specific substance to the overall change in drug overdose mortality—to quantify the contributions of different substances to the increase in overdose mortality.ResultsSince 2015, drug overdose mortality among non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older increased more than among younger Black adults, older adults of other racial/ethnic groups, and younger adults of all racial/ethnic groups, many of whom experienced more modest increases during this period. By 2019, this group had the highest overdose death rate nationwide. CR analyses showed that cocaine and its co-involvement with fentanyl contributed most to the mortality increases among this population. Regional patterns varied, with methamphetamine-related deaths emerging in the U.S. West. Overdose mortality involving cocaine without opioid co-involvement rose steadily among older non-Hispanic Black adults across all regions, potentially reflecting heightened age-related vulnerability.ConclusionsThe rapid rise in overdose mortality among non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older—largely associated with cocaine with and without fentanyl—signals an urgent public health crisis requiring targeted interventions.

Introduction

Since 1999, drug overdose deaths have risen in the United States (US), with an estimated 80,000 deaths occurring in 2024 despite recent declines (Jalal et al., 2018, CDC/National Center for Health Statistics, 2025). The overdose crisis has evolved through four distinct “waves” marked by changing predominant substances: prescription opioids, heroin, illicit manufactured fentanyl and other synthetic opioids, and most recently, polysubstance deaths involving fentanyl-stimulant combinations (Friedman and Shover, 2023, Ciccarone, 2021). In 2023, approximately 40% of drug overdose deaths involved both fentanyl and stimulants—up from less than 1% in 2010 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). The current fourth wave has disproportionately impacted racial/ethnic minorities, with Black Americans now experiencing higher overall and cocaine-related overdose mortality than White populations (Rawy et al., 2024, Slavova et al., 2023, Friedman et al., 2021, Friedman et al., 2024, Han et al., 2021, Ray et al., 2020, Barocas et al., 2019, Smith et al., 2024).

Another concerning trend is the sharp rise in overdose mortality rates among non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older (hereafter “older” adults). Their mortality rates are increasing more rapidly compared to rates among non-Hispanic Black adults under age 55 (hereafter “younger” adults) and older adults of other racial/ethnic groups (Mason et al., 2022, Jones et al., 2023, Monnat, 2022, Volkow, 2024, Friedman et al., 2024). This trend has been reported in multiple East Coast jurisdictions, including New York City (NYC Health: NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2023), Baltimore (Zhu et al., 2024), and Connecticut (Wu et al., 2022). Several factors related to cohort effects (e.g., shared social, cultural, and/or historical experience within a birth cohort that lead to distinct effects for that cohort) (Friedman et al., 2024) and structural racism have been proposed to explain the increase. Additionally, research identifies higher doses of prescription opioids for chronic pain among older adults (Han et al., 2017) and lower rates of initiating medications for opioid use disorder among the older population (Ganz et al., 2025). While recent studies have examined overdose mortality among Black or older adults (Alexander, Monica et al., 2018, Friedman et al., 2024, Gondré-Lewis et al., 2023, Jones et al., 2023, Kiang et al., 2021, Kyei and Leveille, 2023, Mason et al., 2022, Smith et al., 2024, Yunusa et al., 2024), many have focused on specific substances (e.g. opioids, without considering other drugs), presented the national-level trends without regional breakdowns, or examined either racial/ethnic or age-related disparities, without addressing their intersections.

In this study, we analyzed the publicly available national drug overdose death records from 1999 to 2023, providing a detailed characterization of trends in drug overdose deaths with a focus on older non-Hispanic Black adults. We compared the trends of overdose mortality rates across intersectional racial/ethnic and age subgroups, specific substances involved, and census regions. To quantify the relative contribution of specific substances to rising overdose deaths, we proposed a metric named the contribution ratio (CR), which measures the ratio of the increase in overdose mortality involving a specific substance to the increase in overall drug overdose mortality within a given population and time period. Our objective was to elucidate the longitudinal patterns of rising overdose mortality among older non-Hispanic Black adults and to identify the key substances driving this trend.

Methods

We obtained aggregated death records from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC’s WONDER) database for 1999–2023 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). This population-level database captures all deaths occurring in the US and is not subject to sampling variability.

We identified drug overdose deaths using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). We selected codes specific to the underlying cause of death, including unintentional drug poisoning (X40–44). We focused on unintentional drug overdoses as they constitute the vast majority of drug poisoning deaths and are most directly targeted by overdose prevention efforts. Specific substances were determined using multiple cause of death codes: heroin (T40.1), fentanyl (T40.4), all opioids (T40.0-T40.4, T40.6), cocaine (T40.5), and methamphetamine (T43.6). We also identified co-involvement of cocaine with fentanyl, methamphetamine with fentanyl, and heroin with fentanyl using “AND” queries in CDC’s WONDER data interface. We examine these three fentanyl co-involvement combinations to capture the major polysubstance patterns across the third and fourth waves of the overdose crisis that have disproportionately affected racial/ethnic minority populations. Deaths involving cocaine without opioids were calculated by subtracting the number of deaths involving both cocaine and any opioid (identified using 'AND' queries in CDC WONDER) from the total number of cocaine-involved deaths. Detailed methods for data manipulation and category definitions are provided in the Appendix Table A1.

For race/ethnicity, we used the definition in CDC’s WONDER data (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025, Smith et al., 2024). Racial/ethnic groups were defined first by ethnicity (Hispanic Origin, yes vs. no) and subsequently by race. From 1999–2017, race was categorized into four groups; from 2018 to 2023, six race categories were available. Based on these classifications, we defined the following mutually exclusive racial/ethnic groups: non-Hispanic White (White), non-Hispanic Black (Black), non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native (AI/AN), Hispanic or Latino (any race) (Hispanic), and other race/ethnicities (i.e., Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, more than one race, and race/ethnicity not stated or available) (Other). We further stratified the first four groups (excluding Other) by age into two categories: younger than 55 years old (“younger”), and 55 years old and older (“older”). The Other group was not stratified due to small counts. In total, nine subgroups were analyzed.

Our primary focus was on trends among older Black adults, with the remaining groups included for comparison. We examined the following trends of age-adjusted drug overdose death rates from 1999 to 2023, categorized by: 1) type of substance involved and demographic subgroup, and 2) type of substance involved and demographic groups further stratified by four U.S. Census regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West). Age-adjusted rates were calculated by applying age-specific death rates to the 2000 U.S. standard population. Rates were computed by extracting the number of unintentional drug poisoning deaths — categorized by substance type, racial/ethnic group, and region — for ten-year age groups within the younger and older categories. To identify significant temporal shifts in mortality trends, we conducted joinpoint regression analysis using age-adjusted rates obtained from the CDC WONDER database. This approach allowed for the estimation of specific breakpoints and the calculation of Annual Percent Changes (APC) for each identified period.

To assess the contribution of specific substances to the rise in drug overdose mortality, we proposed a new metric called the contribution ratio (CR), inspired by the concept of the population attributable fraction (PAF) (Mansournia and Altman, 2018). The PAF is a metric widely used in epidemiology to quantify the proportion of a disease or death outcome that is attributable to a particular exposure, typically expressed as a percentage. PAF is calculated by taking the difference between the observed number of cases and the expected number of cases under no exposure, and then dividing that difference by the observed number of cases (Mansournia and Altman, 2018). We defined CR conceptually analogous to PAF; however, due to a lack of data on changes in exposure levels for specific substances, we used observed changes in age-adjusted death rates involving specific substances as a proxy for changes in exposure to the substances within a defined time period. In particular, CR is calculated using the following CRperiod=ΔdeathratespecificdrugΔdeathratealldrugΔdeathrate=Δdeathrateend−Δdeathratestart

CR represents the ratio of the change in overdose mortality involving a specific substance to the overall change in drug overdose mortality within a given population. Unlike PAF, which is bounded between 0 and 1, CR can exceed 1 (when change in mortality involving a specific substance surpasses the net change in total overdose mortality). This metric enables the identification of key substances that may be driving increases in overall drug-related deaths, with larger CR indicating a greater contribution.

We applied the CR to assess the contributions of various substances to drug overdose death rate increase across six demographic groups, excluding the AI/AN and Other groups (counts of deaths fewer than 10 were suppressed for confidentiality reasons in CDC’s WONDER), with a particular focus on the older Black adults. We calculated the CR over two periods when the overdose death rate among older Black individuals showed the greatest increase (i.e., 2015–2019 and 2019–2023).

All statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using R (version 4.3.0) and Python (version 3.9.21). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.

Results

Fig. 1 presents national trends in drug overdose mortality across demographic groups. Since 2015, the older Black group has experienced the most rapid increase in overdose death rates across all demographic groups, from 11.0 deaths per 100,000 population in 2015–61.1 deaths per 100,000 population in 2023 (i.e., a 457% increase, detailed trends for the 2015–2023 period are shown in Appendix Figure A1). By 2019, their mortality rate surpassed that of younger White individuals (who had the highest drug overdose mortality before 2019) and remained the highest across all groups ever since. While both age groups within the Black population exhibited increasing trends, the rise was notably steeper and reached higher levels among the older group. After 2020, rising mortality rates among younger Black adults began to slow, with a 34% increase measured between 2020 and 2023. In contrast, mortality rates among older Black adults continued to rise during this four-year time period, with 70% increase. In the South, while cocaine and fentanyl co-involvement also contributed to rising overdose deaths in this population, the rate of increase was less steep. Discussion

In this study, we confirmed a sharp and sustained increase in drug overdose mortality among non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older in the US, beginning in 2015 and accelerating after 2019, outpacing all other demographic subgroups defined by the intersection of age and race/ethnicity from 2015 to 2023. We also introduced a novel metric, CR, to quantify the relative contribution of specific substances to the overall increase in drug overdose mortality rates.The overdose mortality increase among older Black adults was closely associated with increasing deaths involving cocaine, and its co-involvement with fentanyl, suggesting these are likely to be key drivers of the trends. While these patterns were observed nationwide, regional differences emerged: the Northeast and Midwest closely mirrored national trends; the South showed a more modest increase; and in the West, a distinct rise in methamphetamine-related deaths was observed.

Our findings align with previous research highlighting the growing rate of drug overdose mortality among older Black adults (Mason et al., 2022, Jones et al., 2023, Friedman et al., 2024). By incorporating the most recent data up to 2023 and conducting stratified analysis by substance type and region, our study provides an updated and more nuanced characterization of this population. Cocaine (and its co-involvement with fentanyl) played key roles in the increase of drug overdose mortality in the older Black population. Between 2015 and 2019, during the "third wave" of the opioid crisis—marked by the proliferation of synthetic opioids—we observed a sharp rise in fentanyl-involved overdose deaths and its co-involvement with other substances (i.e., cocaine and heroin). These patterns were especially pronounced in the Northeast and Midwest. After 2019, in the "fourth wave," which is defined by increasing co-involvement of fentanyl and stimulants, the trends intensified and became more geographically widespread. Our findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that the fourth wave has disproportionately impacted Black populations (Friedman and Shover, 2023, Smith et al., 2024, Friedman et al., 2024, Barocas et al., 2024).

The finding that Black individuals ages 55 and older experienced higher overdose mortality rates than their younger counterparts represents a unique age-related pattern not observed in other racial/ethnic groups. This finding is consistent with previous research (Friedman et al., 2024). Although the co-involvement of cocaine and fentanyl contributed substantially to the steep rise in overdose deaths among older Black adults, this population also experienced a markedly elevated risk of fatal cocaine overdose compared to younger Black individuals, even in the absence of other opioids. This pattern was consistently observed across all four U.S. Census regions. While previous studies have shown that cocaine-involved overdose deaths without opioid co-involvement remained relatively stable in other subgroups over time (Estadt et al., 2024, Kline et al., 2023), our findings indicate a clear increase in these deaths among the older Black population since 2015.

Long-term cocaine use poses significant health risks—especially for older individuals who are more likely to have underlying cardiovascular conditions—as it can acutely and chronically affect the cardiovascular, respiratory, central nervous, and renal systems (Han et al., 2019). These age-related physiological vulnerabilities may increase the likelihood of fatal outcomes following cocaine use, even in the absence of opioids. Another possible explanation is the cohort effect (Jones et al., 2023, Friedman et al., 2024). After 2015, Black individuals born in the 1960s began entering the 55 + age group. Of this cohort, many were disproportionately affected by the “crack epidemic” of the 1980s, which may have led to a higher prevalence of long-term cocaine use in this population (Fryer et al., 2013). As this cohort aged, the risks associated with cocaine use likely became more pronounced—both due to age-related physiological vulnerability and the increasing presence of fentanyl in the cocaine supply particularly after 2019. Beyond substance use itself, the “crack epidemic” also had long-lasting consequences stemming from structural racism that negatively impacted this generation and contributed to lower educational outcomes (Evans et al., 2016), elevated risk for arrest, and harsher sentencing guidelines resulting in mass incarceration (Palamar et al., 2015). Those factors may compound susceptibility to overdose in later life (Xu et al., 2023, Joudrey et al., 2019).

Our findings highlight the need for targeted overdose prevention strategies focused on older Black adults, particularly given that Black individuals often have less access to harm reduction services and overdose prevention training (Jones et al., 2021). One approach to expanding prevention efforts in this population is to partner with established institutions that have strong community ties, such as social service organizations, churches, and schools. Culturally tailored awareness campaigns and programs specifically designed for older adults may also enhance outreach and engagement. For example, previous studies have documented a community-based overdose education and naloxone distribution program targeting overdose hotspots for older adults (Beiting et al., 2022), as well as a naloxone awareness program targeting home health workers and their older adult clients (McQuade et al., 2021). Given the disproportionate impact of cocaine use amongthe older Black population, it is also critical to address stimulant-related overdoses. Contingency management, an evidence-based intervention, has been shown to effectively reduce stimulant use and may be a promising strategy that should be expanded (Bentzley et al., 2021). Beyond the older Black population, our findings also showed that AI/AN populations experienced high rates of drug overdose mortality, particularly involving methamphetamine and fentanyl, consistent with previously published trends (Qeadan et al., 2022, Zhu et al., 2025). Targeted, culturally appropriate interventions for AI/AN communities are similarly warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, as a descriptive analysis, it cannot establish causal relationships. Racial/ethnic disparities in overdose mortality likely reflect underlying structural and social determinants. Future research should investigate upstream factors, such as poverty, education, and access to healthcare, as data permit. Second, our reliance on CDC’s WONDER data means findings are subject to potential misclassification of drugs involved in overdose deaths. Third, while overdose death data are available, there is a lack of comprehensive, publicly available data on drug use behaviors and patterns in this population, which limits interpretation of underlying behavioral trends. Fourth, our analysis was limited to unintentional drug overdose deaths, although understanding and preventing intentional drug overdose deaths remains an important public health priority but is beyond the scope of this study. Because CDC WONDER data may be subject to misclassification between unintentional and intentional overdose deaths, our analysis that excluded intentional overdose deaths may be affected by this potential misclassification.

Conclusion

Drug overdose deaths have increased more among non-Hispanic Black adults ages 55 and older than in any other demographic group. This rise was closely associated with overdoses involving cocaine and its co-involvement with fentanyl. Future research should prioritize identifying the upstream social, structural, and behavioral factors contributing to this trend and examine their causal relationships with overdose risk, to inform more effective public health strategies.
