Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) -related Hodgkin lymphoma in a 1-year-old child with DiGeorge syndrome
Authors: Zhongmei Rao, Hong Wang, Shouping Chen, Yuting Yang, Huanyu Liu, Chunling Ma, Jing Zhou, Zhixu Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper Reported a Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) -Related Pulmonary Hodgkin Lymphoma in 1-Year-Old Child with DiGeorge Syndrome (Chromosome 22q11.2 Deletion), which was one of the youngest patient reported to develop lymphoma due to this syndrome. The 1-year-old infant was plagued by recurrent infections, concurrent with cardiac malformations and thymic hypoplasia. The pulmonary infection exhibited a remarkably rapid progression. Initially identified as mild interstitial-alveolar inflammation, it swiftly progressed into multiple lung nodules. Laboratory investigations further disclosed T cell defects, which presumably contributed to the child’s immunocompromised condition. Moreover, there was clear evidence of EBV virus infection, and the EBV DNA copy number progressively increased over time. Crucially, thoracoscopic biopsy of the lung nodules showed Reed-Sternberg (R-S) cells on HE staining, with positive immunohistochemical staining for CD30 and in situ hybridization for EBER, strongly supporting the diagnosis of Hodgkin lymphoma. Finally, Whole-Exome Sequencing revealed a copy number deletion of approximately 2.63 Mb at chromosome 22q11.21. In conclusion, this case emphasizes that for children with recurrent severe infections, early immune evaluation, timely genetic examinations and comprehensive tumor screenings are crucial for early precise diagnosis.Keywords: DiGeorge syndrome, Chromosome 22q11.2 deletion, Epstein-Barr virus, Hodgkin lymphoma, Pulmonary nodules, Immunodeficiency

Introduction

DiGeorge syndrome (DGS), which caused by Chromosome 22q11.2 deletion, is characterized by thymic hypoplasia, cardiac anomalies, and immunodeficiency [1]. Based on immunophenotype and the extent of thymic hypoplasia, patients with DGS can be categorized into partial DiGeorge syndrome (pDGS) and complete DGS [1]. It may lead to cellular immunodeficiency and recurrent infections [2]. Despite low odds, DGS can trigger malignancies, especially lymphoma and leukemia, due to defective immune surveillance and T cell function [3, 4].

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a Gamma - herpesvirinae subfamily member, is a widespread human tumor virus linked to various malignancies [5]. It infects B lymphocytes and persists in memory B cells [6]. The immune system is vital for controlling EBV [6]. Thanks to the balanced virus-host interaction, most individuals carry EBV asymptomatically as T cell immunity constantly surveils against EBV [7]. In children with DGS, thymic hypoplasia disrupts lymphocyte function, resulting in a defective immune response to EBV [2, 8]. This even permits EBV to trigger proliferative disorders and malignancies [9]. However, reports on patients with clinical pDGS and EBV - associated lymphoma are scarce [10, 11].

This study reports an extremely rare case of EBV-associated Hodgkin lymphoma in a 1-year-old child with DiGeorge syndrome, potentially representing one the youngest documented occurrence of this complication.

Case introduction

The male infant was the fourth child born via full-term cesarean section, weighing 2500 g at birth. Notably, he had no postnatal recurrent infections and his motor and language development was normal. His 32 - year - old father and 38 - year - old mother were healthy and not closely related, and he had three healthy elder brothers. At 11 months, the infant started having recurrent fever, cough, and two convulsions. He was first hospitalized at a local hospital, and after comprehensive examinations, central nervous system infection was ruled out. A Chest CT revealed diffusely distributed punctate high-density shadows with uneven sizes, predominantly in the lower lobes, consistent with mild interstitial-alveolar inflammation (Fig. 1). The local hospital diagnosed severe pneumonia and gave anti - infective treatment. After treatment, the infant’s cough improved, but the fever lingered. He also developed severe ear discharge and tonsillar exudate. Given his complex condition, he was transferred to our hospital for further care at 1 year old. Physical exam showed the 7 - kg infant had a malnourished, anemic look, with copious purulent discharge from both ears and tonsillar exudates. No dysmorphic facial features were observed. There was no heart murmur, lung rales, or enlarged superficial lymph nodes, liver, or spleen. After admission, two of the infant’s cell lineages were found to be decreased. Hemoglobin was 87 g/L, peripheral white blood cell count was 2.82 × 10⁹/L, platelet count was normal, absolute lymphocyte count was 1.42 × 10⁹/L. Serial serum calcium measurements were within normal limits. Inflammation assessment: ESR 60 mm/h (reference:0–15 mm/h), PCT > 100 mg/mL(reference: <0.05 ng/mL), CRP 88.23 mg/L(reference: 0–10 mg/L). Immunological evaluation revealed normal immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE) but demonstrated significant lymphopenia in cellular immunity. Lymphocyte subset analysis showed: CD3⁺ T cells at 53.97% (793 cells/µL; reference 50–84% [1600–6000 cells/µL]); CD4⁺ T cells markedly reduced at 5.93% (102 cells/µL; reference 27–51% [1020–4000 cells/µL]); CD8⁺ T cells at 46.03% (680 cells/µL; reference 15–44% [570–2100 cells/µL]); and CD19⁺ B cells at 10.46% (129 cells/µL; reference 10.25–31.88% [236–1785 cells/µL]).

The complement level was normal, and the autoimmune disease-related antibodies were all negative. The EBV antibody test showed evidence of previous infection (high-affinity anti-EBV capsid antibody IgG positive, nuclear antibody IgG positive). The EBV DNA quantification was 4.54 × 10⁴ IU/ml. Bone marrow cytology and bone marrow lymphoma immunophenotyping tests were negative, and cerebrospinal fluid tests were negative too. Echocardiography revealed cardiac malformations, manifested as atrial septal defect, right aortic arch, and formation of Kommerell’s Diverticulum of the left aberrant subclavian artery. Following a comprehensive treatment with immunoglobulin, meropenem, vancomycin, and anti - EBV therapy (intravenous ganciclovir 5 mg/kg every 12 h for 14 days), the infant showed significant improvement. Laboratory tests indicated that inflammatory markers like ESR, PCT, and CRP returned to normal. Meanwhile, all symptoms such as recurrent fever, ear discharge, and tonsillar exudate, along with physical examination findings, completely resolved, suggesting a marked regression of the disease. However, the infant developed fever again one week later. A repeat chest CT revealed multiple lung nodules and thymic hypoplasia (Fig. 1). Whole-abdominal CT did not detect any definite lesions. Subsequently, a repeat EBV DNA quantification assay demonstrated a value of 1.56 × 10⁵ IU/ml. Analysis of EBV - infected lymphocyte subsets revealed that EBV DNA was detected in B cells at a level of 5.9 × 10³, while it was absent in CD4⁺ T, CD8⁺ T, and NK/NKT cells. To establish the diagnosis of the multiple pulmonary nodules, lymph node biopsy was performed via thoracoscopy (Fig. 2). Given the boy’s rapid progression of pulmonary nodules and severe immunodeficiency (T-cell defects, thymic hypoplasia), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of lung tissue was urgently performed to rule out atypical/opportunistic infections. The assay detected 5,701 EBV sequences without bacterial/fungal/parasitic pathogens, redirecting focus to EBV-driven lymphoproliferative disease and guiding targeted pathological evaluation. The pathological examination of the lung biopsy revealed abnormal lymphoid tissue growth (marked hyperplasia) with scattered large atypical cells. Reed-Sternberg (R-S) cells—characteristic of Hodgkin lymphoma—were identified, alongside areas of tissue necrosis (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemical staining revealed the following profile: the large neoplastic cells were positive for CD30 (Fig. 4), PAX5 (weak/nuclear), and p53, but were negative for CD15, CD10, BCL6, ALK, and EMA. Critically, the neoplastic cells were negative for CD3. The background inflammatory milieu was composed of CD3-positive T cells (which also expressed CD5, CD7, and cytotoxic markers Granzyme B and TIA1) and CD20/CD79a-positive B cells. Other markers including MUM1, CD21, BCL2, and CD13 were positive in a subset of the background lymphocytes. Additionally, the Ki67 proliferation index reached 90% in the tumor foci (data not shown). In situ hybridization for EBER was positive, with strong nuclear staining restricted to the large neoplastic cells (Fig. 4). Based on the immunohistochemical marker results and the positive EBER in situ hybridization, classic Hodgkin lymphoma was considered. Given the potential immunodeficiency, a Whole-Exome Sequencing was performed on the patient, which revealed a copy number deletion of approximately 2.63 Mb at 22q11.21 on the long arm of chromosome 22 (copy number = 1) (Fig. 5). This was a de novo deletion variant in the patient, determined to be pathogenic. Due to financial constraints, the parents strongly requested to abandon treatment and signed the discharge forms.

Discussion

DGS presents with diverse clinical manifestations. We describe a 1-year-old child with DGS who developed lymphoma, which to our knowledge represents one of the youngest reported cases of this association in the medical literature. In this case, the patient presented with recurrent severe infections. Since these infections were unresponsive to conventional anti - infectives, immunodeficiency should be highly suspected. Immediate immune function assessment and genetic testing are crucial for diagnosis, as seen in DGS - related cases. DGS is mainly caused by a deletion in the chromosome 22q11.2. This chromosomal deletion disrupts the normal function of multiple genes, leading to highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations. Patients typically have a 1.5–3mb hemizygous deletion on chromosome 22q11.2 [12, 13]. In this case, the patient have a 2.63 Mb deletion on chromosome 22q11.2. This region contains the single dose sensitive gene TBX1.To date, the haploinsufficiency of three genes (TBX1, CRKL and MAPK1) on 22q11.2 has been reported to be closely associated with this syndrome [12, 13].

In our case, the patient initially presented with a pulmonary infection that rapidly progressed to multiple pulmonary nodules, ultimately diagnosed as lymphoma. The morphological and immunophenotypic features observed in this case, including the presence of R-S cells, CD30⁺ cells, and EBV association (EBER⁺), align with the 2022 WHO criteria for classic Hodgkin lymphoma. Importantly, the inflammatory background, typical of Hodgkin lymphoma, further supports the diagnosis [14–16]. While CD15 expression is observed in 70–85% of classic Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) cases, its absence does not preclude the diagnosis when other definitive criteria are met [14–16]. These findings, combined with the exclusion of major mimics, unequivocally support the diagnosis of EBV-associated classic Hodgkin lymphoma in this immunocompromised host [14–16].In the scarce reports of lymphoma associated with DiGeorge syndrome, there have been reports of patients predominantly presenting with pneumonia and pulmonary nodules previously [10, 17, 18]. Thus, it serves as a clinical reminder to maintain a high level of suspicion for lymphoid malignancy in patients with DGS who present with pneumonia or multiple pulmonary nodules.

Regarding the immunological aspects, the thymic hypoplasia and significant reduction in T cell numbers potentially play a central role in the patient’s clinical course, given the known role of the thymus in T cell development and maturation [2, 19]. T-cell deficiency in DGS is variable in incidence and severity. It was found that most patients with DGS have normal T-cell immunity, which does not require correction or continuous monitoring of their immune system [20]. However, other studies have shown that up to 75% of patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS) have cellular immune defects. It is speculated that these T - cell defects increase the infection of oncogenic viruses such as EBV or human papillomavirus. Moreover, due to impaired immune monitoring, patients are rendered susceptible to hematological malignancies [3, 17]. Benjamin Fournier et al. reported a case of an 18 - year - old patient with DGS who demonstrated a particularly pronounced, widespread, and life - threatening susceptibility to EBV, ultimately leading to EBV - positive smooth muscle tumors [9]. Similarly, others have reported EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma with DGS [11].In our case, the infant began to experience recurrent severe infections at the age of 11 months, accompanied by a significant reduction in the number of T cells, especially CD4⁺ cells. The markedly decreased CD4⁺ cell counts indicate a severely compromised cellular immune function. Thus, T cell deficiency emerges as the central factor in the abnormal response to EBV and the consequent tumor development.

Although DGS may be associated with an increased risk of malignancy, such as lymphoma and acute leukemia. However, it should be emphasized that relevant reports in this regard are still relatively scarce [3]. To date, only a limited number of studies have explored patients with DGS and malignancies. In a study of the 22q11.2DS patient cohort, it is speculated that the overall incidence of malignant tumors is 5.7 per 1000 individuals [3]. A recent literature review summarizes 33 cancer patients associated with 22q11.2 DS or DGS reported so far, with 27 under 18 years old, and a median age of 9 years old. Among the observed tumors, hematological tumors were the most common, accounting for 21% [4]. However, there are scant reports of patients with DGS and EBV-driven lymphoma. Compared to previous records, this early onset lymphoma with DGS is extremely rare, strongly suggesting the underestimated risk in infants and demanding urgent medical attention.

In conclusion, our case details EBV - associated Hodgkin lymphoma in DGS, emphasizing the ties between immunodeficiency, viral infection, and tumorigenesis. We stress that for children with recurrent infections, prompt immunity assessment and genetic examination are crucial for early diagnosis. In DGS patients with severe immunodeficiency, neoplastic disease screening is of utmost importance.
