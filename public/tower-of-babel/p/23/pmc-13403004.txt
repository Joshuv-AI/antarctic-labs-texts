Zwitterionic Molecule Intercalation in Nanoporous Multilayer Graphene Oxide Membrane for Metal Ion Recovery from Spent Li-Ion Batteries
Authors: Jeong Pil Kim, Junhyeok Kang, Ju Yeon Kim, Jiwon Kim, Go Eun Lee, Kiok Kwon, Dae Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for lithium-ion batteries has intensified the need for efficient recovery of lithium and transition metals from spent battery materials. Membrane-based ion separation provides a low-energy alternative or can be combined with conventional hydrometallurgical purification routes. In this study, graphene oxide (GO) membranes were engineered through the intercalation of a zwitterionic molecule, 3-(1-vinyl-3-imidazolio)­propanesulfonate (VIPS), followed by hot-press treatment to produce structurally stabilized VIPS-intercalated GO membranes. Structural and spectroscopic analyses reveal that VIPS molecules expand the interlayer spacing of GO and introduce positively charged groups, which suppress the permeation of divalent cations. Hot-pressing further generates nanopores and reinforces the layered structure, enabling improved Li ion transport. Concentration-driven permeation tests demonstrated that the GO membrane containing a 1:1 weight ratio of GO to VIPS achieved a lithium-ion permeation rate of 33.2 mmol m–2 h–1 with markedly enhanced selectivity over divalent ions, including a lithium/manganese selectivity of 18. Long-term forward osmosis operation conducted for 480 h exhibited stable Li-selective permeation behavior, yielding permeation rates of 33.0, 8.3, 4.0, and 3.8 mmol m–2 h–1 for Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+, respectively. Although extended operation caused slight swelling and partial loss of selectivity, a multistep forward osmosis enrichment process produced high-purity lithium solutions (>99%) and decreased the concentrations of Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ to undetectable levels after three cycles. This study demonstrates that the combination of zwitterionic molecule intercalation and hot-press treatment produces structurally stable graphene oxide membranes capable of selective lithium transport and high-purity lithium enrichment, offering a practical and energy-efficient platform for lithium recovery from hydrometallurgical recycling streams.

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have become the dominant energy-storage technology owing to their high energy densities, long cycle lifetime, and reliable performance across electric vehicles. − The rapid expansion of LIB-dependent industries has intensified the demand for essential components of cathode materials (particularly Li, Co, Ni, and Mn). However, the natural reserves of these metals are geographically concentrated, with Li primarily extracted from brine resources in Australia and South America, and Co largely sourced from the Democratic Republic of Congo. , This uneven distribution not only poses geopolitical and supply chain risks but also highlights the long-term unsustainability of primary resource extraction. In addition, the accumulation of spent LIBs has emerged as an environmental concern, as their leachates contain toxic and corrosive metal ions. Considering these aspects, efficient recovery and purification of valuable metals from spent batteries are crucial for establishing a circular materials economy and reducing environmental burden. ,

Two types of processes are generally employed for ion recovery, namely pyrometallurgy − and hydrometallurgy. − Pyrometallurgical processes rely on high-temperature melting to extract mixed metal alloys, − including recent electrically driven approaches such as flash joule heating. , By contrast, hydrometallurgical routes typically involve acid leaching followed by solvent extraction, chemical precipitation, or electrodeposition. − However, these methods suffer from high energy consumption, large chemical usage, and limited selectivity in multicomponent ionic environments, resulting in elevated operating costs and environmental impact. By contrast, membrane-based separation technologies involve highly integrated systems and low energy consumption, rendering them relatively cost-effective. In particular, forward osmosis operates without externally applied hydraulic pressure, leading to significantly lower energy consumption compared to reverse osmosis. A wide range of nanomaterials-based membranes, such as those incorporating polymer, metal–organic framework, and carbon-based materials, have been developed for ion separation. − Among these, two-dimensional (2D) materials have attracted particular interest because their angstrom-level interlayer spacing enables precise molecular and ionic sieving. Despite these advantages, most 2D membranes suffer from limited permeance due to the long transport pathways imposed by their high aspect ratio and the densely stacked layer morphology. Among them, graphene oxide (GO) is especially promising because its interlayer spacing can be readily tuned through chemical functionalization, intercalation, or thermal treatment. − However, GO membranes still exhibit water-induced swelling and unstable spacing in ionic environments, which limits their selectivity in monovalent/divalent mixtures.

Herein, we report a zwitterionic-molecule-intercalated nanoporous multilayer GO membrane (HGO/VIPS) designed to address these limitations. The zwitterionic molecule 3-(1-vinyl-3-imidazolio)­propanesulfonate (VIPS) expands the GO interlayer spacing and introduces electrostatic repulsion. A subsequent confined hot-pressing step induces partial reduction and nanopore formation while suppressing excessive swelling. We systematically investigate how VIPS loading affects GO rheology, membrane morphology, interlayer spacing, and ion transport. Finally, we demonstrate long-term Li+-selective permeation using a forward-osmosis system and validate multicycle enrichment of high-purity lithium solutions. This work establishes a design strategy for chemically robust graphene-based membranes and provides mechanistic insights into how zwitterionic intercalation and thermal compaction synergistically control ion selectivity in complex battery-recycling environments.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Hot-Pressed GO/VIPS (HGO/VIPS) Membranes

GO was synthesized using a modified Hummers’ method described in our previous work. The resulting GO powder was dispersed in deionized (DI) water at 30 mg mL–1 to form a lyotropic nematic liquid crystal dispersion with viscoelastic properties. To investigate the effect of polymer charge on GO rheology, three different charged polymers were selected: 3-(1-vinyl-3-imidazolio) propanesulfonate (VIPS, zwitterionic molecule), polyethylenimine (PEI, positively charged polymer), and poly­(styrene sulfonic acid) (PSSA, negatively charged polymer). GO/VIPS mixtures were prepared by adding VIPS into GO dispersions at GO: VIPS weight ratios of 20:1, 10:1, 5:1, 2:1, and 1:1 to systematically investigate the effect of VIPS loading. For comparison, GO/PEI and GO/PSSA dispersions were prepared at a fixed GO: polymer weight ratio of 1:1, serving as charge-controlled reference systems.

Membranes were fabricated via a bar-coating process onto poly­(ether sulfone) (PES) filters (GVS filter technology, 47 mm diameter, 0.22 μm pore size). A Baker Applicator YBA-7 was used with a coating gap of 1 μm and a coating speed of 6 m min–1. Bar coating promotes shear-driven alignment of GO sheets, whereas vacuum filtration relies on pressure-driven stacking. After coating, the membranes were dried in an oven to remove residual DI water. Hot-pressing is expected to introduce additional permeation pathways via nanoscale defects/nanopores, consistent with our prior HR-TEM and gas-transport evidence. , Hot pressing was performed in air using an Auto Series Compact Automatic Benchtop Press (Carver Inc.), with the membrane sandwiched between flat plates to ensure uniform pressure distribution. The membranes were hot-pressed at 150 °C and 20 bar for durations ranging from 0.5 to 24 h.

Rheological Characterization of GO Dispersions

Rheological measurements were performed using a modular compact rheometer (MCR 302e, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with parallel-plate geometry (25 mm diameter, 1 mm gap). All measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Steady shear flow curves were carried out over shear rates from 10–2 to 1000 s–1 for GO/VIPS dispersions with various molecule loadings, as well as GO/PEI and GO/PSSA control dispersions. The zero-shear viscosity was determined at the lowest measured shear rate (0.00251 s–1). These measurements were used to evaluate the influence of polymer charge and zwitterionic intercalation on GO dispersion stability and processability.

Characterization of HGO and HGO/VIPS Membrane

The morphology of the fabricated HGO/VIPS membrane was characterized using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). High-resolution images were obtained using a JEOL Ltd. JSM-7610F-Plus FE-SEM. Functional groups present on the membrane were identified using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. FT-IR spectra were collected on a Cary 670 spectrometer (Agilent). Crystallinity and interlayer spacing were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Measurements were performed using a high-resolution Rigaku X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on an ESCALAB 250 XI spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to investigate the chemical composition. The zeta potential was measured using an ELSZ-2000ZS system (Otsuka Electronics Co. Ltd., Japan). The surface hydrophilicity of the membrane surface was evaluated by measuring the water contact angle using an Attesion Theta Lite instrument (Biolin Scientific, Sweden). Finally, ion concentrations in permeate and feed solutions were determined using ion chromatography (Metrohm AG 925 Eco IC system).

Cation Permeation Experiments of HGO/VIPS Membranes

Cation permeation experiments were conducted using custom-designed U-shaped permeation cells. The HGO and HGO/VIPS membranes were clamped between two stainless-steel cells, defining an effective area of 4.95 cm2. A mixed salt solution (150 mL) containing Li2SO4, CoSO4, NiSO4, and MnSO4 (each cation at a concentration of 0.1 mol L–1) was loaded into the feed chamber. Four different cations (Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+) were selected because they represent the major constituents of NCM-type lithium-ion battery cathodes. Sulfate salts were used as counterions, consistent with standard hydrometallurgical leaching conditions for recovering transition-metal ions from spent batteries. The permeate chamber was filled with an equal volume of DI water. The solutions were magnetically continuously stirred at 150 rpm to minimize concentration polarization near the membrane surface. Therefore, the concentration at the membrane interface was assumed to be close to the bulk concentration. The test duration was fixed at 6 h. The cation permeation rate was determined by analyzing the concentration variation of the permeate solution using ion chromatography. All ion permeation experiments were repeated three times (n = 3) using independently prepared membrane samples to ensure reproducibility. For water flux measurements, the test was conducted using the same U-shaped permeation cell. The feed chamber was filled with the mixed ion solution described above (150 mL), while a 2 M sucrose solution (150 mL) was placed in the permeate chamber as the draw solution to generate the osmotic driving force. Sucrose was selected as a nonionic draw solute to avoid introducing additional ions on the permeate side and to suppress reverse solute diffusion due to its large molecular size. Mixed Cation Separation Using a Forward Osmosis Setup

The long-term ion separation performance was evaluated in a continuous forward-osmosis (FO) system, which comprised two peristaltic pumps maintaining a flow rate of 10 mL/min on both sides of the membrane. The HGO/VIPS membrane (GO/VIPS ratio of 1:1) was mounted between the feed and permeate chambers, providing an effective area of 4.95 cm2. The feed solution consisted of a mixed sulfate system in which each cation concentration (Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, Mn2+) is 0.1 M. Total cation concentration is 0.4 M. DI water served as the permeate solution. Both reservoirs contained 2 L of solution and were stirred at 200 rpm. The variation in cation concentration of the permeate solution over time was monitored and analyzed using ion chromatography. The ion permeation rate and selectivity were calculated using the same methodology described in Section .

Results and Discussion

Intercalating zwitterionic molecules into GO layers enhances Li+ separation by creating charge-balanced, hydration-controlled nanochannels. Zwitterions stabilize confined pathways that preferentially permeate the small, weakly hydrated Li+ while excluding multivalent ions with strongly bound hydration shells. Intercalation also precisely tunes GO interlayer spacing, suppresses swelling, and maintains narrow cutoffs required for selective permeation. Figure presents the overall synthesis of the zwitterionic VIPS and the fabrication process of the HGO/VIPS membrane. VIPS molecule was synthesized following a previously reported method (Figure a). Briefly, 0.22 mol (20 mL) of 1-vinylimidazole was dissolved in 100 mL of acetonitrile, after which an equimolar amount (0.22 mol, 22 mL) of 1,3-propane sultone was slowly added dropwise. To prevent interference from oxygen and moisture, the reaction mixture was purged with nitrogen for 20 min in a sealed flask. The mixture was stirred at 34 °C for 24 h, yielding an 80% conversion to a white precipitate. The product was washed several times with diethyl ether to remove unreacted species and impurities, followed by vacuum drying for 48 h to ensure complete solvent removal.

The fabrication process of a zwitterionic molecule-intercalated nanoporous multilayer graphene oxide (HGO/VIPS) membrane is illustrated in Figure b. A 30 mg/mL GO suspension is prepared in DI water, which forms a lyotropic nematic liquid crystal (LC) phase exhibiting viscoelastic properties. The VIPS were then mixed into the GO dispersion at predetermined weight ratios, allowing the VIPS to intercalate between GO lamellae during vigorous stirring. The resulting GO/VIPS suspension was coated onto a PES filter via a bar-coating process, as shown in Figure c. The coated membrane was dried at 60 °C and subsequently hot-pressed at 150 °C and 20 bar for 30 min (Figure d). This relatively low hot-pressing temperature was intentionally selected to minimize the formation of oversized, nonselective nanopores caused by excessive decomposition of oxygen-containing groups. The epoxy and carboxylic groups decompose preferentially at 150 °C while most other oxygen functional groups remain intact. This enables controlled nanopore formation while preserving membrane integrity. Additionally, the high pressure confines the GO layers and prevents expansion caused by CO/CO2 gas evolution during decomposition of oxygen-containing groups, which would otherwise enlarge the interlayer spacing and diminish selectivity. After the hot-pressing treatment, the membrane transitioned from brown to black due to thermal reduction (Figure e), signifying the reduction of GO. Notably, no cracks or delamination in the membrane were observed, indicating that the multilayer structure remained well-preserved.

Understanding the rheological properties of GO dispersions is important for achieving uniform and defect-free bar-coated GO membranes. Rheological measurements were performed to examine how VIPS and charged polymers influence the viscoelastic behavior of GO dispersions (Figure ). Figure a shows the effect of zwitterionic molecule concentration on the viscoelastic behavior of GO solutions. In the absence of the VIPS molecule, the pristine 30 mg mL–1 GO solution exhibited a solid-like behavior and adhered to the vial walls after inversion, which is a typical characteristic of strong liquid-crystalline ordering of GO nanosheets. The influence of polymer charge on solution viscoelasticity was further examined by introducing two oppositely charged polymers (PSSA (negative) and PEI (positive)) because VIPS has a neutral charge in a molecule. When PEI was mixed with GO, the dispersion rapidly aggregated and solidified due to the strong electrostatic attraction between the positively charged polymer and negatively charged GO sheets. In contrast, PSSA has the same charge polarity as GO and therefore did not cause aggregation, allowing the GO dispersion to retain its solid-like behavior. Additionally, the GO/VIPS solution exhibited consistent adhesion to the vial walls upon inversion across all VIPS concentrations. This observation indicates that the zwitterionic VIPS, despite containing a positively charged imidazolium group, did not significantly impact the viscoelastic properties of the GO solution.

To further support these observations, rheological measurements were carried out for GO/VIPS and GO/polymer dispersions with varying molecule or polymer concentrations (Figures b and S1). All samples exhibited shear-thinning behavior, as evidenced by viscosity decreasing with increasing shear rate, confirming their suitability for shear-induced coating techniques such as bar-coating. , A more detailed comparison of zero-shear viscosity is provided in Figure c. The zero-shear viscosity of pristine GO was 236.1 Pa·s. Upon incorporation of VIPS, the viscosity increased slightly, likely due to physical intercalation and the formation of weak associative networks between GO sheets and VIPS molecules. However, the magnitude of this increase was small and did not impede shear-induced alignment or uniform membrane formation during bar-coating.

The SEM images (Figure ) of the HGO/VIPS illustrate the morphology of HGO/VIPS membranes prepared with varying VIPS concentrations. The top-view SEM images show no noticeable macroscopic defects even after hot-pressing, indicating that the membrane structure remained intact. Cross-sectional SEM images further reveal that the pristine GO membrane had a thickness of approximately 1 μm, which gradually increased to about ∼1.7 μm at a GO/VIPS ratio of 1:1. This increase in thickness confirms the successful intercalation of VIPS into the GO interlayer spacing, leading to expansion of the multilayer structure. Although the overall surface appears smooth at the microscale, the incorporation of VIPS and subsequent hot-press treatment subtly alters the nanoscale surface morphology. This effect is reflected in the water contact angle measurements (Figure S2), which show that the membrane becomes more hydrophobic after hot-pressing. Interestingly, the hydrophobicity increased even at higher VIPS molecule loadings despite the intrinsic hydrophilicity of VIPS. This behavior can be explained by the fact that VIPS molecules primarily occupy the interlayer space rather than the membrane surface, while their intercalation slightly modifies the surface texture, resulting in an overall increase in apparent hydrophobicity. At the higher VIPS loading (e.g., GO/VIPS ratio of 2:1 and 1:1), the contact angle tends to decrease slightly, suggesting a partial recovery of hydrophilicity. This may arise because excess VIPS is not fully accommodated within the interlayer galleries and becomes more exposed near the outer surface, allowing sulfonate-containing moieties to contribute more directly to surface wettability. Overall, the contact-angle trend can be understood as the combined outcome of GO reduction during hot pressing, preferential interlayer incorporation of VIPS, and subtle changes in nanoscale surface texture.

FT-IR and XRD analyses were performed to investigate the chemical structure and interlayer characteristics of the HGO/VIPS membranes (Figure ). The FT-IR spectra (Figure a) show the characteristic functional groups of both GO and the VIPS molecule. Pristine GO exhibited typical oxygen-containing vibrational peaks, including O–H stretching (∼3400 cm–1), CO stretching (∼1720 cm–1), and C–O–C vibrations (1050–1250 cm–1). After the incorporation of VIPS, additional peaks corresponding to the zwitterionic molecule appeared, such as C–N+ stretching vibration at 1650 cm–1 and SO stretching modes of the sulfonate group at 1205 and 1032 cm–1. Furthermore, SO stretching vibrations attributable to sulfate species in the system were observed at 1179 and 1033 cm–1. These VIPS-related and sulfate-associated peaks intensified with increasing molecule loading, confirming the successful incorporation of VIPS into the GO layers. To further confirm VIPS incorporation, XPS analysis was performed (Figure S3). Compared to pristine GO, GO/VIPS membranes show distinct N 1s and S 2p signals, and the N 1s spectra can be deconvoluted into components at ∼399 eV (imidazole-ring N; C–N/CN) and ∼401 eV (cationic imidazolium N+), together with the characteristic sulfonate S 2p doublet. , The N and S atomic percentages increase systematically with VIPS loading (Figure S3d), supporting successful incorporation of the zwitterionic VIPS moieties that can modulate the electrostatic environment of GO nanochannels. To further examine the electrostatic modification induced by VIPS incorporation, zeta potential measurements were conducted (Figure S4). Compared with pristine GO (−37.7 mV), the GO/VIPS dispersion exhibited a less negative potential (−29.9 mV), indicating partial electrostatic modification of GO nanosheets by the zwitterionic VIPS molecules.

XRD measurements were conducted to further examine how molecule intercalation and thermal treatment influence the interlayer structure of the HGO/VIPS membranes (Figure b). Understanding the evolution of interlayer spacing is essential for elucidating the molecular separation mechanism of layered graphene-based membranes. A summary of the extracted d-spacing values is provided in Figure c. The pristine GO membrane exhibited a distinct diffraction peak at 11.6° (d-spacing: 7.6 Å), a value typically associated with oxygen-containing groups that support the lamellar structure of graphene oxide. After the incorporation of VIPS, the diffraction peak shifted to lower angles (9.3°, d-spacing: 9.5 Å), indicating expansion of the interlayer spacing due to molecular insertion between adjacent GO layers. Subsequently, hot-pressing was applied to improve membrane stability, during which partial removal of oxygen-containing functional groups occurred, leading to a controlled reduction of the interlayer spacing. Notably, the absence of a reduced GO-related peak in the XRD patterns suggests that the 30 min hot-pressing duration was insufficient to induce substantial graphitization or the formation of reduced graphene oxide.

The effect of VIPS on the structural evolution of hot-pressed membranes is summarized schematically in Figure d. Unlike pristine GO, the HGO/VIPS membranes did not show a substantial decrease in d-spacing upon hot-pressing. Instead, when the VIPS loading exceeded a GO/VIPS weight ratio of 2:1, the interlayer spacing increased even after hot-pressing. This behavior suggests that the zwitterionic VIPS acted as interlayer spacers, preventing thermally induced collapse of the GO layers and, at higher loadings, promoting additional expansion. For instance, in the HGO/VIPS membrane at a GO/VIPS ratio of 1:1, the d-spacing increased from 9.5 Å to 10.4 Å after hot-pressing. Since practical ion separation involves hydrated environments, the stability of the interlayer structure in water was also examined (Figure S5). While most HGO/VIPS membranes exhibited an increase in d-spacing due to swelling, the GO/VIPS weight ratio of the 1:1 membrane maintained nearly the same interlayer spacing after immersion. This result suggests that VIPS molecules are densely intercalated within the interlayer spacing, effectively restricting layer expansion and preventing excessive swelling in aqueous conditions by suppressing the repulsion between remaining oxygen-containing groups. These structural changes are expected to be a dominant factor governing ion transport through the membrane.

To evaluate the effect of VIPS molecule concentration on ion permeation through HGO/VIPS membranes, concentration-driven permeation experiments were carried out using a homemade U-shaped setup (Figure ). The experimental setup is depicted in Figure a, while the detailed experimental procedure is provided in Section . Before comparing the effect of VIPS concentration, the influence of hot-pressing time on membrane performance was examined (Figure S6). Increasing the hot-press time reduced the interlayer spacing and substantially lowered water permeation, and thus a pressing time of 30 min was chosen as the optimal condition. For the hot-pressed GO membrane (HGO), the permeation rates of Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ were 13.3, 2.5, 2.7, and 2.3 mmol m–2 h–1, respectively (Figure b). Increasing the VIPS molecule concentration progressively enhanced the permeation of all cations, with the most pronounced improvement observed for Li+. At a GO/VIPS weight ratio of 1:1, the Li+ permeation rate increased to 33.2 mmol m–2 h–1, while Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ exhibited rates of 3.5, 1.8, and 1.8 mmol m–2 h–1, respectively. Figure c presents the Li+/divalent selectivity values (Li+/Co2+, Li+/Ni2+, and Li+/Mn2+). The HGO membrane without VIPS molecule exhibited selectivities of 5.4, 5.0, and 5.8, respectively. Interestingly, Li+/divalent selectivity increased with increasing VIPS loading. For example, the Li+/Mn2+ selectivity rose dramatically to 17.9 for the HGO/VIPS membrane with a GO/VIPS ratio of 5:1. The Li+/Ni2+ selectivity reached 15.1 at a 2:1 ratio, and the Li+/Co2+ selectivity increased to 9.5 at a 1:1 ratio.

The corresponding mechanism is illustrated in Figure d. In the absence of VIPS, the hot-pressed GO membrane displayed a d-spacing of 7.1 Å. Incorporation of VIPS significantly expanded the interlayer spacing, reaching 10.4 Å for the HGO/VIPS membrane with a 1:1 ratio, which directly contributed to the enhanced Li+ permeation. In addition, the positively charged N+ functionalities within the zwitterionic molecule effectively suppressed the transport of divalent cations, thereby improving Li+/divalent selectivity. Based on these results, the HGO/VIPS (GO/VIPS = 1:1) membrane was selected as the optimal composition for subsequent permeation and long-term separation experiments.

To evaluate the applicability of the HGO/VIPS membrane for continuous ion recovery, a FO separation system was constructed (Figure a) and operated for 480 h. Ion permeation performance was measured using a two-chamber FO cell, in which the chamber facing the HGO/VIPS membrane contained a mixed cation solution while the opposite chamber was filled with DI water. This continuous operation mode offers a significant advantage by revealing the long-term structural and transport stability of graphene-based membranes under realistic recovery conditions. In contrast, most previous studies have relied on short-duration U-shaped diffusion tests or dead-end filtration setups. , Figure b presents the permeation rates of each cation over time. During the initial 24 h, the Li+ permeation rate exhibited fluctuations before stabilizing, likely due to gradual equilibration of the membrane’s interlayer spacing. After 480 h of continuous operation, the permeation rates of Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ reached 33.0, 8.3, 4.0, and 3.8 mmol m–2 h–1, respectively. These results indicate that intercalation of VIPS molecules reinforces van der Waals interactions between GO sheets, stabilizing the interlayer spacing and enabling sustained Li+-selective transport over extended periods. The structural integrity of the membrane after FO operation was further examined by SEM. The membrane surface remained intact after 24 h of operation without visible macro-cracks or delamination (Figure S7).

Figure c summarizes the molar composition of ions in the permeate solution. The initial feed contained 25 mol % of each cation. After 12 h of operation, Li selectivity became evident, with the permeate composition shifting to 73.6% Li+, while Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ decreased to 13.6%, 6.5%, and 6.0%, respectively. After 480 h, the Li+ molar fraction decreased to 67%. This decline suggests that despite partial reduction by hot-pressing and stabilization by VIPS intercalation, the HGO/VIPS membrane gradually swells over prolonged operation due to residual oxygen-containing groups. Extended ion permeation may also promote additional ion intercalation, expanding the interlayer spacing and thereby permitting transport of hydrated divalent ions. , As a result, long-term ion separation performance decreases as the membrane structure becomes progressively more hydrated and expanded.

To further increase the Li+ molar fraction, a multistep FO separation approach was implemented using the setup shown in Figure S8. Each step was operated for 24 h. The first feed contained 25 mol % of each cation. After each step, the permeate composition was used as the feed composition for the next step, enabling a three-stage enrichment process. After the first stage, the permeate contained 82.5%, 8.7%, 4.4%, and 4.4% of Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+, respectively. In the second stage, the Li+ fraction increased to 92.1%, while Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ decreased to 5.4%, 0, and 2.5%, respectively. After the third stage, only Li+ remained detectable. These results demonstrate that multistep FO processing enables the production of high-purity Li+ solutions, offering a promising low-energy and low-cost strategy for practical metal recovery applications.

Conclusions

In this study, the influence of zwitterionic molecule (VIPS) intercalation on ion permeation through layered GO/VIPS membranes was systematically investigated. Membranes fabricated with a GO/VIPS weight ratio of 1:1 exhibited significantly enhanced Li+ permeation as well as improved Li+/divalent selectivity. The intercalated VIPS molecules expanded the interlayer spacing of GO, facilitating ion transport and suppressing divalent-ion permeation through their positively charged moieties, which in turn enhanced Li+ selectivity. In addition, the hot-pressing method generated nanopores within the GO structure, further improving ion permeation. To assess the practical applicability of the developed membranes, forward osmosis-based Li recovery systems were constructed and evaluated. The membranes effectively extracted Li+ from mixed-ion solutions containing transition metals typically found in spent LIB cathodes. Moreover, a multistep FO process enabled the production of high-purity Li solutions, demonstrating the commercial potential of GO/VIPS membranes as a low-energy platform for lithium recovery and purification.
