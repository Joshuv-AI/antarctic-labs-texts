Re-irradiation with image-guided radiotherapy for recurrent cervical cancer: an analysis of outcomes and toxicity
Authors: Xi Qi, Zhiyong Xiang, Weiping Wang, Kai Liu, Xiaorong Hou, Fuquan Zhang, Ke Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRecurrent cervical cancer within previously irradiated fields remains a major therapeutic challenge. This study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes and safety of image-guided re-irradiation and to identify prognostic factors associated with tumor control and treatment-related toxicity.MethodsAll consecutive patients who underwent image-guided re-irradiation between March 2020 and December 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Re-irradiation was delivered using image-guided external beam radiotherapy and/or image-guided brachytherapy. Concurrent chemotherapy or immunotherapy was administered when clinically indicated. Organ at risk doses were recorded from re-irradiation plans. Logistic models were applied to evaluate tumor control probability and normal tissue complication probability.ResultsA total of 102 patients were included. At recurrence, 45.1% had central relapse, 14.7% pelvic relapse, 17.6% combined central and pelvic relapse, and 22.5% had distant metastasis. The median re-irradiation dose was 56.8 Gy (a/β = 10). With a median follow-up of 13.7 months, the 1- and 2-year LC were 67.6% and 59.2%, and the 1- and 2-year PFS were 52.7% and 42.6%. Higher re-irradiation prescription EQD2 was associated with improved tumor control. A progression-free interval > 2 years after initial radiotherapy predicted better progression-free survival and local control, whereas distant metastasis was associated with inferior progression-free survival. Higher rectal and bladder D2cc values were significantly associated with increased late toxicity. The risk of grade ≥ 2 late complications rose substantially when the rectal or bladder D2cc exceeded approximately 50 Gy (a/β = 3). No grade 4 or 5 toxicities were observed in this study.ConclusionRe-irradiation is a feasible and effective salvage option for patients with recurrent cervical cancer and provides moderate local control with acceptable toxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16594-y.

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as one of the most prevalent malignant tumors in the female reproductive system. In 2022, there were 661,021 new cases and 348,189 deaths from cervical cancer worldwide, placing it fourth in terms of incidence and mortality among global female malignancies [1]. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), which encompasses external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), brachytherapy, and cisplatin-based chemotherapy, stands as a principal treatment modality for locally advanced cervical cancer. However, despite CCRT, the PFS of patients ranges from 68% to 75% [2–5], and recurrence of cervical cancer remains a significant clinical challenge. For patients who experience recurrence after radiotherapy, re-irradiation can lead to severe complications due to the high cumulative radiation dose, which may cause serious organ damage [6]. The efficacy of chemotherapy is also relatively limited [7, 8]. Salvage surgery, which can alleviate symptoms and prolong patients’ lives, is the most curative intervention for managing in-field recurrences [9, 10]. However, the incidence of severe complications in patients undergoing pelvic exenteration can reach 50% [11, 12], which has a profound impact on patients’ physical and psychological well-being.

After radiotherapy, normal tissues exhibit some recovery, with early responding tissues regaining much of their original tolerance within months to years post-treatment, while the recovery of late-responding tissues such as bladder is slower [13]. With the advancement of radiotherapy techniques in recent years, including image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), and 3D-image-guided brachytherapy (3D-IGBT), the precision of radiotherapy has been significantly enhanced. Some retrospective studies have reported that patients with high-dose-rate brachytherapy have a local control rate of 44% at 3 years [14], and patients with VMAT have a local control rate of 38% at 3 years [15]. Also recent landmark trials, such as KEYNOTE-826 and KEYNOTE-A18 shows that immunotherapy is effective in managing recurrent or advanced cervical cancer [16, 17].While these regimens significantly improve local control and survival, the integration of immunotherapy with radiotherapy may also exacerbate treatment-related toxicities.

In light of the positive outcomes from recent studies, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical outcomes of patients who underwent re-irradiation with modern radiotherapy techniques at our institution. We further explored the relationship between clinical characteristics and irradiation doses with survival outcomes and complications in these patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study initially included 110 patients with local, nodal or local and nodal recurrent cervical cancer who received re-irradiation in our institution from March 2020 to December 2024. The diagnosis of recurrence was established through histological, clinical, and radiological assessments. Pathological confirmation was obtained in 23 patients (22.5%) via biopsy. Criteria for eligibility were as follows: (1) Histologically confirmed primary cervical cancer; (2) Prior pelvic and/or para-aortic radiotherapy (3) Radiologically or pathologically confirmed recurrent lesion within the previously irradiated field, with or without additional out-of-field disease. (4) Treated with pelvic re-irradiation delivered with radical or localized disease control intent. Patients were excluded if they didn’t complete the re-irradiation course (n = 4). A total of 102 patients were finally included in our study. Some patients received chemotherapy and/or surgery before reirradiation. All patients were classified into Type I reirradiation based on ESTRO and EORTC consensus [18].

Radiation technique selection

Before treatment, all patients underwent gynecological examination and imaging evaluation including colposcopy, CT, MRI, and/or PET-CT. For small, localized lesions confined to the cervix or vagina and suitable for radical brachytherapy, image-guided brachytherapy (IGBT) or interstitial brachytherapy (ISBT) was selected. For patients not eligible for radical brachytherapy, external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) was administered, with or without a local boost using EBRT or brachytherapy depending on disease extent. EBRT was delivered using Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), Helical tomotherapy (TOMO), depending on the tumor location and target geometry. All patients underwent CT-guided IGRT prior to treatment fractions. Electron beam therapy was selectively delivered as a boost for patients with superficial vulvar recurrences or involved inguinal lymph nodes.

Target delineation and dose prescription

Patients receiving EBRT were immobilized within a thermoplastic shell. Secondly, Intravenous contrast-enhanced CT simulation was performed at 5-mm slice thickness on the whole abdomen plus pelvic region by using a CT simulator. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included all radiologically visible recurrent lesions. The clinical target volume was confined to the recurrent lesion and its adjacent high-risk tissue (CTV-T). Elective lymphatic drainage area (CTV-N) irradiation was only performed selectively according to clinical indications. Patients treated for localized GTV typically received hypofractionated regimens (4–10 fractions of 4–6 Gy per fraction). For patients requiring elective CTV coverage, conventional fractionation (18–28 fractions of 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction) was used, followed by optional field reduction for dose boosting (4–5 fractions of 5–6 Gy per fraction).

For brachytherapy, high-dose-rate 192Ir unit was used to conduct three-dimensional image guided brachytherapy (3D-IGBT) or interstitial implantation (ISBT). For the high-risk clinical target volume (HR-CTV), prescription doses ranged from 10 to 40 Gy in 5–7 fractions. Given the limited evidence on dose-response relationships for OARs in re-irradiation, OAR doses were kept as low as reasonably achievable without prospectively specifying fixed D2cc thresholds, with individualized adjustments based on prior radiotherapy dose and the interval between treatments [19]. For the small bowel and sigmoid colon, a more stringent constraint of re-irradiation D2cc ≤ 42 Gy was applied based on center experience. The irradiation dose received by 2 cm3 (D2cc) of the high-dose area to the bladder and rectum were recorded. We used the quadratic linear model (a/β = 10 was adopted for gross tumor volume, and a/β = 3 was adopted for OARs). Then, the doses of brachytherapy and EBRT were added to obtain the cumulative to the target area and the cumulative D2cc to the OARs.

Chemotherapy and immunotherapy

Some patients received chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy during re-irradiation to enhance radiosensitivity or control distant metastasis. Immunotherapy regime included tislelizumab, camrelizumab, and pembrolizumab. Platinum-based concurrent chemotherapy was administered according to patient tolerance.

Statistical analysis

Patient and tumor-related details such as age, primary tumor stage and histology, duration to progression, location of relapse were recorded. Details of prior radiotherapy, including field, technique, and dose, were also collected. As all re-irradiation courses were image-guided, both prescription doses and OAR parameters (rectum and bladder D2cc) were recorded. For comparison and accumulation among different radiation courses, all dose was standardized as EQD2 using a linear quadratic model. EQD2 was estimated using the equation: EQD2 = n × d × [(α/β) + d]/[(α/β) + 2], where n is the number of treatment fractions, and d is the dose per fraction (Gy). Because complete OAR dosimetric data from prior radiotherapy were not always available, the cumulative D2cc values for the rectum and bladder were estimated by summing the prior target prescription dose and the current re-irradiation D2cc (α/β = 3).

Treatment-related adverse effects data were collected from electronic medical records and summarized as per CTCAE version 5.0. Acute toxicities were evaluated weekly during the course of re-irradiation and 1 month after completion. Late toxicities were defined as adverse events that occur 3 months after completion. Patients with a follow-up of less than 6 months who did not experience late adverse events were excluded from the late toxicity analysis. The cumulative incidence of late toxicity was estimated using the cumulative incidence function。.

Local control (LC) was calculated from the date of beginning salvage treatment to the date of any subsequent recurrence within the irradiated field. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time between the completion of re-irradiation and the date of any subsequent disease progression or death. LC and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with a log-rank test used to examine differences. P < 0.05 was considered to be significant. Tumor control probability (TCP) and Normal Tissue Complication probability plots as a function of dose and varying α/β were generated by fitting logistic regression models to binary clinical outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29) and R (version 2023.12.0). This study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (I-26PJ0506), the requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

Patient and treatment characteristics

A total of 102 cervical cancer patients who received re-irradiation from March 2020 to December 2024 were included in the study. Patient and treatment characteristics are presented in (Table 1). The median age of the patients was 55 years (IQR 47–59 years), with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) being the most common histological type in 85(83.3%). Regarding initial radiotherapy, the median prescription EQD2 (α/β = 10) was 89.6 Gy (IQR 65.6–89.6 Gy). Among the 102 patients, 90 (88.2%) received brachytherapy as part of their initial treatment. The purpose of the primary radiotherapy was categorized as “definitive,” “postoperative,” “salvage,” and “palliative.” “Definitive” refers to definitive radiotherapy for the primary tumor, “postoperative” to postoperative radiotherapy after radical surgery for the primary tumor, “salvage” to salvage radiotherapy for recurrence after radical surgery for the primary tumor, and “palliative” to previous palliative radiotherapy. The patterns of recurrence were classified into four groups: central (cervix or vaginal stump), pelvic (pelvic lymph nodes or pelvic side wall), central and pelvic, distant metastases with central or pelvic. The most frequent pattern of recurrence was central (46 patients, 45.1%), followed by distant metastases with central or pelvic (23 patients, 22.5%), central and pelvic (18 patients, 17.6%), and pelvic (15 patients, 14.7%). The median disease-free interval before re-irradiation was 18.3 months (IQR: 8.7–37.1 months), and the median time between primary RT and re-irradiation was 26.0 months (IQR 11.4–42.4 months). Twenty patients (19.6%) chose other treatments as initial treatment for recurrence before re-irradiation, including surgery, chemotherapy, and combined therapies, and re-irradiation was used as salvage treatment after local disease progression after initial treatment. The median D2cc (α/β = 3) of rectum and bladder for re-irradiation were 48.1 Gy (IQR 28.0–61.3 Gy) and 53.3 Gy (IQR 42.0–64.1 Gy). During re-irradiation, 30 patients (29.4%) received concurrent immunotherapy, and 57 patients (55.9%) received concurrent chemotherapy. No patients received bevacizumab or other VEGF inhibitors either concurrently with or sequentially to re-irradiation.

Survival outcomes

The median follow-up time was 13.7 months (IQR 6.8–24.8 months). Among the patients, 36 (35.3%) experienced in-field disease progression after re-irradiation, and 51 (50.0%) had recurrence or metastasis. The median PFS was 8.4 months, while the median LC times were not reached. The overall 1- and 2-year LC were 67.6% (95% CI 58.6%-77.9%) and 59.2% (95% CI 49.0%-69.7%), and the overall 1- and 2-year PFS were 52.7% (95% CI 43.3%-64.3%) and 42.6% (95% CI 32.8%-55.5%) (Fig. 1A and B). Tumor control probability (TCP) as a function of reirradiation prescribed dose (EQD2) is depicted in Fig. 1C. The 2-year local control of those who received reirradiation prescription EQD2 (α/β = 10) ≥ 50 Gy and < 50 Gy was 72.1% vs. 0.49.9% (p = 0.018).

We analyzed the impact of various clinical and radiotherapy-related factors on survival outcomes, including age, histology, purpose of primary radiotherapy, disease-free interval, interval between two radiotherapies, SCC-Ag antigen level at recurrence, re-irradiation modality and prescription EQD2 (α/β = 10), pattern of recurrence and presence of distant metastasis. The disease-free interval (≤ 2 years vs. >2 years) was significantly associated with PFS and LC (Fig. 2A and B), with patients having longer intervals tending to have better PFS and LC. Patients with distant metastasis had significantly worse PFS (p = 0.04, Fig. 2C), although there was no significant difference in LC (p = 0.79, Fig. 2D). Toxicity evaluation

Acute toxicities of grade ≥ 2 was observed in 45 patients (44.1%), including hematologic, gastrointestinal (GI), and genitourinary (GU) adverse events. Specifically, 19 patients (18.6%) developed grade ≥ 2 hematologic toxicity, 16 patients (15.6%) experienced grade ≥ 2 GI toxicity, and 15 patients (14.6%) had grade ≥ 2 GU toxicity. Overall, six patients (5.8%) experienced grade ≥ 3 acute toxicities. No grade 4 or 5 toxicities were observed in this study. The incidence of grade ≥ 2 acute toxicity was analyzed according to clinical and dosimetric factors, including age, concurrent immunotherapy, concurrent chemotherapy, and prescription EQD2 (α/β = 10) using multivariant regression (Supplementary Table 1). A higher prescription EQD2 was significantly associated with severe acute complications. Figure 3B and C compared NTCP values between patients who received concurrent chemotherapy or concurrent immunotherapy and those who did not. NTCP plots for any grade ≥ 2 acute toxicity with re-irradiation rectal D2cc (α/β = 10) showed similar trends. (Supplementary Fig. 2) .

Late toxicities were analyzed in 86 patients. Sixteen patients were excluded from this analysis because they had a follow-up less than 6 months and did not experience any late toxicities. Grade ≥ 2 late toxicities occurred in 46 patients (53%), whereas 42 patients (48.8%) had grade ≥ 2 late GU toxicities, 18 patients had grade ≥ 2 late GI toxicities. 23 patients (26.7%) had grade ≥ 3 severe complication, included 8 rectovaginal fistulas, 11 vesicovaginal fistulas, 3 severe radiation cystitis, 1 severe radiation proctitis. Four cases of Grade 1 hematochezia and 1 case of Grade 2 hematochezia were observed. Risk factor analysis was performed for late adverse events. The cumulative incidence of grade ≥ 2 late toxicity was 36.2% (95% CI: 25.2–45.7%) at 1 year and 56.6% (95% CI: 43.7–66.6%) at 2 years. The cumulative incidence of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity was 18.1% (95% CI: 9.4–26.0%) at 1 year and 30.4% (95% CI: 18.4–40.6%) at 2 years. (Supplementary Fig. 2). Higher rectal irradiation D2cc (α/β = 3) was significantly associated with severe late complications (Fig. 4A), patients who received a rectal irradiation D2cc (α/β = 3) ≥50 Gy had a higher incidence of any grade ≥ 2 late toxicity (84.3% vs. 31.2%, p < 0.001) and overall grade ≥ 3 toxicity (37.5% vs. 15.6%, p = 0.04). Bladder D2cc (α/β = 3) was significantly associated with severe late complications (Fig. 4B), patients who received a bladder irradiation D2cc ≥ 50 Gy had a higher incidence of any grade ≥ 2 toxicity (75.6% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.001), overall grade ≥ 3 toxicity (35.1% vs. 14.8%, p = 0.06). However, higher prescription EQD2 (α/β = 3) for re-irradiation was not significantly correlated with late toxicity (p = 0.16, Fig. 4C).

We further analyzed the impact of cumulative organ doses, calculated by summing the prescription EQD2 dose from prior radiotherapy and the rectal or bladder D2cc from re-irradiation (as detailed in Methods). The median cumulative rectal D2cc (α/β = 3) was 140.3 Gy (IQR: 111.1–160.6 Gy). Patients with a cumulative rectal D2cc ≥ 140 Gy had significantly higher rates of any grade ≥ 2 late toxicity (85.3% vs. 30.2%, p < 0.001, Fig. 5A) and grade ≥ 3 toxicity (38.7% vs. 13.3%, p = 0.02, Fig. 5B). Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that cumulative rectal D2cc values of 100 Gy, 140 Gy, and 170 Gy corresponded to 16%, 27%, and 37% probabilities of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity, respectively. Cumulative bladder D2cc, Cumulative EQD2 (α/β = 3) was also significantly associated with late toxicity risk (Fig. 5C and D). Multivariate analysis including age, history of pelvic surgery, interval between radiotherapy courses (> 2 years vs. ≤2 years), and cumulative rectal D2cc (α/β = 3) revealed that only the cumulative rectal dose remained an independent predictor of late toxicity (Table 2).

Discussion

The recurrence rate of cervical cancer within the irradiated field after radical or adjuvant radiotherapy is 20–40% [20]. The treatment of in-field recurrent cervical cancer remains a major challenge, because many patients are not suitable for surgery due to the difficulties associated with operating on previously irradiated tissue, and radiotherapy can serve as an effective treatment for local recurrent lesions. Re-irradiation has a higher risk of toxicity, and a balance must be achieved between the prescribed dose to the tumor region and the dose limits of normal tissues [21]. Over the past decades, the development of image-guided radiotherapy techniques, such as IMRT, VMAT, and IGBT, has greatly improved the safety and efficacy of re-irradiation [22–24]. Brachytherapy techniques such as ICBT and ISBT are suitable for central recurrences with small tumor volume [25–27], while external beam techniques such as IMRT, VMAT and SBRT are appropriate for larger tumors or non-central recurrences [15, 27–29]. For many patients, a combination of external beam radiotherapy and brachytherapy is required in order to maximize tumor dose while controlling dose to organs at risk. However, there are still few studies on the relationship between re-irradiation dose and tumor control, as well as OAR dose and toxicity, although these factors are very important for treatment selection. One study including 70 re-irradiated patients found that cumulative EQD2 ≥ 130 Gy was associated with a higher rate of grade ≥ 3 late toxicity (34.6% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.008) [30]. However, that study did not analyze the relationship between specific OAR doses and toxicity events, nor did it include patients receiving immunotherapy.

Recent studies on cervical cancer re-irradiation have reported 2-year LC rates of 51%-65% and 2-year PFS rates of 38%-61% [14, 24, 28]. In our study, the 2-year LC rate was 59.2% and the 2-year PFS rate was 42.6%, which were comparable to previous reports. Interval to re-irradiation has been found to be significantly associated with prognosis in several studies [24, 29]. In our study, the interval between the two radiotherapy courses showed a certain correlation with LC and PFS after re-irradiation, but did not reach statistical significance. However, the PFS interval from completion of initial radiotherapy to the detection of recurrence or metastasis was significantly associated with PFS after re-irradiation, and its association with LC approached significance. This may be because, in our study, some patients underwent surgery or multiple cycles of chemotherapy or immunotherapy after disease progression was detected, resulting in a delay in the initiation of re-irradiation, and therefore the PFS after the initial radiotherapy may better reflect tumor characteristics than the simple time interval between two radiation courses. In our cohort, 23 patients had distant metastases at the time of re-irradiation. These patients had significantly worse PFS than those without metastasis, but LC was not different, indicating that re-irradiation can serve as an effective local tumor control method even in patients with distant metastases. There are few studies on the dose–response relationship for TCP. One study found that EQD2 ≥ 50 Gy was associated with improved in-field control, and our study reached a similar conclusion [30].

In the TCP and re-irradiation dose relationship curve (Fig. 1C), tumor control increased with re-irradiation dose. However, this correlation was weaker than the relationship between toxicity and dose, which may be due to the many factors influencing tumor control (such as tumor proliferation rate and tumor size). In our study, 55 patients had received at least one cycle of immunotherapy after recurrence: 8 patients received immunotherapy before re-irradiation, 30 patients received concurrent immunotherapy during re-irradiation, and 44 patients continued consolidation immunotherapy after re-irradiation. Because the proportion of patients with distant metastasis was much higher in the immunotherapy group than in those without immunotherapy (36.3% vs. 6.3%), and the immunotherapy regimens varied greatly among patients, our study could not investigate the relationship between immunotherapy and patient prognosis. The relationship between combined re-irradiation plus immunotherapy and treatment efficacy requires further research.

Because all re-irradiation in this study was image-guided, we were able to investigate the relationship between OAR dose and toxicity. For acute toxicity, we found that prescription EQD2 and rectal D2cc both predicted grade ≥ 2 toxicity (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 3). Among patients receiving concurrent chemotherapy, the rate of grade ≥ 2 toxicity was 52.6% (30/57), compared with 33.3% (15/45) among those without concurrent chemotherapy. Concurrent chemotherapy did not significantly increase the risk of grade ≥ 2 hematologic toxicity (25.6% vs. 18.4%, P = 0.477). Among the 30 patients receiving concurrent immunotherapy, 14 had grade ≥ 2 toxicity (46.67%), compared with 40.28% (29/72) among those without concurrent immunotherapy. None of these differences were statistically significant.,

For late toxicity, using rectal D2cc or bladder D2cc from the re-irradiation plan to predict grade ≥ 2 toxicity was superior to using re-irradiation prescription EQD2 alone (Fig. 4A, B and C). Since late toxicity mainly involves GI/GU organs, prescription EQD2 alone cannot accurately represent rectal or bladder dose. Although voxel-wise dose summation represents the most accurate method for calculating cumulative OAR dose, its implementation in clinical practice is extremely challenging. Previous studies have relied on the direct summation of prescription EQD2 [19, 31]. In this study, the prior prescription EQD2 was added to D2cc of current re-irradiation plan. Although this approach has inherent limitations compared to voxel-wise dose summation, our analysis (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 4) demonstrated that this summation was a superior predictor of late toxicity compared to a simple summation of two prescription doses. Using this method, the median cumulative rectal dose was 140.3 Gy and the median cumulative bladder dose was 145.9 Gy, both higher than the recommended limits in several guidelines [19, 32, 33]. Several recent studies have reported rates of severe late toxicity following pelvic re-irradiation. A Japanese survey including 165 patients treated with HDR brachytherapy re-irradiation reported a 30% rate of Grade ≥ 3 late toxicity, with 34 patients (20.6%) developing severe complications associated with vaginal ulceration or necrosis [14]. Another recent study using image-guided radiotherapy reported a Grade ≥ 3 late toxicity rate of 23% in a recurrent gynecologic cancer cohort treated with pelvic re-irradiation [30]. The etiology of certain complications, such as fistulas, can be multifactorial. In our study, five patients experienced fistulas alongside local tumor progression. While tumor infiltration significantly contributes to tissue friability, we conservatively classified these events as treatment-related late toxicities due to the difficulty in isolating the primary cause.Among 8 patients who developed rectovaginal fistula, the median re-irradiation rectal D2cc was 61 Gy and the median cumulative rectal dose was 162 Gy. Among 11 patients with vesicovaginal fistula, the median re-irradiation bladder D2cc was 58 Gy and the median cumulative bladder dose was 147 Gy. These elevated cumulative OAR doses were likely a contributing factor in the development of these complications, consistent with the dose-toxicity relationship demonstrated in our cohort analysis. These findings echo the Delphi consensus principle that OAR constraints should generally take priority over target volume coverage during re-irradiation planning [19]. Accordingly, more stringent adherence to cumulative OAR dose constraints should be emphasized in future re-irradiation practice, particularly in patients whose cumulative rectal or bladder doses approach or exceed accepted tolerance limits, even if some compromise in target coverage is required.

This study reports the largest single-center cohort of re-irradiation for recurrent cervical cancer. However, our study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis, which inherently carries risks of selection bias. Second, there was significant heterogeneity in patient scenarios and treatment regimens, including various prior radiation doses and different combinations of EBRT and brachytherapy during re-irradiation. Third, the median follow-up of 13.7 months is relatively short for a comprehensive evaluation of late complications. Although multi-institutional data would be a valuable next step to increase the sample size, a prospective study is ultimately warranted to provide higher-level evidence and to further standardize re-irradiation protocols for recurrent cervical cancer.

Conclusion

Re-irradiation is a feasible and effective salvage option for patients with recurrent cervical cancer and provides moderate local control with acceptable toxicity.
