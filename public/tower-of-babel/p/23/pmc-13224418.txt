Social inequalities in cause-specific premature mortality in rural and urban France: a pre-pandemic population attributable fraction analysis
Authors: Nour Mahrouseh, Florian Bonnet, Orsolya Varga, Aline Scohy, Olivier Bruyère, Cyrille Delpierre, Brecht Devleesschauwer, Grant M. A. Wyper, Romana Haneef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial inequalities remain a major determinant of mortality across Europe. This study aimed first to quantify cause-specific premature mortality across urban and rural areas in mainland France during the pre-pandemic period, taking into account social inequalities, and second to assess the extent of these social inequalities using the population attributable fraction (PAF) approach.MethodsCause-specific deaths were identified from the French national mortality database and linked to municipality-level deprivation quintiles using French-European Deprivation Index (F-EDI). Residual life expectancy at age of death was defined according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 reference life table. Age-standardized years of life lost rates (ASYRs) were calculated by sex, deprivation quintile, and rural–urban setting. Social inequalities in mortality were assessed using absolute and relative gaps between the least and most deprived quintiles (Q1-Q5) and PAF, with Q1 as the reference.ResultsASYRs for Level 1 GBD causes increased consistently with area deprivation in both urban and rural mainland France. Non-communicable diseases accounted for most premature mortality (85–90% of total YLL) in both sexes, followed by injuries and communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional causes. In urban areas, 18–31% of cause-specific premature mortality was attributed to social inequalities, compared with 8–20% in rural areas, with higher contributions among males. The absolute difference in ASYRs was slightly larger in rural than urban areas for both sexes (females: 2,854 vs. 2,463; males: 6,607 vs. 5,618). Relative inequalities were similar across both settings (females: 1.30; males: 1.39, comparing Q5 with Q1). By cause, breast cancer showed the largest inequality among females (11–12% higher in Q5), while lung cancer exhibited the highest disparity among males (75% higher in Q5 in urban areas and 43% in rural areas).ConclusionsSocial inequalities substantially contributed to cause-specific premature mortality in pre-pandemic mainland France. ASYRs increased with deprivation in both urban and rural areas and were consistently higher among males. The persistent deprivation gradient and higher PAFs highlight the particularly marked impact of social inequalities in urban areas. These findings provide a pre-pandemic baseline for evaluating post–COVID-19 trends in premature mortality and health disparities in France.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-026-00486-1.

Introduction

Despite major gains in life expectancy across Europe, mortality remains strongly structured by social inequalities, with individuals in lower socioeconomic strata continuing to face substantially higher risks of premature death [1]. A landmark multicohort analysis by Stringhini et al. demonstrated that low socioeconomic status (SES) is strongly associated with premature mortality, independently of conventional behavioral and metabolic risk factors, across 1.7 million adults from several European cohorts [2]. More recent evidence quantified that a considerable proportion of premature deaths in England between 2003 and 2018 [3], and subnational inequalities in premature mortality across Europe [4], were directly attributable to socioeconomic inequality, highlighting the enduring impact of deprivation on early mortality.

In Italy, Alicandro et al. (2018) observed strong associations between lower education and higher premature mortality across nearly nine million adults, confirming the role of educational attainment as a critical social determinant [5]. Similarly, Hrzic et Vogt (2024) showed that socioeconomic patterning in avoidable mortality continues to drive disparities in life expectancy across Europe [6]. These inequalities could be increasing as highlighted by Long et al. (2023) who found that educational inequalities in mortality have widened in more recent birth cohorts across six European countries, despite advances in healthcare and prevention [7]. Together, these studies underscore that socioeconomic disadvantage remains a fundamental and persistent driver of premature and avoidable mortality within high-income European settings, and that its reduction could yield substantial additional improvements in life expectancy.

In France, dedicated peer-reviewed studies that specifically examine cause-specific premature mortality by SES remain comparatively scarce. However, national statistical sources provide consistent evidence of pronounced inequalities [8]. Analyses from Directorate for Research, Studies, Evaluation and Statistics (DREES-2025) similarly indicate that premature deaths (< 65 years) represent around 15% of all deaths, with persistent excess risk observed in socioeconomically disadvantaged and deprived areas [9]. These national figures highlight that, despite France’s universal healthcare system, significant social inequalities in mortality remain a pressing public health concern [10]. France ranks among the countries with the highest life expectancy worldwide, yet it continues to experience substantial levels of premature mortality as well as marked social inequalities in health. Quantifying the contribution of these two factors is essential for informing targeted interventions, with the potential to further increase life expectancy in France to levels approaching, or even surpassing those observed in Japan, the country with highest life expectancy.

Addressing socioeconomic inequalities remains a major global public health challenge. Several epidemiological and demographic methods have been developed to quantify health inequality. Many commonly used measures describe absolute or relative differences in health outcomes between socioeconomic groups but do not account the population size of each group [11, 12]. Other indicators, such as slope index of inequality (SII) and relative index of inequality (RII), are distributional measures that incorporate both the population size and the relative socio-economic position of groups. Another widely used indicator is the population attributable fraction (PAF), which estimates the potential reduction in the frequency of a health outcome if all individuals had experienced the same rate as those in the highest, most advantaged socioeconomic category [13]. PAFs have been used to quantify the proportion of deaths that could be theoretically avoided if everyone had the same mortality risk as the least deprived group [12, 13]. Simple and interpretable measures are needed to communicate inequalities to policymakers, monitor trends over time, and identify population groups and diseases most affected.

Years of life lost (YLL) quantify the population-level impact of premature mortality by incorporating both the frequency and age-distribution of mortality [14]. Compared with simple death counts or mortality rates, YLL give greater weight to deaths occurring at younger ages and therefore better capture the burden of premature mortality. As a result, YLL are particularly useful for informing policy strategies aimed at reducing health inequalities, particularly as they characterise the proportionate impact of different causes. YLL have been applied in subnational [15, 16] and global contexts [17], and are key metric of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies. Previous studies have applied the PAF framework to estimate disease burden attributable to environmental exposures, including cause-specific mortality [18, 19]. For example, Sell et al. estimated national-level PAFs for multiple health outcomes attributable to long-term NO₂ exposure in Germany, using exposure models that distinguish rural and urban background concentrations [18]. However, their PAF estimates were not stratified by rural–urban residence or by social groups, and social inequalities were not explicitly quantified. Other recent work has examined how urban development levels or social equity may moderate relationships between environmental or climate exposures and health outcomes, but without estimating cause-specific premature mortality using a PAF approach or explicitly decomposing inequalities across rural and urban populations [19]. To our knowledge, no previous study has jointly quantified social inequalities in cause-specific premature mortality using the PAF approach while explicitly comparing rural and urban populations in France.

Using national mortality data from 2017, previously employed to estimate YLL, this study aimed first to quantify cause-specific premature mortality across urban and rural areas in mainland France during the pre-pandemic period, taking into account social inequalities, and second to assess the extent of these social inequalities using the population attributable fraction (PAF) approach. As a proof of concept, we used the 2017 data to develop and validate a methodological framework for incorporating social inequalities into estimates of cause-specific premature mortality by urban–rural context. This framework can be applied to more recent datasets in future analyses.

Methods

French - European deprivation index

Social inequalities in cause-specific mortality were assessed using the 2017 version of the French-European Deprivation Index (F-EDI), which was most recent available at the time of this study was undertaken. F-EDI is a proxy for social deprivation measured at the municipal level in mainland France [20]. The F-EDI is derived from French census data provided by Institute National de la Statistique et des études économiques (INSEE), using a standardized methodology that combines ten census-based variables aggregated at the area level and associated with individual-level deprivation [20, 21]: proportion of individuals of foreign nationality ; proportion of households without a car ; proportion of individuals employed as managers or intermediate professionals ; proportion of single-parent families ; proportion of households with at least two individuals ; proportion of non-owner occupied households ; proportion of unemployed individuals ; proportion of individuals without post-secondary school education ; proportion of overcrowded dwellings, and proportion of non-married individuals). In the F-EDI, the first quintile (Q1) represents the least deprived areas, and the fifth quintile (Q5) represents the most deprived. Quintiles were constructed by weighting municipalities according to population size, ensuring equal population proportions across the five quintiles. This population-weighted quintile approach facilitates interpretation, comparability with previous public health research, and transparent assessment of social gradients in premature mortality. The use of discrete deprivation categories is well suited to the PAF framework, in which estimates are interpreted across exposure groups. Continuous modeling of deprivation was therefore not pursued to avoid reduced interpretability and unnecessary model complexity in PAF estimation.

Mortality data

We used the French mortality database for the year 2017 including all causes of deaths based on individual level data, from the Center for Epidemiology on Medical Causes of deaths (CépiDc: Centre d’épidémiologie sur les causes médicales de décès) [22]. Death certificates received by CépiDc are coded according to the rules of the 10th revision of the international classification of diseases (ICD-10) and related health problems on 3–4 digits by the World Health Organization (WHO) [23]. Mortality data underwent standard cleaning and harmonization procedures, as described in priviously published studies [16, 24]. Some cause of death are classified as ill-defined causes of death (IDDs) (also known as “garbage codes”), and are not regarded as sufficiently informative causes of death for public health analysis [25]. These IDDs were redistributed to plausible underlying causes using a probabilistic redistribution algorithm adapted from the Belgian Burden of Disease approach [15]. This four-step method first identifies a set of predefined ICD-10 target codes for selected ill-defined codes, exploits multiple-cause-of-death data where available, applies internal redistributions based on co-occurring specific codes, and finally redistributes remaining IDDs across all-causes. Common types of ill-defined codes include unspecified or intermediate causes that cannot be considered valid underlying causes of death (e.g., unspecified stroke, ill-defined cancer sites, heart failure). In France, IDDs accounted for approximately 36% of all deaths in 2017 [16]. The list of ICD-10 target codes used for redistribution is described in the study by [15] and IDDs were redistributed using established probabilistic method, which has been applied in recent European burden of disease studies [24]. Further details on the method used to map and group ICD-10 causes of deaths according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) cause hierarchy are described elsewhere [15, 16]. Deaths were attributed to the municipality of residence. We adopted a binary urban–rural residence classification based on the official framework of the French National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE), derived from the 2017 census and the degree of urbanization at the municipality level [26]. Urban areas correspond to municipalities or groups of municipalities forming an urban unit with more than 2,000 inhabitants living in a contiguous built-up area, while all remaining municipalities, including those with low or very low population density, are classified as rural according to INSEE’s municipal density typology. Under this framework, peri-urban or intermediate municipalities are not treated as a separate analytical category but are classified as either urban or rural based on population density and built-up continuity criteria. This binary classification was chosen because it represents the only standardized, nationwide urban–rural typology available for linkage with cause-specific mortality data in France. This classification is widely used in policy and official statistics, including EUROSTAT, facilitating interpretation and comparability. In addition, cause-specific mortality and socioeconomic data are consistently available at this level of aggregation, whereas more granular or gradient-based urbanicity measures would complicate estimation and interpretation within a PAF framework.

For this study, we included all individuals who died as residents of France, covering approximately 30,220 municipalities (4141 urban and 26,079 rural). As a proof of concept, we used the 2017 data to develop and validate a methodological framework for incorporating social inequalities into estimates of cause-specific premature mortality by urban–rural context. This framework can be applied to more recent datasets in future analyses.

Age-standardized years of life lost rates

Firstly, the F-EDI was integrated with mortality data at the municipality level, extracted from the INSERM-CépiDc mortality database using INSEE municipality codes. These deaths were then matched with the corresponding municipality codes from the F-EDI to assign a deprivation quintile to each municipality in mainland France. Therefore, deaths recorded in the French overseas territories (~ 335 municipalities, representing approximately 2% of all deaths in France in 2017), were excluded from the analysis. Each municipality was also classified according to the INSEE’s rural–urban typology. Causes of death were then described by cause-specific outcome, sex, area-level deprivation and rural-urban classification. Secondly, to calculate YLL, the number of deaths aggregated at the municipality level were multiplied by the reference life expectancy from the GBD 2019 study reference life Table [27]. We used the GBD reference life table to facilitate the comparison of these results with other studies. Using these crude YLL estimates, age-standardized years of life lost rates (ASYRs) were then calculated by applying the age structure of the 2013 European Standard population [28], to the observed age-specific rates in France, which were derived from French population [29]. This approach enabled like-for-like comparisons across various geographical areas. Figure 1 illustrates the methodological flow chart with different analytical steps followed in the analysis. AllASYRs results were expressed per 100,000 population. We ranked causes of premature death by ASYR in the least deprived quintile (Q1) for each sex and for both urban and rural settings to enable comparison across other deprivation quintiles.

Absolute and relative inequalities

We measured absolute and relative inequalities in cause-specific ASYRs, defined as the range between the most (Q5) and least (Q1) deprived quintiles. Absolute inequality was defined as the difference in ASYRs between Q5 and Q1, while the relative inequality was defined as the ratio of ASYRs between these two groups (either Q5 divided by Q1 or Q1 divided by Q5) [12, 30].

Population attributable fraction

The population attributable fraction (PAF) was estimated using the least socially deprived quintile (Q1) as the reference category, stratified by cause of death, sex, area-level deprivation, and rural–urban classification. For each stratum, the PAF quantifies the proportion of observed ASYRs that would be theoretically reduced under a counterfactual scenario in which all population groups experienced the same mortality rates as the reference group (Q1). Accordingly, PAFs are interpreted as counterfactual estimates of the mortality burden associated with social inequalities.

Statistical uncertainty was quantified using 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for the three key metrics: ASYRs, absolute and relative inequalities and PAFs. Because ASYRs and range metrics represent non-negative quantities, a log-normal distribution was assumed to ensure positive lower bounds. In contrast, PAFs can take both negative and positive values; therefore, uncertainty was modeled using a normal distribution to accommodate the full range of estimates. Each UI was derived from 2,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated distributions defining the lower and upper bounds, respectively. This approach provides realistic uncertainty that reflect the direction and magnitude of estimated associations and inequalities. Similar distributional assumptions and simulation-based uncertainty estimation have been applied in previous burden of disease and PAF-based studies, including those by Otavová et al. [31] and in WHO-aligned environmental burden of disease analyse [18]. We also conducted regression analysis using negative binomial model to examine the robustness of the observed social gradients in cause-specific YLL across municipalities. These models included social deprivation quintiles (F-EDI) as the main exposure and were adjusted for age group, sex, area type (urban/rural), and GBD level 2 to account for variation across major cause-of-death groups. Cardiovascular diseases were used as the GBD level 2 reference because they are the leading contributor to YLL, providing a stable, high-burden benchmark for comparing cause-specific YLL. Cause-specific YLL was used as the outcome, with the municipality population included as an offset to account for differences in population size. Results are presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed in R 4.1.0. This study is reported in adherence with the STROBOD statement [32] (Supplementary material 1: Additional file 1).

Results

Figure 2 displays sex-specific ASYR values across deprivation quintiles for Level 1 GBD cause (non-communicable diseases, injuries and communicable diseases), distinguishing between urban (Fig. 2a) and rural mainland France (Fig. 2b). Overall ASYRs for non-communicable diseases, injuries, and communicable diseases in urban mainland France showed a consistent gradient across area deprivation for both sexes (Fig. 2a). Among females, these rates varied from 8,263 [95% UI: 6,737-9,966] per 100,000 in the least deprived quintile (Q1) to 10,726 [95% UI: 8,821 − 13,026] in the most deprived (Q5), representing an absolute difference of about 2,463 per 100,000. The relative inequality was 1.30, indicating a 30% higher rate in Q5 compared to Q1. Among males, rates increased from 14,271 [95% UI: 1,698 − 17,168] to 19,889 [95% UI: 16,321 − 24,032], an absolute difference of approximately 5,618 per 100,000, with a relative inequality of 1.39 (39% higher in Q5 compared to Q1).

Rural mainland France has higher levels of ASYR than urban, with a similar relative inequality between the most and least deprived areas (Fig. 2b). Among females, total ASYRs were observed from 9,571 [95% UI: 7,830 − 11,600] per 100,000 in the least deprived quintile (Q1) to 12,425 [95% UI: 10,100 − 15,000] in the most deprived (Q5), an absolute difference of around 2,854 per 100,000, with a relative inequality of 1.30 (30% higher in Q5 than in Q1). Among males, rates increased from 16,791 (95% UI: 13,700 − 20,900) to 23,398 [95% UI: 18,100 − 27,600], a difference of roughly 6,607 per 100,000, with a relative inequality of 1.39 (39% higher in Q5 than in Q1).

In both urban and rural areas, non-communicable diseases accounted for most of the premature mortality, in both sexes (approximately 85–90% of total YLL), followed by injuries and communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional causes. The consistent deprivation gradient across sexes indicates that social inequalities in cause-specific premature mortality persist in both rural and urban settings, with relatively larger absolute differences among males.

Figure 4 presents the ten leading Level 2 GBD causes of premature mortality in urban (Fig. 4a) and rural mainland France (Fig. 4b), for both males and females. These causes were consistently higher across deprivation quintile for both sexes. Among females, other non-communicable diseases rank among the top ten causes of YLL in both urban and rural settings. Among males, transport injuries appear prominently in urban areas, while infectious causes, such as diarrheal and lower respiratory diseases, contribute more substantially to the top ten in rural areas. Across sexes and settings, neoplasms, cardiovascular diseases, and neurological disorders were the leading contributors to premature mortality. These causes increased consistently with area deprivation for both sexes. In urban areas, among females, ASYRs for neoplasms varied from 3,867 [95% UI: 3,162-4,686] per 100,000 in the least deprived quintile (Q1) to 4,870 [95% UI: 3,971-5,849] in the most deprived (Q5), representing an absolute difference of about 1,004 per 100,000 a relative inequality of 1.26 (26% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 4a). Among males, rates increased from 6,106 [95% UI: 4,998-7,363] to 8,343 [95% UI: 6,831 − 10,141], an absolute difference of approximately 2,236 per 100,000, a relative inequality of 1.37, indicating ~ 37% higher rates in Q5 (Fig. 4a). In rural areas, neoplasms ASYRs showed consistent gradient across deprivation quintiles for both sexes. Among females, rates extended from 4,378 [95% UI: 3,579-5,331] per 100,000 in Q1 to 5,238 [95% UI:4,287-6,308] in Q5, an absolute difference of 860 per 100,000 and a relative inequality of 1.20 (~ 20% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 4b). Among males, rates varied from 7,134 [95% UI: 5,769-8,622] to 9,207 [95% UI: 7,519 − 11,101], an absolute difference of approximately 2,073 per 100,000 a relative inequality of 1.29 (29% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 4b). Overall, ASYRs for neoplasms and other leading causes, were consistently higher and showed steeper gradients across deprivation quintiles in urban than in rural areas, with males showing larger inequality gaps than females across both settings.

Figure 6 presents the 15 leading Level 3 GBD causes of premature mortality in urban (Fig. 6a) and rural mainland France (Fig. 6b), for both males and females. ASYRs for these outcomes were consistently higher across deprivation quintile in urban and rural settings for both sexes. In urban areas, among females, breast cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality, followed by Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias and tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer. Across deprivation quintiles, Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias showed the highest burden in Q3 (Fig. 6a). For instance, ASYRs for breast cancer rose from 904 [95% UI: 739–1095] in Q1 to 1001.5 [95%UI: 822–1203] in Q5, an absolute increase of 97 per 100,000 and a relative inequality of 1.11 (11% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 6a). Among males, tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality, followed by ischemic heart disease (IHD) and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. Across deprivation quintiles, stroke, self-harm, and colon and rectum cancer exceeded Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, highlighting a shift toward more deprivation-attributable causes (Fig. 6a). For instance, ASYRs for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer increased from 1,492 [95% UI: 1,226-1,799] to 2,614 [95% UI: 2,131-3,171], an absolute difference of 1,122 per 100,000 and a relative inequality of 1.75 (75% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 6a).

In rural areas, among females, breast cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality among the top 15 causes, followed by tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias (Fig. 6b). For instance, ASYRs for breast cancer ranged from 919 [95% UI: 747-1,109] in Q1 to 1025 [95%UI: 842-1,240] in Q5, an absolute difference of 107 per 100,000 and a relative inequality of 1.12 (12% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 6b). Among males, tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality among the top 15 causes, followed by ischemic heart disease (Fig. 6b). For instance, ASYRs for tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer varied from 1,937 per 100,000 [95% UI: 1,575-2,344] in Q1 to 2,764 [95% UI: 2,273-3,346] in Q5, an absolute difference of about 827 per 100,000 and a relative inequality of 1.43 (43% higher in Q5 than in Q1) (Fig. 6b).

In urban areas, the highest PAFs were observed for self-harm among females (49% [95% UI: 39–60%]) and cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases among males (49% [95% UI: 40–58%]) (Fig. 6a). Other major contributors included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus (females), tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer, ischemic heart disease, and stroke (male), with PAFs ranging approximately from 14% to 43% in urban settings (Fig. 6a).

In rural areas, the highest PAFs were observed for COPD for both sexes (females: 28% [95%UI: 22–33%]) vs. males: 30% [95%UI: 24–36%]), followed by diabetes mellitus 25% [95%UI: 20–29%] in females and road injuries in males 27% [95%UI: 22–32%] (Fig. 6b).

We also observed some negative PAF estimates, particularly for certain cancers. In urban areas, negative values were noted among females for uterine cancer (− 19% [95% UI: −23% to − 15%]) and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias (− 1% [95% UI: −1.3% to − 0.8%]). In rural areas, negative PAFs were observed among females for brain and central nervous system (CNS) cancer (− 10% [95% UI: −14% to − 6%]) and ovarian cancer (− 5% [95% UI: −6% to − 4%]). We reported the ASYR estimates for the diseases shown in the figures, including the absolute and relative inequalities, PAFs with their uncertainty intervals, by sex and urban–rural areas, in Supplementary Material 1: Additional File 2.

Overall, deprivation-attributable mortality impacts (PAF) across GBD Levels 1, 2, and 3 causes were consistently greater in urban than in rural areas and more pronounced among males than females. These patterns underscore the pervasive influence of socioeconomic inequality on premature mortality in mainland France.

Among urban males, lung cancer (2,000 ASYRs) represents the leading contributor to premature mortality, followed by IHD (1,400 ASYRs), self-harm injuries (760 ASYRs), and then strokes and colorectal cancers (717 ASYRs). However, this ranking differs when considering causes associated with the largest counterfactual reductions in ASYRs under a scenario of reduced inequalities. While lung cancer and IHD remain at the top, cirrhosis emerges next, driven by an exceptionally high PAF (49%), the highest among all causes considered. Self-harm injuries and COPD follow, both showing relatively large counterfactual YLL reduction despite comparatively lower overall YLL, corresponding to high PAF values. For rural males, the ranking of the three leading causes is similar, with lung cancer, IHD, and self-harm injuries again taking the top positions. However, differences between these causes are much narrower when considering expected reductions in ASYR rather than total ASYR. In terms of counterfactual reductions in premature mortality, road traffic injuries and cirrhosis follow, ahead of strokes and colorectal cancers. COPD again stands out for its relatively large counterfactual reductions, corresponding to a high PAF (30%).

For females, the contrast between total premature mortality and the counterfactual reductions associated with social inequalities is even more pronounced. In urban areas, breast cancer is the leading cause of premature mortality (approximately 900 ASYRs), followed by lung cancer (720 ASYRs), Alzheimer’s disease (680 ASYRs), and stroke (530 ASYRs). However, this ranking changes markedly when focusing on the counterfactual reduction in ASYR under a scenario in which the entire population experiences the mortality rates of the most privileged quintile. In particular, both breast cancer and Alzheimer’s disease drop out of the ranking of causes with the largest counterfactual reductions, reflecting extremely low or near-zero PAF values. In contrast, lung cancer ranks first in terms of counterfactual reductions in ASYRs (around 200 ASYRs), followed by self-harm injuries, corresponding to a very high PAF (49%), then IHD (around 100 ASYRs), and finally colorectal cancer and stroke. Notably, lung cancer and COPD display similar levels of counterfactual reductions in ASYR, despite lung cancer accounting for roughly six times more total YLL in the population. In rural areas, counterfactual reductions in ASYRs related to breast cancer remains very low. However, and in contrast to the urban context, Alzheimer’s disease shows relatively larger counterfactual reduction, indicating larger observed inequalities in premature mortality across deprivation groups. IHD and stroke follow in terms of counterfactual ASYR reductions. Diabetes also stands out, with comparatively larger counterfactual reductions (around 60 ASYR), corresponding to a relatively high PAF (25%) despite a more moderate contribution to total premature mortality.

The sensitivity analysis showed that higher levels of social deprivation (Q2 to Q5) were associated with lower cause-specific YLL compared with the least deprived municipalities (Q1), with IRRs decreasing consistently across F-EDI quintiles. Urban areas had substantially lower YLL compared with rural areas, after adjusting for age group, sex, and GBD Level 2 cause of death. Discussion

This is the first study to quantify the contribution of socioeconomic inequalities, measured by F-EDI, to cause-specific premature mortality using the PAF approach while accounting for the rural–urban context. In mainland France, ASYRs increased with increasing area social deprivation in both urban and rural settings and were generally higher among males and in urban areas. Non-communicable diseases accounted for most of the premature mortality (approximately 85–90% of total YLL) across both sexes and settings, followed by injuries and communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional causes. Among females in urban areas, the leading causes of premature mortality were breast cancer, followed by Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, and lung cancer. In rural areas, breast cancer remained the leading cause, but lung cancer ranked second, surpassing Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. Among males, lung cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality in both urban and rural areas. In urban areas, it was followed by ischemic heart disease (IHD) and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, whereas in rural areas, IHD ranked second, followed by self-harm and colon and rectum cancer.

Absolute differences in ASYRs were slightly larger in rural areas for both sexes, whereas relative inequalities were similar across urban and rural settings (1.30 for females, 1.39 for males), indicating comparable proportional inequalities. Thus, while the inequality gradient was similar in relative terms, but absolute levels are higher in rural areas, suggest a larger overall burden.

Counterfactual mortality burden (PAFs) was consistently greater in urban than in rural areas and more pronounced among males than females. For Level 1 GBD causes, an estimated 31–34% of premature deaths in urban areas and 22–28% in rural areas could have been avoided if all deprivation quintiles had experienced the same mortality rates as the least deprived group (Q1). The greatest inequalities in premature mortality were observed for chronic respiratory diseases, digestive diseases, and self-harm and interpersonal violence, whereas smaller disparities were seen for neoplasms and neurological disorders. These patterns varied by sex and between urban and rural areas, consistent with a previous study [31].

Our results showed increasing premature mortality rates from lung cancer across all deprivation groups, with the highest rates observed in the most deprived quintile (Q5). PAFs were higher among males than females (urban: 38% vs. 27%; rural: 16% vs. 17%). This pattern reflects the strong influence of socioeconomic disadvantage on smoking-related behaviors and exposure, as well as differences in prevention, early detection, and treatment access between sexes and settings. Smoking remains the main risk factor for lung cancer, accounting for 80–90% of all cases [33]. However, occupational exposures to carcinogens such as asbestos, silica, and diesel motor exhaust also contribute to lung cancer risk and may help explain part of the social inequalities observed in lung cancer incidence [34]. Cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases emerged as a leading cause of premature mortality among males, with a markedly higher contribution of inequality in urban areas compared to rural areas (49% vs. 25%, respectively). These findings are consistent with previous studies [35, 36]. Alcohol consumption is strongly linked to cirrhosis risk and contributes substantially to liver disease mortality [37]. Although several causes contributed substantially to premature mortality among females, diabetes mellitus stood out for its relatively high inequality impact, with PAFs of 29% in urban areas and 28% in rural areas. These results are consistent with previous European studies showing that diabetes-related mortality is more strongly associated with socioeconomic disadvantage among females than males, confirming the higher inequality impact observed in our analysis [38, 39]. We observed that despite lower ASYRs for self-harm among females compared with males (urban: 278 vs. 848 per 100,000; rural: 277 vs. 1,423 per 100,000), the relative inequality impact was markedly higher in urban females (PAF: 49%) than males (19%), whereas in rural areas, the inequality effect was greater in males (PAF: 20% vs. 6%). These patterns suggest that while males bear a greater overall burden of self-harm mortality, deprivation exerts a comparatively stronger relative influence among urban females. This finding is consistent with previous research highlighting pronounced socioeconomic gradients in self-harm and suicide risk, particularly among individuals living in deprived areas [40, 41]. The divergent urban–rural and sex patterns observed here may reflect contextual differences in social support, help-seeking behaviours, and access to mental health and other supporting services. These findings underscore the strong and persistent influence of socioeconomic inequality on premature mortality in mainland France, with variations by sex and between urban and rural settings.

Some causes of premature mortality for certain cancers, such as uterine (urban), ovarian (urban), brain cancers (rural), and prostate cancer (rural), showed negative or near-zero PAF estimates. This suggests the absence of a clear socioeconomic gradient or, in some cases higher mortality in less deprived areas. These patterns may reflect variations in disease etiology, diagnostic intensity, or access to screening and healthcare, particularly for cancers more frequently detected in advantaged populations [37, 42]. In comparison, Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias in urban areas showed PAF values of -1% for females and 1% for males, highlighting an inverse association among females. This finding aligns with previous research that examined gender-specific socioeconomic gradients in dementia mortality, showing that females, with the lowest educational attainment had approximately 1.29 times higher dementia mortality than those with highest education, indicating greater mortality in lower socioeconomic groups [43]. However, this gradient may be influenced by healthy-survivor bias, whereby individuals in the most deprived groups may follow a different life-course trajectories and may not survive earlier causes of death, such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) or cancer. As a result, fewer deprived individuals may live long enough for Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, to emerge as the cause of death, which could attenuate the observed socioeconomic gradient in dementia mortality. Sensitivity analyses using negative binomial regression yielded consistent results, confirming the robustness of the observed social gradients in cause-specific YLL across municipalities. In particular, the inverse association between deprivation and YLL and the substantially lower YLL observed in urban areas persisted after adjustment for age, sex, urbanicity, and cause of death. Overall, our findings highlight several counterintuitive patterns. While absolute premature mortality rates were generally higher in rural areas, a larger proportion was attributable to deprivation in urban settings, indicating steeper social gradients in cities. This contrast likely reflects differences in population structure and the social distribution of risk factors: rural areas tend to have older populations and a higher burden of chronic and degenerative conditions, contributing to elevated age-standardised rates, whereas socioeconomic differences in risk exposures and healthcare access are more strongly differentiated in urban areas, producing larger relative inequalities. Cause-specific mechanisms appear to vary. Inequalities in lung cancer reflect socially patterned smoking and occupational exposures; chronic liver disease is shaped by socioeconomic gradients in harmful alcohol use and access to treatment; and self-harm is influenced by deprivation-related psychosocial stressors, social isolation, and unequal access to mental health services. Although men experienced higher absolute mortality from self-harm, deprivation accounted for a larger share of female deaths in urban areas, suggesting sex-specific vulnerability to contextual disadvantage. Finally, near-zero or negative PAFs for some cancers and dementia point to complex or reversed socioeconomic gradients, likely driven by differences in screening, diagnosis, and survival rather than an absence of inequality in underlying risk.

This study has several strengths. First, it provides, to our knowledge, the first empirical overview of cause-specific premature mortality across urban and rural areas in mainland France during the pre-pandemic period, while also offering a methodological proof-of-concept for integrating social inequalities within a PAF framework. By explicitly quantifying the contribution of social inequalities to premature mortality, this approach illustrates how PAFs can be used to assess the magnitude of social disparities across causes of death. Second, the detailed cause-specific analysis based on the F-EDI, applied consistently across urban and rural contexts. This allowed us to capture geographic variations in health inequalities and provided a more comprehensive understanding of contextual differences in cause-specific premature mortality. Third, the use of F-EDI as a standardized, multidimensional, and validated measure of social deprivation strengthens the assessment of social inequalities [44]. By integrating key indicators related to education, employment, housing, and family structure, such as foreign nationality, unemployment, non-home ownership, single-parent families, and overcrowded dwellings, the F-EDI captures multiple components relevant to assessing social inequalities. Finally, the use of cause-specific data covering the entire mainland French population ensures high statistical power, minimizes selection bias, and supports robust and nationally representative estimates of premature mortality and inequality patterns.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, this study is based on a cross-sectional ecological design that links municipality-level social deprivation (F-EDI) to individual mortality data. Such a design is inherently subject to ecological fallacy and does not allow individual-level causal inference. It is therefore not possible to determine whether social deprivation directly contributes to premature mortality or whether changes in its components would lead to mortality reductions. Individual-level deprivation data were not available. PAF estimates should be interpreted as counterfactual estimates, rather than as causal or actionable effects. Second, we acknowledge that the baseline mortality estimates are from pre-pandemic periods. However, cause-specific mortality patterns tend to remain relatively stable over time, and socioeconomic inequalities are deeply rooted; therefore, the overall scale and direction of the results are likely to remain valid. Third, this study used the 2017 version of the F-EDI, which did not include data from the French overseas territories. This represents a limitation, as these territories often experience more pronounced health inequalities compared with mainland France [45], and their exclusion may lead to an underestimation of the overall magnitude of socioeconomic disparities. Fourth, we did not report ASYRs within each deprivation quintile by age group, which may limit the understanding of how deprivation-attributable disparities vary across age categories. This choice was driven by our primary objective, which was to highlight disparities by sex and between rural and urban settings. Moreover, because YLL inherently weights deaths by age at death, this measure already captures much of the age-related contribution to premature mortality. Finally, we acknowledge the presence of unmeasured confounding factors, such as regional variations in healthcare access, occupational structure, migration patterns, and environmental exposures, which may differ systematically between deprived and non-deprived areas as well as between urban and rural settings. These factors were not explicitly accounted for in the analysis due to the lack of harmonized data at the national level and may partly contribute to the observed social and geographic gradients in premature mortality. Further research is needed to examine the contributions of the individual components of the F-EDI and to identify which factors most strongly drive premature mortality. In addition, the lack of a time-trend analysis limits our ability to determine whether cause-specific premature mortality related to social inequalities has increased or remained stable over time. Updating these estimates using the most recent data, with the current results serving as a baseline, would allow assessment of the post-COVID-19 pandemic impact on cause-specific premature mortality and health inequalities in France. Finally, further methodological work could explore how variation in life expectancy assumptions, including alternative life tables, influences estimates of cause-specific premature mortality and PAFs.

Implications to public health policy

The observed urban–rural disparities in cause-specific premature mortality across mainland France suggest that national policies may have uneven impacts depending on local contexts. These differences should be considered not only in relation to deprivation, but also how policies are implemented and experienced in urban and rural settings. The higher cause-specific premature mortality rates and larger inequality-attributable burden in urban areas underscore the need for policies that are sensitive to both socioeconomic and geographic contexts. To address these urban–rural disparities, public health interventions should be adapted to local contexts. In rural areas, improving access to prevention and care through telemedicine and mobile health services may help reduce premature mortality by overcoming geographic barriers and healthcare workforce shortages [46]. In urban settings, targeted community-based interventions, such as neighborhood-level smoking cessation programs [47], alcohol harm reduction strategies, and improvements in the built environment, may be more effective in reducing socially patterned risk exposures in deprived populations. Such context-specific approaches are essential to reduce both socioeconomic and geographic inequalities in premature mortality. The public health policy should therefore focus on the following priorities: Address structural determinants of inequality: Reducing premature mortality requires policies that address the upstream drivers of health such as income, employment, education, and housing. Because these determinants manifest differently in urban and rural settings, national strategies must anticipate varying local impacts. Strengthen local environments: Area-level conditions shape how socioeconomic disadvantage translates into health outcomes. Deprived urban areas may require stronger regulation of unhealthy commodity environments, whereas rural areas may benefit from improved service availability and connectivity. Enhance prevention and access to care: Although upstream action is essential, health systems also play a vital role in mitigating downstream harm. Improving access to preventive services and tailoring outreach to underserved populations can help reduce persistent disparities. Use YLL and PAF jointly for priority-setting: YLL reflects the absolute burden, while PAF quantifies the share attributable to inequalities. Considering both measures allows policymakers to distinguish high-burden causes requiring broad population-wide action from those where targeted efforts to reduce inequalities would yield the greatest proportional gains. Differences by sex, age, and place of residence suggest that interventions should be adapted to local needs, whether in deprived urban areas facing concentrated risk exposures or rural communities facing limited access to services.

Conclusions

This study quantified the contribution of social inequalities to cause-specific premature mortality in mainland France during the pre-pandemic period, across rural and urban areas. ASYRs increased with area deprivation in both urban and rural settings and were consistently higher among males and in urban areas. Non-communicable diseases accounted for the most premature mortality (about 85–90% of total YLL), followed by injuries and communicable causes. Marked differences in leading causes emerged by sex and geography: among females, breast cancer was the primary contributor in both settings, while Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, and lung cancer varied in rank between urban and rural areas. Among males, lung cancer dominated across both settings, followed by IHD and Alzheimer’s disease in urban areas, and by IHD, self-harm, and colorectal cancer in rural areas. The consistent deprivation gradient observed for most causes confirms the persistence of social inequalities in cause-specific premature mortality, with larger absolute gaps among males. Deprivation-attributable fractions were also greater in urban areas and more pronounced in males than in females. These findings provide a valuable foundation for developing context-specific public health policies to reduce socioeconomic and area-specific disparities in cause-specific premature mortality. Tailoring health interventions to specific demographic groups and emphasizing early prevention could help reduce premature mortality and improve population health outcomes. Future research should update these estimates using the most recent data, with the current results serving as a baseline, for assessing the post-COVID-19 pandemic impact on premature mortality and health inequalities in France.
