Aging Effects on Lipid Metabolism in Response to Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Regina F. Fernandez, Paulina Weglarczyk, Shaghayegh Zokaei, Suzette Huguenin, Joseph Scafidi, Jessica M. Ellis, Susanna Scafidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraumatic brain injury (TBI) is highly prevalent among very young and older adults, with poorer outcomes and longer recovery in aged individuals. The brain is a lipid‐rich organ and high rates of membrane remodeling occur during recovery, yet remain poorly delineated, especially in aged. To determine the impact of age on lipid metabolism during TBI recovery, TBI was induced by control cortical impact in 3 (young) and 20 (aged) month‐old mice. The ipsilateral cortex was harvested at 1‐, 3‐, 7‐, and 28‐day post injury (dpi) for analysis of gene expression, membrane lipidomics, total lipid fatty acid profile, and intermediates of fatty acid β‐oxidation, acylcarnitines. Lipid metabolizing genes were largely downregulated in response to TBI in young mice, yet remained unchanged or were increased in aged mice, resulting in significantly higher expression in aged compared to young. TBI increased acylcarnitines by a robust ~3‐fold in both young and aged cohorts acutely following injury and restored to sham levels by day 28. Phospholipidome compositional analysis reveals largest changes in the aged mice at 28 dpi. Aged mice phospholipid profiles shifted towards higher mono‐ and di‐ unsaturated and lower saturated and highly polyunsaturated species. Phospholipids with 4 unsaturated bonds, predicted to contain arachidonic acid, tended to increase post‐TBI, whereas species containing 6 unsaturated bonds, predicted to contain docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) steadily declined during recovery. Total fatty acid analysis confirmed decreased DHA at 28 dpi in aged mice and more severely after TBI. In summary, TBI in aged led to a loss of transcriptional repression, more profound phospholipid change, earlier upregulation of acylcarnitines, and significant decrease in brain DHA content. Together, these data suggest that TBI occurrence in aging, compared to young, has less impact on gene expression of lipid metabolic genes but greater impact on lipid content.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a significant global health concern, affecting millions of individuals annually and leading to substantial morbidity and mortality (Mofidi et al. 2025). While TBI can affect individuals of any age, older adults are one of the highest prevalence groups suffering from TBI (Marquez de la Plata et al. 2008; Thompson et al. 2006). Furthermore, TBI in the aged population accelerates the development of neurodegenerative disorders. Despite these factors, our understanding of neuropathological changes following TBI in the aged population is small compared to evidence gained from TBI studies of young individuals. Accumulated evidence has shown that TBI is characterized by acute neuronal and axonal injury, disrupted mitochondrial metabolism, and upregulated protracted neuroinflammation, all of which alter brain lipid metabolism (McKee and Daneshvar 2015). While the majority of the studies focus on the effect of TBI on young brains, only a few studies concentrate on TBI and aging; however, both are often limited to the acute and subacute period (day 1–7 post‐injury) (Early et al. 2020; Kumar et al. 2013; Norden et al. 2016).

It is well established that lipids are important mediators of inflammation, and a variety of lipid species are implicated in playing a significant role as pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory modulators in the brain's response to injury. Lipids are integral components of membranes, and following injury, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are liberated and subsequently metabolized to inflammatory molecules such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes to promote inflammation, while others propagate inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction via lipid peroxidation. In contrast, the anti‐inflammatory effect is produced by eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which are essential ω‐3 fatty acids. Despite long‐standing knowledge that the brain is a lipid‐rich organ and that the aging population is at greater risk of TBI and subsequent neurologic disorders, to date, little is known about changes in brain lipid metabolism specifically in the aged brain acutely post‐injury and during long‐term recovery. Recent advances in lipidomics, the large‐scale study of pathways and networks of cellular lipids, have provided new insights into the role of lipid blood and brain metabolism in TBI (see review by Nessel and Michal‐Titus) (Thomas et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2022; Pulliam et al. 2024; Nessel and Michael‐Titus 2021). However, these preclinical lipidomics studies were carried out using young rodent brains and blood and often in the acute period only, while clinical studies examined primarily blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Thus, the effects of TBI on the brain lipid profile specifically in the aged brain have not been examined.

Recently, a metabolome atlas of the aging mouse brain became available (Ding et al. 2021). This atlas provides much needed information regarding developmental and regional changes in brain metabolites and underscored that age is a major determinant of brain metabolism which can affect brain function. To date, much of the lipidomics clinical data from the patients suffering from neurodegenerative disorders were collected using biofluids (blood, CSF) and brain tissue studies are still few (Akyol et al. 2021; Thomas et al. 2024; Aldrich et al. 2024). Nevertheless, this is an important step towards the application of large‐scale lipidomics to advance our understanding of pathology. Preclinical studies provide a possibility to delineate whether temporal changes occur and identify key time points to consider in clinical studies.

Therefore, in this study we aimed to delineate time‐dependent changes in the cortex of TBI‐injured aged mice and compare them with the changes in genes and lipids in the cortex of young TBI mice. Furthermore, we aimed to delineate whether the brain's innate ability to recover from injury and restore brain phospholipid composition is impaired by age and/or injury itself and get a comprehensive temporal lipid profile at different ages.

Methods

Animals

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animal. Experiments were performed using C57BL/6 male mice purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (stock no. 000664). Young adults (3 months old; ~27.5 g) and aged mice (20 months old; ~34 g) were housed in a standard mouse cage with two to five mice per cage in the animal facility of Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine on a 12‐h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to chow food and water.

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

TBI was induced using controlled cortical impact (CCI) with modifications (Romine et al. 2014; Abdullah et al. 2014; Agrawal et al. 2023). In brief, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction 4% and maintenance 2%) administered via nose cone. Following a 10 mm midline skull incision, the skin and fascia were retracted, the skull was exposed, and a 4 mm craniotomy was made over the left parietal area. Moderate‐level TBI was induced using a 3 mm stainless steel tipped impact device at a constant speed of 4 m/s, dwell time of 0.1 s, rotated 11° degrees, and a deformation depth of 1 mm (LeicaBiosystems, Deer Field, IL, USA). After CCI, the skull was placed back and sealed with dental cement, and the scalp was sutured using 4.0 proline. Sham‐operated mice underwent the same procedures except for craniotomy and CCI. Following the surgery, mice were allowed to recover in the clean cage over a warming pad and were placed back into their home cage once they regained their righting reflex. All the surgeries were performed in the morning. Mice were not fasted or limited to food and water prior to surgery, during recovery, or prior to tissue collection. Brain tissues were collected at 1‐, 3‐, 7‐, and 28‐day post CCI injury in the morning. Briefly, mice received deep anesthesia with isoflurane; mice were decapitated, brains were harvested, and the ipsilateral to lesion cortex was microdissected and frozen for subsequent analyses. Both TBI young (3 months old) and TBI aged (20 months) groups were comprised of n = 6 for each time point. Sham groups (young, n = 6; aged, n = 6) served as a control and comprised animals sacrificed at different time points (day 1—one sham, day 3—two shams, day 7—two shams, and day 28—one sham). A total of 88 male mice were used for this study and randomly assigned to the different groups (sham, 1dpi, 3dpi, 7dpi, or 28dpi) following simple randomization procedures using Excel. No animals were excluded; however, one aged TBI mouse died on day 25 post‐injury.

RNA Analysis

Ispilateral cortical RNA was isolated, converted to cDNA, and gene expression was determined using RT‐PCR via SYBR green of targeted lipid metabolizing genes. Specifically, total tissue RNA isolation was performed using TRIzol (Life Technologies), quantified by NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific; RRID:SCR_016517), and used to synthesize cDNA with High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase (Applied Biosystems). Real‐Time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio‐Rad) and primers for the target genes (Table S1) and analyzed using the QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems; RRID:SCR_018712). Gene expression was normalized to the average Ct values of the housekeeping gene Rpl22 and expressed as 2−ΔCT. Outliers were identified using Grubb's test in GraphPad Prism software, alpha = 0.05.

Phospholipid Lipidomics

High‐throughput lipid profiling was performed by direct injection mass spectrometry (DIMS) to identify phospholipid species composition performed with Purdue University's Bindley Bioscience Center Metabolite Profiling Facility, and the accuracy of the method has been as previously described (Panda et al. 2025; Franco et al. 2018; Pereyra et al. 2020). Ipsilateral cortical tissue taken from directly adjacent to CCI was weighed, homogenized in a 100× weight: volume ratio in water following a series of methanol and chloroform additions to obtain the lipid/organic phase using the Bligh Dyer method (White et al. 2020). The lipid phase was dried and resuspended based on mg equivalents of tissue in ACN:MeOH:NH4Ac and directly injected via a micro‐autosampler (G1377A), using optimized dilutions that gave ideal total phosphatidylcholine lipid signal into a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (QQQ6410 from Agilent Technologies, San Jose, CA). Samples were run in order of dpi for ease of analysis. The equipment was operated in the positive ion mode and equipped with a Jet Stream ESI ion source (Agilent Technologies). The MRM profiling approach is an analytically exploratory strategy that correlates well with untargeted LC–MS and lipid imaging (Panda et al. 2025) and absolute quantification of MRM profiling data by LC–MS/MS (Franco et al. 2018; Pereyra et al. 2020). Lipid standards were used as quality control samples every 10 to 15 injections to monitor instrument performance. Over 605 transitions for phospholipids were targeted based on headgroup, specifically 180 phosphatidylcholine (PC), 145 phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), 139 phosphatidylserine (PS), and 141 phosphatidylinositol (PI) species. Data were analyzed by calculating the percent distribution for each ion (ion peak of m/z intensity/total ion intensity) and identified based on the LipidMaps database. Raw data was collected in Multiple Reaction Monitoring mode, and ion intensities were further normalized to total ion count.

Acylcarnitines

Acylcarnitines from frozen ipsilateral cortex were quantified following a well‐described method with modifications (White et al. 2020; Jernberg et al. 2017). All samples were randomized prior to extraction and LC–MS measurement. Acylcarnitines were extracted by homogenizing the tissue in a solution of methanol containing the acylcarnitine internal standards (Cat No. NSK B; Cambridge Isotopes). Samples were sonicated for 10 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 4 min at 4°C. After centrifugation, the liquid phase was transferred to a glass tube and dried under nitrogen. The dried samples were resuspended in 60 μL 3 N HCl in n‐butanol and incubated at 65°C for 15 min. Afterward, samples were evaporated under nitrogen, and the residue containing butylated acylcarnitines was reconstituted in 100 μL of mobile phase acetonitrile/water/formic acid (H2O:CH3CN:HCOOH; 80:19.9:0.1 v/v%). Samples were vortexed, transferred to a centrifuge filter, and spun. The filtrates were then transferred to injection vials. Acylcarnitine analysis was performed using an API 4500 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Foster City, CA) operated in positive‐ion mode employing precursor ion scan for m/z 85, a characteristic product ion of the butyl ester of acylcarnitine species. Data was processed using the Chemoview (AB SCIEX) application. Samples are reported as pmol/mg.

Total Lipid Fatty Acid Analyses

Chemicals, reagents, and materials used were of the highest quality available. MilliQ water, filtered deionized water is available in the laboratory. Methanol, optima grade, was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ). Chloroform, HPLC grade, was from Burdick & Jackson (Muskegan, MI). Stable‐isotope‐labeled internal standards (deuterated) were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Andover MA, or CDN Isotopes, Quebec, CAN. Natural fatty acid standards were purchased from Nu Chek Prep (Elysian, MN) and were checked for purity by capillary gas chromatography by analysis on two capillary columns, a nonpolar, DB1, and a polar, SP2560, capillary columns (50 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm) purchased from Supelco, Sigma‐Aldrich Inc., St. Louis, MO.

Frozen cortical tissue was weighed and extracted with chloroform: methanol 2:1, v/v according to Folch et al. (1957). Total lipids were dried under N2 and weighed. The lipids were solubilized in 1 mL chloroform: methanol 2:1, and approximately 1 mg lipid from each sample was transferred to a 13 × 100 mm screw‐capped test tube for analysis of hydrolyzed total lipid fatty acids (fatty acids derived from extracted lipid content), including saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids of chain length C10 to C30 by capillary gas chromatography electron capture negative ion mass spectrometry, GCMS, as pentafluorobenzyl bromide esters by the method of Lagerstedt et al. (2001) except that a 50 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm SP2560 column was used for analysis. All fatty acids were analyzed on an Agilent GCMS ChemStation and were quantitated using the Agilent MSD Chem Station software G1701EA version E.02.02.1431. Peak integrations were checked, and each fatty acid was measured using a standard curve that was assayed with each set of analyses. For quality control, a reagent blank, a normal, and an abnormal plasma control sample were assayed with each set of analyses. Data was processed in Excel and samples were reported as % of total fatty acids.

Statistical Analysis

The number of animals per group was established based on previous studies of similar nature, thus no sample size calculation was performed (Fernandez et al. 2021). Data are presented as the mean ± SEM for each group unless otherwise stated. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (Boston, MA). Data were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk Normality test (p = 0.05) and Q‐Q plot evaluation. Statistical analysis by unpaired Student's t‐test and 2‐Way ANOVA with uncorrected Fisher's LSD post hoc was performed to determine statistical significance between the groups. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the total fatty acid lipid analysis experiment, we observed low internal standard recovery in four samples (1 young sham, 2 young TBI, and 1 aged TBI). Hence, we did not include these results in the subsequent analysis.

Results

TBI Induces Age‐Specific Transcriptional Changes Acutely and in Recovery

Moderate TBI was induced by control cortical impact in 3‐month‐old (young) and 20‐month‐old (aged) mice to investigate the impact of age and time on the brain lipid profile during injury recovery. To examine whether aged mice following TBI exhibit changes similar to young post‐injury, we performed transcriptomic analysis and compared profiles of young and aged mice over 1 month after injury. It is well documented that TBI in young brains results in significant transcriptional alterations due to neuronal injury, gliosis, and inflammation. These changes occur in a time‐ and region‐specific manner. TBI was induced using controlled cortical impact, and the ipsilateral parieto‐temporal cortex was examined during acute and subacute periods (1‐, 3‐, and 7‐day post‐injury (dpi)) and long‐term (28 dpi). Both aged and young TBI‐injured mice showed injury‐induced gliosis and neuroinflammation evident by increased mRNA abundance of cytokines and microglia and astrocytic markers compared to sham age‐matched animals (Figure 1A–D). These injury markers increased significantly during the first week after injury and subsequently decreased by 1 month post injury in both young and aged TBI‐injured mice. Neuronal loss post‐injury was suggested by a significant reduction in neuronal mRNA marker glutamate ionotropic receptor AMPA type subunit 1r, Gria1, and neuronal postsynaptic density protein 95, Psd95 (Figure 1E). Interestingly, our results show that by 28 dpi, young TBI‐injured mice had significantly higher changes in genes responsible for inflammation and significantly decreased expression of neuronal genes compared to aged TBI‐group, while aged TBI‐injured mice showed higher expression in GFAP suggesting higher astrogliosis in aged mice (Figure 1B–E). These data show that both young and aged injured mice have the expected neurological response, albeit with age‐specific differences over time in response to brain trauma.

Transcriptional Alterations in Lipid Metabolizing Genes Are Greater in Young Mice

To assess the transcriptional regulation of the membrane lipid environment post‐injury, the expression of lipid metabolizing genes was determined across the time course. Several genes that facilitate the uptake and cellular retention of fatty acids increased during recovery, including Fabp5, Fabp7, Lpl, and Apoe, which were increased in both young and aged mice in response to TBI, suggesting increased exogenous fatty acid uptake after TBI (Figure 2A). An exception was Mfsd2a, a transporter that mediates the transport of DHA into the brain, which was increased in aged mice but decreased in young mice after TBI (Figure 2A). A majority of genes that control intracellular lipid metabolism demonstrated that young mice tended to downregulate lipid metabolism genes in response to injury, whereas minimal gene changes were observed in aged mice. These trends were observed for genes involved in intracellular lipid metabolism such as fatty acid elongation and desaturation (Elovl/Fads), fatty acid activation by acyl‐CoA synthetases (Acsl/Fatp), fatty acid deactivation by acyl‐CoA thioesterases (Acot), and complex lipid synthesis by acyltransferases (Agpat/Lpcat) (Figure 2B–E). Specifically, in response to TBI, the young mice downregulated several genes over the recovery period, including Elovl2, Acsl1, Acsl4, Acsl5, Acsl6, Fatp3, Fatp5, Acot7, Acot11, Agpat3, and Agpat5 (Figure 2B–E). Conversely, the aged mice did not downregulate these genes; rather, many were significantly higher in aged compared to young mice, including Fads2, Elovl2, Elovl5, Acsl1, Acsl3, Acsl4, Acsl5, Acsl6, Fatp1, Fatp2, Fatp3, Fatp5, Acot7, Acot9/10, Acot11, Agpat3, Agpat5, and Lpcat2 (Figure 2B–E). Overall, the young mice tended to repress fatty acid metabolism genes in response to TBI, whereas aged mice tended towards unchanged or increased expression of lipid metabolism genes in response to TBI.

TBI Results in Altered Phospholipid Composition up to 1 Month After Injury

To determine the response on membrane lipid composition during the injury‐recovery time course, lipids were extracted from the ipsilateral cortex at the injury site, and untargeted phospholipid lipidomics was performed (Pereyra et al. 2020; Fernandez et al. 2021). Over 605 phospholipid species were analyzed across four headgroups: 180 phosphatidylcholine (PC), 145 phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), 139 phosphatidylserine (PS), and 141 phosphatidylinositol (PI) species. To assess global shifts across all phospholipid species, the number of significantly different phospholipids was analyzed across the time course in young and aged cohorts (Figure 3). Both cohorts demonstrated an upward shift in total species changed across the 1‐month time course, relative to the sham group, with ~2‐fold higher phospholipid species changed at 28 days post injury (dpi) compared to 1 dpi (Figure 3A). Upon comparison of phospholipid species changed relative to the previous day of analysis, the young mice showed relatively constant changes in total species across the time course; however, between dpi 3 and 7, the largest shifts were observed in PS, while the lowest shifts occurred in the two headgroups that compose the vast majority of membranes, PC and PE (Figure 3B). In the aged mice, total species changes remained relatively constant over the first week, but unlike young mice, total changes increased between dpi 7 and 28 (Figure 3B). To determine the impact of age on changes in phospholipid species post‐TBI, young compared to aged lipids revealed an upward shift occurring at the later dpi time points (7 and 28 dpi) (Figure 3C). Only ~20% of the changed species between sham and 28 dpi were similar between the young and aged mice (Figure 3D) demonstrating that aging remarkably impacts lipid remodeling in response to TBI. Together, these data suggest abundant phospholipid remodeling occurring across the time course in young and aged mice. The higher lipid alterations 1 month after recovery in aged compared to young mice suggest that aging creates the most striking distinctions at later stages of recovery.

Alterations in Phospholipids Across Injury‐Recovery Are Dependent Upon Level of Acyl‐Chain Saturation

Analysis of individual phospholipid species revealed several patterns largely dependent upon either age or TBI response by 2‐way ANOVA. Several of the species impacted by age were acyl‐chain saturation dependent. Specifically, various fully saturated PC species were lower in aged mice, relative to young mice, at various dpi (Figure 4A). Conversely, mono‐ and di‐unsaturated PC and PE species tended to be higher in the aged compared to young mice (Figure 4B,C). PLs with 3 or 4 unsaturated bonds demonstrated moderately increased levels in young compared to aged mice (Figure 4D,E). A minor highly unsaturated lipid species, PE 44:12, was higher in aged mice relative to young mice (Figure 4F).

Species demonstrating TBI recovery effects were also acyl‐chain dependent. Specifically, PL species containing 4‐unsaturated bonds increased, and 6‐unsaturated bond‐containing phospholipids decreased across the time course in both young and aged mice (Figure 5A,B). Two species demonstrated both age and TBI effects: PC 34:0 was lower in aged mice and decreased during recovery, whereas PE 38:5 was higher in aged mice and increased during recovery (Figure 5C). Two minor species showed interaction between age and TBI, both with lower levels in aged mice that increase with recovery in aged mice but decrease with recovery in young mice (Figure 5D). Several species were not impacted by age or TBI (Figure S1). Together, these data demonstrate that aged mice have higher mono‐ and di‐unsaturated and lower saturated and highly poly‐unsaturated phospholipids during recovery. These data also suggest that phospholipids containing arachidonic acid (AA, C20:4) tend to increase, and those containing docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6) tend to decrease during recovery.

Increased Lipid Catabolic Flux Following TBI

Phospholipid remodeling, which is crucial for maintaining membrane fluidity and function, reflects the major anabolic pathway in the brain lipid metabolism and often involves diacylation. Hence, we next assessed whether lipid catabolism via mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO), a relatively minor metabolic fate of fatty acids in the normal brain, is affected by TBI.

To delineate the effects of TBI on fatty acid oxidation, acylcarnitine profiles were analyzed. Free carnitine levels were abundant and increased during the first week after injury, indicating that trans‐mitochondrial membrane transport of long fatty acids for subsequent oxidation will not be limited (Figure 6A). While the 2‐carbon acetyl‐carnitine remained constant across recovery and similar between ages, total acylcarnitines, ranging from 3 to 20 carbons long, were significantly increased during the first week after injury at 1, 3, and 7 dpi (Figure 6B,C). Peak acylcarnitine levels were reached at 3 dpi, an observation that was remarkably consistent across nearly all acylcarnitine species in both young and aged mice (Figure 6C,D). Both young and aged mice demonstrated an increase in short‐chain acylcarnitines during the first week after injury, peaking at 3 dpi by ~2‐fold relative to sham (Figure 6E). Medium‐ and long‐chain acylcarnitines were similarly increased in young and aged mice, peaking at day 3; however, the aged, but not young, mice demonstrated early‐onset increases in both medium‐ and long‐chain acylcarnitines occurring at 1 dpi (Figure 6E). By 28 dpi, brain acylcarnitines were restored to sham levels (Figure 6).

We next examined the gene expression of key enzymes involved in FAO. The major regulators of fatty acid β‐oxidation are the carnitine palmitoyl‐transferases (Cp1a/Cpt2) genes, which were upregulated in young and aged mice at 3 dpi compared to 1 dpi, consistent with an increase in acylcarnitines at 3 dpi (Figure 2S). Among additional genes of fatty acid β‐oxidation, young mice tended to repress expression, whereas aged mice did not, yielding significantly higher expression in aged compared to young mice for Cact, Acadl, Acadm, Acadvl, and Hadha (Figure 2S). These similar trends observed for lipid metabolism genes (Figure 2) and β‐oxidation genes suggest young mice repress lipid metabolism genes in response to injury, whereas aged mice do not. Together, these data demonstrate that TBI results in fatty acids mobilization for mitochondrial oxidation independent of age, suggesting that FAO may play a role in TBI recovery.

TBI Results in Long‐Term Changes in Brain Total Lipid Fatty Acid Composition Specifically in Aged Injured Mice

Recovery from injury triggers both lipid anabolism and catabolism that can cause a shift in the total fatty acid composition of the brain. To determine the long‐term effects of TBI on fatty acid composition, total fatty acid profile analysis was performed in the ipsilateral cortex of sham and injured young and aged mice at 28 dpi (Figure 7). While long‐term (28dpi), the percent of total lipids per mg of cortex was not affected by age or injury; however, the composition of fatty acid classes changed with age (young vs. aged) and injury (sham vs. TBI) (Figure 7A). Specifically, the percent of total ω‐9 fatty acids increased only in aged mice following TBI (Figure 7A). Notably, oleic acid (C18:1ω‐9), one of the most abundant monounsaturated fatty acids in phospholipids, was elevated in the aged mice and further increased by TBI (Figure 7B). In contrast, the ω‐5/7 fatty acids were trending towards an increase by TBI only in young mice (Figure 7A,B). Although the total percent of ω‐6 fatty acids remained unchanged by age or injury (Figure 7A), most of the ω‐6 di‐unsaturated fatty acids containing ≥ 20 carbons were reduced in aged mice (Figure 7B). The percent of total ω‐3 fatty acids, which is mainly comprised of DHA (22:6ω‐3), was lower in aged mice, and this decrease was further exacerbated following TBI (Figure 7A,B). Overall, when comparing TBI‐induced changes in fatty acids between young and aged mice, TBI had a greater long‐term impact in the aged group (Figure 7C).

Both the amount and balance of the essential ω‐6 and ω‐3 fatty acids are critical for proper brain function. In aged mice, the triene/tetraene ratio (mead acid (C20:3ω9)/arachidonic acid (AA; C20:4ω6) ratio), a clinical indicator of essential fatty acid deficiency, was increased independently of TBI (Figure 7D,E). The ω‐3 DHA was greatly impacted by age and TBI (p < 0.05, 2‐way ANOVA), indicating that injury such as TBI further diminishes aging brain ω‐3 DHA, thus affecting long‐term recovery (Figure 7D). Furthermore, aged TBI had an increased AA/DHA ratio, which has been associated with increased pro‐inflammatory signaling and accelerated neurodegeneration (Figure 7E). Altogether, these findings suggest that TBI induces pronounced long‐term changes in brain fatty acid composition than aging alone.

Discussion

Our study results demonstrate that following TBI, brain lipid composition undergoes dynamic changes during the acute (1–3 dpi) and subacute periods (7 dpi) in both young and aged brains and results in a long‐term (28 dpi) altered brain lipid profile, specifically in TBI‐injured aged mice. We determined that brain fatty acid oxidation is upregulated in the acute period post‐TBI in both young and aged brains, while the total lipid fatty acid profile is perturbed long‐term in aged brains. These changes are accompanied by TBI‐induced transcriptional increases in genes responsible for inflammation and gliosis, repression of lipid metabolism genes in young, and a loss of this repression in aged mice.

Indeed, it is now well characterized that TBI results in elevation of GFAP, which is now used as a sensitive marker reflective of neuroinflammation and reactive gliosis. While this phenomenon is well documented in young TBI (Agrawal et al. 2023; Ciechanowska et al. 2020), the extent of GFAP changes in aged only recently has been reported (Early et al. 2020; Wangler et al. 2022). Our results corroborate these studies which demonstrated age‐dependent amplification and protracted gliosis in the cortex of aged mice post‐TBI at 7 and 30 dpi. Similarly, our gene transcription analysis demonstrated that TBI resulted in increased transcription of genes responsible for cytokines/chemokine. However, in contrast to these aforementioned studies, our results show that upon normalizing to baseline sham levels of gliotic and inflammatory genes, young adult TBI demonstrated increased induction of genes responsible for cytokine/chemokine production compared to aged TBI. This, in part, may be explained by differences in the TBI injury, normalization, and astrocyte‐specific preparation used in the analysis.

Neuronal injury is well‐documented after TBI and is characterized by the loss of neurons and surviving neurons that have decreased numbers of synapses and altered glutamatergic transmission. Indeed, our results showed that young mice following TBI had an acute decrease in the expression of both Gria1, involved in glutamatergic transmission, and Psd95, which is crucial for regulating synaptic density. These findings are consistent with previous reports that showed decreased expression of PSD95 protein and Gria1 (Wang et al. 2016; Wakade et al. 2010). Aged TBI‐injured animals showed similar changes in Gria1 gene expression; however, they did not display significant changes in Psd95 transcription. This is not surprising since synapses with long protein lifetimes are preserved in aged animals and are enriched in cortex (Bulovaite et al. 2022).

A new and key finding in this study was that following TBI, lipid metabolism mRNA profiles differed in young and aged mice. The brain is a lipid‐rich organ; however, following the TBI injury, lipid metabolism has not been completely delineated. Our results show that young TBI‐injured brains showed temporal changes in genes responsible for both the transport of fatty acids and subsequent metabolism (both anabolic and catabolic). Specifically, these genes were downregulated in young mice in both the acute period post TBI and failed to recover by 1 month post trauma. Surprisingly, aged TBI brains differed from young and showed that transcriptional regulation of lipid metabolism was stymied. This was surprising considering that: (a) age‐dependent changes in lipid profile have been reported and are often implicated in the development of neurodegenerative diseases; (b) the mechanical disruption to membranes due to TBI disrupts lipid homeostasis (Torres et al. 2021) and (c) several preclinical and clinical studies demonstrated that abnormal serum lipidomic analysis correlates with the degree of injury and thus proposed to use specific lipid species as biomarkers. Thus, we performed a temporal lipidomics profile of injured cortex from aged mice and compared it with the lipid profile of young TBI‐injured mice.

Since phospholipids are major components of membranes and responsible for structural integrity, we performed untargeted phospholipid analysis at multiple time points and compared these between the young and aged brains. Our results show that phospholipid profile has dynamic temporal changes in both young and aged TBI brains, however, the rate of changes differs between the ages. Our findings in young mice are consistent with previous report by Abdullah et al. (Abdullah et al. 2014), who reported that at 3 months post TBI injury, cortex had significantly decreased levels of PC and PE. The same group showed that young mice (3 months old) following closed head injury of mild repetitive TBI had significant changes in hippocampal phospholipids long‐term (up to 15 months studied). Specifically, they reported that phospholipids species containing saturated, mono‐ and poly‐unsaturated (PUFA) were significantly decreased at 24 h, while the ratio of AA to DHA was increased (Ojo et al. 2019). Similarly, our broad lipidomic profiling showed emerging patterns of alterations to membrane species across the time course of recovery. Specifically, we showed increases in AA‐containing PLs and decreases in DHA‐containing PLs. Membrane PL species with 4‐unsaturated bonds are composed largely of arachidonic acid (AA C20:4), whereas those with 6‐unsaturated bonds are composed of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA C22:6) which are extensively esterified and incorporated into membranes. Brain fatty acid composition depends on both de novo synthesis of saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, as well as active import of essential PUFAs (EPA and DHA) which are extensively esterified into phospholipids (see review by Bazinet and Laye (Bazinet and Layé 2014)). DHA levels increase during brain development, and high abundance of DHA concentrated in neuronal plasma membranes, particularly at the synapse (Sambra et al. 2021), may explain high DHA enrichment specifically in the cortex (Oliveira et al. 2022). Decreased brain DHA levels are associated with impaired cognition, and requires Acsl6 as a key mediator of brain DHA enrichment (Fernandez et al. 2021, 2018) specifically in aging. We should underscore that the most pronounced decrease was observed in aged TBI mice.

In addition to the abundance of lipids in membrane structures, lipids are also a key bioenergetic source for many tissues. Since we observed the biggest PL decrease in young mice between 3 and 7 dpi (acute and subacute time points), we asked whether these changes are due to upregulated brain fatty acid oxidation which is dismal in the normal brain. However, the use of fatty acids for bioenergetics under pathologic conditions remains unknown. Neurons do not express the metabolic enzymatic machinery that is required for FAO. However, astrocytes are predicted to use FAO to a small extent for bioenergetics, with mitochondrial oxidation of fatty acids accounting for up to ~20% of the brain's energy (Panov et al. 2014). To facilitate repair post‐TBI, FAO may be relied on as an energy source (Oft et al. 2024). For long‐chain fatty acid oxidation to occur, fatty acids are transported into the mitochondrial matrix through the acyl carnitine shuttle. Indeed, we observed up‐regulation of mitochondrial Cpt1a and Cpt2, albeit modest. Acylcarnitines accumulate under numerous conditions that put stress on bioenergetics and thus serve as a biomarker for mitochondrial β‐oxidation (McCann et al. 2021). In our study, both young and aged mice showed remarkably consistent accumulation of acylcarnitines following TBI. Acylcarnitines were significantly increased on 3 and 7 dpi in young TBI brains, and these results are consistent with previously reported (Agrawal et al. 2023; Mallah et al. 2019). Recently, Mallah et al. study used matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging following experimental TBI in young rats and showed that palmitoylcarnitine was elevated at day 3 post‐injury and was so‐localized with microglia (Mallah et al. 2019). Interestingly, aged TBI‐injured mice showed more extensive and early (1 dpi) increase in generation of acylcarnitines, indicating earlier and more extensive mobilization of fatty acids towards mitochondrial β‐oxidation. Recent reports using genetic approaches delineated that aged astrocytes are responsible for increased fatty acid oxidation in the brain (Morant‐Ferrando et al. 2023; Mi et al. 2023). Thus, it is anticipated that aged TBI mice are better equipped to mobilize fatty acids towards mitochondrial oxidation quicker than their young counterparts. The role and requirement of fatty acid oxidation during TBI recovery warrants further investigation.

Our results show that at 1 month following TBI, total lipid/mg of brain tissue was not affected; however, total lipid fatty acid profile was altered in both young and aged TBI‐injured brains. Young TBI‐injured mice had 9 fatty acids differing from their age‐matched controls and were increased primarily in ω‐5/7. We should point out that young TBI mice had increased mead acid, which is often increased in response to DHA deficiency and in patients with Alzheimer's disease (van Goor et al. 2010). Aged TBI mice fatty acid profile displayed changes in 13 different fatty acids, ~25% more than that of the young. Interestingly, aged sham controls and TBI mice displayed elevated levels of mead acid. In contrast, we did not observe changes in AA, while levels of DHA were lower in aged control and even further decreased following TBI. Following TBI, both AA and DHA are precursors for lipid mediators which regulate inflammation and resolution. Similar to cytokines, lipid mediators are important signaling molecules in inflammatory processes and are known to be altered after TBI (Poblete et al. 2022). The lack of change in AA may represent a controlled effort to reincorporate AA into PL membranes, reducing AA availability for pro‐inflammatory lipid mediator production. Conversely, the decline of DHA and DHA‐containing PLs may suggest a controlled effort to increase the use of DHA for anti‐inflammatory lipid mediator production and thus shifting DHA away from membrane incorporation. In agreement, we previously reported that ACSL6 is a major enzyme regulating the incorporation of DHA into neuronal membranes (Fernandez et al. 2021, 2018) and here we show that Acsl6 is highly downregulated post‐TBI, suggesting that DHA membrane integration may be impeded to allow for anti‐inflammatory lipid mediator production.

Many previous studies reported neuroprotection following DHA supplementation in young rodents after TBI (Russell et al. 2013; Schober et al. 2020; Harvey et al. 2015; Begum et al. 2014). While DHA levels decrease after TBI, increasing DHA exposure through direct contact at the injury site or via oral consumption greatly improves recovery responses from injury. Specifically, administration of DHA alone as a single bolus improves recovery post‐TBI (Thau‐Zuchman et al. 2020). Studies on spinal cord injury have shown that DHA ingestion reduces microglia and macrophage activation and axonal injury (Huang et al. 2007). DHA is also shown to strengthen functions of the blood–brain barrier and prevent edema formation (Liu et al. 2020). It is unclear whether DHA is most effective for TBI recovery when administered acutely post‐injury or supplemented/injected in doses over numerous days or weeks (Thau‐Zuchman et al. 2020). The optimum dose of DHA needed after TBI for neuroprotective effects is also still unclear. However, it is clear that restoring brain DHA levels improves recovery after TBI and mitigates pathological cascades contributing to secondary injury.

In summary, we show that following TBI, membrane phospholipid changes are dynamic and long‐lasting specifically in aging brains. Specifically, the extent of the decrease in DHA and increase in AA/DHA is significantly more pronounced in aged brains compared to young TBI‐injured brains and has not been previously reported. These dysregulations in brain lipid metabolism following TBI in injured brains may explain increased gliosis in aging brains. These results provide new evidence that alterations in lipid metabolism after TBI, particularly in aging brains, govern poor neurologic outcomes in the aged.
