IgA-Dominant Mesangial Proliferative Glomerulonephritis in Prolidase Deficiency
Authors: Taryn Stokowski, Pram Thennakoonwela, Rebecca Brassington, Wirongrong Churngchow, Anukul Ghimire, Samih H Nasr, Manjula Gowrishankar, Ainslie Hildebrand, Kim Solez
Published: 2025

Introduction

Prolidase deficiency (PD) is a rare disorder caused by a recessive mutation in the gene encoding prolidase D, PEPD, which disrupts the metabolism of collagen and other proline-containing proteins.1 This defective pathway is responsible for the chronic skin ulcers, defective wound healing, facial and skeletal abnormalities, and developmental delay (from disorganization of the pial basement membrane in the cerebral cortex) that are often seen in PD.

Patients with PD most commonly present with skin ulcers, frequent infections, dysmorphic features (e.g., ocular hypertelorism), delayed development, and/or splenomegaly.2 PD also causes immunological dysregulation similar to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).1 Here, we present a PD case and aim to compare PD and SLE from a renal perspective.

Case Presentation

In 2021, a 15-year-old woman with a history of recurrent pneumonias, skin infections, and skin lesions was referred for genetic testing following an intensive care unit admission for diabetic ketoacidosis, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, and bacteremia.

Laboratory results revealed pancytopenia, elevated serum IgA, antinuclear antibody positivity, SSA and SSB positivity, normal C3 and C4 levels, and negative anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA). Abdominal ultrasound showed marked hepatosplenomegaly and echogenic kidneys. These findings prompted testing for PD, and she underwent genetic testing that showed she was homozygous for PEPD c.418a>T.p.(Lys140∗), consistent with a diagnosis of PD. She was also diagnosed with secondary Sjögren syndrome in addition to type 2 diabetes after subsequent testing revealed a glycated hemoglobin of 15%.

Following PD diagnosis, the patient had mild microscopic hematuria with intermittent mild proteinuria but maintained a normal glomerular filtration rate. In 2022, her proteinuria became persistent; and in 2023, a kidney needle biopsy was performed to investigate its etiology.

Kidney Biopsy Findings

On light microscopy, glomeruli were markedly enlarged and exhibited marked global mesangial hypercellularity (Figure 1a, hematoxylin and eosin, 100×). One of 33 glomeruli samples displayed segmental endocapillary hypercellularity (Figure 1b, periodic acid–Schiff, 200×) and 3 were globally sclerotic. No glomeruli showed crescents or necrosis. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed coarsely granular mesangial and glomerular capillary wall positivity for IgG (1+; Figure 1c), IgA (3+; Figure 1d), IgM (1-2+), C1q (1+), C3 (2-3+), kappa (2+), and lambda (2-3+), without extraglomerular deposits. Electron microscopy revealed abundant mesangial and segmental subepithelial and intramembranous immune-type electron-dense deposits (Supplementary Figures S1a and S1b), some of which were associated with glomerular basement spikes. Basement membranes were moderately thickened (Supplementary Figure S1c; mean: 593 nm). Finally, tubuloreticular inclusions were seen within glomerular and peritubular capillary endothelial cells (Supplementary Figure S1d).

Treatment

In 2022, the patient was started on hydroxychloroquine. She also received rituximab and i.v. Ig but showed no response to either therapy. In 2023, she started ramipril, but her proteinuria continued to fluctuate. Her diabetes was managed during this period but precluded steroid interventions.

Discussion

PD was described by Goodman et al.3 in 1968 as a disease characterized by imidodipeptiduria and decreased collagen crosslinking. Later, these features were linked to a mutation in PEPD, which encodes for an enzyme that hydrolyzes proline-containing dipeptides and is responsible for collagen catabolism. This recessive mutation is rare, but has higher prevalence in certain populations, such as the Druze of Israel.4 As of 2021, only 161 patients with PD had been described in the literature.2 These patients were found to experience skin ulcers (62.4%) as well as other dermatological symptoms (e.g., rash [27.5%] and scarring [29.3%]), frequent infections (48.3%), dysmorphic features (65.7%; e.g., ocular hypertelorism [35.8%]), splenomegaly (44.9%), and/or hematologic abnormalities (39.3%).2 PD presents variably, with some patients being asymptomatic and others suffering fatal complications.

The disease’s immunological nature has recently been established. Up to 12% of patients with PD are formally diagnosed with autoimmune disease, and 20.2% have autoantibodies.2 Though the exact pathophysiology remains unclear, the link may be through the production of nonfunctional complement protein C1q (a normally proline-rich molecule)5 and/or through spontaneous T-cell activation.1 The presentation is described as “lupus-like,” and approximately 6% of patients with PD are dual-diagnosed with SLE whereas another 3% have partial lupus.2 The patient here did not have typical SLE characteristics; she experiences no chest or joint pain, facial rashes, or unexplained edema. Her normal C3 and C4 levels and her negativity for anti-dsDNA do not support an SLE diagnosis. Her positivity for SSA and SSB has been attributed to her secondary Sjögren syndrome.

In 2023, a Pepd –/– murine model was used to characterize PD. Like our patient, the knockout mice were antinuclear antibody positive, negative for anti-dsDNA, and had elevated serum IgA but not IgG or IgM.1 However, negativity for anti-dsDNA is not universal because some patients with PD in the literature (possibly those diagnosed with SLE) show positivity.2 Both our patient and the knockout mice had glomerular deposition of IgA, IgG, and C1q (the others in Figure 1 were not investigated), but neither exhibited impaired renal function.1 However, the mice used in the study are known to be relatively resistant to kidney injury, so it is possible that the model does not reflect the spectrum of potential complications.

Several PD case reports describe ongoing proteinuria, and a few have included kidney biopsies, usually evaluated by the lupus nephritis classification system. Bissonnette et al.6 described a case resembling class III lupus nephritis which, like this case, featured mesangial deposits of IgA, IgM, and C3. Glomerular changes accompanied by immune complex deposition have also been observed elsewhere.7 One case report noted the absence of electron-dense deposits but did observe basement membrane thickening and mesangium expansion as was seen in this case.8 Finally, 3 cases in the literature, 2 of which required transplantation, exhibited changes consistent with class IV lupus nephritis.4,9

The mesangial hypercellularity and focal endocapillary hypercellularity in the present case would be categorized as class III lupus nephritis according to the ISN/RPS 2018 classification. However, the IgA-dominant deposition observed is distinct from the typical presentation of lupus nephritis, which, though also full-house, is usually IgG-dominant. The present case lacks extraglomerular deposits typical of SLE pathology.

Given the diversity of PD, the characteristics above may not be universal in distinguishing PD and SLE. Instead, PD should be recognized as a differential diagnosis or potential underlying cause in atypical lupus nephritis cases, especially when the clinical presentation is also atypical for SLE. A possible PD case should be investigated through genetic testing and/or imidodipeptiduria testing. Diagnosis can expand treatment options to include the oral supplementation or topical application of proline.2

Conclusion

PD results from an autosomal recessive mutation in PEPD, which causes disruptions in the metabolism of collagen and proline-containing proteins. Patients with PD exhibit a range of clinical manifestations, including skin ulcers, recurrent infections, dysmorphic features, delayed development, and/or splenomegaly. PD can be distinguished from SLE by the unique presence of imidodipeptiduria. This PD case had similarities with a Pepd –/– murine model, such as antinuclear antibody positivity, absence of anti-dsDNA, and elevated serum IgA. However, given the overlap of SLE and PD, other patients with PD have been dsDNA positive. In the renal context, IgA-dominant immune deposition appears to be a distinguishing feature, which can prompt consideration of PD as an alternative (or underlying) diagnosis to SLE (Table 1). This awareness should guide genetic testing, urinalysis, and facilitate precise therapeutic interventions.
