Clinical relevance of cytochrome P450-mediated drug-drug interactions in hospitalised older adults: implications for community pharmacy
Authors: Roberto Lozano, Carina Bona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytochrome P450 (CYP)-mediated drug-drug interactions constitute a relevant safety issue in hospitalised older patients, due to multimorbidity and polypharmacy. The objective was to characterise these interactions and analyse the factors associated with their occurrence.A retrospective observational study was conducted in a cohort of patients aged ≥65 years admitted between 2020 and 2023. Data from the electronic prescribing system were analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression, evaluating the association between clinically relevant interactions and clinical and demographic variables.CYP3A4 was the most frequently involved isoenzyme, followed by CYP2C19 and CYP2D6. Although the total number of interactions was high, only a proportion was considered clinically relevant. In the multivariate analysis, polypharmacy was independently associated with the presence of interactions, whereas age, sex, Charlson index and admission diagnosis showed no significant association.These results highlight the need for pharmacist-led medication reconciliation and coordinated deprescribing strategies between hospital and community pharmacy, emphasising the role of the latter in detecting clinically relevant interactions, especially during care transitions after hospital discharge.

INTRODUCTION

Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes metabolise more than 70% of prescribed medicines, making them a central element in drug interactions (drug-drug interactions, DDI), both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic, in clinical practice [1]. These enzymes form a highly versatile superfamily responsible for oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis reactions that determine drug clearance, bioavailability and steady-state concentrations. Importantly, CYP activity varies widely between individuals, depending on factors such as age, comorbidities, inflammation, concomitant exposure to other drugs and genetic polymorphisms.

In older adults, the combination of multimorbidity, frailty, chronic inflammation and extensive polypharmacy markedly increases the likelihood of clinically relevant DDIs [2]. This population frequently presents cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment, chronic pulmonary disorders, diabetes, chronic pain and psychiatric symptoms, leading to the prescription of multiple long-term treatments targeting different organ systems [2]. The simultaneous use of medicines metabolised by the same CYP pathway increases the risk of metabolic competition, reduced clearance and unexpected drug accumulation.

Physiological ageing further contributes to this risk through several well-documented pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes. Hepatic blood flow progressively declines with age, while hepatocyte mass and enzymatic expression decrease, limiting overall metabolic capacity [3]. Renal function also declines, even when serum creatinine appears “normal”, since many older adults produce insufficient amounts of creatinine due to sarcopenia. At the same time, ageing reduces serum albumin and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein levels, altering the protein binding of acidic and basic drugs. These changes increase the free fraction of several CYP-metabolised medicines, thereby amplifying the clinical consequences even of moderate interactions. In addition, alterations in receptor density and sensitivity increase pharmacodynamic variability [3].

Polypharmacy is especially prevalent in hospitalised older patients, in whom therapeutic regimens frequently exceed ten concomitant medicines, substantially increasing the likelihood of metabolic competition and enzymatic inhibition or induction [4]. In addition to chronic treatments, older hospitalised patients are often exposed to acute medication such as antibiotics, corticosteroids, anticoagulants and analgesics, many of which are potent CYP inducers or inhibitors. This dynamic clinical environment creates numerous opportunities for DDIs that may not exist in stable outpatient settings.

Among CYP isoenzymes, CYP3A4, CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 dominate metabolic pathways in older adults, with CYP3A4 being responsible for most clinically relevant DDIs due to its broad spectrum of substrates and its sensitivity to multiple inhibitors and inducers [5]. CYP3A4 interacts with macrolides, calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines, statins, antifungals and immunosuppressants, many of them common in geriatric pharmacotherapy. CYP2D6, although involved in a smaller overall proportion of metabolism, plays a key role in antidepressants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers and opioids. CYP2D6 polymorphisms also contribute to unpredictable metabolic responses. CYP2C19 is fundamental in the metabolism of proton pump inhibitors, clopidogrel, some antidepressants and certain antiepileptics. CYP1A2 and CYP2C9, although involved in fewer interactions, metabolise drugs with a narrow therapeutic margin, such as theophylline and warfarin, where even small interaction-induced changes may have serious clinical consequences [5].

Despite the importance of recognising DDIs, automated detection systems often generate a large number of theoretical alerts, many of which lack real clinical relevance. Their low specificity contributes to alert fatigue and may hinder clinical decision-making, since professionals may become desensitised to warnings or fail to distinguish between theoretical interactions and clinically actionable ones [6]. Several systematic reviews indicate that many electronic alerts identify potential DDIs without adequately incorporating factors such as dose, treatment duration, organ function, genetic profile or concomitant pharmacodynamic risks. Consequently, recent efforts have focused on identifying clinically significant DDIs - those with robust evidence and measurable impact on clinical outcomes - rather than cataloguing all theoretically possible combinations [7].

Care transitions add an additional layer of vulnerability. Medication changes introduced during hospitalisation often persist after discharge without adequate reconciliation or pharmaceutical follow-up in community pharmacy, increasing the risk of unrecognised interactions in the early post-discharge period [8]. At discharge, patients may leave the hospital with a modified regimen that includes new treatments, discontinuations, dose adjustments and substitutions influenced by formulary differences or by the acute illness. Without a structured process that ensures communication between hospital teams, primary care physicians and community pharmacists, these discrepancies may go unnoticed. Older adults are particularly vulnerable, as cognitive impairment, sensory limitations and lack of caregiver support may hinder adherence or understanding of medication changes.

The present study was formulated from an unified cohort model. This approach strengthens statistical robustness, eliminates artificial contrasts between medical specialties and improves the clinical interpretability of the results, in accordance with contemporary principles of geriatric pharmacotherapy [9]. This approach avoids artificial differences derived from prescription patterns specific to each medical specialty and improves the clinical interpretability of the results [10]. In addition, this framework strengthens the applicability of the findings to the continuum of care, explicitly including the role of community pharmacy in detecting clinically relevant interactions and in medication reconciliation after hospital discharge. Moreover, by focusing specifically on CYP-mediated DDIs, it recognises their disproportionate contribution to medication-related harm and underlines their relevance in personalised pharmacotherapy in ageing populations.

Accordingly, the objectives of the present study were:

To characterise CYP-mediated DDIs in hospitalised older adults using a unified cohort design;To evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified interactions using a standardised severity framework;To examine prescribing determinants associated with DDI patterns; andTo place the findings within the continuum of care, including the role of community pharmacists.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A retrospective observational study was conducted in a cohort of hospitalised older patients. An anonymised dataset from patients managed in the Geriatrics and Internal Medicine departments within the hospital pharmacovigilance programme was analysed and integrated into a unified cohort of 294 patients aged ≥65 years who had been hospitalised (mean length of stay 12.6 ± 8.6 days; median: 10 days) and had a complete medication list available over a three-year period (2020–2023).

This dataset was originally created to support pharmacist-led medication review and deprescribing initiatives within the hospital pharmacovigilance programme, which routinely evaluates older adults for potential drug-drug interactions (DDIs) as part of standard care.

Although the database contained complete medication lists and associated demographic or diagnostic fields, their structure had not originally been designed for research purposes, which required extensive harmonisation, cleaning and standardisation procedures to ensure data integrity and methodological transparency [11].

Medication lists were standardised using generic nomenclature, converted to uppercase and alphabetically ordered to remove duplicate entries. Incomplete or inconsistent records were checked against the associated narrative pharmacovigilance notes in order to resolve discrepancies [12]. All medications were extracted, regardless of whether or not they contributed to an interaction, thereby ensuring a comprehensive assessment of the potential role of CYP.

CYP-mediated DDIs were identified using validated pharmacological compendia, including Stockley’s Drug Interactions, Micromedex and Lexicomp. Interacting drug pairs were identified from structured medication fields and narrative comments. Subsequently, each interaction was assigned to the main CYP metabolic pathways (CYP3A4, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP1A2 and CYP2C9), according to the available pharmacological evidence [13,14]. Pairs considered artefactual - for example, those derived from incomplete records or database noise - were systematically excluded before analysis.

Interactions were categorised as major (probability of causing significant harm and requiring treatment modification, avoidance or intensive monitoring), moderate (requiring dose adjustment or closer follow-up) or minor (unlikely to produce clinically relevant effects) [15]. This classification directly addressed reviewers’ concerns regarding the need to differentiate clinically relevant interactions from merely theoretical alerts.

A descriptive analysis of the global cohort was performed. Continuous variables were summarised using mean and standard deviation or range, as appropriate, and categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages.

The distribution of CYP isoenzymes, implicated drug pairs and main diagnoses was described in terms of frequency in the total cohort. Information from the different care settings was retained only for secondary descriptive purposes.

An association analysis was performed between the presence of clinically relevant drug interactions and the clinical and demographic variables of the cohort. The dependent variable was defined as the presence of at least one clinically relevant drug-drug interaction (DDI), coded as a dichotomous variable (yes/no); and independent variables included age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index, total number of prescribed medications (as a measure of polypharmacy) and admission diagnosis.

Admission diagnosis was grouped into three main clinical categories (cardiovascular, infectious and respiratory) in order to avoid excessive fragmentation of the variable.

Bivariate analysis was performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Subsequently, a multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors independently associated with the presence of clinically relevant DDIs. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 [16,17].

Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of fully anonymised secondary databases, approval by an ethics committee was not required according to institutional regulations and current legislation. No identifiable patient data were accessed at any stage.

RESULTS

The results are presented according to the predefined objectives of the study in order to improve clarity and interpretability of the findings and were organised into four sections: cohort characteristics, CYP isoenzyme distribution, interaction patterns and association analysis of DDIs.

Cohort characteristics

Anthropometric characteristics of the cohort´s patients (n=294) reflected a relatively homogeneous population: mean body weight was 63.8 ± 10.6 kg, mean height was 156 cm and mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.9 kg/m². Although this value approaches the overweight threshold, 18% of individuals showed indicators of malnutrition or nutritional risk.

Likewise, a six-month weight loss of 1.3 kg and considerable functional dependence, with a mean Barthel Index score of 45 ± 12, were observed, indicating a frail and vulnerable population with moderate to severe limitations in basic activities of daily living [18,19].

Chronic diseases were highly prevalent and consistent with the typical multimorbidity profile of older adults, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment or dementia, musculoskeletal disorders and depression [25] (Table 1).

CYP isoenzyme distribution

In the analysis of detected cytochrome P450-mediated DDIs (n=187), CYP3A4 emerged as the predominant metabolic pathway, accounting for more than half of all identified interactions, in line with global prescribing patterns in older adults and with the central role of this isoenzyme in drug metabolism [20].

CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 contributed smaller but clinically relevant proportions of interactions, especially those involving antidepressants, antipsychotics, proton pump inhibitors, beta-blockers and other centrally acting drugs [21].

Less frequent but clinically important interactions were observed through CYP1A2 and CYP2C9 pathways, often involving drugs with a narrow therapeutic margin, such as theophylline, warfarin and certain sulfonylureas [2].

Overall, the distribution of CYP isoenzymes showed involvement of CYP3A4 in approximately 56% of interactions, followed by CYP2C19 with around 20% and CYP2D6 with about 13%, while CYP1A2 and CYP2C9 represented smaller proportions. These results reflect the predominance of pharmacological families widely used in hospitalised older patients [20-22] (Table 2).

Interaction patterns

The observed interactions are consistent with the previous literature, which describes a high frequency of combinations involving statins, anticoagulants, benzodiazepines and antimicrobials in geriatric patients. Among the most frequent interactions were simvastatin with azole antifungals, warfarin with fluconazole, macrolides combined with drugs that prolong the QT interval and benzodiazepines co-administered with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors [23] (Table 3).

Most interactions were classified as moderate in severity, requiring dose adjustments or closer clinical monitoring; however, a subset of major interactions - especially those involving anticoagulants, potent enzyme inhibitors or sedatives - presented substantial clinical risk and required immediate therapeutic modification or drug avoidance [24].

Association analysis of DDIs

The association between the presence of clinically relevant drug interactions and the clinical and demographic variables of the cohort was evaluated.

In the bivariate analysis, the presence of DDI was significantly associated with the number of prescribed medications, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed according to sex or grouped admission diagnosis.

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, polypharmacy was identified as the main factor independently associated with the presence of clinically relevant DDIs.

Age, sex and Charlson index showed no independent association after adjustment. Likewise, admission diagnosis, grouped into cardiovascular, infectious and respiratory categories, was not significantly associated with the occurrence of interactions (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The unified cohort approach used in this study allows a more integrated description of drug interaction patterns in hospitalised older patients, avoiding interpretations conditioned by healthcare organisation [27]. The high multimorbidity, polypharmacy and frailty observed in the cohort reflect the usual clinical complexity of hospitalised older adults. Thus, in the multivariate analysis, polypharmacy emerged as the main factor associated with the presence of clinically relevant drug interactions, in line with previous evidence. This finding reinforces the idea that the risk of interactions in hospitalised older patients is more closely related to the overall medication burden than to individual factors.

Conversely, admission diagnosis showed no independent association with the presence of drug interactions, reinforcing the importance of focusing prevention strategies on the global optimisation of pharmacological treatment rather than on the specific clinical context of admission.

Indeed, the coexistence of multiple chronic diseases, together with polypharmacy and age-related physiological changes, may increase susceptibility to drug-drug interactions, particularly those mediated by the CYP system.

This approach allows interpretation to focus on the clinical relevance of the identified interactions rather than on possible differences between care settings. As a result, the cohort used reflects real hospital practice and allows valid extrapolation of the findings to broader geriatric populations, including the primary care setting.

This analysis also confirms the predominance of CYP3A4-mediated interactions, in line with international evidence demonstrating its centrality in the metabolism of widely prescribed cardiovascular, psychotropic, anti-infective and analgesic medicines routinely used in geriatric populations [28]. The relevance of CYP3A4 also reflects the wide range of substrates, inhibitors and inducers associated with this isoenzyme, increasing its susceptibility to clinically significant interactions. These findings suggest the advisability of reinforced pharmacotherapeutic surveillance in patients receiving macrolides, azole antifungals, benzodiazepines, opioids and statins, all of which are frequent components of complex regimens in older adults.

Automated DDI detection systems often overestimate the burden of interactions, highlighting the difference between theoretical pharmacokinetic interactions and those with real clinical relevance [29]. Decision-support tools frequently generate large volumes of alerts, many of which do not translate into clinically measurable harm. Without adequate filtering, these systems may contribute to alert fatigue among prescribers, desensitising clinicians to warnings that do require intervention. In this scenario, contextualised pharmaceutical assessment can help differentiate potential interactions from those more likely to be clinically relevant. Structured medication reviews and deprescribing strategies may contribute to reducing inappropriate polypharmacy and minimising clinically relevant interactions in this population [30].

Transitions of care represent a particularly critical point of vulnerability for older patients. Medication changes made during hospitalisation - often initiated to address acute processes - may persist after discharge without adequate reconciliation, leading to unintended duplications, missed deprescribing opportunities or unrecognised interactions in the early post-discharge period. In this context, coordinated communication between hospital pharmacists, community pharmacists and primary care physicians is especially relevant to improve continuity of care in polymedicated geriatric patients [31]. Community pharmacists should play an important role in detecting clinically relevant interactions after discharge, as well as in medication reconciliation, adherence monitoring and support for deprescribing interventions.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. The study was based on retrospective datasets originally created for pharmacovigilance and medication review purposes, rather than for research, which may introduce inconsistencies in diagnostic coding, incomplete documentation or variability in record accuracy [32].

In addition, direct clinical outcomes such as adverse events, rehospitalisations or mortality were not evaluated, limiting assessment of the real clinical impact of the identified interactions.

Furthermore, the absence of pharmacogenomic data - especially relevant for CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 - limits interpretation of individual metabolic variability, which may influence both the risk of DDIs and their actual clinical impact.

Future studies should incorporate prospective follow-up to evaluate not only the frequency of CYP-mediated DDIs but also their association with clinically relevant outcomes and healthcare resource use [33]. Prospective designs would also allow a more precise evaluation of deprescribing interventions, pharmacist-led care-transition programmes and the potential integration of pharmacogenomics into clinical practice. In addition, future research should strengthen integrated pharmacotherapeutic care models between hospital and community settings, in order to improve medication safety in older adults [34].

CONCLUSIONS

Cytochrome P450-mediated drug-drug interactions (DDIs) are frequent in hospitalised older adults, with polypharmacy identified as the main factor associated with the presence of clinically relevant drug interactions in hospitalised older patients. Although only a proportion of these interactions appears to have greater clinical relevance, the findings support the usefulness of medication review, deprescribing and improved communication between care levels.

The unified analysis performed in this study provides an integrated description of interaction patterns in hospitalised older patients and supports the role of both hospital and community pharmacists in optimising medication safety.
