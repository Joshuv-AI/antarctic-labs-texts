A randomised equivalence study of the EQ-5D-5L Shona versions: evaluation of measurement equivalence between digital and paper formats
Authors: Jermaine M Dambi, Fredrick D Purba, Beatrice K Shava, Anotida R Hove, Shalom R Doyce, Tariro Tunduwani, Ben Domingue, Dixon Chibanda, A Simon Pickard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRoutine assessment of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of people living with HIV (PLHIV) is essential. Background

The Sub-Saharan region bears the highest burden of HIV/AIDS worldwide, with 69% of the 39 million individuals living with HIV/AIDS (PLHIV) located in Sub-Saharan Africa [1, 2]. Unfortunately, non-communicable diseases, particularly common mental health disorders (CMDs), are highly prevalent in PLHIV [3]. For example, up to 30% of PLHIV suffer from comorbid depression [4]. Comorbid common mental disorders (CMDs) have been linked to adverse outcomes throughout the care continuum, such as elevated morbidity and mortality rates, diminished mental health functioning, and an overall decrease in health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Early detection and treatment of CMDs are vital for better outcomes and improved HRQoL in PLHIV [3].

Generic patient-reported health status and HRQoL measures have become central to routine outcome assessment in chronic-care settings, including services for people living with HIV, where they facilitate structured evaluation of patient wellbeing [5]. While condition-specific outcome measures offer a more nuanced, disease-specific HRQoL assessment due to their targeted content validity, generic multi-attribute, patient-reported outcome measures have greater utility because they can be applied in a wider range of contexts, such as routine health evaluations, and comparing overall health outcomes across populations and health conditions [6]. The European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) is a widely used routine health status/HRQoL measurement in health systems [7, 8]. The EQ-5D assesses health in five domains, i.e. mobility, usual activities, self-care, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, with the visual analogue scale (VAS) capturing overall self-rated health [7]. The EQ-5D is extensively used globally and has been translated and standardised in over 150 languages [9]. Also, the EQ-5D is available in different versions, such as the five-level (5L) and three-level (3L) forms, which assess the same five domains but differ in the number of response options per domain [9]. The EQ-5D-3L consists of three response options: “no problem”, “some problems”, and “severe problems”, whereas the 5L version uses five levels: no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems and extreme problems/unable to perform the activity [9].

The EQ-5D-3L has been translated and validated into Shona, a Zimbabwean native language. The translated version demonstrates strong validity and reliability, with utility values that are available and have been widely utilised in research [10–12]. However, the EQ-5D-3L is prone to floor and ceiling effects [13]. A large cohort study (N = 1,023,218) found lower ceiling effects with the EQ-5D-5L (43.8%) than with the EQ-5D-3L (54.4%) [14]. Consequently, the EuroQol Group recommends the EQ-5D-5L version as their generic HRQoL outcome of choice. The EQ-5D is available in paper or digital formats and can be self-, interviewer-, or proxy-administered [15]. Owing to global digitalisation, digital versions are increasingly used compared to paper-based ones [15]. Digital administration increases clinical utility and psychometric performance as it eliminates missing responses, automatically creates a database and facilitates sharing clients’ HRQoL valuations in real-time, optimising clinical care further [16, 17].

Previous studies have demonstrated measurement equivalence between digital and paper-based versions of the EQ-5D [15–17]. Given the floor and ceiling effects identified in the 3L version, the Zimbabwean 3L versions were upgraded to 5L versions in this study. This process included translation and validation of the newly introduced 5L version, which had not previously been empirically tested in Zimbabwe. We therefore set out to evaluate the psychometric performance of the digital EQ-5D-5L Shona version in PLHIV by examining the following properties:

Compare the measurement equivalence of the paper and digital versions.

Compare the measurement equivalence of the paper and digital versions.

Examine known-groups validity by comparing scores across participants’ characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education, and socioeconomic status), thereby identifying factors associated with HRQoL in PLHIV.

This study focused on PLHIV because HIV care in Zimbabwe represents a large, established chronic-care platform in which routine HRQoL assessment is clinically relevant, feasible, and of immediate practical value. Although the EQ-5D-5L is a generic rather than HIV-specific instrument [9], PLHIV provide an appropriate validation cohort because HIV is a prototypical long-term chronic condition in the Zimbabwean context and is frequently accompanied by multimorbidity, psychological distress, stigma, food insecurity, financial vulnerability, and functional limitations [12]. These overlapping physical, psychological, and socioeconomic challenges provide a strong basis for validating the EQ-5D-5L given its multi-attribute nature. Findings on the digital EQ-5D-5L Shona among PLHIV can also inform future validation work in other chronic conditions and in the broader population.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Family Care Centre (FCC) at Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals in Harare, Zimbabwe, from July 2022 to June 2023. The FCC offers in-house testing and medical and psychological services to PLHIV. The FCC is the largest referral centre for HIV-related services nationwide, serving about 3,000 clients per month.

Study design

A parallel, individually randomised design was used to compare the digital and paper EQ-5D-5L Shona versions. A crossover design was not adopted because immediate repeat completion of the same brief instrument in a busy HIV clinic could introduce recall and learning effects, increase respondent burden, and contribute to attrition [18]. The parallel design was therefore a more pragmatic and internally robust approach for isolating mode-of-administration effects under routine clinical conditions while avoiding contamination from repeated testing [18]. After eligibility screening and written informed consent, participants were randomised to complete either the digital or paper version. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing the administration-mode allocation code. These were prepared before data collection, kept concealed from recruiters, and opened only after enrolment.

Study participants’ selection criteria

We recruited consenting adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of HIV and proficiency in English or Shona. Participants with a documented severe psychiatric diagnosis or acute mental health condition in their clinical notes were excluded. This criterion was applied for ethical and methodological reasons: to avoid enrolling individuals who might have impaired capacity to provide informed consent, experience additional burden during an acute psychiatric episode, or have difficulty completing self-report questionnaires reliably at the time of assessment.

Sample size calculation

A minimum of 234 participants per group was required to demonstrate equivalence to rule out differences of 0.3 standard deviations across the paper and digital versions at a 95% confidence interval and 80% power [18].

Outcome measures

Comparator measures and socioeconomic variables were selected a priori because mental health symptoms, food security, financial adequacy, adverse events and substance use are expected to vary with HRQoL among PLHIV and therefore support known-groups validity assessment; these are described below.

Demographic questionnaire

This questionnaire captured age, gender, education level, employment status, marital status, perceived financial adequacy, food security, smoking status, alcohol use, substance use and recent adverse events. Perceived financial adequacy and food security were assessed using self-report items categorised as adequate/inadequate. Recent adverse events were assessed by asking whether the participant had experienced a major adverse event during the previous month.

EQ-5D-5L

The EQ-5D is a standardised, self-report HRQoL measure with five dimensions, i.e., mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Participants also rated their HRQoL on a visual analogue scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. The EQ-5D-3L paper version has been translated into Shona, a Zimbabwean native language, and validated in both the general population and PLHIV [10, 12]. Normative utility scores are available for the Zimbabwean population [10]. In this study, we validated the newly upgraded EQ-5D-5L Shona versions (paper and digital), which were developed through the EuroQol translation/adaptation process, including forward translation, reconciliation, back-translation, expert review, and cognitive debriefing prior to field testing.

Shona symptoms questionnaire (SSQ-14)

The SSQ is a proven, psychometrically sound, native Zimbabwean 14-item tool for screening CMDs [19]. Respondents indicate whether they experienced the 14 psychological symptoms in the past seven days. A reported symptom (“yes” response) is scored as one, and a “no” response is scored as zero. A score of eight is regarded as indicating psychiatric morbidity. The SSQ only takes 2–3 min to complete and is validated in PLHIV in the research setting [19].

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a generic nine-item depression screening tool. Respondents rate the frequency of the listed depressive symptoms over the previous two weeks on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from “not at all = 0” to “nearly every day = 3,” yielding a cumulative score range of 0–27. Scores in the ranges 0–9, 10–14, 15–19, and 20–27 are commonly interpreted as none/minimal, mild, moderate, and severe depression, respectively [19]. The PHQ-9 has been extensively used for clinical and research purposes and is validated in PLHIV in the research setting [19].

Generalised anxiety disorder scale (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 is a seven-item, self-report anxiety screen [21]. Respondents rate the frequency of anxiety symptoms over the previous two weeks on a four-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” (0) to “nearly every day” (3), yielding a total score of 0–21. The criteria values for low, moderate and severe anxiety are 5,10 and 15, respectively [21]. The GAD-7 has been validated in PLHIV in the research setting [19].

Data collection procedure

Prospective participants were recruited as they presented for routine HIV care. Three research assistants (RAs) initially approached patients in the waiting areas, and those who expressed interest were given identification stickers. Two additional RAs then approached individuals with stickers, confirmed eligibility, and initiated the consent process. Participants who met the selection criteria and provided consent were asked to select a sealed envelope containing the random allocation code for one of the two study arms (tablet or paper). The research team then administered a demographic questionnaire to collect participant characteristics such as age and sex. They also assessed literacy and familiarity with technological devices to determine the feasibility of self-completing the EQ-5D questionnaires. The EQ-5D and the accompanying test battery were self-completed, although the research team remained available to assist participants when needed.

Training

To standardise the data collection procedures, we conducted a training workshop for the research team. The research team received training in good clinical practice, including informed consent and administering questionnaires.

Data analysis plan

Descriptive statistics (e.g., means and frequencies) were used to analyse participants’ characteristics. Measurement equivalence was assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to compare adjusted mean EQ‑5D‑5L index and EQ‑VAS scores across administration modes (digital vs. paper). ANCOVA provides covariate‑adjusted estimates of mean differences and effect sizes appropriate for a parallel‑group design in which participants completed only one mode. To examine determinants of HRQoL, we fitted binary logistic regression models for each EQ‑5D‑5L dimension (problem vs. no problem). Logistic regression is the appropriate framework for these categorical outcomes and serves as a sensitivity analysis, allowing us to assess whether patterns of association between demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors and EQ‑5D‑5L responses are consistent with the ANCOVA findings for continuous summary scores. Together, ANCOVA evaluates adjusted mean differences relevant to mode equivalence, while logistic regression offers dimension‑level insight and functions as a complementary sensitivity check on the robustness of HRQoL determinants. The analysis was conducted using SPSS (Version 29). The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Data management/ethical considerations

Data were collected electronically using REDCap on Android tablets. The tablets required two-step authentication: a fingerprint scan and a personal identification number (PIN). These security features were used by authorised study staff to unlock REDCap-enabled tablets; participants were not required to provide fingerprints. Raw data were de-identified using arbitrary numeric participant codes. Consent forms were stored in a secure locker at the University of Zimbabwe, and digital data were stored on a password-protected laptop with backup on OneDrive. Participation was voluntary, and participants were treated as autonomous agents. Those who showed distress or moderate-to-severe scores on the PHQ-9, GAD-7, or SSQ-14 were discreetly assessed by the study team and referred to FCC psychological services for additional support. Parirenyatwa Group of Hospitals granted institutional approval, and ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (Ref: MRCZ/A/2496).

Results

CONSORT flowchart

We screened 930 participants, of whom 700 met the inclusion criteria and were randomly allocated equally to the paper and digital groups (350 participants per group). In the digital group, 340 participants completed the form; 10 subsequently withdrew because of time pressure, leaving 330 participants for analysis. In the paper group, 337 participants completed the form. However, 38 participants withdrew because of time pressure, and 18 had missing VAS scores, resulting in 281 participants for analysis (See Fig. 1).

Measurement equivalence evaluation

The digital and paper groups had comparable utility scores [(mean 0.81 ± 0.14) vs. (0.82 ± 0.11); mean difference = − 0.01; 95% CI approximately − 0.03 to 0.01; Cohen’s d = − 0.08] and VAS scores [(mean 77.5 ± 23.8) vs. (80.5 ± 19.9); mean difference = − 3.0; 95% CI approximately − 6.47 to 0.47; Cohen’s d = − 0.14]. These small effects were within the pre-specified 0.3 SD equivalence framework [18], supporting equivalence between the two modes of administration. In addition, ANCOVA showed no significant effect of administration mode on EQ-5D VAS scores, F (1, 598) = 1.40, p = 0.238, partial η² = 0.002, or EQ-5D utility scores, F (1, 607) = 1.14, p = 0.285, partial η² = 0.002, after controlling for age and education. The overall models were statistically significant for both EQ-5D VAS scores, F(3, 598) = 6.77, p < 0.001, R² = 0.033, and EQ-5D utility scores, F (3, 607) = 5.98, p < 0.001, R² = 0.029; however, the effect of administration mode was negligible in both models. Overall, these findings indicate that EQ-5D VAS and utility scores were not meaningfully affected by whether the measure was completed digitally or on paper, supporting the equivalence of the two administration formats.

Discussion

Overall synthesis

Electronic patient-reported outcome measures are increasingly used in routine care and research. In this study, we sought to validate the digital EQ-5D-5L Shona version, compare its measurement equivalence with the paper version, and assess determinants of HRQoL among PLHIV. The digital and paper-based versions of the EQ-5D-5L Shona demonstrated measurement equivalence. In addition, older age, food insecurity, financial insecurity, and elevated symptoms of CMDs were associated with lower HRQoL.

Measurement equivalence of digital and paper versions

This study demonstrated equivalence between the newly translated paper and digital versions of the EQ-5D-5L Shona, with psychometric performance independent of administration mode. This finding is important because it supports the use of both versions in routine care and research [16, 17, 22, 23]. A randomised crossover study among American patients with irritable bowel syndrome (N = 72) similarly demonstrated measurement equivalence between the paper and digital/mobile-device versions [22]. In that study, intraclass correlation coefficients for the EQ-5D-5L VAS and index scores were 0.822 and 0.77, respectively, indicating high agreement across administration modes [22]. A UK-based randomised crossover study among healthy participants (n = 60) and patients with chronic conditions (n = 30) also supported the measurement equivalence of paper and multiple digital EQ-5D-5L formats, including handheld, tablet, interactive voice response, and web-based administration [16]. In a parallel randomised study, Mulhern et al. (2015) likewise demonstrated measurement equivalence between paper and digital versions of the EQ-5D-5L among UK adults with and without chronic conditions. Unlike studies from high-income countries, however, the present study provides local evidence from a Shona-speaking, low-resource HIV care setting. Differences in health literacy, language, device familiarity, data access, trust in digital health systems, and cultural perceptions of health may influence the transferability of mode-equivalence findings from high-income settings [24].

Digital versions can be programmed to prevent missing responses, thereby improving their utility and psychometric performance [16, 17]. Our findings support this proposition, as the paper version had 6.0% missing data. However, in low-income settings such as Zimbabwe, paper-based versions remain essential because not all studies and healthcare facilities have the infrastructure required for electronic HRQoL data collection. Practical barriers in LMIC settings include limited access to devices, unreliable electricity and connectivity, staff training requirements, data security concerns, cost, and varying levels of digital literacy [24]. Measurement equivalence should therefore not be interpreted as support for digital-only implementation. Indeed, the ISPOR ePRO task force cautions against bias toward digital over paper-based patient-reported outcome measures [23]. Access to both administration modes is therefore valuable and may reduce inequity while supporting patient preference. In Zimbabwean HIV services, this means digital administration can be integrated into routine care where devices, electricity, connectivity, and staff capacity permit, while paper versions remain indispensable in settings with limited digital infrastructure. Although digital administration may reduce missing data and automate scoring, responses may still be influenced by device unfamiliarity, privacy concerns, social desirability, and staff assistance [24]. These factors should be considered carefully before implementation.

HRQoL determinants

Findings from the adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression showed that elevated CMD symptoms were significantly associated with lower HRQoL on both the VAS and utility scores. Comorbid mental disorders are highly prevalent among PLHIV, and given the range of daily challenges they face, fluctuations in mental health status are unsurprising [19]. Zimbabwe has experienced prolonged socioeconomic hardship, with many people living in poverty; this likely contributes to the high burden of CMDs in PLHIV and is consistent with the significant association between lower HRQoL and financial insecurity observed in this study and elsewhere [18, 19, 25]. Similarly, PLHIV reporting food insecurity had higher odds of lower HRQoL than those reporting adequate food security, consistent with our previous study in the same setting [18, 25]. Food insecurity is an important predictor of mental health functioning and HRQoL in PLHIV, particularly in low-resource settings. Finally, the decline in HRQoL with older age is also unsurprising, as ageing in PLHIV is associated with comorbid HIV-related and age-related conditions. For example, immune function declines with age regardless of treatment status, and age-related musculoskeletal disorders become more common, both of which may reduce HRQoL over time [26]. Together, these patterns support the validity of the EQ-5D-5L Shona in distinguishing between groups expected to differ in HRQoL while also highlighting intervention targets for HIV services.

Strengths

These findings should be interpreted in light of several methodological strengths and limitations. First, the randomised study design is a major strength because it enhances internal validity. Second, this is the first study to evaluate the equivalence of translated EQ-5D-5L versions in a low-resource setting, adding to the growing evidence base on the psychometric robustness of digital EQ-5D-5L formats [16, 22]. Electronic versions of the EQ-5D-5L may also facilitate multinational comparative studies. Third, we exceeded the recommended minimum sample size for measurement equivalence studies by recruiting 611 participants, compared with the recommended 234 [18]. For comparison, a similar study recruited 90 participants [17]. Finally, recruiting participants with diverse characteristics, including variation in sex, age, education, and economic status, strengthens the external validity of the findings.

Limitations

Our study was also prone to some biases. First, all items in the electronic data collection system were set to mandatory. Thus, there were no missing responses in the EQ-5D-5L digital version compared with the paper version; this may have led to biased data completeness for the electronic version. Second, no clinical variables were collected. We initially planned to collect clinical variables (e.g., treatment duration, CD4+) to compare the EQ-5D-5L’s known-groups validity. However, collecting clinical variables was not possible at the time of data collection because the electronic data collection system was being upgraded, and only a few participants had meaningful data recorded in paper records. Future studies should collect salient clinical predictors of HRQoL. Third, we did not record the completion time for the paper versions; the time taken for the digital versions could be deduced from the system. Completion time would offer more information on the feasibility of the different administration modes, which should be explored in future studies. Fourth, we recruited only PLHIV, which limits the study’s external validity. Nevertheless, individuals living with HIV often experience multiple comorbid conditions that are likely applicable to other chronic illnesses. Also, condition-specific nuances, for example, HIV-related stigma, may impact HRQoL evaluations and perceptions, hence the need for additional studies in other conditions, including comparative studies recruiting healthy participants. Fifth, the high proportion of women likely reflects sex differences in HIV clinic attendance and engagement with care in this setting, as reported in other Zimbabwean clinic-based studies. This may limit sex-specific inference, particularly for men. Although randomisation reduces the likelihood that mode-equivalence findings were driven by sex imbalance, future studies with larger male samples should confirm the EQ-5D-5L Shona’s performance among men.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated measurement equivalence between the digital and paper-based versions of the EQ-5D-5L Shona, supporting their interchangeable use in routine clinical care and research. Age, food security, financial adequacy, and psychological distress were key determinants of HRQoL, providing evidence of known-groups validity and confirming the instrument’s ability to distinguish between groups expected to differ in HRQoL. These findings also identify important intervention targets for HIV services and underscore the need for tailored strategies to improve HRQoL among PLHIV.
