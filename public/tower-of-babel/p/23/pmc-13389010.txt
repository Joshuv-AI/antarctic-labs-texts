Activity-guided substructure prioritization accelerates discovery of gut microbiota-derived immune-regulating metabolites
Authors: Haoduo Zhao, Liang Chi, Zhenfa Zhang, Yun-Chung Hsiao, Chih-Wei Liu, Yifei Yang, R Balfour Sartor, Kun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGut microbiota exert significant influences on host physiology through bioactive molecules such as indole derivatives. The characteristic aromatic heterocycle substructure shared among various indole derivatives is intrinsically associated with the activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), suggesting a potential structure-activity relationship (SAR). Here, we develop a substructure–activity relationship mass spectrometry pipeline aiming at elucidating SAR information encompassed within mass spectrometry data, further applying it to identify the unknown gut microbiome-derived, indole-like derivatives with AhR agonistic potential. A total of 22 metabolites exhibiting activity-related substructures are identified, four of which were previously uncharacterized. Notably, the discovered sulfonated and methylthiolated compounds represent two previously underexplored microbiome-mediated reactions. Subsequent assays validate their AhR agonistic activities and downstream immune regulatory effects in male mice. Overall, this study presents an exploratory framework for MS data mining focusing on activity-guided substructure prioritization, enabling the discovery of bioactive microbiota-derived molecules and distinct microbiome-mediated reactions.Subject terms: Mass spectrometry, Mass spectrometry, Metabolomics

Introduction

Human gut microorganisms biochemically synthesize a plethora of bioactive small molecules that often significantly impact the host physiological functions and metabolic homeostasis1. Indole derivatives, a broad class of organic compounds derived from bacteria-catalyzed tryptophan metabolism2, have garnered significant attention due to their pivotal role in mediating the intricate communications between commensal bacteria and host health3. Various indole derivatives can activate aromatic hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)4,5 and pregnane X receptor6,7, thereby playing critical roles in regulating host physiological functions, including neurotransmission, hormonal regulation, anti-inflammation, immune response, etc.8–10. Previous studies have also suggested potential linkages between microbiome-derived indole derivatives and pathological conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease11, autism spectrum disorders12, and inflammatory bowel diseases13. Over the past few decades, researchers have focused on characterizing the host-microbiome metabolomic interactions14, while also seeking to postulate and identify novel indole derivatives15. For instance, Chyan et al. screened a series of neuroprotective indole derivatives and discovered an endogenous metabolite, indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), which exhibited superior hydroxyl radical scavenging activity compared with melatonin16. Besides, Song et al. isolated and annotated a previously unknown indole derivative, 2-(1′H-indole-3′-carbonyl)-thiazole-4-carboxylic acid methyl ester from porcine lung, with conspicuously potent AhR binding and activation ability17. Additionally, Poeggeler et al. reported a novel indole derivative, indole-3-propionamide, which reversed the age-related decline in mitochondrial energy capacity and extended the lifespan in rotifers18. However, given the vast metabolomic chemical space and numerous uncharacterized gut microbiome-mediated enzymatic reactions, many indole and indole-like derivatives likely remain unidentified19. Consequently, the discovery of unknown indole derivatives with neurological activities and pharmaceutical potential remains a promising and compelling pursuit.

Liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) has been widely employed in metabolomic profiling and unknown compound identification20,21. Accurate information obtained from both MS1 (e.g., m/z and retention time) and MS2 (e.g., fragmentation pattern) is crucial for determining chemical formulas and elucidating molecular structures. Specialized HRMS data acquisition and processing workflows greatly facilitate the identification of unknown compounds, including techniques such as precursor ion scan (PIS), product ion scan, neutral loss scan (NLS), etc.22. For instance, the PIS technique has proven highly valuable for identifying clusters of compounds sharing common substructures, thus offering a unique opportunity to link MS data with bioactivity profiles. Lafaye et al. applied PIS and NLS to murine urine samples, tentatively identifying 4-ethylphenyl sulfate as a biomarker for heavy metal toxicity23. For decades, significant efforts have been devoted to systematically uncover structure–activity relationships embedded in MS data (i.e., SAR by MS), including precursor-product ion pairing24, substructure prioritization25, diagnostic ion-based annotation26, and MS/MS networking27. However, a substantial knowledge gap remains in integrating and extrapolating these approaches to the identification of bioactive molecules produced by gut microbiota, which functions as a key commensal regulator in the host through exogenous metabolic derivatives28.

Hence, this study seeks to (i) develop a substructure-activity relationship mass spectrometry (StAR-MS) pipeline to facilitate the automated integration and analysis of PIS data; (ii) identify both known and unknown gut microbiome-derived molecules with AhR activity; and (iii) evaluate their bioactivity and internal exposure (Fig. 1). Overall, this study presents an exploratory framework for MS data analysis focusing on activity-guided substructure prioritization, enabling the discovery of bioactive microbiota-derived compounds and previously underexplored microbiome-mediated reactions. These findings contribute to decoding the bioactivity information encompassed within MS data, enhancing the understanding of host–microbiome interactions and advancing the frontiers of human exposome.

Results

Characteristics fragmentation of indole derivatives

A schematic overview of the StAR-MS pipeline was detailed in the method section (Fig. 1). StAR-MS is an HRMS-based, substructure-guided metabolite discovery framework that builds on existing SAR knowledge; thus, no new quantitative SAR analyses were performed in the current study. According to previous studies, varieties of indole derivatives can exhibit similar fragmentation patterns under high-energy collisional dissociation29,30 (Fig. 2a). Considering the abundance of product ions and representative structural scaffolds, four fragments namely methyl indole ion (C9H8N+, m/z 130.06513) with its de-methyl form (C8H7+, m/z 103.05423), and indole ion (C8H6N+, m/z 116.04948) with its HCN-eliminated form (C7H5+, m/z 89.03858) were investigated as diagnostic product ions in search for indole-like derivatives. Two fragment combinations were set as: 130.1 and 103.1 (with/without other ions) and 89.1 and 116.1 (with/ without other ions) due to the resolution constraint on a QQQ instrument (Fig. 1.3b). For method validation, we prepared a standard chemical mixture composed of known indole derivatives and performed StAR-MS for untargeted analysis. All six features of indole derivatives were successfully captured and annotated by StAR-MS, exhibiting accurate m/z, retention time (RT), and consistent fragment pair matching (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, during subsequent untargeted analysis in fecal sample, the StAR-MS workflow also uncovered two additional compound classes, namely quinoline and tetrahydroharman derivatives (Fig. 2a). While similar fragmentation patterns arising from common aromatic heterocyclic scaffolds were expected, these findings introduced considerable challenges and uncertainties in downstream metabolite identification and unknown structure elucidation.

StAR-MS analysis of murine feces

Next, the established StAR-MS workflow was applied to fecal samples from germ-free (GF) and conventionally raised specific pathogen-free (CV) mice to identify known metabolites and discover previously uncharacterized compounds. As shown in Fig. 3a, a total of 9133 precursor-fragment ion pairs were obtained from four parallel PIS experiments and processed using StAR-MS. 8635 unique pairs remained after de-redundancy filtering (to eliminate overlapping features with thresholds of Δ m/z ≥ 1 Da and Δ RT ≥ 0.1 min), serving as an intra-correction for ion pairs with identical fragments. The PIS dataset was then cross-mapped, yielding 8178 precursor–fragment combinations (i.e., a single precursor with multiple fragments matched). To minimize mismatches and fragment duplications, the ion combinations were filtered using the same criteria as the de-redundancy step, serving as an inter-correction for combinations with identical m/z and RT. After fragment combination filtering and manual inspection for intensity and peak shape, 330 features were selected as targets for parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) to collect their MS2 spectra. Subsequently, 36 candidate features were retained after excluding potential adducts and in-source fragments (ISF), with their accurate MS1 validated using LC-Orbitrap. In conclusion, 18 known metabolites were successfully annotated with a confidence level of 1. These features were further merged with untargeted metabolomics full MS1 data and localized in the differential expression space between CV and GF mice (Fig. 3b). The annotated metabolites included indole derivatives, quinoline derivatives and tetrahydroharman derivatives, with diagnostic product ions efficiently captured and mapped by StAR-MS (Fig. 3c). In summary, the findings highlight the effectiveness of StAR-MS in processing complex biological samples, even under low abundance and matrix interference conditions, demonstrating its ability to detect, deconvolute and resolve MS features with substructure relevance.

Identification of unknown indole derivatives

Two indole derivatives were identified by StAR-MS. One chromatogram peak (peak 1) was extracted at 3.97 min by the ion at m/z 285.0532 (C11H13N2O5S+, 2.42 ppm) (Fig. 4a, b). The intensity of peak 1 in CV mice was significantly higher than that in GF mice (log2FC = 2.34, padj = 1.83e−10) (Fig. 3b), which suggested its microbiome-associated origin. For MS2 spectrum (Fig. 4c), the diagnostic fragments at m/z 188.07050 (C11H10NO2+, 0.56 ppm), m/z 146.06001 (C9H8NO+, 0.21 ppm), m/z 130.06522 (C9H8N+, 0.73 ppm), m/z 118.06530 (C8H8N+, 1.48 ppm) and m/z 91.05467 (C7H7+, 4.87 ppm) were proportionally consistent with those in the MS2 spectrum of tryptophan (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The neutral loss of 79.95626 between m/z 285.05328 (C11H13N2O5S+, 2.42 ppm) and m/z 205.09702 (C11H13N2O+, 0.65 ppm) suggested the elimination of a sulfonate group (SO3, 0.08 ppm). This hypothesis was further supported by the PRM spectrum of peak 1 in negative ion mode, in which the product ions at m/z 79.95615 (SO3-, 1.46 ppm) and m/z 80.96405 (HSO3−, 0.51 ppm) were detected. Thus, these results indicate that peak 1 was tentatively ascribed to sulfonate-conjugated tryptophan (TRP-SO3). Due to the unavailability of commercial standard, peak 1 was identified as 2-amino-3-(2-sulfo-1H-indol-3-yl)propanoic acid with a confidence level of 2.

Peak 2 was extracted at 4.43 min, corresponding to an ion at m/z 241.0631 (C10H13N2O3S+, 4.31 ppm) (Fig. 4d, e). The intensity of peak 2 showed no significant difference between CV and GF mice (log2FC = 0.95, padj = 0.068) (Fig. 3b). The MS2 spectrum (Fig. 4f) revealed diagnostic ions at m/z 161.10728 (C10H13N2+, 0.28 ppm), m/z 144.0808 (C10H10N+, 0.17 ppm), m/z 115.05449 (C9H7+, 2.29 ppm) and m/z 91.05465 (C7H7+, 4.65 ppm), which were proportionally matched with those in the MS2 spectrum of tryptamine (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Similar to TRP-SO3, the neutral loss of 79.95582 between m/z 241.0631 (C10H13N2O3S+, 4.31 ppm) and 161.10728 (C10H13N2+, 0.28 ppm) was indicative of a sulfonate group (SO3, 5.58 ppm). This hypothesis was also supported by the negative ion mode PRM spectrum, where the product ion m/z 79.95615 (SO3-, 1.46 ppm) and m/z 80.96405 (HSO3-, 0.51 ppm) were detected. Thus, these results indicate that peak 2 was tentatively ascribed as sulfonate-conjugated tryptamine (TSH-SO3). Similar as TRP-SO3, the commercial standard for TSH-SO3 was unavailable. Based on observed fragmentation patterns, the sulfonate group is likely located at the ortho-position of the pyrrole ring31. Taken together, peak 2 was identified as 3-(2-aminoethyl)-1H-indole-2-sulfonic acid, with a confidence level of 2.

Identification of unknown quinoline derivatives

Two quinoline derivatives were identified by StAR-MS. Peak 3 was extracted at 6.81 min by the ion at m/z 192.0478 (C10H10NOS+, 0.48 ppm) (Fig. 5a, b). The intensity of peak 3 in CV mice was significantly higher than that in GF mice (log2FC = 10.49, padj = 4.39e-11). In the MS2 spectrum (Fig. 5c), the fragments at m/z 146.06000 (C9H8NO+, 0.28 ppm), m/z 128.04955 (C9H6N+, 0.58 ppm), and 91.05469 (C7H7+, 5.09 ppm) were observed and consistent with those in the MS2 spectrum of 2-hydroquinoline (2-HQ) (Supplementary Fig. 1c), which implicated a shared core structure. The characteristic neutral loss of 15.02347 between m/z 192.04767 (C10H10NOS+, 0.48 ppm) and m/z 177.02420 (C9H7NOS+, 0.49 ppm) indicated the demethylation process (-CH3). Notably, a subsequent neutral loss of 30.9642 between 177.02420 and 146.06000 matched that between 159.01366 and 128.04955, which both implicated a desulfurization process (+H-S). The demethylation and desulfurization were sequentially observed on the MS/MS fragmentation pattern, implying peak 3 contains a methyl-thiol substructure. Additionally, a distinct neutral loss of 18.01054 between m/z 177.02420 (C9H7NOS+, 0.49 ppm) and m/z 159.01366 (C9H5NS+, 0.39 ppm) suggested the dehydration process (-H2O) occurred independently from the de-methylthiolation. Together, these observations support the hypothesis that peak 3 represents a methylthiolated derivative of 2-HQ. To characterize the site of reaction, we conducted density function theory (DFT) calculation of the parent compound 2-HQ. The Fukui function analysis indicated that carbon C-3 displayed a global maximal value of the lowest unoccupied molecule orbital (LUMO) density, suggesting it as the site susceptible to electrophilic addition (Supplementary Method. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on this, we proposed a reaction mechanism involving the nucleophilic substitution of a methyl-thiol group at the C-3 position on 2-HQ backbone32. To our knowledge, no public spectral database has documented this compound with no commercial standard available. Therefore, we synthesized the compound (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4) and confirmed its identity with identical MS1 retention time (Fig. 5d) and MS2 fragmentation pattern (Fig. 5e) as observed in fecal samples. Collectively, peak 3 was identified as 3-(methylthio)quinolin-2(1H)-one (2-HQ-3-MeS) with a confidence level of level 1. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported methylthiolated metabolite produced by gut microbiota based on available MS/MS libraries and literature, revealing a previously unrecognized microbial catalytic reaction.

Peak 4 was extracted at 5.62 min by the ion at m/z 208.0426 (C10H10NO2S+, 0.21 ppm) (Fig. 5f, g). The intensity of peak 4 in CV mouse feces was significantly higher than that in GF mice (log2FC = 9.51, padj = 2.34e−8). In the MS2 spectrum (Fig. 5h), the diagnostic ions at m/z 146.06012 (C9H8NO+, 0.55 ppm), m/z 116.04978 (C8H6N+, 2.62 ppm) and 89.03911 (C7H5+, 5.99 ppm) were observed and consistent with those in the MS2 spectrum of 2,8-quinolinediol (2,8-Q) (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Similar as 2-HQ-3-MeS, demethylation (neutral loss of 15.02342 between 208.04272 and 193.01930), dehydration (neutral loss of 18.0106 between 193.01930 and 175.00870) and desulfurization (neutral loss of 31.97206 between 193.01930 and 161.04724) were observed in the MS/MS spectrum of peak 4. A mono-hydroxylation mass defect was observed both in MS1 signal and MS2 product ions between peak 3 and peak 4, which indicated the tentative structure of peak 4 as methylthiol-conjugated 2,8-quinolinediol (2,8-Q-3-MeS). Like 2-HQ-3-MeS, no current spectra database has documented this structure, and the commercial standard was unavailable. Unfortunately, the synthesis trial failed due to unexpected rearrangement reactions on the phenol group of 2,8-Q during electrophilic addition. Taken together, peak 4 was identified as 8-hydroxy-3-(methylthio)quinolin-2(1H)-one with a confidence level of level 2.

In total, four gut-microbe-derived indole and quinoline derivatives were identified by StAR-MS, representing previously unreported metabolites according to available MS/MS libraries and literature. The identified sulfonated and methylthiolated metabolites were distinguished from ISF or adduct artifacts by their RTs, which deviated from their parent compounds by more than ±0.1 min. The reduced retention of sulfonated metabolites on reverse-phase columns reflects inherently enhanced hydrophilicity, further supporting their metabolic origins. All identified metabolites contained the expected activity-related substructures, highlighting the high sensitivity and specificity of the developed StAR-MS workflow.

Quantification of the identified metabolites in mice organs

To characterize the internal exposure landscapes and evaluate the tissue distribution of the four identified metabolites in vivo, we conducted semi-quantitative measurements of their levels across various organs from CV mice (Supplementary Fig. 5). All four metabolites exhibited their highest abundances in fecal samples among all tissue compartments analyzed. TRP-SO3 and TSH-SO3 were detected in feces, colon, liver, and lung, whereas 2,8-Q-3-MeS and 2-HQ-3-MeS were found in feces, colon, serum, lung, and liver. Notably, none of the discovered metabolites were detected in the brain, likely due to the restrictive nature of the blood-brain barrier33. To provide a more precise comparison of physiological concentrations with those of known metabolites, we adopted a targeted metabolomics workflow with internal standard correction to quantify the concentrations of 2-HQ-3-MeS and Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) in mouse serum and feces (Supplementary Fig. 6). The concentrations of IAA in mice were 2377 ± 529 ppb (~13 μM) in feces and 106 ± 53 ppb (~609 nM) in serum, which are comparable to previous quantitative studies34. In contrast, 2-HQ-3-MeS exhibited much lower physiological concentration, with 38 ± 11 ppb (~199 nM) in feces and 26 ± 12 ppb (~136 nM) in serum (Supplementary Table. 1). Notably, the serum-to-feces concentration ratio percentage of 2-HQ-3-MeS (~68%) was substantially higher than IAA (~4.5%), suggesting a higher retention rate and greater bioaccumulation potential for 2-HQ-3-MeS. Given the limited knowledge currently available on the identified metabolite, the quantitative analysis of 2-HQ-3-MeS provides valuable insights into its ADME properties and lays the foundation for future biochemical and mechanistic investigations. Collectively, these findings indicate that the discovered metabolites are systemically distributed across multiple organs, highlighting the urgent need to elucidate their biochemical significance.

Assessment of AhR binding and activation

Furthermore, in vitro (reporter cell line) and in silico (molecular docking) binding assays were employed to assess the AhR activation potential of metabolites identified by StAR-MS. As shown in Fig. 6a, all compounds identified by StAR-MS exhibited significant AhR agonist activities, albeit with varying potencies. In addition to known indole derivatives, quinoline derivatives such as 2-HQ and 2,8-Q demonstrated significant agonistic effects, consistent with previous findings that identified 2,8-Q as a full AhR agonist in a human cell line35. Notably, β-carboline derivatives like Tetrahydroharman-3-carboxylic acid (THHCA) and 2,3,4,9-Tetrahydro-1H-beta-carboline-3-carboxylic acid (TBCCA) showed moderate and strong AhR activation, respectively, aligning with earlier reports36. They are likely biosynthesized via Pictet–Spengler condensation reactions37, with tryptamine serving as the precursor38. The presence of an aromatic heterocyclic substructure may underlie their AhR activity. However, neither THHCA (log2FC = 0.89, padj = 0.13) nor TBCCA (log2FC = -0.08, padj = 0.39) showed significant abundance difference between CV and GF mice, indicating non-microbial sources of origin such as diet. Furthermore, molecular docking-based virtual screening supported the AhR binding potential of the discovered metabolites, which exhibited binding free energies comparable to known AhR ligands (Fig. 6b).

Having synthesized and structurally confirmed 2-HQ-3-MeS, we next evaluated its AhR agonistic activity using both in vitro and in vivo assays (Fig. 6c–e). In an AhR luciferase reporter assay, 2-HQ-3-MeS exhibited AhR activation in a dose-response manner (Fig. 6e). As indicated by RT-qPCR, both 2-HQ-3-MeS and IAA induced CYP1A1 expression in LS-180 cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with 2-HQ-3-MeS showing a consistently higher induction across the tested dose range (Fig. 6h). At 100 μM, 2-HQ-3-MeS caused an approximately 17-fold increase in CYP1A1 mRNA, compared with only ~5-fold induction by IAA. The observed AhR-activating potency suggests that 2-HQ-3-MeS may play a more prominent role than IAA in modulating AhR-mediated host–gut interactions, immune responses, and metabolic homeostasis. Molecular docking simulation revealed two hydrogen bonds formed between 2-HQ-3-MeS and key residues Ser346 and Cys333 within the AhR ligand binding domain (LBD) (Fig. 6c, d). Similarly, multiple hydrogen bonds were established between 2,8-Q-3-MeS and Ser346, Cys333, Tyr322, and His337 (Fig. 6f, g), accompanied by extensive hydrophobic interactions contributing to a stable and rigid binding conformation within the LBD. Together, results from the binding assays provided critical insights into the molecular recognition mechanisms of the identified metabolites, offering valuable knowledge for future structure–activity relationship studies and elucidation of their mode of action (MoA).

Immune modulation effects of 2-HQ-3-MeS

AhR has been identified as playing a pivotal role in regulating the development and function of the immune system, primarily through the activation of ligands derived from the microbiome39. To uncover the potential immune modulation effects induced by the identified compound, C57BL/6 CV male mice were administered the ligand (2-HQ-3-MeS; 5 mg/kg*BW per day) and vehicle (5% DMSO) via oral gavage, with colon tissue (lamina propria) collected for flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 7a). Sex-based analysis was not performed, as it was beyond the scope of the current metabolite discovery-centric study. Quantitative analysis of serum samples from dosed mice revealed a 2-HQ-3-MeS concentration of 44.81 ± 7.8 ppb, representing an approximately 1.7-fold increase compared with the control group (26.0 ± 12.0 ppb). This dose level was selected to achieve circulating concentrations within a biologically relevant range, sufficient to elicit measurable biological effects without inducing overt toxicity. As shown in Fig. 7b, c, 2-HQ-3-MeS administration increased the level of CD8+ T cells but decreased the level of Treg cells, while CD4+ T cells were not affected (Fig. 7d, e). No significant difference was observed between CD8+ T cells and Treg between vehicle group and 2-HQ-3-MeS-treated group in mesenteric lymph nodes (Fig. 7f), although a similar trend was observed. CD8+ T cells, which are key mediators of cytotoxic responses and immune surveillance, play a critical role in controlling infections and tumor progression in the colon40,41. Conversely, Tregs, which function to suppress immune responses and maintain tolerance, are essential for preventing excessive inflammation and autoimmunity in the gut42. Therefore, the increased CD8+ T cells and decreased Tregs may indicate a shift toward an immune-activating environment in the colon. This was at least partially contributed by the ligand binding and activation of AhR, which was reported to promote resident memory CD8+ T cells (TRM) differentiation and granzyme B production among human immune cell populations39. While no significant change in IAA concentration was observed, dysregulation of other unquantified AhR ligands and potential synergistic activation may also contribute to the observed immune-regulating effects, suggesting potential cumulative or combinatorial modulation, which remains to be investigated. Besides, these immune modulation effects of 2-HQ-3-MeS suggest potential implications in conditions such as colorectal cancer, where enhancing CD8+ T cell responses may improve anti-tumor immunity37. Modulation of 2-HQ-3-MeS, as well as bacteria producing this compound, may serve as potential microbiota-targeted immunotherapies for relevant diseases.

Discussion

Exploring novel bioactive compounds with significant exposure implications remains a critical yet challenging endeavor of human exposome research43. In the context of gut microbiota, various secondary metabolites have been elucidated, with their biochemical functions well-characterized. However, limited understanding of gut-mediated biotransformation reactions, along with the lack of advanced analytical strategies, has emerged as a major bottlenecks19. In this study, we established an untargeted metabolomics-coupled, activity-guided substructure prioritization workflow and identified four gut microbiome-derived metabolites with confirmed bioactivities. Notably, the discovered sulfonated and methylthiolated compounds revealed two previously underexplored microbiome-mediated reactions, which contributed valuable insights into gut-specific transformation mechanisms and significantly advanced the understanding of human exposome.

SAR analysis is widely recognized in toxicology and pharmacology studies, linking chemical substructures or functional groups with specific biological effects. Recent advancements in MS data acquisition and analysis have substantially facilitated the development of untargeted metabolomics and unknown identification44,45, paving the way for the integration of SAR insights with MS-based workflows. Notably, activity metabolomics has emerged with increasing research interest46, representing an outstanding advancement in leveraging metabolomics for activity-based screening and the identification of bioactive molecules. Analytical strategies such as expression screening, chemical proteomics, and in silico modelling have not only accelerated the discovery of bioactive metabolites but also enabled comprehensive elucidation of their MOA through multi-omics integration. A similar concept, SAR by NMR, was first introduced in the late 1990s47, wherein high-affinity ligands were identified based on the residue chemical shifts in LBD derived from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). While effective, SAR by NMR is limited by its intensive workload, low throughput, and high cost. In contrast to the analysis of protein, our established StAR-MS approach focuses on the decoding of bioactivity-associated substructures in ligands, offering a high-throughput, cost-effective alternative for ligand profiling. It should be clarified that StAR-MS is an activity-driven, substructure-guided metabolite discovery framework, distinct from traditional quantitative SAR analyses that systematically relate structural variations to graded biological responses. Starting with AhR as a proof-of-concept receptor, the combinatory strategy of diagnostic ion-guided annotation and precursor–product ion pairing intrinsically allows for scalable exploration and application across diverse ligand–receptor systems. For example, cholic acids, ligands for Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), exhibit orderly fragmentation patterns in low m/z range, making them promising candidates for analysis by StAR-MS. Besides, the StAR-MS pipeline can be compatible with complementary activity-oriented approaches such as pull-down assays and chemical proteomics. This structural and functional flexibility distinguishes StAR-MS from a receptor-specific case study, positioning it as a generalizable platform for ligand–receptor investigations. While certain optimization may be required under different study contexts, the utility, scalability, and compatibility of StAR-MS support it as a flexible framework for exploring and extrapolating substructure–activity relationships via MS analysis.

Biotransformation refers to the enzymatic modification of chemicals, typically altering their physiochemical properties, bioactivity, and fate within biological systems48. Among the well-characterized biotransformation pathways, phase I reactions (e.g., oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, etc.) and phase II reactions (e.g., glucuronidation, acetylation, sulfation, etc.) are predominant processes in mammalian systems. However, the prevalence and mechanisms of biotransformation processes in commensal bacteria remain poorly understood. Sulfonation, a key phase II conjugative reaction, involves the enzymatic transfer of a sulfonate group to hydroxyl-containing compounds and plays a critical role in host xenobiotic detoxification49. Recent studies have identified a sulfotransferase-encoding gene cluster in Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron that mediates sulfonation of metabolites in vitro50, suggesting that gut microbiota may possess intrinsic sulfonation capabilities. In this study, we provide compelling evidence supporting the contribution of commensal bacteria to in vivo sulfonation, either directly via bacterial enzymatic catalysis or cooperatively through modulation of host gene expression, substrate availability, or cofactor levels. Nevertheless, the tentatively identified structures for TRP-SO3 and TSH-SO3 require definitive structural confirmation prior to further elucidation of their biosynthetic pathways and underlying MoA.

Another noteworthy finding is the previously uncharacterized two gut microbiota-derived methylthiolated metabolites. The microbial sulfur cycle has been increasingly recognized as an crucial regulator of host physiology51. Sulfate-reducing bacteria produce various sulfur-containing species such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and H2S-conjugated molecules, which have been implicated in a range of pathological conditions. Of relevance, bacteria methylthiotransferases have been characterized to catalyze the transfer of a methylthiol group to macromolecules such as tRNA and proteins, in a reaction dependent on S-adenosylmethionine radicals52. This modification plays a role in stabilizing codon–anticodon interactions and enhancing translational fidelity53. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has reported gut microbiota-catalyzed methylthiol transfer to small-molecule metabolites. Future work is needed to identify the specific enzymes involved and to elucidate the underlying biosynthetic pathway. Furthermore, broader efforts should be directed towards the extrapolatory discovery of other microbe-dependent biotransformation reactions, contributing to a comprehensive understanding of host-microbiome interactions and potential therapeutic applications.

Several factors may account for the discrepancy between serum levels of 2-HQ-3-MeS in vivo and the concentrations needed to elicit AhR activity in vitro. First, serum levels reflect systemic circulation and likely underestimate local concentrations in the colon, which may be sufficient to induce localized immune-regulatory effects. Second, chronic in vivo treatment (14 days) may trigger cumulative modulatory effects or adaptive responses uncaptured in acute in vitro assays (24 h). Third, even at low-nanomolar levels, 2-HQ-3-MeS may contribute to synergistic AhR activation alongside a complex milieu of other endogenous ligands, such as identified indole and quinoline derivatives. Finally, the enhanced production of CD8+ T cells and deregulation of Treg cells may not rely solely on AhR/CYP1A1 activation and could involve additional regulatory pathways yet to be explored.

Limitations of this study and future outlooks are discussed. The current study primarily focused on indole alkaloid heterocycles as a proof-of-concept scaffold, while its scalability to other metabolite classes remained largely unexplored. The product ion pairs investigated should be both abundant and highly selective, which restricts their applicability when applied to compounds lacking patterned fragmentation behavior, such as lipids and highly aliphatic compounds. Besides, the proposed StAR-MS framework relied heavily on the knowledge-guided interpretation of existing spectral data, which may introduce bias or confounding factors. Incorporation of complementary strategies, such as machine learning–aided SAR approaches, may extend the current workflow by establishing systematic associations between bioactivity-related substructures and MS fragmentation patterns. Of the 36 unknown features investigated for AhR-related substructures, only 22 were successfully identified, underscoring the need for more comprehensive structural characterization. Notably, the identified metabolites may act as AhR modulators rather than dominant agonists in vivo, given their relatively low concentrations and comparable potencies to those of known agonists, indole alkaloids, for example. Systemic measurements may not fully capture localized exposure, as gut or tissue-specific concentrations may differ from circulating levels and could be biologically relevant. No direct enzymological evidence or responsible microbial taxa is identified. The definite origins and enzymatic pathway of the discovered sulfonation and methylthiolation reactions remain to be elucidated. Finally, as an analytical study within the gut microbiome paradigm, the investigation of human biomonitoring data and disease associations remains an ongoing challenge. Tailored analytical methods have not yet been established in line with prior studies, precluding targeted or retrospective screening in existing human cohorts. Building on the characterized physiological existence and elucidated biochemical mechanisms, future epidemiological analysis and clinical investigations are warranted to establish human relevance and assess potential health implications.

Mice sample collection and preparation

For metabolomics study, CV C57BL/6J mice and GF C57BL/6 J mice were used. For flow cytometry study, only CV C57BL/6J mice were adopted. All mice were obtained from UNC National Gnotobiotic Rodent Resource Center and provided a standard pelleted rodent diet and tap water ad libitum. Mice were of standard genetic background, of male only, and aged 8–12 weeks at the start of experiments. CV (N = 15) and GF (N = 9) mice were housed separately at 20 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity on a 12 h:12 h light: dark cycle, with urine and feces samples collected once per day. Mice organ samples were harvested after sacrifice and conserved at −80 °C until use.

Instrumental analysis

Instrumental analysis of mice feces samples was performed in three parts. For the 1st part, pooled mix fecal samples collected from CV mice were analyzed on a TSQ Quantis triple quadrupole (LC-QQQ) mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to obtain PIS data for selected fragment ions. Four fragment ions were selected based on literature review, which suggested the most common substructures of indole derivatives: m/z 89.0, m/z 116.1, m/z 103.1, and m/z 130.1. The precursor scan window was set as m/z 100-400 (according to other known indole derivatives), and only HESI positive mode was performed. For chromatographic separation, a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) was used. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in ACN (B), flowing at a rate of 0.4 mL/min under 40 °C with a 15-min gradient: 2%B, 0–1 min; 2 to 15% B, 1–3 min; 15 to 50% B, 3–6 min; 50 to 98% B, 6–7.5 min;98% B, 7.5–11.5 min; 98 to 2% B, 11.5–11.6 min; 2% B, 11.6–15 min. Other parameter configurations were set as follows: scan rate 1000 Da/s, collision energy 30 V, Q1 resolution 0.7 FWHM, Q2 resolution 0.7 FWHM, CID Gas 1.5 mTorr.

For the 2nd part, fecal extracts from CV (N = 15) and GF (N = 9) mice were analyzed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Vanquish UHPLC coupling to a high-resolution accurate-mass Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Orbitrap) to acquire full MS1 scan and PRM data. The chromatography and mobile phase conditions were consistent with LC-QQQ experiments. Only HESI positive mode was performed under following ion source settings: sheath gas 60 L/min, aux gas 10 L/min, sweep gas 1 L/min, spray voltage 2.75 kV, capillary 325 °C, and aux gas heater 400 °C. Background blank, method blank, and indole standard mixture samples were injected every five injections parallelly during MS data acquisition sequence.

For the 3rd part, tissue and fecal samples collected from CV mice were analyzed on LC-Orbitrap for the quantification of identified metabolites following the same MS1 settings as described above. For quantification of 2-HQ-3-MeS in mice serum and feces, an internal standard-based calibration method was adopted. 50 ppb of isotope-labeled IAA (IAA-d7) was added to a nine-point calibration curve generated by serial dilution of IAA and 2-HQ-3-MeS, covering concentrations of 1000, 500, 100, 50, 10, 5, 1, 0.5, and 0 ppb. In parallel, 50 ppb IAA-d7 was spiked into the fecal and serum samples collected from C57BL/6 mice (N = 5), followed by metabolite extraction and LC-MS analysis as described above. Analyte concentrations were calculated from the calibration curve and corrected by dilution factors. All instrumental analysis protocols were adapted from previous research14.

StAR-MS workflow

StAR-MS is a self-constructed platform developed for joint analysis of precursor ion scan data collected from multiple product ions. The StAR-MS workflow was shown in Fig. 1 and described in following subsections, step 1 to step 3.

Step 1: LC-QQQ-MS Precursor Ion Spectrum Build-up. For PIS on LC-QQQ, multiple precursor signals were included in a single peak, consequently generated a precursor ion spectrum. Utilizing the Thermo Xcalibur embedded peak picking algorithm, PIS information was acquired per peak for each intended product ion. StAR-MS assembled and cleaned the imported PIS, further combined into a single peak list. The precursor peak list was finally filtered by unit mass to keep the precursor with maximal fragment intensity, and ready for joint-fragment precursor mapping.

Step 2: Joint Fragment-Precursor Mapping. The core of StAR-MS is to merge PIS obtained from different product ion targets, further prioritizing and determining candidate features with multiple product ions of interest. To achieve this goal, StAR-MS first rounded retention time to the nearest one decimal, which separated coeluting signals and reduced false mapping rate greatly. As QQQ-MS is a low-resolution technique, m/z value was round to the nearest integer, which contributes to reducing redundancy caused by divergent mass mapping. After data transformation and organization, StAR-MS started precursor mapping by the attribute alignment (identical RT and m/z) among different product ion groups. The precursor list with reasonable fragment pairs and fair total ion intensity (in this study 5000) were finally extracted for performing PRM on high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Step 3: LC-Orbitrap-MS Accurate Mass Verification. After acquiring the MS/MS pattern of the PRM candidate, it is necessary to confirm whether the m/z of the product ion of interest is accurate or undesired. This concern came from the resolution gap between QQQ (~100 ppm) and Orbitrap (~5 ppm) as well as the rounding algorithm, which was directly linked to the correct substructure identification. Metabolite identification

For metabolite identification, an untargeted metabolomics compound annotation workflow was adopted54. Briefly, for the obtained MS1 profiling data, XCMS was firstly run for peak picking and feature alignment55,56. PRM assays were then performed for features identified by StAR-MS to collect accurate m/z, RT, and MS2 information, further matched with in-house metabolite library and mzCloud database to identify known metabolites. The remaining unannotated features were manually curated to remove potential noise, non-protonated adducts, and ISF57–59. For the identification of ISF and adducts, coeluting ions were recognized and grouped into unique ion groups based on the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N ≥ 10) and RT shift (ΔRT ≤ 0.1 min). Spectra similarities and chromatographic correlations were calculated within each ion group using MSDial (ver 4.8.0) and CAMERA package (ver 1.28.0) in R, respectively. ISFs were identified and excluded based on the convergence of chromatographic behavior, MS/MS triggering windows, spectral consistency (MS/MS similarity ≥ 0.5), and fragmentation interpretability57,60,61. Next, chemical formula was assigned to the filtered features with the limitation of C0−100H 0−200O0−10N0-1S1−3 with mass accuracy tolerance of 5 ppm21. The MS/MS fragmentation pattern was further investigated for unknown structure elucidation, and the possible theoretical formula was assigned for each product ion within mass accuracy threshold. For metabolic differential expression analysis, fold changes and P values were calculated using a two-sided Student’s t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction for multiple comparisons54. For the synthetic compound, 1H NMR was applied to confirm the structure. Identification confidence level was indicated separately for each concluded metabolite62.

AhR reporter gene assay

Commercially available human AhR reporter gene assay (male origin, catalog No. IB06001-32, INDIGO Biosciences, Inc.) was used to determine potential agonism effect of the discovered indole derivatives. The AhR reporter cell incorporated the luciferase reporter gene linked to an AhR-responsive promoter region, which allowed the sensitive measurement of AhR agonist activity63. The cell type and its characterization are proprietary and were not further authenticated in this study. Detailed information of the reporter gene assay was described in Supplementary Method 2.

Cell culture and RT-qPCR

Human colon carcinoma cell (LS-180) was obtained from ATCC (female origin, catalog No. CL-187) utilized to investigate the AhR-activation effects of 2-HQ-3-MeS. The cell line was chosen for its high AhR expression and physiological relevance to gut-derived metabolites64. The cells were obtained from ATCC and were not further authenticated in this study. LS-180 cells were cultivated in Eagle’s minimum essential medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 4 mM L-glutamine, 1% non-essential amino acids. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis, LS-180 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at an initial density of 0.3 × 106 for each well. Upon reaching ~80% confluency, cells were treated with gradient concentrations of 2-HQ-3-MeS at 0, 0.16, 0.8, 4, 20, and 100 μM (n = 3). IAA, an established endogenous AhR ligand, was administered in parallel to separate batches of cells at the same gradient levels for gene expression comparison. Cell viability following 2-HQ-3-MeS exposure was assessed via the resazurin assay (n = 3). After 24 h treatment, cells were harvested with total RNA extracted, purified, and subjected to reverse transcription followed by qPCR tests. Primer sequences of CYP1A1 are as follows: (F) GATTGAGCACTGTCAGGAGAAGC, (R) ATGAGGCTCCAGGAGATAGCAG. Primer sequences of β-actin are as follows: (F) CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC, (R) AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT. All primers were synthesized by IDT Technologies. Detailed experimental information regarding RT-qPCR procedure was described in Supplementary Method 3.

Molecular modeling

Density function theory (DFT) simulation was applied to compute single point energy and molecular orbitals by Schrödinger software (NY, USA). Molecular docking-based virtual screening was carried out to assess protein-ligand binding affinity by Autodock Tools 1.5.6 and Autodock Vina following previous studies65–67. Detailed information of the molecular modelling was described in Supplementary Method 4.

Oral gavage and colon tissue preparation

2-HQ-3-MeS were first dissolved in DMSO and then diluted by PBS to 1 mg/mL (5% DMSO). Male C57BL/6 mice around 13-week-old were treated with 2-HQ-3-MeS or vehicle by daily oral gavage for 2 weeks. Murine tissues were processed to isolate cells as described previously68. Briefly, mice were euthanized using CO₂, and the colon lamina propria and mesenteric lymph nodes were collected for analysis. The colons were opened longitudinally and washed with cold PBS to remove luminal contents. For the isolation of lamina propria leukocytes, the tissues were incubated in a buffer containing 5 mM EDTA and 0.145 mg/ml dithiothreitol at 37 °C for 20 min to remove epithelial cells. The remaining tissue was then digested in a digestion buffer (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, nonessential amino acids, 55 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 20 mM HEPES, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, 0.5 mg/ml DNase, and 0.1 mg/ml Liberase TL purified enzyme blend) at 37 °C for 25 min. The digested tissues were mechanically dissociated and filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer. Leukocytes were enriched using a 37.5% Percoll gradient and subsequently washed with PBS, yielding cell suspensions ready for downstream analysis. Mesenteric lymph nodes were processed by smashing, followed by filtration through a 70 µm cell strainer. The resulting cell suspensions were washed with PBS and prepared for downstream applications.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells from tissues were processed as described previously69. Single-cell suspensions were prepared and incubated with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain for 15 min on ice to exclude dead cells. For surface marker staining, the cells were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (detailed in Supplementary Table. 2) in the presence of purified anti-mouse CD16/32 and purified rat gamma globulin to block Fc receptors for 30 min at 4 °C. After surface staining and subsequent washing, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (ThermoFisher Scientific) for 1 h at 4 °C. Fixed cells were then stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for intracellular cytokines and transcription factors, incubating for at least 1 h at 4 °C. Data acquisition was performed using a BD Fortessa X-20 flow cytometer with FACSDiVa software (BD Biosciences), and data analysis was conducted with FlowJo software (TreeStar).
