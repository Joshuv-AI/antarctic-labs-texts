Higher rates of immune-related adverse events in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-negative patients following adjuvant checkpoint inhibitor therapy
Authors: T. Powles, K. Madjar, B.I. Balas, C. Carter, L. Essioux, A. Tehrani, S. Mariathasan, D. Heinzmann, F. Arellano, R. Mohindra, G.S. Chandler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIMvigor010 demonstrated potential for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) to guide treatment in muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma (MIUC), in which adjuvant atezolizumab (AA) reduced the risk of death by approximately 40% in ctDNA-positive patients. However, ctDNA-negative patients received no survival benefit from AA and had higher rates of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Here, we further explore the relationship between ctDNA and irAEs.Patients and methodsAssociation between baseline (BL) and on-treatment (OT) ctDNA and time to first irAE was assessed in MIUC patients treated with AA (n = 384) versus observation (n = 397). Cox proportional hazard model was used with adjustment for BL covariates, sensitivity analyses were carried out at 6 months to address survival bias, and landmark analysis was carried out at day (D) 43 for patients with OT ctDNA.ResultsAll grade irAE rates were higher in AA patients with negative BL ctDNA versus positive (54.7% versus 32.5%, respectively) and mainly driven by low-grade irAEs [hazard ratio (HR) 1.91 (1.25; 2.90)]. irAE rates decreased with increasing ctDNA levels as a continuous variable [ctDNA log-MTM/ml: HR 0.92 (0.87; 0.97)]. There was no association with BL ctDNA and high-grade irAEs [HR 0.87 (0.38; 1.98)], but discontinuation for AEs was higher in BL ctDNA-negative patients versus positive (3.3% versus 0%). Patients who cleared OT ctDNA after D43 had no association between ctDNA and irAEs versus non-clearance [HR 1.13 (0.35; 3.59)]. Patients who progressed to positive ctDNA after D43 did not experience any irAEs versus non-progressors.ConclusionsLow-grade irAEs and discontinuation due to AEs were higher in ctDNA-negative patients receiving AA despite the lack of a survival benefit. Continued evaluation of the potential role of ctDNA is warranted, as it may help identify patients who may be curable with only local therapy and where additional systemic treatment may only increase toxicity without improving survival.

Introduction

The potential for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) profiles to guide treatment of patients with cancer has been demonstrated across tumor types and treatment regimens.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 In particular, the potential for ctDNA to be used in the curative setting to determine presence of residual tumor and to inform the nature and timing of adjuvant therapy was recently demonstrated in the IMvigor010 trial evaluating adjuvant atezolizumab versus best supportive care in patients with muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma (MIUC) after radical cystectomy: in the intent-to-treat population, there was no benefit of atezolizumab therapy compared with the observation group8; however, a post hoc analysis demonstrated that patients who were ctDNA-positive had improved survival in those who received atezolizumab therapy compared with those who did not, where atezolizumab reduced risk of death by approximately 40%.3,5

Interestingly, patients in that analysis who were ctDNA-negative received no benefit from atezolizumab therapy and also had higher rates of toxicity than those who were ctDNA-positive.3,5 These findings led to the development of the IMvigor011 trial, a similar global phase III study evaluating ctDNA-directed treatment with adjuvant atezolizumab in patients with MIUC after radical cystectomy that demonstrated the effectiveness of using ctDNA status to guide therapy.9

Thus, ctDNA may help identify those patients with non-metastatic tumors who may be cured after local therapy and where additional systemic treatment may only increase the risk of toxicity without improving long-term outcomes.10 Additionally, ctDNA may be helpful to optimize benefit/risk balance in the metastatic setting by monitoring tumor evolution, characterizing resistance mechanisms, and indicating when to switch to an alternate therapy.10

In this analysis, we further explore the safety profile of the IMvigor010 study and assess the relationship between ctDNA status and the development of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). The goal is to further characterize how both patient-specific and tumor-specific factors may drive response to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy and better understand who to treat and when in the individual patient journey.

Patients and methods

Analysis population

Retrospective analysis of IMvigor010, a global phase III, open-label, randomized, controlled trial sponsored by F. Hoffmann-La Roche/Genentech (NCT02450331) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of adjuvant treatment with atezolizumab compared with observation in patients with high-risk MIUC after surgical resection. The safety analysis population was used, including all patients being exposed to study treatment who had current consent in place and analyzed according to the treatment that they actually received (n = 384 patients in the atezolizumab arm and n = 397 patients in the observation arm).

The ctDNA biomarker-evaluable population (BEP) used for this analysis included all safety evaluable patients with baseline [cycle 1 day 1 (C1D1)] plasma samples evaluated for ctDNA status (n = 295 patients in the atezolizumab arm and n = 275 patients in the observation arm). All references to C1D1 are pre-dose.

For the on-treatment ctDNA analysis, a subset of the BEP was used that had available ctDNA data at C3D1. For the analysis of patients with ctDNA clearance (i.e. change from ctDNA-positive status at baseline to negative status at C3D1), only patients with baseline ctDNA-positive status were included. For the analysis of patients with ctDNA progression (i.e. change from ctDNA-negative status at baseline to positive status at C3D1), only patients with baseline ctDNA-negative status were considered.

Biomarker data

Blood samples at C1D1 (median of 79 days after surgical resection) and C3D1 were prospectively collected for exploratory biomarker assessments and analyzed at a later time point. ctDNA was detected using personalized and tumor-informed ctDNA testing (Natera Signatera assay) as previously described.3,5 ctDNA-positive status at C1D1 was defined as two or more mutations detected in the plasma samples. In addition to ctDNA status (positive versus negative), continuous ctDNA measurements [mean tumor molecules per milliliter of plasma (MTM/ml)] were analyzed. MTM/ml was defined as the average of tumor molecules across all variants per milliliter of plasma and reported if at least two variants were detected in the sample. On-treatment ctDNA was assessed at cycle 3 day 1 (C3D1, which is 6 weeks postbaseline). Only on-treatment samples collected between study days 36 and 50 (i.e. ±7 days around day 43, corresponding to C3D1) were included in the analysis. The ctDNA change from baseline was considered in the analysis in terms of continuous measurement (log fold change) and status change [clearance (change from positive to negative) and progression (change from negative to positive)].

Adverse events

For the purpose of analysis of irAEs, a set of comprehensive definitions comprising sponsor-defined, Standardized Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA Version 24.1) Queries, high-level terms, sponsor-defined adverse events Grouped Terms, and sponsor-defined adverse events of special interest (AESI) were used to identify and summarize irAEs by medical concept (AESIs listed in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107701).

The definitions used to capture the medical concepts were broad in nature and were applied to capture adverse events (AEs) both in the atezolizumab and observation arms as they were reported by investigators and irrespective of causality. The medical concepts represented are known or potential class effects associated with ICIs. NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events were used for the severity grading of AEs and immune-related AEs.

To assess whether the correlation between ctDNA and immune-mediated toxicity would be consistent across different reporting frequencies, severities, and outcomes, we focused on the following irAEs: any grade (G), low grade (G1-2), and high grade (G3-5); AEs leading to study discontinuation; AESIs; commonly reported (i.e. cutaneous irAEs and thyroid disorders); high mortality (e.g. myocarditis); high morbidity [i.e. pneumonitis, hepatitis, colitis, and severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR)]; and high patient burden (i.e. diabetes and adrenal insufficiency).

Outcomes

For each patient, the time-to-onset of the first irAE of a specific type was considered, i.e. for a specific irAE type, every patient was counted only once, even if the patient had more than one event of the same type. The irAEs selected for this analysis were considered separate outcomes.

Death was considered a competing event, and event-free patients were censored at the end of the AE reporting period (i.e. discontinuation or completion, or last treatment date plus 30 days, whichever was earlier).

The entire safety follow-up period was considered in the analysis. However, ctDNA-negative patients were on average treated longer with atezolizumab than ctDNA-positive patients (median treatment exposure time 10.4 versus 5.5 months in ctDNA-negative versus positive patients). To ensure that the observed higher risk of low-grade irAEs in ctDNA-negative patients was not due to different lengths of treatment exposure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted considering only irAEs occurring within the first 6 months from the date of first treatment administration (atezolizumab arm) or randomization (observation arm) (i.e. landmark analysis at 6 months landmark time point).

Statistical analysis

The association between ctDNA and the time-to-onset of the first irAE of a specific type per patient was assessed separately in the atezolizumab and observation arms using a Cox proportional hazards regression model adjusted for the following baseline characteristics: PD-L1 status (IC 2/3 versus IC 0/1), prior chemotherapy (yes versus no), race (Caucasian, Asian, Other), sex (female versus male), and age (years). Results were reported in terms of hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and nominal (unadjusted) P values for an unbiased assessment of on-treatment ctDNA, which is a time-varying covariate.11 Additionally, a more complex causal analysis, such as a principal stratum analysis, using the monotonicity and equipercentile assumption outlined in Bornkamp, was not considered, given the low irAE count in the control arm.12 Only P values < 0.05 were considered potentially meaningful.

In the baseline analysis, ctDNA was assessed separately as status (positive versus negative) and as a continuous measurement (MTM/ml on a log-scale). A sensitivity analysis was carried out using a 6-month cutoff for the baseline ctDNA data to address potential bias due to differences in treatment exposure between baseline ctDNA subgroups. For the on-treatment ctDNA analysis, the change in ctDNA at C3D1 (i.e. 6 weeks postbaseline) from C1D1 (i.e. baseline) was considered by ctDNA status (clearance and progression) and by continuous measurement (log-fold change of MTM/ml). Clearance was defined as patients whose ctDNA status changed from positive at baseline to negative at C3D1, and compared with patients with a persistent ctDNA-positive status (nonclearance). Progression was defined as patients whose ctDNA status changed from negative at baseline to positive at C3D1, and compared with patients who remained ctDNA-negative (non-progressors). The effect of on-treatment ctDNA change on irAE risk after C3D1 was assessed using landmark analyses with day 43 (D43, corresponding to the C3D1 ctDNA assessment) as landmark time. Similar to the analysis of baseline ctDNA, a sensitivity (landmark) analysis was carried out using a 6-month cutoff for the on-treatment ctDNA data to address potential bias due to differences in treatment exposure between on-treatment ctDNA subgroups.

Plots of the cumulative incidence function (CIF) estimated by the Aalen-Johansen (AJ) estimator were used to visualize the irAE risk over time in each treatment arm. Death was considered a competing event in all safety time-to-event analyses: in the CIF plots, the AJ estimator accounted for competing events, and in the Cox model, the cause-specific hazards approach was used, treating death as censoring. Missing values were not imputed. Missing data were discarded from the analysis, and a complete case analysis was carried out.

Results

The distribution of baseline characteristics was similar between the overall safety evaluable population and the BEP, which included all patients with available baseline ctDNA (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107701). These baseline characteristics were also similarly distributed between baseline ctDNA-negative and ctDNA-positive patients (data not shown). In particular, within the treatment arms, the proportion of ctDNA-positive patients was 35% (n = 95/275) in the observation arm compared with 39% (n = 114/295) in the atezolizumab arm.

The distribution of baseline ctDNA (both as status and as continuous measurement) was very similar across PD-L1 IHC subgroups for both PD-L1 IC and TC scoring (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107701).

Patients who were ctDNA-positive at baseline and cleared ctDNA to become on-treatment negative after D43 showed no clear association in all grade irAEs compared with those who did not clear ctDNA [HR 1.13 (0.35; 3.59)]. Discussion

In this study, we further explored the relationship between ctDNA and the onset of irAEs in the IMvigor010 study evaluating adjuvant treatment with atezolizumab in patients with MIUC. Patients who were ctDNA-negative at baseline had higher rates of irAEs following adjuvant atezolizumab treatment than ctDNA-negative patients who underwent observation only. Given that patients who were ctDNA-negative at baseline had generally longer survival, we attempted to address this imbalance of duration of therapy and potential for survival bias with a landmark analysis at 6 months. Nevertheless, the potential for patients in the ctDNA-negative arm to have a longer duration of therapy is inherently problematic, given the potential for overtreatment and increased toxicity in the absence of any benefit. These findings are significant because these patients who were ctDNA-negative at baseline and received adjuvant atezolizumab therapy did not have a survival benefit compared with those who were ctDNA-negative and underwent observation only.

Although the overall safety profile between ctDNA-negative and ctDNA-positive patients was similar to previous studies and representative of the known atezolizumab safety profile, there was an increased rate of low-grade (G1-2) irAEs, in particular thyroid toxicity (Table 1). There was also a slightly higher rate of study discontinuation due to AEs in ctDNA-negative patients than in ctDNA-positive patients. There was no association between high-grade (G3-5) irAEs and ctDNA status; however, the numbers of both study discontinuation and high-grade irAEs were small.

There was no clear association between on-treatment ctDNA profiles and irAEs; however, the number of events was small. Nevertheless, the slight increase in irAEs in patients with ctDNA clearance compared with patients remaining positive is notable, given that it is consistent with the increase in irAEs demonstrated in patients who were ctDNA-negative at baseline. Additionally, this phenomenon was also seen in patients who experienced ctDNA progression, as they did not have any irAEs, in contrast to patients remaining ctDNA-negative. Lastly, in patients who were ctDNA-negative at baseline and experienced a relapse of their tumor while on study, there was no substantial difference in the number of patients who experienced an irAE compared with those who did not (21% versus 18%, respectively). However, in the patients who were ctDNA-positive at baseline and experienced a relapse of their tumor while on study, the number of patients who experienced an irAE was approximately half of those who did not (25% versus 49%, respectively).

Interestingly, the higher rate of irAEs due to thyroid dysfunction in patients who received atezolizumab and were ctDNA-negative at baseline was more than twice that of patients who were ctDNA-positive (19.3% versus 7.9%, respectively) (Table 1). This was also nearly twice the rate of those reported in a prior analysis of 15 atezolizumab clinical trials across five indications (19.3% versus approximately 11%, respectively).13 However, it should be noted that the specific rate of immune-mediated thyroid dysfunction, as well as other irAEs, following the single-agent use of ICIs in the adjuvant setting, is not yet known because most large-scale studies in this specific patient population are still ongoing. Additionally, potential differences in the schedule of thyroid function testing across study protocols could have impacted the rates of thyroid dysfunction identified in various studies. Despite the challenge in comparing irAE rates across studies, in later stages of disease, and with different treatment regimens, this higher rate of immune-mediated thyroid toxicity in ctDNA-negative patients is notable, especially given prior findings that similar thyroid toxicity was associated with improved OS in patients receiving atezolizumab for treatment of advanced cancers across multiple indications, and that this was also associated with increased polygenic risk for autoimmune thyroid disease.14

Two potential mechanisms may be involved in this phenomenon of increased irAEs in ctDNA-negative patients. One possibility is that patients with residual tumor, as indicated by detectable ctDNA after surgery with curative intent, still have sufficient systemic immune suppression via engagement of immune-suppressive pathways by these remaining circulating tumor cells such that a systemic response to checkpoint inhibition could be sufficiently blunted compared with patients who are ctDNA-negative. Similarly, it is possible that the remaining circulating tumor cells in ctDNA-positive patients provided enough target-mediated drug disposition by the intended target (i.e. tumor cells) with comparatively less target-mediated drug disposition by unintended targets, such that a sufficient amount of atezolizumab in ctDNA-negative patients was available to cause greater unintended inflammation of healthy tissues and subsequent irAEs. This is supported by the fact that baseline PD-L1 status in the surgical specimens was similar regardless of ctDNA status (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107701) and that PD-L1 status only correlated with improved survival in patients who were ctDNA-positive.3

The other possibility is that patients without residual tumor have a higher immune set point15,16 that enabled better immune surveillance of the tumor, thus making it more amenable to complete resection, and also potentially resulted in a more robust immune response to ICI therapy, as demonstrated by a higher rate of irAEs. This phenomenon may also be involved, although to a lesser degree, in patients who were ctDNA-positive post-operatively and subsequently cleared ctDNA after adjuvant therapy, and then experienced a slightly higher rate of irAEs than patients who did not clear ctDNA. Such mechanisms could also be involved in patients who progressed from ctDNA-negative to ctDNA-positive while on treatment, where patients who became ctDNA-positive experienced no irAEs, in contrast to patients who remained ctDNA-negative. Although the number of events in this group is very small, and this retrospective analysis was not powered to identify such differences, these findings are supported by emerging evidence demonstrating that underlying factors associated with the immune set point, such as germline genetics, auto-antibody profiles, T cell profiles, and T cell receptor diversity, may contribute to the robustness of immune response following ICI therapy and a corresponding increased risk of irAEs.14,17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that specific tumor mutations detected via ctDNA may be associated with increased risk of irAEs.22

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. This was an exploratory, post hoc analysis of the IMvigor010 study, which was not designed to assess the association between ctDNA and safety endpoints. Additionally, there were small numbers of patients with high-grade irAEs, and patients with ctDNA available for analysis of on-treatment clearance of ctDNA and on-treatment progression of ctDNA; thus, findings regarding these patients should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, as the ctDNA-negative patients were treated almost twice as long as the ctDNA-positive patients, the use of a landmark analysis to address potential survival bias may not have been sufficient to account for any potential impact of the small number of late-onset irAEs on the findings. Lastly, this analysis was based upon a single study in adjuvant bladder cancer, and results may not be generalizable to other ICIs and/or tumor types, given that the degree of tumor shedding; thus, the utility of ctDNA monitoring may differ across tumor types.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates an increased risk of irAEs in patients who are ctDNA-negative and received adjuvant atezolizumab therapy after radical cystectomy for MIUC compared with patients who were ctDNA-positive and received adjuvant atezolizumab therapy. It provides a rationale for further exploration of ctDNA as a predictive marker for identifying who to treat and when in an individual’s cancer journey in order to ensure a favorable benefit/risk balance. Further work is needed on understanding the sensitivity and specificity of monitoring ctDNA at the level of the individual test and the ability of each test to accurately detect ctDNA, as well as the suitability of ctDNA monitoring across tumor types, given the different rates of DNA shedding by different tumors.
