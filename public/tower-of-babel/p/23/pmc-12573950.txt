Seroprevalence of COVID-19 neutralizing antibodies among multi-ethnic staff of an Asian primary healthcare institution: insights from point-of-care testing and implications for booster vaccination decisions
Authors: Prawira Oka, Huan Jia, Patthara Kongsuphol, Say Yong Ng, Vivekanandan Saravanan, Chirk Jenn Ng, Aminath Shiwaza Moosa, Mengfei Xiong, Shih Ying Gun, Li Ping Marianne Tsang, Jingyi Lim, Kayshini Vijaykumar, Cassandra Xin Yi Ho, Patrina W L Chua, Sharon Y H Ling, Megan E McBee, Peter R Preiser, Hadley D Sikes, Ngiap Chuan Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 vaccines have been crucial for establishing immunity; however, emerging data suggest vaccine efficacy is reduced within six months. Healthcare staff face an elevated COVID-19 risk and should make an informed decision to receive timely boosters to maintain their immunity.This study aims to determine the COVID-19 neutralizing antibody (nAb) seroprevalence among primary care staff and the impact of serological testing on their vaccination decision.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved multidisciplinary primary healthcare professionals working in 10 public primary care clinics from December 2022 to July 2023. A questionnaire captured sociodemographic data, COVID-19 related history and attitudes toward serological testing. Their COVID-19 nAb levels were measured via point-of-care CoVIm™ Rapid SARS-CoV-2 nAb Test and laboratory cPass™ SARS-CoV-2 nAb Detection Kit.ResultsThe study included 474 subjects, mostly female (88.8%), with a mean age of 40.6 years (SD = 12.3). All received at least two COVID-19 vaccinations, and 80.6% reported at least one infection. COVID-19 nAb seroprevalence was high (99.2%). Post-vaccination, 79.7% contracted COVID-19, with the median time to infection being 163 days. Most staff (93.9%) desired to know their COVID-19 immunity status through a finger pick test (77.0%) instead of venepuncture. Over two-thirds (68.1%) indicated the results would influence their booster vaccination decision.ConclusionThe study revealed a high seroprevalence of COVID-19 nAb among the fully vaccinated participating staff. The necessity for timely boosters is underscored by 79.7% contracting COVID-19 post-vaccination. Most subjects were willing to undergo point-of-care testing, with results potentially influencing their decisions for booster vaccination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11865-8.Keywords: Booster, COVID-19, Healthcare worker, Primary care, Vaccination

Introduction

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, extensive vaccination programs were implemented globally to confer protective immunity and reduce the risk of infection and hospitalization [1]. However, recent data reveal that vaccine efficacy is reduced as early as 6 months post-vaccination [2]. An individual’s immunity against COVID-19 can be determined by measuring their immunoglobulin and neutralizing antibody (nAb) levels [3]. High concordance has been reported between protective immunity and nAb levels [2, 4, 5]. As nAb levels and immunity towards COVID-19 wane over time, monitoring these levels may potentially inform individuals on the optimal timing to receive booster vaccinations.

Timely COVID-19 booster vaccinations are crucial in maintaining herd immunity with the potential to significantly reduce morbidity and mortality [6]. A North American study among 100,000 individuals found that a booster strategy would result in a gain of 3.7 quality-adjusted life years while reducing direct medical costs by USD 6.7 million [7]. The findings were echoed in Thailand, with booster vaccinations associated with a three-fold reduction in mortality and an estimated economic saving exceeding 30 billion Thai Baht [8]. While there is a strong argument for COVID-19 vaccination for public health, it is imperative to promote autonomy by providing individuals with the requisite information to make an informed decision on booster vaccinations. This finding was shared by Al-Taie et al., where, despite 85% of individuals viewing booster vaccinations positively and believing that it would reduce their risk of COVID-19 infection or complications, only 57.4% were willing to receive a booster if they were given adequate information [9]. By providing individuals with their nAb levels, they can make an informed decision to receive timely booster vaccinations.

As the individual immune response to vaccination may vary, a personalized approach to assess the protective immunity status may be required before booster vaccinations. A “test-and-boost” approach would facilitate the timely administration of booster doses while ensuring judicious vaccine utilization. Currently, most methods of determining nAb levels involve protracted laboratory-based methods that require a phlebotomist for venous blood collection, blood sample processing, laboratory facilities, and skilled operators to run the tests. A simple point-of-care (POC) tool that can measure nAb levels would be preferred to a more resource-intensive test.

CoVIm™ is a POC rapid neutralizing antibody test that detects nAb from a drop of fingertip blood within ten minutes and has shown comparable performance to the laboratory cPass™ SARS-CoV-2 nAb Detection Kit [10–12]. The test determines the nAb level by assessing the disrupted complex formation between the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptor and the viral receptor binding domain located on the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, the causative agent of COVID-19. The nAb level is interpreted as an inhibitory percentage, with high inhibition corresponding with high nAb levels. CoVIm™ was developed and manufactured by Thrixen Pte. Ltd. (Singapore).

Staff at healthcare institutions are at increased risk of contracting COVID-19 through their work and would benefit from informed booster vaccinations. A prospective UK and US study found that frontline healthcare workers were 11 times more likely to contract COVID-19 infection than the public [13]. In Singapore, most acute respiratory illnesses are managed in primary care [14]. As COVID-19 is now endemic, healthcare workers should be adequately protected through timely booster vaccinations to minimize their infection and disease transmission risk.

This study aims to evaluate the seroprevalence of COVID-19 nAb levels and the acceptability of a “test-and-boost” approach among the staff of a primary healthcare institution using the CoVIm™ rapid neutralization antibody test.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study comprised three sub-studies: (1) seroprevalence (2), “test-and-boost” and (3) clinical evaluation of the CoVIm™ POC test.

Setting

Singapore has a dual private-public primary healthcare system, with public primary care provided by three healthcare clusters. SingHealth Polyclinics (SHP) has ten primary care clinics (polyclinics) across Eastern Singapore that serve over a third of the population.

Study population

The target population was SingHealth Polyclinics staff aged 21 years and above who received at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose. The study set 21 years old as the cut-off because it is the legal age of consent in Singapore. Unvaccinated individuals were excluded, as the study aimed to assess the intention to receive COVID-19 booster doses rather than to initiate primary vaccination. Staff who received a blood transfusion or systemic immunoglobulins in the past 3 months, suffered from a bleeding disorder, immunosuppressive or immunodeficient state, were acutely ill, received an investigational COVID-19 or Middle East Respiratory Syndrome vaccine were excluded.

SHP staff comprise 1,454 allied health professionals, nurses, doctors, dentists, ancillary, pharmacy and research staff. Most staff (84%) are female, with ancillary staff forming the largest group (28.7%).

Subject recruitment

Between December 2022 and July 2023, all staff working across the 10 SingHealth Polyclinics were invited to participate in the study via email invitation and clinic presentations. Interested staff met with a clinical research coordinator to learn more about the study. During this meeting, they were screened using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Informed consent was obtained from staff who were eligible and agreeable to participate in the study.

Outcome

Sub-study 1: Prevalence of seropositive and seronegative COVID-19 virus among individuals working in SingHealth Polyclinics.

Sub-study 2: Percentage of individuals working in SingHealth Polyclinics who agree to the “test-and-boost” approach to COVID-19 vaccinations.

Sub-study 3: Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value of the CoVIm™ POC test compared to the gold standard cPass™ test.

Research instruments

Sub-study 1 and 2: A questionnaire captured the staff’s sociodemographic information, clinical data that included their COVID-19 infection and vaccination history, and the acceptability of the “test-and-boost” approach. Questions relating to the “test-and-boost” approach were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale.

Sub-study 3: Staff underwent the CoVIm™ POC finger prick test and a venepuncture cPass™ test to detect COVID-19 nAb levels.

nAb level testing

CoVIm™ test was run at SingHealth Polyclinics by a study team member per the CoVIm™ quick card (Supplementary Figure 1). GenScript cPass™ SARS-CoV-2 Neutralization Antibody Detection Kit was run in the laboratory according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Sample size calculation

Sub-study 1 & 2: For these studies, the sample size was calculated based on the seroprevalence of 50%, which gave the largest sample size required for a study [15]. The precision of 5% and 95% confidence interval were assumed, with an estimated sample size of 385.

Sub-study 3: For the clinical evaluation study involving the medical device CoVIm™ test, the Health Sciences Authority of Singapore required at least 50 positive and 50 negative specimens under the Pandemic Special Access Route (PSAR) [16]. Based on a conservative estimated 10% of SHP staff who would have a negative test, 500 staff would be required.

The final sample size of this study is based on Sub-study 3 (N = 500), as it requires the largest sample size to answer the research question.

During the study, as the COVID-19 pandemic transitioned towards an endemic state, the PSAR requirements were no longer valid [17]. Hence, recruitment was halted at a final sample size of 474 staff.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analysis was used to describe the baseline socio-demographic and clinical data; Mean with standard deviation (normally distributed data) or median (skewed data) was used for continuous variables, while percentages were used for categorical variables. Univariate analysis was performed using Chi-square (categorical variables) and student’s t-test (continuous variable) to determine associations. Kaplan-Meier plot was used for survival analysis. For grouped comparisons, Welch’s t test was used. P < 0.05 was used as the level of significance. GraphPad Prism (Version 10) was used for statistical analyses and graph plotting.

COVID-19 infection and Vaccine-related information

All participants underwent nAb assessment using the lab-based cPass™ test and the POC CoVIm™ test. Individual data points from all 474 subjects are presented in Supplementary Figure 2. Both tests employ a competitive assay detection principle [10–12, 18], where high nAb levels lead to loss of signal detection, while low nAb levels lead to high signal detection. Test results were reported as percentage (%) inhibition, where higher nAb levels corresponding with greater % inhibitions. Results demonstrated that the cPass™ produced inhibition levels over 70%, whereas CoVIm™ yielded a wider distribution (Supplementary Figure 2).

The seroprevalences of COVID-19 nAb with the cPass™ and CoVIm™ tests were 99.2% and 98.9%, respectively (Table 3). The CoVIm™ test demonstrated an accuracy of 98.5% using cPass™ results as a reference. PPV (positive predictive value) and sensitivity of the CoVIm™ test were 99.4% and 99.2%, respectively. There was a limited number of negative samples (n = 4), corresponding with a calculated specificity of 25.0%.

Discussion

As of January 2023, Singapore has achieved a full vaccination rate of over 90%, greater than globally (64%) [19]. The high vaccination rates were consistent in this study, with 99.4% of SingHealth Polyclinics (SHP) staff receiving at least three vaccine doses. The high seroprevalence (99.2%) of neutralizing antibodies (nAb) among staff at SHP is consistent with studies conducted among healthcare workers (HCWs) and the general public in other countries [20–23]. Given the strong protection conferred by COVID-19 vaccination, nearly 99% of staff did not require hospitalization during an infection, reinforcing the crucial role of effective vaccination programs.

The timing of vaccinations is important as COVID-19 immunity wanes over time, evidenced by over half of fully vaccinated staff suffering an infection within six months post-vaccination. In contrast, a meta-analysis by Feikin et al. found that vaccine efficacy is reduced by 21% at six months among fully vaccinated individuals [24]. The reduced vaccine efficacy seen among the study population could be due to the increased risk faced working in a healthcare setting [13]. Concerningly, nearly 45% of Algerian HCWs declined boosters, believing primary vaccination would suffice [25]. Additionally, HCWs worldwide have reported vaccine hesitancy due to concerns over vaccine durability [26, 27]. The uncertainty surrounding their immunity could be addressed through pre-booster serology testing to facilitate timely booster vaccinations. Most study participants (93.9%) expressed a similar desire to know their immunity levels, with over two-thirds stating it would impact their decision to receive booster vaccinations. Thus, a “test-and-boost” approach could provide HCWs with information on their immunity status and empower them to make more informed vaccination decisions.

Currently, a “test-and-boost” approach is challenging to implement because available COVID-19 serological tests are resource and time-intensive. The cPass™ test requires not only a phlebotomist to collect a venous blood sample but also a trained laboratory technician with access to a laboratory facility. Over three-quarters of study participants preferred less invasive blood collection via finger prick instead of venepuncture. In addition to the resources required to perform the laboratory cPass™ test, it also has a turnaround time of one to two hours, thereby limiting its utility as a pre-booster test. The desire for an accessible point-of-care (POC) test was echoed by nearly three-quarters of the study population that wanted results to be available within 15 min. Therefore, the POC CoVIm™ test provides a suitable alternative to the more resource-intensive cPass™ test with comparable accuracy (98.5%).

The assessment of the POC CoVIm™ test was limited by only four staff being seronegative, with three testing falsely positive. This occurred despite attempts to mitigate the impact of known causes of false positives, including testing by trained staff following a standardised protocol to minimise contamination, temperature and humidity fluctuations, and practice variation [28]. While the test has been proven effective in detecting seronegative samples [10–12], future studies could be conducted among seronegative individuals to validate the findings. However, given the time elapsed since the pandemic, identifying an adequate number of seronegative individuals could prove challenging.

Aside from the individual impact of healthcare staff suffering an infection, significant costs reported at an average of USD671.4 per infected staff are incurred from work absenteeism specifically due to COVID-19 infection [29]. Community transmission and the economic implications of healthcare service disruptions, accounting for up to 8% of total health expenditure [30]. By adopting a “test-and-boost” approach, these costs can be prevented through timely booster vaccinations, ensuring a non-disrupted healthcare service in hospitals and primary healthcare settings. Moreover, this approach would also be more cost-effective, with most study participants willing to pay Singapore Dollars (SGD) 10 (£5.83) or more for the test, compared with the SGD 150 (£87.51) vaccine [31]. Therefore, healthcare staff with adequate immunity can avoid taking a booster shot prematurely, which reduces the potential risk of side effects and allows vaccine resources to be redirected to other essential services. Importantly, future studies are required to establish a COVID-19 nAb cutoff point to guide operationalising a test-and-boost strategy.

Strength and limitations

The study population was mostly female (88.8%), potentially limiting the generalizability to populations with a more equal gender distribution. The study population was consistent with the literature that women comprise up to 90% of healthcare staff [32].

Additionally, as study participants were HCWs who volunteered, the results may be biased towards a higher acceptability of the ‘test-and-boost’ approach. While this may limit the generalisability of our findings to the broader population, the findings remain relevant to HCWs in primary healthcare settings.

Although the POC CoVIm™ test can measure nAb percentage inhibition, which can translate to nAb levels, it is currently designed as a qualitative test focused on nAb detection. Future iterations of the test, focused on quantifying nAb titres, are required to establish an appropriate threshold to inform a test-and-boost strategy.

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first study focusing on the acceptability of a “test-and-boost” approach among staff of a primary healthcare institution. The findings provide valuable insight into the decision-making mindset of primary healthcare workers (HWCs), who are on the frontlines of providing preventive health service to the general population. These HCWs play a pivotal role in influencing COVID-19 vaccination uptake among the public. Their personal rationale and willingness to be vaccinated are often conveyed through their interaction with the many patients and families they serve in the primary care setting. Ultimately, the success of the national COVID-19 response relies on achieving widespread vaccination across the population.”

Conclusion

The study revealed a high seroprevalence of COVID-19 nAb among the fully vaccinated SingHealth Polyclinics staff. However, despite being fully vaccinated, over half of the staff contracted COVID-19 within six months of vaccination highlighting the necessity for timely boosters. Point-of-care testing, which most staff are agreeable to, has the potential to aid informed booster decisions and safeguard staff health. Healthcare policies informed by these insights hold the potential to facilitate appropriate national vaccination recommendations, balancing autonomy, clinical necessity and optimal utilization of healthcare resources.
