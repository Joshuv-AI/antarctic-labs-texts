Evolution of oxidation markers and rancid off-flavors in Tenebrio molitor mealworm oil during thermal oxidation
Authors: Ji Yoon Choi, Min Kyung Park, In Hee Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to elucidate the thermal oxidation process of mealworm oil, thereby providing insights into the potential development of oxidation or rancidity during thermal treatment. The oxidative status of Tenebrio molitor (mealworm) oil was assessed via oxidative indices, which tended to increase with accelerated thermal oxidation. Acid and peroxide values exhibited a rapid increase within the initial 30 min across all heating temperatures, whereas the p-anisidine value showed a gradual increase in the early stages of heating, followed by a sharp rise after 4 h of heating at 180 °C. Furthermore, differences among oils heated for 8 h were visualized using a partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) models, based on volatile oxidation compounds that changed during the heating oxidation process. Low-molecular-weight aldehydes, the thermal oxidation products of oleic, linoleic, and α-linolenic acids, played a key role in this distribution, and the patterns became more pronounced at higher heating temperatures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10068-025-01999-5.

Introduction

The escalating global population has placed a significant burden on global food systems. To address this challenge, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) has advocated for edible insects as a sustainable and nutritious food source (van Huis et al., 2013). The increasing recognition of edible insects as a valuable food source has led to their application in diverse food products. Among various edible insects, Tenebrio molitor larvae, commonly known as mealworms, have been increasingly popular in Korea. Since the enactment of the Act on the Fostering and Support of the Insect Industry in 2010, mealworms have been extensively utilized in a range of food applications (e.g., baked goods, processed foods, and traditional Korean foods) (Seo and Cho, 2018; Draszanowska et al., 2024; Shin, 2019). Recent investigations have explored the potential of mealworms as protein precursors for Maillard reaction products, which form when carbonyl groups in carbohydrates react with amino groups in amino acids, peptides, or proteins under thermal treatment, ultimately leading to the optimization of reaction flavors derived from mealworms (Seo et al., 2020; Park et al., 2022, 2023).

Mealworms also constitute a significant dietary source of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), including omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, with levels comparable to those in fish and exceeding those of beef and pork (Van Huis et al., 2013). The lipid fraction of mealworms, rich in these PUFAs, hold potential as a novel edible oil source. However, while PUFA-rich oils offer notable physiological benefits (Sales-Campos et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2015), their susceptibility to oxidative rancidity is heightened under conditions of prolonged storage, heat exposure, or cooking (Martin-Polvillo et al., 2004). Oxidation, a complex free radical chain reaction involving fatty acids and molecular oxygen, initiates with the formation of hydroperoxides (primary oxidation products). These hydroperoxides subsequently decompose into a diverse array of secondary volatile oxidation products, including aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and hydrocarbons (Frankel, 1991; Choe and Min, 2006), which contribute to the development of off-flavors associated with oxidized lipids, negatively impacting their sensory and nutritional attributes (Choe and Min, 2006). Notably, the decomposition further yields additional free radicals, thereby propagating and accelerating the oxidative process and generating specific undesirable volatile oxidation products such as short-chain aldehydes and ketones, often characterized by fishy, painty, and rancid odors (Jacobsen, 2010). The profile of these specific undesirable volatile oxidation products can vary depending on the UFAs involved in the oxidation process. For instance, oleic acid (C18:1) primarily yields aldehydes such as octanal, nonanal, 2-decenal, decanal, and 2-undecenal; the oxidation of linoleic acid (C18:2) results in the formation of hexanal, 2,4-nonadienal, 2,4-decadienal, 2-pentylfuran, 2-octenal, 2-heptenal, and 1-octen-3-ol; and α-linolenic acid (C18:3) generates volatile compounds such as 2,4-heptadienal, 3-hexenal, and 2-pentenal (Frankel et al., 1981).

The significant concern regarding off-flavor development and oxidative deterioration (rancidity) in mealworm-derived oils, attributed to their high lipid and PUFA content, necessitates a comprehensive quality assessment of the entire oxidation process. Investigating the complete oxidation pathway of mealworm-derived oils is crucial to derive valuable insights into their potential as a novel and sustainable lipid source for diverse culinary applications. Given that oil is crucial in thermal treatments, such as stir-frying and frying, which typically involve temperatures ranging from 100 to 200 °C, this study aimed to evaluate the oxidative stability of mealworm oil under various thermal conditions (heating temperature and duration) by monitoring the formation of primary, secondary, and volatile oxidation products.

Materials and methods

Material and reagents

Mealworm oil was purchased from BDMI (Jangsu-si, Jeollabuk-do, Korea). Ethyl ether, ethyl alcohol, sodium thiosulfate, chloroform, acetic acid, potassium hydroxide, potassium iodine, phenolphthalein, and starch were obtained from Daejung (Siheung-si, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Methanol, 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (iso-octane), p-anisidine, and dichloromethane were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Sodium chloride solution, 14% boron trifluoride-methanol solution (BF3), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), undecanoic acid, cis-11-vaccenic acid methyl ester, all-cis-7,10,13,16,19-docosapentaenoic acid methyl ester, a standard mixture of 37 mixed fatty acid methylated esters (FAMEs), n-alkanes mixtures (C8–C22), and benzyl acetate were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Thermal treatment of mealworm oil

Mealworm oil (200 mL) was heated in a brown Pyrex beaker at 120 ± 5 ℃, 140 ± 5 ℃, 160 ± 5 ℃, and 180 ± 5 ℃ for 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h, respectively.

The acid value of mealworm oil

The acid value was determined according to the American Oil Chemists Society (AOCS) method Cd 3d-63 (AOCS, 2017). Each oil sample (5 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of an ethyl ether: ethyl alcohol solution (2:1, v/v). Subsequently, a 1% phenolphthalein indicator solution in ethyl alcohol was added as an indicator. The samples were then titrated with 0.1 N potassium hydroxide solution until the appearance of a pink coloration that persisted for 30 s. p-anisidine value of mealworm oil

The p-anisidine value was determined according to the AOCS method Cd 18–90 (AOCS, 2017). Oil samples (0.5 g) were dissolved with 25 mL of iso-octane and absorbance (Ab) of resulting solution was measured at 350 nm using a spectrophotometer (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Subsequently, 2.5 mL of sample solution and 1 mL of 0.25% p-anisidine solution (0.25 g/100 mL, dissolved in acetic acid) were mixed and allowed to react for 10 min. Analysis of fatty acid composition in mealworm oil

The fatty acid composition was analyzed according to the Association of Official Analysis Chemists (AOAC) method 969.33 (AOAC, 1990). Oil samples (25 mg) were combined with 1 mL of undecanoic acid (1 mg/mL, dissolved in iso-octane) as an internal standard, followed by the addition of 1 mL of a 0.5 N sodium hydroxide solution (dissolved in methanol). The resulting mixture was then evaporated under a gentle stream of nitrogen and subsequently heated at 100 ℃ using a heating block. Methylation was performed by adding 2 mL of 14% BF3 and heating at 100 ℃ (in a heating block) for 30 min. Upon completion of the methylation, 1 mL of iso-octane was added, immediately followed by 5 mL of a sodium chloride solution. The organic extract was then dehydrated over sodium sulfate anhydrous and prepared for analysis. The FAMEs were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatography (GC) (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and an SP™-2560 capillary column (100 m length × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.2 μm film thickness, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The oven temperature program was as follows: held at 100 ℃ for 5 min, increased to 230 ℃ at a rate of 4 ℃/min, and then held at 240 ℃ for 10 min. The injector and FID temperatures were 225 ℃ and 285 ℃, respectively. A 1 μL aliquot of the extract was injected with a split ratio of 50:1. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The FAMEs present in the samples were identified by comparing their retention times and elution orders to those of a 37-component mixed FAMEs standards (20 mg/mL, dissolved in iso-octane), cis-11-vaccenic acid methyl ester (1 mg/mL, dissolved in heptane), and all-cis-7,10,13,16,19-docosapentaenoic acid methyl ester (1 mg/mL, dissolved in heptane). The quantification of each fatty acid was performed by comparing its peak area to that of the internal standard, undecanoic acid, on the GC chromatogram.

Analysis of volatile oxidation compounds in mealworm oil

The oil samples (5 mL) were placed in 20 mL headspace screw-top vial, with internal standard (0.1 mL of 1000 ppm dissolved in distilled water, v/v) added to each sample before extraction. The samples were allowed to equilibrate for 15 min in a water bath maintained at 50℃ under agitation at 200 rpm. The 65 μm polydimethylsiloxane/divinylbenzene (PDMS/DVB) fiber (Supelco Inc.) was inserted into the headspace of the sample and adsorption was performed for 20 min. After adsorption, the fiber was desorbed in the gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) injection port (configured in split mode with a ratio of 5:1) at 220 ℃ for 5 min. Volatile compounds were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B GC coupled with a 5977B MS (Agilent Technologies Co.) equipped with an DB-5MS (30 m length × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness, J & W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). The carrier gas was helium at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was held at 40 ℃ for 6 min, then raised 5 ℃/min to 250 ℃, and held at 250 ℃ for 5 min. The temperatures of injector and detector were 220 ℃ and 250 ℃, respectively. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization mode with an ionization energy of 70 eV and a scan range of 35–500 m/z. Volatile compounds in mealworm oils were identified by comparing their mass spectra and retention index (RI) with those of the NIST 17 (version 2.2) and Wiley 7.0 databases, or by manual interpretation and confirmed with previous literature (Babushok et al., 2011; Yan et al., 2015). RIs of volatile compounds in mealworm oils were calculated using n-alkanes (C8–C22) as external references. The quantification of volatile compounds in mealworm oils was performed by comparing their peak areas to that of the internal standard on the GC–MS total ion chromatogram.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate. Significant differences of acid value, peroxide value, p-anisidine value, fatty acid composition, and volatile compound profiles were evaluated using SPSS software (ver. 29.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and differences were indicated by performing Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05). Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) and partial-least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were conducted using SIMCA-P software (ver. 11.0; Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) to elucidate the differences and/or changes in secondary volatile oxidation products of mealworm oil subjected to different heating temperatures over an 8-h duration.

Results and discussion

Changes in acid value of mealworm oil

Figure 1A illustrates that the changes in the acid value (mg KOH/g), a key indicator of free fatty acid content and thus oil quality, in mealworm oil subjected to varying different heating temperatures (120 °C, 140 °C, 160 °C, and 180 °C) and durations (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h). The initial acid value of unheated mealworm oil was 0.49 mg KOH/g, which was notably lower than the reported initial values for other vegetables such as cold-pressed olive oil (0.84 mg KOH/g), perilla oil (1.96 mg KOH/g), and grapeseed oil (5.05 mg KOH/g) (Moon et al., 2005; Kim et al., 2019). In this study, a general trend of increasing acid value in mealworm oil was observed with both elevated heating temperatures and prolonged durations. This increase was pronounced and rapid at higher temperatures (160 °C and 180 °C), especially within the first 2 h of heating. While the acid value of mealworm oil heated at 120 °C reached 0.60 mg KOH/g after 2 h and 0.67 mg KOH/g after 8 h, the acid value of mealworm oil heated at 180 °C significantly increased to 0.87 mg KOH/g after 2 h and further rose to 1.01 mg KOH/g after 8 h. Changes in peroxide value of mealworm oil

The peroxide value, a measure of primary lipid oxidation products (hydroperoxides), was determined in mealworm oil subjected to different temperatures and durations, as shown in Fig. 1B. The peroxide value consistently increased during heating across all tested temperatures. A rapid increase was evident within the initial 30 min across all temperature conditions, followed by a more gradual increase up to 4 h, after which a slightly steeper rise was again observed. This overall pattern was consistent with findings from previous studies on oil oxidation (Solak et al., 2018; Multari et al., 2019). This suggests that thermal treatment significantly accelerates the initial lipid oxidation in mealworm oil, leading to a rapid accumulation of hydroperoxides, particularly during the early stages of heating. Notably, the changes in peroxide value exhibited a more similar pattern across the three higher heating temperatures (140 °C, 160 °C, and 180 °C). The increase in peroxide value during the initial 30 min for these temperatures ranged from 12.93 to 13.28 meq/kg, showing no statistically significant difference. After 8-h of heating, the peroxide values of mealworm oil reached 19.91 meq/kg at 120 °C, 25.51 meq/kg at 140 °C, 26.51 meq/kg at 160 °C, and 28.49 meq/kg at 180 °C, respectively. However, the maximum peroxide values measured during the thermal oxidation of mealworm oil were approximately half the limit of 50 meq/kg established by the Codex Alimentarius Commission.

Changes in ρ-anisidine value of mealworm oil

The ρ-anisidine value measures the content of 2-alkenals, which are secondary oxidation products formed from the β-scission of hydroperoxides (Oh et al., 2023). Figure 1C demonstrates a continuous increase in the ρ-anisidine value in mealworm oil samples heated for 8 h. This secondary oxidation rate also exhibited a positive correlation with heating temperature, as evidenced by the marked elevation in the ρ-anisidine value in samples heated at higher temperatures as heating progressed. In particular, a steep increase in the ρ-anisidine value was clearly evident after 4 h of heating at 180 °C. This suggests that while primary hydroperoxides formed during the initial stages of heating, their subsequent degradation into secondary oxidation products became considerably more significant around the 4-h mark, especially at higher temperatures. The ρ-anisidine values of mealworm oil heated for 8 h increased to 9.53 at 120 °C and reached 15.79 at 140 °C. A more substantial increase was observed at 160 °C, culminating in a value of 31.18. The most pronounced rise was evident at 180 °C, where it peaked at 59.72. This result indicates that prolonged thermal exposure, particularly at elevated temperatures, significantly promotes the secondary oxidation of lipids in mealworm oil, leading to a substantial accumulation of 2-alkenals, as reflected by the increasing ρ-anisidine values.

The predominant fatty acid profile of unheated mealworm oil consisted of oleic acid [C18:1(n-9)], linoleic acid (C18:2), palmitic acid (C16:0), myristic acid (C14:0), stearic acid (C18:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3), consistent with previous reports (Jeon et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2022). Notably, oleic acid and linoleic acid were the most abundant, accounting for over 65% of the total fatty acids. These UFAs are recognized for their beneficial physiological activities in human. Specifically, oleic acid, a major MUFA of mealworm oil, has demonstrated positive effects on cancer, autoimmune disorders, and inflammatory diseases (Sales-Campos et al., 2013). Similarly, linoleic acid, a PUFA, has been associated with the reduction of carcinogenesis, atherosclerosis, inflammation, obesity, and diabetes, as well as promoting growth and bone formation (Yang et al., 2015), highlighting the potential health benefits of mealworm oil. However, exposing edible oils rich in these UFA to high heating temperatures leads to a significant alteration in their fatty acid composition over time (Multari et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020). During the heating oxidation of mealworm oil, the proportion of SFAs, such as palmitic acid, and the content of MUFAs, represented by oleic acid, generally tended to increase. However, this trend reversed after 8 h of heating at 120 °C and 140 °C, and after 4 h of heating at 160 °C and 180 °C, showing a subsequent decrease. Conversely, the PUFA content of mealworm oil, primarily represented by linoleic acid, generally decreased across all heating temperatures. This observation of increasing oleic acid content concurrent with the degradation of linoleic and α-linolenic acids in heated mealworm oil aligns with findings from previous studies investigating the oxidation of various edible oils (Multari et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020). Furthermore, the differential changes in MUFA and PUFA content within the UFAs of mealworm oil became more pronounced with increasing heating temperature and duration (Wang et al., 2019).

Moreover, the ratio of palmitic acid to linoleic acid (P/L), calculated as an indicator of the oxidation stability of mealworm oil (Nosratpour et al., 2017; Kim and Choe, 2008), is also presented in Table 1. Typically, palmitic acid exhibits high resistance to heat and oxidation, whereas linoleic acid is highly susceptible to oxidation, particularly at elevated temperatures. Upon heating the oil, linoleic acid undergoes rapid degradation due to its multiple double bonds, while palmitic acid remains comparatively stable under the same conditions. Consequently, the P/L ratio tends to increase over time with continued heating. However, in the present study, while the P/L ratio of mealworm oil showed a slight increase with increasing heating temperature, statistically significant linear increase was not observed as heating progressed.

Changes in volatile oxidation compounds of mealworm oil

A total of 70 volatile compounds were identified in mealworm oil subjected to different heating temperatures over an 8-h duration (Table 2). There compounds included 27 aldehydes, 10 alcohols, 9 ketones, 2 furans, 17 aliphatic hydrocarbons, and 5 nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds. In the analysis of unheated mealworm oil, quantitative assessment revealed the presence of 8 aldehydes, 4 alcohols, 3 ketones, 8 aliphatic hydrocarbons, and 2 pyrazines, with hexanal being the most predominant compound. While unheated edible oils are typically characterized by odorless quality, short-chain aldehydes, alcohols, hydrocarbons, ketones, and acids, have been found in various edible oils (Oh et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019). Although these compounds, at low concentrations in unheated edible oils, may not contribute significantly to the characteristic odor property, their increasing concentration during the progression of Effects of heating on volatile compound formation

As the heating of mealworm oil progressed, consistent detection of increased amounts of low molecular weight aldehydes and alcohols, including hexanal (no. 7), heptanal (no. 10), nonanal (no. 19), pentan-1-ol (no. 30), oct-1-en-3-ol (no. 33), was observed. Furthermore, upon heating the mealworm oil, 18 aldehydes (butanal (no. 2), (E)-pent-2-enal (no. 6), (E)-hex-2-enal (no. 8), (Z)-hept-4-enal (no. 9), (Z)-hept-2-enal (no. 11), (E)-hept-2-enal (no. 12), octanal (no. 14), (2E,4E)-hepta-2,4-dienal (no. 15), (E)-oct-2-enal (no. 17), (E)-non-4-enal (no. 18), (E)-non-2-enal (no. 20), (E)-dec-4-enal (no. 21), decanal (no. 22), (2E,4E)-nona-2,4-dienal (no. 23), (E)-dec-2-enal (no. 24), undecanal (no. 25), (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26), and (E)-undec-2-enal (no. 27)), 6 alcohols ((E)-but-2-en-1-ol (no. 28), pent-1-en-3-ol (no. 29), hexan-1-ol (no. 31), heptan-1-ol (no. 32), (E)-oct-2-en-1-ol (no. 36), and octan-1-ol (no. 37)), 6 ketones (oct-1-en-3-one (no. 40), 2-methyloctan-3-one (no. 41), nanan-2-one (no. 43), (E)-non-3-en-2-one (no. 44), decan-2-one (no. 45), and 5-butyloxolan-2-one (no. 46)), 2 furans (2-pentylfuran (no. 47) and 2-butyltetrahydrofuran (no. 48)), 9 aliphatic hydrocarbons (oct-1-ene (no. 50), pentane (no. 51), (Z)-oct-2-ene (no. 52), (E)-oct-2-ene (no. 53), nonane (no. 57), 2,2,7,7-tetramethyloctane (no. 60), butylbenzene (no. 61), pentylbenzene (no. 63), and undecane (no. 62), and 3 nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds (pyridine (no. 66), 2-ethlypridine (no. 67), and 2-pentylpyridine (no. 70)) were newly generated. Among them, (2E,4E)-nona-2,4-dienal (no. 23), undecanal (no. 25), hexan-1-ol (no. 31), and octan-1-ol (no. 37) were exclusively detected only after heating at temperatures of 160 °C or higher for 2 h or more. Notably, (E)-hept-2-enal (no. 12), which was absent in unheated mealworm oil, became the most quantitatively dominant compound when heated at 120 °C for over 8 h, 140 °C for over 4 h, 160 °C for 2 h, and 180 °C for over 30 min, respectively. Following (E)-hept-2-enal (no. 12), (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26), nonanal (no. 19), hexanal (no. 7), 2-pentylfuran (no. 47), and (E)-dec-2-enal (no. 24) were quantitatively significant volatile oxidation compounds in the heated mealworm oils.

The role of fatty acid composition in volatile compound formation

The formation of these compounds is mainly influenced by the oil’s fatty acid content and composition (Frankel et al., 1981; Zhuang et al., 2022). Heat exposure significantly increases lipid oxidation products, especially in oils with a high content of thermally unstable unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs). In particular, PUFAs such as linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid are susceptible to rapid degradation during thermal oxidation, leading to the formation of a large number of volatile aldehydes like hexanal, 2,4-decadienal, propanal, and nonanal.

In previous study on sunflower oil, which has a very high content of the omega-6 fatty acid linoleic acid (approximately 65–75%), showed a pronounced reduction in linoleic and α-linolenic acid content after repeated deep-frying for over 90 min. This was accompanied by a several-fold increase in volatile aldehydes like hexanal and 2,4-decadienal. In contrast, canola oil, which is rich in the omega-9 monounsaturated fatty acid oleic acid (60–65%), exhibited comparatively smaller increases in aldehydic compounds. The loss of PUFAs and the formation of aldehydes are far less significant in canola oil than in oils rich in PUFAs (Moumtaz et al., 2019).

In addition, fatty acid composition plays a crucial role in diversifying the volatile oxidation compounds generated during the thermal oxidation of mealworm oil. It is believed that the major unsaturated fatty acids in mealworm oil (e.g., oleic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid) (Table 1) contributed to the following results: oleic acid decomposition yields 8-, 9-, 10-, and 11-hydroperoxides, which subsequently generate octanal (no. 14), nonanal (no. 19), decanal (no. 22), and (E)-undec-2-enal (no. 27) in heated mealworm oil. Thermal decomposition of hydroperoxides derived from oxidized methyl linoleate produces volatiles expected from the cis-9-, 10-, 12-, and 13-hydroperoxides, such as hexanal (no. 7), (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26), (2E,4E)-nona-2,4-dienal (no. 23), 2-pentylfuran (no. 47), (E)-oct-2-enal (no. 17), (E)-hept-2-enal (no. 12), (E)-dec-2-enal (no. 24), and 1-octen-3-ol (no. 33). Similarly, the decomposition of 9-, 12-, and 13-, 16-hydroperoxides of α-linolenic acid generate (2E, 4E)-hepta-2,4-dienal (no. 15), (E)-pent-2-enal (no. 6), and (E)-hex-2-enal (no. 8) (Frankel et al., 1981).

Overall, as the thermal oxidation of mealworm oil progressed, consistent with the decrease in PUFA content and increase in SFA proportion (Table 1), (E)-hept-2-enal (no. 12), (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26), hexanal (no. 7), and 2-pentylfuran (no. 47), which can be generated during the oxidation of linoleic acid (the predominant PUFA in mealworm oil), became the quantitatively dominant volatile oxidation compounds. Moreover, (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (described as deep-fried odor note), octanal (described as citrus-like and green odor notes), nonanal (described as citrus-like and soapy odor notes), and hexanal (described as grass, green, and fruity odor notes) were not only found to exhibiting high FD factors in rancid oils but also demonstrated a further increase in the FD factors as oxidation progressed (Xu et al., 2021), thus identifying as key contributors to rancid off-flavors in rancid oils.

Analysis of volatile compounds using PLS-DA

To investigate the changes in secondary volatile lipid oxidation products induced by the thermal oxidation of mealworm oil, both PCA and PLS-DA were applied to the volatile compound data in our study. PCA and PLS-DA are widely used multivariate techniques that differ in purpose and methodology. PCA is an unsupervised approach that reduces dimensionality by maximizing variance, enabling exploration of data structure and detection of outliers without reference to class labels (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016). By contrast, PLS-DA is a supervised method that incorporates class membership to maximize group discrimination, making it particularly effective for classification tasks and biomarker discovery (Scott and Crone, 2021). PCA is variance-driven and exploratory, whereas PLS-DA is covariance-driven and hypothesis-oriented. In our study, PCA was first performed to explore the data structure and remove potential outliers, based on the compounds generated or altered during the oxidative process of mealworm oil at different heating temperatures (S-Fig. 1). The results showed that, across all heating conditions, mealworm oil samples shifted along the PC1 axis from the positive to the negative direction with increasing temperature. To further identify the variation patterns of volatile compounds that predominantly contributed to these temperature-dependent changes in mealworm oil, PLS-DA was also conducted. Mealworm oils subjected to different temperatures over an 8 h-duration were distributed in PLS-DA models calculated using cross-validation based on the 70 volatile compounds in Table 2 (Fig. 2). PLS-DA is an efficient statistical technique to clarify the separation between samples, build their classification models, and elucidate how underlying variables influence this separation. The distribution of mealworm oil samples in this study was primarily determined by the first t[1] and second components t[2] of the PLS-DA plot. The PLS-DA models demonstrated a high degree of explained variance (R2Y = 0.993–0.998) and cross-validated predictive capability (Q2Y = 0.993–0.997) across all tested heating temperatures (120–180 °C), with corresponding R2X values ranging from 0.991 to 0.997.Fig. 2PLS-DA models of volatile oxidation compounds generated from mealworm oil subjected to varying heating temperatures over an 8-h duration: A 120 °C, B 140 °C, C 160 °C, and D 180 °C

Trajectory of samples in the PLS-DA score plot

Intriguingly, across all heating temperatures, the trajectory of mealworm oil samples in the PLS-DA score plot exhibited a sequential distribution along the direction of the arrows as heating progressed, with this shift occurring more rapidly at higher heating temperatures. The initial differentiation was observed along the t[1] axis, demarcating oil samples heated up to 2 h at 120 °C, up to 1 h at 140 °C and 160 °C, and those heated more than these durations, with 30 min serving as the demarcation point at 180 °C. This evolution further manifested as a separation along the t[2] axis. Specifically, at 120 °C, the early heating phase was subdivided into oil samples heated for up to 30 min and those heated from 30 min to 2 h, while the late phase distinguished between oil samples heated for 4 h and those heated for 8 h. For oil samples heated at 140 °C and 160 °C, the early heating phase consistently separated unheated oil samples from those heated for up to 30 min and up to 1 h, respectively. Furthermore, the late heating phase differentiated oil samples heated for 2 h and those heated for 4 h or more at 140 °C, and oil samples heated for up to 2 h and those heated for longer than this duration at 160 °C. Finally, at 180 °C, the early heating phase was characterized by the distinction between unheated oil samples and those heated for 30 min, and the late phase separated oil samples heated for up to 1 h from those heated for longer than this duration.

Key contributors to rancid off-flavors

The newly generated or increasingly concentrated volatile oxidation compounds (excluding aliphatic hydrocarbons) are highlighted in Fig. 2. These volatile oxidation compounds were observed to associate more rapidly with the mealworm oil samples as the thermal processing temperature increased. Furthermore, considering the elevated levels of secondary oxidation products, indicated by p-anisidine values, in mealworm oil heated over 160 °C for more than 4 h, the overlapping compounds within the two boxed regions of Fig. 2 [e.g., butanal (no. 2), (2E,4E)-hepta-2,4-dienal (no. 15), (E)-non-4-enal (no. 18), (E)-non-2-enal (no. 20), (E)-dec-4-enal (no. 21), (2E,4E)-nona-2,4-dienal (no. 23), (E)-dec-2-enal (no. 24), undecanal (no. 25), (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26), (E)-undec-2-enal (no. 27), hexan-1-ol (no. 31), 5-butyloxolan-2-one (no. 46), and 2-pentylfuran (no. 47)] could be key secondary volatile oxidation products responsible for the development of rancid off-flavors in the mealworm oil. These secondary volatile oxidation products primarily originate from the fatty acid oxidation of oleic and linoleic acids (Frankel et al., 1981). Notably, among these, (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal (no. 26) has been previously identified in previous studies as a major contributor to rancid off-flavors in fats and oils (Xu et al., 2021; Neugebauer et al., 2020); consequently, it was anticipated to be the most significant contributor to the development of undesirable rancid off-flavors in mealworm oil, particularly under conditions of rapid heating at elevated temperatures.

In conclusion, while mealworm oil exhibits relatively favorable initial oxidative stability, prolonged exposure to high temperatures leads to an increase in acid, peroxide, and p-anisidine values due to the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. The subsequent accumulation of secondary oxidation products, particularly low molecular weight aldehydes, elevates the susceptibility of mealworm oil to the development of rancid off-flavors. Specifically, the PLS-DA analysis distinctly differentiated the volatile oxidation compound profiles of mealworm oil based on heating temperature and duration, revealing an accelerated rate of change at higher temperatures. Volatile oxidation compounds, such as butanal, (2E,4E)-hepta-2,4-dienal, (E)-non-4-enal, (E)-non-2-enal, (E)-dec-4-enal, (2E,4E)-nona-2,4-dienal, (E)-dec-2-enal, undecanal, (2E,4E)-deca-2,4-dienal, (E)-undec-2-enal, hexan-1-ol, 5-butyloxolan-2-one, and 2-pentylfuran, which progressively increased across all heating temperatures, thus potentially contributing significantly to the rancid off-flavors resulting from the thermos-oxidative degradation of mealworm oil. The results clearly indicate that the impact of thermal treatment on mealworm oil is highly temperature-dependent: at lower heating temperatures (e.g., 120–140 °C), the progression of fatty acid degradation and volatile oxidation compound formation occurred more gradually, whereas at higher temperatures (160–180 °C), rapid and extensive transformations were observed within a much shorter timeframe. This suggests that temperature plays a more decisive role than duration in accelerating lipid oxidation and flavor deterioration in mealworm oil. Consequently, to preserve the flavor and quality of mealworm oil, it is crucial to avoid prolonged heating at high temperatures and to establish appropriate processing and storage conditions that consider both temperature and duration.
