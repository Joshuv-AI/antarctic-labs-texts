Dynamic behavior of fractured gabbro treated by high temperatures
Authors: Xianhuan Liu, Hailiang Jia, Chao Wang, Li Han, Qiang Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rock masses in deep engineering are often subjected to high geothermal effect, and blasting is a key technique for deep rock excavation. It is crucial to study the dynamic characteristics of fractured rock masses under high geothermal conditions and blasting excavation. This paper focuses on fractured gabbro, examining the effects of high temperatures on its surface chromaticity and mass. Dynamic splitting tests were conducted using a Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) apparatus, and the microstructure was analyzed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results indicate that: (1) High temperatures significantly affect the surface chromaticity and mass of gabbro samples. (2) Elevated temperatures lead to the progression from a microstructure with no noticeable microcracks to the development of extensive microcracks and mineral particle fragmentation. (3) The impacts of high temperatures on the splitting strength, peak strain, and dynamic elastic modulus of gabbro samples reach a critical point around 500 °C. At this temperature, the strengthening effect of high temperature is evident, whereas at 800 °C, these properties significantly decrease due to extensive mineral crystal fragmentation and microcrack expansion, weakening the sample's strength, stiffness, and deformation resistance. (4) From 25 °C to 600 °C, the fissure angle significantly influences the splitting strength, peak strain, and dynamic elastic modulus of gabbro. Although the fissure angle still impacts these properties at 800 °C, the overall effect of high temperature is more pronounced. (5) The failure of fractured gabbro samples is primarily characterized by tensile and shear failures, with the crack initiation angle closely related to the fissure angle.

Introduction

Rock mass in deep engineering, such as ultra-deep mining, deep disposal of high-level radioactive nuclear wastes, geothermal resource exploitation, and underground coal gasification, always suffers from high geothermal effect [1,2]. The influence of high geotherm on rock mechanical characteristics has become a research hotspot in rock mechanics [3,4]. Fractured rock masses are common in these deep rock engineering projects, and blasting is a key technique for excavation. Thus, deep rock engineering often involves blasting fractured rock masses under high geothermal conditions. The combination of high geotherm and dynamic stress increases the likelihood of engineering disasters [5,6]. Studying the dynamic characteristics of fractured rocks under these conditions is crucial for analyzing the stability of deep surrounding rocks during blasting. This research provides valuable insights for ensuring stability in such environments.

Multiple researches have demonstrated that high temperatures significantly affect the physical and mechanical properties of rocks [[7], [8], [9]]. By using MTS815 servo-controlled rock mechanics testing system, Chen et al. conducted Brazilian splitting tests for sandstone specimens, which were heated to six kinds of temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 1000 °C and then cooled down to room temperature naturally, to investigate the damage evolution caused by high temperature [7]. Liu et al. used a hydraulic servo pressure testing machine to study the physical and mechanical properties of granite and sandstone after high-temperature treatment. They found that temperature had a more significant effect on the mechanical properties of sandstone compared to granite, with 600 °C and 800 °C being the critical temperatures for the brittle-plastic transition of sandstone and granite, respectively [10]. Additionally, researchers have used techniques such as CT, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), ultrasound, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to analyze the impact of high temperatures on the mineral structure of rocks. Studies have found that high temperatures lead to the development of primary fractures and the formation of new microcracks within the rock [11,12]. As the temperature increases, changes occur in the fractures and thermally unstable minerals within the rock [13], resulting in non-uniform deformation and stress, which can induce thermal cracking and subsequently affect the physical and mechanical properties of the rock [14].

The deformation and failure of fractured rock masses under dynamic loads, such as blasting, exhibit significant strain rate effects [15,16]. The splitting test based on the Hopkinson bar apparatus is a method for determining the dynamic characteristics of fractured rocks under impact loads. Researchers have highlighted that fracture [17,18] and hole angles [19] have a substantial influence on the dynamic mechanical properties and fracture behavior of rocks. Additionally, the dynamic characteristics of rocks are closely related to their internal structure [20]. Other researchers have used high-speed cameras and digital image correlation techniques to preliminarily analyze the dynamic splitting and surface strain field evolution [21]. The presence of fractures significantly influences the location and shape of stress concentration areas in rock masses, thereby altering their mechanical properties and failure modes [22]. Additionally, the complexity of the internal structure of rock masses, such as fracture morphology [23] and fracture scale [24], also has a significant impact on their mechanical behavior under impact loads.

Currently, SHPB test with high temperature has been attempted [25,26]. Using the SHPB test system, researchers have conducted dynamic tensile tests on granite [26] and marble [27] subjected to different high-temperature treatments, and have determined the dynamic fracture toughness of these rocks. However, gabbro, a common and important intrusive rock in deep rock engineering, has mineral compositions and structural characteristics significantly different from granite and marble. Current research on gabbro under high temperature conditions mainly focuses on fracture toughness [28,29], while studies on the dynamic splitting characteristics of fractured gabbro under high temperatures are relatively scarce.

In this paper, dynamic splitting tests were conducted on fractured gabbro samples using a φ50 mm SHPB apparatus and a high-temperature chamber under five temperature conditions (25 °C, 300 °C, 500 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C). The tests involved seven different pre-cut fissure angles. The study systematically investigated the chromatic characteristics, dynamic splitting properties, crack initiation and propagation, and failure modes of gabbro with different fissure angles treated by high temperature conditions. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to analyze the thermal damage process of gabbro's microstructure, elucidating the impact of high-temperature-induced microstructural changes on the dynamic parameters of fractured gabbro.

Material and method

Sample preparation

The samples used in this test are gabbro, commonly found in deep rock engineering. To reduce data variability, all samples were taken from a single, large, intact block of gabbro. The gabbro was processed into cylindrical shapes with dimensions of Φ50 × 25 mm. Each sample was pre-fabricated with a central fissure measuring 30 mm in length and 2 mm in width, with fissure angles set at 0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75°, and 90°. The ends of the samples were polished smooth using an SHM–200 double-sided grinding machine to ensure surface unevenness was less than 0.02 mm, meeting the test requirements. The processed samples were then dried in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h and stored in a desiccator for future use. A total of 110 gabbro samples were used in this test.

Experimental description

High temperature treatment

The high temperature treatment was performed using a PAN-GLOBE P909X intelligent high-temperature box furnace. The samples were subjected to six temperature gradients: 25 °C, 300 °C, 500 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. To ensure uniform heating, the heating rate was set at 5 °C/min. Once the target temperature was reached, it was maintained for 3 h, and then the samples were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature in the furnace. After treatment, a colorimeter was used to measure the chromaticity of the gabbro samples at different temperatures. Additionally, the SEM was used to observe the microstructural characteristics of the samples after high temperature treatment, assessing the changes in chromaticity and microstructure of the gabbro.

Dynamic splitting test

The Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) was first designed by Hopkinson in 1914 [30]. It is now a common technique for studying the dynamic properties of rocks at high strain rates. The basic principle of the SHPB test is based on the propagation theory of elastic stress waves in elastic rods [31], relying on two fundamental assumptions: 1) the one-dimensional stress wave assumption in the rod [32], and 2) the uniformity assumption [33]. The dynamic mechanical parameters of the specimens are indirectly calculated from the time-voltage plots on an oscilloscope using the following Eq. (1):(1)dεdt=−2CLSεr(t)ε=−2CLS∫0tεr(t)dtσ=AASEεt(t)where σ is stress (MPa), dε/dt is strain rate (s⁻1), ε is strain, C is the stress wave propagation speed in the pressure bar (m/s), LS is the length of the specimen (mm), εr and εt(t) are the reflected and transmitted strain signals, A is the cross-sectional area of the pressure bar (mm2), AS is the cross-sectional area of the specimen (mm2), and E is the elastic modulus of the bar (GPa).

The SHPB test setup includes the following components: impact bar (bullet), incident bar, transmission bar, absorption bar, damper, and data acquisition system (Fig. 1). The components have a diameter of 50 mm, with the incident bar measuring 3700 mm in length, the transmission bar 2500 mm, and the bullet 300 mm. The equipment has an elastic modulus of 210 GPa, a Poisson's ratio of 0.25, and a density of 7800 kg/m³. During the test, the heat-treated sample is placed in the test box of the SHPB setup, ensuring that both sides of the sample are in tight contact with the loading bars. Vaseline is evenly applied to both ends of the sample to reduce friction effects. During loading, the working gas pressure is first set to a fixed value of about 0.1 MPa. Then, high-pressure nitrogen gas propels the bullet, which strikes the incident bar, generating an incident wave. This incident wave propagates along the bars and applies a load to the sample. Strain gauges on the incident and transmission bars record the incident, reflected, and transmitted waves during loading. This allows for the determination of the dynamic parameters of the fractured gabbro samples after high-temperature treatment using the SHPB test.Fig. 1Schematic of SHPB test system.Fig. 1

Result

Physical characteristics of gabbro treated by high temperature

High temperature-induced changes in chromaticity

The mineral composition and structure of gabbro undergo significant changes under high-temperature treatment. The results indicate that high temperatures markedly alter the chromatic characteristics of gabbro samples (Fig. 2). At 25 °C, gabbro primarily consists of dark green or black clinopyroxene and gray-white or white basic plagioclase (Fig. 2a). As the temperature increases, the brightness of the gabbro samples gradually decreases (Fig. 2b–e). The pyroxene minerals within the samples change color from dark to brown, turning reddish-brown at 800 °C, indicating oxidation of iron in the clinopyroxene at high temperatures. Meanwhile, the feldspar minerals shift from white to grayish-white due to structural changes in plagioclase crystals induced by high temperatures [28], which affect light reflection and absorption, leading to a change in color. This phenomenon reflects the complex physical and chemical changes in the microstructure and composition of gabbro samples induced by high temperatures.Fig. 2Chromaticity characteristics of gabbro under high-temperature treatment.Fig. 2

Chromaticity characteristics of gabbro under high-temperature treatment.

Variation of mass with high temperatures

The mass of gabbro samples changes during drying, heating, and saturation processes. The results indicate that the mass of gabbro samples under different high temperatures are generally consistent (Fig. 3a). Taking a gabbro sample at 500 °C as an example, its average mass in a natural state is 140.21 g. After drying, the mass decreases by 0.48 g, primarily due to water loss. Upon saturation, the mass increases by 0.47 g, attributed to the added water content in a saturated state. After heating, the mass decreases by 1.76 g, due to the loss of weakly bound water, surface mineral exfoliation under high temperature, and possibly some strongly bound water. Upon re-drying, the mass remains mostly unchanged. Finally, after re-saturation, the mass increases to 140.15 g, a reduction of 0.06 g compared to the initial state.Fig. 3(a) Variation of sample mass under Different Conditions; (b) Variation of sample mass loss with high temperatures.Fig. 3

(a) Variation of sample mass under Different Conditions; (b) Variation of sample mass loss with high temperatures.

Fig. 3b shows the mass loss of dried gabbro samples before and after exposure to different high temperatures. Between 300 °C and 800 °C, the mass loss of the dried samples was 1.1 g, 1.23 g, 1.34 g, and 1.52 g, respectively. The results indicate that as the temperature increases, the mass loss of gabbro samples gradually increases. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the spalling of surface minerals in the gabbro samples at high temperatures.

High temperature-induced changes in microstructure of gabbro

The microstructure of gabbro was observed using the SEM (magnified 3000 times). The thermal damage process inside the gabbro was inferred based on the micro-morphological characteristics of the rock samples (Fig. 4). As shown in the figure, at 25 °C, the gabbro samples exhibit no obvious cracks, and the microstructure is intact (Fig. 4a). As the temperature increases, the microstructure of gabbro progresses from having no visible cracks to showing cracks and broken mineral particles, and eventually to the generation of numerous cracks and crystalline microparticles (Fig. 4b–e). Particularly at 600 °C and 800 °C, the gabbro samples display a significant number of clear cracks and fragmented mineral crystals, indicating that the thermal expansion of mineral particles under high temperatures leads to stress concentration, causing the microstructure of the rock to deteriorate.Fig. 4Scanning electron microscope images of gabbro under high temperatures.Fig. 4

Scanning electron microscope images of gabbro under high temperatures.

Dynamic splitting characteristics of gabbro treated by high temperatures

Variation of the splitting strength with high temperatures

High-temperature treatment significantly affects the splitting strength characteristics of gabbro. Under different fissure angles, the splitting strength of gabbro shows a similar trend with temperature change: as the temperature increases, the splitting strength initially rises and then decreases, reaching a turning point around 500 °C (Fig. 5a). Notably, microcracks were observed at 500 °C (Fig. 4c), suggesting that the splitting strength at this temperature might have surpassed its peak but still remains close to the maximum strength. When the temperature rises from 25 °C to 500 °C, the strength of the gabbro samples increases compared to room temperature, indicating that within this temperature range, heat has a strengthening effect on the samples. This strengthening effect may be due to changes in the mineral composition and structure of gabbro at high temperatures, leading to the evaporation of absorbed water and enhancing the bonding strength between mineral particles and cementing materials [34]. This effect can partially offset the detrimental impact of microcrack formation and propagation caused by high temperatures, resulting in relatively high splitting strength at specific temperatures. When the temperature rises between 500 °C and 800 °C, the splitting strength of the gabbro samples begins to decrease, reaching the lowest at 800 °C, especially noticeable at 30° and 60° fissure angles. The primary cause is the significant fragmentation of mineral crystals and the expansion of microcracks at high temperatures, which severely compromise the overall structure of the samples. Additionally, samples with fissure angles of 30° and 60° exhibit lower resistance to failure under high temperatures, likely due to the relative angle between the fracture orientation and the direction of the applied force, which results in more pronounced stress concentration. This accelerates crack propagation and coalescence, ultimately leading to a significant reduction in splitting strength.Fig. 5(a) Variation of the splitting strength and (b) the splitting strength variation rate with high temperatures.Fig. 5

(a) Variation of the splitting strength and (b) the splitting strength variation rate with high temperatures.

To more comprehensively reflect the impact of high temperatures on the gabbro splitting strength, the concept of splitting strength variation rate ησ is introduced, as illustrated in Eq. (2):(2)ησ=σ(T)−σ(T0)σ(T0)where, σ(T) is the splitting strength with high temperatures; σ(T0) is the splitting strength at 25 °C.

As shown in Fig. 5b, the splitting strength variation rate ησ initially increases and then decreases with the rise in temperature. When the temperature increases from 25 °C to 500 °C, the ησ values of the samples show an overall upward trend and remains positive, indicating that the strengthening effect of temperature on gabbro samples gradually increases within this range. However, when the temperature rises from 500 °C to 800 °C, the ησ values start to decrease, becoming negative at 800 °C, especially noticeable at fissure angles of 45° and 60°. This indicates that within the high-temperature range (500 °C–800 °C), the destructive effect of temperature on the samples dominates, with the degree of damage varying according to the fissure angles of the gabbro samples.

Variation of the peak strain with high temperatures

The peak strain of rock indicates its deformation capability. By analyzing the peak strain of gabbro samples, we can understand the deformation ability and damage extent under impact loading. Under high-temperature treatment, the peak strain of gabbro samples with different fissure angles generally shows an increase-decrease-increase trend, with peaks and troughs mainly at 300 °C and 500 °C (Fig. 6a). In the temperature range of 25 °C–300 °C, the peak strain of gabbro samples with fissure angles between 0° and 60° increases with temperature, while samples with fissure angles of 75° and 90° have peak strains similar to those at 25 °C. As the temperature rises from 300 °C to 500 °C, the peak strain of all gabbro samples decreases to varying extents, possibly due to phase transitions or microstructural reorganization within the rock, resulting in higher rigidity and resistance to deformation. When the temperature continues to rise to 800 °C, the peak strain of gabbro samples increases again, especially at 800 °C with the 0° fissure angle, where the peak strain reaches its maximum. This is mainly due to the extensive fracturing of mineral crystals and microcrack crack expansion caused by high temperatures, significantly reducing their deformation resistance.Fig. 6(a) Variation of the peak strain and (b) the peak strain variation rate with high temperatures.Fig. 6

(a) Variation of the peak strain and (b) the peak strain variation rate with high temperatures.

Similarly, to more comprehensively reflect the impact of high temperatures on the gabbro peak strain, the concept of peak strain variation rate ηε is introduced, as illustrated in Eq. (3):(3)ηε=ε(T)‐ε(T0)ε(T0)where, ε(T) is the peak strain with different high temperatures; ε(T0) is the peak strain at 25 °C.

As shown in Fig. 6b, the peak strain variation rate ηε of gabbro samples increases, then decreases, and increases again with rising temperature. At 500 °C, a few gabbro samples with certain fissure angles exhibit a negative ηε values, while others show positive ηε values. Notably, at 800 °C, the ηε value typically reaches its maximum value. This indicates that high temperatures significantly weaken the deformation resistance of gabbro samples and show more pronounced effects on the rock's microstructure and mechanical properties.

Variation of the dynamic elastic modulus with high temperatures

The dynamic elastic modulus of rock is a crucial parameter for assessing rock stiffness and dynamic damage. As temperature increases, the dynamic elastic modulus of gabbro with different fissure angles shows a decrease-increase-decrease trend, with a peak around 500 °C (Fig. 7a). This suggests that at this temperature, the reorganization of mineral structures and the evaporation of absorbed water might lead to tighter bonding between mineral particles and cementing materials, resulting in temporarily higher stiffness in the rock. Beyond 500 °C, the dynamic elastic modulus of gabbro rapidly decreases, reaching its lowest at 800 °C, due to significant intergranular and intragranular cracking, severely damaging the structure.Fig. 7(a) Variation in the dynamic elastic modulus and (b) the dynamic elastic modulus variation rate with high temperatures.Fig. 7

(a) Variation in the dynamic elastic modulus and (b) the dynamic elastic modulus variation rate with high temperatures.

Similarly, to more comprehensively reflect the impact of high temperatures on the gabbro dynamic elastic modulus, the concept of dynamic elastic modulus variation rate ηEd is introduced, as illustrated in Eq. (4):(4)ηEd=Ed(T)‐Ed(T0)Ed(T0)where, Ed(T) is the dynamic elastic modulus with different high temperatures; Ed(T0) is the dynamic elastic modulus at 25 °C.

As shown in Fig. 7b, the dynamic elastic modulus variation rate ηEd of gabbro samples decreases, then increases, and decreases again with rising temperature. At 500 °C, a few gabbro samples with certain fissure angles exhibit the positive ηEd values, while others show negative values. Notably, at 800 °C, the ηEd value typically reaches its minimum value. This indicates that high temperatures significantly weaken the stiffness of gabbro samples. It is especially noteworthy that, compared to the peak strain variation rate, the trends are exactly opposite. This phenomenon indicates that the deformation resistance of gabbro significantly decreases under high temperatures, leading to a reduction in its dynamic elastic modulus.

Dynamic splitting characteristics of gabbro samples with various fissure angles

Variation of the splitting strength with fissure angles

The splitting strength of gabbro under high temperatures shows two main trends based on fissure angles (Fig. 8). Results indicate two distinct patterns: First, from 25 °C to 500 °C, the splitting strength of gabbro samples fluctuates with increasing fissure angles, showing an increase-decrease-increase-decrease-increase trend. Two noticeable troughs are at 30° and 60°, with peaks at 15° and 45°. The overall splitting strength increases with temperature, consistent with the conclusions of Fig. 5a. In this range, the increase in temperature alters the mineral composition and structure of the gabbro, causing the evaporation of absorbed water. This results in tighter bonding between mineral particles and cementing materials, which enhances the strength of the gabbro. However, the fissure angle remains the primary factor affecting the splitting strength of the gabbro samples. Second, between 500 °C and 800 °C, the splitting strength of gabbro generally decreases with increasing fissure angles. Specifically, at 600 °C, the lowest splitting strength is at 45° fissure angle, while at 800 °C, the lowest splitting strength is at 60° fissure angle. During this stage, high temperatures cause significant mineral crystal breakage and microcrack expansion. The combined effect of high temperature and fissure angle alters crack propagation paths and energy release modes, significantly affecting gabbro splitting strength.Fig. 8Variation in the splitting strength with fissure angles.Fig. 8

Variation in the splitting strength with fissure angles.

Variation of the peak strain with fissure angles

The presence of fissure angles significantly affects the gabbro peak strain. In the temperature range of 25 °C–600 °C, the peak strain of gabbro samples decreases initially and then increases with the rise of fissure angle (Fig. 9). At a fissure angle of 30°, the peak strain reaches its minimum, indicating optimal deformation resistance of the gabbro sample. At 800 °C, the peak strain reaches its maximum across fissure angles (0°, 30°, 60°, 70°, and 90°), consistent with the conclusions in Fig. 6a. High temperatures cause significant physical and chemical changes within the rock, including the fragmentation of mineral crystals and the expansion of microcracks. These changes considerably reduce the structural integrity and load-bearing capacity of the rock. While fissure angle still influences peak strain, the dominant effect of high temperatures causes the peak strain to reach its maximum for samples with various fissure angles. This indicates a significant decrease in the overall deformation resistance of gabbro samples at extremely high temperatures.Fig. 9Variation in the peak strain with fissure angles.Fig. 9

Variation in the peak strain with fissure angles.

Variation of the dynamic elastic modulus with fissure angles

In high-temperature environments, the dynamic elastic modulus of gabbro samples generally shows an increase-decrease-increase-decrease trend with increasing fissure angles, reaching a minimum at a 60° fissure angle (Fig. 10). This is due to the uneven stress distribution caused by changes in the fissure angle, which affects the elastic response of the rock. Notably, compared to the curve at 25 °C, the overall trend of the dynamic elastic modulus gradually decreases as the temperature rises to 300 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. This indicates that, although fissure angles still influence the dynamic elastic modulus, the dominant effect of high temperatures reduces the dynamic elastic modulus across all fissure angles. At 800 °C, except for samples with a 0° fissure angle, the dynamic elastic modulus of the remaining gabbro samples reaches its minimum. This is consistent with the conclusions in Fig. 7a, showing that the stiffness of gabbro samples significantly decreases at extremely high temperatures.Fig. 10Variation in the dynamic elastic modulus with fissure angles.Fig. 10

Variation in the dynamic elastic modulus with fissure angles.

Dynamic splitting failure modes of gabbro treated by high temperatures

The dynamic splitting failure modes of gabbro under high-temperature treatment are primarily influenced by the fissure angle and the stress wave propagation path. Fig. 11 illustrates the splitting failure morphology of fractured gabbro samples treated by high temperatures. The fissure angle significantly affects the propagation of cracks within gabbro samples, with cracks typically expanding symmetrically from the tips of pre-cut fissure, closely following the angle of inclination. Additionally, under varying high temperature conditions, the crack propagation paths in gabbro samples with the same fissure angle remain similar, primarily exhibiting tensile and shear failures. This indicates that while high temperatures have a significant impact on the splitting strength of gabbro, they have a lesser effect on the dynamic splitting failure modes.Fig. 11Splitting failure morphology of fractured gabbro samples under high temperatures.Fig. 11

Splitting failure morphology of fractured gabbro samples under high temperatures.

Effect of fissure angle on gabbro failure modes

Fissure angle significantly affects the crack propagation in gabbro. The results show that when the fissure angle is 0°, the pre-cut fissure is parallel to the incident bar. Upon wave entry into the sample, tensile cracks form at the both ends of the pre-cut fissure and extend further in the loading direction, leading to fracture. When the fissure angles are 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 75°, the crack propagation process under impact load is generally consistent. The incident wave creates cracks at the contact point on the sample surface, and as the stress wave continues to propagate, stress concentration effects appear at both ends of the pre-cut fissure, resulting in symmetrical tensile cracks, followed by shear failure. At a 90° fissure angle, the pre-cut fissure is perpendicular to the incident bar. The incident wave induces cracks perpendicular to the fissure at its midpoint and parallel cracks at the ends, which further propagate to complete the fracture, ultimately splitting the sample into four pieces. The failure of gabbro samples with different fissure angles is mainly characterized by tensile and shear failures. According to fracture mechanics theory [35], the failure patterns of the fractured gabbro samples in this study are categorized as Type I-II composite crack propagation.

Variation of gabbro crack initiation angle with fissure angles

To better understand the influence of fissure angle on the gabbro failure modes, we defined the crack initiation angle as the angle between the propagating crack and the extension line of the pre-cut fissure. We then measured the crack initiation angles of gabbro samples subjected to different high-temperature conditions. The results show that with increasing fissure angle, the trend of crack initiation angles is generally consistent across all temperature conditions, displaying an overall increasing trend (Fig. 12). For instance, in gabbro samples treated at 800 °C, the crack initiation angle increases monotonically with the fissure angle, although the rate of increase slows down. This is primarily because the increase in pre-cut fissure angle alters the location and shape of the stress concentration zones within the sample, leading to a larger crack initiation angle.Fig. 12Variation in gabbro crack initiation angle with fissure angles.Fig. 12

Variation in gabbro crack initiation angle with fissure angles.

Conclusions

Meanwhile, the SEM result was used to elucidate the microstructural thermal damage, and the effects of high-temperature treatment on the physical and dynamic properties of fractured gabbro were analyzed.
