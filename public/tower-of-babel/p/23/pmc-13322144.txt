Can Small Open-Source Language Models With Retrieval-Augmented Generation Match GPT-4 Performance in Breast Cancer Clinical Decision Support?
Authors: Chanhee Park, In Hae Park, Minhyuk Kim, Youngjoon Jang, Heuiseok Lim, Yuna Hur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEThe rapidly evolving breast cancer treatment landscape creates significant information synthesis challenges for clinicians. We evaluated whether small open-source large language models (LLMs) augmented with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) could match proprietary model performance for clinical guideline queries.METHODSWe developed a domain-specialized RAG pipeline using HTML-structure-preserving chunking of 1,356 ASCO breast cancer guideline documents. Five LLMs were each evaluated with and without RAG: GPT-4-turbo, GPT-3.5-turbo, Qwen2.5-14B (14 billion parameters), LLaMA3-8B, and OpenBioLLM-8B. Performance was assessed using 98 expert-curated question-answer-context triplets across seven breast cancer categories. Evaluation used both rubric-based scoring (six metrics: fluency, relevance, reliability, consistency, clarity, and clinical impact) and exhaustive pairwise ranking by GPT-4-turbo as judge. Human validation was conducted with 15 practicing oncologists on a 10-query subset.RESULTSRAG-enhanced Qwen2.5-14B achieved mean rubric scores of 3.77 versus 3.96 for GPT-4-turbo and pairwise ranking performance of 0.72 versus 0.81 (normalized scale). Although absolute rubric gains were modest (0.02-0.05 on a five-point scale), relative improvements in head-to-head win rates ranged from 16% to 46%. Human expert scores confirmed RAG superiority but were consistently more conservative than LLM judge scores (mean 3.81 v 4.12 across all metrics). Optimal retrieval used top-5 contexts; performance degraded sharply at higher context volumes.CONCLUSIONSmall open-source LLMs with optimized RAG can approach state-of-the-art proprietary model performance for clinical decision support. This approach enables scalable, cost-effective, privacy-preserving deployment without recurrent fine-tuning, suggesting potential for real-world clinical implementation on single-graphics processing unit infrastructure under expert supervision.

INTRODUCTION

The breast cancer treatment landscape is rapidly evolving across four biologic subtypes. Hormone receptor–positive/HER2-negative disease has been transformed by CDK4/6 inhibitors and PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitors; HER2-positive disease by antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and intensified HER2-targeted strategies; and triple-negative breast cancer by immunotherapy integration, TROP2-directed ADCs, and genomic biomarker-directed targeted therapies.1,2 Major guidelines are revised annually or more frequently; however, treatment strategies are further conditioned by country-specific labeling and reimbursement policies defining companion diagnostic requirements, line-of-therapy eligibility, and permitted sequencing. Clinicians must reconcile evolving and sometimes discordant recommendations with payer policies at the point of care.

This complexity creates critical need for decision-support tools that rapidly synthesize evolving, multisource information. Large language models (LLMs) augmented with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can integrate external knowledge in real time, enhancing response accuracy and contextual relevance.3-5 Interest is growing in applying LLMs as domain-specific decision support tools in health care.6,7 Recent work in cancer informatics has demonstrated that guideline-augmented LLMs can generate standardized adjuvant therapy recommendations for breast cancer,8 that advanced LLMs can approach expert-level oncology decision making in pairwise evaluations,9 and that domain-specific embedding fine-tuning can improve retrieval quality in oncology question-answering.10

Despite their potential, static LLMs struggle in domains such as breast cancer care, where clinical guidelines vary by country and are frequently updated,11,12 risking outdated or hallucinated recommendations. Fine-tuning on domain-specific data can improve performance but requires costly retraining whenever new knowledge emerges, posing scalability and security challenges,13,14 with additional risk of catastrophic forgetting.15 Although RAG mitigates some limitations, existing health care work focuses on large proprietary LLMs, overlooking small open-source models.

Moreover, medical-domain LLMs have been predominantly evaluated on multiple-choice benchmarks rather than in clinical implementation settings,16 and open-ended generation remains insufficiently tested.17 Proper evaluation of LLM performance in medical domains is itself an open challenge, given the ethical considerations inherent to clinical decision support.18

In this study, we designed a domain-specialized RAG pipeline for breast cancer guidelines featuring expert-curated query-answer-document triples, HTML-preserving chunking for guideline retrieval, and integration with both proprietary and open-source LLMs. To address the difficulty of evaluating open-ended clinical responses, we developed a multi-axis evaluation framework combining rubric-based scoring, pairwise ranking, and cross-evaluator validation.

All models were evaluated with and without RAG to isolate its effect across scales and architectures. RAG-enhanced Qwen2.5-14B19 achieved a mean rubric score of 3.77 compared with 3.96 for GPT-4-turbo,20 with a similar gap in pairwise ranking (0.72 v 0.81 normalized). These results suggest that RAG can substantially narrow the performance gap between small open-source and large proprietary LLMs for clinical question-answering on single-graphics processing unit (GPU) infrastructure.

METHODS

Data Collection and Preprocessing

Figure 1 shows the RAG system pipeline. We exclusively crawled web-based guideline sources with HTML markup, enabling fine-grained extraction and reliable hierarchical section/subsection parsing. Traditional PDF extraction methods introduce errors such as misrecognized characters and lost document structure.21 Our HTML-based approach minimizes information loss and ensures semantic consistency compared with fixed-length chunking, as shown in Data Supplement (Fig S1). Metadata—author names, URLs, titles, and publication types—were stored alongside text content to support reference verification. Each chunk maps to semantic units (sections, paragraphs, and subheadings) within the HTML hierarchy, avoiding arbitrary token-based splits and ensuring context relevance.22,23

Medical Database Construction

We used text-embedding-3-large and Pinecone as the vector database for semantic search. Each chunk was pre-encoded into a vector representation; during retrieval, input queries were embedded and matched via cosine similarity, returning the most relevant contexts for LLM inference. Chunk length distribution is provided in the Data Supplement (Fig S2).

Clinical Question-Answer Data Set

Evaluating open-ended LLM responses is challenging due to response variance and lack of singular correct answers.24 We collaborated with practicing oncologists to curate 98 question-answer pairs with supporting evidence, reflecting clinically significant queries across seven breast cancer categories: general chemotherapy (27), HER2 (26), TNBC (19), brain metastasis (10), hereditary breast cancer (7), pregnancy (5), and oncologic (4), as shown in the Data Supplement (Table S1). Each entry included the question, reference answer, and supporting context.

Questions were developed independently by domain experts from multiple specialties (medical oncology, breast surgery, radiology, and pathology) and pooled for breadth across clinical scenarios. For each query, experts prepared a reference text identifying the critical clinical information expected in a guideline-consistent response. These reference texts serve as evaluative anchors rather than singular correct answers: the rubric assesses whether responses accurately reflect the critical information, not verbatim reproduction, thereby accommodating variation in structure and emphasis.

Model Selection

We selected proprietary models—GPT-3.5-turbo25 and GPT-4-turbo20—with outstanding performance on specialty benchmarks, although API-based inference raises cost and privacy concerns, and open-source models—LLaMA3-8B26 and Qwen2.5-14B19—offering substantial advantages through on-premise deployment. OpenBioLLM-8B,27 a medical-domain-fine-tuned derivative of LLaMA3-8B, was included to evaluate domain adaptation effects on RAG-integrated performance. Additionally, we report the performance of Meerkat-8B,16 another medical derivative of LLaMA3-8B, in our cross-validation result, as shown in the Data Supplement (Table S3).

Open-source models were served via vLLM (v0.8.5) with temperature 0.6, top-p 0.9, and maximum output of 8,192 tokens. For RAG inference, top-k was set to 5 based on preliminary experiments, as shown in Table 1.

Prompt Design

We adopted standardized, minimalistic prompts for inference to enhance reproducibility,28,29 while evaluation used detailed structured criteria to maximize scoring objectivity. All prompt templates are provided in the Data Supplement (Figs S3 and S4).

Evaluation Methods

Model evaluation in medical NLP must go beyond classification accuracy and string matching.30,31 N-gram metrics (BLEU and ROUGE) fail to capture semantic correctness and clinical utility.32 LLM-as-a-judge approaches evaluate generated text from semantic and holistic perspectives with high correlation to expert ratings.33-37

Following established methodologies,38,39 we designed a rubric with six criteria, each on a five-point Likert scale: fluency (grammatical accuracy, professional tone), relevance (addressing key clinical aspects), reliability (accuracy and evidence basis), consistency (logical structure), clarity (readability), and clinical impact (potential to improve outcomes), yielding 5,880 individual scores across all configurations.

For pairwise ranking, we conducted exhaustive comparisons across all model combinations for all 98 queries. To mitigate positional bias,40,41 presentation order was randomized (A v B and B v A) and both configurations evaluated, yielding 8,820 judgments. GPT-4-turbo served as judge, outputting one of three labels: A (superior), B (superior), or tie. Ties were counted as a separate category and not redistributed to either model in the win/tie/loss aggregation.

Human Validation

To assess generalizability, 10 randomly sampled queries with Qwen2.5-14B base and RAG responses were scored by 15 breast cancer clinicians (6 male and 9 female; mean age 45 years, range, 39-55; subspecialties: medical oncology [n = 9], breast surgery [n = 3], radiology [n = 2], and pathology [n = 1]; mean clinical practice: 9.7 years) using the same six-metric rubric on a 1-5 Likert scale.

Statistical Analysis

For rubric evaluation, we calculated mean scores across all metrics. For pairwise ranking, we computed win rates and normalized scores. Context window ablation studies systematically varied the number of context chunks (k = 1, 3, 5, 10, 30, 50) to measure performance impact.

To quantify the formatting-detail trade-off, we performed automated surface-level analysis of Qwen2.5-14B responses. Formatting metrics counted markdown syntax elements (bold markers, bullet points, and numbered lists); detail metrics counted numeric values, evidence citations, and domain-specific medical terminology. Counts were averaged across all 98 responses for both base and RAG conditions.

We report means with standard deviations (SD) and 95% CIs for all rubric scores (N = 98 per model-metric combination). Cross-evaluator validation used DeepSeek-V342 as an independent judge, compared via Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall's τ. All analyses used Python 3.11 (SciPy v1.11).

Ethical Considerations

The data set contains expert-curated question-answer pairs derived from publicly available clinical guidelines and does not include personally identifiable patient data. The study did not involve human subjects and therefore did not require institutional review board approval.

RESULTS

Data Collection and Corpus Statistics

Using Selenium (v4.12.0), we scraped ASCO breast cancer guidelines as of October 2024, collecting 1,356 documents (613 abstract-only and 743 full-length). The resulting corpus contained 15,607 chunks (mean, 123.53 words; SD, 91.70), totaling 1,927,938 words.

Conversely, GPT-4-turbo and OpenBioLLM-8B decreased by 0.04 and 0.22 points, respectively.

Trade-Off Between Formatting and Clinical Detail

Table 3 shows the quantitative analysis of Qwen2.5-14B responses, showing changes in output characteristics when RAG is applied. Formatting-related metrics, such as the use of bolding (9.32 to 7.05) and bullet points (1.38 to 0.90), decreased. By contrast, detail-related metrics increased: numeric details more than doubled (2.06 to 4.38) and evidence citations nearly doubled (1.01 to 1.96). This formatting-detail trade-off was reflected in Qwen2.5-14B's per-metric rubric scores: RAG decreased fluency (4.63 to 4.50) while increasing relevance (3.72 to 3.87) and reliability (3.64 to 3.76). Performance Comparison of Model Sizes

Qwen2.5-14B outperformed GPT-3.5-turbo across all metrics, achieving a normalized pairwise score of 0.72 versus 0.81 for GPT-4-turbo. OpenBioLLM-8B scored lower than its base Llama3-8B in both conditions (average 2.69 v 3.36 with RAG; 2.92 v 3.34 without) and produced markedly shorter responses (mean 401 v 1,447 characters), frequently adopting terse, single-sentence formats absent in general-purpose models.

Human Expert Validation

As a preliminary consistency check, a subset of 10 queries was assessed by 15 practicing breast cancer clinicians, who scored Qwen2.5-14B base and RAG responses using the same six-metric rubric. Both the LLM judge and human experts assigned higher scores to RAG-augmented responses. Human experts assigned lower scores than the LLM judge across all metrics (mean 3.81 v 4.12). Full human assessment scores are reported in the Data Supplement (Table S2).

Context Window Optimization

Ablation studies on retrieval volume, as shown in Table 1, shows that optimal performance was achieved at k = 5, with average score 3.77 from Qwen2.5-14B. Performance remained relatively stable at k = 1 and k = 3 but experienced a sharp decline at k = 10, reaching a minimum score of 1.37 at k = 50.

Cross-Evaluator Validation

Cross-evaluator validation using DeepSeek-V3 showed strong concordance across 60 model-metric score pairs (Pearson r = 0.876, Spearman ρ = 0.874; all per-metric r > 0.91). Model rank ordering was near-identical (Kendall τ = 0.956, P < .001), with only a single adjacent-rank swap. Pairwise win-rate correlation was r = 0.994 (Spearman ρ = 0.939; 81/90 pairs concordant [90.0%]), supporting the robustness of the reported performance hierarchy, although evaluator influence cannot be ruled out. Full DeepSeek results, including Meerkat-8B, are provided in the Data Supplement (Table S3 and Fig S7).

DISCUSSION

The performance variations highlight how RAG interacts with model architectures and training objectives. For GPT-4-turbo, the slight score decrease suggests injected context can act as noise when a model already possesses high-density internal knowledge.43 Conversely, Qwen2.5-14B outperformed GPT-3.5-turbo despite fewer parameters regardless of RAG condition, suggesting that architecture and training data quality may be more determinative than raw scale, as shown in the Data Supplement (Fig S5).

The underperformance of domain-fine-tuned models—OpenBioLLM-8B and Meerkat-8B—relative to their base Llama3-8B, as illustrated in the Data Supplement (Table S3), reflects a task-format mismatch. Training on closed-ended benchmarks rewards concise answer selection, producing responses poorly suited to open-ended clinical Question Answering (QA). Dorfner et al44 reported similar gaps on unseen medical benchmarks. This is exemplified in the Data Supplement (Fig S6), where Llama3-8B produces a structured, clinically detailed response, while OpenBioLLM-8B generates a single-sentence non-sequitur. These findings suggest that augmenting general-purpose instruction-tuned models with high-quality external knowledge via RAG may be more effective than domain-specific fine-tuning for open-ended clinical question-answering.

RAG-augmented Qwen2.5-14B approached GPT-4-turbo performance (3.77 v 3.96), with practical implications: clinically useful decision support can be deployed on-premises using a single GPU, addressing cost and privacy concerns. Small-scale LLMs possess fewer parameters and limited training coverage, resulting in performance gaps in specialized domains.45,46 Our RAG approach compensates via in-context learning with external domain knowledge,47 enabling a parameter-efficient model to substantially narrow the gap with larger proprietary systems.

Table 2 shows that Qwen2.5-14B's per-metric rubric scores mirror the formatting-detail trade-off in Table 3: RAG improves clinically relevant metrics, such as relevance and reliability, at the cost of fluency. This trade-off may favor clinical utility, where verifiable numerical details carry greater practical value than stylistic formatting. In addition, since ASCO guidelines are natively published as web content, our HTML-structure-preserving chunking leverages the document hierarchy to maintain metadata—document title, author information, document type, and source URL—within each chunk, as shown in the Data Supplement (Fig S8), enabling clinicians to verify cited evidence and trace references as guidelines evolve.

Using GPT-4-turbo as both generator and evaluator introduces circularity risk by potentially favoring stylistically similar outputs. Although this limitation cannot be fully eliminated, DeepSeek-V3 cross-validation yielded near-identical rank orderings, suggesting the performance hierarchy is not entirely driven by evaluator-specific bias, though such influence cannot be excluded. Pairwise rankings were evaluated solely by LLM judges and were not validated by human experts. The 10-query human rubric assessment represents a preliminary directional check, not comprehensive methodological validation. The conservativeness of human scorers relative to the LLM judge likely reflects the strict evidentiary standards of clinical practice.

In artificial intelligence (AI)–based clinical decision support systems, safety, ethical accountability, and hallucination risk represent paramount concerns before clinical deployment.48 RAG is theoretically expected to reduce hallucination risk by grounding responses in retrieved evidence; however, this assumption was not empirically tested in this study, and hallucination frequency was not systematically quantified. Moreover, RAG's benefit is contingent on retrieval quality: irrelevant or outdated retrieved passages may actively degrade response accuracy—a failure mode potentially more dangerous than hallucination from parametric knowledge alone. In the high-stakes oncology domain, deployment must follow a physician-in-the-loop model where AI augments rather than replaces expert judgment, with active error monitoring before clinical integration. All claims regarding clinical applicability remain explicitly provisional pending prospective validation.

To illustrate that RAG-augmented responses may appear well structured on the surface yet contain factual inaccuracies requiring expert verification, a domain specialist (I.P.) inspected the response pools from GPT-4-turbo and Qwen2.5-14B under the RAG condition. This qualitative, nonsystematic review identified specific failure modes in both models. In Qwen2.5-14B: abbreviation misinterpretation (n = 3, eg, “PABC” incorrectly expanded as “positive axillary breast cancer”), cross-indication PARP inhibitor extrapolation (n = 1), and incomplete 2023 ASCO/CAP HER2 SISH algorithm description (n = 1). In GPT-4-turbo: incomplete HER2 SISH description (n = 1), incorrect denosumab citation for hypercalcemia (n = 1), and an off-topic response (n = 1). These failure modes—abbreviation drift, cross-indication extrapolation, and algorithmic incompleteness—carry patient-safety risks and underscore that RAG responses require clinician oversight even when they appear fluent and well organized. A systematic hallucination rate analysis across all queries and models was not conducted and remains a priority for future work.

Table 1 shows that the performance degraded sharply beyond optimal retrieval levels, with rubric scores declining 64% from 3.77 at k = 5 to 1.37 at k = 50, attributable to the “lost-in-the-middle” phenomenon49 and the concentrated nature of clinical queries—relevant information resides in few chunks while additional passages dilute precision. In clinical practice, the ultimate LLM role is not to replace clinician judgment but to augment it; prior evidence has demonstrated that AI-assisted systems can improve treatment decisions and guideline adherence in breast cancer care.50 Real-world deployment must also address latency, real-time guideline updates, and regional policy variations.

This study has several limitations. The RAG database was limited to ASCO breast cancer clinical guidelines, indicating need for expansion to other cancer types and regional specificities such as health insurance policies, labeling differences, and payer criteria. Due to intrinsic limitations of small-scale LLMs, complex multidisciplinary queries and long-term patient history summarization may still present challenges. GPT-4-turbo served as both a response-generating model and the primary LLM judge, introducing potential evaluator bias that cross-validation with DeepSeek-V3 mitigates but cannot fully eliminate. This study did not independently evaluate retrieval quality; retrieval failures—where irrelevant passages enter the context window—could degrade response quality, and characterizing their frequency and impact remains an open question.

Human validation was limited to rubric-based scoring of 10 queries (10.2% of the data set) by 15 clinicians for a single model (Qwen2.5-14B); pairwise rankings were not validated by human experts. This preliminary assessment provides directional consistency with LLM judge trends but does not constitute comprehensive confirmation of the evaluation framework. Hallucination frequency was not systematically quantified; only qualitative error characterization was performed. Future work should evaluate varied embedding models, retrieval strategies, and multihop reasoning. Broader human validation across multiple models and evaluation dimensions remains a priority.

In conclusion, small open-source LLMs with optimized RAG approached proprietary model performance in rubric-based evaluation, demonstrating that general-purpose instruction-tuned models can substantially narrow the performance gap with high-quality guideline context. Domain-fine-tuned medical LLMs exhibited output patterns misaligned with open-ended clinical QA, suggesting RAG augmentation with authoritative sources may be more practical than benchmark-oriented fine-tuning. Combined with structure-preserving chunking that retains source metadata for clinician verification, this approach enables scalable, privacy-preserving deployment on single-GPU infrastructure. However, validation was limited to ASCO breast cancer guidelines, hallucination frequency was not systematically quantified, and human validation covered only a subset of queries. Generalizability to other guideline sources and regional contexts remains to be established through prospective clinical evaluation.
