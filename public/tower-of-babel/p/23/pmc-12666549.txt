Conventional and Algorithmic Music Listening During Hemodialysis: A Case Report
Authors: Alfredo Raglio, Giuseppe Sileno, Federica Boschetti, Federica Liuzzo, Marta Arazzi, Filippo Sangregorio, Ciro Esposito, Alessia Cardella, Paola Gabanelli, Paola Baiardi, Marina Rita Manera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients undergoing hemodialysis frequently experience significant emotional distress, including anxiety and depressive symptoms. Music therapy has emerged as a promising nonpharmacological intervention to alleviate these psychological burdens. This study explored the effects of 2 distinct music listening protocols—Therapeutic Music Listening and algorithmic music listening—on anxiety, stress, and physiological parameters in a female patient with end-stage kidney disease in comorbid condition with a major depression and anxiety symptoms. Over a two-week period, the patient participated in 6 Therapeutic Music Listening sessions (3 per week), each lasting 20 minutes during the initiation phase of hemodialysis. Following a washout period, algorithmic music listening was introduced. Anxiety and stress were assessed pre- and postsession using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (Y1) and Distress Thermometer, while physiological parameters (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate) were recorded simultaneously. The results indicated a reduction in anxiety after each session, with a more pronounced effect observed during algorithmic music listening. No significant changes were noted in physiological parameters. The patient reported a high level of subjective enjoyment of the experience, suggesting potential psychological benefits. These findings highlight the need for randomized controlled trials incorporating objective biomarkers to validate the therapeutic role of music listening in hemodialysis patients.Index Words: Hemodialysis, kidney disease, nonpharmacological approaches, music therapy, therapeutic music listening, algorithmic music listening, psychological symptoms, anxiety

Introduction

Hemodialysis (HD) is the most frequently employed renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage kidney disease. Although clinical complications associated with HD treatment are well-documented, psychological aspects remain less understood, as they involve a complex interplay between patient personality traits and various stressors that can significantly affect overall health. In addition to these psychological factors, a unique biomarker environment must be considered due to uremic syndrome, which is only partially mitigated by HD treatment.1 HD exerts a direct influence on the mental health of patients, as emotional distress and symptoms of anxiety and depression are frequently observed in individuals with chronic kidney disease. The continuous demands of dialysis, its effect on patients’ daily routines, and its interference with occupational and familial responsibilities contribute to a decline in mental well-being. This deterioration in psychological health adversely affects their quality of life and significantly influences their clinical trajectory and prognosis. Consequently, it is imperative to monitor and address the psychological well-being of HD patients to enhance their ability to cope with the demands of treatment.2,3

Music therapy represents an innovative and promising nonpharmacological intervention that has been shown to alleviate anxiety and stress, including in HD patients.4, 5, 6, 7 In some cases, its effects have also been associated with pain reduction. Within music therapy, music listening plays a pivotal role. Receptive approaches can be classified into 2 main categories: those grounded in psychological models and those not explicitly associated with specific relational frameworks.8,9 The latter category, commonly referred to as self-selected or researcher-selected music listening, is often described as “music medicine” interventions. In these approaches, music serves as the primary therapeutic agent without necessitating a structured relational process during listening.8 Such interventions have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential across diverse clinical and preventive settings.10 Research has shown that music listening activates both cortical and subcortical brain regions11 and elicits profound neurochemical effects.12 Bernardi et al13 reported that variations in rhythmic and dynamic elements of short musical segments corresponded with changes in physiological parameters such as heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and skin conductance. This suggests a link between music-induced modulation of autonomic nervous system responses and physiological regulation. Additionally, studies have identified effects on the cardiovascular autonomic system,14, 15, 16 highlighting potential applications in clinical practice.10

The present study employed 2 distinct music listening approaches: Therapeutic Music Listening10 and algorithmic music listening.17,18 Both interventions involved the use of personalized playlists, curated by the music therapy team, to alleviate transient symptoms such as anxiety and stress while modulating physiological parameters in patients undergoing HD. Therapeutic Music Listening is based on pieces of music from the conventional music repertoire that are chosen based on the patient’s musical tastes and musical parameters, and in this specific case, potentially induce de-activation/relaxation. In contrast, algorithmic listening is based on unconventional music that the patient may not have heard previously, that is, on a melodic line generated by an algorithm on the basis of parameters predefined by the researchers with the aim, in this case, of determining a de-activating/relaxing effect, similar to that hypothesized in the other listening modality (Therapeutic Musical Listening).

The existing literature on music interventions in HD patients is characterized by heterogeneous methodologies and methodological biases. The heterogeneity of the existing data on the role of music prompted the development of a Cochrane Review intervention protocol to assess the effect of music-based intervention on HD patients.19 The present case report introduces a systematic protocol and corresponding assessment framework to support HD treatment.

Case Report

We report the case of a 57-year-old woman with end-stage kidney disease secondary to autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, undergoing HD since 2014 via an arteriovenous fistula. Her comorbid conditions included hypertension, anemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, gastroesophageal reflux disease, osteoporosis, and major depressive disorder, for which she was receiving treatment with sertraline and prazepam. In 2015, she underwent a hysterectomy for fibromatosis, which was complicated by severe hypotension and a cardiac arrest due to ventricular tachycardia, followed by spontaneous recovery. In 2016, she experienced arteriovenous fistula thrombosis, necessitating the insertion of a tunneled central venous catheter in the right internal jugular vein. A right brachiocephalic arteriovenous fistula was subsequently created, allowing for the removal of the catheter. In 2018, she was diagnosed with and underwent excision of an in situ melanoma on her right leg, which resulted in her exclusion from the kidney transplant waiting list. In September 2023, an arteriovenous fistula infection at buttonhole cannulation sites led to Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, which subsequently progressed to cervical spondylodiscitis with multiple secondary muscle abscesses. This required prolonged hospitalization for intravenous antibiotic therapy.

Although her general condition improved over the following months, symptoms of major depression and anxiety persisted, prompting the initiation of a music therapy program. At the beginning of each HD session, the patient underwent music listening therapy using earphones. The first cycle of treatment involved an individualized 20-minute playlist, curated by a music therapist following the Therapeutic Music Listening protocol,10 with a focus on relaxation and emotional deactivation. This playlist was designed to integrate the patient’s musical preferences with specific therapeutic structures and parameters, ensuring a personalized listening experience. The intervention was administered 3 times per week over a 2-week period (6 sessions in total). After a washout period, a new algorithmic music playlist was introduced. This playlist was generated using an algorithm designed for therapeutic purposes, based on predefined music parameters established by researchers.17,18 Like the initial intervention, this playlist was intended to promote relaxation and was administered in an identical manner.

To assess the effects of the interventions, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (Y1) scale and the Distress Thermometer were administered by a psychologist before and after each session to evaluate anxiety and stress levels. Additionally, vital signs, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and respiratory rate, were recorded at the same time points. As illustrated in Fig 1, anxiety levels decreased after each session, with a more pronounced effect observed during algorithmic music listening. No significant changes were detected in other measured parameters, either before and after music listening or between the listening and washout periods.

At the conclusion of the intervention, a semistructured interview was conducted to gather qualitative feedback. The patient reported that the music listening experience facilitated more adaptive emotional and behavioral responses, allowing her to cognitively reframe the HD experience. Specifically, she described a shift in focus from feelings of fragility and vulnerability to a more engaging and distracting experience, ultimately restoring a sense of equilibrium by mitigating the distressing thoughts that typically overwhelmed her during dialysis. Furthermore, she noted that music listening helped her endure HD sessions in a more relaxed state, reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms. She expressed a desire to repeat the intervention and recommended it for other patients in similar conditions.

Discussion

This study introduces a structured music listening protocol with well-defined content and application methods (Fig 2). A novel aspect of the intervention is the inclusion of algorithmic music listening alongside a conventional therapeutic music protocol guided by specific procedural guidelines. Another strength of this study is the integration of both clinical and physiological assessment measures, enhancing the robustness of the findings. The results support the potential efficacy of music listening in reducing anxiety, consistent with existing literature.7 No additional significant effects were observed, possibly due to the limited duration of the intervention (20 minutes per session). The observed effects of algorithmic music listening are noteworthy, as prior studies have reported comparable outcomes with conventional music listening.20 Furthermore, the patient’s high level of subjective enjoyment underscores the relevance of this approach. This preliminary case study provides a foundation for future randomized controlled trials incorporating larger sample sizes, extended exposure durations, and comparisons between no music, conventional music, and algorithmic music. Future studies should also explore psychosocial measures in greater depth to reinforce the integration of music listening as an adjunctive therapy for psychological distress in chronic kidney disease. Additionally, biomarker analysis (eg, prolactin, cortisol, and free triiodothyronine) could serve as objective indicators of anxiety and stress regulation through music therapy.
