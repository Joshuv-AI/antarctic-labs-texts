Gold‐Mediated Base Pairs in Nucleic Acids: Selective Coordination to Natural and Thiocarbonyl‐Modified Cytosines Dependent upon the Oxidation State of Gold
Authors: Kai Kosugi, Ayano Sugawara, Erika Iwase, HeeJu Park, Shoji Fujiwara, Hiroki Kanazawa, Akira Ono, Jiro Kondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGold‐mediated base pairing in nucleic acids has remained poorly understood, despite structural analogies with mercury and silver ions known to coordinate selectively to mismatched base pairs. Here, the crystal structures of a C—Au(I)—C base pair and a C—G—Au(I)—C base triple formed with natural nucleobases are reported. Although solution‐phase thermodynamic analysis of Au(I) coordination is technically unfeasible, structural evidence supports its selective insertion into the base mismatches. In contrast, duplexes incorporating 2‐thiocytosine form square‐planar complexes with Au(III), and melting temperature analysis shows significant thermal stabilization. The distinct coordination geometries of Au(I) and Au(III) arise from differences in oxidation state and preferred coordination numbers, with Au(I) favoring linear two‐coordinate structures and Au(III) forming square‐planar complexes stabilized by thiocarbonyl donors. These findings establish a structure‐guided strategy for oxidation‐state‐selective metal coordination in nucleic acids, paving the way for the design of metal‐responsive DNA architectures with tunable properties.Keywords: DNA, gold, RNA, thiocarbonyl, X‐ray analysis

Introduction

Metal‐mediated base pairing in nucleic acids has attracted considerable attention as a promising approach for constructing structurally defined and functionally tunable DNA‐based materials.[ 1 , 2 – 3 ] Previous studies on metal‐mediated base pairs formed by natural nucleobases have shown that mercury ions (Hg(II)) selectively bind to T—T mismatches, while silver ions (Ag(I)) preferentially bind to C—C mismatches.[ 4 , 5 , 6 – 7 ] The crystal structures ofDNA‐silver nanowires,[ 8 ] DNA‐mercury nanowires,[ 9 ] DNA‐silver nanoclusters,[ 10 , 11 ] and other nucleic acid oligomers[ 12 , 13 ] have also revealed that Hg(II) can bind to T—G mismatches, and Ag(I) can associate with a variety of mismatches beyond C—C. Molecular structures of Hg(II)‐ and Ag(I)‐mediated base pairs, determined by X‐ray crystallography[ 14 , 15 ] and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy,[ 16 , 17 , 18 – 19 ] consistently exhibit an N‐metal‐N linear coordination geometry.[ 20 ] This suggests that monovalent gold ions (Au(I)), which can adopt a similar linear geometry, may also mediate base pairing through insertion into mismatched base pairs, similar to Hg(II) and Ag(I). For example, the preferential bindings of Au(I) ions or Au atoms to C—C or C—T mismatches have been previously reported based on theoretical studies and spectroscopic observations.[ 21 , 22 – 23 ] However, controlling the oxidation state of gold is more difficult than it is for mercury and silver, and gold‐mediated base pairing remains largely unexplored. To date, the only reported crystal structure of a gold‐mediated base pair involves a G—Au(III)—C interaction observed in the HIV‐1 dimerization initiation site RNA.[ 24 ] In this context, we aimed to experimentally investigate gold‐mediated base pairing using both natural and thiocarbonyl‐modified nucleobases, and to elucidate their structural and thermodynamic properties by X‐ray crystallography and melting temperature analyses.

Results and Discussion

Structure of C—Au(I)—C Base Pair

We successfully captured the molecular structures of gold‐mediated base pairs by cocrystallizing an oligonucleotide RNA‐C4C9 containing C—C mismatches (Table 1 ) with AuCl3. The RNA adopts the common A‐form RNA duplex structure. In the C—Au—C base pair observed in the RNA‐C4C9 duplex (Figure 1 ), a gold ion makes linear coordination with the N3 atoms of the C4 and C9 residues, suggesting that it should be a monovalent ion, Au(I). A possible reason for this result is that the trivalent ion Au(III) used for crystallization was reduced to Au(I) in situ during the crystallization process. This reduction may have been facilitated by the nucleobase environment, particularly electron‐rich purines such as guanine, which are capable of donating electrons to metal centers, as supported by sequence‐specific oxidation mechanisms observed in guanine‐rich sequences.[ 25 ] In addition, the presence of multivalent amines, such as spermine, in the crystallization buffer could contribute to the redox process as weak reducing agents.[ 26 ] Supporting this hypothesis, we observed a visible color change of crystals over time (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Crystals imaged 1 week after setup appeared colorless and transparent, while those examined 10 weeks later exhibited a distinct red hue. This coloration is consistent with the formation of colloidal gold nanoparticles in the solvent channels of the single crystals, suggesting that reduction of Au(III) had occurred gradually within the crystallization droplets, resulting in linear N3‐Au(I)‐N3 coordination to cytosine bases.

The Au(I)‐N3 distances observed in our structures (2.0–2.3 Å) are comparable to those reported for bis(pyridine)gold(I) complexes such as [Au(C6H7N)2]+, in which high‐resolution crystallographic analyses revealed Au—N bond lengths around 2.02–2.03 Å.[ 27 ] This agreement supports the assignment of a linear Au(I)‐N3 coordination geometry. The propeller twist angles of C—Au(I)—C base pairs are considerably larger (−32.0 to −18.5°) than those of canonical Watson‐Crick base pairs in A‐form RNA (12°), likely due to a lack of additional stabilizing interactions and repulsion between N4 amino groups. Similar structural features were found in the previously reported mercury‐ and silver‐mediated base pairs, with propeller twist angles ranging from −22° to −20° for T—Hg(II)—T and from −29° to −27° for C—Ag(I)—C, respectively.[ 14 , 15 ]

Incidentally, structural analysis was also conducted on the reddish crystals mentioned above. However, no electron density corresponding to gold nanoparticles was observed in the diffraction data. Nucleic acid crystals typically contain a large proportion of solvent channels, often occupying up to half of the crystal volume. It is possible that gold nanoparticles formed within these solvent channels and remained freely mobile. In such cases, they would not be visible in the averaged electron density maps.

C—G—Au(I)—C Triplets: Unexpected Base Triple Formation

Oligonucleotide DNA‐C6C7 (Table 1), designed to form a double helix structure containing two consecutive C—C mismatches at the center, was also cocrystallized with AuCl3. Unexpectedly, in the DNA‐C6C7 structure (Figure 2 ), the duplex was largely distorted, resulting in the formation of a unique duplex containing four C—G—C triplets at its center. In the C4—G9*—C7* and its symmetrically identical C7—G9—C4* base triplet (* indicates residues on the opposite strand), C4 forms the Watson‐Crick base pair with G9*, and C7* forms the cis Watson‐Crick/Hoogsteen base pair with G9* through N4(C7*)—H…;O6(G9*) and N3(C7*)—H…N7(G9*) hydrogen bonds. To form the base pair, the N3 position of C7* must be protonated. In the C5—G8*—C6* and its symmetrically identical C6—G8—C5* base triplet, C5 forms the Watson‐Crick base pair with G8*, and C6* forms the cis Watson‐Crick/Hoogsteen base pair with G8*. Interestingly, a gold ion is inserted into the cis Watson‐Crick/Hoogsteen C6*—G8* base pair, thereby creating a gold‐mediated C6*—Au—G8* base pair. Since the gold ion makes linear coordination N3(C6*)—Au—N7(G8*), it should be a monovalent ion, Au(I), as discussed above. The Au—N3(C6*) and Au—N7(G8*) bond lengths are 1.9 and 1.8 Å, respectively, which are shorter than those of Au—N3(C) observed in C—Au(I)—C base pairs.

Gold ions are illustrated as gold spheres with an anomalous difference map (pink mesh: 5σ contour level). N7(G)—Au(I)—N3(C) linear coordination and hydrogen bonds are shown as dashed lines.

Thermodynamic Evaluation of C—Au(I)—C Base Pair

To assess whether the formation of C—Au(I)—C base pairs contributes to duplex stability, we initially attempted melting temperature (T m) analysis using a DNA duplex containing two consecutive C—C mismatches in which both cytosine residues were in the 2′‐O‐methyl form (DNA‐CC in Table 1). This modification was introduced to favor the A‐form duplex structure, as observed in the crystal structure of the RNA‐C4C9/Au(I) complex. However, ultra violet (UV) melting experiments with Au(I) precursors proved technically impractical. Water‐soluble Au(I) sources such as K[Au(CN)2] are kinetically inert, showing limited ligand exchange. In contrast, more reactive Au(I) complexes like Me2SAuCl and Ph3PAuCl are poorly soluble in water and are not easy to use at micromolar concentrations required for T m analysis. Given these limitations, UV melting experiments to evaluate the contribution of Au(I) coordination could not be reliably performed. We therefore proceeded to investigate duplex stabilization using AuCl3, employing the same conditions as used for crystallization. As shown in Figure 3 , the addition of AuCl3 to the C—C mismatch‐containing DNA duplex did not result in a significant change in T m, with both the AuCl3‐free and AuCl3‐containing samples showing values of 44 °C, as determined by the midline method from the sigmoidal melting curves. This indicates that AuCl3 does not appreciably enhance the thermal stability of this duplex under the conditions tested. However, the post‐transition absorbance plateau (above ≈50 °C) in the presence of AuCl3 exhibits a noticeable downward shift compared to the control. A similar trend is also seen in melting curves reported for an Ag(I)‐mediated system by Green and coworkers.[ 23 ] While the origin of this downward shift remains unclear, it may reflect subtle structural changes or intermediate states induced by in situ reduction of Au(III).

Melting curves of DNA‐CC (left) and DNA‐2SC2SC (right) in the absence and presence of AuCl3. The relative absorbance was calculated using the equation A = (A t ºC−A min)/(A max−A min).

Au(III) Coordination with Thiocarbonyl‐Modified Cytosines

Given that gold has a high affinity for sulfur ligands, we hypothesized that introducing a thiocarbonyl group to cytosine could promote stronger and more stable coordination with gold. Therefore, we introduced 2‐thiocytosine (2SC), which is known as a modified base found in tRNA,[ 28 ] into DNA strands to form 2SC‐2SC mismatches (DNA‐2SC6 2SC7 in Table 1). In this oligonucleotide fragment, both 2‐thiocytosine residues were in the 2′‐O‐methyl form.

DNA‐2SC6 2SC7 was successfully cocrystallized with AuCl3, forming an A‐form duplex (Figure 4 ). In this structure, a gold ion makes square‐planar‐like coordination with the N3 and S2 atoms of the two 2SC residues, consistent with the coordination geometry of Au(III). The Au(III)—S distances are 2.3–2.4 Å, and the Au(III)—N3 distances are 2.8–2.9 Å, indicating stronger coordination through the sulfur atoms and a weaker interaction at the N3 positions. The square‐planar geometry and short Au—S distances suggest a stable coordination of Au(III) with thiocarbonyl groups, likely enhanced by the soft character of sulfur and its favorable orbital overlap with gold's 5d orbitals. The observed Au(III)—N3 distances are considerably longer than typical covalent Au(III)—N distances (≈2.0 Å),[ 29 ] suggesting that N3 may participate only in a secondary or semicoordinative fashion. While such an interaction is unusual in conventional Au(III) complexes, it may be structurally accommodated and stabilized by the helical DNA scaffold and the bidentate geometry of the 2‐thiocytosine residues.

Gold ions are illustrated as gold spheres with an anomalous difference map (pink mesh: 5σ contour level). S2—Au(III)—S2 N3—Au(III)—N3 square planar coordination is shown as dashed lines.

To test whether this interaction would translate into duplex stabilization, melting temperature analyses were performed for duplexes with 2SC—2SC mismatches in which both 2‐thiocytosine residues were in the 2′‐O‐methyl form (DNA‐2SC2SC in Table 1). The sequence is identical to that of the DNA‐CC duplex used above, except that cytosines were replaced by 2‐thiocytosines. Unlike the natural C—C mismatches, the addition of AuCl3 led to a substantial increase in T m for the 2SC‐containing duplex, demonstrating that chemical modification successfully enabled thermodynamically favorable gold binding (Figure 3). Interestingly, when sub‐stoichiometric amounts of AuCl3 (less than one equivalent per 2SC—2SC mismatch) were added, the melting curve exhibited a biphasic profile, suggesting partial formation of 2SC—Au(III)—2SC base pairs. In contrast, when equimolar amounts of AuCl3 were used, the melting transition became monophasic, indicating more uniform and complete base pair formation. These results suggest that full occupancy of the 2SC—Au(III)—2SC interactions requires at least one equivalent of gold per mismatch site, and that sub‐stoichiometric conditions result in heterogeneous duplex populations with varying thermal stabilities.

Conclusion

In the present study, we have provided experimental validation of these predictions by resolving crystal structures in which Au(I) coordinates to C—C mismatches and participates in G—Au—C base triple formation. Furthermore, melting temperature analyses revealed that Au(III) coordination to thiocarbonyl‐modified cytosines leads to substantial thermal stabilization. While previous studies have successfully employed artificial ligands to promote the coordination of metals other than Hg(II) and Ag(I) in DNA, our approach demonstrates that selective incorporation of Au(III) into natural base pairs can be achieved through a structure‐guided modification strategy.[ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 – 34 ] These findings highlight the importance of oxidation‐state control and base modification in enabling gold‐mediated base pairing.

The structural insights obtained here provide a foundation for the rational design of DNA‐gold hybrid nanomaterials and metal‐responsive nucleic acid architectures.[ 35 , 36 , 37 – 38 ] More broadly, our results suggest that metal‐mediated base pairing can be finely tuned by structural and chemical parameters, opening new opportunities for engineering dynamic, sequence‐programed nucleic acid devices. This knowledge could be applied in developing stimuli‐responsive DNA materials, biosensors, or metallonucleic acid frameworks with catalytic or electronic functions, thereby contributing to the growing field of DNA‐based nanotechnology.

Oligonucleotides containing 2′‐O‐methyl‐2‐thiocytosine used in this study were prepared as follows. First, the amidite unit of a uridine analog was synthesized (Figure 5 ). These amidites were incorporated into oligonucleotides by the phosphoramidite method, followed by postsynthetic conversion of the 4‐O‐dimethylphenyl group to an amino group (Figure 6 ). Synthesized oligonucleotides were listed in Table 1. The RNA and DNA sequences used for crystallization contain either two consecutive C—C or two spatially separated 2SC—2SC mismatches, configurations that have previously facilitated successful RNA and DNA crystallization in our studies.[ 15 ] In contrast, the sequences used in UV melting experiments were designed to position the mismatches consecutively in the central region of the duplex, enabling more reliable and cooperative thermal transitions.

Cocrystallization of oligonucleotides with AuCl3 was performed using the hanging‐drop vapor diffusion method. Crystallization droplets were prepared by mixing 1 μL of a sample solution containing RNA or DNA and AuCl3 with 1 μL of crystallization solution, and then equilibrated against 250 μL of 40% 2‐methyl‐2,4‐pentanediol. Optimized crystallization conditions are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. Single crystals were scooped up from the crystallization droplet using CryoLoops (Hampton Research) and flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen.

X‐ray datasets were collected at BL‐17A and BL‐5A in Photon Factory and then processed using XDS.[ 39 ] The initial phases were determined by the single anomalous dispersion method and the molecular replacement method using AutoSol and Phaser in the Phenix suite.[ 40 , 41 – 42 ] The molecular structure is constructed and manipulated with Coot.[ 43 ] The atomic parameters were refined using phenix.refine from the Phenix suite.[ 40 , 44 ] Observations and molecular drawings of the structure were done using PyMOL. The statistics of data collection and structure refinement are summarized in Table S2, Supporting Information. The atomic coordinates and experimental data of the present structures have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the 7EDU, 7EDV, and 9UYT.

Melting temperature analyses were carried out using an absorption spectrophotometer UV‐1650 PC (SHIMADZU) connected to a temperature controller. A buffer solution containing 10 mM MOPS (pH 7.0) and 100 mM NaClO4 was used in this experiment.
