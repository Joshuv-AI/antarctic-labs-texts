Effects of environmental complexity on cerebral gene expression in broiler chickens reared under different housing systems
Authors: Aneta Jaszczyk, Joanna Marchewka, Patryk Sztandarski, Anja B. Riber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Environmental complexity can influence neural plasticity, stress responses, and behavioral adaptation; however, its molecular effects in the avian brain remain poorly understood. The expression of 13 genes related to neuronal plasticity, neurotransmission, neuroendocrine regulation, cellular stress, and immune function was examined in broilers reared under four housing conditions differing in free-range access and environmental enrichment. Analyses were performed using pen level means with false discovery rate correction. Free-range access affected 12 genes, environmental enrichment affected five, and eight genes showed an interaction between the two factors. Broilers provided with both free-range access and environmental enrichment was associated with the highest expression of genes related to neuronal plasticity and glutamatergic signaling, whereas neuromodulatory, neuroendocrine, and immune related genes showed more variable responses. Principal component analysis showed the clearest separation between the control group and the group provided with both free-range access and environmental enrichment, whereas the indoor housing with environmental enrichment and free-range without environmental enrichment groups displayed more intermediate expression profiles. No sex related effects remained significant after correction. These findings identify candidate molecular correlates of broiler housing conditions.

Introduction

Environmental conditions are a major determinant of neural plasticity, behavioral regulation and stress physiology across animal species (Baroncelli et al., 2010; Mandolesi et al., 2017). Increasing evidence indicates that environmental complexity and environmental enrichment can influence brain structure and function through molecular and transcriptional mechanisms regulating neuronal plasticity and neuroendocrine responses (Baroncelli et al., 2010). In the present study, environmental enrichment refers specifically to the provision of wooden perches, whereas environmental complexity is used as a broader concept describing the increased diversity of sensory and behavioral stimuli resulting from environmental enrichment, free-range access, or their combination. The more general expression environmental conditions refers to the overall physical and management conditions under which the birds were reared. Birds represent a particularly informative model for studying these processes because they exhibit experience dependent neural plasticity and adult neurogenesis in several brain regions (Barnea and Pravosudov, 2011). In modern poultry production, however, broiler chickens are typically kept in intensive housing systems characterized by high stocking densities, rapid growth rates and limited environmental complexity, conditions that have been associated with compromised welfare outcomes including metabolic disorders, restricted behavioural expression and increased health problems (Bessei, 2006; Humphrey, 2006; Riber et al., 2025).

In contrast to conventional intensive systems, housing conditions that incorporate environmental enrichment or access to outdoor ranges have been shown to improve welfare indicators in broiler chickens by promoting natural behaviors and reducing stress (Hofmann et al., 2020; Riber et al., 2018; Sztandarski et al., 2025). Such environments can enhance behavioral flexibility, adaptive capacity and immune competence, and are increasingly implemented in response to growing societal expectations for improved animal welfare standards (Campbell et al., 2019; Campderrich et al., 2019). Despite these advances, the biological mechanisms through which environmental complexity influences welfare related phenotypes remain incompletely understood.

The brain plays a central role in mediating behavioral and physiological adaptation to environmental stimuli through dynamic regulation of gene expression. Genes involved in neuronal plasticity, learning and synaptic remodeling, including Gap43, Map2, Camk2a and Creb1, are key regulators of structural and functional plasticity in the vertebrate brain (Benowitz and Routtenberg, 1997; Dehmelt and Halpain, 2004; Lisman et al., 2012; Lonze and Ginty, 2002). In particular, the neurotrophin Bdnf, which regulates neuronal survival and synaptic growth, is highly responsive to environmental stimuli and has emerged as an important molecular mediator of experience dependent neural plasticity (Park and Poo, 2013).

Neurotransmission systems also play a key role in behavioural regulation and stress coping. In particular, subunits of the NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptor, including Grin1 and Grin2b, are essential for synaptic plasticity, learning and cognitive flexibility (Korinek et al., 2024). In parallel, monoaminergic pathways contribute to the regulation of behavioural responses to environmental challenges. Serotonergic and dopaminergic systems, modulated by Slc6a4 (encoding the serotonin transporter) and Th (tyrosine hydroxylase), respectively, influence stress responsiveness, behavioural reactivity and motivational processes (Fujita et al., 2023; Yamamoto and Vernier, 2011). Previous studies suggest that environmental enrichment can modulate these neurochemical systems, indicating a broader neurobiological basis for behavioural and physiological benefits observed in enriched environments (Campbell et al., 2019; Riber et al., 2018). Environmental conditions can also influence neuroendocrine and immune pathways involved in stress responses. Stress related genes such as Crh, a key regulator of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis, and Hsp90aa1, which encodes a molecular chaperone involved in cellular stress responses, have been shown to respond to environmental conditions, particularly under intensive production systems (Balakrishnan et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Environmental influences are also reflected in immune signalling pathways. Cytokines such as Il6 and acute phase proteins including Saa are sensitive indicators of inflammatory and physiological stress responses and can be modulated by environmental and management conditions in poultry (Hofmann et al., 2020; Pal Singh et al., 2020).

Despite growing interest in alternative housing systems, comprehensive characterization of molecular brain adaptations to environmental complexity in broiler genotypes remains limited. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for developing science-based strategies to improve animal welfare without compromising productivity.

In this study, we investigated telencephalic gene expression profiles associated with neuronal plasticity (Gap43, Camk2a, Creb1, Map2, Bdnf, Grin1, and Grin2b), neurotransmitter signaling (Th and Slc6a4), stress regulation (Crh and Hsp90aa1), and immune function (Il6 and Saa) in medium-growing Hubbard JA757 broilers reared under four housing conditions. We hypothesized that environmental enrichment and free-range access would be associated with differences in the expression of genes involved in neuronal plasticity, neurotransmission, stress regulation, and immune function. We further hypothesized that housing conditions providing greater environmental complexity would be associated with higher expression of genes related to neuronal plasticity and glutamatergic signaling, and that broilers provided with both environmental enrichment and free-range access would show the largest overall transcriptional differences relative to the control group. By integrating univariate and multivariate analyses, this study aimed to identify candidate molecular correlates of broiler housing conditions.

Material and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a controlled experiment involving four housing conditions differing in environmental complexity: indoor housing without environmental enrichment or free-range access (control), indoor housing with environmental enrichment (IF+E), free-range access without environmental enrichment (FR-E), and free-range access combined with environmental enrichment (FR+E). The replicate pens were randomly assigned to the four housing conditions, with three pens allocated to each condition. Broilers were randomly assigned to the replicate pens, with each pen containing three females and three males. The pen was considered as experimental unit, whereas individual broilers were considered observational units for gene expression measurements. The study aimed to assess the effects of environmental enrichment and free-range access on gene expression in the telencephalon.

Experiment location and animals

The experiment was conducted between September and October 2023 at the experimental farm of the Institute of Genetics and Animal Biotechnology, Polish Academy of Sciences, located in the Mazovian region of Poland (52.032404, 20.826490).

A total of 72 medium-growing broilers (Hubbard JA757) were used in the study. One day old broilers were assigned to four housing conditions, each of 18 broilers housed in three replicate pens. Each pen contained six broilers, including three females and three males. In this way, each housing condition was independently replicated in three pens.

Housing systems

Broilers were maintained under one of four housing conditions: control, IF+E, FR-E, or FR+E. Broilers were raised under the specified housing conditions for 56 days and were fed the same commercial starter, grower, and finisher diets. The 56-day rearing period was chosen to reflect the growth characteristics of the medium-growing Hubbard JA757 genotype. Feed presentation was adjusted according to the broilers’ age, in accordance with the breeder’s husbandry recommendations for Hubbard Premium chickens (Hubbard, 2022). Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experiment. The nutritional composition and feeding schedule of the diets are presented in Supplementary Table S1A, while detailed feed ingredient composition is provided in Supplementary Table S1B.

The indoor pens (1.5 m × 1.5 m) were covered with sawdust litter and equipped with one round feeder with an accessible rim diameter of approximately 33 cm and one bell drinker with a base diameter of 26.5 cm. With six broilers per pen, this corresponded to approximately 17.3 cm of feeder space and 13.9 cm of drinking space per bird. Natural daylight was the primary source of light and was provided through uncovered windows arranged symmetrically along the building, with one window positioned centrally above each pen. Broilers were exposed to the natural photoperiod, and light intensity at broiler level was not measured. During the initial brooding period, each pen was additionally equipped with a quartz infrared heat lamp emitting red light. The lamps were used to provide localized supplemental heat in accordance with standard broiler brooding practices, and their height was adjusted according to broiler age and behavior. The ambient temperature was maintained at approximately 33°C on the first day and at 31-33°C during the first week. Thereafter, the temperature was gradually reduced by approximately 2-3°C per week until it reached 22°C toward the end of the rearing period. Relative humidity was maintained between 50% and 70%. Chicks were vaccinated against Marek’s disease and Newcastle disease on the first day of life. From the third week of life, broilers from the free-range groups (FR-E and FR+E) had daily access to the outdoors from 7:00 to 19:00 through a separate pophole (45 cm × 50 cm) leading to a designated 30 m² (10 m × 3 m) pasture for each replicate pen. The pastures were botanically homogeneous, without trees or artificial shelters, and were cut a week before the start of the study. Each pasture was provided with the same type of bell drinker as that used in the indoor pens. Environmental enrichment in the IF+E and FR+E groups consisted of placing a two level wooden perch (length 0.6 m; height 10 cm and 30 cm), constructed of 50 mm × 50 mm wooden posts with rounded edges. The indoor pens and outdoor runs of the three identical replicates for each housing system were identical in terms of size, equipment, and management conditions. Reference transcriptomic datasets

Regional gene expression patterns in the chicken brain were examined descriptively using publicly available transcriptomic data from the Chicken Genotype-Tissue Expression Atlas (ChickenGTEx) portal (Hou et al., 2026). Expression profiles for selected genes were inspected across major telencephalic regions, including the hippocampus, hyperpallium, mesopallium, nidopallium and arcopallium. These datasets were used as reference information to illustrate regional heterogeneity of gene expression in the avian brain and to provide anatomical context for the interpretation of gene expression analyses performed in this study. The ChickenGTEx data were not combined with the experimental qPCR data, and no formal statistical comparisons among brain regions were performed. Tissue Collection and RNA Isolation

At 56 days of age, the broilers were euthanized by manual cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel in accordance with Council Regulation (EC) No. 1099/2009 and Directive 2010/63/EU. Manual cervical dislocation is permitted under European Union legislation for poultry weighing less than 3 kg and has been shown to result in a more rapid loss of brainstem reflexes than mechanical cervical dislocation in broilers (Jacobs et al., 2019). At the time of sampling, the mean body weight of the broilers was 2585 ± 230 g. After euthanasia, both cerebral hemispheres were removed, placed in cryogenic tubes, and frozen in liquid nitrogen within approximately 10 min. The samples were subsequently stored at −80°C for one month before RNA isolation and molecular analysis.

The frozen tissue was transferred directly into a homogenization tube containing 1.4 mm ceramic beads and the lysis buffer supplied with the Tissue RNA Mini Kit (Syngen, Poland). The samples were immediately homogenized, and total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each RNA preparation represented one broiler and consisted of tissue from both cerebral hemispheres. An on-column DNase I digestion step (Eurx, Poland) was included to remove residual genomic DNA. RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Transcription and Real-Time qPCR

From each sample, a total of 1000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the High Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Gene expression was analyzed by real-time qPCR using the LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Switzerland) and FastStart Essential DNA Green Master kit (Roche). The primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast, accessed August 1, 2024), targeting different exons and covering all transcript variants. Their specificity was confirmed by temperature gradient PCR (55-65°C) and gel electrophoresis.

Primer sequences, annealing temperatures and amplification efficiencies for all target genes used in this study (Gap43, Camk2a, Creb1, Map2, Bdnf, Grin1, Grin2b, Th, Slc6a4, Crh, Hsp90aa1, Il6, Saa) and the selected reference gene (Hprt1) are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The stability of four candidate reference genes (Actb, Gapdh, Hprt1, Ywhaz) was evaluated using geNorm software (https://genorm.cmgg.be/), and Hprt1 was selected as the most stable gene for normalization. Each qPCR reaction was performed in duplicate with a final volume of 20 µL. Each run included no-template controls and a 5-fold dilution series for efficiency calculation. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis (LightCycler 96 software v1.1). For each sample, target gene expression was normalized to the reference gene Hprt1 using the comparative Ct method. ΔCt was calculated as Ct_target - Ct_Hprt1. Mean ΔCt values obtained from the three qPCR runs were multiplied by −1 so that higher values indicated higher normalized gene expression.

Statistical analysis of data

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Statistical analyses were conducted using the mean -ΔCt value calculated for each broiler and gene. No technical measurements or biological observations were excluded.

The pen was treated as the experimental unit because housing conditions were applied at the pen level. For each gene, data from the six broilers within each pen were averaged to obtain one pen level value, resulting in 12 independent observations and three replicate pens per housing condition. The resulting pen level normalized expression values used for the primary statistical analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

The effects of free-range access, environmental enrichment, and their interaction were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Model assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests.

P values for each ANOVA effect were adjusted across the 13 genes using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Pairwise comparisons among the four housing conditions were based on estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment, followed by FDR correction across the 13 genes separately for each pairwise contrast. Adjusted P values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.

Sex related effects were evaluated using individual broiler data in models including housing condition, sex, and their interaction, with cluster-robust standard errors calculated at the pen level. P values were FDR-adjusted across the 13 genes separately for the sex effect and the housing condition x sex interaction. Principal component analysis was performed using centered and scaled individual -ΔCt values and was interpreted descriptively. Detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Regional gene expression patterns in the chicken telencephalon

The avian telencephalon comprises several anatomically and functionally distinct regions, including the hippocampus, hyperpallium, mesopallium, nidopallium, and arcopallium (Fig. 2A). To provide anatomical context for the interpretation of the present qPCR results, publicly available data from the Chicken Genotype-Tissue Expression Atlas (ChickenGTEx; Hou et al., 2026) were examined descriptively across these telencephalic regions (Fig. 2B). No formal statistical comparisons among brain regions were performed.

Based on visual inspection of the reference expression profiles, Gap43 appeared to show relatively higher expression in the nidopallium and arcopallium than in the hippocampus and hyperpallium. In contrast, Camk2a, Creb1, and Bdnf appeared to show relatively higher expression in the hippocampus and hyperpallium, whereas Map2 expression appeared relatively higher in the mesopallium and nidopallium. The NMDA receptor subunit genes also displayed region-dependent expression patterns in the reference dataset. Grin1 expression appeared relatively higher in the hyperpallium and nidopallium, whereas Grin2b appeared relatively higher in the nidopallium and arcopallium. The expression patterns of genes associated with neurotransmission and neuroendocrine regulation were more heterogeneous. Crh expression appeared relatively higher in the hippocampus and lower in the more lateral pallial regions, whereas Slc6a4 appeared relatively higher in the hippocampus and nidopallium. Th expression was generally low across the pallial regions, with comparatively higher expression in the arcopallium. Genes associated with inflammation and cellular stress, including Il6, Saa, and Hsp90aa1, showed generally low expression and limited apparent regional variation in the reference dataset.

Principal component analysis of gene expression profiles

Principal component analysis demonstrated treatment associated variation in the overall telencephalic gene expression profile (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S7). The first two principal components explained 67.3% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 50.4% and PC2 accounting for 16.9%. Control samples were characterized predominantly by negative PC1 and PC2 scores and formed a relatively distinct cluster. FR+E samples were located mainly at positive PC1 scores and close to zero on PC2, resulting in a clear separation from most Control samples along PC1. FR-E samples occupied an intermediate position, with PC1 scores distributed around zero and consistently positive PC2 scores. Discussion

Housing conditions were associated with distinct and gene specific transcriptional patterns in the telencephalon of medium-growing broilers. After correction for multiple testing, free-range access was the most frequently significant experimental factor, affecting 12 of the 13 analyzed genes. Environmental enrichment significantly affected 5 genes, while 8 genes showed a significant interaction between free-range access and environmental enrichment. These interactions indicate that the effect of enrichment frequently depended on whether broilers also had access to the outdoor range. Therefore, the observed responses cannot be interpreted as a simple linear increase with environmental complexity.

The FR+E group frequently showed the highest expression of genes associated with neuronal plasticity and glutamatergic signaling. Principal component analysis also showed the greatest separation between the control and FR+E groups, whereas IF+E and FR-E generally displayed more intermediate or heterogeneous expression profiles. All interpretations presented below are based on effects that remained significant after FDR correction. Overall, the findings identify candidate molecular correlates of housing conditions but should not be considered direct evidence of changes in cognition, adaptive capacity, or welfare. Previous research has shown that exposure to more complex environments may influence multiple interconnected molecular and cellular processes in the brain, including pathways involved in neuronal plasticity, synaptic function, and responses to environmental stimulation (Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; van Praag et al., 2000).

Neuronal plasticity associated gene expression across housing conditions

Genes associated with neuronal plasticity, synaptic function, and structural remodeling showed pronounced but distinct responses to the housing treatments. Expression of Gap43 was higher in both free-range groups than in the two indoor groups, regardless of whether environmental enrichment was provided. This pattern suggests that free-range access was the primary factor associated with Gap43 expression. As Gap43 is involved in axonal growth, synaptic remodeling, and activity dependent neuronal plasticity, its higher expression may reflect increased engagement of molecular processes related to structural neuronal adaptability in birds exposed to a more variable outdoor environment (Benowitz and Routtenberg, 1997; Mercerón-Martínez et al., 2018).

Expression of Camk2a was higher in all three modified housing treatments than in the control group, while FR+E showed higher expression than either IF+E or FR-E. The absence of a significant interaction, together with significant main effects of free-range access and environmental enrichment, is consistent with contributions from both environmental components. Camk2a is an important mediator of calcium-dependent synaptic signaling and long-term potentiation, and its activity is closely associated with experience dependent modification of synaptic strength (Lisman et al., 2012). The observed expression pattern therefore suggests that either environmental enrichment or free-range access may provide stimulation relevant to neuronal activity, while their combined provision was associated with the strongest response.

A different pattern was observed for Creb1. Higher expression was detected only in FR+E, which differed from all other treatments. The significant interaction between free-range access and environmental enrichment indicates that neither environmental component alone produced the same response as their combined provision. Creb1 encodes a transcription factor involved in activity dependent gene regulation, memory related signaling, and long-term neuronal adaptation (Lonze and Ginty, 2002; Sakamoto et al., 2011). Its selectively higher expression in FR+E may therefore reflect the combined sensory, spatial, social, and motor demands associated with simultaneous access to environmental enrichment and an outdoor range (van Praag et al., 2000).

Although a significant main effect of free-range access was detected for Map2, pairwise comparisons identified a difference only between FR+E and IF+E. The control and FR-E groups had intermediate expression levels and did not differ significantly from the other treatments. Therefore, the result does not support a general increase in Map2 expression across all free-range conditions. Instead, it indicates a more limited treatment associated difference that should be interpreted cautiously. Because Map2 contributes to microtubule stabilization and dendritic organization, its higher expression in FR+E relative to IF+E may be compatible with differences in dendritic structural regulation. However, direct evidence of morphological remodeling would require histological confirmation (Dehmelt and Halpain, 2004).

Expression of Bdnf was higher in all modified housing treatments than in the control group. FR+E also showed higher expression than IF+E, while FR-E displayed an intermediate value and did not differ significantly from either enriched treatment. This pattern suggests that both environmental enrichment and free-range access can influence Bdnf expression, although the significant interaction demonstrates that their effects were not strictly additive. Bdnf plays a central role in neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and experience dependent modification of neural circuits (Park and Poo, 2013). Its consistently higher expression outside the standard control condition supports the sensitivity of neurotrophic signaling to environmental stimulation (Zhao et al., 2014).

In summary, the results for Gap43, Camk2a, Creb1, Map2, and Bdnf are compatible with housing associated differences in the molecular regulation of neuronal plasticity. Previous studies have similarly indicated that enriched or more complex environments may modify neural development, exploratory behavior, and cognitive performance in poultry (Kliphuis et al., 2024; Krause et al., 2006; Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; van Praag et al., 2000). Changes in neuroplasticity related pathways have also been discussed in relation to learning ability, fear responses, behavioral flexibility, and adaptation to environmental stimulation in broilers and other avian models (Drigas et al., 2018). In addition, behavioral studies have shown that environmental enrichment can be associated with greater exploratory activity, altered fear responses, and other welfare relevant outcomes in broilers (Riber et al., 2018). These findings provide the biological context for the present findings on transcription. However, higher levels of gene expression should not be interpreted as direct evidence of improved learning, enhanced cognitive function, reduced anxiety, or structural remodeling of neurons. Such interpretations would require corresponding behavioral, morphological, electrophysiological, and protein level measurements in the same experimental birds.

Neurotransmission and neuroendocrine related gene expression across housing conditions

Genes associated with glutamatergic, monoaminergic, and neuroendocrine signaling showed distinct responses to the housing treatments. The NMDA receptor subunit genes Grin1 and Grin2b were generally expressed at higher levels in the modified housing treatments than in the control group, whereas Th, Slc6a4, and Crh displayed more context dependent patterns associated with the interaction between free-range access and environmental enrichment (Campbell et al., 2018; Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; van Praag et al., 2000).

Expression of Grin1 was higher in IF+E, FR-E, and FR+E than in the control group, with no significant differences among the three modified treatments. This pattern suggests that either environmental enrichment or free-range access was sufficient to alter Grin1 expression relative to the standard indoor environment. However, the significant interaction indicates that the effects of free-range access and environmental enrichment were not fully independent. Expression of Grin2b was also higher in all modified housing treatments than in the control group. In contrast to Grin1, the highest expression was observed in FR+E, which differed from all other treatments. The significant main effects of both free-range access and environmental enrichment, together with the absence of a significant interaction, are consistent with contributions from both environmental components to Grin2b expression.

The NMDA receptor plays a central role in experience-dependent synaptic modification and long-term potentiation (Cull-Candy et al., 2001). Grin1 encodes an obligatory NMDA receptor subunit, whereas Grin2b contributes to receptor kinetics, developmental regulation, and synaptic plasticity (Cull-Candy et al., 2001). Increased Grin2b expression has been associated with learning related processes and enhanced synaptic plasticity in experimental models (Campbell et al., 2018; Chaudhury et al., 2010). Research also indicates that glutamate signaling and NMDA receptor regulation are sensitive to environmental experiences and neuronal activity (Korinek et al., 2024).

The present results suggest that both environmental enrichment and free-range access may provide stimulation capable of modifying glutamatergic receptor gene expression, with the combined FR+E condition producing the strongest response for Grin2b. This interpretation is consistent with the broader concept that environmental complexity can influence molecular pathways involved in experience dependent neural plasticity (Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; van Praag et al., 2000). Nevertheless, higher levels of gene expression alone do not establish changes in receptor number, subunit composition, receptor localization, or glutamatergic activity. These findings should therefore be interpreted as housing associated transcriptional variation rather than direct evidence of enhanced excitatory neurotransmission or learning capacity.

In contrast to the relatively consistent responses of the NMDA receptor subunit genes, the expression patterns of Th, Slc6a4, and Crh were more complex. All three genes showed a significant interaction between free-range access and environmental enrichment, indicating that their responses depended on the particular combination of housing resources.

Expression of Th was lower in IF+E than in the control group. In contrast, FR+E showed higher expression than both IF+E and FR-E, while the control group had an intermediate value and did not differ significantly from either free-range treatment. Therefore, Th did not show a uniform increase or decrease with increasing environmental complexity. As Th encodes the rate limiting enzyme in catecholamine synthesis, these differences may reflect context dependent regulation of dopaminergic or other catecholaminergic pathways rather than a simple directional response to environmental enrichment or outdoor access (Yamamoto and Vernier, 2011). Catecholaminergic signaling contributes to the regulation of motivation, activity, stress responsiveness, and behavioral adaptation, although its effects depend on the neural region and physiological context in which changes occur (Decuypere et al., 2000; Yamamoto and Vernier, 2011).

Similarly, Slc6a4 expression was higher in FR+E than in IF+E and FR-E, but it did not differ significantly from the control group. Consequently, the results do not demonstrate a general increase in serotonin transporter expression relative to standard housing. Instead, they indicate that the association between environmental enrichment and Slc6a4 expression differed between indoor and free-range conditions. Serotonergic signaling contributes to behavioral regulation, emotional reactivity, fear responses, and adaptation to environmental stimuli in poultry, but its biological consequences depend on brain region, developmental stage, and physiological context (Altan et al., 2013; Decuypere et al., 2000; Popa et al., 2022).

Expression of Crh was lower in IF+E than in the control group, while FR+E showed higher expression than both IF+E and FR-E. The control group again displayed an intermediate value and did not differ significantly from either free-range group. Therefore, the results do not support the interpretation that free-range access without enrichment specifically reduced Crh expression relative to the control treatment. Instead, they indicate that the association between environmental enrichment and Crh expression differed between indoor and free-range conditions.

Environmental stimulation does not necessarily produce uniform activation or suppression of neuroendocrine pathways. Rather, the direction and magnitude of the response may depend on the type, intensity, predictability, and duration of environmental exposure, as well as the physiological and developmental state of the animal (Campbell et al., 2018; Løtvedt et al., 2017; Smith and Vale, 2006; Yang et al., 2022). Crh is an important component of neuroendocrine stress regulation, particularly within hypothalamic circuits involved in activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (Smith and Vale, 2006). However, the present measurements were obtained from the telencephalon rather than the hypothalamus. The observed differences should therefore not be directly interpreted as activation or suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Overall, the responses of the analyzed neurotransmission and neuroendocrine related genes were not uniformly directed across housing conditions. While Grin1 and Grin2b generally showed higher expression in the modified housing treatments, Th, Slc6a4, and Crh displayed interaction dependent and non-monotonic patterns. Previous studies indicate that glutamatergic, catecholaminergic, serotonergic, and neuroendocrine pathways may all contribute to responses to environmental stimulation, but changes within these systems cannot be assigned a universally positive or negative biological meaning (Altan et al., 2013; Campbell et al., 2018; Decuypere et al., 2000; Løtvedt et al., 2017; Popa et al., 2022; Smith and Vale, 2006; Yang et al., 2022). To determine the functional significance of the observed transcriptional differences, it would therefore be necessary to conduct additional measurements of receptor abundance and activity, neurotransmitter concentrations, blood hormone levels, and behavior.

Cellular stress, inflammation, and acute phase related gene expression across housing conditions

Genes associated with cellular stress and immune regulation also displayed distinct treatment dependent expression patterns. Expression of Hsp90aa1 was higher in both free-range groups than in the control group, while IF+E showed an intermediate value. The similarity between FR-E and FR+E indicates that outdoor access, rather than environmental enrichment, was the primary factor associated with this response. Hsp90aa1 encodes a molecular chaperone involved in protein folding, cellular homeostasis, and responses to physiological and environmental challenges. Its higher expression in free-range birds may reflect increased cellular demand for proteostasis under more variable environmental conditions rather than necessarily indicating harmful stress. Heat shock protein expression may contribute to maintaining cellular function during thermal, metabolic, oxidative, or other environmental challenges (Rimoldi et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2024).

Expression of Il6 was lower in all three modified housing treatments than in the control group. The lowest expression was observed in IF+E, which also differed from FR+E. The significant interaction indicates that the effect of environmental enrichment on Il6 depended on access to the outdoor range. Although reduced Il6 transcript abundance may be compatible with lower local proinflammatory signaling, it cannot by itself demonstrate a systemic anti-inflammatory state. Il6 has context dependent functions in immune regulation, neural signaling, metabolism, and tissue homeostasis, and its biological effects also depend on protein concentration, receptor signaling, and the wider cytokine environment (Guo et al., 2020; Prakasam et al., 2013).

The interpretation of Saa also requires caution. FR+E showed higher expression than IF+E and FR-E, but it did not differ significantly from the control group. Therefore, the results do not support the conclusion that FR+E induced an acute phase response relative to standard housing. Rather, they indicate that Saa expression differed between the combined treatment and treatments providing only one environmental component. Because Saa may respond to inflammation, metabolic regulation, tissue remodeling, and environmental exposure, its expression should be interpreted alongside additional inflammatory and physiological indicators (Pal Singh et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024).

Overall, the expression patterns of Hsp90aa1, Il6, and Saa suggest that housing conditions influenced cellular stress and immune related transcription, but these responses were gene specific and not uniformly directed. The data therefore do not indicate generalized activation or suppression of immune pathways. Previous work has suggested that neural plasticity and immune related pathways may respond simultaneously to environmental conditions while remaining partly independently regulated (Hofmann et al., 2020). In the present study the limited targeted gene panel and absence of formal network analysis do not allow conclusions regarding coordinated gene modules or direct interactions between neural and immune pathways. Protein level measurements and a broader panel of inflammatory, oxidative, and metabolic markers would be required to determine the functional significance of these transcriptional differences.

Principal component analysis of gene expression profiles

Principal component analysis supported treatment-associated variation in the overall telencephalic gene expression profile. PC1 and PC2 together explained 67.3% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 50.4% and PC2 accounting for 16.9%. Control samples were located predominantly at negative PC1 and PC2 scores, whereas FR+E samples were generally characterized by positive PC1 scores and PC2 scores close to zero. This produced the clearest separation between the control and FR+E groups.

FR-E samples occupied a more intermediate position, with PC1 scores distributed around zero and generally positive PC2 scores. IF+E samples also tended to have positive PC2 scores but displayed greater dispersion along PC1. Therefore, the PCA does not demonstrate complete separation among all four treatments. Instead, it indicates that the greatest multigene difference occurred between the standard control environment and the treatment combining free-range access with environmental enrichment, while the treatments providing only one environmental component showed more intermediate or heterogeneous expression profiles.

These patterns are consistent with the view that environmental stimulation may affect multiple functionally related molecular pathways rather than a single isolated response (Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; van Praag et al., 2000). However, because the PCA was based on individual biological samples, whereas housing treatments were assigned at the pen level, it should be regarded as a descriptive visualization of gene expression patterns rather than an independent inferential test of treatment effects. Its overall structure was nevertheless consistent with the factorial analyses, particularly the frequent differences between the control and FR+E groups.

Sex related variation in gene expression

After FDR correction, neither the main effect of sex nor the interaction between housing treatment and sex was significant for any of the analyzed genes. The present results therefore provide no evidence that sex consistently influenced telencephalic expression of the selected genes or modified their responses to the housing treatments.

Previous poultry studies have reported that environmental and management conditions may influence neural, behavioral, or physiological outcomes in both males and females, although the relative contribution of sex may vary among traits and experimental contexts (Riber et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2014). The present findings should therefore not be interpreted as demonstrating that male and female broilers have identical neurobiological responses. Rather, any sex related effects in this dataset were smaller or less consistent than the treatment associated differences and could not be distinguished statistically after correction for multiple testing. Studies including a greater number of independent experimental units may be required to detect more subtle sex dependent responses.

Implications for poultry welfare and management

The present study indicates that housing conditions were associated with molecular variation in pathways related to neuronal plasticity, glutamatergic signaling, neuromodulation, cellular stress, and immune regulation. The frequently higher expression of neuronal plasticity and neurotransmission related genes in FR+E suggests that the combined provision of free-range access and environmental enrichment was associated with a distinct telencephalic transcriptional profile. Previous studies have similarly indicated that greater environmental complexity may influence neural plasticity, behavioral flexibility, and welfare related responses in poultry (Campbell et al., 2019; Hofmann et al., 2020; Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006; Riber et al., 2018). However, the significant interactions and non-monotonic expression patterns observed for several genes demonstrate that the effects of free-range access and environmental enrichment cannot be interpreted as simple progression from less complex to more complex housing.

The present molecular findings may be considered alongside phenotypic observations from our previous work with the Hubbard JA757 genotype. Under comparable housing conditions, access to open space and environmental enrichment was associated with reduced footpad dermatitis, fewer comb wounds and skin injuries, cleaner plumage, and improved locomotor performance (Sztandarski et al., 2025). These earlier observations provide relevant contextual support for the present findings. However, because the phenotypic and transcriptional results were obtained in separate studies rather than from the same individual birds, they cannot be directly linked or interpreted as evidence that the observed gene expression differences mediated the previously reported welfare outcomes.

Gene expression differences should not be considered direct evidence of a positive or negative welfare state. Their functional consequences depend on the specific gene, tissue, cellular context, timing of measurement, and corresponding protein activity. Accordingly, the analyzed transcripts should be regarded as candidate molecular correlates of housing conditions rather than validated biomarkers of poultry welfare. Brain gene expression profiling may complement behavioral, health, and physiological indicators by providing information on internal biological responses that may not be fully captured by external observations. However, its value for welfare assessment requires validation against behavioral and physiological outcomes measured within the same experimental population (Campderrich et al., 2019; Nithianantharajah and Hannan, 2006).

Study limitations

Several aspects of the experimental design should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Housing treatments were applied at the pen level, and statistical inference was therefore based on pen level means. Although this approach accounted for the appropriate experimental unit, each housing condition included only three independent replicate pens. This relatively small number of pen level replicates limits the precision of the estimated treatment effects and may have reduced the ability to detect smaller differences among housing conditions. Birds within the same pen shared the same physical and social environment and should therefore be regarded as observational units rather than independent treatment replicates.

All replicate pens were located within the same building and arranged in a single row. Although the pens were randomly assigned to the four housing conditions and solid partitions prevented visual contact between birds housed in adjacent pens, minor position related differences in natural light exposure, airflow, noise, or proximity to the outdoor access points could not be completely excluded. Such differences may have contributed to variation among replicate pens.

Principal component analysis was conducted using measurements from individual birds and should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive visualization of gene expression patterns rather than as an independent inferential test of treatment effects. In addition, gene expression was measured at a single time point and only at the mRNA level. Consequently, the present study cannot determine whether the observed transcriptional differences resulted in corresponding changes in protein abundance, receptor activity, neural morphology, physiological responses, or behavior. The targeted qPCR panel also represented only a limited selection of genes and did not capture the complete transcriptional response to the housing conditions.

Gene expression was analyzed in whole telencephalic tissue obtained from both cerebral hemispheres. Consequently, the observed transcriptional differences cannot be attributed to specific telencephalic regions, neural circuits, cell populations, or cerebral hemispheres. Interpretations relating individual genes to region specific neurobiological functions should therefore be considered tentative. This limitation is particularly important for Crh, because the analyzed tissue did not include region specific sampling of the hypothalamus. Differences in telencephalic Crh expression should therefore not be interpreted as direct evidence of altered hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity.

Future directions

Future studies should consider integrating longitudinal sampling and multi-omics approaches (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics), as well as correlating molecular outcomes with behavioral and physiological indicators of welfare for validation. Such comprehensive analyses could help evaluate candidate molecular markers of negative and positive welfare states and guide the development of science based husbandry practices that optimize both productivity and animal welfare. Additionally, exploring these adaptations across different broiler genotypes is essential, as slow- and medium-growing birds may exhibit distinct capacities for environmental responsiveness compared to fast-growing genotypes (Burt, 2005; David et al., 2017; Fulton, 2012; Ghayas et al., 2020).

Conclusions

Housing conditions were associated with distinct and gene specific transcriptional patterns in the telencephalon of medium-growing broilers. Free-range access was the most frequently significant experimental factor, while environmental enrichment affected selected genes and frequently interacted with free-range access. The FR+E condition was commonly associated with the highest expression of genes related to neuronal plasticity and glutamatergic signaling, although several neuromodulatory, neuroendocrine, and immune-related genes showed context-dependent and nonmonotonic responses.

These findings identify candidate molecular correlates of broiler housing conditions and suggest that free-range access and environmental enrichment are associated with variation in multiple neural and physiological pathways. Brain gene expression profiling may provide a complementary approach for investigating the biological effects of housing systems, but its relevance to welfare assessment requires validation against behavioral, physiological, health, and protein level outcomes measured within the same experimental population.
