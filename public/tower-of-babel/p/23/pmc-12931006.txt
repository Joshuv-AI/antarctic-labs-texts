Patient-centred postpartum care provision: virtual delivery, tiered escalation, and tensions between efficiency, preference, and clinical concern
Authors: Sergio A Silverio, Julia R B Brown, Vicky Bowyer, Carole H Sudre, Gillian Horgan, Tisha Dasgupta, The RESILIENT Study Group, The COVID Symptom Study Biobank Consortium, Peter von Dadelszen, Laura A Magee, Emma L Duncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGood quality postnatal care is of key importance for maternal and infant health. During the pandemic, healthcare services were reconfigured with increased virtual delivery, reducing infection transmission risk for pregnant women, new mothers, newborn babies, and healthcare workers. Post-pandemic, this shift of maternity care from face-to-face towards virtual care provision has persisted. Whilst some have extolled this trend, the evidence is mixed, both for clinical outcomes and for patient perceptions and experiences. We sought to explore the experiences and perceptions of women affected by these changes, to inform ongoing maternity services development.MethodsThis study reports responses of women participating in the COVID Symptom Study Biobank, drawn from the King’s College London/ZOE COVID Symptom Study, representing a convenience sample of UK women of reproductive age (18–50 years). Participants were invited to complete an online questionnaire, from 7 September to 1 December 2021. Demographic analyses are presented for all respondents. Content analysis was used to analyse all free-text responses.ResultsOverall, 1036 respondents delivered at least one live baby during the pandemic, of whom 821 respondents provided a total of 1466 qualitative responses about their care in-person (n = 550 responses), by video (n = 125), or by telephone (n = 791). Mothers liked the convenience and promptness of virtual care, and generally felt it was a good way to meet their healthcare needs, with two caveats: First, they appreciated the option to escalate care quickly (i.e. a tiered process), which seemed to occur with little difficulty in most cases; second, they perceived physical examination as very important in certain circumstances, particularly when a baby was unwell.ConclusionsOur findings show women are pragmatic and reasonable about the benefits and risks of differing modes of postpartum care delivery, including virtual care. Our study supports a tiered approach to the provision of postnatal care, which balances perceived efficiencies, preferences of the individual seeking care, and level of clinical concern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04683-5.Keywords: Postnatal care, Pandemic, Virtual care, Telehealth, Mixed methods, Survey

Background

The postpartum period is a high-risk time for mothers and babies. Globally, more than half of all maternal deaths occur in the six weeks after birth [1]. Similarly, half of paediatric deaths under five years occur in the first 28 days of life, amounting to over two million babies annually [2]. A strong evidence-base, and WHO guidelines, support structured high-quality postnatal care as an effective means of minimising maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [3]. Such care involves monitoring maternal recovery from pregnancy and birth, maternal mental health and social risk factors, and infant well-being (including breastfeeding), as well as planning contraception and any further care which mothers and babies may require. Recent systematic reviews and consensus-building workshops identified 22 key components of postnatal care, twelve relating directly to the mother and ten to the newborn [3]. Unsurprisingly, the provision and quality of postnatal care varies across health care settings. The UK schedule of postnatal care (per National Institute for Clinical Excellence [NICE] guidelines) is usually delivered in the first week after birth by community midwives, by health visitors (specialised nurses) in following weeks, and finally by primary care physicians for the mother at 6–8 week postpartum [4]. Yet despite such guidelines, fewer women receive the recommended structured postnatal care compared with similarly recommended structured antenatal care [5].

In common with multiple other healthcare components, the recent pandemic brought many changes to routine postnatal care, without prior stakeholder group consultation, testing, or evaluation and augmentation. These changes included the following: (i) reduced in-person care; (ii) increased use of virtual care (telephone and/or video calls); (iii) restrictions on partners and birth companions accompanying the women, for community visits and hospital care, including postnatally [6–9]; and (iv) delays in maternal and neonatal immunisations [10–14]. Some components, particularly partner exclusions, were viewed extremely negatively by women [15–17]; and evidence has suggested a move to virtual modalities of care reduced women’s agency and access to maternity-related healthcare [18], whilst also being more costly for health services [19]. Further, whilst some changes were dictated by pandemic-specific measures, other changes were accelerations of pre-pandemic initiatives (e.g. increasing implementation of digital healthcare) and not necessarily driven by innovation and quality improvement [20]. Whilst many policies have returned to pre-pandemic postnatal practices (including care visit frequency and participation of partners throughout the maternity journey), increased virtual postnatal care has been retained as part of routine practice.

Here, we present data from a large cohort of women who gave birth early in the pandemic. We evaluate their experiences of different modes of postnatal care delivery, particularly virtual care, to inform and refine current and future postnatal digital care practices.

Methods

Design and governance

We took a pragmatic approach to this study [21, 22], recognising ontologically, people see the world in different, and sometimes conflicting, ways, and a single view cannot explain everything. With this, we also accept epistemologically, people’s experiences and knowledge are real and can be studied, whilst considering how these may change over time and across cultures. Thus, gathering many opinions via a large on-line survey was the most appropriate methodologically [23], and the decision to analyse the survey using a content analysis was the most appropriate analytic decision [24].

Participants were recruited from the King’s College London/ZOE COVID Symptom Study [CSS], launched jointly by ZOE Ltd. (London, UK) and King's College London researchers on 24 March 2020, which tracked symptoms, SARS-CoV-2 testing and results, vaccination, and other COVID-19 related events via the KCL-CSS/ZOE app (Research Ethics Committee ref:-LRS-19/20–18210) [25].

Separately, the COVID Symptom Study Biobank was established by King's College London researchers in September 2020, to study longer term effects of the pandemic (CSSB, Research Ethics Committee ref:−20/YH/0298), with participants recruited via specific invitation from KCL-ZOE CSS study participants. The ‘Pregnancy Planning, the Pandemic, and Me’ Study was approved by the CSSB governance committee in May 2021 (ref:−0043).

Recruitment and data collection

A convenience sample of women of reproductive age (18–50 years) in the UK who were current KCL-ZOE app users were invited by personal e-mail to join CSSB. The invitation highlighted the aims of capturing the views of women considering pregnancy, currently pregnant, or recently pregnant. After the initial trial (n = 1000 invitations), all remaining eligible women were invited (n = 81,625) with one reminder to non-responders after a fortnight; recruitment closed on 1 December 2021 with 4042 women joining CSSB. Additionally, 2470 women of reproductive age were already members of CSSB. After receiving detailed information for this particular study, 3219 newly-recruited women, and 349 existing CSSB participants provided informed consent for the current study, which included the use of logged CSS-ZOE app data.

Participants were invited to complete an online questionnaire, created and administered using Qualtrics survey software, from 7 September to 1 December 2021. Three e-mail reminders were sent before data collection closed. The bespoke questionnaire (due to pandemic rapid response) included the following: demographics, pregnancy and postpartum experiences, SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination, and open-ended questions with free-text responses.

Data analyses

The survey from which these data have been derived was mixed methods in design, however, in order to answer this particular research question, we have only drawn upon the qualitative free-text responses and therefore present a purely qualitative analysis, save the demographic data of the participants which is presented for context of the respondents (i.e. women of reproductive age). For the purposes of the present analysis, quantitative demographic data are only presented for information about the sample population. Data for demographics, pregnancy outcomes, and postpartum experiences are therefore presented descriptively.

Free-text responses to specific questions about modalities of healthcare service delivery (in-person; video; telephone) were analysed using a directed content analysis [26]. This approach is on the boundary of inductive and deductive methods, whereby a coding matrix can be created based on pre-determined codes. All text was read and assigned, where possible, to pre-determined codes; any uncoded text was assigned new codes. Long responses could either be broken down and coded separately if the data pertained to different codes, or could be treated as a whole, as appropriate. Participants who provided more than a singular point or response were analysed per the point being made, and not as a whole piece of text (i.e. a singular piece of text could be coded to different codes and therefore be represented in multiple coding clusters). Data were cleaned of word fragmentations and anonymised where identifiable names or places were mentioned, but otherwise left in participants’ parlance. Participants could leave free-text comments at various points in the survey, but no preference was given to participants who had provided data at every point compared to those who provided fewer data points. Data were not stratified according to quantitative demographic data, as this is not typical of qualitative content analyses. Codes were revised iteratively until finalised, eventually achieving 100% analytical coverage (i.e. all data were coded). Findings were ordered in decreasing frequency of occurrence, within service delivery modality, rendering three coding clusters of care provision: in-person; video; and telephone.

Results

Demographic analysis of quantitative data

Demographic data for all women of reproductive age invited to participate (n = 85,092), those recruited (n = 3568), and those who completed the survey (N = 3,453; 96.8% of recruited individuals) have been published [27]. Recruited participants were extremely similar to the overall cohort with respect to key demographic criteria, including age, Body Mass Index (BMI), ethnicity and Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD). As above, our invitation particularly solicited women considering pregnancy, currently pregnant, or recently pregnant during the pandemic. At the time of questionnaire completion, 1250/3453 (36.2%) questionnaire participants had experienced a pandemic pregnancy, and 1036 had delivered at least one liveborn during the pandemic; 851/1250 (68.1%) respondents provided qualitative comments regarding their experiences of postpartum care, including video and telephone virtual care, and breastfeeding (Additional File, Table S1).

In brief, these 851 respondents were a Mean ± SD of 36.6 ± 3.7 years, with BMI of 25.4 ± 4.9 kg/m2. The vast majority (809, 95.1%) were of White ethnicity. The distribution of the IMD was shifted towards those with less social deprivation: quintile 5 (251, 29.5%), 4 (196, 23.0%), 3 (187, 22.0%), 2 (134, 15.7%), and 1 (most deprived, 60, 7.0%). Few women (< 4%) reported co-morbidities, other than asthma or other respiratory disease (107, 12.6%). Mental health screening was not usually positive; with low levels of reported depression (47, 5.5%) and anxiety (105, 12.3%), but more than half (521, 61.2%) of women had concerns about their mental health or emotional wellbeing, most (559, 65.7%) having no history of a mental health condition. These 851 respondents were similar to all respondents who reported pandemic pregnancy, as well as those with a pandemic pregnancy who delivered liveborns.

Data were available for 1012 (97.7%) of the 1036 respondents who reported pandemic pregnancies with liveborns (Additional File, Table S3). For women who reported data for two babies (n = 11), five were part of a twin pregnancy. Most women (678, 67.7%) giving birth to singletons experienced uncomplicated pregnancy; complications were most commonly: preterm birth (49, 4.9%), hypertensive disorder (81, 8.1%), gestational diabetes (80, 8.0%), or urine infection (78, 7.8%). Almost one-third of women (307, 30.7%) were induced. Mode of birth was usually vaginal (657, 64.9%), and in hospital (899, 89.8%). Mean ± SD birthweight was 3439.8 ± 565.0g; and few babies required neonatal care (73, 7.3%). Most babies had no serious problems after discharge (793, 79.2%; Additional File, Table S4). Whilst jaundice was a common concern (409, 40.9%), virtually all babies (379/409, 92.7%) were examined.

The vast majority of babies (909, 90.8%) were seen face-to-face by eight weeks after birth. For most babies (765, 76.43%), vaccinations were easily booked. Calls to NHS 111 or urgent requests for a GP appointment were common (515, 51.4%), and visits to the emergency department or walk-in centres (301, 30.1%) were most commonly on the advice of NHS 111 (139/301, 46.2%), or the GP (55/301, 18.3%). Most women (576, 57.5%) had at least one other child at home. After the pandemic, most women expressed a strong preference for their child to be seen in-person (868, 86.7%), rather than by video call (56, 5.6%) or telephone (52, 5.2%).

Whilst most women (796, 79.5%) were visited at home by the community midwife, visits to the GP, walk-in health centre, or hospital urgently for care in the six weeks after birth were not infrequent (293, 29.3%; Additional File, Table S5). By six weeks’ postpartum, a minority (98, 9.8%) women spent at least one night in hospital after primary discharge home. Most had stitches after birth (831, 83.0%), but some experienced infection (104/831, 12.5%), which was usually treated with antibiotics (96/104, 92.3%), rather than opening the wound (2/96, 2.1%). Most women were offered a six-week health check with the GP (784, 78.3%), usually in person (620/784, 79.1%), but also via telephone call (152/784, 19.4%); just over half of women (447/784, 57.0%) were satisfied with this arrangement, whilst others were neutral (146/784, 18.6%) or dissatisfied (187/784, 23.9%).

Virtually all women breastfed their babies; only a few women (53, 5.3%) reported not breastfeeding at all, with two-thirds (628, 62.8%) breastfeeding for at least six months (Additional File, Table S5). Although the majority of women (782, 78.1%) indicated that their decision to breastfeed and/or duration of breastfeeding was not influenced by the pandemic, many women also reported pandemic-related reasons contributing beneficially to their breastfeeding decisions and/or experience, including: greater ease of breastfeeding whilst staying at home (618/927, 66.7%); and greater partner support (216/927, 23.3%). Half the cohort (438/927, 47.2%) also recognised the potential for post-vaccination breastfeeding contributing towards immunoprotection of their baby from COVID-19 (noting here that the vast majority (98.7%) of the overall cohort were vaccinated; Additional File, Table S2). Nonetheless, some women (6/53, 11.3%) who did not breastfeed indicated this was due to lack of support, though whether pandemic-related or not was unclear.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study presents the views of postnatal care, by a cohort of women of reproductive age, who had one or more liveborn infants during the pandemic, and who responded to our survey about pandemic experiences and pregnancy. Respondents were predominantly of White ethnicity and with little social deprivation; and, after primary discharge home from hospital after birth, reported substantial interaction with the healthcare system for maternal and newborn health concerns. About one quarter of women were dissatisfied with care at their six-week postnatal visit, most of which was undertaken in-person. Qualitative analysis of free-text comments regarding care experiences revealed mothers reported liking the convenience and promptness of virtual care, and generally felt it was a good way to meet their healthcare needs during the pandemic. From our data, we can, however, assume this acceptance was viewed as conditional, and women valued there being an option and ability to escalate care quickly. This idea of a tiered triage process from virtual to in-person care was reported in our survey as having been achieved with little difficulty, in most cases. However, most women still preferred in-person postpartum care; and where escalation of virtual to face-to-face care was not possible or was perceived as difficult to access, women were more likely to perceive the changes to postnatal care access negatively. This was evidenced by the range of negative comments provided about virtual care, in terms of modality and care experiences. Moreover, women expressed a strong preference for in-person care of their child, post-pandemic.

Our data provide valuable learnings regarding virtual postpartum care. Although patients appreciated the convenience, virtual care was often perceived as lower in quality and less thorough than in-person care. Respondents often reported that their virtual care offer had to be escalated to an in-person visit anyway (e.g. when their baby’s illness was difficult to diagnose, when the baby was not improving despite prescribed medications after a virtual consultation, or when the baby’s illness progressed or worsened). It was notable how often physical examination was mentioned, as a necessary part of proper healthcare assessment, especially when considering an infant.

Interpretation

This study emphasises how commonly postnatal care is accessed, for maternal and newborn health concerns. Our findings provide further evidence for the complexity of virtual care in maternity care services. Although the current data were collected at a unique time in history, the pandemic-wrought changes in maternity care were a more rapid implementation of processes already in progress (i.e. a transition towards greater use of virtual clinics in many healthcare settings). Of note, previous studies have already cautioned that rapid implementation without reflection on innovation gains can and has led to disruption of maternity services, and to wider devaluation of care provision [20].

Virtual care is often promoted as being without problem or concern. Certainly, the benefits of being time-efficient and convenient for patients (e.g. no need to come into clinics) and cost-effective for government (e.g. no need to provide fully equipped clinic rooms), are touted as major positives for both healthcare users and healthcare providers. For maternity care in particular, a small body of evidence suggests virtual care—particularly postnatally—is a positive alternative to in-person provision [28, 29], but the evidence remains mixed, with much of the positive support for virtual care coming from the beginning of the pandemic, when most countries had strict ‘stay-at-home’ orders in place and therefore mixed-provision was not yet an option. However, extensive evidence from across the pandemic has cautioned against being blind to the negatives associated with virtual care provision—especially in maternity settings—where it has been found to be more costly to deliver [19], and more burdensome for staff [30]. Moreover, the responsibility of being the complete proxy for an infant can be, for some women, an added burden—with potential to provoke further maternal anxiety.

Additionally, virtual care is often reported as the solution which liberates geographic constraints on healthcare access (e.g. provision of quality tertiary care at a distance for rurally-located individuals, and where the alternative is often no care). However, this does not account for the lack of digital connectivity across the country, or indeed internationally [31]. As such, virtual care may further exacerbate inequalities, by leaving those most vulnerable and those in the lowest socio-economic groups with a further decrement in access to care [32]. This also has the effect of further burdening already-stretched maternity care services [33]. Moreover, and paradoxically, virtual care is frequently more time-intensive than face-to-face appointments for healthcare providers, who are often unable to reach patients, who are scheduled during a broad window (rather than given a specific appointment time), and who are faced with poor digital connectivity and/or have to re-book virtual appointments as face-to-face anyway, due to clinical need [34]. Especially with respect to postnatal care, equitable approaches acts as a vital population health strategy, as relational care and increased agency and navigation of services can empower those who are linguistically and culturally diverse (and therefore more vulnerable to severe morbidity and mortality in settings such as the NHS) as better candidates for care and make them feel healthcare spaces are less difficult to access and safer to utilise [35–37]. However, for healthcare to be experienced in this way, care may need to be face-to-face, in person, and woman-centred [38, 39].

There has been mixed evidence for the ability of virtual maternity care to maintain standards of provision and safety at the same level as in-person provision. Whilst some studies have demonstrated no such increase in risk to health of mother and baby [40]; the overwhelming majority of evidence suggests virtual care worsens maternal and infant outcomes [41], including potential increase in stillbirths [42]; reduction in confidence and agency to access and navigate care [18, 43, 44]; disruption and devaluation of services—often due to the rapidity with which services had to change from in-person care provision to virtual care [20]; poorer psychosocial outcomes [45, 46]; and widening the gap relating to healthcare disparities [31, 32, 34]—already a poignant point of debate within maternity care service provision both nationally across the UK [47], and internationally [35–37].

Despite recent research suggesting in-person care remains the patient-perceived most desirable form of primary care provision, from whence postnatal care in the UK is delivered, a post-pandemic roll-back of virtual care to predominantly in-person provision has not occurred across perinatal and family healthcare services [48, 49]. As our study shows, unless there is an accessible escalation of care provision from virtual to in-person care, there is a risk of loss of faith in maternity services by service users—especially where trust in healthcare services is already low, and against the backdrop of women feeling unable to engage with healthcare services [18] and considering birthing options without healthcare professionals [50]. Nonetheless, the move towards virtual care in all areas of medicine continues, including all aspects of maternity care from preconception to postnatal.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Our large cohort size and granular detail for each participant regarding pregnancy outcomes represents major strengths of this study. We recognise the high proportion of women who provided qualitative comments—which allowed us to extend our understanding through a mixed methods analysis of collected data. However, this was a cohort of convenience, recruited in the suboptimal circumstances of the pandemic, who were drawn from UK ZOE app users. We fully acknowledge that—as with ZOE app users generally—our cohort is not fully representative of the UK population (i.e. they represent a predominantly White population, with a higher socio-economic status). Thus, the experiences may not be generalisable to all women in the UK, or indeed to women internationally—something which has been repeatedly raised in the literature-base regarding diversity of representation in pregnancy research [51–53]. In particular, we must ensure future research makes concerted recruitment efforts to embed those with socially complex lives, those who find healthcare hard to access, and those who are most at risk of maternal morbidity and mortality within the research, which will only move to make the research more holistic for the betterment of healthcare for all populations [37]. We also acknowledge our invitation highlighted our aim to capture the views of women considering pregnancy, currently pregnant, or recently pregnant. It is not possible to know how many women, within the entire ZOE app user cohort of women of reproductive age, might meet these criteria; this then limits our capacity to assess for bias in responders vs. non-responders. Nonetheless, Table S1 shows that our participants were very similar demographically to the invited population, with respect to age, ethnicity, BMI, and IMD. Further, questionnaires were bespoke, created in response to the pandemic experience, and, understandably, unable to be validated in a further population. A logical expansion of this work would be to repeat the survey in a post-pandemic circumstance to assess women’s experiences of postnatal care outside of a health system shock situation. Additionally, tools to measure mental health were not perinatal specific, and therefore may suffer from over-inflation of symptomatology, compared to if perinatal-specific tools had been utilised [54].

Conclusions

High-quality postnatal care, as recommended by the WHO, is key to optimise health outcomes for both mothers and babies. Our study suggests that whilst women prefer in-person postnatal care, they also value flexible access to services based on individual needs and preferences. In particular, many women value physical interaction with care providers for young babies, when a physical examination may be crucial for accurate and rapid diagnosis. Whilst virtual care may be seen as acceptable for minor issues, it must be timely; and technology and connectivity must not be barriers, or virtual care may exacerbate healthcare disparities for marginalised populations. Maternity care providers must therefore navigate the growing shift toward virtual care, alongside the findings of this and other studies, that perceptions of virtual maternity care remain mixed, and outcomes of virtual care (vs. in-person care) may be inferior. Our study suggests widespread support for a personalised, tiered approach to postnatal care provision—one which considers the most efficient and equitable form of care for each situation, the preferences of the mother (especially when infants are involved), and the clinical urgency of the baby’s condition; and where escalation from virtual to in-person care is not only possible, but enacted to ensure the safest possible care for new mothers and their infants.
