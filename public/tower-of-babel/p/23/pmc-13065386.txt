Effects of a 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system on balance and gait performance in older adults
Authors: Dae-Joong Yang, Hyeong-Seok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose] This study examined the effects of a 3D motion-recognition-based virtual training system on balance and gait in older adults.[Methods] Thirty older adults ( ≥ 60 years) were randomly assigned to the experimental group (n = 15) and the control group (n = 15). The experimental group underwent 3D motion-recognition-based virtual training for 8 weeks, while the control group performed a conventional exercise program without 3D motion-recognition-based virtual training. Balance measures (surface area, weight distribution, and limit of stability) and gait measures (cadence, gait speed, and stride length) were assessed before and after the intervention. Data were analyzed using paired t-tests and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).[Results] After the intervention, the experimental group showed significant improvements in all balance and gait parameters (p < 0.05), whereas the control group demonstrated significant improvements in balance and gait parameters except for surface area (p < 0.05). Notably, weight distribution, limit of stability, cadence, and gait velocity showed significantly greater improvements in the experimental group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). However, no significant between-group differences were observed for surface area and stride length.[Conclusion] The 3D motion-recognition-based virtual training system is an effective intervention for improving balance and gait function in older adults, contributing to enhanced mobility and balance ability. Future studies should further investigate the long-term effects of this intervention and work toward optimizing the training protocols.Keywords: 3D motion recognition, virtual training, balance, gait, exergame, older adults

INTRODUCTION

As global aging rapidly progresses, maintaining health in older adults and addressing limitations in daily life activities due to falls and gait disorders, along with the resulting decline in quality of life, have emerged as significant social issues [1]. Falls, in particular, are a major health problem with high incidence rates and severe sequelae in the elderly population, leading to increased healthcare costs and functional decline that result in socioeconomic burden [2]. Balance function, defined as the ability to maintain and control the body’s center of mass within the base of support during static and dynamic tasks, and gait function, defined as the coordinated control of spatiotemporal walking parameters necessary for safe and efficient locomotion, are essential components of functional independence in older adults [2,3]. Deterioration of balance and gait function in older adults not only increases fall risk but also directly impacts the decline in activities of daily living performance and shortening of healthy life expectancy [4]. The pathophysiological causes of such balance and gait deterioration include lower extremity muscle weakness, reduced joint range of motion, impaired proprioception and vestibular function, and decreased sensory integration capacity [3,5]. Furthermore, decline in higher-order cognitive function also acts as a central neurological factor affecting balance and gait [6,7].

Considering these complex physiological and cognitive causes, an integrated training approach that can simultaneously stimulate not only physical function but also cognitive function and sensory integration is necessary for functional improvement in older adults [8]. Recently, Virtual Reality (VR) technology has gained attention as a means to enable such multidimensional intervention approaches. In particular, three-dimensional (3D) motion recognition-based virtual training systems precisely track users’ real-time body movements and provide visual feedback, offering advantages of inducing user motivation and promoting sustained participation through real-time feedback and repetitive learning structures [9,10].

The effectiveness of VR-based training has been demonstrated in various aspects through previous studies. Im et al. reported significant improvements in balance and mobility in older adults after providing a 3D interactive augmented reality program 10 times over 4 wk to 18 elderly participants [9]. Ku et al. demonstrated that a 3D augmented reality-based rehabilitation system provided 3 times per week for 4 wk to 36 older adults was more effective in improving balance and mobility than conventional exercise programs [10]. Lee et al. reported that virtual reality training using 3D video games resulted in significant improvements in static and dynamic balance and lower extremity muscle strength [11]. Lee demonstrated that gait training in a virtual reality environment for 56 older adults (28 in VR group, 28 in control group) brought significant improvements in balance, gait speed, and stride length [12]. Thus, virtual reality-based systems, rather than simple repetitive exercise, can enhance participants’ immersion and motivation, leading to substantial effects in improving balance and gait abilities.

However, although these studies report that VR-based training is effective in improving balance and mobility in older adults, significant limitations remain in the current evidence base with respect to the kinesiological and exercise physiological mechanisms underlying these effects. Most existing studies are confined to vision-centered and restricted interaction environments, which do not sufficiently address the biomechanical complexity of whole-body movement patterns or the neuromuscular adaptations required for functional improvement. Furthermore, evidence regarding exercise physiological responses to VR-based training remains limited, including aspects such as motor learning processes, muscle activation patterns, and sensorimotor integration mechanisms that are fundamental to balance and gait control. Clinical validation studies employing 3D motion recognition-based systems that provide real-time feedback on whole-body movements, together with quantitative biomechanical assessments, are still insufficient. Delgado and Der Ananian examined the potential use of virtual reality head-mounted displays (VR-HMD) as assessment and intervention tools in a scoping review analyzing VR studies using Head-Mounted Displays, but pointed out that clear evidence for this is still lacking [13]. According to a meta-analysis by Lee et al. immersive VR was effective in improving static balance but had limited effects on gait variables [14]. This suggests that intervention approaches focused primarily on simple visual feedback are insufficient for improving actual gait control abilities.

Recently, 3D motion recognition-based virtual training systems that recognize and provide feedback on whole-body movements in real time have gained attention. Jäggi et al. reported that such cognitive-motor integrated Exergames were effective in improving fall risk factors in patients with Parkinson’s disease [15], and Altorfer et al. suggested in a pilot study of elderly inpatients that whole-body response-based cognitive-motor training contributed to functional improvement [16]. However, existing studies have limitations in that they mostly focused on single-variable-centered evaluations or failed to systematically analyze intervention effects through quantified tools.

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the intervention effects on balance and gait function using standardized quantitative assessment equipment (G-Walk, PhysioSensing) in community-dwelling older adults through a 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system. Additionally, based on a randomized controlled design, we aimed to examine both the effectiveness and applicability of the intervention, thereby overcoming the limitations of existing studies and providing a practical foundation as a clinical and community rehabilitation strategy.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This study targeted adults aged 60 y and older who were using day care centers and local government care centers in Gwangju. Study participants were limited to those who voluntarily provided written informed consent after receiving sufficient explanation of the study purpose and procedures. Sample size calculation was performed a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1, Franz Faul, University of Kiel) to ensure adequate statistical power for detecting between-group differences. Based on an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) design with one covariate, an assumed effect size of f = 0.57, a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, and two groups, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 27 participants. The effect size was determined based on previous studies reporting moderate-to-large effects of exergame-based interventions on balance and gait outcomes in older adults [17]. To account for potential dropouts, a total of 30 participants were recruited. This randomized controlled trial allocated participants to either the experimental group (n = 15) or the control group (n = 15) using a computer-generated randomization sequence generated by Research Randomizer (Version 1.0, Mahmood Saghaei, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran). To ensure allocation concealment, group assignments were performed by a research assistant who was not involved in the intervention or outcome assessment. The experimental group received 3D motion recognition-based virtual training, while the control group received a conventional exercise program. Prior to study initiation, all therapists received standardized training on the intervention protocols to minimize potential performance bias related to differences in therapist experience. Pre- and post-intervention assessments were conducted by an assessor who was not involved in the intervention and was blinded to group allocation, thereby maintaining assessor blinding. This procedure was implemented to reduce inter-rater variability and enhance the reliability of the study outcomes. The inclusion criteria were: (a) individuals aged 60 y and older, (b) individuals who had experienced at least one fall within the past 12 months, defined as an unexpected event resulting in coming to rest on the ground or a lower level, and who reported gait instability or walking-related difficulties, (c) individuals without a history of cardiovascular or respiratory diseases, and (d) individuals with cognitive function capable of understanding and performing simple instructions. The exclusion criteria were: (a) individuals with musculoskeletal or neurological problems within 3 mo, and (b) individuals who withdrew from the study for personal reasons. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sehan University (SH-IRB 2025-004).

Exercise Programs

In this study, the experimental group (EG) received 3D motion recognition-based virtual training, while the control group (CG) received conventional exercise therapy. The intervention was conducted over 8 wk, with 5 sessions per week, and each session lasted 30 min. All training was conducted with a specialist.

3D Motion Recognition-Based Virtual Training

In this study, a 3D gross motor and activities of daily living training system (SilverFit 3D, SilverFit BV, Netherlands) was used. This system consists of a 3D motion detection camera (based on Kinect v2) and a display device, providing a game-based training program that recognizes users’ body movements in real time and provides visual feedback [24]. This equipment provides visual information through a screen and projector, with Kinect v2 recognizing the movements of the participant positioned 3 meters away and displaying the position of their feet on the screen.

Each session lasted 30 min, with training consisting of 5 sets of 4 min game performance followed by 2 min rest periods between sets. All training sessions were supervised by a trained specialist to ensure participant safety and adherence to the intervention protocol. The experimental group performed the “Garden hose game” embedded within SilverFit 3D, which is a program with adjustable difficulty levels. The Garden hose game involves scoring points by blocking two leak locations (targets) displayed on the screen with the feet, with the participant’s foot position displayed on the screen in real time to provide direct feedback. Upon success, points are awarded and new leaks are presented to continue the training. The game includes audio feedback, visual effects, performance scores, and narrative elements of blocking garden hose leaks to enhance participants’ immersion and participation motivation [25].

Training intensity and progression were individualized and indirectly controlled by the system. Specifically, reaction speed, target location, and visual stimulus complexity were automatically adjusted based on each participant’s real-time performance, allowing progressive task difficulty without manual load prescription. The game’s reaction speed, target locations, and visual stimulus difficulty are automatically adjusted according to the participant’s performance level, providing training at individualized intensity.

Before training, all participants were sufficiently familiarized with equipment usage and game rules. This familiarization session was conducted to minimize learning effects and ensure stable task execution during the intervention period. Specifically, participants underwent a supervised familiarization session prior to the intervention, which included verbal explanations and demonstrations of the system setup, safe body positioning, and game objectives. Participants were then allowed to perform brief practice trials at the lowest difficulty level under supervision until they demonstrated adequate understanding of the task requirements and safe interaction with the system [26,27] (Table 2).

Such game-based training is effective in improving balance, gait, and cognitive function by simultaneously stimulating trunk control, attention, reaction speed, mobility, and visuospatial cognition rather than simple repetition [28].

Conventional Exercise Program

The control group that received conventional exercise performed an exercise program recommended for improving physical function in older adults. Each session lasted approximately 30 min, consisting of 5 min of warm-up, 20 min of main exercise, and 5 min of cool-down, and was conducted five times per week for 8 weeks, matching the intervention frequency and duration of the experimental group.

The warm-up included light range of motion exercises and stretching focusing on the neck, shoulders, trunk, and lower extremities. The main exercise consisted of low-intensity aerobic walking activities (indoor walking, walking in place, and forward-backward stepping) and basic functional strengthening exercises targeting the lower extremities and trunk, such as sit-to-stand from a chair, arm raising, and weight-shifting movements. The cool-down phase concluded with light breathing control and stretching exercises.

All exercise sessions were supervised by a dedicated exercise instructor to ensure safety and correct performance. Prior to the intervention, participants received standardized instruction on posture, breathing techniques, and safety precautions [29]. Exercise intensity was maintained at a low-to-moderate level, and was individually adjusted by modifying repetition number, movement speed, or rest intervals according to each participant’s physical fitness and coordination ability, while avoiding task-specific balance challenges or external feedback comparable to the virtual training.

Measurement Tools and Procedures

Balance measurement

Balance measurement was performed using a balance and pressure platform (Physiosensing platform, RM Ingenierie, Portugal), which is equipped with more than 1,600 resistance sensors and a sampling frequency of 100 Hz [18-20]. After preliminary education, study participants maintained a static posture on the platform for 60 s, and surface area (SA) and weight distribution (WD) were measured. The limit of stability (LOS) was evaluated through weight shifting using a front screen. Each measurement was repeated three times and the mean value was calculated, with a 120 s rest period provided between measurements.

Gait measurement

Gait measurement was performed using the G-Walk wireless inertial sensor (G-Walk, BTS bioengineering, Italy), which processes data through BTS G-Studio software and transmits data sampled at 100 Hz frequency in real time via Bluetooth [21]. The sensor was fixed to the L5 lumbar region with an elastic fixation band, and study participants walked a 10 m section in response to a ‘start’ signal while wearing their personal shoes [22]. For measurement accuracy, the finish line was set at the 12 m point. Study participants received preliminary education before measurement, and cadence, velocity, and stride length were measured. The reliability of this equipment is 0.85-0.97, and validity is 0.88- 0.97 [23]. All measurements were repeated three times and the mean value was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis in this study was performed using SPSS software (Windows version 27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and all data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). In this study, the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test for equality of variances were used to confirm the normality and homogeneity of all variables. Within-group pre-post changes were analyzed using paired-samples t-tests, and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to analyze differences in post-intervention changes between groups. To control for the increased risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. In the ANCOVA analysis, the covariate was set as the pre-measurement value of each variable, and the statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05. The reason for setting pre-values as covariates was to compare the pure intervention effects between groups on post-values by controlling for initial differences in pre-values.

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled study was conducted to examine the effects of a 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system on balance and gait abilities in older adults, compared with a conventional exercise program. The principal findings indicate that this intervention resulted in greater improvements in selected balance and gait parameters in the experimental group, rather than uniform improvements across all outcome measures.

The results of this study demonstrated that balance-related variables reflecting dynamic postural control and weight-shifting ability, specifically weight distribution and limit of stability, showed significantly greater improvements in the experimental group compared with the control group, whereas no significant between-group difference was observed for surface area. Similarly, gait parameters associated with temporal regulation and walking efficiency, including cadence and gait velocity, exhibited more pronounced improvements in the experimental group following the virtual training intervention, while stride length improved to a comparable extent in both groups without a significant between-group difference.

Taken together, these results suggest that the 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system exerts targeted effects on specific components of balance and gait control, rather than producing generalized improvements across all domains. The selective enhancement of weight distribution, limit of stability, cadence, and gait velocity supports the potential of this intervention as a focused and task-specific training approach for improving functional mobility in older adults. In addition, the high level of participant engagement and satisfaction observed during the intervention period highlights the practical feasibility and clinical applicability of 3D motion recognition-based virtual training systems in community and institutional settings.

The improvements in balance and gait abilities observed in this study can be explained by various physiological mechanisms. The loss of sensory receptors and decline in sensory information processing ability that occur during the aging process interfere with the integration of visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive sensory information essential for maintaining balance [1]. The 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system compensates for insufficient vestibular and proprioceptive sensory information through visual feedback [2] and improves balance control ability through the sensory reweighting process [30]. Repetitive task performance in virtual environments stimulates motor learning pathways in the cerebellum and basal ganglia to increase the efficiency of motor programs through synaptic plasticity, leading to improved coordination of lower extremity muscles during gait and enhanced postural control abilities [31]. Additionally, cognitive-motor dual tasks strengthen frontal-cerebellar networks to improve dual-task performance abilities [32], and weight shifting training in virtual environments enhances muscle strength and reaction speed of lower extremity muscles to promote neuromuscular adaptation [6]. These mechanisms likely contributed to improvements in specific balance and gait parameters, particularly weight distribution, limit of stability, cadence, and gait velocity, rather than uniformly influencing all outcomes.

The balance and gait ability improvement results of this study are consistent with several previous studies that reported the effectiveness of virtual reality-based training. Ku et al. reported significant improvements in weight distribution index and stability index in a randomized controlled study applying a 3D augmented reality-based training system to 36 older adults [10], which is consistent with the results of significant improvements in weight distribution and limit of stability in the experimental group in this study. Im et al. also reported improvements in postural stability and lower extremity balance after providing 10 sessions of 3D interactive augmented reality training over 4 wk to 18 older adults [9], which is similar to the decreased surface area and increased limit of stability results observed in the experimental group of this study. Lee confirmed significant improvements in gait velocity and rhythm after gait training in a VR environment in a randomized clinical trial with 56 older adults over 4 wk, 5 times per week [12], which is consistent with the results of greater improvements in cadence and gait velocity in the experimental group of this study. Percy et al. reported significant effects of VR-based balance training on weight shifting ability and postural stability in a meta-analysis of 265 participants [33], and Rodríguez-Almagro et al. reported in a systematic review that VR therapy more effectively improves static and dynamic balance than traditional rehabilitation methods [2]. These research results support that the improvements in balance and gait abilities observed in the experimental group of this study are not coincidental but are effects of the 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system.

However, some research results that partially contradict the results of this study also exist. Delgado and Der Ananian reported a lack of clear evidence for balance and gait improvement effects in a scoping review of VR interventions using Head-Mounted Displays [13]. This study raised questions about whether HMD-based VR systems are suitable for balance training, which partially corresponds with the results of no significant between-group differences in some variables (surface area, stride length) in this study. These differences may stem from the type of VR technology and intervention methods. The 3D motion recognition technology used in this study allows natural body movements in real space unlike HMDs, so there may have been significant improvements in only some balance and gait variables.

Lee et al. reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis including various studies that immersive virtual reality training was effective for static balance improvement but showed limited effects on gait-related variables [14]. They particularly suggested that interventions of more than 4.5 h were effective, which may be related to the result of no significant between-group difference in stride length in this study. If the intervention period of this study had been longer, significant differences in stride length might have appeared.

Kim reported that community-based virtual reality gait training using 360-degree images showed improvement effects in only some gait spatiotemporal parameters [34], which is similar to the point that the same effects did not appear in all gait variables in this study.

These contradictory research results suggest that the effects of virtual training can vary according to intervention methods, duration, and participant characteristics. Additionally, they suggest that 3D motion recognition technology may not have the same effects on all balance and gait variables, and that individualized approaches and sufficient intervention periods are necessary for effective virtual training.

This study contributed to filling gaps in existing literature by quantitatively analyzing the effects of virtual training systems using 3D motion recognition technology on balance and gait ability improvement in older adults. In particular, unlike existing studies that mainly focused on only some balance or gait indicators, this study verified various effects of 3D motion recognition-based systems by comprehensively analyzing static balance, dynamic balance, and gait variables. In a situation where understanding of the physiological mechanisms of VR training is lacking, as pointed out in the systematic review by Lee et al. this study explained the effects of virtual training considering various physiological mechanisms. Additionally [14], by targeting community-dwelling older adults, it differentiates from existing studies mainly conducted in hospital environments like Duque et al. and presents possibilities as preventive intervention [35]. This is an important research result that can contribute to solving social challenges of supporting independent living and extending healthy life expectancy in the elderly population.

The major strengths of this study are causal relationship verification through a randomized controlled design, quantitative data collection using objective measurement tools, accurate analysis of between-group differences through covariate control using ANCOVA, and reproducibility secured through detailed intervention protocol description. However, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of a no-intervention control group constitutes a major methodological limitation of this study. Because the control group participated in a conventional exercise program rather than receiving no intervention, it is not possible to completely disentangle the specific effects of the 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system from nonspecific influences such as natural recovery, increased general physical activity, or repeated exposure to outcome assessments. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as demonstrating the relative effectiveness of the virtual training system compared with conventional exercise, rather than as absolute intervention effects. Second, the absence of follow-up assessments represents an additional important limitation, as the durability and long-term retention of the observed improvements could not be determined. Balance and gait improvements following exercise-based interventions may be transient and subject to detraining effects once training ceases, which limits the practical and clinical significance of the present findings.

In addition, the limited sample size, relatively short intervention period, difficulty in achieving complete blinding, and inclusion of only older adults with specific health conditions also serve as limitations of this study. Furthermore, the selective nature of the results—with statistically significant between-group improvements observed in only four of the six primary outcomes—indicates that the intervention effects were not uniform across all balance and gait domains.

In conclusion, the present study provides preliminary evidence that a 3D motion recognition-based virtual training system may improve specific balance and gait parameters in older adults, particularly weight distribution, limit of stability, cadence, and gait velocity, whereas no significant between-group differences were observed for surface area and stride length. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the clinical significance of these improvements is tempered by the absence of follow-up data to confirm their long-term sustainability. These findings suggest that such systems may serve as a targeted intervention for enhancing selected aspects of mobility and balance in community-dwelling older adults under controlled conditions, rather than as a comprehensive solution for all balance and gait impairments. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer intervention durations, follow-up assessments to determine retention of benefits, and broader outcome measures are warranted to further clarify the scope, durability, and practical value of these effects.
