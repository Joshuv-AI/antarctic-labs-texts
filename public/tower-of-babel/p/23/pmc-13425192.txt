Diagnostic Overshadowing in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: A Case of Recurrent Syncope
Authors: Antareep Halder, Medhia Afzal, Aylin Ugurlu, Rahul Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) is a frequent cause of acute medical presentations. However, misattribution of the cause of such episodes based on bias from pre-existing diagnostic history (i.e., diagnostic anchoring) can lead to poor appraisal of co-existing life-threatening pathology unrelated to COPD. We present a 67-year-old male with a known background of severe COPD who presented with sudden-onset breathlessness but also recurrent syncope, where a presumed AECOPD served as a diagnostic red herring. Driven by implicit framing biases, the admitting team prematurely closed the diagnostic process and empirically initiated intravenous corticosteroids and nebulised bronchodilators without fully exploring alternative diagnoses. Multidisciplinary team review noted the absence of hypoxia or hypercapnia on arterial blood gas analysis and identified syncope as an atypical diagnostic feature of AECOPD, prompting a pivot away from respiratory management. A pre-hospital ECG had captured an irregularly irregular tachycardia suggestive of a focal atrial tachycardia with variable block or a multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT), and a subsequent electrocardiography (ECG) revealed a consistently short PR interval (110 ms), raising the possibility of an underlying arrhythmic cause of the event. Corticosteroids were safely discontinued, and an outpatient implantable loop recorder (ILR) was arranged for long-term tachyarrhythmia surveillance. This report highlights the risks of diagnostic anchoring and premature closure in patients in the context of a known background diagnosis, the necessity of holistic clinical assessment with maintenance of a broad differential, and the importance of reassessing atypical clinical features through multidisciplinary review.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition that is characterised by chronic airway obstruction with episodes of acute clinical deterioration, defined as acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD).

AECOPD is a common cause of emergency hospital admission; however, its diagnosis is largely clinical and based on symptoms that are often non-specific. This creates a diagnostic challenge, as alternative primary diagnoses must be carefully considered in patients presenting with presumed AECOPD [1]. Dyspnoea is a hallmark symptom of AECOPD but is also a common manifestation of other acute conditions, including heart failure, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrhythmia, and pulmonary embolism. The diagnostic process is further complicated by the high prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidity among patients with COPD [2].

In the high-pressure environment of an emergency department (ED), a known history of a chronic respiratory disease may lead to "diagnostic overshadowing," whereby new or atypical symptoms are prematurely attributed to the existing condition [3]. Syncope, however, is not a typical feature of AECOPD and should be regarded as a clinical red flag. Its presence should prompt reassessment of the working diagnosis and early consideration of cardiac causes, particularly when accompanied by electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities [4].

We present the case of a 67-year-old male with known COPD who presented with acute breathlessness followed by transient loss of consciousness and was initially managed as AECOPD. This report highlights the risk of premature diagnostic closure; the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis; adequate exploration of atypical clinical features and investigations; the value of early involvement of multidisciplinary review; and the need to reassess corticosteroid therapy when the diagnosis of AECOPD is uncertain.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male with a background of COPD and asthma presented to the ED following a sudden episode of transient loss of consciousness. He was an active smoker (smoking one to two cigarettes/day at the time of presentation, but a heavy smoker previously with around 20 cigarettes/day for more than 40 years) established on carbocisteine and once-daily single-inhaler triple therapy (fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol). He had a BMI of 23.84. Previous diagnostic spirometry (Figure 1) completed in 2016 demonstrated severe airflow obstruction, with pre-bronchodilator values showing a forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)​ of 0.87 L (28% predicted), a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 2.62 L (66% predicted), and a corresponding FEV1/FVC ratio of 33.34%. He did not have any oxygen assessments. He had no known history of ischaemic heart disease and no family history of sudden cardiac death, collapse, or blackout.

In the two weeks preceding admission, he reported feeling generally unwell with a productive cough with clear sputum and progressive dyspnoea. The general practitioner (GP) had started him on oral antibiotics (amoxicillin) and oral corticosteroids (prednisolone 30 mg), of which the patient had completed four days by the day of admission. On the day of presentation, the history taken in ED stated that he developed a sudden worsening of his ongoing breathlessness while going upstairs, followed by a sudden collapse. Family members reportedly performed chest compressions as they were not able to feel a pulse for approximately two to three minutes during the episode; however, ambulance documentation shows the presence of a faint but rapid pulse and low consciousness levels. He did not report any worsening of his cough or sputum production. His Glasgow Coma Scale was initially reported as 4/15 and had improved to 15/15 by the time of arrival at the hospital.

Pre-hospital ECG (Figure 2) demonstrated an irregularly irregular heart rhythm with visible P waves, raising the possibility of focal atrial tachycardia with variable atrioventricular (AV) block or a multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT). This finding was not highlighted in the initial ED documentation. A repeat ECG in the department showed sinus rhythm. Chest X-ray was unremarkable. On arrival, oxygen saturations were 94% on air. Arterial blood gas (ABG) samples on air showed a PaO2 of 10.8 kPa and a PaCO2 of 4.74 kPa. His CRP was 2 mg/L (normal range 0-5 mg/L) and blood eosinophil count was 0.03 x 109/L (normal range 0.04-0.4 x 109/L).

The initial working diagnosis was AECOPD, and the patient was initiated on IV corticosteroids and nebulised bronchodilators (salbutamol). Following senior clinical review the following morning, AECOPD remained the working diagnosis, supported by the patient’s respiratory symptoms and left-sided mild wheeze on examination. A respiratory review was subsequently requested.

The respiratory review prompted a diagnostic turning point. The episode of transient loss of consciousness, particularly in the context of reported bystander chest compressions, was felt to be atypical for an uncomplicated COPD exacerbation, and it was proposed that events may have stemmed from a cardiac cause.

On subsequent review on the respiratory ward, further history clarified that prior to the episode, he was going upstairs, developed acute breathlessness with clamminess, sat down on the toilet seat, used his inhalers and then lost consciousness. A subsequent ECG (Figure 3) demonstrated an isolated short PR interval of 110 ms, narrow QRS complexes (80 ms), and borderline-high P-wave amplitudes in the inferior leads. Although the clinical significance of a short PR interval without a delta wave is inherently uncertain, its presence in the context of unexplained syncope prompted a precautionary 24-hour Holter monitor to exclude paroxysmal tachyarrhythmias. As AECOPD was felt to be less likely, IV corticosteroids were discontinued on day 2 of admission. Given that the patient had already completed four days of prednisolone 30 mg prior to admission, the total duration of corticosteroid exposure at the point of discontinuation was in line with corticosteroid stewardship principles. The patient remained clinically stable with no deterioration in respiratory symptoms following cessation.

ECG showing a PR interval of 110 ms (arrows marked)

Holter monitoring reported sinus rhythm with a persistently shortened PR interval and normal QRS complexes, a 5% ventricular ectopy (VE) burden, and very occasional isolated areas of supraventricular ectopic beats. Following cardiology review, an outpatient implantable loop recorder (ILR) was recommended for longer rhythm surveillance on discharge. Inpatient spirometry was deferred given the acute presentation with syncope. Consequently, the patient's respiratory status was monitored via clinical assessment and ABG analysis. Repeat spirometry after discharge (Figure 4) showed pre-bronchodilator values of FEV1 1.35 (48% predicted), FVC 3.37 (95% predicted) and FEV1/FVC as 40.05%.

Review of previous records revealed a similar admission two months earlier with collapse and loss of consciousness. An ABG done on air in that admission showed pO2 of 9.8 and pCO2 of 5.1. A shortened PR interval (Figure 5) had also been documented during that admission but had not been investigated further. In this admission as well, he was treated for AECOPD.

Learning points

Syncope in a patient with known COPD must be treated as a clinical red flag rather than reflexively attributed to a primary respiratory deterioration. When objective data, such as the absence of hypoxia or hypercapnia, the abrupt nature of collapse, and an irregular atrial tachycardia on pre-hospital ECG, fail to support a primary respiratory aetiology, clinicians should maintain a broad differential whilst actively exploring alternate diagnoses, and avoid premature diagnostic closure and anchoring bias based on long-standing established diagnoses. Furthermore, corticosteroid stewardship principles should be applied when AECOPD becomes diagnostically uncertain. It is also important to acknowledge that recurrent unexplained collapse accompanied by reproducible ECG anomalies warrants prompt escalation to definitive, long-term rhythm surveillance, even when individual findings are not independently diagnostic of the underlying mechanism.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges inherent in patients presenting with acute decompensation against a background of established chronic disease, and the role of cognitive bias - particularly diagnostic anchoring and premature closure - in shaping clinical decision-making. Whilst AECOPD might have been the most likely cause of his symptoms based on his established background, several atypical findings, including syncope and sudden unresponsiveness, should have warranted further investigation of non-respiratory aspects of his condition acutely.

The physiological overlap between COPD and cardiac dysrhythmia contributed to the diagnostic difficulty. Dyspnoea may occur in both AECOPD and cardiac rhythm disturbance, and established COPD may make a respiratory diagnosis appear more likely. However, this patient also presented with syncope, which is not a standard feature of AECOPD. According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on syncope, the presence of an abnormal ECG in a symptomatic individual should prompt cardiac evaluation [4]. In this case, the persistently shortened PR interval was an important finding that warranted further assessment.

Neale et al. [5] described several cognitive biases that may influence clinical reasoning in everyday practice. In this case, the diagnostic trajectory was likely influenced by anchoring bias and the representativeness heuristic [3]. Anchoring bias occurs when clinicians give excessive weight to an initial diagnosis and insufficiently adjust their thinking when new information emerges. The representativeness heuristic occurs when a diagnosis is judged to be more likely because it resembles a familiar clinical pattern. In this case, the patient's known severe COPD, dyspnoea, and wheeze may have anchored the team to a diagnosis of AECOPD, while the presentation appeared to fit the familiar pattern of a COPD exacerbation. Together, these factors may have led to premature closure [3], whereby alternative diagnoses were not pursued despite atypical features. The omission of the pre-hospital irregular atrial tachycardia from initial ED documentation may reflect a simple documentation oversight in a busy acute setting; however, it raises the possibility of confirmation bias, whereby findings inconsistent with the working diagnosis of AECOPD may not have been actively sought or incorporated into the clinical reasoning process. Whilst this remains speculative, it serves as a reminder of the importance of actively reviewing all available pre-hospital data before anchoring on an initial diagnosis.

Forced expiratory manoeuvres generate profound intrathoracic pressure swings that can compromise venous return and exacerbate cardiovascular instability; therefore, inpatient spirometry was not performed given the acute admission for syncope [6].

Subsequent post-discharge testing confirmed that the patient did indeed have severe baseline obstructive features. Although the reduced FVC in 2016 introduces the differential diagnosis of a mixed ventilatory defect, the flow-volume loop displays a profoundly scooped, concave expiratory limb alongside an expiratory time extending to 14 seconds on the volume-time curve. These parameters strongly point towards pseudorestriction secondary to severe emphysematous air trapping rather than intrinsic restriction, though definitive differentiation was limited by the lack of static lung volume plethysmography. In retrospect, the FVC in 2016 was likely reduced by a short blow, as evidenced by improved FVC in 2026.

Differentiating acute respiratory pathology from a primary cardiac event is essential here. While the patient had a history of severe COPD, his room-air ABG demonstrated a normal PaCO2 of 4.74 kPa and a PaO2 of 10.8 kPa, making acute hypercapnic or hypoxic respiratory failure an unlikely explanation for his collapse. The abrupt, near-instantaneous nature of the event is also atypical for a purely respiratory cause of deterioration, which would more commonly present with a progressive decline in consciousness level rather than sudden loss of consciousness. The pre-hospital ECG captured an irregular atrial tachycardia suggestive of a focal atrial tachycardia with variable AV block or a MAT in the peri-collapse period; loss of coordinated atrial contraction and impaired diastolic filling during such tachycardias can reduce cardiac output, and a transient reduction of this kind provides a plausible mechanism for cerebral hypoperfusion and syncope. Whilst the precise temporal relationship between the captured atrial tachycardia and the moment of loss of consciousness cannot be established with certainty, the overall clinical picture - normal gas exchange, abrupt collapse, and a tachyarrhythmia recorded in close proximity to the event - is more consistent with a primary arrhythmic mechanism than with AECOPD. This clinical trajectory illustrates how diagnostic anchoring bias and premature closure might have shaped decision-making when a compelling background diagnosis is present, even in the face of atypical features. It should be acknowledged, however, that the precise cause of syncope in this patient remains uncertain; the available evidence supports an arrhythmic aetiology as the most plausible explanation, but does not exclude other mechanisms, including reflex syncope in the context of physiological stress. This diagnostic uncertainty was the primary driver for longer-term rhythm surveillance via ILR.

A shift in diagnostic focus occurred following respiratory specialist review, underscoring the value of early specialist input and multidisciplinary collaboration in broadening the diagnostic frame. Reassessment identified syncope as a clinical red flag and appropriately prompted cardiac evaluation. Notably, the patient had presented several months earlier with collapse alone, without reported breathlessness, and a shortened PR interval had been documented at that time. Although it cannot be established retrospectively whether an earlier investigation would have changed the outcome, this case demonstrates the importance of integrating findings across multiple presentations rather than viewing each episode in isolation.

Whilst the room-air ABG demonstrated normal gas exchange parameters, it is important to acknowledge that AECOPD remains a purely clinical diagnosis, and normal blood gas values, although atypical, do not definitively exclude a concurrent exacerbation, particularly given the pre-admission antibiotic and oral corticosteroid course already received. Nevertheless, in the absence of objective evidence of acute respiratory failure and with a CRP of 2 mg/L not supportive of significant systemic inflammation, the decision to rationalise intravenous corticosteroids remains a clinical decision. In this context, continuing intravenous corticosteroids carried recognised risks without clear additional benefit - corticosteroids have been associated with increased atrial arrhythmia risk through autonomic nervous system activation [7], and supraventricular tachycardia has been documented in up to 34.2% of patients hospitalised with AECOPD, with medications identified as a contributing factor [8]. This arrhythmogenic burden is further compounded by the independently elevated cardiovascular and arrhythmia risk associated with COPD itself [9]. The decision to rationalise systemic corticosteroids therefore reflected sound clinical judgement, balancing uncertain incremental benefit against a quantifiable arrhythmogenic risk in a patient with a documented peri-collapse atrial tachyarrhythmia.

The reproducible finding of a shortened PR interval, documented across two separate admissions with similar clinical presentations, represents a second and potentially distinct line of cardiac concern in this patient. A short PR interval with a narrow QRS complex and absent delta wave has several potential explanations: it may reflect enhanced atrioventricular nodal conduction (EAVNC), in which accelerated conduction through the AV node itself - without an accessory pathway - predisposes to AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT); alternatively, it may indicate a concealed or atypical accessory pathway that conducts only retrogradely or intermittently, producing no delta wave on resting ECG but remaining capable of supporting re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT); or it may represent a normal variant, particularly given that 110 ms lies only marginally below the accepted lower limit of 120 ms [10]. In isolation, a short PR interval of borderline degree would not typically prompt further investigation. However, its reproducibility across two separate admissions, accompanied by recurrent syncope, substantially raises its clinical significance, making a normal variant explanation less convincing and supporting the case for longer-term rhythm surveillance. Sympathomimetic bronchodilators and physiological stress can further shorten AV nodal refractory periods, potentially lowering the threshold for re-entrant tachyarrhythmias in a patient with an underlying conduction abnormality [10]. No sustained tachyarrhythmia was captured on Holter monitoring, and this mechanism therefore remains hypothetical at present; the ILR has been placed specifically to test this hypothesis. This was clinically relevant given the patient’s recurrent syncope, abnormal ECG findings, and the recognised increased cardiovascular and arrhythmia risk in COPD [11].

The broader differential diagnosis of syncope in this patient warrants explicit consideration. Reflex syncope - including vasovagal or situational syncope - was considered, particularly given the prodromal clamminess and the circumstances of collapse. However, several features argue against this as the primary explanation: the patient was seated at the time of collapse, which reduces rather than provokes orthostatic stress; the previous episode occurred without the respiratory prodrome present on this admission, suggesting the trigger was not consistent across events; and vasovagal syncope does not readily account for a reproducible ECG conduction abnormality identified across two separate admissions. A formal tilt-table test was not performed, and reflex syncope cannot be entirely excluded. Orthostatic hypotension is similarly acknowledged as a differential. Furthermore, the exertional onset of symptoms - occurring whilst climbing stairs immediately before the collapse - is itself a recognised red flag for arrhythmic or structural cardiac causes of syncope, and warrants early cardiac evaluation independent of the ECG findings. Nonetheless, in accordance with ESC syncope guidelines, recurrent unexplained syncope with a reproducible ECG abnormality represents an indication for ILR [4], and the ILR therefore serves to characterise this patient's arrhythmia burden comprehensively, whatever the underlying mechanism ultimately proves to be.

The in-hospital ECG also demonstrated a 'P pulmonale' pattern with P-wave amplitudes exceeding 2.5 mm in the inferior leads. While a formal pulmonary hypertension workup was not performed during the acute admission, this finding - in a patient with a normal BMI of 23.84 - highlights chronic right atrial strain secondary to advanced COPD, possibly providing the substrate for his irregular atrial tachycardia.

Classically, acute respiratory exacerbations precipitate cardiac arrhythmias through the mechanism of gas-exchange failure. Hypoxaemia activates the sympathetic nervous system through adrenergic stimulation, triggering atrial ectopy, whilst hypercapnia leads to pulmonary arterial and right ventricular hypertension, resulting in right atrial dilation, increased transmural pressure, and disruption of atrial electrical stability [7]. However, this case underscores that a major arrhythmic event can be triggered even in the absence of acute respiratory failure. In this patient, advanced COPD had already established a vulnerable arrhythmogenic substrate, characterised by the 'P pulmonale' pattern reflecting chronic right atrial strain, such that even in the absence of overt gas-exchange failure, the combination of exertional stress and iatrogenic autonomic surge from sympathomimetic bronchodilator use provided sufficient provocation to precipitate an irregular atrial tachycardia. This intersection reflects a well-documented epidemiological burden; meta-analytic data confirm that patients with COPD carry a twofold higher independent risk of developing both atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias compared to those without the disease [9]. Furthermore, recent prospective data utilising continuous ambulatory monitoring have revealed that moderate-to-severe COPD patients carry a significantly higher rate of actionable arrhythmias compared to controls (48% vs 11%), highlighting the substantial burden of unrecognised rhythm disturbance in this population [12].

A recent Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) Science Committee perspective describes COPD and cardiovascular disease as “syndemic” conditions with shared risk factors, highlighting that exacerbations exponentially increase acute cardiovascular event risks through systemic inflammation, coagulation abnormalities, haemodynamic shifts [2]. Given this patient's recurrent, unexplained syncope, the reproducible ECG abnormality, and a non-diagnostic Holter, an ILR was recommended as the appropriate next step in rhythm surveillance. ILRs have been shown to achieve a definitive aetiological diagnosis in up to 64% of real-world cohorts with unexplained syncope, representing a significantly higher diagnostic yield than short-term modalities such as 24-hour Holter monitoring [13]. At the time of writing, no arrhythmia has been identified in this patient, and the role of the ILR remains to be established - rather than confirmed - as a causal rhythm disturbance.

This case also illustrates the importance of corticosteroid stewardship in the context of diagnostic uncertainty. Unnecessary systemic corticosteroid exposure carries recognised risks including hyperglycaemia, adrenal suppression, and pro-arrhythmic effects via sympathetic stimulation [14]. It is important to acknowledge that the presence of a cardiac cause for syncope does not necessarily exclude a concomitant AECOPD - the two processes can coexist, and the patient's antecedent productive cough and GP-initiated antibiotic course suggest a background respiratory deterioration may have been present. Whilst systemic corticosteroids remain appropriate in confirmed AECOPD, the coexistence of a probable arrhythmic precipitant and an already completed guideline-concordant community course collectively informed the decision to rationalise IV corticosteroids on the second day of admission. On balance, the patient's clinical stability throughout the remainder of the admission, with no deterioration in respiratory symptoms following discontinuation, further supported the appropriateness of this approach [15].

Conclusions

This case emphasises that, although dyspnoea in patients with COPD is commonly attributed to acute exacerbation, these patients remain at increased risk of serious cardiac pathology. Syncope in a patient with presumed AECOPD should be regarded as a clinical red flag and should prompt reassessment of the working diagnosis, particularly when ECG abnormalities are present. In this case, clinically relevant data, including an irregular atrial tachycardia captured on the pre-hospital ECG and a shortened PR interval documented on a previous admission, were available, yet the diagnostic trajectory suggests that a compelling background diagnosis may have contributed to premature closure of the diagnostic process. Whilst the patient's subsequent clinical stability lends retrospective support to the decision to rationalise corticosteroids, it cannot be taken as confirmation that a concurrent exacerbation was absent. This case emphasises that patients with COPD often have significant cardiovascular comorbidities which warrant active consideration during acute assessments, and that maintaining a broad differential, even in the presence of an established diagnosis, remains essential to safe clinical practice.
