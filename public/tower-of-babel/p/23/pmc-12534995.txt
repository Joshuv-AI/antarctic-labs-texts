The burden and determinants of intimate partner violence against reproductive-age women in four East African countries: evidence from 2021/2023 DHS dataset
Authors: Gebresilassie Tadesse, Girum Nakie, Gidey Rtbey, Techilo Tinsae, Setegn Fentahun, Fantahun Andualem, Mulualem Kelebie, Yilkal Abebaw Wassie, Tigist Kifle Tsegaw, Getasew Kibralew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate Partner Violence (IPV) remains a critical public health and human rights issue, disproportionately affecting women in low-resource settings. Despite various interventions, IPV persists in East Africa due to entrenched gender norms, economic dependence, and weak law enforcement. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of IPV among reproductive-age women in East Africa using recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data.MethodsA multilevel analysis was conducted using DHS datasets from Kenya, Madagascar, Mozambique, and Tanzania (2021–2023). A weighted sample of 27,265 reproductive-age women who had ever been in a union was included. IPV was assessed through self-reported experiences of physical, sexual, or emotional violence. Multilevel logistic regression models identified individual- and community-level factors associated with IPV, using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe pooled IPV prevalence was 40.18% (95% CI: 34.83%–45.53%), with emotional violence (29.82%) being the most common, followed by physical (27.56%) and sexual violence (9.94%). The highest pooled prevalence of IPV was found in Kenya (46.34%), followed by Madagascar (41.06%) and Tanzania (39.21%), whereas the lowest prevalence was reported in Mozambique (34.03%). Significant factors associated with IPV included age (25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49), primary (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.62) and secondary education (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.81), being widowed, divorced, or separated (AOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.66–2.04), male-headed household (AOR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.21), age-at first cohabitation before 18 years old (AOR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.19), partner alcohol consumption (AOR = 2.38, 95% CI: 2.22–2.55), witnessing maternal abuse (AOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.82–2.11), justifying wife-beating (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.27–1.46), jealousy (AOR = 3.44, 95% CI: 3.23–3.67), fear of a partner (AOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 3.33–3.81), tobacco use (AOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.26), rural residence (AOR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03–1.23), and media exposure (AOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.08–1.28).ConclusionIPV remains alarmingly high in East Africa, driven by gender norms, economic vulnerabilities, and weak institutional support. Addressing IPV requires strengthening legal enforcement, expanding economic empowerment initiatives, and promoting gender-sensitive education. Tailored interventions should prioritize rural areas, address alcohol-related IPV, and challenge societal attitudes that normalize violence.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) as intentional sexual, physical, or emotional violence inflicted by a current or former partner [1], recognizing it as a significant global public health concern [2]. Intimate partner violence is the most common form of violence against women [3], typically categorized into three types: physical, sexual, and emotional violence [4]. In 1993, the United Nations General Assembly issued a declaration on the “Elimination of Violence Against Women [5]. However, the prevalence of violence against women remains alarmingly high worldwide. More than a quarter (approximately 27%) of women aged 15 to 49 in relationships report experiencing physical, emotional or sexual violence by their intimate partner [6].

A recent global meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of IPV among women of reproductive age varied across the world, ranging from 16% in Central Europe to up to 49% in Oceania [7]. Another recent meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence at 37.3%, with past-year prevalence at 24.2%, showing significant differences across population groups: 31.6% in the community, 25.1% among help-seeking women, and 20.2% among perinatal women [8]. Reports of IPV in low-income countries ranged significantly, from less than 5% in Armenia and Comoros to over 40% in Afghanistan [9]. According to studies conducted in Asian and sub-Saharan African countries, the prevalence of IPV ranged from 28% in Madagascar, 57% in India, 74% in Ethiopia, and 87% in Jordan [10].

It has been confirmed that intimate partner violence is more prevalent than non-intimate partner violence in sub-Saharan Africa [11, 12]. Although the majority of African countries have signed numerous international agreements and enacted various domestic laws aimed at ending violence against women [13], IPV remains pervasive across the continent [14]. A previous multilevel analysis encompassing DHS data from 2010 to 2018 across 27 Sub-Saharan countries reported that over one-third (36%) of women who have ever been in a relationship have, at some point in their lives, been victims of physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner [15].

Prior studies indicate that, IPV is influenced by factors at the individual, partner, household, and community levels. At the individual level, low educational level and current working status among women are associated with experiencing IPV, as they often restricts economic opportunities and awareness of rights [16, 17]. Additionally, male-headed households often uphold traditional gender roles [18], reinforcing power imbalances [19], increasing women’s acceptance of wife-beating [20] and heightening the risk of IPV. Partner-related factors, such as male alcohol consumption [21, 22] and controlling behaviors [23], significantly elevate IPV risk. Research also shows that in some East African communities, traditional norms reinforce male authority, and women deviating from prescribed roles may face justified violence [24, 25]. Furthermore, witnessing maternal abuse [26, 27], pregnancy status [28], partner’s educational and occupational status [29], place of residence [30], and media exposure [31, 32] have all been significantly associated with IPV.

Intimate partner violence (IPV) has a significant and complex effect on women’s health, influencing their reproductive, emotional, and physical well-being [33, 34]. Women who are victims of intimate partner violence frequently sustain physical injuries that range from minor fractures and bruises to potentially life-threatening conditions such as internal organ damage and traumatic brain injuries [35]. IPV is associated with adverse maternal health outcomes such as miscarriage, preterm labor, low birth weight, and complications during childbirth, all of which can have long-term consequences for both the mother and the child [36]. Furthermore, IPV has serious reproductive repercussions because abused women are more likely to become pregnant against their will due to coercion, forced sexual encounters, or contraceptive sabotage [37]. Moreover, their inability to negotiate safe sex increases their risk of contracting sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV/AIDS [38].

Prolonged exposure to IPV may result in severe pain, disability, and even death due to homicide or suicide [39]. Survivors of intimate partner violence are considerably more likely to experience mental health issues such as depression [40], anxiety [41], post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [42], and suicidal thoughts [43, 44]. Problems with sleep, low self-esteem, and substance misuse as a coping mechanism may result from ongoing exposure to fear and emotional distress [45]. Long after the abuse has stopped, these mental health effects can persist, leading to a diminished quality of life [46] and long-term emotional instability [47].

Examining lifetime IPV, rather than past-year, provides a more comprehensive understanding of the cumulative burden, long-term consequences, and determinants of violence, capturing the experiences of women who may not have experienced IPV recently but have endured it at some point in their lives [48, 49]. In contexts such as East Africa, past-year IPV may be underreported due to stigma, fear of retaliation, or ongoing dependence on the perpetrator [17], whereas lifetime measures offer a broader window for disclosure and thus a more complete picture of the true magnitude. In addition, some forms of IPV, particularly sexual IPV, are relatively rare within a one-year period; restricting analysis to past-year cases would substantially reduce statistical power for multivariable modelling [24]. This approach facilitates comparisons across populations, informs targeted interventions, and guides the allocation of resources for prevention and support, ultimately promoting women’s health and well-being [50, 51].

Despite the significant burden of IPV in East Africa, there is a paucity of studies employing multilevel analyses to comprehensively examine the factors associated with IPV among reproductive-age women. It has received very little attention in the developing world, particularly in African nations. This is likely due to a variety of factors, including cultural barriers, poverty, a lack of social support, the absence of relevant government laws and institutions, and other factors [52].

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence and identify determinants of lifetime IPV among reproductive-age women in Kenya, Madagascar, Mozambique, and Tanzania using the 2021–2023 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data. Guided by the WHO ecological model of violence [53], we examined factors at the individual, relationship, community, and societal levels [54]. To account for the hierarchical nature of the DHS data, we employed multilevel logistic regression models with individuals nested within communities, and compared alternative model specifications to identify the best-fitting model. Model fit was assessed using the log-likelihood ratio test, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and we reported measures of between-community variation including the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), median odds ratio (MOR), and proportional change in variance (PCV) [55].

By identifying determinants at the individual, household, and community levels, the findings of this study will inform the development of targeted interventions and policies aimed at reducing IPV and improving the health and well-being of women in the region.

Methods

Study design, study setting, and study period

This is a multi-country, cross-sectional study using the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) datasets from four East African countries conducted between 2021 and 2023. The countries included in this study are Kenya, Mozambique, Madagascar, and Tanzania.

Data source and sampling procedure

The datasets were downloaded in STATA format from the DHS website (http://www.dhsprogram.com). The countries were selected based on the availability of recent, standardized DHS datasets. The DHS provides nationally representative data through four main standardized, pretested, and validated model questionnaires: the Household Questionnaire, the Woman’s Questionnaire, the Man’s Questionnaire, and the Children’s Questionnaire.

The DHS utilizes a two-stage, stratified cluster sampling method. In the first stage, clusters, or enumeration areas, are randomly selected from the sampling frame derived from the most recent publicly available national survey. In the second stage, systematic sampling is employed to select and interview a subset of households within the target population, ensuring that all households in each cluster are covered. These instruments gather data that are comparable across countries, covering key health indicators such as sociodemographic characteristics, fertility, mortality, family planning, reproductive health, decision-making authority, immunization coverage, child health, nutrition, mental health assessments, HIV/AIDS, controlling behaviors, and domestic violence. The data includes records for men, women, children, and households. For this study, the women’s (reproductive-age women) recode file was used.

The domestic violence module (d005) was used to extract data on IPV. A total of 34,610 women were selected and interviewed for this module. Of these, 7,931 women who had never been in a union (v501 = 0) were excluded from the analysis. Therefore, only women who were married, living with a partner, divorced, widowed, or separated were included to assess IPV experience. The resulting unweighted sample size was 27,219. After removing cases with missing responses for the violence questions (n = 6), the final unweighted sample was 27,213. The weighted sample used for analysis was 24,508 (Fig. 1). The distribution of respondents by country indicated that Kenyan women comprised 46.76% of the weighted sample, while the smallest proportion was from Tanzania (15.08%) (Table 1). The survey data encompassed 1,134 clusters (communities/primary sampling units) and 1,692 households across the study countries.

Study variable (dependent and independent variables)

Dependent variables

The dependent (outcome) variable of this study was intimate partner violence (IPV) among reproductive-age women, perpetrated by their partner or husband. The self-reported questionnaire used to measure IPV included 13 Yes/No questions from modified Conflict Tactics Scales of Straus [56]. A respondent was classified as having experienced IPV if she reported at least one form of sexual, emotional, or physical violence. The outcome variable, IPV, was dichotomized as follows: 1 = Yes (experienced IPV) and 0 = No (did not experience IPV). Details of the questions used to assess IPV are provided in Table 2.

Independent variables

Variable selection was guided by the WHO ecological model of violence [53],, which conceptualises IPV as resulting from interacting factors at the individual, relationship, community, and societal levels. Individual-related factors encompassed marital status, educational status, sex of the household head, current working status, age at first cohabitation, number of children under five in the household, current pregnancy status, the respondent’s justification of wife-beating, cigarette/tobacco use, fear of the husband/partner (most of the time or sometimes), witnessing maternal abuse, jealousy of the husband/partner, the husband/partner’s alcohol drinking habits, and wealth index (categorized as poor, middle, or rich). Age is categorized into groups according to the DHS standard age categories (e.g., 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49).

Community-related factors included media exposure (newspaper/magazine, radio, or television), residence, and country. Media exposure was measured by combining exposure to TV, radio, and newspapers. Respondents who had been exposed to any of these media were recorded as “yes,” while those who had not were recorded as “no.” Respondents were further categorized as “yes” if they had been exposed to at least one of the three media sources and “no if they had not been exposed to any.

Data management and analysis

Data analysis

The data were appended, stored, cleaned, recoded, and analyzed using STATA (version 14.0). Frequency tables and graphs were generated as part of the descriptive analysis. To ensure reliable conclusions, the sample was weighted/1,000,000 using the sampling weight (d005). A two-level mixed-effect multinomial logistic regression model was employed due to the hierarchical nature of the dataset. Four models were fitted: a null model with no explanatory variables, a model with individual-level explanatory variables, a model with community-level explanatory variables, and a model with both individual- and community-level explanatory variables.

Model comparison was conducted using the log-likelihood ratio (LR) test to account for the hierarchical data structure and differing numbers of parameters. The model with the lowest LR was selected as the final model. To justify the use of mixed-effects analysis over standard multinomial logistic regression, the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated. The proportional change in variance (PCV) was also computed in the final model to quantify the variability explained by random effects. PCV quantifies the percentage reduction in the between-cluster variance when explanatory variables are added to the model, indicating how much of the variation in the outcome is explained by the included predictors compared to the empty model. Model fit was further evaluated using Deviance and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with the model having the lowest AIC considered the best fit. Additionally, the Median Odds Ratio (MOR) [57] was used to assess the variation in intimate partner violence (IPV) between clusters.

Variables with a p-value less than 0.2 in bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a mean VIF of 1.81 indicating no significant collinearity concerns. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was performed, confirming that the final model was well fitted (p = 0.61). Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were reported for factors statistically significantly associated with IPV.

Four hierarchical logistic regression models were fitted to examine factors associated with intimate partner violence (IPV). Model 1 was an empty (null) model with no predictors, used to assess the baseline variation in IPV across clusters. Model 2 included only individual-level variables to evaluate their associations with IPV. Model 3 incorporated only community-level variables, excluding individual-level factors, to assess their independent effects. Finally, Model 4 was the full model including both individual- and community-level variables, allowing examination of their combined influence on IPV. This stepwise modeling approach helped to disentangle the contributions of variables at different levels.

Model comparison was conducted using likelihood ratio tests and AIC values to identify the most parsimonious model with optimal fit. Although the final model was selected based on these fit statistics, results from all four models are presented to provide a comprehensive understanding of how variable inclusion at different levels affects associations and variance components. This approach illustrates the incremental value of individual- and community-level factors in explaining IPV.

In this study, the term community refers to the primary sampling unit (PSU) used in the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), representing geographically defined clusters of neighboring households. Community-level factors included media exposure, place of residence (urban or rural), and country of residence. These variables capture social and environmental influences on intimate partner violence (IPV) beyond individual characteristics, enabling a comprehensive assessment of IPV determinants across four East African countries.

Results

Sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics of reproductive age women in East Africa

For the final analysis, a total of 24,508 weighted samples of women of reproductive age were selected from four East African countries. The mean age of the respondents was 32.31 years, with a standard deviation of 8.62 years. More than one-fifth of the respondents were aged 25 to 29 years. 46.8% of the women had attained primary education, while 28.1% had secondary education. Two-thirds of the respondents lived in rural areas (67.26%), and 63.11% were currently working. Regarding marital status, most (63.57%) respondents were married, while 18.78% were living with their partner.

Almost two-thirds of respondents (62.28%) had their first cohabitation at age 18 years or above, while 34.66% of respondents use tobacco. Among the respondents, 66.84% had children under five, and 8.01% were currently pregnant. On partner dynamics, 41.1% expressed fear of their partner, and 35.36% justified wife-beating. About 35.04% of respondents reported that their partner consumed alcohol, and 48.22% stated that their husband/partner became jealous when they talked to other men.

In terms of wealth, 43.63% were categorized as rich, 36.58% as poor, and 19.79% as middle-income, while more than two-thirds (72.56%) stated that they were exposed to at least one form of media (magazine, radio, or television (Table 3).

Pooled prevalence of IPV among reproductive age women in East Africa

The pooled prevalence of IPV among reproductive age women in East Africa was estimated to be 40.18%, with a 95% confidence interval of 34.83% to 45.53%. Based on country-specific data, the estimated prevalence of IPV among reproductive age women in Kenya was found to be 46.34% with a 95% confidence interval of 45.43% to 47.25%, while the prevalence of IPV among reproductive age women in Madagascar was found to be 41.06% with a 95% confidence interval of 39.76% to 42.36%. Additionally, the estimated prevalence of IPV among reproductive age women in Tanzania was found to be 39.21% with a 95% confidence interval of 37.64% to 40.78%, while the prevalence in Mozambique was estimated at 34.03% with a 95% confidence interval of 32.53% to 35.53% (Fig. 2). Specific to the violence type, the estimated pooled prevalence of emotional violence was 29.82% (95% CI: 23.72%, 35.92%), followed by physical violence at a prevalence of 27.56% (95% CI: 22.14%, 32.99%). The pooled prevalence of sexual violence was 9.94% (95% CI: 7.70%, 12.18%) (Fig. 3).

Random effect model fit statics

The null model was run to assess whether the data supported evaluating randomness at the community level. The ICC value indicated that 10.43% of IPV variation was attributable to cluster differences. The MOR result showed that the probability of experiencing IPV varied 1.80 times between high- and low-risk clusters, reflecting heterogeneity between clusters. In the final model, the PCV revealed that both individual- and community-level factors accounted for approximately 36.84% of the variation in IPV. Model III was chosen as the best-fitting model due to its lowest deviation, highest log-likelihood, and lowest AIC (Table 4).

Factors significantly associated with IPV among reproductive age women in East Africa (Model III)

Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) estimates were obtained from multilevel logistic regression to identify variables significantly associated with intimate partner violence (IPV) among reproductive-age women. Various demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors were found to be significantly associated with IPV among reproductive-age women in East Africa. Respondents aged 25–29 (AOR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.00–1.38), 30–34 (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.07–1.49), 35–39 (AOR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.04–1.44), 40–44 (AOR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.05–1.48), and 45–49 (AOR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.17–1.67) had significantly higher odds of experiencing IPV compared to those aged 15–19. Women with primary education 1.42(1.25, 1.62) (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.62) and secondary education (AOR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.81) had higher odds of experiencing IPV compared to those women with higher education.

Marital status emerged as a strong predictor; women who were widowed, divorced, or separated were nearly twice as likely to experience IPV compared to married women (AOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.66–2.04). Women who began cohabitation before the age of 18 had higher odds of experiencing IPV compared to those who started at 18 years or older (AOR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.19). Women whose household head was male had slightly higher odds of suffering IPV compared to those with a female household head (AOR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.02–1.21). Women who were currently working had slightly higher odds of IPV (AOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.08–1.25) compared to non-working women.

Several behavioral and community-level factors also influenced IPV risk. Witnessing maternal abuse during childhood more than doubled the likelihood of experiencing IPV (AOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.82–2.11), while women whose husbands consumed alcohol were similarly at an elevated risk (AOR = 2.38, 95% CI: 2.22–2.55). Attitudinal factors such as justifying wife-beating (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.27–1.46) and experiencing jealousy from their husband/partner when communicating with other males (AOR = 3.44, 95% CI: 3.23–3.67) were strongly associated with increased IPV odds. Other factors, including cigarette/tobacco use, and fear of a partner, were also significantly associated with IPV. Women who used cigarette/tobacco were more likely to suffer from IPV (AOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.26), while women who feared their husband or partner were more than three times as likely to experience IPV (AOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 3.33–3.81).

Furthermore, women residing in rural areas (AOR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03–1.23) and those exposed to media (AOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.08–1.28) were more likely to report IPV compared to their urban and non-exposed counterparts, respectively. Lastly, country-level differences were observed: women residing in Kenya (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.11–1.43) had a higher likelihood of experiencing IPV compared to women residing in Mozambique (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a high prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among reproductive-age women in East Africa, with significant variations across countries. The pooled prevalence of IPV was estimated at 40.18% (95% CI: 34.83%, 45.53%), with emotional violence being the most common type (29.82%), followed by physical (27.56%) and sexual violence (9.94%). Country-specific data showed the highest IPV prevalence in Kenya (46.34%), followed by Madagascar (41.06%) and Tanzania (39.21%), while Mozambique had the lowest at 34.03%. The current findings align with a WHO study indicating that nearly 40% of African women who have ever been in a relationship have experienced IPV [58]. Similarly, a multi-country analysis of DHS data (2010–2018) from 27 Sub-Saharan African countries found that 36% of ever-partnered urban women of reproductive age experienced IPV [59]. Another multi-level analysis of DHS data (2010–2016) from 18 Sub-Saharan countries reported a prevalence of 37% [60], further supporting these findings. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis of global studies found a similar IPV prevalence of 37.3% [8], consistent with the current study.

The prevalence of IPV in the current study is higher than that reported in a previous multi-country Sub-Saharan study, which analyzed DHS data from 2015 to 2018 and found a prevalence rate of 30.7% [61]. Meanwhile, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 366 primary studies conducted between 2000 and 2018 worldwide estimated that 27% of women experienced IPV, which is more than 13% lower than the current findings [7]. Several factors may explain the higher IPV prevalence in the current study. Seasonal variations play a role, as post-pandemic financial crises, economic instability, and rising unemployment—factors that exacerbate IPV risk—have contributed to increasing IPV rates in recent years [62, 63].

The findings of the current prevalence was lower than a multi-level study carried out in Liberia [64]. slightly lower than a among pregnant women in Sub-Saharan Africa [65]. On the other hand, the prevalence of IPV among women in 28 European countries was estimated at 51.7%, which is higher than the findings of the current study [66]. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in IPV measurement and reporting, sociocultural and gender norms, broader IPV definitions, stronger reporting mechanisms, economic and partner-related factors [67], and historical and policy influences [68].

Several individual-level characteristics have been significantly associated with IPV. Respondents aged 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, and 45–49 had significantly higher odds of experiencing IPV compared to those aged 15–19. The current finding that women aged 25–49 had higher odds of experiencing IPV than those aged 15–19 is consistent with multi-country analyses from sub-Saharan Africa showing greater IPV risk among women in their mid-20s to late 30s, likely reflecting longer union duration, higher cohabitation/marital exposure, pregnancy/child-rearing stressors, and economic strain that can intensify controlling behaviors and partner conflict [69, 70].

Women with primary or secondary education had a higher likelihood of experiencing IPV compared to those with higher education, while no significant difference was observed among women with no formal education. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that educated women may challenge traditional gender norms, leading to resistance from male partners [16]. Additionally, marital status showed a strong correlation with IPV, as divorced, separated, or widowed women faced nearly twice the risk compared to married women. Earlier studies suggest that societal stigma surrounding divorce or separation, along with economic instability, may increase women’s vulnerability to IPV [29].

Women who began cohabitation before the age of 18 had 11% higher odds of experiencing IPV compared to those who started cohabitation at 18 or older. Early cohabitation may be associated with vulnerabilities such as lower bargaining power in relationships, limited knowledge about healthy relationships, and increased exposure to partners who engage in controlling or violent behaviors [71].

The findings highlight several community-level, partner or husband related and behavioral factors that significantly increase IPV. Women who witnessed their mothers being abused as children are more than twice as likely to experience IPV in adulthood. Growing up in a violent home conditions individuals to accept IPV as a natural part of relationships [72]. Additionally, women who justify wife-beating are significantly more prone to IPV due to deeply entrenched gender norms, economic dependence, and reduced help-seeking behavior, which create a cycle of vulnerability and acceptance. In many patriarchal societies, IPV is normalized, and women who internalize these beliefs are less likely to perceive abuse as a violation of their rights, making them more susceptible to repeated violence [73]. Women whose husbands or partners exhibited jealousy when they communicated with other men and those who feared their husbands or partners were more than three times as likely to experience IPV. Jealousy-driven possessiveness often leads to controlling behaviors, restrictions on women’s social interactions, and ultimately, emotional, physical, and sexual violence. Partner/husband alcohol consumption has also been identified as a more than twice more likely to experiencing IPV. Alcohol use has been widely linked to increased aggression, impaired judgment, and a greater likelihood of violent behavior within intimate relationships [74]. This association is in line with prior studies conducted in Ethiopia [75], East Africa [76], Rwanda [77], and Nigeria [78] other Sub-Saharan African countries [65].

Furthermore, women residing in rural areas and those exposed to media were more likely to report IPV. Rural residence has been associated with increased IPV risk due to factors such as economic dependence, social isolation, and limited access to support services [79]. However, while media exposure was expected to have a protective effect against IPV by raising awareness, this study found that women exposed to media had a higher likelihood of experiencing IPV. This contradicts previous findings that associate media exposure with reduced acceptance of IPV [31]. One possible explanation is that as increased access to information raises awareness and encourage women to recognize and report violence [80], thereby inflating prevalence estimates. Women in Kenya had a 26% higher likelihood of experiencing IPV compared to those in Mozambique., which may be attributed to variations in societal attitudes toward gender-based violence, reporting mechanisms, and women’s empowerment initiatives [81].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its large sample size (27,265) drawn from four East African national recent DHS datasets, ensuring robust and generalizable findings. The extensive dataset provides sufficient statistical power to assess the true impact of independent variables in a region-wide survey. However, the study is limited by the cross-sectional nature of DHS data, which prevents causal inferences. Some variables included in our models, such as sex of household head, duration of cohabitation, and current marital status (widowed/divorced/separated), may represent consequences of IPV rather than determinants, particularly in cross-sectional data. Their inclusion was based on theoretical relevance within the ecological model and their availability in the DHS datasets; however, we advise caution in interpreting these associations, as reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal studies are needed to better disentangle these temporal relationships. Additionally, DHS data exclude highly vulnerable groups such as homeless individuals, refugees, incarcerated populations, and those in institutions. Additionally, IPV is a sensitive topic, and underreporting due to fear of stigma or retaliation remains a potential limitation. Furthermore, the study relied on DHS secondary data, which lacked predefined measurement tools for specific variables and excluded women beyond reproductive age.

Conclusion

This study provides crucial insights into the prevalence and determinants of IPV among reproductive-age women in East Africa. The findings underscore the high prevalence and multifaceted nature of IPV, highlighting individual, household, and community-level factors that contribute to its persistence. Addressing IPV requires a holistic approach that includes legal, social, and economic interventions aimed at transforming societal norms and ensuring the protection and empowerment of women. Future research should explore qualitative methods to capture the lived experiences of IPV survivors and further investigate the role of media in shaping IPV dynamics.

Implications for intervention and prevention

The high prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV) among reproductive-age women in East Africa highlights the urgent need for comprehensive intervention and prevention strategies. At the societal level, interventions that challenge the acceptance of wife-beating and promote gender-equitable norms—such as the SASA (Start, Awareness, Support and Action)! community mobilization approach—have shown effectiveness in reducing IPV [82]. Relationship-level interventions, including alcohol harm reduction programs for men, could address one of the strongest risk factors identified in our study. A multi-pronged approach is essential, encompassing community-based programs that challenge harmful gender norms and promote respectful relationships, as well as the integration of IPV screening and counseling into routine healthcare services, particularly during antenatal care [83, 84]. Furthermore, interventions should focus on women’s economic empowerment through access to education, job training, and financial resources, alongside strengthening legal frameworks and law enforcement to protect women from violence and ensure perpetrators are held accountable [14, 70].
