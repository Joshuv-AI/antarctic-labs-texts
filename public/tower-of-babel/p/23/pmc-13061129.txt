From fields to landscapes: pheromone trapping and feeding damage of Sitona lineatus (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) during range expansion in Canadian pulse crops
Authors: Maggie B MacDonald, Boyd A Mori, Maya L Evenden, Dong H Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pea leaf weevil, Sitona lineatus L. (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) is an invasive pest of field pea, Pisum sativum L. (Fabaceae), and faba bean, Vicia faba L. (Fabaceae) undergoing range expansion in Canada. Pheromone-baited pitfall traps were deployed across commercial field pea sites in major pulse-growing regions of Alberta, Canada to monitor adult populations and assess feeding damage on host plants. Trap capture of S. lineatus indicates that pheromone-baited traps can serve as a predictive monitoring tool for both range expansion and local feeding populations. This study is the first to demonstrate that pheromone-baited trap capture of S. lineatus predicts subsequent adult notching damage on field pea and next generation S. lineatus adults. Abiotic factors including temperature, humidity, and precipitation, influenced trap capture, and landscape composition and heterogeneity affected weevil abundance at multiple spatial scales. These findings highlight the combined effects of local crop characteristics, environmental conditions, and landscape-level dynamics in shaping S. lineatus populations. The study supports the use of pheromone-baited traps for monitoring of S. lineatus, providing critical information to guide integrated pest management strategies for S. lineatus in field pea and faba bean agroecosystems.Keywords: invasive species, agroecosystem, landscape composition, pea leaf weevil, pulse

Introduction

Worldwide intensification of agriculture at both local and regional scales fundamentally changes landscapes (Tscharntke et al. 2005, Baessler and Klotz 2006, Fahrig et al. 2011, Tscharntke et al. 2012). The landscape pattern and spatial heterogeneity of agroecosystems influences ecological systems and functional responses, including animal movement (Fahrig 2007), population persistence (Fraterrigo et al. 2009), species interactions (Polis et al. 2004), and overall ecosystem functions (Wiens 2002, Fahrig et al. 2011). Crop density, field sizes, and landscape composition impact insect pest and natural enemy populations (Fahrig and Nuttle 2005, Ali et al. 2020).

Most insect herbivores move across the landscape to search for resources, making it necessary to assess landscape patterns to understand pest population dynamics in cropping systems (Landis et al. 2000, Rand et al. 2006, Tscharntke et al. 2007, 2012, Bianchi et al. 2013, Levi-Mourao et al. 2024). Simplification of agricultural landscapes can unintentionally increase host-finding by agricultural pests and subsequently impact pest population dynamics (Robinson and Sutherland 2002, Bianchi et al. 2006). Landscape patterns, in addition to local field conditions, affect pest outbreaks and pest control by natural enemies (With 2002). More pest insects occur in agricultural monocultures than in habitats with diverse vegetation (Andow 1991, Bommarco and Banks 2003, Mazzi and Dorn 2012, Plath et al. 2012). The “resource concentration hypothesis” reasons that specialist herbivores accumulate in areas of high host plant concentration compared to areas of diverse vegetation (Root 1973). This argument posits that plant diversity interferes with host plant finding by specialist insect pests (Mazzi and Dorn 2012). Large coverage by suitable host plants on the landscape can attract insect pests and result in large herbivore pest populations in crops. The “resource dilution hypothesis” predicts that specialist herbivores become evenly distributed across large areas of host crops, resulting in diminished local pest densities (Schneider et al. 2015). Landscapes with less host plant area can result in crowding of specialist herbivores and increased local pest densities (Schneider et al. 2015). Pest population dynamics in cropping landscapes is a major problem for agricultural production (Ferreira and Godoy 2014, Garcia et al. 2021). Effective planning and implementation of integrated pest management (IPM) strategies promote sustainable and resilient pest control in agroecosystems (Hassanali et al. 2008).

Legumes (Fabales: Fabaceae) have Near Eastern origins and are some of the world’s oldest domesticated crops, yet they continue to play a vital role in modern agriculture by contributing to global food and nutritional security (De Candolle 1883, Zohary and Hopf 2000, Abbo et al. 2012, Tiwari and Singh 2015, Bohra et al. 2022). The edible seeds of legumes, known as pulses, are economically important for human consumption, animal feed, and various industrial applications (Mikic 2015, Rubiales and Mikic 2015, Siddiq et al. 2022). With nearly three times the protein content of rice and double that of wheat (Roy et al. 2017), pulses play a key role in addressing global malnutrition (Pratap et al. 2020). Additionally, pulses can disrupt weed and disease cycles that occur in cereal crops and enhance soil nitrogen levels (Bliss and Hardarson 1993, Martínez-Romero 2003, Corre-Hellou and Crozat 2005, Powers and Thavarajah 2019). Global pulse production is expected to increase due to the nutritional value (high protein, high fiber, low fat, and low glycemic index) of these crops (Knodel and Shrestha 2018). In North America, pulse production continues to expand in the Northern Great Plains, the Palouse area of the Pacific Northwest, and in southern Ontario, Canada (Knodel and Shrestha 2018). The globalization of agricultural production has facilitated the expansion of pulse cultivation into new regions, while also increasing the risk of pulse pest range expansion.

The pea leaf weevil, Sitona lineatus L. (Coleoptera: Curculionidae) is a global pest of leguminous crops (Hoebeke and Wheeler 1985, Cárcamo et al. 2018). Native to Europe and northern Africa, S. lineatus was first detected in North America in the 1930’s near Victoria, British Columbia, Canada (Downes 1938). Sitona lineatus has since established in Washington, Oregon, northern California, northwestern Idaho and other northwestern regions of the United States of America (Hubert and Reeher 1961, Hoebeke and Wheeler 1985). Detection in the northeastern United States of America occurred by 1984 (Hoebeke and Wheeler 1985) and Florida by 2002 (Bloem et al. 2002). It was first detected in the Prairie Provinces of Canada in southern Alberta in 1997 (Soroka and Carcamo 2006, Coles et al. 2008) and is continuing to expand its range in North America (Vankosky et al. 2009, Marsico et al. 2010, Olfert et al. 2012, MacDonald et al. 2024).

Sitona lineatus is univoltine in North America but undergoes two periods of adult activity in the growing season (Schotzko and O’Keeffe 1986). Adults emerge from overwintering sites in the spring and feed on a variety of plants in the Fabaceae family. Adult weevils orient to field pea, Pisum sativum L. (Fabaceae), and faba bean, Vicia faba L. (Fabaceae) for mating and oviposition (Nielsen and Jensen 1993, Landon et al. 1995). Flight is initiated when temperatures reach 12.5 °C (Hamon et al. 1987), and weevils use olfactory and visual cues to locate hosts and mates (Blight et al. 1984, Landon et al. 1995, Landon et al. 1997, Hanavan and Bosque-Pérez 2017, Evenden 2018). Females oviposit on the soil surface near the base of seedlings (Jackson and Macdougall 1920, Hoebeke and Wheeler 1985). Larvae burrow through the soil to feed on root nodules that contain the nitrogen-fixing bacterium, Rhizobium leguminosarum Frank (Rhizobiales: Rhizobiaceae) (George et al. 1962, Johnson and O’Keeffe 1981). Larvae feed and develop through five instars and feeding activity inhibits nitrogen availability to the plant and results in reduced crop yield (Hamon et al. 1984, Jensen et al. 1989, Cárcamo et al. 2015). Pupation occurs in the soil and new generation adults emerge in July to August (Cárcamo and Vankosky 2011, Cárcamo et al. 2018). In autumn, new generation adults disperse to perennial leguminous crops or field margins to feed and overwinter (Jackson and Macdougall 1920, Schotzko and O’Keeffe 1986). Warmer winters are predicted to increase overwintering survival and post-overwintering fitness, facilitating further S. lineatus invasion (Wijerathna et al. 2024).

Current monitoring tactics of S. lineatus populations in field pea and faba bean include adult feeding surveys to estimate the distribution and density of S. lineatus populations in the Canadian Prairie Provinces (Vankosky et al. 2009, Morley-Senkler et al. 2020, Prairie Pest Monitoring Network 2025, Vankosky and Gavloski 2025). This method assumes that notching damage is positively associated with S. lineatus density (Cantot 1986, Nielsen 1990), but temperature also influences feeding activity (Morley-Senkler et al. 2020). Pheromone-baited pitfall traps can also monitor adult movements and range expansion of S. lineatus and are sensitive at low population densities (Evenden 2018, St. Onge et al. 2018, MacDonald et al. 2024). Because larval feeding causes economic damage (Vankosky et al. 2009), monitoring larvae or measuring larval damage would be beneficial but larval assessment is difficult and time consuming compared to adult damage assessment (Wijerathna et al. 2021). Integration of these monitoring tools at a landscape level is vital for the development of an IPM program for S. lineatus in its expanded range in North America.

Here, we examine factors that influence range expansion and host use by S. lineatus in Alberta, Canada and support the development of an IPM strategy for S. lineatus in field pea and faba bean. The objectives of this study are to (i) evaluate range expansion of S. lineatus in Alberta using pheromone-baited pitfall traps in pulse growing regions, (ii) test for relationships between S. lineatus captured in pheromone-baited traps and adult notch feeding damage, (iii) use abiotic variables including temperature, relative humidity, and soil moisture content to predict the number of S. lineatus captured in pheromone-baited traps, and (iv) determine if landcover composition and heterogeneity drives S. lineatus abundance collected at field pea sites.

Methods

Field Set Up and Weevil Collection

The study was conducted in Alberta, Canada, a major pulse-growing province in western North America. To assess range expansion of S. lineatus in Alberta, pheromone -baited pitfall traps were deployed in commercial field pea fields in 2018 (n = 36 sites) and 2019 (n = 42 sites) across four pulse-growing regions at the edge of the known S. lineatus range in the province at the time (Fig. 1). The study regions included the Peace region in the northwest (2018: n = 12 sites, 2019: n = 17 sites), the Capital region around the city of Edmonton (2018: n = 6 sites, 2019: n = 8 sites), the East region toward the Saskatchewan border (2018: n = 6 sites, 2019: n = 9 sites), and the Central region in the middle of the province (2018: n = 12 sites, 2019: n = 8 sites). Sites were separated by at least 5 km to ensure population independence. Trap transects were established 1 m from the field edge after seeding and before crop emergence in the spring. Trap transects ran parallel to the field edge with four pitfall traps separated by 25 m. To directly compare weevil abundance between faba bean and field pea crops, additional faba bean sites (2018: n = 8 sites, 2019: n = 9 sites) were established near (<5 km) a subset of established field pea sites. This design directly evaluates host plant species as a predictor of S. lineatus abundance in its expanded range.

Pitfall traps made from 473 ml plastic cups (Solo, Lakeforest, Illinois, United States) were embedded into the soil with rims flush with the soil surface. Traps contained propylene glycol (30%) (Home Hardware, Edmonton, Alberta, Canada) to kill and preserve captured arthropods. Traps were positioned randomly along the transect, with two pitfall traps baited with aggregation pheromone (10 mg 4-methyl-3,5-heptanedione) released from a gray rubber septa lure (186.55.2, Chemtica Internacional S.A., Santo Domingo, Costa Rica) and two un-baited traps that served as controls. A 15 × 15 cm piece of white corrugated plastic (Home Depot, Canada) was positioned directly above each trap and secured to the ground with 15 cm nails to serve as a rain cover and to keep out large, non-target vertebrates. For the baited traps, lures were suspended by a wire from the corrugated plastic trap covers (Evenden et al. 2016, St. Onge et al. 2018). Traps were serviced biweekly, captured arthropods removed, and the propylene glycol refreshed in each of two trapping periods in the spring (May to June) and autumn (late-July to September) in both years of the study. Sitona lineatus were removed from the preservative, counted and separated by sex following Jackson and Macdougall (1920). Before the fall adult activity period in late-July, trap placement was re-randomized along each transect and traps were baited with new lures.

To determine if S. lineatus adult feeding damage was related to the number of weevils captured in pheromone-baited traps, feeding damage was measured by counting individual “U” shaped notches on leaves of field pea and faba bean (Cantot 1989). In 2018, the notches were counted on the closest 10 plants to one pheromone-baited and one control randomly selected along each transect when plants were at the fifth to sixth node (Vankosky and Gavloski 2025). In 2018, notch sampling was conducted at eight sites in the Peace, 13 in the Capital region, six sites in the East, and 16 in the Central region. In 2019, five plants were similarly assessed at 13 sites in the Peace region, 16 in the Capital region, 14 in the East region, and 11 in the Central region.

Climatic Data Collection and Processing

In 2018 and 2019, a subset of field pea sites in the Central (2018: n = 12 sites, 2019: n = 9) and Capital (2018: n = 6, 2019: n = 7) regions were selected to test for effects of abiotic factors on the number of S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps in the spring when weevils move from overwintering sites to primary reproductive hosts. Given that S. lineatus dispersal is temperature dependent (Olfert et al. 2012, Carcamo et al. 2018), we hypothesized that abiotic factors such as temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, and soil moisture content would influence weevil dispersal and host-finding in the spring. To test this hypothesis, daily weather station data were obtained from Alberta Climate Information Services (https://acis.alberta.ca), using stations located within 10 km of each field site. Mean temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation values were calculated for the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) week preceding each trap collection. The number of weevils captured were pooled by ISO week and summarized as weekly totals per site for the spring collection period.

To assess the influence of soil moisture on S. lineatus trap capture, daily satellite-derived soil moisture data were obtained from Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada open data (https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset). Soil moisture values were extracted from raster files within a 2 km buffer around each site using the terra (Hijmans et al. 2026) and sp packages (Pebesma and Bivand 2005, Bivand et al. 2013) in RStudio. The coordinate reference system was World Geodetic System 1984 (WHS84) and the projection was Albers Equal Area (Albers 1805). Weekly soil moisture content (%) was calculated for the week preceding each trap collection.

Landscape Variables

We examined the effect of landcover type on the number of S. lineatus collected at pea sites. The Annual Crop Inventory (Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada) (https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset) was used to extract landcover proportion data from circular buffers (250 m, 500 m, 1 km) around pea sites using the terra (Hijmans et al. 2026) and sp packages (Pebesma and Bivand 2005, Bivand et al. 2013) in RStudio. Landcover classes of interest consisted of Water, Urban, Shrubland, Wetland, Grassland, Pasture/Forage, Cereals, Grains, Pea, and Forest types. The landcover data is created using optical (Landsat-5, AWiFS, DMC) and radar (Radarsat-2) based satellite images. This delivers an overall target accuracy of at least 85% at a final spatial resolution of 30 m (https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset). The accuracies of crop classes are 90% for Alberta. The coordinate reference system was World Geodetic System 1984 (WHS84) and the projection was Albers Equal Area (Albers 1805).

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses and data presentation were conducted in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024). Data manipulation was performed with tidyverse (Wickham et al. 2019). Figures were generated using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016) and visreg (Breheny and Burchett 2017). Model assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance, and over- and under-dispersion were tested using DHARMa for model validation (Hartig 2022). Sites where S. lineatus were absent in both collection periods were excluded from analyses. Model selection was based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), and backward model selection was used from MASS (Venables and Ripley 2002). Post-hoc testing was conducted with emmeans (Lenth 2025). Final model structures were selected based on the best fit to the data and the distributional properties of the response variables.

The number of weevils in pitfall traps captured in all pulse-growing regions were compared using a zero-inflated generalized linear mixed effects model (GLMM) with a negative binomial distribution using glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017). Trap treatment (baited vs. control), collection period (spring and fall), and pulse-growing region were included as fixed effects, while site nested within region was designated as a random effect. A separate analysis used the subset of paired pea and faba bean sites to compare the number of weevils captured in each host crop. We used a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution to compare the number of weevils captured by crop type with lme4 (Bates et al. 2015), with similar fixed and random effects. A zero-inflated GLMM with a negative binomial distribution using glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017) used spring weevil capture to predict fall weevil capture. Spring weevil values were log-transformed prior to analysis and site nested within region was designated as a random effect. To test if adult S. lineatus feeding notches differ by pulse-growing region and pitfall traps (baited vs. control), we analyzed notching at all field pea sites. The total number of notches per plant was log-transformed and modeled using a zero-inflated GLMM with Gaussian distribution (Brooks et al. 2017). Pulse-growing region and trap treatment were included as fixed effects. To account for regional variation in plant age, seeding date was used as a random effect in addition to site nested within region.

To test whether spring S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps could predict feeding notch damage, the dataset was filtered to include spring counts from baited and un-baited pitfall traps at pea sites. Similarly, to evaluate whether feeding notches in the spring could predict fall S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps, the dataset was filtered to include fall counts from baited and un-baited pitfall traps at pea sites. Models were created using the glmmTMB package with negative binomial distributions (Brooks et al. 2017). The random effect was site nested in region, and predictor variables (both spring weevils captured in pitfall traps and feeding notches, respectively) were log-transformed for analysis.

Weather and Soil Moisture Analysis

To evaluate the effect of weather variables on the number of S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps, GLMMs were created with glmmTMB and negative binomial distributions (Brooks et al. 2017). Correlation of covariates was tested using Kendall’s τ coefficient with a 0.3 threshold indicating collinearity (Kendall 1938). Due to collinearity between precipitation and relative humidity, these variables were tested in separate models. Additionally, to examine whether soil moisture content predicts the number of S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps, GLMMs were created with the same package and error distribution, and week and site nested within region were specified as random effects. Average soil moisture content was the predictor variable and weevils captured in pitfall traps was the response variable.

Landscape-Scale Analysis

To assess the effect of landcover type surrounding sites on the number of S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps (baited and un-baited) at pea sites, GLMMs were created using glmmTMB with negative binomial error distributions (Brooks et al. 2017). Buffers of 250 m, 500 m, and 1 km were used to calculate the proportion of each landcover type. Each landcover type was treated as a covariate, with weevils captured in pitfall traps as the response variable. Correlation of covariates was tested using Kendall’s τ coefficient with a 0.3 threshold indicating collinearity (Kendall 1938). Due to collinearity between landcover types, models were built using covariates that did not exhibit multicollinearity. Backward model selection was applied to identify the best-fitting model. Additionally, to examine the influence of landscape diversity on S. lineatus captured, landscape diversity was quantified using the Shannon and Simpson diversity indices, as well as evenness with vegan (Oksanen et al. 2017). These GLMMs were also created with glmmTMB using negative binomial error distributions (Brooks et al. 2017), with site designated as a random effect.

Results

Weevil Relative Abundance

Pheromone-baited pitfall traps captured significantly more S. lineatus than un-baited control pitfall traps in 2018 (χ2 = 54.1, df = 1, P < 0.0001) and 2019 (χ2 = 82.2, df = 1, P < 0.0001) across all regions sampled. A similar number of weevils was captured in the spring and fall trapping periods in 2018 (χ2 = 1.57, df = 1, P = 0.21) and 2019 (χ2 = 0.264, df = 1, P = 0.61). Although more S. lineatus were captured at field pea than faba bean sites in both years (Fig. 2), crop type significantly influenced S. lineatus capture only in 2018 (χ2 = 4.18, df = 1, P = 0.04) and not in 2019 (χ2 = 0.46, df = 1, P = 0.50). The number of weevils captured in pitfall traps at each site differed significantly by region in 2018 (χ2 = 12.1, df = 3, P = 0.007) and in 2019(χ2 = 8.2, df = 3, P = 0.04) (Fig. 3). In 2018, average S. lineatus capture was lowest in the East region, followed by the Peace region, while sites in the Capital and Central regions had similar mean captures. In 2019, S. lineatus capture in the East region did not differ significantly from other regions, whereas the Peace region had significantly lower average captures that the Capital region but was similar to the remaining regions. Sites in the East had significantly fewer S. lineatus captured in pitfall traps in 2018 compared to the Central region, but in 2019 capture in the East region was similar to the other regions.

Number of Notches Counted on Field Pea

Weevils were attracted to pheromone lures, and more feeding notches occurred on plants around pheromone-baited than un-baited control traps in 2018 (χ2 = 13.7, df = 1, P = 0.0002) and 2019 (χ2 = 23.6, df = 1, P < 0.0001). There were regional differences in notch counts in 2018 (χ2 = 10.6, df = 3, P = 0.01) and 2019 (χ2 = 48.6, df = 3, P < 0.0001) that reflected the number of weevils captured (Fig. 3). Lower S. lineatus capture rates in the Peace, and to a lesser extent, the East region, led to reduced feeding notches recorded in those regions.

There was a significant relationship between the number of spring weevils captured in pitfall traps and the total number of notches counted per plant around pitfall traps in 2018 (χ2 = 11.4, df = 1, P = 0.0007, estimate = 0.32 ± 0.09) and 2019 (χ2 = 16.1, df = 1, P < 0.001, estimate = 0.27 ± 0.07) suggesting that spring trap catch indicates feeding damage in field peas (Fig. 5). Spring notch counts around pitfall traps were significantly associated with the number of fall generation S. lineatus adults captured in 2018 (χ2 = 18.9, df = 1, P < 0.0001, estimate = 0.83 ± 0.191) and 2019 (χ2 = 29.8, df = 1, P < 0.0001, estimate = 1.68 ± 0.307) (Fig. 5).

Abiotic Variables Predicting Weevil Relative Abundance

Few S. lineatus were captured at high ambient temperatures in both 2018 (χ2 = 7.38, df = 1, P = 0.007, estimate = −0.44 ± 0.162) and 2019 (χ2 = 9.26, df = 1, P = 0.002, estimate = −0.69 ± 0.228) (Fig. 6). Average accumulated precipitation was not a significant predictor of S. lineatus trap capture in 2018 (χ2 = 0.34, df = 1, P = 0.56) but negatively influenced trap catch in 2019 (χ2 = 11.4, df = 1, P = 0.0007, estimate = −0.03 ± 0.01) (Fig. 6). Relative humidity had no effect on weevil counts in 2018 (χ2 = 0.96, df = 1, P = 0.33) but negatively affected trap catch in 2019 (χ2 = 15.2, df = 1, P < 0.0001, estimate = −0.1 ± 0.03) (Fig. 6). Soil moisture content (%) had a significant positive effect on the number S. lineatus collected in 2018 (χ2 = 4.04, df = 1, P = 0.04, estimate = 0.1 ± 0.06). No effect was observed in 2019 (χ2 = 1.56, df = 1, P = 0.214).

Landscape-Scale Effects on Weevil Totals

The proportion of land cover made up of pea (χ2 = 9.76, df = 1, P = 0.002, estimate = 22.0 ± 7.04) in a 250-m buffer around sites positively affected the number of S. lineatus captured in pitfalls traps in 2018 (Fig. 7). No significant associations between S. lineatus capture and the proportion of pea landcover were detected at the 500-m buffer (χ2 = 0.183, df = 1, P = 0.67) or the 1-km buffer (χ2 = 0.284, df = 1, P = 0.594) in 2018.

Discussion

Pheromone-based monitoring for S. lineatus in 2018 and 2019 detected range expansion into the pulse-growing regions of Alberta, Canada. Weevils were attracted to pheromone-baited traps and the number of weevils captured was indicative of feeding damage at sampled field pea sites. Abiotic factors negatively influenced the number of weevils captured, although effects varied between years. At the landscape scale, both field pea landcover and landcover diversity, were associated with weevil counts. These results highlight that the number of S. lineatus captured is shaped by a combination of local crop characteristics, abiotic conditions, and broader landscape compositional dynamics.

Comparison of weevils captured in the different pulse regions indicate range expansion of S. lineatus in 2018 to 2019 in Alberta, Canada. In 2018, weevil capture in the most northwest region, the Peace region, was distinctly lower than the other regions sampled. In 2019, the number of weevils captured was similar across all regions, suggesting active range expansion and establishment in the Peace region during the study. It is possible that the intensified sampling in the Peace region in 2019 also contributed to detection of more weevils that year. Since the initial detection of S. lineatus in the Peace region in 2017 (MacDonald et al. 2024), the number of weevils recovered in pheromone-baited traps increased in our study. The number of weevils captured in traps positioned at sites in the East region of the province also increased in relative abundance from 2018 to 2019, but more sampling sites in 2019 probably contributed to better detection. Adult weevils fed more on field pea plants surrounding pheromone-baited traps than on those surrounding un-baited traps, suggesting that adult weevils that respond to pheromone signals feed locally after orientation. In our study, because feeding damage was assessed at a single location per site, weevil capture and feeding notches are not representative of site-level densities. The observed relationship between trap catch and feeding damage provide evidence that pheromone trap captures reflect localized herbivory at a fine spatial scale. These findings support the use of pheromone-baited traps for monitoring as the number of S. lineatus adults, as spring adult capture were associated with both feeding damage and the number of next-generation S. lineatus adults entering winter.

Our findings provide strong evidence for the continued range expansion of S. lineatus in Alberta’s major pulse producing regions and reinforce the effectiveness of pheromone-baited pitfall traps as a detection tool, even at low population densities. These results align with previous studies (Quinn et al. 1999, Evenden et al. 2016, St. Onge et al. 2018, MacDonald et al. 2024), that used pheromone traps to monitor S. lineatus populations and delineate range expansion. Population growth throughout eastern Alberta may contribute to eastward expansion into other Prairie Provinces. Eastward spread into Saskatchewan was reported in 2007 (Saskatchewan Pulse Growers 2025) and S. lineatus was detected in Manitoba in 2019 (Gavloski 2019). Range expansion in the Prairie ecozone of western Canada is expected to continue to all areas of pulse production (Olfert et al. 2012). Climate change will further increase habitat availability for this species, and further range expansion is predicted (Olfert et al. 2012). Warmer winters may aid overwintering survival and post-overwintering fitness, further facilitating S. lineatus invasion and establishment (Wijerathna et al. 2024).

More S. lineatus trap capture in field pea compared to faba bean fields in 2018 in our study aligns with earlier observations of host plant use in Alberta, Canada (MacDonald et al. 2024). Although adult S. lineatus select faba bean over field pea when given a direct choice (Wijerathna 2021), less faba bean on the landscape in Alberta likely supports field pea host selection in our study. In 2019, however, S. lineatus capture in pheromone-baited traps was statistically similar between crops, which may be driven by more intensive sampling in faba bean in 2019 or the prolonged growing season for faba bean that provides more habitat to weevils. Increased cultivation of faba bean on the Canadian Prairies may promote S. lineatus population growth and support infestation in both reproductive hosts. Now that S. lineatus is established in Manitoba, it will be interesting to see if S. lineatus can use soybean, Glycine max (L.) Merrill as a reproductive host. Wijerathna (2021) showed that low numbers of S. lineatus develop on soybean inoculated with Bradyrhizobium japonicum, suggesting that selection could act to induce a host switch in areas of intensive soybean production.

Our study is the first to illustrate that pheromone-baited trap capture of S. lineatus can predict subsequent adult notching damage on field pea plants. More notching damage occurred on plants that surrounded pheromone-baited traps than control traps suggesting that S. lineatus attracted to pheromone concentrate feeding in aggregations. Counting notches on host plants has long been used to estimate S. lineatus populations (Cantot 1989, Nielsen 1990), but it is time consuming and influenced by environmental conditions (Morley-Senkler et al. 2020). In both years of our study, the number of S. lineatus captured reflected feeding damage, suggesting that pheromone-baited traps can be used for weevil detection (Evenden et al. 2016, St. Onge et al. 2018, MacDonald et al. 2024) and population monitoring. Predictably, the number of feeding notches counted on host plants held under laboratory conditions (Morley-Senkler et al. 2020) is related to the density of S. lineatus adults on field pea and other legumes (Nielsen 1990, Murray and Clements 1992, Landon et al. 1995, Vankosky et al. 2011). Variation in our model to predict notching damage from trap capture may be attributed to the effect of temperature on S. lineatus herbivory on field pea plants (Morley-Senkler et al. 2020) and trap capture, that was discovered in our study.

Our results indicate that average temperature, relative humidity, and accumulated precipitation, affect trap capture of S. lineatus in pea fields, highlighting the role of weather conditions in shaping insect population dynamics (Andrewartha and Birch 1954). Temperature influences S. lineatus flight activity (Hans 1959, Hamon et al. 1987), and cool spring conditions may delay S. lineatus dispersal to fields of reproductive hosts. Phenological plant development prior to colonization by S. lineatus reduces successful larval establishment (Olfert et al. 2012). Previous climatic modelling (Olfert et al. 2012) suggests that S. lineatus is more sensitive to changes in precipitation than temperature, with higher populations expected in wetter seasons and lower populations in dry seasons. Our findings align with these predictions, as temperature, relative humidity, and accumulated precipitation affected S. lineatus trap capture. Soil moisture influences S. lineatus larval success, as larvae prefer moist conditions with high temperatures and dry soil contributes to larval and egg mortality (Fisher 1977, Olfert et al. 2012). Our results suggest that soil moisture content of cropped fields moderately affects relative abundance of S. lineatus adults captured in pheromone-baited traps.

Landscape analyses illustrated that weevil abundance was influenced by landscape heterogeneity and composition at multiple scales. The proportion of the reproductive host (field pea) in the area surrounding the trap location had both positive and negative effects on weevil abundance that depended on scale. This suggests that weevils respond to landcover at multiple spatial scales, making these relationships complex and often inconsistent (Karp et al. 2018, Pellissier et al. 2022). Positive associations between pea landcover and weevil trap catch at smaller spatial scales likely reflect localized attraction to field pea as a reproductive host. Weaker or negative relationships at larger buffer sizes may indicate redistribution of weevils across available host patches and attraction to reproductive hosts in the vicinity away from trap locations at larger scales. Interestingly, landscape predictors of S. lineatus trap capture were more variable and less reliable when a large proportion of the landscape was comprised of field pea (>75%). Variation in this model may be attributed to the use of site-level totals derived from a single transect per site. Limited spatial replication reduces the ability to capture within-site variation. Sitona lineatus moves across the landscape by flight and walking, resulting in movement that is not strictly limited to local field-scale landcover. As a result, associations between weevil abundance and surrounding landcover are variable. Increased spatial replication within sites and fine-scale movement data would improve the ability to detect landcover effects on S. lineatus abundance and should be considered for future research.

Scale-dependent effects of host plant landcover and specialist herbivore abundance is documented in other cropping systems, such as maize (Clemente-Orta et al. 2020), and alfalfa in Europe (Madeira et al. 2022, Pellissier et al. 2022). Positive associations between host plant area and pest abundance are consistent with Root’s resource concentration hypothesis, which predicts that large areas of host plants attract and retain specialist herbivores (Root 1973). Similar results have been reported for the alfalfa specialist herbivore (Hypera postica) in alfalfa cropping landscapes (Pellissier et al. 2022), and corn rootworm in maize dominated landscapes (O’Rourke et al. 2011, Kheirodin et al. 2020). In contrast, negative associations between pea landcover and S. lineatus abundance at larger spatial scales align with the resource dilution hypothesis. This predicts that herbivores distribute across suitable cropping areas, reducing local pest densities (Schneider et al. 2015). Similar patterns of pest population dilution across agricultural landscapes occur in other agroecosystems, including asparagus beetle (Crioceris asparagi) in asparagus, pollen beetles, Brassicogethes spp., in oilseed rape, Andean potato weevils Premnotrypes spp., and Lygus bugs, Lygus hesperus, in cotton (Carriere et al. 2006, Parsa et al. 2011, Zavalnitskaya et al. 2022). For S. lineatus, these scale-dependent patterns may also reflect differences in habitat longevity between annual pea crops and perennial legume systems (Zavalnitskaya et al. 2022). Sitona lineatus reproduction is restricted to field pea and faba bean, adults are oligophagous feeders on legumes and rely on non-crop habitats for overwintering, including woody and herbaceous landscape components (Östman et al. 2001, Thies et al. 2005, 2008, Clemente-Orta et al. 2020, Wijerathna et al. 2024). Woody and herbaceous habitats provide moderate microclimates that support winter dormancy conditions of insect herbivores (Forman and Baudry 1984). The availability of alternative feeding and overwintering habitats may weaken direct associations between reproductive host abundance and pest trap catch, particularly at broader spatial scales.

Landscape analyses derived from community ecology provide a useful framework for assessing how landscape heterogeneity influences pest populations (Nagendra 2002). Positive associations between S. lineatus trap capture, landscape diversity, and evenness at broader spatial scales suggest that heterogeneous landscapes may support higher weevil abundance. Diversity indices at the 500-m and 1-km buffers indicate weevil responses are not driven by a single landcover type but by the overall composition and balance of habitat types across the landscape. Similar scale-dependent responses to landscape heterogeneity and non-linear relationships have been reported is supported by findings in other systems where pest response to landscape features depends on insect dispersal ability, feeding and overwintering strategies, and associated natural enemies (Segoli and Rosenheim 2012). Landscape evenness was consistently associated with increased weevil capture, supporting the idea that landscapes lacking a dominant landcover type may provide more continuous access to resources, microclimates, and movement corridors for overwintering and dispersing weevils (Fahrig et al. 2011). Sitona lineatus occupies different habitats throughout its life cycle and heterogeneous landscapes may facilitate seasonal transitions between overwintering sites, feeding habitats, and reproductive host crops. Adults commonly overwinter in field margins or perennial forage systems before orienting to annual legume crops in spring, and spatial heterogeneity at larger scales may influence both the timing and efficiency of this movement (Schotzko and O’Keeffe 1986, Cárcamo et al. 2018). Taken together, these results highlight the importance of landscape composition and heterogeneity in shaping S. lineatus abundance and reinforce the need for a spatially explicit perspective when assessing herbivory risk. Landscape heterogeneity can simultaneously support pest populations while altering dispersal pathways and resource availability, complicating predictions of pest pressure at individual fields (Bianchi et al. 2006, Rusch et al. 2010, Gray et al. 2024).

This study illustrates the utility of pheromone-baited traps to monitor range expansion and population dynamics of S. lineatus in Alberta’s pulse agroecosystems. Understanding factors that influence the movement and establishment of S. lineatus in rapidly changing agricultural landscapes is important for the development of IPM strategies and novel management tactics. Incorporation of established principles of evolutionary theory can help us combat invasive pests and ensure resilience of our food systems (Mazzi and Dorn 2012). A multi-faceted approach is necessary to improve pest suppression and contribute to the development of multifunctional agricultural systems.
