A deep learning runoff prediction model based on wavelet decomposition and dynamic feature fusion
Authors: Dong-mei Xu, Qi-qi Zeng, Wen-chuan Wang, Xu-tong Zhang, Yi-yang Wang, Hong-fei Zang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To address the stochasticity, time-varying characteristics, and nonlinear dynamics of runoff series, this research proposes a novel deep learning architecture, BWDformer, based on wavelet decomposition and dynamic feature fusion, to enhance the precision of streamflow forecasting. Based on Informer, the model innovatively integrates wavelet decomposition, a dynamic feature fusion module (DFF), and Bayesian optimization to address the limitations of conventional deep learning models in multi-scale feature integration, long-term dependency capture, and dynamic feature adjustment. Specifically, wavelet decomposition is first applied to extract multi-scale features through adaptive time windows, accurately capturing short-term fluctuations, seasonal variations, and long-term trends in runoff data. Then, based on DFF, the feature weights are dynamically adjusted using the attention mechanism to optimize the feature combination, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to analyze complex runoff sequences. Finally, Bayesian optimization is used to efficiently search for hyperparameters, significantly improving the model’s training efficiency. To verify the model’s effectiveness, this study tested it at four hydrological stations: Hongshanhe, Manwan, Baihe, and Tangnaihai. The results show that BWDformer significantly outperforms benchmark models, such as CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Informer, in terms of MAE, RMSE, R, NSE, and KGE. For example, in Hongshanhe, the MAE is 0.1921, a decrease of 18.82% compared to CNN (0.2366), 4.65% compared to LSTM (0.2014), 15.63% compared to Transformer (0.2277), and 7.87% compared to Informer (0.2086). RMSE decreased by 25.46% compared to CNN, KGE was 0.9651, increased by 18.51% compared to Transformer (0.8143), and increased by 13.43% compared to Informer (0.8506). In Baihe, the MAE is 228.6971 m ³/s, a decrease of about 2.35% compared to CNN (243.0662), and the R value reaches 0.9998, an increase of 4.26% compared to CNN (0.9591). NSE is 0.9972, an increase of 18.73% compared to Transformer (0.8398), KGE is 0.9934, an increase of 9.79% compared to Informer (0.9048). These results verify the excellent performance of BWDformer in improving prediction accuracy, robustness, and practicality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21007-4.

Introduction

Research background and challenges

Runoff is a vital element of the water cycle, governing how surface water moves into rivers, lakes, and eventually reaches the sea. This is crucial for water resource governance, flood control, and risk mitigation, as well as ecosystem conservation1,2. As global climate change worsens and human impacts escalate, the frequency of extreme climate events has increased significantly, and the topography of the watershed’s underlying surface has changed dramatically. The combined effect of these factors has made the formation mechanism of runoff more complex and increased the difficulty of predicting the runoff process1,3. Against this backdrop, the occurrence of flood disasters has become more frequent and severe. In the struggle of human beings against flood disasters, it has been proven in practice that integrating structural solutions with non-structural approaches proves to be a highly effective strategy4,5. Among the non-engineering measures, enhancing the precision of streamflow prediction has become a key link6,7.

To enhance the accuracy and reliability of runoff prediction, scholars have developed diverse modeling techniques from multiple perspectives, which can be categorized into two paradigms: classical methods and cutting-edge methods. Classical methods include cause-and-effect analysis and hydrological statistics, primarily relying on historical observational data and physical mechanisms. However, they still exhibit limitations in addressing complex nonlinear relationships and uncertainty factors8,9. The latter mainly includes artificial neural networks, grey system analysis, fuzzy mathematical models, and other methods. In recent years, significant progress has been made in the field of runoff prediction, especially with the impetus of artificial intelligence and deep learning methods. Deep learning models (such as LSTM, attention mechanisms, Bayesian extreme learning machines, etc.) have become mainstream technologies for improving the precision of runoff forecasting10–13. In the field of runoff prediction, various deep learning models-including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and their variants (e.g., LSTM, GRU), Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN), Residual Networks (ResNet), and Graph Neural Networks (GNN)-have become mainstream technical approaches, effectively enhancing the models’ ability to capture nonlinear relationships and complex temporal dependencies10,14,15.

In recent years, the combination of signal decomposition (such as wavelet decomposition, VMD, EMD, etc.) and deep learning models has become the mainstream method in the field of hydrology to improve prediction accuracy16–19. Wavelet decomposition, as an important tool for feature extraction and denoising, is widely used for predicting and modeling various hydrological variables such as flow, runoff, and water quality20–24. The wavelet transform has the characteristic of multi-resolution analysis, which can decompose runoff into components of different scales25–27. Researchers utilized this characteristic to comprehensively analyze short-term and long-term hydrological processes: predicting short-term flood events through daily runoff data and exploring long-term hydrological trends through monthly runoff data, thus achieving a comprehensive evaluation of multi-scale hydrological processes28,29. Through the wavelet transform, it is possible to better capture the instantaneous changes and periodic characteristics in runoff30,31. The decomposed runoff can more clearly reveal the patterns of hydrological change at different scales. For runoff forecasting, by analyzing the high-frequency components, it is possible to capture better the rise, peak appearance, and recession of runoff32–34. For example, in mountainous rivers, where flood rises rapidly, the high-frequency components obtained through decomposition can more accurately predict the flood arrival time and peak flow, offering a more precise foundation for flood management decision-making35–37. The decomposed components have a relatively simple structure and patterns, enabling hydrological forecasting models to fit better and predict the changes of each element38–40. Compared with directly modeling the original complex runoff, the modeling process after decomposition can reduce the complexity of the model and improve the stability and generalization ability of the model41–43. The DWT-DCT model proposed by Moosavi, et al.21, a single-output wavelet transform deep learning model, outperforms multi-output models in daily traffic prediction, with an accuracy improvement of up to 18%. The model proposed by Sorkhabi, et al.46 utilizes wavelet decomposition and deep learning to improve the accuracy of waveform backtracking in satellite measurements, resulting in a 30% increase in water level measurements of Lake Urmia. Wavelet decomposition (WD) is often combined with deep learning models such as LSTM, CNN, QRNN, etc., to decompose hydrological time series, extract multi-scale features, and then make predictions, significantly improving the model’s ability to handle non-stationary and complex signals47,48.

The parameter optimization methods in hydrological time series forecasting promote the improvement of model accuracy and adaptability49. While conventional physics-based distributed hydrological models are theoretically capable of extracting parameters directly from topographic characteristics, the high parameter uncertainty in practical applications makes parameter optimization a key means to enhance the ability of runoff forecasting and simulation50–53. In recent years, advanced metaheuristic algorithms like Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Artificial Bee Colony (ABC), and Dynamically Dimensioned Search (DDS) have been widely used for parameter calibration, which can effectively improve model performance and reduce uncertainty52,53. The integration of advanced deep learning techniques such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and optimization algorithms has further accelerated the parameter optimization process and enhanced the transferability of parameters, making it suitable for data-scarce areas51. In addition, emerging technologies such as Bayesian methods and gradient Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) support the dynamic and time-varying modeling of parameters, better reflecting the hydrological processes under environmental changes54,55. The introduction of multi-objective optimization and multi-source data (such as remote sensing evapotranspiration data) makes parameter optimization more comprehensive, significantly improving the simulation accuracy and the rationality of the physical meaning of parameters50,53. For example, Du, et al.56 proposed a dynamic ensemble based on Bayesian optimization for time series forecasting, and the results were significantly better than classical and the latest methods. Overall, parameter optimization techniques are developing in the direction of intelligence, multi-source integration, and dynamic adaptability, extensively promoting the scientificity and practicality of hydrological time series forecasting51,52,57.

In summary, the present status and key difficulties in runoff forecasting model advancement can be outlined as follows:

Single-scale feature extraction and data nonstationarity: Runoff contains a wealth of multi-scale features, including seasonality, long-term trends, and short-term fluctuations. Single-scale feature extraction methods are unable to fully capture their complexity. Traditional methods often only focus on features at a specific scale. For example, they can only effectively identify seasonal patterns or long-term trends, but fail to take into account short-term fluctuations and other complex changes at the same time58–60. This limitation means that when dealing with hydrological data with multi-scale features, the model fails to fully leverage the abundant information within the data, which in turn compromises the precision and trustworthiness of the predictions61. In addition, runoff is usually non-stationary, with its statistical characteristics changing over time. This nonstationarity further increases the difficulty of feature extraction and modeling62–64, as the model needs to be able to adapt to the dynamic changes in the statistical characteristics of the data in order to precisely depict the intricate hydrological dynamics.The limitation of fixed feature fusion: Traditional feature fusion methods are usually fixed, mainly relying on simple concatenation, weighting, or addition operations, and cannot dynamically adjust the importance weights of features according to different hydrological scenarios65,66. This fixed fusion method has limitations, making it difficult to flexibly adapt to the dynamic changes in feature importance at different time scales, thus limiting the prediction performance of the model. There are complex correlations and complementarities among hydrological features67,68. On the one hand, different features may jointly affect the runoff formation process, such as the mutual influence between precipitation, evaporation, and soil moisture; on the other hand, some features may have complementary effects in specific hydrological events. For example, in flood events, the short-term heavy precipitation feature and the long-term soil saturation feature together determine the intensity and duration of the flood. Traditional feature fusion methods often ignore these complex correlations and complementarities, rendering it challenging to fully exploit the interdependencies among features. Consequently, the model’s comprehension of hydrological mechanisms remains superficial, which compromises the precision and dependability of its forecasts.Difficulty in hyperparameter selection: Deep learning model performance is highly dependent on hyperparameter configuration, yet conventional hyperparameter optimization methods (such as grid search or random search) often suffer from prohibitively high computational costs69. Although grid search is comprehensive, it has a high computational cost in high-dimensional parameter space and is prone to the curse of dimensionality70,71; random search can alleviate this problem to some extent, however, it remains inadequate in thoroughly exploring the parameter landscape and is susceptible to converging at suboptimal solutions, especially in high-dimensional complex spaces70,72. In runoff prediction, the amount of data is often limited and noisy, which makes the model prone to overfitting69. After overfitting on the training data, the model can accurately capture the characteristics of the training samples, but its poor generalization ability leads to subpar performance on new data.

Research contributions

In response to the issues of single-scale feature extraction, rigid feature fusion, and non-stationarity of data in time series analysis within the field of hydrological runoff prediction, this paper proposes a novel deep learning architecture-BWDformer. Based on Informer, this architecture effectively deals with complex time series data and enhances the model’s generalization ability and prediction accuracy by integrating wavelet decomposition, a dynamic feature fusion module (DFF), and Bayesian optimization. The primary advancements and key merits of the BWDformer framework are summarized below:

By employing wavelet decomposition techniques, we can accurately capture multi-scale feature information within time series data, providing comprehensive feature information for the precise characterization of complex hydrological processes, thereby achieving a comprehensive account of water cycle mechanisms. Furthermore, DFF can explore the correlations between features and construct more representative and discriminative feature representations by analyzing the interactions between features. This precise control over model complexity not only enhances the model’s generalization performance across diverse datasets and real-world scenarios but also ensures its robustness and reliability in practical applications.The use of Bayesian optimization can efficiently search for hyperparameters and quickly obtain the globally optimal hyperparameters, effectively improving the model’s training efficiency. This precise control of model complexity further enhances the model’s generalization ability across different datasets and practical application scenarios, ensuring the model’s stability and reliability in real-world applications.

Methodology

Wavelet decomposition

Wavelet analysis is a robust method for signal processing widely applied in time series analysis. It can break down complex signals into sub-signals of different frequencies and time scales, thereby deeply mining the multi-scale features and intrinsic information within the sequence.

Wavelet Function and Scaling Function.

The core of wavelet decomposition lies in the wavelet function (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Psi (t)$$\end{document}) and the scaling function (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi (t)$$\end{document}). The wavelet function generates a series of wavelet basis functions with different scales and positions through dilation and translation operations. These basis functions are used to capture local abrupt changes and high-frequency features in the signal. The mathematical formula is presented below:

1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Psi _{a,b}}(t)=\frac{1}{{\sqrt a }}\Psi \left( {\frac{{t - b}}{a}} \right)$$\end{document}

Here, parameter a determines the scaling transformation of the wavelet function, while parameter b controls its translation position along the time axis.

The scaling function is used to obtain the overall trend and the low-frequency part of the signal. Together with the wavelet function, it forms a multi-resolution analysis framework, enabling wavelet decomposition to characterize the signal at different resolution scales.

The purpose of using wavelet analysis in this study is to provide dual information of “time domain” and “frequency domain” for each sliding window. The specific steps are as follows:

This approach preserves both the trend (cA3) and multi-scale high-frequency details (cDi), which can enhance the model’s ability to synchronously capture flood extremes and long-term trends. It should be pointed out that the downsampling of DWT will cause the sequence length to be halved. Therefore, this paper adopts “single branch reconstruction” instead of direct coefficient concatenation to eliminate the length misalignment problem. If the future is more sensitive to boundary effects, MODWT (Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform) can be used instead, which does not require downsampling and naturally preserves the original length73,74.

In practical applications, the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) is commonly used to decompose time series75–77. By discretizing the scale and position parameters, DWT achieves a multi-scale discrete decomposition of the signal, resulting in a set of discrete wavelet coefficients. These coefficients correspond to different frequency bands and time positions, reflecting the feature changes of the signal at different times and scales78. This study uses DWT to preprocess the runoff time series, decomposing it into low-frequency trend components and high-frequency noise components, which are respectively input into the BWDformer model to capture long-term and short-term fluctuation characteristics. The decomposition process is shown in Fig. 1.

The advantage of wavelet decomposition lies in its ability to analyze signals in both the time and frequency domains simultaneously, overcoming the limitation of the traditional Fourier Transform, which can only analyze signals in the frequency domain. It is particularly well-suited for analyzing non-stationary time series, as it effectively detects abrupt variations and localized characteristics within the signal.

Bayesian optimization

Bayesian optimization, as a class of global optimization methods based on probabilistic models, demonstrates remarkable efficiency and applicability in scenarios such as hyperparameter optimization for machine learning. It constructs the posterior distribution of the objective function, combining prior knowledge and observed data to intelligently select the next sampling point. Thus, it can find the optimal solution of the objective function with fewer iterations. It is especially suitable for scenarios where the evaluation cost of the objective function is high or it is not convenient to directly differentiate.

Bayesian Framework.

Bayesian Framework.

Bayesian Framework.

The essence of Bayesian optimization centers on refining the posterior distribution of the objective function via Bayes’ theorem. The initial distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\theta )$$\end{document} and the likelihood function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(D\left| \theta \right.)$$\end{document}are key components. The posterior probability distribution can be formulated as:

3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\theta \left| D \right.)=\frac{{P(D\left| \theta \right.)P(\theta )}}{{P(D)}}$$\end{document}

where θ represents the hyperparameters, and D is the observed training data. Bayesian optimization incorporates prior beliefs and knowledge by introducing the prior distribution, which allows it to perform well even when data is scarce.

In each iteration, Bayesian optimization first constructs a surrogate model (such as a Gaussian process or random forest) based on the current posterior distribution. This proxy model can emulate the objective function’s behavior and quantify the uncertainty associated with it. The surrogate model selects the next most promising sampling point by maximizing the acquisition function, such as Expected Improvement (EI) or Probability of Improvement (PI). Thus, it effectively balances local search and global exploration.

Sampling strategy.

The sampling strategy determines how to select the next sampling point from the surrogate model, which is one of the cores of Bayesian optimization. Among them, the Expected Improvement (EI) strategy is a commonly used sampling strategy. Its mathematical expression is:

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$EI(\theta )=E\left[ {\hbox{max} (f({\theta ^ * }) - f(\theta ),0} \right]$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f({\theta ^ * })$$\end{document} is the best known objective function value at present, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(\theta )$$\end{document} is the objective function value at the hyperparameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}. The purpose of the EI strategy is to select a hyperparameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} that maximizes the expected improvement of the objective function value compared with the current best value.

Bayesian optimization iteratively updates the posterior distribution and the sampling strategy, gradually narrowing the search range, and finally converges to the global optimal solution. In contrast to conventional grid search and random search, Bayesian optimization demonstrates superior efficiency and convergence performance when tackling high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces and intricate objective functions. Therefore, it has been widely used in the fields of model tuning and automated machine learning (AutoML). It can make full use of the information of each evaluation to find a better combination of hyperparameters within limited computing resources. The detailed procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Informer

Informer is a neural network framework tailored for extended-range time-series prediction. It demonstrates outstanding accuracy in extended-range sequence prediction through the collaborative action of the self-attention mechanism, feed-forward network (FFN), and auto-regressive module. The core strength of Informer lies in its capacity to effectively manage long-term relationships in long sequences. Moreover, it solves the problems of high computational complexity and insufficient memory capacity faced by traditional models when dealing with long sequences through innovative sparse strategies for self-attention mechanisms and a streamlined encoder-decoder architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Self-attention mechanism.

Autoregressive Module.

The Autoregressive Module is a key component in Informer that is used to generate the prediction sequence. It iteratively leverages the forecast outcomes from the prior time interval to estimate the value at the present step. Formally, the output of the Autoregressive Module can be represented as:

The overall architecture of Informer includes three parts: the encoder, the decoder, and the autoregressive module. The encoder converts the input sequence into a rich, high-dimensional feature vector. The decoder then uses the feature representation generated by the encoder and the autoregressive module to recursively generate the prediction sequence. Through this design, Informer can effectively handle long-time series data, capture long-term dependencies within the sequence, and maintain high computational efficiency and accuracy during the prediction process.

Model construction

Data preprocessing

Time series runoff data cannot be directly used for prediction. We need to preprocess the time series to convert it into a unified format. The detailed preprocessing procedures are outlined below:

Data reading and standardization.

Data reading and standardization.

Data reading and standardization.

We use the read_csv function of pandas to read the runoff data file in CSV format and rename the runoff column to “target” for subsequent processing. To enhance the robustness and accelerate the convergence rate of model training, we standardize the runoff data using StandardScaler, making its mean 0 and variance 1. This operation linearly maps the data to a space with zero mean and unit variance while preserving the skewness and kurtosis characteristics of the original data distribution, ensuring that the data range is stable within the interval of [-2.5, 2.5]. This standardization process, which eliminates the influence of dimensions, fully retains the non-stationary characteristics and nonlinear evolution laws of the hydrological sequence.

(2)Sliding window and wavelet decomposition.

(2)Sliding window and wavelet decomposition.

Sliding window and wavelet decomposition.

We construct input-output sample pairs according to the specified historical window length (base_config[“hist_len”] = 12) and prediction sequence length (base_config[“pred_len”] = 1). For each window position i, we extract a sequence of length 12 as the input, and the values at positions i + 12 to i + 13 are used as the output. Using the pywt.wavedec function to perform wavelet decomposition on each input window sequence, the basis type used for decomposition is “db4” (Daubechies wavelet, 4th order), which is a recommended choice in hydrological time series analysis based on the research of Sang (2013), Nourani et al. (2009), and Sang et al. (2010). This function reconstructs wavelet coefficients through upsampling to match their length with the original time series, facilitating subsequent model training and result validation. The decomposition level is 3, aiming to balance the resolution and computational efficiency of multi-scale analysis17,79–82. We then use pywt. upcoef to reconstruct the decomposition coefficients at different scales, making their lengths consistent with the original sequence. After that, we merge these reconstructed coefficients into a multi-scale feature matrix, thereby extracting the feature information of the signal at different time scales and providing richer input features for model training.

(3)Dataset division.

(3)Dataset division.

Dataset division.

The dataset is randomly split into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 8:1:1. During the division process, we do not shuffle the data randomly to maintain the order of the time series, ensuring that the model can capture the temporal dynamics of the time series during training. This division method helps the model to effectively evaluate its performance on the validation and test sets, while avoiding the confusion of time order caused by random shuffling, thus more realistically reflecting the model’s predictive ability in practical applications.

Dynamic feature fusion module (DFF module)

The Adaptive Feature Integration Module (AFI Module) is utilized to analyze the input data, thereby strengthening the model’s capacity to identify critical patterns and boosting prediction precision. The DFF Module integrates multi-scale features and historical features through attention mechanisms and convolutional operations. It involves the concatenation of input features, global average pooling to obtain global feature information, convolutional operations to generate attention weights, and the use of the Sigmoid function to normalize the weights. This process highlights important feature information while suppressing irrelevant features, achieving dynamic selection and fusion of features. Under this configuration, the dimension of the input data is (number of samples, number of features, number of channels), where the number of features is determined by the scale of the original data’s features, and the quantity of channels corresponds to the output channel count of the preceding neural network layer. After processing by the DFF Module, the dimension of the output data remains unchanged, but the feature representation is effectively enhanced.

The specific implementation of the DFF Module includes several key parts: First, global average pooling is applied to the input features to compress them and obtain a global feature representation. Then, a convolutional layer (conv_atten) performs convolutional operations on the global features, with the input channel number being dim2 and the output channel number also being dim2, and the kernel size is 1. After convolution processing, the Sigmoid function is applied to compress the output values into the range of (0,1), generating attention weights. Subsequently, these weights are multiplied element-wise with the original features to obtain the enhanced feature representation through the attention mechanism. Subsequently, another convolutional layer (conv_redu) is used to reduce the dimension of the fused features in the channel dimension, with the input channel number being dim*2, the output channel number being dim, and the kernel size being 1, resulting in the final fused feature representation. Finally, two convolutional layers (conv1 and conv2) perform convolutional operations on two input features, respectively, with the input channel number being dim, the output channel number being 1, and the kernel size being 1, in preparation for subsequent nonlinear fusion. The dynamic fusion effect of features is further enhanced through nonlinear transformation (Nonlin, i.e., the Sigmoid activation function). The architecture of the DFF Module is illustrated in Fig. 4.

The parameter settings of the DFF Module have been adjusted through experiments and experience to achieve the best feature fusion effect. The input channel number dim is determined by the output channel number of the previous neural network layer and is related to the feature dimension of the entire model, which can be adapted according to the scale of the input data’s features and other parts of the model. The convolutional layer with a 1 × 1 kernel is widely adopted, enabling cross-channel feature blending while preserving the spatial dimensions of the feature map. By carefully tuning these parameters, the DFF Module enables adaptive feature integration and enhances model performance. During training, the model autonomously optimizes these parameters via backpropagation to reduce the discrepancy between predictions and ground truth.

Model architecture

The novel hydrological sequence prediction model proposed in this study achieves efficient modeling of the complex nonlinear relationships and long- and short-term dependencies in hydrological sequences by deeply integrating multi-scale feature mapping and probabilistic sparse attention mechanisms. During the data preprocessing stage, wavelet decomposition technology is employed to decompose the original sequence into multi-scale features, constructing a temporal feature dataset that includes approximation coefficients and multiple layers of detail coefficients. The model architecture mainly consists of an input embedding layer, a positional encoding layer, an encoder layer, a probabilistic sparse attention layer, a decoder layer (containing a Dynamic Feature Fusion module, DFF), and a fully connected layer. The input embedding layer determines the input feature dimension based on the number of wavelet decomposition layers plus one and maps it to a d_model-dimensional space; The positional encoding layer incorporates spatial information into the sequence elements by leveraging a blend of sinusoidal and cosine-based functions; the encoder layer extracts and transforms features through multiple sublayers (including probabilistic sparse attention, layer normalization, feedforward networks, and dropout); the probabilistic sparse attention layer captures the dependencies between elements via the query-key-value mechanism and reduces complexity through sparse computation; the decoder layer receives the output from the encoder and dynamically fuses features through the DFF module in combination with attention and convolution operations; and finally, the dense layer transforms the extracted features into the output space matching the prediction target’s dimensionality. Model parameters (d_model, n_heads, e_layers, d_ff, dropout, batch_size, num_epochs, learning_rate) are automatically adjusted within a specified range through Bayesian optimization to achieve the best configuration. The meanings of each hyperparameter are as follows: d_model is the dimension or embedding dimension of the model, representing the number of features of the input and output vectors. The number of heads in the multi-head attention mechanism affects the parallel processing capability of the model. The number of layers in the encoder determines the depth of the model. D_ff is the hidden layer dimension of the feedforward network, which affects its nonlinear transformation ability. Dropout is the dropout rate used for regularization to prevent overfitting. Batch_Size is the batch size of each training sample, which affects training efficiency and convergence. Num_ epochs is the number of training epochs, which determines the number of iterations for the model. Learning_rate is the learning rate that controls the step size of parameter updates. The specific construction process is as follows: First, the preprocessed multi-scale features are input into the input embedding layer for dimension mapping; then, the positional embedding module incorporates positional context into the sequence tokens; next, the encoder layer processes the sequence features through multi-layer feature extraction and transformation; the probabilistic sparse attention layer captures the dependencies between elements; the decoder layer further fuses and enhances features through the DFF module; and finally, the dense layer transforms the extracted features into the runoff prediction space to produce the final output. Case study

Data

Study area

This study selects the Hongshanhe Hydrological Station, Manwan Hydrological Station, Baihe Hydrological Station, and Tangnaihai Hydrological Station as the research objects. By comparing and analyzing the hydrological characteristics of each station, the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed model across diverse geographic and climatic settings are confirmed.

Hongshanhe Station is located at the northern foot of the Qilian Mountains in the western part of the Heihe River Basin (with a drainage area of about 8000 km² and a total length of 156 km of the main stream). It belongs to the arid area of the Hexi Corridor, with an average annual precipitation of only 120 mm and an evaporation rate of up to 2200 mm. The runoff supply is mainly from melted ice and snow. The selection of this station is based on the integrity of its long-term observation data and representativeness of the upstream ice and snow melting process, making it suitable for studying multi-scale hydrological characteristics. The Manwan Hydropower Station is located at the junction of Jingdong County and Zhenyuan County in Yunnan Province, upstream of the Lancang River (about 300 km from Kunming). It is the first hydropower station in the cascade hydropower development of the lower reaches of the Lancang River, with a large annual runoff in the river section. Its selection is based on considerations of data quality and relevance to hydropower scheduling, reflecting upstream runoff characteristics. Compared to export sites, the complex terrain and natural process data of these sites are more suitable for model validation, and export sites may be more affected by downstream regulation and human activities. The Baihe River originates from the west of Arkansas, with a length of about 1102 km and a basin area of about 7.25 million square kilometers. The average annual flow is 909 cubic meters per second. The Yellow River Tangnaihai Hydrological Station is located in Tangnaihai Township, Gonghe County, Qinghai Province, controlling a basin area of 13.3 ten thousand square kilometers. The multi-year average runoff of the upper reaches of the Yellow River, where it is located, is about 32.7 billion cubic meters, but it is greatly affected by seasonal and interannual changes.

Dataset

In developing the multi-scale hydrological prediction dataset, this research compiled monthly runoff records from Hongshanhe Station (1960.1–2004.12) and Manwan Station (1953.1–2004.12), as well as daily runoff observation data from Baihe Station (1997.1.1–2013.12.4) and Huanghe Tangnaihai Station (1956.1.1–2018.12.31) to build a multi-scale hydrological prediction dataset. The dataset was divided by a temporal composite partitioning strategy into training set, validation set, and test set in the ratio of 8:1:1, preserving the integrity of the annual cycle and interannual trends.

The runoff data show significant seasonal and interannual variations, with an average of 5.0 m ³/s at Hongshanhe Station and a small variance. The average value of Manwan Station is 120 m ³/s, with significant fluctuations. The daily average runoff of Baihe Station and Tangnaihai Station is 900 m³/s and 320 m³/s, respectively, reflecting the diversity of upstream hydrological characteristics. Data time scale

This study explicitly used two time scales of data in cross-scale hydrological forecasting: monthly runoff and daily runoff, to achieve a comprehensive analysis of different hydrological processes. The monthly runoff data (such as Hongshanhe Station 1960.1-2004.12 and Manwan Station 1953.1-2004.12) are highly stable and relatively smooth with small fluctuations, which can better reflect the medium-term variation trend of river runoff, reduce the impact of short-term fluctuations and extreme values, and make the model more stable on the medium-term scale. It is suitable for analyzing the seasonal variation patterns of rivers and medium- and long-term water resources planning. Moreover, the data is relatively easy to obtain, usually derived from long-term observation and collation by hydrological stations, with a stable and systematic data source and a relatively rich accumulation of historical data, providing a more adequate data basis for model training. In addition, the model parameters are stable. Due to the relative stability and regularity of the data, the model parameters established using monthly runoff data are relatively stable over a certain period, which is convenient for model calibration and verification, enhancing the robustness and precision of the model.

Daily runoff data (such as Baihe Station from January 1, 1997 to December 4, 2013 and Tangnaihai Station on the Yellow River from June 1, 1956 to December 31, 2018) has a high temporal resolution and can more finely reflect the short-term variation characteristics of river runoff, capturing the fluctuations in runoff within a day, and providing a more precise description of short-term hydrological processes. It is suitable for short-term runoff prediction and hydrological studies that respond quickly to runoff changes, such as runoff forecasting and real-time water resources scheduling. Meanwhile, it helps to better capture extreme runoff events, such as flood peaks and the lowest flows during the dry season, which is of great significance for assessing flood risks of rivers, ensuring ecological flow, and the safe operation of water conservancy projects.

Comparative models

This study constructs all models using the PyTorch framework in Python 3.9, with experiments conducted on hardware configured with an i7-14700HX processor and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 graphics card.

Baseline models

In this study, Informer, Transformer, LSTM, and CNN models were selected as comparative models to verify the superior performance of the proposed BWDformer model through comparative experiments. To maintain the impartiality of the comparative analysis, this research investigated how hyperparameter configurations influenced the predictive performance of various models. Bayesian optimization was utilized, alongside referencing the optimal parameter guidelines established in prior studies for this dataset, to calibrate each model’s hyperparameters83, to ensure that the models could perform optimally at different sites. In this study, the objective function employed for streamflow forecasting was the Mean Squared Error (MSE) criterion, with the Adaptive Moment Estimation with Weight Decay (AdamW) optimizer chosen and supplemented by L2 regularization to improve the precision of monthly runoff series forecasting. In addition, for all models, data was read using Pandas, normalized using StandardScaler, and windowed datasets were constructed. To ensure the consistency of experimental conditions, corresponding parameters of other models, such as input sequence length and prediction sequence length, were synchronized. The summary table of model input features is shown in Table 2. The parameter settings of each predictive model are shown in Tables 3, 4 and 5, and 6.

The performance of the proposed BWDformer model is influenced by several key hyperparameters, including learning rate, Dropout rate, number of attention heads, encoding dimension, wavelet basis type, and decomposition level. Precise configuration of these hyperparameters is vital for model efficacy. In deep learning applications, hyperparameter optimization entails systematic experimentation with neural network architectures and parameter settings to achieve an ideal hyperparameter ensemble. In the code implementation, dynamic learning rate scheduling combined with an early stopping mechanism was used for training optimization. Probabilistic sparse attention methodology was utilized in the attention module, and wavelet decomposition, along with the dynamic feature fusion module (DFF), was used to extract multi-scale features. Among them, setting the learning rate to 0.0001/0.0002 may lead to slow convergence, but dynamic learning rate scheduling gradually adjusts the learning rate, and the early stopping mechanism terminates training when the performance of the validation set no longer improves, effectively balancing the convergence speed and overfitting risk; Although the dropout rate may discard some information through random deactivation (0.06–0.15), its moderate value combined with probability sparse attention mechanism reduces the retention of key features during overfitting contracts; To address the potential issue of insufficient dependency capture, wavelet decomposition and DFF enhance the model’s ability to learn time series dependencies through multi-scale feature extraction. The early stop mechanism stops training on time by monitoring the performance of the validation set, further avoiding overfitting. The existing performance indicators, such as significant reductions in MAE and RMSE, indicate that these strategies perform well in BWDformer without significant overfitting or insufficient dependency capture issues. The input sequence was decomposed by wavelets to strengthen the model’s capacity to extract multi-scale features, and the prediction error was measured using the MSE loss function. The hidden layer dimension was controlled by d_model. In this study, the value range of d_model was determined by the Bayesian optimization algorithm within the interval [128, 384], and parameters such as the feedforward layer number that were not explicitly defined were removed from the tuning range. The parameter sensitivity analysis will focus on verifying the synergistic effect of the time series window length and model structure, along with the influence of wavelet basis selection and decomposition depth on model efficacy.

Evaluation indicators

In this study, to comprehensively quantify the model’s performance in runoff prediction, process shape reproduction, and hydrological mechanism expression, the subsequent assessment metrics shall be employed: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), correlation coefficient (R), Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE), and Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE). MAE is a widely adopted metric that quantifies the mean absolute deviation between forecasted and observed data points. It provides a clear measure of predictive precision and exhibits robustness against extreme values. RMSE represents the root of the MSE, quantifying the typical magnitude of prediction discrepancies by averaging the squared differences between estimated and true values. Lower MAE and RMSE values indicate smaller deviations between model predictions and observed data, reflecting higher prediction accuracy. The R-value measures the linear correlation between predicted and observed values, ranging from − 1 to 1, with values closer to 1 signifying stronger correlation. NSE and KGE are key indicators for evaluating the performance of hydrological models. NSE measures the relative error variance between simulated and observed values, with a range of (- ∞, 1). When NSE = 1, it represents a perfect fit, while NSE ≤ 0 indicates that the model performance is not better than directly using the observed mean as a prediction. KGE is further decomposed into three components based on NSE: correlation, deviation, and variability ratio, which can comprehensively evaluate the performance of the model in terms of flow process line shape, water balance, and variability matching. Its value range is (- ∞, 1], and the closer it is to 1, the more ideal the simulation effect. Therefore, when NSE and KGE both approach 1, it can be considered that the model has achieved a high level of accuracy, deviation, and dynamic characteristics in simulating actual hydrological processes. Benchmark experimental results

To fully demonstrate that the BWDformer model can achieve effective and outstanding performance in the field of runoff prediction, this study compared it with several typical models, including CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Informer. The comparison test was conducted using the test set data of four hydrological stations: Hongshanhe, Manwan, Baihe, and Tangnaihai on the Yellow River. Ablation experiment results

This study constructs the BWDformer model based on the Informer framework through the following improvements: introducing wavelet decomposition at the data input stage for multi-scale feature extraction, designing a feature fusion module in the decoder layer to enhance information integration capabilities, and employing Bayesian optimization for automated hyperparameter tuning. To quantify the contribution of each improvement module, we conducted systematic ablation experiments and evaluated performance comprehensively using five metrics: MAE, RMSE, R, NSEC, and KGE, with detailed results presented in Table 9. Furthermore, to investigate the applicability differences of the model across various hydrological stations, we performed a comparative performance analysis for each station. Discussion

Validity

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the BWDformer model in runoff prediction, this study selected four mainstream models (CNN, LSTM, Transformer, and Informer) as baseline comparisons, and validated the superiority and reliability of BWDformer through comparative experiments. The specific comparison is conducted on the test sets of four hydrological stations (Hongshanhe, Manwan, Baihe, and Tangnaihai), and the evaluation index results of these stations are shown in Figs. 9, 10, 11 and 12, and 13. In the comparative experiments, the BWDformer model demonstrated outstanding performance in the monthly runoff prediction task, significantly outperforming other traditional deep learning models. In the bar chart-dot line chart with dual y-axes, the bar chart represents MAE, and the dot line chart represents RMSE, both of which are indicators that are better when smaller. For example, at the Hongshanhe station, the MAE of the BWDformer model is 0.1921, significantly lower than CNN’s 0.2466, LSTM’s 0.2014, Transformer’s 0.2277, and Informer’s 0.2086. The RMSE also performs well, with the BWDformer’s RMSE being around 0.26, lower than the other models’ RMSEs of above 0.30. In the radar chart, purple represents R, light blue represents NSE, and light green represents KGE, all of which are better when closer to 1. It can be seen from the chart that the BWDformer performs best in terms of R, NSE, and KGE indicators. Figures 10, 11 and 12, and 13 are the linear regression scatter plots of each station, with small dots representing predicted values. The closer the distribution of the points is to the diagonal line, the greater the model’s predictive precision. The confidence interval indicates the uncertainty of model parameter estimation, while the prediction interval indicates the uncertainty of individual predicted values. The narrower the confidence and prediction intervals, the more accurate the model’s predictions.

From the comprehensive analysis of the bar chart-dot line chart, and the radar chart, it can be seen that the BWDformer model has significantly lower values in both MAE and RMSE error indicators compared to other models, reflecting reduced prediction discrepancies and enhanced predictive precision. At the same time, the BWDformer model performs well in the three evaluation indicators of R, NSE, and KGE, indicating its advantage in calibration, predictive precision, and representation of hydrological characteristics relative to alternative models. Figures 10, 11 and 12, and 13 show the slopes, Pearson’s r coefficients, fitted line equations, intercepts, and R2 of each model at each station. Figure 13 presents the linear regression scatter plot for Tangnaihai station, illustrating the model’s predictive accuracy with a high R² value. It can be seen from the figures that the BWDformer model performs best in terms of prediction accuracy, achieving the highest alignment between forecasted and actual values, the highest R-square value, and the narrowest confidence and prediction intervals. As the model’s complexity increases, its prediction accuracy gradually improves. This indicates that the BWDformer model performs better in handling hydrological data.

The superiority of the BWDformer model is mainly attributed to its innovative wavelet decomposition and dynamic feature fusion module (DFF) design. The multi-scale feature extraction of wavelet decomposition combined with adaptive time window adjustment accurately captures the short-term fluctuations, seasonal variations, and long-term trends of non-stationary runoff data. For example, in monthly runoff prediction, wavelet decomposition decomposes the time series data into subsequences of different frequencies, with each subsequence corresponding to different scale features of the runoff data, providing comprehensive feature information that improves the framework’s capacity to simulate intricate hydrological processes. At the same time, the adaptive time window of wavelet decomposition can adjust its size according to the data characteristics, allowing for flexible handling of data nonstationarity and ensuring the stability and adaptability of the model across different time scales. The DFF module dynamically adjusts feature weights using the attention mechanism, mines feature correlations, and constructs better feature representations. In runoff, the importance of each feature changes with hydrological conditions, and the DFF module dynamically allocates weights to enhance the influence of key features and suppress the interference of irrelevant features. For example, during flood periods, the weights of features related to rapid runoff changes are increased, enabling the model to capture flood dynamics more accurately.

Additionally, the DFF module enhances feature discriminability through feature fusion, enabling the model to understand complex hydrological processes better and improve prediction accuracy and reliability. Moreover, the introduction of Bayesian optimization allows efficient search for hyperparameters, thereby further enhancing model performance. Hyperparameters are crucial to model performance, and traditional grid search or random search methods are inefficient and prone to local optima. It also mitigates the risk of overfitting and improves generalization performance, ensuring the stability and reliability of the model across different basins and time scales.

As emphasized in the conclusion, the ability of the BWDformer model to accurately capture multi-scale features in time series data is mainly due to the key component of its architecture, wavelet decomposition. At Baihe Station, wavelet decomposition decomposes the daily runoff sequence into approximate coefficients (low-frequency trends) and detailed coefficients (high-frequency fluctuations), enabling the model to distinguish seasonal patterns and long-term dependencies, as demonstrated by the high R-value (0.9998). This multi-resolution analysis effectively isolates the main hydrological cycle driven by seasonal precipitation, helping the model achieve robust trend alignment within a watershed of 72,500 square kilometers. The integration of these multi-scale features and attention mechanisms further enhances the extraction of key temporal dependencies, consistent with recent research on wavelet-based time series modeling. As shown in Fig. 14.

The high R value indicates robust trend alignment, likely due to the wavelet decomposition and attention mechanisms effectively extracting long-term dependencies in the time series. However, the elevated RMSE suggests that absolute errors are amplified by the model’s limited ability to precisely predict peak magnitudes85. This may be attributed to the smoothing effect of wavelet decomposition, which prioritizes low-frequency trends over high-frequency fluctuations, potentially missing rapid changes associated with extreme events. Additionally, data normalization across the large spatial scale of the Baihe basin may introduce scale mismatches, further contributing to absolute error accumulation87,91. A similar trend of peak underestimation at Hongshanhe Station is shown in Appendix A Figure A1, which is consistent with that of Baihe Station.

The observed underestimation of peaks is supported by the loss values (e.g., MSE ≈ 0.015 for key periods), which indicate that while the model generalizes well across the dataset (as evidenced by NSE and KGE), it struggles with the non-linear dynamics of extreme runoff. This finding aligns with previous studies on large-scale basins, where seasonal variability and extreme events challenge model precision despite good trend correlation86. The smoothing effect of wavelet decomposition that prioritizes low-frequency components also has limitations. The observed underestimation of peak runoff events (e.g., exceeding 1600 m ³/s) indicates that high-frequency details, such as rapid changes during extreme events, are weakened, leading to an increase in RMSE (324.81 m ³/s). This trade-off reflects the challenge of balancing trend accuracy and peak accuracy in large basins with high variability. This discovery has significant implications for hydrological applications: wavelet decomposition enhances the applicability of the model for medium - to long-term water resource management by ensuring stable trend predictions, but its limitations in predicting extreme events require additional techniques, such as enhanced high-frequency feature integration.

At Baihe Station, the BWDformer model underestimated peak runoff, although the trend correlation was high (R = 0.9998), supplementary analysis from other stations further confirmed this finding. The detailed forecast results of Hongshanhe Station, Manwan Station, and Tangnaihai Station can be found in the supplementary data (including the predicted results of Hongshanhe Station, Manwan Station, and Tangnaihai Station). These graphs compare the predicted and observed monthly and daily runoff, revealing consistent patterns: the model effectively captures seasonal trends at different watershed scales (e.g., Hongshanhe’s snowmelt-driven variability, Tangnaihai’s optimized performance), but exhibits peak underestimation due to the smoothing effect of wavelet decomposition. In Manwan, the DFF module enhanced KGE, while in Tangnaihai, Bayesian optimization significantly reduced MAE and RMSE. These supplementary results emphasize the advantages of the model in multi-scale feature integration and its limitations in extreme event prediction, supporting the need for future improvements. Future improvements may focus on optimizing the dynamic feature fusion module (DFF) to better integrate high-frequency components or adjusting the preprocessing pipeline to preserve peak information. Adaptive wavelet bases or hybrid methods can also be explored to alleviate these limitations, which may reduce RMSE while maintaining the trend capture ability of the model, addressing the current trade-off issues observed in the results of Baihe Station, and expanding the applicability of BWDformer in different hydrological environments.

Limitations

In this study, the proposed BWDformer model has demonstrated superior performance in the monthly runoff prediction task, especially in capturing multi-scale features and long-term dependencies. It has significant advantages compared with traditional models. However, the model still has certain limitations in practical applications. On the one hand, similar to most deep learning models, the BWDformer model has the “black box” characteristic. Its internal feature extraction and transformation processes are relatively complex and cannot be directly correlated with the physical significance of hydrological processes. This may affect the trust of decision-makers in the hydrological field in the model’s prediction results.

On the other hand, while the model demonstrates strong capability in handling intricate temporal data, its stability and applicability still need to be further verified when facing extreme climate conditions or data-scarce basins. Additionally, the model’s insufficient consideration of external factors, such as land use changes and human activities, may limit its comprehensiveness in practical hydrological predictions. In future research, it is possible to try to incorporate physical constraints (such as the water balance equation) into the BWDformer model to explore the optimization paths of the model structure. This approach aims to enhance the model’s transparency and robustness without compromising its efficiency, and to expand its applicability to different basins and complex conditions.

The DFF module, by deeply exploring the correlation between features of different scales and dynamically optimizing the combination weights of features, constructs a more representative and discriminative fused feature representation. This mechanism significantly enhances the model’s ability to understand the complex intrinsic laws of runoff. At the Manwan station, the KGE of the BWDformer is 0.9316, which is 10.7% higher than that of the Informer model without the DFF module; at the Baihe station, the BWDformer model achieved an R-value as high as 0.9998, representing a 4.4% performance improvement over the LSTM model without the DFF module. This notable enhancement confirms the critical role of the DFF module in strengthening the multi-scale feature fusion capability of deep learning models, effectively improving the model’s integration efficiency of multi-scale information and prediction accuracy.

Despite these advances, the model exhibits limitations. The wavelet decomposition’s smoothing effect leads to underestimation of peak runoff events, as observed at Baihe station, particularly during extreme flows, due to its focus on low-frequency trends. This may stem from scale mismatches in data normalization across large basins or insufficient capture of high-frequency dynamics. Additionally, the model’s performance may be constrained by the specific hydrological conditions of the studied basins, limiting its generalizability to diverse climates or ungauged regions. Future research could explore integrating physical constraints into BWDformer to improve interpretability and reliability, and extend its applicability across varied hydrological contexts.
