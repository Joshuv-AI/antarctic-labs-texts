Oncogenic EME1 promotes tumor progression and immune modulation in human cancers with therapeutic targeting potential
Authors: Muhammad Alaa Eldeen, Abdelrahman Mostafa, Farag Mamdouh, Waleed K Abdulsahib, Dalal Sulaiman Alshaya, Eman Fayad, Hassan M Otifi, Hesham M Hassan, Mohammed Alshehri, Aiysha Althobaiti, Ghadi Alsharif, Mohamed A Soltan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEME1, a critical DNA repair endonuclease, has emerged as a potential oncogene implicated in genome instability and cancer progression. However, its pan-cancer roles, prognostic significance, immune interactions, and therapeutic targeting remain underexplored.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis integrating multi-omics data from public databases, including TIMER2.0, GEPIA2, TISIDB, and cBioPortal, to evaluate EME1 expression, genetic alterations, and their association with clinical outcomes, immune infiltration, and molecular pathways. Virtual screening of 3180 FDA-approved drugs and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to identify and validate potential EME1 inhibitors.ResultsEME1 was significantly overexpressed in various human cancers and positively associated with advanced tumor grade and stage. High EME1 expression and mutations were linked to poor overall and disease-free survival. Immunogenomic profiling revealed strong positive correlations between EME1 and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), alongside a negative association with endothelial cell function, suggesting immunosuppressive roles. Machine learning models based on EME1-associated genes demonstrated high predictive accuracy for liver hepatocellular carcinoma (AUC > 0.90). Virtual screening identified eight promising drug candidates, including Everolimus and Dioscin, with strong binding affinities. MD simulations confirmed the stability of these interactions, particularly for Dioscin.ConclusionThis study reveals the multifaceted oncogenic roles of EME1 in tumor progression, immune evasion, and prognosis. It proposes EME1 as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target across multiple cancer types. Introduction

Cancer is the primary global cause of mortality, posing a significant public health obstacle that impacts physical health, financial resources, and overall quality of life [1]. Conventional cancer treatments include surgery, targeted, chemo-, radio-, and immune-therapy. Despite advancements, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors often show limited patient response rates and survival outcomes [2]. This requires the determination of new diagnostic biomarkers and the creation of fresh treatment approaches [3]. Although tumors vary greatly across various organs, many fundamental cancer initiation and progression mechanisms are shared [4]. Examining the gene expression patterns, clinical consequences, and treatment possibilities of important genes across various cancers is essential. Pan-cancer research, leveraging advances in public databases, has emerged as a powerful approach for identifying new therapeutic targets through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis [5].

Human EME1 (Essential Meiotic Structure-Specific Endonuclease 1) was initially identified as essential for meiosis, forming a complex with Mus81 (Methyl Methanesulfonate and Ultraviolet-Sensitive Gene 81) [6]. This complex cleaves replication forks (RFs), 3' DNA flaps, and Holliday junctions (HJs)[7]. Early studies in yeast manifested that EME1 is involved in homologous recombination (HR). The loss of Mus81, EME1, or Mms4 (Methyl Methanesulfonate Sensitivity 4) caused meiotic failure due to improper DNA processing following double-strand breaks (DSBs)[8–10]. Recent investigations have emerged that EME1 contributes to regulating cell volume, proliferation, and migration processes linked to cancer recurrence, particularly in bladder cancer (BLCA), glioma, and prostate cancer cells [11–13]. Higher EME1 expression has been connected with worse survival outcomes in pancreatic cancer [14]. In esophageal adenocarcinoma, MacGregor et al. found that elevated EME1 expression, analyzed from mRNA levels of 280 DNA repair genes in patients before treatment, correlated with reduced overall survival (OS), highlighting EME1 as a prognostic biomarker [15]. EME1 forms a heterodimer with MUS81, which plays a critical role in resolving DNA double-strand breaks and replication fork collapse, which is frequently dysregulated in cancer. Initial pan-cancer screening using TCGA and GEPIA2 revealed that EME1 is significantly upregulated in multiple cancer types, including LIHC, KIRC, LUAD, and UCEC, with strong prognostic relevance. Unlike other DNA repair endonucleases, EME1 showed consistent positive correlation with immunosuppressive markers, particularly MDSC (Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cell) infiltration and immune checkpoint genes. Previous studies have suggested that EME1 dysregulation may lead to genomic instability, promoting oncogenesis in a more context-dependent manner than other endonucleases [16, 17]. Although significant progress has been made in characterizing tumor-immune interactions across cancer types, a comprehensive understanding of how specific DNA repair genes modulate the tumor immune microenvironment remains limited. In particular, the immunomodulatory role of EME1—a key DNA endonuclease involved in resolving DNA double-strand breaks—has not been systematically explored in a pan-cancer context. This study addresses this critical gap by investigating the expression, prognostic value, and immune infiltration associations of EME1 across diverse tumor types using integrative immunogenomic approaches. Additionally, we screened for potential anti-cancer drugs targeting EME1, exploring new avenues for targeted therapy.

Materials and methods

EME1 differential expression analysis

The overexpression of oncogenes in malignant tissues distinguishes malignancy [16]. During the preliminary stage of our work, we used data from TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/, accessed on March 13, 2024) to conduct a comparative examination of EME1 gene expression in tumor and normal tissues [17]. Filtering thresholds of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were as follows: |log₂ Fold Change|≥ 1; Adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). To compare tumor models without access to normal tissue, we used the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2) database available at http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/ [18].

Correlation between EME1 and tumor grade and stage

Tumor grade and stage are crucial factors in evaluating tumor abnormalities, such as tumor growth and invasion, respectively. These attributes are essential for assessing patient survival results. To examine the possible connection between EME1 expression and tumor stage and grade, we employed online resources encompassing the GEPIA2 and TISIDB platforms [18, 19].

Survival prognosis analysis

To evaluate the predictive significance of EME1 expression in different forms of cancer, we performed a thorough investigation of survival prognosis. We used the GEPIA2 database[18] and the Kaplan–Meier (KM) plotter, which may be accessed at https://kmplot.com/analysis/. The filtering thresholds were as follows: Patients stratified based on median EME1 expression; Log-rank test p < 0.05 considered significant. The primary purpose of the "Survival Analysis" module in GEPIA2 is to create a heatmap that visually represents the relationship between EME1 expression and OS and Disease-Free Survival (DFS). DFS measures the time interval from the first diagnosis to the recurrence of the disease or death for any reason. On the other hand, OS represents the duration between the initial diagnosis and death for any reason. Afterward, we verified and enhanced our discoveries by using the KM plotter [20] to analyze five specific types of cancer: breast, ovarian, lung, stomach, and liver malignancies. This enabled a comprehensive statistical examination of the correlation between EME1 expression and patient survival. To assure the dependability of our data, we accounted for potential confounding variables that might influence survival outcomes. Adjusted analyses were performed to enhance the accuracy of the findings by including characteristics encompassing age, gender, treatment modalities, and tumor stage. This rigorous approach improves the reliability of our results on the predictive importance of EME1 expression, providing a comprehensive understanding of its impact on OS and DFS in various cancer types.

Association between EME1 genetic alterations and patient survival

Cancer progression involves intricate genetic changes disrupting the regulation of normal cell proliferation, culminating in malignant transformation [21]. In this investigation, we analyzed the genetic changes in the EME1 gene in tumor specimens by using the cBioPortal database to ascertain the landscape of these modifications (https://www.cbioportal.org/) [22]. We aimed to elucidate the diverse types, locations, and consequences of EME1 mutations on clinical outcomes, shedding light on their potential impact on patient survival.

In silico ADMET prediction

In silico ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) profiling was conducted utilizing cheminformatics-based prediction platforms to evaluate the pharmacokinetic properties of the chosen compounds: Dioscin, Troxerutin, and Everolimus. The forecasts were derived from molecular characteristics such as molecular weight, topological polar surface area (TPSA), LogP, hydrogen bonding potential, and rotatable bonds.

Water solubility was assessed utilizing three distinct models: ESOL, Ali, and SILICOS-IT. Pharmacokinetic characteristics, including gastrointestinal absorption, blood–brain barrier permeability, P-glycoprotein substrate status, skin permeation (Log Kp), and cytochrome P450 enzyme inhibition (CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4), were anticipated. Drug-likeness was assessed utilizing established rule-based filters, encompassing Lipinski's Rule of Five, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge criteria. The bioavailability score, lead-likeness, and synthetic accessibility were assessed. These methods facilitated a thorough evaluation of the drugs' pharmacokinetic characteristics before experimental validation.

EME1 metamorphosis: exploring its implications on infiltration and functionality of varied immune components

A comprehensive understanding of the human immune system's role in tumor development has revealed the existence of distinct cell populations with diverse impacts [23]. This research investigates the influence of EME1 genetic changes on many aspects of the immune system in tumor settings. Recent investigations have manifested a robust correlation between elevated levels of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and adverse consequences in cancer and resistance to immunotherapies. This correlation is particularly prominent in breast, colorectal, lung, and hematologic cancer[24–27]. Endothelial cells have a crucial function in the process of tumor angiogenesis and metastasis, releasing various substances into the bloodstream that facilitate metastasis to other parts of the body [28]. Hence, this research aims to evaluate the penetration and condition of MDSCs and endothelial cells to reveal possible associations with changes in the EME1 gene.

Using the TIMER2 web server [17], we investigated the connection between EME1 modifications and the presence of MDSCs, as well as the invasion of endothelial cells. In addition, the data obtained from the SangerBox website were evaluated to investigate the relationship between EME1 expression in cancerous tissue and tumor mutational burden (TMB), as well as immunological checkpoints[29]. This comprehensive approach aims to elucidate the interplay between EME1 alterations and immune cell dynamics within TME, providing valuable insights into their potential impact on tumor progression and therapeutic responses.

EME1 expression level at the single-cell level

We utilized the TISCH platform to evaluate EME1 expression at the single-cell level within the TME (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/) [30]. To establish a more accurate baseline representation of EME1 expression, we deliberately excluded datasets from treated, metastatic, and relapsed patients, focusing solely on untreated tumors. While this selective approach allows for a concentrated analysis, it does introduce certain limitations. These exclusions primarily impact the generalizability of our results across various tumor stages and may introduce some level of bias. The analysis employing the "pheatmap" tool in R provides insights into EME1 expression patterns.

Analysis of EME1 enrichment

Before doing the enrichment analysis, we carefully selected and organized two sets of proteins: one consisting of proteins interacting with EME1 and the other including proteins connected with EME1 in the TME. To compile these lists, we utilized the STRING (https://string-db.org/) [31] and GEPIA2 [18] databases, respectively. The filtering thresholds were as follows: Minimum interaction score: 0.7 (high confidence); Clustering algorithm: MCODE plugin, default settings; Hub gene selection via degree centrality ≥ 5. A Venn diagram was constructed to illustrate the proteins common to both lists, accessible at this link: http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/. To enhance the accuracy of cancer identification using machine learning techniques, we meticulously obtained LIHC (Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma) gene expression data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; v2023.01; Accessed on March 12, 2024) [32], employing the TCGAbiolinks package within the R programming environment [33]. This extensive dataset includes 50 samples from persons in good health and 371 samples from individuals with primary malignancies. To enhance the accuracy of our analysis, we specifically retrieved the gene expression profiles of genes common to both the STRING and GEPIA2 datasets.

We used a thorough methodology to implement four distinct machine learning models (Table S3): logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and XGBoost. The performance of these models was extensively evaluated and contrasted using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, which was conducted using the Scikit-learn package in Python [34]. Enrichment analysis was conducted on the proteins obtained from the lists above to understand the molecular pathways involved in the oncogenic role of EME1, employing the DAVID [35].

Training/Validation Strategy The dataset was split into an 80/20 stratified train-test split to maintain proportional distribution of high vs. low EME1 expression groups.

Cross-Validation Within the training set, we applied fivefold cross-validation to tune model performance and prevent overfitting. Cross-validation results were averaged and used to select optimal hyperparameters before evaluating on the held-out test set.

Feature Selection We employed Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with cross-validation to rank features by importance and eliminate redundancy. Feature importance was evaluated using the mean decrease in Gini index (for RF/XGBoost)and coefficient magnitudes (for LR/SVM).

Hyperparameter Optimization A grid search was performed over the following ranges:

LR:penalty = ['l1', 'l2'], C = [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10].

SVM:kernel = ['linear', 'rbf'], C = [0.1, 1, 10], gamma = ['scale'].

RF:n_estimators = [100, 200], max_depth = [5, 10, None].

XGBoost learning_rate = [0.01, 0.1], n_estimators = [100, 200], max_depth = [3, 6]

Performance Metrics and Overfitting Control: Models were evaluated using accuracy, AUC, F1-score, and confusion matrices on both training and test sets. No evidence of overfitting was observed, as training and test performance remained comparable across all four models.

Reproducibility: All code was executed using scikit-learn (v1.2.2) and XGBoost (v1.7.3) libraries in Python. Random seeds were fixed to ensure reproducibility of the result.

Discovering promising pharmaceuticals: high throughput virtual screening identifies potential EME1 inhibitors

Receptor structure, a 3D predicted Crossover Junction Endonuclease EME1 protein model, was obtained from the AlphaFold structure prediction website (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/Q96AY2) [36]. The model was generated by modeling the UniProt entry (Proteome ID: UP000005640) as a template. The structure was constructed using AutoDock Vina after obtaining it in PDB format [37]. Subsequently, polar hydrogens were included, and the energy reduction process was carried out using the prepare_receptor4.py program from AutoDockTools (ADT). The Kollman-united charge technique was used to calculate partial atomic charges, and the resultant file was stored in PDBQT format.

A dataset comprising 3180 FDA-approved drugs was obtained from the ZINC database in SDF format [38]. Using AutoDockTools (ADT, v1.5.6), the prepare_ligand4.py function was employed to generate the dataset in PDBQT format. Since no binding pocket was reported, a blind docking protocol was employed to allow structures to be docked in all protein pockets, aiming to discover appropriate binding sites. Docking parameters were set according to the Lamarckian genetic algorithm, with an energy evaluation of 2.500, 100 cycles, and a population size of 150 [39]. Ten binding modes were generated for each ligand, with an energy difference of no more than 3 kcal/mol between each mode. Conformations displaying the lowest binding free energy were considered optimal and retrieved. Two-dimensional interaction figures were generated using the BIOVA Discovery Studio Visualizer 2021 [40]. From the pool of 3180 compounds, the eight most potent pharmaceuticals were identified through molecular docking based on their binding scores (< -8 kcal/mol).

Unveiling molecular interactions: molecular dynamics (MD) simulation of EME1 with Troxerutin, Everolimus, and Dioscin

Troxerutin, Everolimus, and Dioscin emerged as promising candidates due to their favorable binding interactions and high bioavailability. MD simulations lasting 100 ns were performed for these three ligands against EME1 employing GROMACS-2023.1[41]. The CHARMM36 force field was employed to generate the protein structure, while the General force field (CGenFF) servers were employed to generate the ligand topology. Solvation was accomplished by using a dodecahedral unit cell form and implementing periodic boundary constraints set at a distance of 10 Å to prevent atom interactions from occurring beyond the box's border. The steepest descent minimization approach was used for ion addition, namely sodium and chloride ions, to achieve protein neutralization. The steric incompatibilities were addressed by minimizing energy utilizing the steepest descent minimization technique. The force threshold was set at 10.0 kJ/mol, and a maximum of 50,000 steps was allowed. Subsequently, two equilibration processes, specifically NVT and NPT, were conducted for 50,000 steps, equivalent to 10 picoseconds. The modified Berendsen thermostat and leap-frog integrator were employed in these procedures. In the end, molecular dynamics simulations were performed for 100 ns, with each step taking 2 femtoseconds. The MM/PBSA approach was employed to compute the binding free energy of the interaction between the receptor and ligand. This was done using the gmx MMPBSA v1.4.1 program based on MMPBSA.py [42].

The observed disparity in EME1 levels between tumor and normal tissues prompted an investigation into the potential impact of EME1 expression on patient survival outcomes. Utilizing GEPIA2 and the KM plotter, we conducted comprehensive analyses across various cancer types. EME1 Genetic alterations: implications for patient outcome across common human cancers

Analysis of the cBioPortal database revealed genetic abnormalities in the EME1 gene across various cancer types, including BRCA, pleural mesothelioma (MESO), and endometrial cancer, among others. The frequency of these alterations was approximately 6%. "Amplification" emerged as the primary type of genetic alteration in most tumors studied, except for head and neck cancer, where "mutation" predominated, and ocular melanoma, where "deep deletion" was prevalent (Fig. 5A). An in-depth analysis of EME1 mutation patterns highlighted missense mutations as the most common type, with site F93 being particularly affected, exhibiting three documented missense mutations in patients with uterine-related cancer (Fig. 5B). Remarkably, the investigation into EME1 genetic alterations manifested a negative connection between these alterations and patient survival in both DFS and PFS models (Fig. 5C).

Impact of EME1 expression on the TME and immune interactions

The TME comprises various cell types with distinct functions. In this investigation, we ascertained the connection between EME1 expression in malignant tissues, immunosuppressive MDSCs, and the anti-cancer activity of endothelial cells. Our study found that almost 90% of the examined tumors showed a significant positive correlation between EME1 expression and MDSC levels (Fig. 6A). No negative correlations were observed between EME1 and MDSCs across tumor samples. Specifically, a positive association between EME1 and MDSCs was identified in BLCA, BRCA, COAD, KIRC, LUAD, PAAD, SARC, STAD, THCA, and THYM. Conversely, an analysis of endothelial cells revealed that 11 tumors emerged with an adverse connection between EME1 expression and endothelial cell activity, with no tumors exhibiting a positive correlation (Fig. 6A). This negative association underscores the potential impact of EME1 on inhibiting the anti-cancer functions of endothelial cells.

ADMET profile

The in silico ADMET study identified numerous critical pharmacokinetic features and constraints related to the selected compounds. All three drugs had elevated molecular weights (Dioscin: 742.68 g/mol; Everolimus: 958.22 g/mol), substantial TPSA values (> 200 Å2), and a significant quantity of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, characteristics commonly linked to inadequate membrane permeability and diminished oral absorption. Gastrointestinal absorption was consistently anticipated to be minimal for all substances. Moreover, none of the drugs were anticipated to traverse the blood–brain barrier, and both Dioscin and Everolimus were recognized as P-glycoprotein substrates, indicating possible efflux from enterocytes.

Notwithstanding its high polarity and molecular complexity, Troxerutin exhibited adequate water solubility (ESOL LogS = 2.79; soluble), whereas Dioscin showed intermediate solubility. In contrast, Everolimus had poor solubility across all models (e.g., ESOL LogS = 8.89). Significantly, none of the substances inhibited key cytochrome P450 enzymes, suggesting a low likelihood of metabolic drug-drug interactions. Nonetheless, their bioavailability scores were poor (0.17), and all three compounds contravened several drug-likeness criteria (e.g., Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Muegge), mostly due to excessive molecular size, hydrogen bonding, and topological polar surface area (TPSA). The synthetic accessibility varied from 7.4 (Dioscin) to 10.0 (Everolimus), indicating their structural intricacy and possible formulation difficulties. The ADMET results align with the established pharmacokinetic constraints of natural products and macrocyclic medicines.

Using data from the SangerBox online server, we observed that ACC, KICH, HNSC, THCA, KIRC, liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and PRAD displayed a positive correlation between EME1 expression and various immunological checkpoints. Unlocking the cellular landscape: single-cell profiling of EME1 expression across diverse cancer types

The Tumor Immune Single-Cell Hub (TISCH) was used to thoroughly analyze the EME1 expression at the single-cell level inside the TME. We conducted an analysis that included 136 datasets covering 45 distinct forms of cancer. The heatmap shown in Fig. 8 provides useful insights by visually representing the expression of EME1 in forty different cell types, encompassing functional, stromal, malignant, and immunological cells. Our research shows that the EME1 expression is significantly increased in immune cells, namely in T cells and monocyte/macrophage populations. Moreover, notable amounts of gene expression are seen in malignant cells. This comprehensive analysis provides insight into the unique cell types inside the TME where EME1 is selectively expressed. This enhances our nuanced comprehension of the many functions of EME1 in different cellular situations.

Deciphering Cellular Diversity: Exploring EME1 Expression in a Spectrum of Cancer Types through Single-Cell Analysis

Nevertheless, it is imperative to evaluate these findings in the framework of the exclusion criteria employed in the data analysis, which predominantly considered untreated tumors. The intentional omission of certain variables may impact the reported pattern of EME1 expression in various cell types. Hence, it is advisable to use care when applying these findings to different tumor stages and treatment settings. It is necessary to do more research that includes a wider range of tumor situations to improve and build upon the established gene expression patterns.

Unraveling EME1 interactome: insights into molecular pathways across diverse cancer types

The EME1 importance in cancer prognosis and its implications on immune cells within the TME highlight the need to unravel its molecular pathways across diverse cancer types. To explore these pathways, we initially extracted the 50 most rigorously confirmed EME1-interacting proteins from the STRING database, constructing a network to visualize their interactions (Fig. 9A). Using the GEPIA2 webserver, we subsequently discovered 100 genes that showed a connection with EME1 expression in the TCGA tumor panel. The "Gene Corr" module in TIMER generated a heatmap confirming substantial positive correlations between EME1 and five genes across all TCGA cancers (Fig. 9B). Further analysis revealed three duplicated genes in our datasets (Fig. 9C). Next, using the "Correlation Analysis" module, we visualized the top five genes exhibiting the strongest association with EME1: C17orf53, PLK4, FANCD2, ORC6, and KIF2C (Fig. 9E).

Screening FDA-approved drugs against the EME1 protein using virtual methods

In this study, we conducted virtual screening on a dataset comprising 3180 FDA-approved drugs against the EME1 protein. The 3D structure of EME1 was downloaded from the AlphaFold website (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/Q96AY2), and its 3D structure, the model's Predicted Aligned Error (PAE), and AlphaFold's per-residue model confidence score (pLDDT) are shown in Fig. 10A–B

EME1 3D structure obtained from the AlphaFold website showing AlphaFold per-residue model confidence score (pLDDT) (A), and the predicted model's TED Domains and Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) (B)

The screening results identified 8 drugs with favorable binding energies (> 8 kcal/mol) (Table 1). Through blind screening, we identified a single druggable active pocket within the EME1 protein featuring key residues such as Asp16, Glu18, Arg483, and Asn502. Among the eight hits, Sodium aescinate (SA) emerged as the most promising candidate. Known for its anti-inflammatory and anti-exudative properties, SA formed four hydrogen bonds with key residues Val471, Arg483, and Asn502. However, its clinical utility may be limited due to reported issues with bioavailability and side effects.

Micafungin sodium, a semi-synthetic echinocandin used as an antifungal agent, exhibited favorable interactions with EME1, including five hydrogen bonds and a pi-cation interaction with Asp16, Arg483, Asn502, and Glu536. Similarly, Neoral, an immunosuppressant medication, showed potent inhibition of EME1 with one hydrogen bond formation with Asp14, suggesting a potential mode of action through hydrophobic interactions. Escin, a saponin with anti-inflammatory and vasoconstrictive properties, and ginsenosides, another saponin, demonstrated strong binding energies with EME1 despite reported challenges with oral bioavailability. Troxerutin, a semi-synthetic bioflavonoid, formed four hydrogen bonds with Gln487, Gln488, Arg491, and Arg543, suggesting a potential molecular pathway for its reported pharmacological actions. Furthermore, Everolimus and Dioscin, known for their anti-cancer activities, exhibited favorable binding affinities with EME1. Everolimus inhibits kinase receptors by blocking mTOR protein, while Dioscin inhibits the STAT3 signaling pathway in human hepatocellular carcinoma cells.

These findings emphasize the possibility of repurposing FDA-approved drugs for targeting EME1 and provide insights into potential therapeutic avenues for cancer treatment.

Unraveling stability dynamics: molecular insights into protein–ligand interactions through MD simulations

This study employed two methods, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and measurement of solvent accessible surface area (SASA), to investigate conformational changes in the protein–ligand complex during a 100-ns MD simulation, aiming to assess the simulated system stability. After applying the trjconv function of GROMACS to re-center and re-wrap the complex within the unit cells, RMSD and SASA parameters were calculated. The protein backbone RMSD plot (Fig. 11A) highlights the remarkable stability of the EME1 protein backbone upon interaction with the three investigated ligands. Dioscin complex exhibited the highest stability, followed by Everolimus, while Troxerutin demonstrated lower stability with increased oscillations. Similarly, comparable RMSD patterns were observed for the ligands when bound to the target protein (Fig. 11B). Dioscin and Everolimus displayed relatively consistent fluctuations, remaining within the stable range of approximately 0.1 nm. In contrast, Troxerutin exhibited significant unstable fluctuations, particularly evident after 60 ns into the simulation. The evaluation of SASA data for the three complexes indicated significant stability over the first 20 ns of the simulation, with fluctuations consistently ranging between 335 and 350 nm2, indicating their compactness (Fig. 11C).

Discussion

Oncogenes contribute to inducing DNA replication stress and chromosomal instability, which are both important factors in the development of tumors. Additionally, these factors are intricately linked to medication resistance and poor prognosis [43]. The interplay between oncogene-induced stress and genomic instability not only accelerates the evolution of cancer but also complicates therapeutic strategies, leading to challenges in achieving effective and sustained treatment outcomes [44]. EME1 is a crucial element in preserving the stability of the genome since it plays several important functions in repairing DNA and resolving intricate DNA structures. EME1 contributes to DSB repair, one of the most severe types of DNA damage. If DSBs are not repaired, it may result in genomic instability and the development of cancer [17]. In addition, EME1 is involved in the resolution of HJs and replication forks, which are intricate structures that arise throughout the processes of DNA replication and recombination. These structures are crucial for ensuring accurate chromosomal segregation and stability [17, 45].

Recent studies have expanded our understanding of EME1's functions beyond its traditional roles in DNA repair. EME1 has been shown to contribute to regulating fundamental cellular processes encompassing volume regulation, proliferation, and migration. These functions are particularly relevant in cancer biology, where dysregulation of these processes can lead to tumor progression and metastasis [46]. For instance, EME1's role in cell volume regulation is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to environmental stress. At the same time, its involvement in proliferation and migration underscores its significance in cancer cell growth and dissemination.

The implications of EME1 in cancer recurrence are profound, particularly in cancers such as bladder, glioma, and prostate cancers. In these cancers, EME1 expression levels and activity have been correlated with tumor aggressiveness and resistance to therapy [46]. For example, in BLCA, high EME1 expression is linked to a worse prognosis and increased likelihood of recurrence. Similarly, in gliomas and prostate cancers, EME1's role in DNA repair and cell cycle regulation contributes to the tumors' ability to survive and proliferate despite therapeutic interventions [11–13].

Replication stress-induced scenarios reveal the critical role of the MUS81-EME1 endonuclease complex, particularly in maintaining chromosome stability at common fragile sites (CFSs). This process often results in breaks or gaps in chromosomes, especially in regions where replication is impeded. The subsequent cleavage of incompletely replicated DNA exacerbates the issue, leading to faulty chromosomal segregation and promoting carcinogenesis [17, 47]. Clinical correlations further emphasize the significance of EME1 in cancer prognosis. Elevated EME1 expression is correlated with worse survival rates in individuals with pancreatic cancer, underscoring its possibility of it as a prognostic marker [14]. Additionally, studies such as that by MacGregor et al. illuminate the prognostic utility of EME1 in esophageal adenocarcinoma, where elevated EME1 mRNA levels correlate with decreased OS, underscoring its relevance as a prognostic biomarker [15]. Overall, the multifaceted involvement of EME1 in critical cellular processes and its clinical implications in various cancers underscore its pivotal role in tumorigenesis. These findings collectively identify EME1 as a promising target for therapeutic interventions and a potential predictive marker for cancer management strategies.

Numerous studies have endeavored to elucidate the EME1 function in cancer pathogenesis across diverse tumor types. However, a significant gap persists in the literature regarding comprehensive research that thoroughly explores the differential effects of EME1 on various human cancers. Understanding the complexities of the TME is paramount, as it encompasses multiple components influencing tumor development, immune responses to abnormal growth, patient responsiveness to therapy, and OS [48]. Establishing correlations between specific genes and tumor development necessitates a comprehensive approach that accounts for the intricate nature of the problem, demanding analysis from multiple perspectives. To address this, our study undertook an extensive investigation across several cancer types to assess the carcinogenic impact of EME1. Initially, we examined the EME1 distribution in various human tissues, revealing its presence across numerous organs. Oncogenic proteins often exhibit heightened expression in tumor tissues compared with their normal counterparts, prompting us to evaluate the specific expression patterns of EME1 in different human malignancies. Our findings unveiled a significant rise in EME1 expression across a spectrum of tumor types, including BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PRAD, rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), STAD, THCA, UCEC, CESC, DLBC, LGG, SARC, THYM, and UCS.

Our work extended to investigating the connection between EME1 expression levels and tumor stage and grade, yielding significant findings. Specifically, we observed a marked elevation in both stage and grade in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) tumors, which correlated strongly with elevated EME1 expression levels. Our work thoroughly determined the EME1 expression patterns in various forms of cancer and correlated these patterns with clinical factors such as tumor stage and grade. This analysis yielded significant insights into the EME1 function in the evolution of cancer. These outcomes underscore the potential utility of EME1 as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker and suggest promising avenues for further research into its therapeutic targeting in oncology.

Survival analysis is essential for assessing the disease progression and the effectiveness of treatments [49]. Our study sought to clarify the relationship between EME1 expression and OS in cancer patients. An analysis of the GEPIA database manifested a significant correlation between elevated EME1 expression and a more adverse prognosis in MESO, LGG, KIRP, and LIHC. This correlation was further corroborated by an examination of both DFS and OS. KM plot analysis consistently demonstrated that higher EME1 expression is associated with unfavorable prognosis across many cancer types, demonstrating EME1's potential as a prognostic biomarker. Furthermore, various genetic mutations are recognized as prognostic indicators in human cancers. For instance, mutations in KRAS and NRAS have been linked to adverse outcomes in pancreatic, lung, and metastatic melanoma cancers [50–52]. Expanding on this knowledge, our study ascertained the implications of EME1 genetic alterations on patient survival. We identified a clear association between EME1 genetic mutations and poorer prognosis regarding both OS and DFS. These outcomes emphasize the critical role of EME1 in cancer prognosis and suggest potential avenues for its therapeutic targeting.

Tumor immunotherapy has manifested as a transformative method in cancer treatment [53], with immunotherapy agents such as αPD-1 demonstrating significant efficacy across various malignancies, encompassing malignant melanoma, gastric carcinoma, and HCC [54]. To deepen our understanding in this domain, we ascertained the connection between elevated EME1 expression in tumor tissues and immune cell infiltration within the TME. Our findings indicated a predominant occurrence of amplification mutations in the EME1 gene across most cancer cases, suggesting a potential role for EME1 in modulating immune responses within the TME. Our study provides compelling evidence of the prognostic significance of EME1 expression and genetic mutations in cancer patients. Furthermore, these findings suggest an intricate interplay between EME1 expression and the immune response in tumor immunotherapy, thereby warranting further investigation into the therapeutic potential of targeting EME1 in cancer treatment strategies.

We initially focused on MDSCs, known for their role in promoting tumor cell survival and dissemination [55]. MDSCs also enhance tumor angiogenesis and contribute to the development of cancer stem cells [56, 57]. Consistent with previous studies, our findings indicate that a greater MDSC infiltration is linked to worse clinical outcomes in cancer patients [58]. Nevertheless, further study is necessary to clarify the direct connection between EME1 expression and MDSC invasion.

Furthermore, tumor angiogenesis, characterized by forming new blood vessels, is critical for supplying nutrients and oxygen to tumors, facilitating their growth and progression. The endothelium plays multiple roles that support tumor dissemination, including intravasation, where invasive cancer cells enter the bloodstream [59]. Our comprehensive analysis identified a robust positive link between EME1 and MDSCs. At the same time, a significant negative connection was found between EME1 and endothelial cells across various cancer types, including BLCA, BRCA, COAD, KIRC, LUAD, PAAD, SARC, STAD, THCA, and THYM. These findings underscore the multifaceted function of EME1 in cancer progression and immune modulation, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in oncology.

Our exploration of molecular interactions identified RAD54L, BRCA1, and KIF14 as consistently appearing in the "EME1-interacting" and "EME1-correlated" protein groups. Considering the well-documented functions of these proteins in human cancer [60], understanding their interaction with EME1 presents a compelling avenue for developing novel antitumor therapies. In addition, our work showed that machine learning models effectively use EME1-related protein expression patterns to identify LIHC. Our models had strong predictive skills, with high AUC values spanning 0.90 to 0.92 for the LR, SVM, XGBoost, and RF algorithms. Specifically, the RF model exhibited outstanding performance, with the highest AUC value of 0.92. ROC curve analysis provided visual confirmation of the model's discriminative ability, underscoring its efficacy in distinguishing between normal and primary tumor samples based on the expression of EME1-associated proteins. In conclusion, our study employed a comprehensive approach to uncover the biochemical pathways underlying the cancer-promoting role of EME1. We elucidated its interaction with key molecular players, its association with components of the TME, and its utility in predictive modeling for cancer detection. Herein, we offer valuable knowledge on the complex function of EME1 in cancer biology and propose possible targets for therapeutic intervention.

The virtual screening study of 3180 FDA-approved drugs against the EME1 protein yielded significant findings, identifying several promising candidates for further evaluation. Among the screened compounds, eight drugs exhibited favorable binding energies (< 8 kcal/mol) against EME1. A blind screening pinpointed a singular druggable active pocket within the protein, highlighting key residues such as Asp16, Glu18, Arg483, and Asn502. The top hits, including Sodium aescinate, Micafungin sodium, Escin, Ginsenoside, Neoral, Troxerutin, Everolimus, and Dioscin, demonstrated binding energies ranging from 8.8585 to 7.8839 kcal/mol. Sodium aescinate emerged as particularly potent, forming crucial hydrogen bonds with Val471, Arg483, and Asn502, though its clinical application may be limited by bioavailability and side effects. Micafungin sodium, primarily an antifungal agent, showed notable interactions with EME1 but also suffers from bioavailability issues. Neoral, an immunosuppressant, exhibited significant inhibition of EME1, likely due to hydrophobic interactions within the ligand pocket. Escin and Ginsenoside, saponins with anti-inflammatory properties, displayed robust binding energies despite oral bioavailability challenges. Troxerutin, a semi-synthetic bioflavonoid, demonstrated significant interactions with EME1, suggesting a potential molecular mechanism underlying its pharmacological effects. The compounds examined in this study do not entirely conform to Lipinski's Rule of Five; nonetheless, such deviations are prevalent among bioactive natural products and FDA-approved medications, especially those featuring intricate glycosidic or steroidal architectures. Dioscin and Troxerutin possess substantial molecular weights, increased polar surface areas, and numerous hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. Nonetheless, these attributes do not negate biological efficacy, as demonstrated by the comprehensive pharmacological literature about these drugs. Everolimus, although its anticipated poor oral absorption is predicted in silico, is a therapeutically utilized mTOR inhibitor with recognized formulation techniques to enhance bioavailability. Consequently, the identified rule infractions are rectified by utilizing recognized in vivo activities and existing delivery methods, such as lipid-based carriers or nanodelivery systems, which may improve pharmacokinetics. This highlights the necessity for contextual analysis of rule-based filters when assessing structurally intricate, pharmacologically significant substances.

The study highlights the potential for repurposing FDA-approved drugs as EME1 inhibitors, warranting further experimental validation to assess their therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles. Everolimus and Dioscin, recognized for their anti-cancer properties, also exhibited favorable binding affinities, indicating their potential as EME1 inhibitors. MD simulations provided further insight into protein–ligand complex stability, with RMSD analyses showing stable interactions between EME1 and the investigated ligands. Although Troxerutin complexes showed some variability, SASA assessments confirmed overall stability during the simulation period.

In conclusion, this study presents a compelling foundation for repurposing FDA-approved drugs as EME1 inhibitors, with several candidates demonstrating significant potential. Additional research and experimental validation are essential to verify these outcomes and explore their clinical applications in cancer therapy. Additional analysis of root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) and hydrogen bond formation provided detailed insights into the dynamic behavior of protein–ligand complexes over time. Our integrated computational analyses demonstrate a robust correlation between EME1 expression, immune regulation, and potential druggability across several cancer types; nevertheless, these findings are still predictive. Experimental validation, including in vitro knockdown/overexpression studies, co-culture experiments with immune cells, and in vivo models, is crucial to establish the biological and therapeutic significance of EME1 before clinical application. Dioscin consistently exhibited robust hydrogen bonding throughout the simulation, indicating strong binding affinity. Everolimus displayed stable interactions, albeit with varying hydrogen bond counts, suggesting a reliable yet flexible binding profile. In contrast, Troxerutin demonstrated fluctuations in hydrogen bond formation, correlating with observed instability in long-range simulations. Moreover, molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area (MM/PBSA) analysis shed light on the energetics of protein–ligand interactions, emphasizing the contributions of van der Waals and electrostatic energies across the examined complexes. This analysis highlighted the nuanced interplay of forces stabilizing these interactions.

In summary, integrating virtual screening findings with MD simulations offers valuable insights into the potential therapeutic significance of the identified compounds targeting EME1. These results underscore the necessity of further experimental validation to fully elucidate their clinical implications and potential as cancer therapeutics.

Conclusions

Herein, we utilized a multi-omics approach to dissect the EME1 function in tumor progression, revealing critical insights. EME1 was significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, with this overexpression closely connected with advanced tumor stages, higher grades, and poorer clinical outcomes across a spectrum of human cancers. Additionally, genetic alterations in EME1 were found to predict reduced patient survival. They were associated with increased infiltration of immune suppressor cells, underscoring EME1's oncogenic potential and highlighting its promise as a target for antitumor therapies. We identified several potential EME1 inhibitors through a chemoinformatic approach, paving the way for in-depth investigations. We are actively engaged in experimental wet lab validations of these inhibitors, and we look forward to disseminating our findings soon.

Limitations and future directions

To conclude our investigation, it is crucial to acknowledge and tackle many inherent limitations that might affect the interpretation and applicability of our results. Firstly, the use of bioinformatics analysis and publically accessible datasets may induce biases and underscore the need for careful interpretation, emphasizing the need for further experimental validations. Furthermore, the observed connections between EME1 expression and clinical outcomes should be seen as links rather than suggesting causality. This emphasizes the need for investigations into the mechanisms behind the functional effects of EME1 dysregulation in tumor formation. While EME1 expression positively correlates with MDSC infiltration across multiple cancer types, this association does not confirm a causal relationship. Further experimental validation—such as EME1 knockdown or CRISPR perturbation followed by immune profiling—is needed to establish mechanistic insights into its role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Although the pan-cancer study offers a thorough understanding of cancer, it is important to do further research specifically targeting different cancer types to account for their unique characteristics. Moreover, our work primarily focused on the overexpression of EME1, requiring experimental verifications to determine the functional repercussions in cancer cells. Finally, the chemoinformatics method used to identify potential EME1 inhibitors is an initial stage that highlights the importance of conducting thorough experimental validations, such as in vitro assays and preclinical studies, to accurately evaluate the therapeutic potential of the identified candidates. Recognizing these constraints improves the clarity of our results and offers significant perspectives for directing future research efforts.
