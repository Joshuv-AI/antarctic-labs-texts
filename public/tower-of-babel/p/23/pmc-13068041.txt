Acoustoelectric control of optoelectronic anisotropy for reconfigurable polarimetry
Authors: Chang Jiang, Jiaming Gu, Hanlin Lu, Junchen Zhou, Enze Zhang, Jinwen Guo, Cheng Zhang, Faxian Xiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harnessing light polarization provides a powerful degree of freedom for optical communications, imaging, and sensing. Two-dimensional (2D) anisotropic semiconductors have emerged as a promising platform for miniaturized on-chip polarimetry; however, their polarization responses remain constrained by intrinsic crystal symmetries and static device geometries, limiting functional tunability. Here, we demonstrate continuous and dynamic control over optoelectronic anisotropy in a 2D rhenium disulfide semiconductor through acoustoelectric coupling with surface acoustic waves (SAWs). SAW propagation through the semiconducting channel substantially enhances the photovoltage response via an acousto-drag photovoltaic mechanism. This acoustoelectric coupling not only amplifies the global photoresponse but also continuously rotates its polarization symmetry axis—shifting from the intrinsic orientation of the rhenium disulfide crystal to that of the lithium niobate substrate—as the acoustic power increases. Crucially, by adopting a machine learning algorithm, i.e., random forest, we achieve independent and simultaneous detection of both optical power and linear polarization angle within a planar, integrated device. These findings establish a previously unknown paradigm in acoustoelectronics for dynamically reconfigurable polarimetry, mediated by hybrid phonon-charge interactions in 2D materials.

INTRODUCTION

Polarization, a fundamental property of light alongside intensity and wavelength, carries rich information critical to optical communications, imaging, and environmental sensing. Conventional polarimeters decode polarization states via time-division (1, 2), amplitude-division (3), aperture-division (4, 5), or focal plane–division (6) schemes. These methods rely on discrete optical components, such as polarizers, retarders, and beam splitters, to spatially or temporally separate the light field. Despite their effectiveness, these systems are often bulky, mechanically complex, and poorly compatible with integration in compact photonic platforms. In contrast, their underlying photodetectors are typically polarization insensitive.

An emerging alternative leverages the intrinsic anisotropy of low-symmetry two-dimensional (2D) materials—such as ReSe2 and black phosphorus—which exhibit polarization-dependent photocurrent aligned with their crystal axes (7–11). Under linearly polarized illumination, these materials produce an anisotropic photocurrent following a cos(2θ−β) dependence, where θ denotes the incident polarization angle and β represents the crystal orientation (9). While this response enables direct electrical detection of polarization, its intrinsic twofold symmetry prevents discrimination between mirror-symmetric linear polarization states, thereby limiting comprehensive polarization characterization.

To overcome this symmetry constraint, several strategies have been explored to break the symmetry and enhance polarization detection capability. Approaches include stacking 2D materials with misaligned crystallographic axes (12–15), as well as integrating nanostructured meta-surfaces that impose artificial optical anisotropy (16–18). Although these schemes can extend polarization sensitivity—e.g., enabling Stokes vector detection—their responses remain static, determined by fabrication-defined geometry or interlayer registry, and thus lack dynamic tunability.

Here, we demonstrate an approach that enables dynamic, in situ control of the polarization response in optoelectronic devices. This is achieved through the acoustoelectric coupling between an exemplary anisotropic 2D semiconductor (ReS2) and a propagating surface acoustic wave (SAW) on a piezoelectric LiNbO3 substrate. SAWs, consisting of coupled mechanical and electric fields propagating along the surface of the substrate, provide a powerful means to dynamically modulate electric and optoelectronic properties of low-dimensional systems (19–25). Through this interaction, photoexcited carriers undergo acousto-drag motion, giving rise to a pronounced photovoltaic effect whose magnitude can be effectively tuned by acoustic power. Critically, this SAW-mediated effect not only enhances the overall photovoltage but also reconfigures its polarization dependence. As the SAW amplitude increases, the symmetry axis of the photoresponse continuously rotates from alignment with the crystallographic orientation of the ReS2 lattice toward that dictated by the LiNbO3 substrate, resulting from the local modulation of acoustic power by light. Notably, with the help of the random forest algorithm, integrating two orthogonally propagating SAWs enables simultaneous and decoupled measurement of light intensity and linear polarization angle, all within a compact, planar architecture. Photovoltage mapping reveals a strong SAW-induced response in the substrate region without ReS2 coverage, while polarized Raman spectroscopy confirms that the observed polarization switching aligns with the intrinsic anisotropy of the LiNbO3 substrate. These findings establish an acoustoelectric mechanism for dynamic symmetry control in optoelectronic systems and offer a pathway toward adaptive, multifunctional polarimetric detection in integrated photonics.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Figure 1 compares three distinct approaches to modulating optoelectronic anisotropy. The first strategy uses stacked 2D crystals with intentionally misaligned crystallographic axes (Fig. 1A), producing heterostructures with broken symmetry that enable discrimination across a broader range of polarization states (13, 14). In particular, twisted bilayer graphene exhibits gate-tunable sensitivity to both linear and circular polarization due to twist-angle–induced modifications in band structure (12). Alternatively, metamaterial-integrated photodetectors were designed to exhibit artificial anisotropy (Fig. 1B), allowing full-Stokes vector detection through subwavelength structuring and thermoelectric coupling (16–18). Figure 1 (D and E) schematically illustrates the polarization discrimination mechanisms of these two approaches. The polarization response from a single anisotropic material (Fig. 1D) is mirror symmetric, while the combination of two anisotropic crystals, or two regions of metamaterial, can have a response without mirror symmetry (Fig. 1E). Figure 1C introduces our optoelectronic anisotropy modulation through acoustoelectric interaction. An anisotropic crystal is placed on a piezoelectric substrate, with misalignment of the crystal axes. When increasing the power of SAW, the symmetry of polarization response continuously changes from the anisotropic crystal to the piezoelectric substrate, as illustrated in Fig. 1F. Thus, this device can distinguish linear polarization through dynamic SAW power control.

We next demonstrate acoustoelectric-controlled optoelectronic response in device D1, which consists of a thick ReS2 flake (25 nm; note S1) transferred onto a 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrate (see Materials and Methods for device fabrication). The 1T′-ReS2 is a layered semiconductor with a bandgap of 1.37 eV (26). Its crystal belongs to P1¯ space group (27) and has been reported to have ferroelectricity (28) and polarization-dependent photoelectric response (26, 29). LiNbO3 is a piezoelectric material (30) that belongs to the R3c space group (31). Among all the geometries of the crystal, the 128° Y-cut x-propagating plane is testified to have the maximum electromechanical coupling coefficient for the Rayleigh-type SAW (32). In our experiment, the commercially available black 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrate is adopted. The interdigital transducer (IDT) pairs exciting SAW are fabricated to align with the x axis of the crystal with a work frequency of ~300 MHz, and the b axis of the ReS2 flake is placed at 45° to this direction.

Figure 2A presents the optical image and photovoltage mapping of device D1 under 633-nm illumination (note S2). A laser with a wavelength of 633 nm and a power of 40 μW is normally incident on the sample. Notably, the region without ReS2 exhibits a pronounced photovoltage, yielding a responsivity of ~819 V/W, approximately three times larger than that observed in the ReS2-covered region. While our previous work focused on photoresponse within 2D materials on LiNbO3 (23), the strong signal from the bare substrate region is unexpected, particularly given LiNbO3’s wide bandgap (3.56 eV) (33). This below-bandgap behavior suggests an indirect optothermal-acoustoelectric mechanism beyond the traditional electron-hole generation.

To account for this phenomenon, we proposed its underlying mechanism, illustrated in Fig. 2B. SAWs generated by the IDTs propagate across the LiNbO3 surface. Localized laser illumination raises the local temperature, inducing volume expansion and altering material properties—including elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric coefficients—which, in turn, modify the local SAW wavelength under excitation frequency. This variation causes scattering and reflection of SAWs, attenuating their amplitude. This attenuated SAW interacts with the ReS2 flake, generating an acoustoelectric response with additional attenuation. Collectively, these observations demonstrate an optoelectronic effect controlled by optothermal-acoustoelectric interaction.

To validate our proposed mechanism, we measure the transmission coefficient S21 of the IDT pairs (note S3). When illuminating a 633-nm light with a power of 2.4 mW at the central region of the IDT pairs, S21 displays a decrease around the center frequency, as shown in Fig. 2C, confirming the light-induced SAW attenuation. We design focusing IDTs with a similar center frequency to further examine the temperature variation. The focusing IDT consists of concentric circular-arc electrodes. Different from the conventional IDT with parallel electrodes, the SAW generated by focusing IDT is spatially concentrated near the focal point (34), which is located at the center of the concentric circles. Illumination at the focal region induces a shift of frequency in S21 (Fig. 2D). The light spot is intentionally sized to ~50 μm wide, which is comparable to the scale of the focal region. Thus, most wavefront is tuned in the case of focusing IDTs, while only a small portion of the SAW is influenced in the case of the conventional IDTs. These results enable us to unveil a pure effect of illumination rather than an interfered signal and provide unambiguous evidence of light-SAW interaction.

We measure S21 of the focusing IDT at various temperatures, as shown in Fig. 2E. The frequency shift caused by illumination exhibits the same directional dependence as temperature-induced variations. Assuming a linear response regime within this temperature range, we can estimate that the temperature increase caused by the illumination of 2.4 mW is about 17 K. Furthermore, finite-element simulations incorporating temperature-dependent elastic, piezoelectric, and dielectric coefficients and volume expansion (note S4) predict a 0.020-dB reduction of S21 at its center frequency (Fig. 2F) when the temperature increases by about 20 K—consistent with experimental observations and strongly supporting the optothermal origin of SAW attenuation.

We further investigate the linear polarization dependence of the acoustoelectric interaction. The light is focused on the LiNbO3 region adjacent to ReS2. The photovoltage presents a clear polarization response (Fig. 3A). To reveal the origin, we measure the polarized Raman spectra of the 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrate (note S5). Primary Raman modes show the polarization dependence (Fig. 3B). Two representative modes are plotted in polar coordinates (Fig. 3C). The photovoltage response has the same symmetry as the Raman modes rather than the propagating direction of SAW. Furthermore, the variation of phase in a focusing SAW also shows a clear polarization dependence (note S3). These findings establish that the polarization dependence of the acoustoelectric interaction is rooted in the symmetry of the 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrate. Previous studies also support the distinct polarization dependence in the polarized Raman spectra of LiNbO3 crystals (35), although the substrate orientation may differ.

Leveraging this mechanism, we design and fabricate a photodetector for simultaneous power and polarization detection (device D2). A few-layer ReS2 (2 nm; note S1) was transferred onto a preprepared 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrate equipped with two orthogonal IDT pairs. The SAW-X is defined as the SAW propagating along the x axis of the LiNbO3 crystal, while the SAW-Y is defined as the one orthogonal to SAW-X. The b axis of ReS2 is intentionally aligned to be 45° to both IDT pairs. The optical image of the few-layer ReS2 and IDT pairs is presented in Fig. 4 (A and B). When varying the power of SAW, the symmetry of polarization dependence changes, as shown in Fig. 4 (C to E) for SAW-X and Fig. 4 (F to H) for SAW-Y, respectively. The light is focused on the ReS2 region. Because the few-layer ReS2 in device D2 is relatively transparent, sufficient light can transmit and illuminate the underneath LiNbO3 and thus influence the acoustoelectric interaction. Notably, the angle of the polarization dependence converges to the same value under SAW-X and SAW-Y excitations, suggesting that the polarization dependence of acoustoelectric interaction is not connected to the direction of SAW but to the symmetry of the LiNbO3 substrate.

Last, we demonstrate the simultaneous detection of optical power and polarization using this ReS2/LiNbO3 device. By applying SAW-X, SAW-Y, and without SAW, three different photovoltage parameters, namely, Vx , Vy , and V0 can be acquired. First, we illustrate that the light power and polarization can be distinguished from these parameters. In the scatter plot of Vx and Vy (Fig. 5A), the data points from different laser powers can be separated by a series of parallel lines with a specific slope. Meanwhile, in the scatter plot of Vx and V0 (Fig. 5, B and C), the data points from different polarizations constitute an ellipse. The polarization angle of a data point can be determined from the position where it is located on the ellipse. Thus, we adopt a machine learning algorithm, random forest, to extract the information of light encoded in the optoelectronic responses (note S6). The schematic of the random forest algorithm is illustrated in Fig. 5D. The dataset is input to 200 decision trees. Each decision tree has randomly generated decision nodes and is trained by data with labels, i.e., light power and polarization angle. Different labels are trained by different forests. The orange nodes and lines schematically denote a path where data would go through when inputting to the algorithm. The performance of the algorithm is shown in Fig. 5 (E and F). The prediction of power is excellent, with the root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.40, while that of polarization angle is 10.21. RMSE of both labels is smaller than the measurement error, as depicted by the gray region in Fig. 5 (E and F). This result demonstrates the viability of integrated acoustoelectric systems for multidimensional light sensing.

In conclusion, we establish an optoelectronic effect enabled by acoustoelectric interaction. The illumination modulates the amplitude of SAW and thus acoustoelectric response. Experiment and simulation of the transmission coefficient suggest its origin from temperature-induced volume expansion and alteration of material coefficients. Polarized Raman spectroscopy confirms the governing role of substrate symmetry. Critically, SAWs enable continuous, dynamic reconfiguration of polarization response in ReS2/LiNbO3 heterostructures, facilitating simultaneous measurement of optical power and linear polarization state within a compact architecture. This work demonstrates acoustoelectric symmetry engineering in 2D materials and unlocks paradigms for adaptive multidimensional photodetection.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Device fabrication

The IDT pairs were patterned on commercial black 128° Y-cut LiNbO3 substrates by direct-write photolithography. The center frequency was designed to be 300 MHz. A metal layer of Cr (5 nm)/Au (75 nm) was deposited using an electron-beam evaporator. ReS2 flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals (HQ Graphene) onto Si/SiO2 substrates with Scotch tape and then transferred onto the LiNbO3 substrates using polycarbonate/polydimethylsiloxane stamps. A high-precision transfer stage (MetaTest) was adopted to place the flakes at the center of a pair of IDTs. High-quality ReS2 flakes were selected under optical microscopy, with thickness initially estimated by optical contrast and further verified by atomic force microscopy. The crystal axes were determined on the basis of the flake morphology and confirmed by polarization-dependent measurements. The patterns of electrodes were fabricated using the standard electron-beam lithography process. The contacts of Ti (5 nm)/Au (60 nm) were deposited via magnetron sputtering.

Photovoltage measurement

Photovoltage was measured using a home-built scanning microscope at room temperature. Samples were illuminated with 520-, 633-, or 690-nm lasers focused through a ×5 or ×40 objective. The beam was modulated using a mechanical chopper, and the photovoltage signal was detected via lock-in amplification (Stanford Research), with the R-component representing the photovoltage. Spatial mapping was achieved using a galvo mirror system, and polarization dependence was studied with a linear polarizer and a half-wave plate.

SAW measurement

SAWs were excited by a signal generator (Stanford Research) through conventional or focusing IDT pairs at their center frequencies. The center frequency of IDT pairs in device D1 and the focusing IDT is 311.91 and 289.00 MHz, respectively. The center frequency of SAW-X and SAW-Y in device D2 is 310.35 and 295.15 MHz, respectively. The acoustic power at the device is estimated to be 25% of the output value of the signal generator, which is presented in the Results and Discussion and the Supplementary Materials. The SAW amplitude and phase were characterized using a 600-MHz lock-in amplifier (Zurich Instruments). The transmission coefficient S21 was derived from source and transmitted voltage amplitudes. Temperature-dependent measurements were performed in a Physical Property Measurement System (Quantum Design).
