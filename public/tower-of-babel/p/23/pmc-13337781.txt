Clinical Utility of Positive End-expiratory Pressure-incorporated PaO2/FiO2 Ratio in Prognosticating Mortality and Severity Reclassification in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Vignesh Prabhu, Souvik Chaudhuri, Shwethapriya Rao, Pratibha Todur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPaO2/FiO2 (PF) ratio is globally used for defining and classifying acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), yet it does not take ventilatory support into consideration, which is crucial in achieving oxygenation. Incorporating the positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) into the PF ratio (PaO2/FiO2*PEEP; PFP ratio) may provide a more physiologically meaningful index of disease diagnosis, severity, and prognosis.ObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical utility of the PFP ratio in prognosticating mortality and its impact on reclassification of ARDS severity as compared to the traditional PF ratio.Materials and methodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) was performed following PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251110685). A search for relevant studies from inception to July 2025 was performed, and the studies on adults with ARDS receiving invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) and those that reported PFP ratio and mortality outcomes were included. Summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) model was used to evaluate the prognostic accuracy, while the reclassification of ARDS severity using PFP vs PF ratio was descriptively analyzed.ResultsA total of five studies with 4,454 patients were included for meta-analysis. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of the PFP ratio for prognosticating mortality were 0.55 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.41–0.67] and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.80–0.96), respectively. The area under the sROC was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.85), inferring good discriminant ability. For reclassification of ARDS severity based on PFP ratio, a total of four studies were included, and 30–71.9% of patients were reclassified, showing significant bidirectional shifts.ConclusionPositive end-expiratory pressure incorporation to the PF ratio improves the prognostic ability and leads to meaningful reclassification of ARDS severity.How to cite this articlePrabhu V, Chaudhuri S, Rao S, Todur P. Highlights

The positive end-expiratory pressure-incorporated PaO2/FiO2 (PFP) ratio gives a physiologically meaningful oxygenation index in ARDS, showing good discriminant ability for mortality prediction with high specificity. Replacing the conventional PF ratio with the PFP ratio resulted in significant bidirectional severity reclassification of ARDS, highlighting the limitations of the Berlin definition and important implications for prognostication and ventilator-related risk stratification.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a form of life-threatening hypoxemic respiratory failure often requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) support and is a significant contributor to mortality in the ICU.1,2 The past two decades have witnessed several advances in ventilator strategies, including lung-protective ventilation and other rescue measures; however, mortality in ARDS still remains high, highlighting the need for better stratification of disease severity and prognostic markers.3,4

The Berlin definition defines ARDS and classifies ARDS severity by using the ratio of partial pressure of oxygen in the arterial blood (PaO2) to the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), often termed the PF ratio, measured at a minimum positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) level.5 Due to its simplicity and ready availability at the bedside, the PF ratio is widely used in oxygenation assessment of a hypoxemic patient on IMV. However, this oxygenation index does not consider the ventilatory support that is required to achieve the desired oxygenation. Patients with similar PF ratios may vary in the extent of lung injury severity, their clinical trajectories, and different degrees of lung recruitability. As a result, using the PF ratio alone may lead to inappropriate diagnosis of the disease and misclassification of disease severity in ARDS.6

In the current IMV strategies of patients with ARDS, the ventilator variable PEEP plays a significant role in improving oxygenation. Positive end-expiratory pressure splints up the collapsed alveoli, maintains the functional residual volume of the lungs, aids in lung recruitment, and, as a result, reduces the shunt and improves gas exchange.7–9 During MV of ARDS, the requirement of higher levels of PEEP indicates that the lung impairment is more severe. Palanidurai et al. incorporated the ventilator variable PEEP into the conventional PF ratio and proposed a meaningful novel oxygenation index, PaO2/FiO2*PEEP (PFP ratio).6

In the recent past, a few studies have explored the utility of the PFP ratio in ARDS and have highlighted its predictive ability of outcomes and reclassification of ARDS severity when compared to the PF ratio.6,10–14 The findings across these individual studies varied in the study design, patient characteristics, time points of measurement and the assessment of the PFP ratio, and definition of the outcomes. Therefore, the utility of the PFP ratio in predicting clinical outcomes and severity stratification in ARDS remains uncertain due to the heterogeneity of the reported findings and needs a systematic synthesis of the available evidence.

This SRMA aims to evaluate the prognostic utility of the PFP ratio in predicting the clinical outcomes and assessing the ARDS disease severity using the PFP ratio instead of the PF ratio of Berlin's criteria. The primary objective is to evaluate the discriminative ability of the PFP ratio to predict mortality in patients with ARDS on IMV, and the secondary objective is to study the extent to which the PFP results in reclassification of ARDS severity categories.

Materials and Methods

The review protocol was predefined and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251110685), and for reporting findings, the PRISMA 2020 guideline was followed. The need for ethics approval was waived as the study nature was an SRMA.

Search Methods for Study Identification

A systematic search from inception to July 2025 was performed using four electronic databases – PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane by creating search strings using keywords and related mesh terms such as “P/FP ratio,” “PaO2,” “FiO2,” “PEEP,” “acute respiratory distress syndrome,” “ARDS,” “PaO2/FiO2,” “P/F ratio,” “positive end-expiratory pressure,” and “PEEP.” The duplicates were removed manually using an Excel sheet.

The studies were screened by two independent reviewers. The title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening for eligibility, were performed. In case of disagreement between the two reviewers, the third reviewer was involved. The reasons for exclusion criteria were documented throughout the study screening process.

Study Selection Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

The study population included patients with ARDS aged ≥18 years and receiving IMV; retrospective/prospective observational studies or randomized controlled trials, those published in the English language, and those that documented the mortality rate with the cutoff value of PFP ratio.

Exclusion Criteria

Review articles, conference abstracts, case reports, case series, those in a non-English language, pediatric population, studies that used noninvasive ventilation, studies on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) patients, studies that did not use PEEP or PFP ratio, or if the mortality outcomes were not reported.

Population: Patients with ARDS with age ≥ 18 years on IMV.

Index/prognostic tool: PFP ratio.

Outcome: Mortality.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessments

We used a predefined data extraction sheet that was created by the reviewers for the study characteristics and data extraction. The extracted information included as follows: Author name, year, country name, study design, ARDS [traditional/COVID-ARDS (CARDS)], study sample size, age, gender, ARDS definition, ARDS classification (mild, moderate, and severe ARDS) as per Berlin criteria, PFP (mean/median), PF ratio (mean/median), and outcomes such as mortality, length of stay (LOS) (ICU/hospital), days on MV. The sensitivity and specificity were collected from each study that reported them or were later extracted or calculated.

For the quality assessment/risk of bias (ROB), the quality in prognosis studies (QUIPS) tool was used.15 Two reviewers were involved in the assessment, and in the event of conflicts, a third reviewer was consulted. The ROB of the included study was graded as low, moderate, or high based on answering the questions mentioned in six domains of the tool.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Statistical software Stata (StataCorp 2025, Stata statistical software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC) was used for the analysis. After the data were collected from the studies, we performed a bivariate random-effect model analysis to pool the sensitivity and specificity data, assuming a moderate level of heterogeneity among the studies.

Given the heterogeneous cutoff levels of PFP ratio predicting mortality and variability across the different classifications of ARDS, a cutoff-independent approach to data synthesis was followed by using a summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) model. When the threshold value varies across the included studies, the sROC model is recommended and is consistent with the methodological guidelines from the PRISMA-DTA and Cochrane Diagnostic Test Accuracy working group.16,17

As most studies lacked 2 × 2 contingency data, the true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative were back-calculated from reported sensitivity, specificity, total sample size, and event rate. These were used to derive the study-level sensitivity and specificity, which were pooled using a bivariate random-effect model (Reitsma method) in Stata to generate the sROC.

The prognostic accuracy was analyzed using the sROC model, which calculates the area under the curve (AUC) and obtains a summary point estimate of sensitivity and specificity. The ability of the PFP ratio to prognosticate mortality using AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity was used to plot the sROC. The AUC of the sROC curve reflects the summary measure, using all the reported cutoffs instead of a single cutoff value. The bivariate random-effect model (Reitsma method) was used to calculate joint pooled sensitivity and specificity across studies and, therefore, to construct the sROC. Heterogeneity was assessed using the variance components of the bivariate model and by visual inspection of the sROC plot, including the 95% confidence interval (CI) and prediction regions.

In sROC, each point represents an individual study. The red line represents the sROC curve, and the black diamond indicates the pooled summary point. The dashed ellipse depicts the 95% CI region around the summary estimate, and the dotted ellipse represents the 95% prediction region. Each study is represented by a point corresponding to its sensitivity and 1 – specificity. The red sROC curve represents the overall diagnostic performance of the index test, while the black diamond denotes the pooled summary estimate. The 95% CI region around the summary point reflects the precision of the pooled estimates, whereas the broader prediction region illustrates expected variability in sensitivity and specificity if the test were applied in future similar populations.

For the secondary objective of the study, an evaluation of the changes in ARDS severity classification when the PFP ratio was used instead of the PF ratio, the studies reporting the ARDS severity categories based on both the PFP and the PF ratios were included. The data related to the severity classification, PFP threshold values, and the number or the proportion (n%) of patients that were reclassified to a different severity category were extracted. Reclassification means any shift in the ARDS severity category (to a lower or higher severity category) on application of the PFP ratio. Due to the reason of heterogeneity in the study design, assessment timepoints, cutoff selection, and whether the values were reported at the patient or measurement level, the quantitative pooled analysis was not performed. The result of this outcome is described in a summary table.

Results

A total of 24,661 articles were identified using an electronic search database. After excluding the duplicates, a total of 19,454 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts. In the process, 7,462 articles remained for the full-text screening. Predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were thoroughly applied, and finally, six studies involving patients with ARDS with PFP ratio as a predictor of mortality were included. Details of the search and study selection process are displayed as a PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). Of these, one study reported mortality as a hazard ratio, and therefore, five studies were included in the meta-analysis.6,10–13

Characteristics of Included Studies

Among the five included studies, four were retrospective cohort, and one was a prospective study design published between 2021 and 2024, cumulatively total sample of 4,454 patients with ARDS on MV.6,10–13 The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. The age of study participants ranged between 39 and 73 years, with a mean age of 61.5 ± 13.9 years. The study had a predominantly male population, comprising over 50% across the included studies. The definition for ARDS was the Berlin definition in four studies, and one study incorporated the American-European Consensus (AECC) definition of ARDS. With respect to the outcomes, all five studies reported mortality as the primary outcome; two studies did not report the time point of mortality, and two reported 28-day mortality, and the other reported ICU and hospital mortality.6,10–13 Length of MV days was reported in four studies, and length of ICU/hospital stay was reported in three studies, as depicted in Table 1. The timing of measurement of PFP and PF ratio varied among the studies; some were day 1/day 3, within 24 hours of initiation of IMV, after 24 hours, indicating a lack of uniform assessment of time points. A total of five studies with 4,454 patients were included for the primary meta-analysis. Of these, only four studies reported data on ARDS severity reclassification and were included in the secondary analysis.

ROB Assessment

Risk of bias of the included studies was assessed by prior mentioned reviewers using the QUIPS tool, and three studies had low, and two had moderate ROB. Risk of bias of all the included studies is shown in the traffic light plot in Figure 2.

Traffic light plot showing the ROB in the included studies

The ROB assessment was done using the QUIPS ROB tool across six domains: Study participation, study attrition, prognostic factor assessment, outcome assessment, handling confounding variables, statistical analysis, and reporting. Overall, the methodological quality was good, with most studies demonstrating a low ROB across the domains.

Three studies were listed as low risk, indicating robust study design, reliable measurement, and appropriate statistical analysis. However, two studies showed a moderate ROB in study participation, prognostic factor measurement, and confounding domains.

Confounding was the most affected domain, which reflects inadequate adjustment for important clinical variables. Despite these limitations, the overall ROB was considered acceptable, and the included studies provide reasonably reliable evidence, although the influence of confounding cannot be excluded.

Primary Outcome: Clinical Utility of PFP in Prognosticating Mortality in Patients with ARDS

Variable PFP cutoffs across studies supported the use of a threshold-independent sROC approach. To investigate the primary outcome of the study, the prognostic ability of the PFP ratio in predicting mortality in patients with ARDS, a sROC curve was plotted as depicted in Figure 3. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of the PFP ratio in predicting mortality were 0.55 (95% CI: 0.41–0.67) and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.80–0.96), respectively. The relatively low sensitivity indicates that a proportion of non-survivors may not be identified by PFP alone. On the sROC plot, the summary point is positioned toward the upper left area, suggesting that the PFP has high specificity but moderate sensitivity to prognosticating mortality. The relatively narrow confidence ellipse indicates acceptable precision of the pooled estimates. The moderately wider prediction ellipse suggests some between-study heterogeneity in prognostic performance across studies. The AUC of the sROC is 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.85), indicating good overall discriminative ability of PFP in predicting mortality. The diagnostic odds ratio of PFP was 11.1 (95% CI: 6.0–20.2), the positive likelihood ratio was 5.5 (95% CI: 3.0–9.8), and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.50 (95% CI: 0.35–0.72).

Secondary Outcome: Reclassification of ARDS Severity Categories Based on PFP Ratio

Only four studies reported the changes in severity classification of patients with ARDS by replacing the PFP ratio with the traditional PF ratio.6,10–12 The findings of these studies are described in Table 2. The use of the PFP ratio led to notable bidirectional reclassification of ARDS severity across the studies. A study by Palanidurai et al., having the largest cohort of patients with ARDS, noted that 30% of the patients were reclassified using PFP with an identical cutoff of severity as the PF ratio.6 The reclassification was bidirectional with shifts from moderate to severe category, mild to moderate/severe categories, and also a shift from severe/moderate to less severe categories.

Another study showed more extensive reclassification of 71.9% of patients reclassified to different severities of ARDS.11 Using the PFP ratio, more patients were reclassified to more severe categories as compared to the PF ratio, and only a small proportion shifted from higher severity to lower. A study by Martos-Benítez et al. used time-dependent reclassification measured on day 1 and day 3.12 On day 1, 31.8% of patients with ARDS were reclassified, whereas on day 3, 77% of patients were reclassified. This study also noted that the reclassification shifted the less severe to the severe categories. Contrastingly, a study by Vadi et al., which analyzed repeated measurements, showed that reclassification occurred in 34% of measurements.10 This study predominantly moved to less severe categories.

The descriptive findings in Table 2 indicated that the PEEP-incorporated PF ratio alters the ARDS severity classification significantly. Across the studies, there is significant variability in the magnitude and direction of reclassification due to the heterogeneity of the patient population, timepoints of assessment, and varied directions.

Heterogeneity Based on the sROC Curve

In this study, we assessed between-study heterogeneity using the bivariate random-effect sROC model. In this model, the traditional univariate heterogenicity such as I2 is not reported due to the reason that the sensitivity and specificity are modeled together. In such cases, instead of I2, the heterogeneity is inferred through visual inspection of the spread of individual study estimates and by comparing the relative size of the 95% prediction region with that of the confidence region. In the sROC plot of our analysis, the prediction region is significantly wider than the corresponding confidence region. This implies moderate to considerable heterogeneity in the prognostic determination ability of the PFP ratio. The narrow confidence region around the pooled estimate indicates acceptable precision in average sensitivity and specificity. Contrarily, the broad prediction region infers that the prognostic performance of PFP may vary when applied to different ARDS populations. The heterogeneity is also evident from the dispersion of individual study points along the sROC curve. The wide dispersion of sensitivity and specificity around the summary point indicates between-study heterogeneity rather than random error. Due to diverse practices, such variability is expected in the research methodology of ARDS study participants.

Several factors may contribute to this heterogeneity, including, first, the heterogeneity in cutoff values of the PFP ratio. The studies included in the SRMA used different PFP threshold values to predict mortality risk. These differences in threshold sensitivity and specificity trade-off along the sROC plot and also justify the use of cutoff-independent sROC analysis. Second, the clinical heterogeneity existed, as the included studies had different ARDS types (traditional ARDS vs COVID-19 ARDS), as well as variability in severity of disease and ventilator management strategies, including the PEEP level used at the time of PFP measurement, PEEP level reflects both disease severity and physician decision, and therefore likely to influence the PFP ratio and prognostic meaning. Third, there was heterogeneity in the timing of assessment. The measurement of the PFP ratio varied across the included studies (early vs late measurement) and also sometimes included repeated measurements. Acute respiratory distress syndrome is a dynamic syndrome, and changing ventilator strategies over time may result in considerable variability. Fourth is the heterogeneity in outcome definition; the mortality outcome in studies was not uniform, and some defined it at 28 days, others at ICU or in-hospital mortality, leading to heterogeneity of prognostic accuracy.

Discussion

This SRMA evaluated the clinical utility of incorporating PEEP into the traditional PF ratio, i.e, PFP ratio, for prognosticating mortality and classification of ARDS severity levels in patients with ARDS receiving IMV. The findings of this review suggest that the PFP had a good overall discriminative ability to predict mortality. The application of PFP resulted in a substantial reclassification of the severity category of patients with ARDS compared with the PF ratio alone. These findings imply that the oxygenation assessment must not be done in isolation and depends highly on the ventilatory support used to achieve it.5,18

Prognostic Ability of the PFP Ratio in ARDS

Pooled diagnostic performance of the PFP ratio showed good discriminant ability of the ratio to predict mortality with high specificity and moderate sensitivity, with a 0.84 AUC under the sROC curve. This implies that a lower value of the PFP ratio reliably identifies the high-risk patients with poor outcomes, as indicated by its high specificity, whereas its moderate to low sensitivity implies that this ratio may not be able to identify the proportion of non-survivors. Hence, this limits the utility of PFP as an independent marker of mortality and should be used in conjunction with other ventilatory and clinical parameters.9,19–21 These findings also suggest that the PFP ratio can be utilized as a prognostic enrichment tool, as it can identify patients with physiological derangements.

To summarize, the SROC analysis demonstrates a modulation between-study heterogeneity, as shown by the broad prediction region and dispersed study estimates, and the overall robustness of the pooled discriminant capacity of PFP. It highlights the need for prospective studies to standardize the PFP ratio measurement cutoff values and measurement timepoints.

A prior SRMA performed by Yoshimura et al. to evaluate the prognostic capacity of PF ratio in predicting mortality showed limited discriminant ability of the PF ratio with the AUC of sROC of 0.5.22 These findings suggest that PFP may offer additional physiological insight compared to PF ratio; however, this was based on indirect comparisons and requires validation through direct comparative studies. This is due to the physiological role of PEEP in enhancing oxygenation of a hypoxemic patient. Appropriate PEEP levels enhance the end-expiratory volumes, prevent alveolar collapse, reduce alveolar shunt, and improve the ventilation-perfusion ratio.7,8 In ARDS, similar PF ratios may be achieved with different PEEP levels, and the higher PEEP levels reflect more severe lung disease and lower lung compliance. This fact is masked in the conventional PF ratio and therefore limits its use in prognosticating adverse outcomes and explains the better prognostic ability of PFP across the included studies.

Reclassification of ARDS Severity Categories Using the PFP Ratio

An important secondary finding of this study is the extent of the ARDS severity reclassification by using the PFP ratio instead of the PF ratio. Across the studies included, reclassification affected a considerable proportion of patients with ARDS, approximately one-third to nearly three-quarters, with the shifts both in upward and downward directions. Some of the studies that predominantly had patients with higher levels of PEEP shifted the classification toward higher severity levels. Also, some studies reported reclassification to less severe levels when repeated measurements were analyzed.6,10–12 The bidirectional magnitude of changes during reclassification reinforces that ARDS is a dynamic syndrome and highlights the importance of timing, ventilatory parameters, and rescue strategies deployed in treatment and the analytic approach to the assessment of severity.

Clinically, these findings point out the shortcomings of the Berlin definition, which relies on fixed PF ratio levels that do not consider significant ventilator variables like PEEP levels.6,20,23 Subsequently, this miscalculation of ARDS severity category has manyfold implications on clinical care and research, such as inaccurate prognostication, improper stratification of risk for therapeutic interventions, and heterogeneity within the severity-defined cohorts.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this SRMA include that the study has been registered protocol in PROSPERO and followed the PRISMA guidelines for reporting. This is the first SRMA that studied the prognostic utility of the novel PFP ratio in predicting important outcomes of mortality and the effect of the PFP ratio in reclassification of ARDS severity. The review used diagnostic meta-analysis using the sROC model due to the heterogeneity in cutoff values.

The limitations include that most of the included studies were retrospective or secondary analyses of different studies. The limited and small number of studies (n = 5) restricts the statistical power of the analysis. The study had heterogeneity in terms of patient characteristics, threshold values, variability in timing of PFP assessment, levels of PEEP used, and the endpoints of mortality. The variability in ARDS definitions (AECC and Berlin definition) was not specifically accounted for in the analysis, which may have contributed to clinical heterogeneity. The patient positioning at the time of PFP measurement was not reported and may have led to the heterogeneity in oxygenation indices. The review could not find the cutoff values of the PFP ratio, and hence limiting it from immediate clinical applications. The absence of a direct comparison between PFP and PF ratio limits definitive conclusions regarding superiority.

This SRMA included only patients with ARDS on IMV, limiting the generalizability to patients with ARDS who are non-ventilated. As PFP-incorporated PEEP, its interpretation is applicable only to patients receiving MV.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this review infer that the PEEP-incorporated PF ratio, PFP, provides a clinically relevant prognosis as compared to the PF ratio alone. Positive end-expiratory pressure-incorporated PaO2/FiO2 ratio is a simple yet physiologically effective refinement of the oxygenation index that can be used in hypoxic patient on IMV. The variables PaO2, FiO2, and PEEP are readily available at the bedside, and therefore, the calculation of the PFP ratio does not add to the cost or complexity. In this review, the PFP ratio had high specificity, which indicates that the patients with low PFP (more disease severity) represent the high-risk category who had physiological compromise and would benefit from closer monitoring and early escalation of rescue treatment, which can improve the outcomes of those subgroups.6,12,24 Positive end-expiratory pressure-incorporated PaO2/FiO2 ratio can improve risk stratification and thereby lead to more homogeneity within the subgroup of patients.

However, until the threshold values are standardized and the prospective validations are available from multicenter trials, at present, the PFP ratio should be used as a complementary tool in assessing the oxygenation status, risk stratification, or to prognosticate outcomes. Positive end-expiratory pressure-incorporated PaO2/FiO2 ratio is a physiologically accurate clinical index that has the ability to correctly identify the severity of ARDS and determine the future management strategies, such as prone position, ECMO consideration, and trial inclusion.

Future Directives

Using standard ICU and ventilator protocols, future research should aim at prospective validation of the PFP ratio with predefined timepoints of measurements, as well as assessment of outcomes. Consensus threshold values for PFP must be established to facilitate the severity classification of patients with ARDS. Future studies should focus on determining whether the ARDS severity classification based on the PFP ratio is more associated with clinical outcomes and treatment than the conventional PF ratio. Addressing these concerns will add to the role of the PFP ratio in improved decision making, its clinical benefits, and advancing the precision-based care of patients with ARDS.

Conclusion

The PFP ratio, a simple bedside-available oxygenation index, gives a meaningful assessment of oxygenation status in patients with ARDS on IMV. The prognostic ability of the PFP ratio reflects the influence of ventilator support on oxygenation and may aid in reclassification of ARDS severity. Although promising, the novel PFP ratio should currently be used as a complementary tool until future studies confirm the findings.
