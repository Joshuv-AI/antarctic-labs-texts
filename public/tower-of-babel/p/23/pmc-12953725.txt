Mortality and Associated Factors Among Adult Tetanus Patients Admitted at Public Hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mulugeta Tesfaw, Hailemariam Awoke Engedaw, Teshome Alene, Zemenu Shiferaw Yadita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTetanus, caused by Clostridium tetani, results in rigor spasms and remains as major cause of mortality in areas with poor vaccination and sanitation. Mortality has been cited to be 20%–55% in Ethiopia, possibly due to variation in healthcare, immunization, and intensive care access. However, there is limited data at the local level. This study was conducted to determine Tetanus mortality and identify determinants among adults in the Bahir Dar public hospitals.MethodsA multicenter, hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted using medical records of 251 patients admitted between January 1, 2019, and December 2024. Data were collected using Kobo Collect software and entered into EpiData 4.6, where they were cleaned and checked for completeness. Analysis was performed using SPSS version 27. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify significant factors. A p‐value of < 0.05 in the multivariable model was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mortality rate for tetanus was 32.27% (95% CI: 26.75%, 38.33%). Several factors were significantly associated with tetanus mortality, including coexisting diseases (AOR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.65, 6.30), onset of symptoms within 2 days (AOR = 4.10; 95% CI: 1.84, 8.16), generalized tetanus type (AOR = 6.5; 95% CI: 2.05, 11.42), failure to receive tetanus antitoxin (AOR = 2.51; 95% CI: 1.04, 10.43), and undergoing a tracheostomy procedure (AOR = 2.3; 95% CI: 2.3 (1.32, 5.81).ConclusionsThe mortality rate among tetanus patients was found to be high. Key factors include coexisting diseases, acute onset (within 2 days), generalized tetanus, failure to administer Tetanus Antitoxin, and the performance of tracheostomy. Hence, the study highlights the importance of early diagnosis and treatment, advocating for the use of antitoxin, providing special care for patients with generalized tetanus, and reconsidering the effectiveness of tracheostomy as a treatment option.Keywords: Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, factors, mortality, tetanus

Introduction

Tetanus is a life‐threatening condition that results from the neurotoxin tetanospasmin produced by Clostridium tetani. The infection results in severe muscle rigidity and spasms [1]. It can be presented as localized, cephalic, or generalized tetanus. Localized tetanus is very rare, with rigidity confined to the wound. Cephalic tetanus presents with paralysis of the face on one side and overactivity on the opposite side. Generalized tetanus is the most severe form and accounts for approximately 95% of cases. It usually warrants hospitalization. The symptoms include lockjaw, Risus sardonicus, diffuse muscle rigidity, and spasms that may be triggered by such stimuli as noise or light. Severe cases will cause autonomic dysfunction, which may present as tachycardia, hypertension, fluctuating blood pressure, excessive sweating, increased secretions of the respiratory tract, and gastrointestinal disturbances [2]. Severity is gauged by using the Ablett score: grades 1–2‐mild to moderate disease; grades 3–4‐severe disease; grade 4 demonstrates autonomic dysfunction [3]. Management includes tetanus immunoglobulin, wound debridement, antibiotics, and symptom control with benzodiazepines and neuromuscular blockers [1]. Despite these treatments, mortality remains high (20% to 55%) in Ethiopia, as well as other low‐income countries, and there is a desperate need for better management and further research [4, 5, 6].

Tetanus is yet one of the major public health burdens, especially in resource‐poor settings. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease study, incomplete vaccination, limited access to healthcare, and socioeconomic barriers, in addition to delays in diagnosis and low awareness in areas like Sub‐Saharan Africa and South Asia, lead to high mortality rates [7]. Agricultural activities in addition to livelihoods, hot climate, poor wound care, and low vaccination coverage, are some contributory factors in Africa [8]. These determinants are not well understood, hence calling for more research to reduce mortality. Despite deaths from tetanus declining from 275,000 in 1990 to 35,000 in 2019, mortalities are still high [9]. Laryngeal spasm leading to respiratory failure is the most common cause of death among unventilated patients, and autonomic dysfunction predominates in ventilated patients [9]. Early respiratory intervention, along with the management of autonomic instability, could manage mortalities effectively. There is variation in mortality by the Socio‐Demographic Index, with high mortality in low SDI regions [7].

Tetanus mortality is influenced by a range of interdependent factors. Hospital studies such as those conducted at Black Lion and Felege‐Hiwot Referral Hospital have shown that dysautonomia, laryngospasm‐induced respiratory arrest, and respiratory failure are leading causes of immediate death. These are commonly linked to disease severity and can be prevented with adequate medication, mechanical support of the respiratory system, and, in resource‐constrained settings, through the restriction of external stimulation (e.g., dark and quiet rooms). Other risk factors include advanced age, shortened incubation periods, male gender, and secondary infections such as sepsis and aspiration pneumonia. Despite improvements in vaccine coverage, tetanus remains a major cause of death throughout much of the low‐resource world, particularly where access to care is limited and there is conflict, such as in Northwest Ethiopia [6, 8, 10].

These all highlight the necessity for more localized data on in‐hospital mortality and determinants. To plug this gap, the present study has sought to assess the magnitude of mortality and its determinants in adult tetanus patients presenting to the public hospitals of Bahir Dar City, Amhara Region, in 2024. Findings of the present study are expected to guide improved management practices, enhance patient outcomes, and support alleviation of the tetanus burden in Ethiopia and similar settings across the globe.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted among adult tetanus patients admitted to the public referral hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Amhara Region, Ethiopia, in 2024. The study took place at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH) and Felege Hiwot Specialized Hospital (FHSH), using medical records from January 1, 2019, to December 2024. Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital, opened on November 10, 2018, is a key healthcare facility with over 450 beds, serving a population of 5–7 million. It offers various clinical specialties and is owned by Bahir Dar University. Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital, established in 2011, is a major tertiary healthcare provider with a 600‐bed capacity, serving both Bahir Dar and remote areas of Northwest Ethiopia. It is affiliated with the Amhara Regional Health Bureau.

Population and Sample Size

The source population for this study consisted of all adult patients diagnosed with tetanus and admitted to the public referral hospitals in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia. The study population included adult patients (aged 15 and above) diagnosed with tetanus and admitted to these hospitals from January 2019 to December 2024.

To determine the sample size, both single and double population proportion formulas were applied. The maximum sample size was calculated to be 338, based on a previous study reporting a tetanus mortality rate of 32.7% [10], with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error.

Over the 5 years, a total of 336 tetanus patients were admitted to the two hospitals. However, 85 patients were excluded due to incomplete medical records, leaving against medical advice, or being transferred to other facilities. Consequently, 251 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study.

Variables and Measurement

The outcome variable for this study was tetanus mortality (YES/NO), while the independent variables included socio‐demographic factors such as age, sex, education, marital status, occupation, monthly income, and place of residence; disease‐related conditions, including the type and severity of tetanus, incubation period, onset time, complications, and treatment received; and comorbid conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiac diseases, and renal disease.

Tetanus Mortality is defined as the outcome of a patient's hospitalization due to tetanus, categorized as follows: 1) YES – the patient died during the hospitalization period due to complications related to tetanus, and 2) NO – the patient survived the hospitalization and was discharged without a fatal outcome attributed to tetanus [11].

Tetanus Severity is determined by assessing symptoms such as muscle rigidity, spasm, and their impact on the patient's health. The Ablett score is a clinical tool used to assess the severity of tetanus, guide treatment decisions, and predict patient outcomes. It categorizes the disease based on the severity of muscle rigidity, spasms, and other clinical features [12].

Data Collection Tools and Procedures

Tetanus‐related data, along with information on associated illnesses, comorbidities, and socio‐demographic details, were collected from patients' medical records using a structured checklist, which involved the use of secondary data. The data collection process was carried out by two well‐trained BSC nurses, who utilized KoboCollect software on smartphones. This process was conducted over approximately 21 working days. The data collectors, proficient in reading and writing in English, were familiar with the checklist, so there was no need to translate the tool into the local language.

Under the supervision of an internist, the trained data collectors followed the established protocol. A 1‐day training session was provided to familiarize the data collectors with the procedure for extracting the necessary information and inputting it into the KoboToolbox software. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample size, prior to the official data collection to ensure consistency and resolve any ambiguities in the checklist. The process was closely supervised by the research team. Additionally, content validity was reviewed and confirmed by senior internists with expertise in the field. To enhance reliability, inter‐rater consistency was also assessed during the pretest. Daily data monitoring was carried out to assess the completeness and accuracy of the collected data.

Data Processing and Analysis

The investigators conducted daily checks to ensure the completeness of the data. The collected data were then exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 for analysis. Descriptive analysis was performed, including the computation of means, standard deviations, medians, frequencies, and percentages to summarize the data. To identify factors associated with tetanus mortality, a Binary Logistic Regression Model was applied, involving both bivariable and multivariable analyses. p‐values and odds ratios were used to assess the significance of associations. Factors with a p‐value < 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) in the multivariable analysis, along with their corresponding odds ratios, were considered statistically significant. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test yielded a significance level of 0.451, while the omnibus test of model coefficients showed significance at 0.01, indicating a good model fit.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval and clearance were granted by the Ethical Review Board (ERB) of Bahir Dar University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Protocol Number 3063/2024). As the study involved the use of retrospective medical record data without direct contact with patients, individual informed consent was not required. However, written permission to access and use the patients' medical records was obtained from the respective hospital administrations. Formal letters were also sent to participating public referral hospitals to ensure institutional transparency and compliance. All data handling procedures adhered strictly to national and institutional ethical guidelines to ensure participant confidentiality and data protection. Collected data were anonymized and securely stored on password‐protected devices, accessible only to the research team.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

Of the 251 tetanus patients analyzed, the majority were male (229 cases, 91.23%), with 74 male deaths (29.48%). The average age of patients was 42.75 years (SD = 1.06; 95% CI: 40.66–44.84). Most patients (209, 83.27%) were from rural areas, where 69 deaths (27.49%) occurred. Regarding the time of onset, 205 patients (81.67%) presented with symptoms two or more days after onset, among whom 48 (19.12%) died. In contrast, only 46 patients (18.33%) presented within 2 days, but they had a higher proportion of deaths (33 cases, 13.15%) (Table 1).

Clinical‐Related Characteristics

Of the total patients, 71 (28.28%) had preexisting medical conditions, with hypertension being the most common, affecting 36 patients (14.40%). Ischemic heart disease was the second most frequent comorbidity, found in six patients (2.40%). Regarding smoking history, 160 patients (63.75%) have unknown smoking status.

The primary cause of tetanus in this study was post‐traumatic, accounting for 185 cases (73.71%). Trismus was the most common manifestation, present in 210 patients (83.67%). Dysphagia was observed in over a quarter of patients, affecting 117 (46.61%). More than half of the patients (137, 54.58%) exhibited muscle stiffness, and 221 (88.05%) experienced muscle spasms during their hospital stay.

Fever was reported by 45 patients (17.93%) during hospitalization, while 37 patients (14.75%) had urinary retention. Regarding vaccination status, 103 patients (41.04%) had an unknown vaccination history, and 148 patients (58.96%) had not received the tetanus vaccine. However, 106 patients (42.23%) received the tetanus vaccine following sustained trauma (Table 2).

Management of Tetanus Patients

In this study, 56 patients (30.43%) received wound care upon admission, and 55 patients (21.91%) were treated with beta‐blockers during their hospital stay. Tetanus antitoxin was administered to most patients (230; 91.63%), and tracheostomy was performed in 27 cases (10.76%). Most patients (239; 95.22%) received a combination of muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and diazepam. Additional treatment regimens included vecuronium, analgesics, and antibiotics in 39 patients (15.4%); muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and chlorpromazine in 171 patients (68.13%); and muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and magnesium sulfate in 135 patients (53.78%). Similarly, 171 patients (68.13%) received muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and morphine, while 112 patients (44.62%) were treated with muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and wound care. The most frequently used combination, administered to 240 patients (95.62%), included muscle relaxants, analgesics, antibiotics, and metronidazole. The overall in‐hospital mortality rate during the study period was 32.27% (81 patients), while 170 patients (67.73%) survived.

Factors Associated With Tetanus Mortality

Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with mortality among adult tetanus patients. In the bivariable analysis, a range of variables were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model, including sex, residence, wound location, trismus, dysphagia, muscle stiffness, vaccination status (both general and post‐trauma), urinary retention, beta‐blocker use, tracheostomy, coexisting diseases, smoking status, cause of tetanus, wound care, onset time, spasms, fever, type of tetanus, renal failure, medications received, administration of tetanus antitoxin and tetanus immunoglobulin (TIG), mechanical ventilation, aspiration pneumonia, dysautonomia, and other complications.

In the multivariable analysis, six factors were found to be significantly associated with mortality: coexisting disease, onset time, type of tetanus, administration of tetanus antitoxin, administration TIG, and tracheostomy. Patients with coexisting diseases had 2.12 times higher odds of mortality compared to those without (AOR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.65, 6.30). Patients with an onset time of less than 2 days had a markedly higher risk of death, 4.10 times greater, compared to those with a longer onset time (AOR = 4.10; 95% CI: 1.84, 8.16). Generalized tetanus was associated with a 6.5‐fold increase in mortality risk compared to other forms (AOR = 6.5; 95% CI: 2.05, 11.42). Patients who did not receive tetanus antitoxin were 2.51 times more likely to die than those who received it (AOR = 2.51; 95% CI: 1.04, 10.43). Regarding TIG, those patients who did not receive TIG were 1.4 times high likely to die than their counterparts (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.01, 3.67). Finally, undergoing a tracheostomy was linked to a 2.3‐fold increase in mortality risk compared to those who did not have the procedure (AOR = 2.3; 95% CI: 2.3 (1.32, 5.81) (Table 4).

Discussion

This retrospective cross‐sectional study assessed the incidence and determinants of mortality among tetanus patients admitted to Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital and Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital in Ethiopia. The study revealed a high mortality rate, with significant risk factors including coexisting illnesses, shorter onset time, generalized tetanus, absence of tetanus antitoxin, and tracheostomy. These findings provide crucial baseline data to support clinical decision‐making in resource‐limited settings.

The overall mortality rate was 32.27% (95% CI: 26.75%–38.33%), aligning with previous studies from the Philippines (29%) [17], Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital (32.7%) [10], and Tikur Anbessa Hospital (35.3%) [5]. However, this rate was higher than those reported in Slovenia (12.9%) [1], Europe (14%) [18], and Somalia (14.3%) [19], likely due to differences in sample size, duration of hospitalization, healthcare infrastructure, and patient characteristics. Conversely, the rate was lower than in Sudan (48.4%) [20], Turkey (48.5%) [4], Cameroon (48.8%) [13], and Uganda (52.8%) [21], where higher mortality may be linked to older patient populations and greater comorbidity burden.

Coexisting illnesses significantly increased mortality risk, corroborating findings from Bangladesh [6], and Pakistan [22]. Comorbidities such as diabetes, cardiovascular, and respiratory diseases may impair immune response and exacerbate tetanus complications, leading to poorer outcomes [23].

A shorter onset time (< 2 days) was also linked to higher mortality, consistent with findings from Turkey [4]. Rapid disease progression often indicates severe tetanus with complications such as respiratory or cardiac failure [1]. Similarly, earlier onset of generalized spasms or trismus (within 8 days) was associated with increased mortality, supporting data from Nigeria [24]. Fawibe et al. further noted that severe, prolonged spasms are more likely to cause cardiorespiratory complications and death [25].

Generalized tetanus was associated with higher mortality, as reported in Turkey [4]. This may result from severe complications such as autonomic dysfunction and respiratory failure [8]. However, a study in Pakistan found higher mortality in cephalic tetanus [26], possibly due to the involvement of cranial nerves and rapid neurological deterioration [27].

Patients who did not receive tetanus antitoxin and TIG faced a higher risk of death, consistent with U.S. data [28]. The antitoxin neutralizes tetanospasmin, the toxin responsible for clinical manifestations of tetanus, and its absence increases the risk of fatal complications [29].

Tracheostomy was also associated with increased mortality, as observed in an Ethiopian study [30]. This may be due to complications such as infections, respiratory failure, or cardiac arrest [24]. In addition, tracheostomy was mainly performed in patients with severe generalized tetanus and respiratory compromise. Its association with higher mortality likely reflects disease severity rather than a causal effect, as these patients inherently had a higher baseline risk of death. However, U.S. studies have reported reduced mortality with early tracheostomy [31], possibly due to better airway management, shorter mechanical ventilation duration, and lower incidence of ventilator‐associated pneumonia [32]. Similarly, Wang et al. in China emphasized that early tracheostomy improves airway control, reduces lung infections, and allows for better sedation management [33].

Conclusions and Recommendations

This retrospective cross‐sectional study reveals a high mortality rate among tetanus patients admitted to two referral hospitals in Bahir Dar city. Several key risk factors for mortality were identified, including coexisting diseases, the timing of onset, the type of tetanus, the administration of tetanus antitoxin, and the need for tracheostomy. Based on these findings, the study recommends the importance of early diagnosis and prompt treatment, particularly ensuring timely administration of tetanus antitoxin. Special attention should be given to patients with generalized tetanus and those with comorbidities through intensified monitoring and comprehensive care. Additionally, the role of tracheostomy in tetanus management should be critically reassessed to ensure it contributes positively to patient outcomes. Additionally, the study recommends further longitudinal research using primary data to gain a deeper understanding of the factors affecting tetanus outcomes and to refine treatment strategies.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into tetanus mortality and is the first of its kind conducted at the study hospitals. By including two major referral and specialized hospitals, the study enhances its representativeness. It offers baseline data that can inform the development of targeted tetanus control strategies in the region and guide future research.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although a retrospective cohort design with survival analysis would be methodologically preferable, this was not feasible due to incomplete documentation of time‐to‐event variables in the hospital records. Consequently, Cox proportional hazards regression could not be performed, and mortality was analyzed as a binary outcome. The reliance on secondary medical records may have omitted important variables, such as social, economic, and behavioral factors, which could have expanded the analysis. Additionally, due to the rarity of some variables and the smaller sample size, some adjusted odds ratios (AOR) had wide confidence intervals, which may affect the precision of the findings.
