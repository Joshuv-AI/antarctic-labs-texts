Impact of climate change on water-related physical events, consequent human migration, and burden of drowning in India: An evidence synthesis
Authors: Deepti Beri, Jane Elkington, Sandeep Moola, Soumyadeep Bhaumik, Jagnoor Jagnoor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Disrupted weather patterns are associated with climate change. Between 2001 and 2018, nearly 74% of disasters were water-related, including floods and cyclones. Such water-related cataclysmic events increase the risk of drowning. We aimed to map evidence on the impact of climate change on water-related physical events, associated human migration, and drowning burden in India.Materials and Methods:We searched electronic databases, government reports, and relevant websites to map evidence on water-related physical events (including but not limited to sea-level rise, glacier bursts, lake bursts, floods, rainfall, cyclones, and droughts) and consequent human migration using narrative review approach, while drowning burden through scoping review approach. We summarized the results narratively.Results:Evidence from 48 studies and seven reports suggest that India will witness the greatest sea-level rise, significantly impacting poor coastal communities. An increase in droughts, cyclonic rainfall, storms, and floods, with increasing surface rainwater and streamflow water, due to melting glaciers is expected. Climate change–triggered migration is expected notably in northeast and south India, making farmers, drivers, street vendors, women, and youth most vulnerable. No direct evidence was identified on the impact of climate change, water-related disasters, meteorological events, or seasonal variations on drowning from India.Conclusion:Our study highlights a significant gap in the availability of context-specific and localized data to improve disaster response and strengthen public health systems, especially for areas most vulnerable to climate change. There is an urgent need to generate new knowledge and understanding of climate change, water-related or meteorological events, and seasonal variations’ impact on drowning burden as the level of risk remains unknown.

Introduction

The health and wellbeing of humans are intricately related to their environment. According to Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report, global warming will lead to an increase in temperature beyond 1.5°C between 2030 and 2052.[1] This will have a detrimental impact on our environment, with increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and disasters.[2] Increased warming also amplifies the exposure of small islands, low-lying coastal areas, and deltas to the risks associated with sea-level rise (SLR) for many human and ecological systems.[1]

Water-related events are the most dominant form of disasters.[3] Between 2001 and 2018, nearly 3,00,000 people died due to more than 5,000 water-related disasters, accounting for 73.9% of all natural disasters.[4] Floods, cyclones, hurricanes, typhoons, and storms accounted for displacements of about 23 million people globally in 2018.[5] These events also increase human interaction with water and subsequent drownings in an already vulnerable population.

Drowning is a public health problem responsible for an estimated 2,36,000 annual deaths globally with up to 48,774 of these deaths happening only in India every year.[6] However, these numbers are underestimated as these exclude drowning deaths due to flooding and other cataclysmic events. People from poor and rural communities carry a disproportionate burden of drowning. This is mainly due to high exposure to open water bodies with minimal safety and supervision, inadequate housing, weak health system infrastructure and primary care, and deficient warning and evacuation systems during water-related disasters.[789]

India is ranked the seventh worst-hit country in the world in 2019, in terms of fatalities and economic losses due to extreme weather events and disasters.[10] Between 1901 and 2018, India’s average temperature has risen by around 0.7°C leading to a rise in sea levels, extreme temperatures and precipitation, flooding and cyclones, erosion, productivity losses, and climate-related migration.[11121314]

It is, therefore, important to understand the impact of climate change and climatic variations on water-related physical events which also increase the risk of migration and drowning, especially in vulnerable populations. We undertook this evidence synthesis exercise to summarize and report available evidence on the topic, to facilitate a dialogue on the preparedness of climate resilient infrastructure, to strengthen rescue and public health systems, and to reduce the impact of climate change and associated migration. As existing research shows that drowning is also associated with climate change,[15] the data from this study can also provide the basis for capacity building of primary care physicians for drowning prevention, as they serve as the first point of contact for drowning treatment, care, rescue trainings, and counseling in resource-limited settings like India.

The specific objectives of the study are:

Objective I: To summarize and report evidence on the impact of climate change on water-related physical events and phenomena (including but not limited to SLR, glacier bursts, lake bursts, floods, extreme rainfall, cyclones, and droughts) and consequent human migration in India.

Objective II: To summarize and report evidence on the impact of climate change, any water-related physical event, any meteorological event, or a seasonal variation that contributed to drowning incidences or reported an association with its risk factors (including but not limited to age, gender, access to water, and water transport) in India.

Materials and Methods

For objective I, we mapped the evidence using a narrative review approach, in view of reporting a broad perspective and identifying specific areas for future research to be undertaken. While there are other inter-related issues, like extreme temperatures and geophysical and biological phenomena, we restricted our scope to water-related events and phenomena for objective I. The rationale was to identify and synthesize evidence that is more directly linked to changes in exposure to water. For objective II, we broadened this scope, recognizing the scarcity of literature, and included studies reporting the impact of climate change, any water-related physical event, any meteorological event, or a seasonal variation that reported an association with drowning or its risk factors. We mapped evidence for this objective using a scoping review approach, to organize our data specific to drowning and highlight gaps in drowning research.

For objective I, we restricted our search to the previous six years (published from 2016-2021) to enable capturing the most relevant and up-to-date data in line with advances in the field of climate sciences. However, for objective II, we searched databases from inception till 2021, to be inclusive and in recognition of the complexities of mapping drowning in relation to climate change and other physical events. We included all study designs except commentaries, letters, opinion pieces, editorials, or other nonstudy-based pieces. Studies published in the English language were included in the review.

Sources of evidence

Electronic database search

For objective I, we searched three electronic databases (PubMed, CINAHL, and GreenFILE).

For objective II, we searched five electronic databases (PubMed, Embase Classic + Embase, Web of Science, Environment complete-EBSCO, and GreenFILE te-EBSC). We used a comprehensive search strategy to search for evidence for objective II, provided in Appendix I.

Other methods for searching

We screened reference lists of included articles and hand-searched major global and regional reports along with Indian government reports on climate change. We also hand-searched websites of key multinational organizations and Indian government websites and repositories. The list of websites searched (for both objectives) is provided in Appendix II.

Screening process and selection of studies

For objective I, a single reviewer (SM) searched for evidence. For objective II, DB and SB independently screened each study based on titles and/or abstracts. This process was done using Rayyan (a web-based platform for article screening). Disagreements on inclusion/exclusion at this phase were resolved by discussion, between the two authors to reach a consensus. In the second phase of screening, we obtained full texts of all studies marked as “include” in the previous phase. The decision for inclusion was performed by consensus between DB and SB for objective II, with JJ acting as an arbitrator for the final inclusion of studies.

Data extraction and management

Single reviewers performed data extraction for objective I (S.M.) and objective II (D.B.), using a predesigned data extraction form. Data were extracted for various parameters, that is, author and year, study objectives, study design, setting (national/state), theme or domain of interest, and key findings.

We narratively synthesized findings of the study without conducting any additional statistical analyses for both objectives. No additional information was sought from the authors of the identified studies and only data available in the published versions of studies were included.

Results

Search results

We conducted electronic database screening and hand-searching of relevant websites. We included 48 peer-reviewed studies and seven reports that we found relevant under objective I.

For objective II, we retrieved 157 records from database screening and 12 additional records through hand-searching relevant websites. After removing duplicates (n = 24), 145 records were screened for inclusion and exclusion based on title and abstracts. After excluding 134 records, we retrieved full texts of 11 articles that potentially met the inclusion criteria. However, after full-text screening and detailed discussion on one study, the research team came to the consensus that no study met the inclusion criteria to be included for this objective.

Synthesis of study findings

Objective I

Sea-level changes

Ten studies reported on sea-level changes, consistently showing SLR with a higher impact on inhabited coastal areas.

According to a Government of India report for 2020, SLR in the north Indian Ocean (under Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s Representative Concentration Pathway 4.5 scenario) is projected to rise by approximately 300 mm by the end of 21st century, with coastal cities such as Goa and Surat most vulnerable to flooding.[11] Studies reported that coastal zones along the Bay of Bengal will see increased flooding not only due to SLR but also because of large river discharge during the southwest monsoon period (June-September).[11] An expected increase in magnitude and incidence of tropical storms, and saltwater intrusion, in coastal zones along the Bay of Bengal, Mumbai, and the Arabian Sea areas due to sea-level fluctuations is also reported.[1617] The confidence interval on the level of SLR on the south and east coast is wide, with an expected increase ranging from 7 cm to 78 cm between 2025 and 2100 for Tamil Nadu and Puducherry coast.[18] Additional risks of SLR-related contamination of drinking water sources are also reported.[19]

Four studies reported on the relationship between SLR and coastal vulnerability index (assessment of coastal vulnerability to SLR, in particular, due to erosion and/or inundation) covering the Krishna-Godavari delta region in Andhra Pradesh,[20] Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta,[21] Odisha state coastline,[22] and Nagapattinam district of Tamil Nadu.[23] Each of these studies found that SLR increased coastal vulnerability.

Extreme weather events

Droughts

Studies (n = 6) show that nearly 68% of India’s geographical land area is prone to drought with an increasing trend in frequency and severity, notably in eastern, west-central, central north-east, northern, north-western, and peninsular India. Increasing trends in crop failures, alienation from land, unemployment, indebtedness, and predominantly migration of men, disproportionately increasing socio-economic stress for women and children have been reported.[112425262728]

Extreme rainfall, flood, and cyclonic events

We identified 12 studies covering rainfall, flood, and cyclonic events. Findings indicated multifold increases in extreme rain events between 1950 and 2015, with further warming of the Arabian Sea, leading to an increase in the number of such events.[2930] Studies also identified an increase in glacial flooding resulting from glacier melting, associated with increasing air temperatures. Glacial floods are expected to increase in the Himalayan region with increased flooding for Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra rivers.[3132]

Eight studies on cyclonic events were included in the review. These studies show an increasing trend in the frequency and intensity of cyclonic events (storms, rainfall, and flooding) in eastern, central, and northern India with increasing temperatures due to climate change. Cyclone-associated rainfall is expected to increase, with rainfall patterns depending upon geographic location and time of year (premonsoon or postmonsoon period).[333435363738] Two studies showed the increased air temperature was associated with a higher probability of flash flooding in Western Ghats and daily extremes of rainfall in the northeast India.[3940]

Changes in bodies of water

We included eight studies that reported changes in bodies of water. These studies suggested there has been an increase in surface-level water (including lakes, rivers, and wetlands) and stream flows with uncertainty and variability in the amount of surface water in the Indian subcontinental basins.[41424344] Such increases are projected for regions like the Sundarbans, Ganga, and Godavari River Basins, river catchments of the Western Ghats, Chandra-Bhaga Basin, and Mahanadi River Basin.[3942454647] Increases in glacial outbursts, lake expansion, and monsoon flows are also expected to enhance flood potential in delta regions.[4647]

Migration

We identified 12 studies that reported aspects of climate-induced migration. Poverty, inequality, population density, and hazard intensity are some factors that influenced the vulnerability of the Indian population to climate-induced migration.[48] Approximately 8% of all migration from rural to urban areas between 2005 and 2012 in India was attributed to weather, including flood and erosion risk.[4950]

Evidence suggests that climate-induced weather events (floods, cyclones, and droughts) have already put a lot of pressure on a large portion of the Indian population, particularly that which is largely dependent on climate-sensitive sectors such as agriculture, fisheries, and forestry.[515253] Such weather events result in increasing crop failures, unemployment, indebtedness, and migration leading to poverty and borrowing, majorly impacting the lives of women and children.[245455] The loss of income was found to be due to increased rainfall and flood events, especially forcing young people to migrate in search of employment.[50] Farmers were reported to be the most vulnerable to climate change variability and subsequent migration, followed by drivers and street food sellers.[56]

Evidence also shows diminishing snowfall due to increased temperatures, especially in lower altitudes, and changes in snowfall periods led to altered migration patterns in the northeast region.[57] However, some communities in South India were identified to have developed a profound knowledge of climate change variability with effective adaptive capacities and therefore were found to be less vulnerable to migration.[58]

Discussion

Summary of main results

Our review synthesizes evidence on the impact of climate change on water-related physical events and phenomena and associated migration and drowning in India. With limited literature identified, it is expected that India is going to witness the greatest SLR, with an increase in frequency, and intensity of droughts, extreme rainfall, floods and cyclones, glacial outbursts, lake expansion, and monsoon flows. Climate change–triggered migration will primarily impact northeastern and southern parts of India. No direct evidence was found on the impact of climate change, or any water-related physical events, meteorological events, or seasonal variations on the drowning burden in India, highlighting the need for generating new knowledge and understanding with the level of risk currently remaining unknown.

Implications for policy, practice, and research

A report by the Ministry of Earth Sciences highlighted the need for more independent studies that provide regional sea-level projections and their attribution (i.e., the role of external forces and natural variability).[11] The evidence from our review confirmed this gap with an identified lack of context-specific or localized data. Factors leading to inconsistent and inaccurate findings appear to be due to the use of different modeling approaches, the complexity of forecasting various water-related outcomes of climate change, and decentralized data systems.

India is expected to experience one of the greatest SLRs in the future. Our review confirmed that riverine, low-lying areas, and coastal belts will be most impacted by climate change. With increased inundation and erosion, these areas with large populations of low socio-economic status and high dependence on climate-driven occupations like farming and fishing are the most adversely impacted. Sea-level projections in coastal wetlands (and vulnerability among people in specific targeted populations), notably in West Bengal and Gujarat, are lacking.[59] There are limited published studies available for informing subnational or national mitigation plans. It was found that many studies have broad regional coverage, such as “South Asia”,[21] which do not provide details needed for localized planning. There is a need to enhance the present observational and modeling capabilities to better quantify the processes associated with regional sea-level variability.[16]

In many parts of India, extreme rainfall events, the frequency of cyclones, floods, and storms are increasing and remain unpredictable. These cataclysmic events not only lead to loss of income, livelihood, and increased migration but also have the potential to elevate the risk of drowning, especially in vulnerable populations in low-lying and coastal areas. Need for stronger cloud systems and connected databases are needed to reinforce early warnings.[60]

Our review shows that droughts will become increasingly intense and impact wider geographic areas in India in the future. In 2022, India entered the United Nations’ Global Drought Vulnerability Index.[61] However, when comparing individual studies, findings are somewhat inconsistent in terms of which areas will be impacted, probably due to different modeling approaches and the complexity of factors that determine these extreme weather events. The review also indicated that extreme rainfall events are increasing in many areas but are largely unpredictable. Continuing urbanization and changes in agriculture patterns call for an ongoing reassessment of the risk of such events on the Indian population.

The evidence on the impact of climate change on surface-level water is scarce. There is a need for a deeper understanding of factors that influence changes in bodies of water leading to increased water flow, glacial melting, and monsoonal rains in India. This calls for context-specific and localized data to inform local-level mitigation strategies.

Our review also identified climate-change–related migration events. However, this evidence was mostly derived from northeast and southern India. There is a need for further research to better understand the combination of factors influencing migration, including exposure to dangerous water routes and hazards, economic sensitivity, geographic location, and shifting livelihood opportunities. This can help inform relevant provisions for the displaced and identify adaptation strategies that are local, workable, and safe for affected communities.

Our review highlights a significant knowledge gap in terms of climate change-triggered drowning burden or drowning burden associated with water-related physical events or other meteorological and seasonal variations in India. This is in sharp contrast to the evidence available on links between climate change, extreme weather events, and drowning, which is mostly generated from high-income countries, probably due to stronger reporting systems and funding resources for research.[15] However, even if the level of drowning risk in relation to climate change is unknown, through existing literature and in addition to the knowledge through our study, we know that water-related disasters will be on the rise in the coming years and may increase the drowning burden.

Despite India carrying a huge drowning burden, it unfortunately fails to make a mark on the health policy agenda and remains poorly researched.[62] Given the global agenda prioritization, it is timely to integrate drowning prevention with the agenda on climate actions.[6364] In India, the lack of official data due to poor reporting systems for tracking drowning mortality and morbidity, makes it difficult to assess the drowning burden accurately, especially during extreme weather events and disasters due to high population displacements. Generation of local-level primary data in the first place is important to develop contextually relevant drowning prevention interventions, considering climate change risks according to varied geographical locations and vulnerability of populations. There is also the need to strengthen primary care for drowning prevention, as primary care physicians serve as the first point of contact for drowning treatment. They also play a significant role in providing training on resuscitation, rescue techniques, counseling, and emergency care at the primary level. They have also been found helpful in identifying the best drowning prevention strategies needed at the local level, which may prove highly effective to reduce the drowning burden, especially during disasters.[656667]

There is a need for more collaborative research which needs to be long-term and engages multidisciplinary teams of primary care physicians, epidemiologists, social workers, anthropologists, climate, and conservation scientists with in-depth knowledge in the field of drowning, climate change, and its consequences.

Strengths and limitations of the review

We searched multiple electronic databases and hand-searched websites to identify the maximum evidence available. We used climate change as a broad term for various water-related physical events and phenomena, migration, and drowning, to be more inclusive. We acknowledge limiting our search for the previous six years for objective I, which was in line with advances in the field of climate sciences and ways to capture data for rapidly changing climatic conditions. We also adopted different approaches, namely, narrative review and scoping review which we found relevant to two different objectives, to retrieve the maximum evidence available for mapping. We acknowledge including studies published only in the English language as a limitation but contend this would not significantly affect study results, as English is the dominant medium of written policies and research in India.

Conclusion

This review identified evidence that India will experience one of the world’s greatest SLRs, changes in monsoonal rain periods, increased frequency, and intensity of droughts, rainfall, floods, and cyclones in the coming years, which may also increase the risk of drowning. These changes in the environment are expected to have a profound effect on human migration and displacements in all future scenarios. However, there is a significant gap in the availability of context-specific and localized data for developing stronger prediction, disaster response, and public health systems, especially for coastal areas identified as most vulnerable to climate change. There is a need for adopting environmental management strategy with stricter policy measures to mitigate the risk of rising SLR. With no study identified, and considering known associations in global literature, there is an urgent need to generate understanding and new knowledge on the impact of climate change, water-related physical events, and meteorological or seasonal variations on drowning to reduce the burden in India.
