Mapping the scientific landscape of Lassa virus: a 55-year bibliometric study (1970–2025)
Authors: Uwem Okon Edet, Clement Meseko, Md Zulfekar Ali, Edema Enogiomwan Imalele, Agusi Ebere Roseann, Aniekan-Augusta Okon Eyo, Bassey Edet, Olayinka Asala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLassa fever is a zoonotic disease, caused by Lassa virus (LASV), that was first reported in 1965. After 55 years, its research landscape remains largely underexplored. Therefore, a bibliometric study is needed to identify LASV research landscape.MethodsScopus database was searched and the resulting data was analyzed using VOSviewer (version 1.6.15) for Windows, bibliometrix (version 3.0.0) with Biblioshiny, and R (version 4.5.2) to reveal trends in LASV publications, including keywords, authors’ productivity over time, and collaboration among research institutions and universities.ResultsFrom Scopus, 1566 studies were retrieved, and the trend analysis indicated three distinct phases: a low productivity phase (1970–2002), consistent increase phase (2003–2014), and high productivity phase (2015–2025). The Journal of Virology included the most LASV publications (132 articles). Original research articles dominated the LASV publications with 1270 (81.10%), followed by reviews 296 (18.90%). Keyword analysis revealed evolution over time, with terms used to describe LASV forming the most dominant network across clusters. Factorial analysis revealed keywords absence linked to health and environment role in LASV spread. Among the top 50 authors, 20% were from Africa (Guinea, Mali, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone), indicating minimal locally driven research. Country collaboration network analysis revealed the dominance of the United States, Germany, France, and China, while Nigeria was prominently represented in Africa. The findings further indicate few studies on vaccines and therapeutics, and less involvement of authors from LASV-endemic regions.ConclusionDespite significant growth in LASV research, critical gaps in vaccines, therapeutics, and endemic region representation highlight the urgent need for locally driven One Health studies and equitable research partnerships.

Introduction

The Lassa virus (LASV) is a single-stranded, enveloped RNA virus belonging to the Arenaviridae family. Several transmission routes for the virus exist, from rodents to humans and from humans to humans, including contact with infected body fluids and secretions (blood, urine, and saliva), eating foods contaminated by the excreta or urine of its reservoir host, namely Mastomys rodents [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The index case of LASV was first reported in Lassa, a community in Borno State, Nigeria, where the virus was named [9]. Since then, multiple outbreaks have been recorded across Nigeria and several West African countries, including Benin, Ghana, Liberia, Guinea, Mali, and Sierra Leone, where the virus is endemic. In these endemic regions, an estimated 100,000–500,000 infections and approximately 5000 deaths occur annually [1,5,7,10]. In addition to LASV, other hemorrhagic fever viruses, such as Ebola and Marburg, have also been reported [11,12]. Rai et al. [11] emphasized the urgent need for integrated zoonotic research by governments. Similarly, the 2024 Marburg virus outbreak in Rwanda underscores a parallel urgency, revealing shared deficiencies in zoonotic disease preparedness and highlighting the critical absence of a single health framework in endemic settings [12]. The frequent recurrence of Lassa fever outbreaks, particularly in Nigeria, poses a major national and global public health threat that is exacerbated by the absence of approved antiviral drugs or vaccines [[13], [14], [15]]. As expected, several primary and secondary studies have been conducted worldwide. An urgent need to understand this trajectory exists. Studying the LASV trends can help reveal existing knowledge gaps, and guide policies at the community, national, and international levels.

Bibliometric studies provide quantitative and qualitative insights into research conducted over time [16]. “Bibliometric” research, first coined in the same year that LASV was first reported in Nigeria [17], is a tool that allows for the visualization and evaluation of the scientific and social relevance of a research area over time [18]. Although bibliometric studies have limitations, such as reliance on selected databases and language bias, they remain highly valuable for understanding the growth, volume, trends, and distribution of research outputs across authors, institutions, and countries. This study design has been utilized in various instances to examine influenza viruses globally [19], human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) in Nigeria [20], climate change and human health [21], scholarly productivity [22], and other topics [23]. They offer valuable insights for stakeholders (researchers, policymakers, and funders), guiding decisions and policies for intervention and control.

To the best of our knowledge, only one bibliometric analysis of the LASV has been reported in 2018 [18]. This study examined publication trends, top journal destinations, the most prolific authors, and the most productive institutions over a 47-year period following the LASV index case. Their findings indicated slow growth, as expected in the 1970s, when the virus was first reported, and identified a linear rather than exponential growth pattern, unlike in other biomedical research fields [23]. The study further highlighted that the United States, a high-income country, was the most prolific contributor to LASV research globally because of research funding. However, it did not report the emergence or evolution of keywords or their thematic clustering over time. The present study identifies the top 20 countries involved in the LASV research. Unfortunately, only five African countries were represented: Nigeria (5th), Sierra Leone (6th), Guinea (11th), South Africa (14th), and Liberia (17th). As the virus continues to spread across West Africa, there is a growing need to comprehensively re-evaluate the global trajectory of LASV research.

Although insightful, the 2018 study has several limitations. It did not analyze keywords to capture thematic patterns, thereby leaving major research trends in the LASV unexplored. In addition, the study utilized only “Lassa fever” as a keyword in their search. By leaving out other standard terms, including “Lassa hemorrhagic fever,” “Lassa haemorrhagic fever,” “Lassa virus,” and “LASV,” the study may have excluded important publications. To bridge this gap, we encompassed all the terms used by authors to describe the virus and its infection, including “Lassa hemorrhagic fever” OR “Lassa haemorrhagic fever” OR “Lassa virus” OR “LASV.” Covering all the standard terms used for the virus ensured that all relevant studies were captured, including those necessary for evaluating the role of One Health in addressing the virus. Moreover, given the passage of time, it is necessary to assess the volume of research produced over the past 55 years, identify the most active authors and institutions, and evaluate the thematic evolution of keywords in greater detail. This aligns with the position of Hastie et al. [24] who noted that significant gaps remain, underscoring the importance of updating our understanding of global LASV research. Understanding the thematic clusters of keywords and their emergence can reveal the temporal direction of LASV research. Given that cases of the virus are still being reported in Nigeria and other West African countries where the main rodent reservoir Mastomys natalensis is found, there is a need to further update our knowledge of the research landscape, the re-emergence of keywords, their clusters, and thematic areas in the last decade, as well as the prolific authors and institutions involved in LASV research. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate research outputs from the past 55 years, identify prolific authors and institutions, map keyword clusters, and track the evolution of thematic areas. In addition, to understand how the research volume has fared over time, we constructed a scatterplot (studies versus years) and fitted it with both a trend line and a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) curve at a 95% confidence interval [25].

Methods

Choice and screening of databases

We searched the PubMed and Scopus databases. Our choice of these databases was informed by the extensive coverage of relevant literature, which helped us address our research questions. PubMed was selected as it is dedicated to indexing medical and related health research worldwide [26]. In contrast, Scopus was chosen for its broader multidisciplinary scope, encompassing studies in the life, physical, and social sciences, as well as the arts and humanities, from over 5000 publishers across all major disciplines [27]. This suggests that PubMed, as a database, underrepresents veterinary, environmental, and social science literature, which are central to the health dimension. Our initial search yielded 1116 studies in PubMed, whereas in Scopus, we retrieved 1983 documents without the use of filters. Based on these considerations, we used Scopus for a comprehensive search and selection of studies.

Retrieval of studies from Scopus

The Scopus database was searched on 4 November 2025. To ensure a comprehensive search, the following query was applied: TITLE-ABS-KEY (“Lassa hemorrhagic fever” OR “Lassa haemorrhagic fever” OR “Lassa virus” OR LASV) AND (LIMIT-TO (DOCTYPE, “ar”) OR LIMIT-TO (DOCTYPE, “re”)) AND (LIMIT-TO (PUBSTAGE, “final”)) AND (LIMIT-TO (LANGUAGE, “English”)). The search period was set from 1970 to 2025 to capture all studies published within this 55-year time frame. This approach was based on previously reported methods [18,28]. The initial search retrieved 1983 documents without filters, which were subsequently refined to 1727 documents when limited to articles and reviews. Further restriction of the search to English-language publications resulted in 1566 documents. Two authors independently verified the results to ensure reproducibility.

Bibliometric analyses of the data

This was performed as described previously reported [18,19,28]. In performing the bibliometric analyses, the VOSviewer (version 1.6.15) for Windows [29], R (version 4.5.2) running in RStudio (version 2025.09.0) [30], and Datawrapper (https://www.datawrapper.de/) [31] were used. To analyze the keywords and authors, both network and overlay visualizations were generated using VOSviewer to identify clusters, bubble sizes, and the temporal distribution of keywords. Using bibliometrix (version 3.0.0) with Biblioshiny implemented in RStudio, a factorial plot (conceptual structure map) or Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was produced using all the authors’ keywords to further illustrate the diverse clusters of keywords. The same tool was used to visualize authors' productivity trends over time, showing the number of publications and total citations per year for the top 10 authors. With Datawrapper, the global distribution of publications was mapped, and R was used to infer the top collaboration frequencies among countries involved in LASV research worldwide.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive temporal analysis was performed to quantify the annual publication trends using the R package “ggplot2” [30]. A scatterplot with connected points was generated to visualize the year-wise variations. To evaluate the long-term trend direction, a linear regression model was fitted. However, owing to nonlinear growth patterns, a LOESS curve with 95% confidence intervals was applied to better capture the inflection periods [32]. Using the same tool, the top 25 journals that published at least 11 documents on LASV were visualized using a simple bar chart, whereas a global map showing authors’ collaborations was illustrated with a chord diagram.

Summary of the search results and analysis performed

A bibliometric analysis of LASV-related publications indexed in Scopus between 1970 and 2025 identified 1566 documents published across 493 sources, including journals, books, and conference proceedings (Table 1). The annual growth rate was 6.81%, and the average document age was 13.6 years. Each publication received an average of 42.98 citations, with 6862 cited references. The dataset included more than 10,070 keywords and 2367 author keywords. A total of 7058 authors contributed, with 108 single-authored documents and an average of 8.09 co-authors per publication. Notably, 35.82% of the publications involved international co-authorships. Research articles accounted for 1270 documents (81.1%), and reviews accounted for 296 documents (18.9%).

Study trajectory in the last five and half decades

Within the study period (1970–2025), 1566 LASV-related studies indexed in the Scopus database were retrieved and included in the final dataset. Analysis of the publication trajectory revealed notable variations in research output over time, which can be categorized into three distinct phases: (1) a low-productivity phase (1970–2002), (2) a steady or consistent growth phase (2003–2014), and (3) a high-productivity phase (2015–2025). During the first phase (1970–2002), the number of annual publications was low (below 20), with only minor intermittent fluctuations (Fig. 1). A consistent increase was observed between 2003 and 2014, indicating a second phase. The third phase, starting in 2015, saw a sharp rise in publications, peaking at 107 in 2019. Over the last eight years (2018–2025), research productivity has remained high, with annual outputs ranging from 90 to 110, although a slight decline to below 80 publications was observed in 2025.

Article types and journal destinations

Table 2 summarizes the study types filtered during the search process. The highest number of original research articles was 1270, representing 81.10% of the total studies, whereas reviews, including systematic reviews, represented 18.90% of the total volume, with 296 studies. The top 25 journals destinations publishing LASV studies from all over the globe with at least 11 documents is illustrated in Fig. 2. Journal of Virology had the highest number of documents (132), followed by Viruses (70), PLoS Neglected Tropical Diseases (47), and Antiviral Research (42). Other journals, including Emerging Infectious Diseases, Virology, PLoS Pathogens, and Journal of Infectious Diseases, each had between 28 and 38 publications. Several journals, such as Lancet, Emerging Microbes & Infections, and Virus Research, published fewer studies, ranging from 11 to 14. Collectively, the top 25 journals published 638 studies, representing 41% of all the studies.

Clusters, network visualization, top 10 and factorial analysis of keywords

Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5 and Table 3 present various aspects of the keyword analyses performed in this study, with the minimum occurrence threshold set at five keywords. Fig. 3 illustrates the distribution of keywords employed by different authors across the reviewed studies, where the size of each keyword node reflects its frequency of use. “Lassa virus” and “Lassa fever” were the most frequently occurring keywords, as indicated by their large node sizes. This observation is further supported by Table 3, which lists the top 20 keywords identified in literature. The five most frequently used keywords were “Lassa virus” (258 occurrences), “Lassa fever” (161), “Arenavirus” (133), “Vaccine” (46), and “Ebola virus” (37) (Table 3, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

The clustering pattern reveals a distinct thematic grouping. The red cluster comprised keywords such as “SARS-CoV-2,” “Ebola virus,” “Coronavirus,” “Pandemic,” “Cancer,” and “Enveloped virus,” indicating research overlap between LASV and other viral pathogens. The green cluster includes “Lassa virus,” “Innate immunity,” “Molecular docking,” “Matrix protein,” and “Neutralising antibodies.” The orange cluster encompassed “Filovirus,” “Dengue virus,” “Hantavirus,” “Hemorrhagic fever,” “Infection,” “Pregnancy,” and “Junin virus.” Keywords displayed in various shades of blue dominated the network visualization, including “Lassa fever,” “Mastomys natalensis,” “Phylogeny,” “Sensitivity analysis,” “Optimal control,” “Guinea,” “Seroprevalence,” “West Africa,” “Ebola,” “Treatment,” “Epidemic,” “Outbreak,” “ELISA,” “Marburg,” and “Emerging infectious diseases.” The brown cluster consisted of “Membrane fusion,” “Fusion inhibitor,” and “Viral entry,” while the yellow cluster included “Guinea pig,” “Antiviral,” “Glycoprotein,” “Arenaviridae,” “Virus,” and “LASV.”

Fig. 4 shows an overlay visualization of the keywords used in LASV research globally over time. Newer keywords that emerged in 2020 included “Molecular docking,” “Sensitivity analysis,” “Prevention,” “Mammarenavirus,” “Sierra Leone,” “SARS-CoV-2,” and “Neutralising antibodies,” whereas older keywords were “Filovirus,” “Dengue virus,” “Hantavirus,” “Hemorrhagic fever,” “Infection,” “Pregnancy,” and “Junin virus.”

Fig. 5 shows a factorial plot or MCA of the various keywords used in Lassa fever research. The factorial analysis of the keyword analysis plot revealed two distinct thematic orientations in LASV research, with dimensions 1 and 2 jointly explaining 46.11% of the variance (Fig. 5). From the MCA plot, four primary clusters emerged: (1) West African hemorrhagic fevers linked with M. natalensis and regional phylogenetic studies (top left hand side); (2) Lassa fever–focused research emphasizing viral entry, pathogenesis, and antivirals using models (top right hand side); (3) filovirus and arenavirus investigations and other hemorrhagic viruses, including Ebola, Marburg, and Junin viruses, and (4) emerging virus studies highlighting SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, and vaccine development. Overall, the spatial dispersion of the authors’ terms indicated diversified yet interrelated research themes in viral hemorrhagic fevers and emerging zoonoses.

The influence and productivity of authors within each cluster indicate that Stephan Günther, Juan Carlos de la Torre, Elisabeth Fichet-Calvet, Igor S. Lukashevich, and Joseph B. All 50 authors had 1112 documents. Institutions and affiliations analyses

We further analyzed the institutions of the leading authors to identify the most productive ones, and the results are presented in Fig. 9, Fig. 10 and Table 5. Fig. 9 illustrates the various institutions involved in LASV research. The top three institutions were the Bernhard Nocht Institut Fur Tropenmedizin Hamburg, leading with 435 articles, followed closely by Irrua Specialist Teaching Hospital, also known as the Institute of Lassa Fever Research and Control (Edo State, Nigeria) with 397 articles, and the Scripps Research Institute with 264 publications. Overall, the United States–based institutes and universities dominate.

Table 5 provides a summary of the single- and multi-country publications (SCP and MCP). In both categories, the United States, Germany, Nigeria, China, the United Kingdom, and France were among the top six countries with the highest number of SCP and MCP articles. Furthermore, Fig. 10 shows various countries around the globe publishing LASV research worldwide and their collaborative networks. The results indicate that countries between Turkey (Table S1) and France, except Thailand, dominated LASV research worldwide, whereas countries between New Zealand and UAE were largely recipients of these collaborations/publications. The results further indicate that the United States, followed by Nigeria, Germany, and China, led the collaborations/publications around the globe. This is further supported by collaborative data showing the geographical spread of the publications (Table S1). These data show a clear lead of the United States and further indicate that in Africa, only the West African region dominates LASV research, whereas in Europe and Asia, the dominance of Germany and China was also very visible.

Discussion

Research trajectory analysis

Our search returned 1556 studies on LASV from the Scopus database published over the past 55 years. The total volume indicates a 50% increase in the volume of studies from an earlier study that reported 1101 studies across the globe over a period of 47 years, using Scopus as their choice database [18]. This difference indicates that the volume of studies has increased in the last eight years, probably driven by reported outbreaks of LASV across endemic regions in Africa [2,33]. Furthermore, our scatterplot and fitted LOESS curve indicate three main phases: a period of stagnation (1970–2002), a distinct transition phase (2003–2014), and an era of rapid growth (2015–2025). The period 1970–2002 recorded the least number of studies or the lowest rate of publications in the first three to four decades this period covers, which is similar to previous studies, including those on HIV/AIDS [22], highly pathogenic avian influenza virus [19], and LASV [18], which reported low production in their early days. In particular, a previous report on the LASV recorded 64 documents with the highest number of publications in 2017, which was the highest volume of studies reported in a single year within the 47-year period in their study [18].

The transition and distinct growth phases, spanning 2003–2025, recorded the highest volume of studies, which can be attributed to steady growth and a surge in the volume of studies driven by reported LASV outbreaks across Nigeria and other endemic countries in Africa [2,33], including the 2017/2018 [34] and reported burden of the disease between 2020 and 2023 in Nigeria [35]. Additionally, global initiatives, such as the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI) and the WHO R&D Blueprint for Epidemics, have contributed to LASV research, as they prioritized the pathogen, and funded vaccines and epidemiological studies, build capacity in endemic regions, facilitate collaboration, and accelerate countermeasure development. These efforts have contributed to enhancing the research volume, quality, relevance, and equity, and aligning scientific outputs with public health and outbreak preparedness needs.

The increase in study volume could also be explained by the increased national, regional, and international interest in the virus as it continues to spread across West Africa, with the likelihood of exporting or spreading beyond the region, and becoming pandemic [[36], [37], [38]]. The increase in publications from 2015 onwards, in particular, could be attributed to the heightened awareness of zoonotic diseases, including the outbreak of Ebola in 2014 and COVID-19 in 2019 [39], and reports of other hemorrhagic fever viruses, including the Zika, Ebola, and Marburg viruses, within the same period [12,40,41]. In addition, studies may have been driven by recent modelling studies based on climate, land use, and population changes, which revealed that the Central and East African regions in the next decade will become suitable for the spread of the virus [42]. With the recently concluded LASV conference in Abidjan, held between 8 and 11 September 2025, there is bound to be an increase in the number of publications. Overall, since the index case of the virus was reported in Nigeria five and a half decades ago, there has been a slow but steady increase in research output worldwide.

Study type and journal destinations

In addition to the number of publications over the 55-year period, we evaluated the study types and top scientific journal destinations that published LASV studies within the same period. Among the 1566 studies, original studies far exceeded reviews, by a ratio of 4:1. This suggests a 4:1 synthesis rate of original studies into reviews. Original studies, also known as primary studies, aimed to generate empirical evidence from experiments, fieldwork, surveys, clinical trials, laboratory analyses, and direct observation [43]. The dominance of original articles stemmed from the need for ample empirical data on all aspects of the viral life cycle, ecology, disease mechanisms, transmission routes, prevention and control strategies, and therapeutics as indicated by some primary studies [44,45]. Moreover, original research is at the core of evidence-based practice, especially during outbreaks of diseases such as LASV, as it provides timely data that provide insights and are critical for public health responses and policy decisions [46]. Conversely, reviews, also known as secondary research or studies, aim to synthesize and analyze findings from multiple primary studies to summarize the current state of knowledge on a topic of interest [47]. The substantial number of review articles indicates that knowledge on LASV has expanded over time and that there is a growing need to synthesize evidence to identify gaps, inform future research, and guide policy. Several types of reviews exist, including scoping, narrative, and systematic reviews, with or without meta-analyses [47].

Systematic reviews are regarded as having the highest level of evidence in medicine, and when written well, they can provide baseline data that guide policies and reveal additional gaps [47]. Five systematic reviews have been reported on the LASV. Doohan et al. [48] synthesized mathematical models and disease parameters to better understand transmission and control dynamics. Eberhardt et al. [49] assessed the effectiveness of ribavirin treatment and reported inconsistent outcomes owing to limited clinical data. Ogundele et al. [50] analyzed epidemiological parameters such as attack and fatality rates across West Africa. Kayem et al. [51] examined adverse pregnancy outcomes, whereas Takah et al. [52] evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic assays, emphasizing diagnostic limitations. It is clear that there is a need for more systematic reviews, especially those relating to vaccine development progress, antiviral alternatives beyond ribavirin, and long-term sequelae in Lassa fever survivors. Other important areas include evaluation of genomic variability and the effectiveness of integrated One Health surveillance systems linking human, animal, and environmental reservoirs, which can provide insights into preventive strategies. Additionally, synthesizing evidence on community-based interventions, risk communication, and health system readiness could aid in outbreak preparedness.

Our analysis of journal destinations revealed that the top 25 journals accounted for approximately one in every four publications on LASV over the past 55 years, indicating a clear preference among authors for these outlets. Researchers' choice of journals may be influenced by several factors, including open-access availability, impact factors, journal visibility, audience reach, publication speed, indexing in reputable databases, and alignment with the journal’s thematic scope or geographic focus [53,54]. Notably, the top 25 journals publishing LASV-related research were predominantly high impact factor outlets. Since journals with high-impact factors are often associated with a wider dissemination of scientific findings, this likely contributes to authors' selection preferences [55]. Other considerations, such as the availability of publication fee waivers, collaborative partnerships, and perceived prestige within the scientific community, may have also influenced these decisions [53,54].

Among the top 25 journals, the leading five journals were primarily domain-specific, focusing on virology, neglected tropical diseases, and antiviral research, reflecting the dominant emphasis on viral biology and anti-LASV therapeutic development. In addition, multidisciplinary journals, such as Scientific Reports, Nature Communications, and PLoS One were represented, underscoring the cross-disciplinary nature of LASV research and its alignment with the One Health Framework. Overall, publications were concentrated within a subset of virology and infectious disease journals, whereas a broader range of journals contributed fewer articles. This pattern highlights the need for a dedicated LASV-focused journal or for periodic special issues that solicit research contributions to the LASV to promote sustained scholarly engagement in this critical area.

Keywords trend and analyses

Keyword analysis was conducted to reveal the diverse terms used globally in LASV research, identify thematic clusters, and track their evolution over time. In most studies, authors provide keywords that summarize the essence of the work and facilitate indexing and retrieval [20,28]. Our network and overlay visualizations highlighted the diversity of keywords, their frequency of use, thematic clusters, interconnections, and temporal trends. The keywords with the highest frequencies were the most interconnected across clusters, reflecting their central role in LASV research. The top 10 keywords included terms used to describe the LASV, “Arenavirus,” “viral hemorrhagic fever,” “hemorrhagic fever,” and the host, “M. natalensis,” indicating that the studies focused on multiple aspects of the virus and its reservoir [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8],56]. The inclusion of other hemorrhagic fever viruses, including “Ebola,” “Filovirus,” “Marburg virus,” and “Junin,” aligns with previous studies [11,12]. The prominence of keywords, such as “vaccine” and “antiviral” reflected a sustained research focus on the development of antiviral agents [14,49].

The various clusters indicated distinct thematic areas within LASV research. The green cluster reflects immunological and molecular modelling studies, emphasizing host-virus interactions, protein structure-function relationships, and the identification of vaccines or therapeutic targets. The orange cluster represents comparative virology and clinical research focusing on the pathogenesis, cross-reactivity, and coinfection dynamics of viral hemorrhagic fever. The blue cluster highlights epidemiological and ecological research directions encompassing surveillance, outbreak modelling, host ecology, and transmission dynamics across endemic regions. Notably, this cluster aligns closely with the One Health framework, integrating the human, animal, and environmental dimensions of the LASV research. The brown cluster emphasizes antiviral drug discovery and viral pathomechanism studies, particularly the mechanisms of viral inhibition. The yellow cluster highlights experimental and preclinical investigations, including animal models, viral replication, and glycoprotein-mediated immune responses. Collectively, these clusters illustrate the multidisciplinary nature of LASV research and bridge the gaps between molecular biology, epidemiology, immunology, and therapeutic development.

Our MCA results further supported the thematic clusters revealed by keyword analysis. The MCA analysis revealed four main clusters, West Africa as a region, and hemorrhagic fevers linked with M. natalensis. Regional phylogenetic studies, specifically linked to Nigeria and Guinea, further confirmed the host and regional nature of the disease [57]. The presence of Nigeria and Guinea further aligned with their dominance in authorship and institutional analyses. The second thematic area showed the presence of LASV-focused research emphasizing viral entry, pathogenesis, and antivirals using models, indicating that primary studies are still being conducted to further understand the pathogen and develop antiviral agents [14,58]. The third cluster focused on other hemorrhagic viruses, including filovirus, Ebola, Marburg, and Junin viruses, indicating an increased awareness of these viruses, as indicated by recent studies on these agents from Africa [11,12]. The fourth cluster showed the presence of emerging virus studies focusing on SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, vaccine development driven by the outbreak of the coronavirus, and the urgent need for a vaccine for LASV [28,39,59]. The gross absence of keywords, such as “One Health” and “environment,” represents a system silo and an urgent gap that warrants immediate attention. Arruda et al. [60] emphasized incorporating system dynamics within a broader One Health framework to better identify effective interventions for controlling the key drivers of LASV outbreaks. Addressing these gaps and sustaining molecular advances could strengthen integrated strategies for controlling LASV infections in endemic regions. The 2023 Marburg outbreak in Tanzania exposed gaps in zoonotic surveillance and health integration, similar to those evident in Lassa fever research over the past 55 years [61].

Prolific authors conducting LASV research

Our analysis aimed to provide a snapshot of the most prolific contributors, their clusters, and their evolution over the last five and a half decades of LASV research. As expected, a diverse range of authors has been involved in global LASV research. The top 50 authors, each with at least six publications, were identified. Among these, Stephan Günther, Juan Carlos De La Torre, Elisabeth Fichet-Calvet, Igor S. Lukashevich, and Joseph B. McCormick, had the highest number of LASV publications, highlighting their significant contributions as pioneering authors. Our temporal overlay analysis indicates that from 2020 onwards, a newer generation of authors has emerged, including Alexandra Journeaux, Caroline Picard, and Caroline Carbonnelle. Notably, the top 50 authors contributing to LASV research were primarily from outside West Africa despite the majority of LASV outbreaks occurring in this region. Overall, only 20% of the top 50 authors are from Africa (Guinea, Mali, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone). This disparity in country affiliation may be attributed to several factors, including limited research infrastructure, funding constraints, inadequate access to international collaborations, limited access to advanced technologies and other resources, and broader funding inequalities [[62], [63], [64]]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for enhanced capacity-building initiatives and greater financial and technological support for researchers in endemic regions to ensure a more equitable and comprehensive global LASV research landscape. Notably, several authors, including Pardis Sarbeti and Christian Happi, have contributed immensely to LASV research. Pardis Sarbeti is well-known for genetic studies on humans infected with the virus [65]. Additionally, Christian Happi’s study on other potential reservoirs of the virus has contributed immensely to our understanding of its ecology [66].

Prolific institutes and universities conducting LASV research

Our bibliometric analysis of author affiliations revealed that research on LASV is geographically concentrated, with the highest output from the United States, followed by significant contributions from Nigeria, Germany, and China. The distribution of affiliations further shows that, despite the majority of LASV outbreaks occurring within West Africa, a significant proportion of research was conducted by authors and institutions outside endemic areas. The dominance of the United States, Germany, and China in the field reflects their advanced research infrastructure and funding capacity. In contrast, Nigeria stands out within Africa as the leading contributor, which is consistent with its status as the epicenter of Lassa fever outbreaks. Nigeria’s dominance in LASV research can be attributed to the establishment of the specialist Institute of Lassa Fever Research and Control in Southern Nigeria, whose sole mandate is to conduct LASV research. Again, the observed disparity may be attributed to several factors, including limited research infrastructure in low-income countries, funding constraints, limited international collaboration opportunities, and reduced access to advanced technologies and other resources [67].

The prominence of European research institutions in LASV research, including France and Germany, may also reflect historical ties, global health priorities, and the presence of well-established funding mechanisms supporting research on tropical diseases. The Bernhard Nocht Institute for Tropical Medicine in Hamburg mandated the study of viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF), particularly Lassa fever, which explains its central role in LASV research. The dominance of Nigeria in the field can also be attributed to the presence of a dedicated infrastructure and the commitment of several research centers in Nigeria with the capacity to handle the LASV, including the Irrua Specialist Teaching Hospital/the Institute of Lassa Fever Research and Control and Redeemer’s University. Other West African nations, such as Sierra Leone and Guinea, also contributed, although at comparatively lower levels than Nigeria, which could be an indication of fewer outbreaks or an absence of adequate research infrastructure and funding in these countries. Despite the dominance of the top four countries, collaborative networks were observed in various nations. However, such collaborations appear to be lacking among the top four countries leading LASV research globally. Our findings also align with those of a previous bibliometric study that noted that collaboration and research were largely driven by high-resource countries [18], indicating that this trend has persisted. Beyond Lassa virus, other re-emerging zoonotic viruses, such as Zika, exist in the region, underscoring the urgency of sustained research investments in Africa’s tropical diseases [41].

Limitation of the study

This study has some limitations. This study focused only on the Scopus database. By excluding other databases, some published studies may have been inadvertently excluded. However, our use of Scopus as the sole database was justified in that it is a dedicated database for studies in life, physical, and social sciences, as well as in the arts and humanities. Scopus, being multidisciplinary in nature, is unlike other databases. Furthermore, we filtered out studies not written in English, which may have excluded studies written in other languages. Additionally, the use of non-standard terms, including Lassa virus disease, may have resulted in a few missed studies.

Conclusion

Despite the inherent limitations of bibliometric analysis, which stem from our sole utilization of the Scopus database, our study provides a comprehensive overview of the evolving landscape of LASV research over the past 55 years. Our findings reveal gross growth in studies on LASV, broken into three distinct phases: (1) slow productivity (1970–2002), (2) steady increase (2003–2014), and (3) rapid growth phase (2015–2025), reflecting heightened scientific interest driven by recurring LASV outbreaks in endemic regions, including Nigeria and other West African countries, reports of other hemorrhagic fever viruses, and global health crises. The spread of the most prolific authors showed a dominance of authors from non-endemic areas compared to those from endemic regions at a ratio of 4:1. Furthermore, resource-rich nations dominated the affiliation analysis, as expected. An under-representation of authors and institutions from endemic regions was observed, with the notable exception of Nigeria, which ranks as the second most prolific country in LASV research and has the potential to further expand its contributions, especially with the operationalization of One Health collaborations. Thematic analysis of the keywords revealed that ongoing studies covered diverse aspects, including the regionalization of studies on the phylogeny of the virus, other hemorrhagic viruses, therapies, and vaccines. However, terms linked to the One Health framework were missing, indicating an under-exploration of the One Health concept. Fostering equitable research collaborations, enhancing local capacity building, and strengthening international partnerships are essential for advancing global strategies for the prevention, control, and management of LASV.
