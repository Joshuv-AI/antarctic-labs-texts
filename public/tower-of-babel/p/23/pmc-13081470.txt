Relationship between screen use, physical activity, sleep duration and bladder and bowel dysfunction in Thai children
Authors: Varisa Piriyakitphaiboon, Michael Yong Hwa Chia, Terence Buan Kiong Chua, Yupaporn Amornchaicharoensuk, Ornatcha Sirimongkolchaiyakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) is prevalent among children and can cause chronic kidney disease. Multiple factors linked to LUTD, including genetic, physical, and behavioral characteristics, have been identified. However, research on the links among time spent on screens (television, computer, smart phones and tablets), physical activity, sleep duration and LUTD is limited. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationships among screen use, physical activity, sleep duration and LUTD among Thai children.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study the parents or guardians of children aged 3–6 years in Thailand anonymously answered the online questionnaires (Dysfunctional Voiding Symptom Score and the surveillance of digital media habits in early childhood questionnaire®). Multivariable analysis was used to identify associated factors.ResultsParticipants were 1,891 adult parents or guardians. The median age of the children was 4.9 years, and 53.8% were boys. Bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD) was highly prevalent among girls and young children. Female sex and high average screen use were associated with BBD, with odds ratios of 3.74 (95% CI: 2.96–4.74; P < 0.001) and 1.10 (95% CI: 1.03–1.16; P = 0.002), respectively. In subgroup analysis based on gender and age, the analysis revealed that prolonged screen use was significantly associated with BBD only in girls (Adjusted OR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.04–1.21; P = 0.002) and only in children aged 3 to < 5 years (Adjusted OR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.05–1.22; P = 0.001), with no significant association found in their counterparts. However, increased amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and short sleep duration were not associated to BBD.ConclusionsHigher screen use was associated with early lower urinary tract symptoms and bowel symptoms in preschool-kindergarten aged 3–<5 years and in girls. Parents and clinicians should be aware of the links between BBD and children’s prolonged screen use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06666-0.Keywords: Bladder and bowel dysfunction, Children, Physical activity, Screen time, Sleep

Introduction

Lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) is common among children, affecting 10%–20.9% of children globally [1, 2]. Clinical symptoms may include voiding or storage problems, such as incontinence, urgency, increased or decreased urinary frequency, hesitancy, or intermittency [3]. The cause of LUTD is delayed or incomplete maturation of the central nervous system or lower urinary tract [4]. Most LUTDs exist concurrently with constipation; this co-occurrence accounts for 35%–75% of pediatric cases of LUTD [5, 6]. Voiding disorders that accompany constipation or encopresis are defined as “bladder and bowel dysfunction” (BBD). Lower urinary tract problems may be overlooked in children, and LUTD can result in recurrent urinary tract infections, which may lead to renal scarring and chronic kidney disease. The Dysfunctional Voiding Symptom Score (DVSS) is a practical tool for detecting symptoms of LUTD. Factors related to LUTD include sex, obesity, sleep disorders, long-term use of disposable diapers, a history of urinary tract infection, and a family history of lower urinary tract symptoms [7–9], as well as behavioral and psychological issues [10].

Internet usage has increased significantly, particularly among children and adolescents, since the beginning of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [11]. Despite the American Academy of Pediatrics [12] and World Health Organization (WHO) [13] recommendation of limiting time spent on television and computers (screens) for preschool-aged children, their use of screens remains higher than 1 hour [14]. The effects of screen time on developmental and psychological issues in a short and longer term are deserving of research attention. In addition, children who use screens may have decreased physical activity, and being seated or sedentary for long periods can result in physical childhood problems such as obesity, sleep disorders, and constipation [15–17]. Urinary incontinence is less prevalent among women who are physically active than among those who are sedentary [18]. Furthermore, increased screen time among children may negatively affect their sleep duration or quality and contribute to increased body weight, which may be associated with BBD. However, the association between screen use, physical activity, sleep duration and LUTD in preschool children is unclear.

Preschool-age children found constipation or fecal incontinence linked to lower urinary symptoms (LUTS) in past studies [19]. Although BBD was diagnosed at more than 5 years, children aged 3–4 years capture the early emergence of voiding problems during the transitional phase of bladder control and many children can well control their bladder [20]. Behaviour or emotional problems, initiated toilet training after 24 months and difficult temperament in young children was associated with childhood urinary incontinence [21]. Long-term studies suggest that urinary urgency and frequency in preschool children are indicators of persistent LUTS later in childhood. Studying young children is crucial not to diagnose issues too early but to identify the social, environmental, and behavioral factors that lead to later LUTS. Therefore we investigated relationship between screen use, physical activity, sleep duration and BBD in preschool children which is crucial for early detection and prevention.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in preschools and kindergartens across Thailand from June to August 2022. Data were collected through surveys of parents and guardians of children aged between 3 and 6 years who did not have congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract, spinal or neurological diseases. A multistage cluster sampling approach was used. We chose four regions of Thailand and applied stratified sampling to select two provinces from each region. Within these selected provinces, three schools were randomly chosen, and the final sample included children from 24 schools. This methodology ensured coverage of varied geographical areas of the country. The selected preschools and kindergartens were contacted via electronic email or postal mail. After obtaining school consent, we enlisted teachers to assist in distributing the questionnaires to the children’s parents and guardians. The questionnaire packages, consisting of the surveillance of digital media habits in early childhood questionnaire (SMALLQ®) [22] and the DVSS questionnaire, were accessed and completed via a provided link or QR code, and the responses were stored on the Qualtrics platform (Qualtrics, Seattle, WA, USA). Consent was provided by 2,400 parents and guardians, of whom 1,891 (79%) completed the questionnaires.

Measurements

We used the Thai version of the 25-question SMALLQ®, which was translated from the original questionnaire in English developed by Chia et al. [22], to document demographic characteristics of children and parents or guardians and to record their physical activity and sleep habits. The translation, including adaptations of certain answer choices to fit Thai contexts, such as those related to parents’ nationality and household income was made by an expert in the physical activity team. The English version was translated to Thai through the forward–backward translation method based on guidelines of the WHO [23].

The demographic independent variables included the sex, age, weight status, and sibling status of the children and the nationality, education, and household income of their parents or guardians. Through the SMALLQ®, we gathered anthropometric data for children, including their weight in kilograms and height in centimeters, and used these data to compute body mass index and assess the children’s weight status according to the WHO’s child growth standards. Overweight and obesity are classified as exceeding + 1 and + 2 standard deviations, respectively, for age and sex, whereas underweight is defined as below 2 standard deviations for age and sex [24]. For caregiver education levels documented in the original questionnaire, we combined the categories of secondary school and postsecondary institution for consolidated analysis.

The questionnaire assesses screen time usage, encompassing both fixed-screen devices (e.g., televisions and desktop computers) and modern portable devices (e.g., smartphones, tablets, and handheld gaming consoles). The children’s screen habits were assessed from two primary perspectives: the age at which children first used screens and the time they spent on screens outside school (for entertainment, education, and communication). The average durations of both moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and sleep duration (recorded on both weekdays and weekends) were calculated through an analogous method.

BBD diagnosis was based on answers to the DVSS questionnaire, as translated into Thai by Piyaphanee et al. [25]. This version demonstrated strong validity and reliability: Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.86, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.906. The 10 questions were initially developed by Farhat et al. [26] and were designed for assessing issues related to bladder and bowel functions in children aged 3–10 years. The response options for indicating the severity of the problem range from “occurring nearly every day” to “occurring nearly every time” the child voided or defecated, scored on a scale of 0 (not severe) to 3 (extremely severe). The cut-off values for identifying BBD are total scores of 9 for boys and 6 for girls [25, 26]. These thresholds were chosen due to their high reported sensitivity and specificity in both the original and translated versions of the instrument [25, 26]. Although BBD is primarily applied to children aged more than 5 years, we included children aged 3–4 years to observe the early emergence of LUTS.

Ethical considerations

The research adhered to ethical principles and guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, confidentiality was maintained, and the study design and procedures were approved by the relevant institutional review board or ethics committee to ensure the protection of participants’ rights and well-being (COA 108/2565).

Statistical analysis

The categorical variables, including the children’s sex, weight, sibling status, and health information, were calculated as numbers and percentages. The chi-square test was used to identify differences between healthy children and those with BBD. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test revealed that the numerical data did not have a normal distribution; therefore, we calculated the data as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and used the Mann–Whitney U tests to determine the differences between the two groups of children. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess potential associated factors for BBD, we used univariable and multivariable logistic regression. The multivariable model included the variable with a P value < 0.05 from univariable analysis. While obesity and sibling is not statistically significant in univariable analysis, they were included in this model based on prior literature indicated the association with BBD [8]. We conducted a stratified analysis by gender (boys and girls) and age groups (3 to below 5 years and 5 years or above). The age cut-off was determined based on diagnostic guidelines for BBD [3]. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed within each subgroup. The multivariable models included the same factors as in the overall analysis, excluding sex and age, based on the specific subgroup. To evaluate multicollinearity, we used variance inflation factors, for which < 5 was considered an acceptable value. R software, version 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023, Vienna, Austria), was used to analyze the data.

Results

The questionnaires were completed by 1,891 adults of children, of whom 430 (22.73%) had BBD. The prevalence of BBD among children aged 5–6 years was 17.9%. The median age of the children was 4.9 years, and the range was 3–6.9 years; 53.8% were boys. BBD was more prevalent among younger children and among girls. We found no statistically significant differences in weight, sibling status, or parent characteristics between children with and without BBD (Table 1).

Discussion

The study explored the factors linked to BBD in preschool and kindergarten children, emphasizing three main aspects: screen use, physical activity, and sleep duration. This approach is aligned with monitoring activity compliance to the Asia–Pacific consensus statement on integrated 24-hour activity guidelines for early childhood [27].

The study sought to assess the prevalence of BBD among Thai children using the DVSS questionnaire, revealing a prevalence rate of 22.73% and 17.9% in age 5–6 years. This figure is notably higher than the 8.6% reported by Sumboonnanonda et al. [1]. Two main factors may explain this discrepancy: first, the study was conducted in a school environment, akin to a Brazilian study by Vaz et al. [28], which found a similar prevalence of 21.8%. Second, this study specifically targeted younger children (ages 3–6), whereas Sumboonnanonda et al. included a wider age range of 4–12 years.

The study revealed that the prevalence of BBD declines as individuals age, with each passing year lowering the risk by 23%. This finding is consistent with the observations made by Xu et al. [29] in China. Additionally, Verkuijl et al. [19] emphasize the need to tackle BBD in infants and young children to prevent it from continuing into older age groups.

While the prevalence of BBD varied between two studies conducted in Thailand, both studies identified female sex as a significant factor associated with BBD, a conclusion also supported by Westwell-Roper et al. [30]. Kajbafzadeh et al. [31] observed that girls exhibited more joint hypermobility than boys, which might be related to pelvic floor laxity and voiding dysfunction. Furthermore, girls may be more inclined to report symptoms, which could lead to earlier detection of issues such as LUTD. Other factors, including school schedules, social situations, and stress, may also play a role in making girls more susceptible to these conditions. We diagnosed BBD in children using the validated DVSS which had validity and reliability in age 3–10 years [26]. While DVSS is effective for initial screening, clinical settings require supplemental tools for definitive diagnosis. Analyzing symptom severity as continuous (vs. categorical) variables better captures nuanced associations. Combining questionnaires, clinical exams, and follow-ups improves diagnostic precision and holistic BBD understanding.

Prolonged screen time is associated with negative cognitive neurodevelopment, mental health, and physical health. The median average screen time of children with and without BBD was slightly different (2.02 vs. 2.00 h) in this study, but the difference was statistically significant. This may be due to the non-normal distribution of the data and the large sample size. Given the large sample (n = 1891), this small but consistent difference may still be meaningful in context. Our findings demonstrate increasing screen use is related to one of physical health: LUTD. Poor sitting posture and long hours in front of screens can lead to pelvic floor dysfunction and urinary problems. It’s essential to maintain good posture for the proper alignment of pelvic organs and to support the muscles. Sitting for extended periods, which is often the case in school environments, can interfere with fluid balance and bladder habits. Moreover, emotional stress, which can arise from too much screen time, might also impact bladder function. Research by Chien et al. [32] and Benzamin et al. [33] indicates that sedentary lifestyles, particularly those involving prolonged screen use, are linked to a higher risk of constipation and LUTD. Furthermore, constipation can affect bladder function by causing fecal retention in the rectum, which reduces bladder capacity and increases detrusor overactivity, [4] potentially explaining the elevated risk of BBD in children who spend more time on screens.

Screen time might play a role in behavioral issues among children, as a meta-analysis has found a link to a higher risk of attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [34], which is often seen in children with BBD [10, 35]. The mechanism underlying the relationship between ADHD and BBD remains uncertain, but it could be linked to the natural hyperactivity response when children with ADHD feel the urge to urinate. This idea is backed by a study from Yang et al. [35], which revealed that children experiencing both ADHD and BBD had higher scores on the statement “I cannot wait when I have to pee”.

Notably, our study found that prolonged screen use was a significant risk factor for BBD in specific subgroups: girls and younger children (aged 3 to < 5 years). This suggests that these groups may be particularly vulnerable to the effects of screen use. Female trend to have mental health problem that one of the risk of BBD when use screen time compare to male [36, 37]. This finding was also observed in preschool use screen time of 2–4 h/day [38]. This finding could support tailored public health interventions targeting these specific groups to mitigate the risk of voiding and bowel symptoms associated with screen time.

In a population-based cross-sectional survey, Qiu et al. [18] discovered that engaging in recreational physical activity was protective against overall urinary incontinence in Chinese women. This finding suggested that the mechanism underlying this protective effect may be attributed to the enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes, which could lead to improvements in pelvic floor and bladder function. However, the pathogenesis of BBD in children is more likely to be associated with a delay in the maturation of voiding function [4]. This could explain why we found no significant association between moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and BBD. In addition, this study showed that age was a protective factor for BBD because when children grow up, the maturity of the central nervous system and bladder function is consolidated.

Short sleep duration and sleep problems are known to impact brain development, including cognitive function, mental health, and the risk of obesity [39, 40]. However, in this study, no significant association was observed between sleep duration and BBD. This finding is consistent with previous research by Von Gontard et al. [41], who reported similar results. Conversely, a prospective study demonstrated that sleep problems or reduced sleep duration at age 3.5 years were associated with trajectories of daytime urinary incontinence [42].

There are strengths and limitations in the present study. This study appears to be the first to identify a link between screen use and BBD within the framework of our nationwide school-based research using valid questionnaires. One limitation is that it was a cross-sectional analytical investigation, which means we cannot definitively determine a cause-and-effect relationship between extended seated screen use and BBD. Although children older than 3 years are typically expected to have developed daytime bladder control, we did not evaluate the toilet training status, which may affect the diagnosis of BBD in this group. Children aged 3–4 years, for whom International Children’s Continence Society criteria may not strictly apply. Symptoms in younger children may reflect physiological maturation rather than clinical dysfunction. Consequently, findings for this age group were interpreted with caution. Future research should involve a longitudinal study with a broader age range to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

The present study indicates that female sex and extended screen use are linked to voiding and bowel symptoms in young children. Parents and physicians should consider limiting screen usage. Recommendations should focus on monitoring screen use and providing support to parents and teachers in implementing activity breaks from screens. This approach could benefit young children’s development in various ways, such as reducing LUTD, preserving eye health and reducing myopia, improving weight management, and enhancing motor skills. Further research is needed to explore interventions aimed at lowering the risk of LUTD in these vulnerable groups of children.
