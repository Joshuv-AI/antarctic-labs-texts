Non-linear associations between red blood cell distribution width/serum albumin ratio with diabetic kidney disease: results from two nationwide studies in the United States and China
Authors: Yao Cheng, Pan Yan, Hongyu Yue, Chongyang Han, Baodong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to better investigate the association between the red blood cell distribution width to serum albumin ratio (RA) and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) by utilizing data from the United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and clinical data from Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanxi Medical University, China (SMU). A total of 3,734 and 393 participants were selected from NHANES (2011–2018) and SMU, respectively. The primary diagnosis of DKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urinary albumin-creatinine-ratio (ACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, propensity score matching (PSM), and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis were used to evaluate the relationship between RA levels and DKD. The prevalence of DKD was estimated to be 40.38% in NHANES and 54.96% in SMU among diabetic patients. After full adjustment, higher RA levels were significantly associated with increased DKD risk in both cohorts (NHANES: OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.13–1.87; SMU: OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.01–1.60). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between RA and all-cause mortality in DKD patients (P for nonlinearity < 0.001), with an optimal cutoff of 3.33dL/g. Patients with RA ≥ 3.33dL/g had significantly lower cumulative survival rates (log-rank P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations across most strata. Higher RA level is independently associated with increased risk and poor prognosis of DKD in diabetic patients. An RA cutoff of 3.33dL/g may serve as a useful clinical marker for risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45999-9.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disease with high incidence, and its prevalence has been continuously increasing in recent years. Diabetes can lead to various complications such as kidney disease, diabetic foot, coronary heart disease, retinopathy, and atherosclerosis. Among these, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the most common and serious complication, and it is also a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) globally. Approximately 30% to 50% of ESRD cases worldwide are attributable to DKD1, and DKD has become the primary cause of ESRD in middle-aged and elderly populations in China2 DKD, along with diabetic retinopathy and neuropathy, is considered a form of microvascular complication. Studies indicate that about 30% of patients with type 1 diabetes and about 40% of those with type 2 diabetes develop such microvascular complications3. Furthermore, research has shown that compared to non-diabetic individuals, the lifespan of diabetic patients is reduced4. When diabetes is complicated by DKD, the risk of cardiovascular diseases5, COVID-196, and other conditions increases.

The red blood cell distribution width (RDW) normally ranges from 11% to 15% and is a parameter reflecting the heterogeneity of red blood cells (RBCs) measured by hematology analyzers7. A higher RDW value indicates greater variation in the size and shape of RBCs, and elevated RDW is associated with severe disturbances in erythrocyte homeostasis, as well as abnormalities in RBC production and survival. Simultaneously, increased RDW may result from mechanisms such as oxidative stress and vascular inflammation8. Previous studies have found that RDW is generally elevated in patients with diabetes and its complications, and excessively high RDW may increase mortality related to diabetes, macrovascular, and microvascular diseases. In addition, albumin is closely related to inflammation9,10. Albumin, produced by hepatocytes, accounts for 40%–60% of total plasma proteins11 and is closely linked to nutritional status. It is considered a reliable predictor of outcomes in critically ill patients with infections. The RA ratio, defined as RDW to albumin ratio, is a novel inflammatory biomarker. It was initially used to assess prognosis in patients with solid stroke9 and acute respiratory distress syndrome12.

The pathogenesis of DKD is complex, with factors such as hyperglycemia, lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia proven to influence its progression13. Diabetes is also recognized as an inflammatory disease. Hyperglycemia can induce cellular damage, promote the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, and lead to the accumulation of inflammatory cells in the kidneys, ultimately resulting in declined renal function. Therefore, inflammation plays a crucial role in the development of DKD14. In this study, we utilized data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and Shanxi Medical University Hospital (SMU) to evaluate the relationship between RA levels and diabetic kidney disease across broader ranges of age, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, aiming to provide a new biomarker option for the early identification of DKD.

Materials and methods

Study design and populations

The NHANES is a program of studies designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The survey became a continuous program in 1990 and recruits a nationally representative sample of approximately 5,000 participants from 15 counties each year. The NHANES cross-sectional questionnaire collects data on demographics, socioeconomic status, diet, and health-related factors. The examination component includes medical and physiological measurements, and laboratory tests. The website provides a detailed introduction (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about_ nhanes.htm).The NHANES study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. All NHANES data collection procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (e.g., the Declaration of Helsinki). The NHANES data are publicly accessible, with released demographic, examination, and laboratory data updated through 2018. In this study, we initially screened 39,156 participants from NHANES (2011–2018). After applying the eligibility criteria, 3,734 participants from NHANES were included in the final cohort, excluding those under 18 years of age (N = 15,331), participants with missing red blood cell distribution width (RDW) or albumin data (N = 2,541), missing urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (N = 310), and individuals without diabetes (N = 17,240) (Fig. 1).

The SMU cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. 764).All methods in this part of the study were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Using a retrospective cohort design, 393 patients with type 2 diabetes who visited the hospital between January 2024 and December 2024 were included. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) coexistence of other kidney diseases, such as anti-glomerular basement membrane nephritis, IgA nephropathy, or membranous nephropathy; (2) autoimmune diseases; (3) heart failure (New York Heart Association class II–IV); (4) acute infection or inflammatory diseases; and (5) recent use of medications that may affect RDW or albumin levels, such as erythropoietin or glucocorticoids. Ultimately, 393 participants were enrolled, among whom 216 were diagnosed with diabetic kidney disease (Fig. 1).

Measurement of red blood cell distribution width and serum albumin

Venous blood was collected from participants after an overnight fast to determine the red blood cell distribution width to serum albumin ratio (RA). RA was calculated using the unified formula: RA = RDW (%) / Serum Albumin (g/dL). In the NHANES cohort, albumin was reported in g/dL and used directly. In the SMU cohort, albumin was originally reported in g/L; to ensure direct comparability with NHANES, all SMU albumin values were converted to g/dL by dividing by 10 prior to RA calculation (e.g., 40 g/L → 4.0 g/dL). The resulting RA is expressed in units of dL/g for dimensional consistency, and the numerical values are directly comparable across cohorts. The normal reference range for red blood cell distribution width (RDW) is 11.5%–14.5%, and for serum albumin it is 3.5–5.5 g/dL; a serum albumin level < 3.5 g/dL was defined as hypoalbuminemia. Albumin concentration was measured using the DxC800 and LX20 analyzers based on a bichromatic digital endpoint method. In the reaction, the albumin combined with Bromcresol Purple (BCP) reagent to form a complex. Detailed analytical procedures are available on the NHANES website. The measurement protocol complies with the requirements of the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988.

Endpoint definition

Due to inconsistencies in endpoint definitions with other datasets and the absence of key survival data, the SMU cohort was excluded from analyses of all-cause mortality and survival curves. Only the NHANES cohort was used for these assessments.

Diabetes diagnosis in NHANES was determined using a composite of glycemic indicators (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L), self-reported physician diagnosis, and current use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents, in accordance with the American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2023 standards15.DKD in NHANES was defined strictly according to laboratory criteria following the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) clinical practice guidelines16: the presence of either (i) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, or (ii) urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥30 mg/g.The time of the endpoint was defined as the time of death, the time of missing follow-up or the last follow-up time, which depended on the event which firstly occurred among them. Participants in NHANES cohort were followed from the date of enrollment prospectively until December 31, 201917. Mortality data were obtained from the National Death Index death certificate records and matched using the following identifiers: Social Security number, name, date of birth, race/ethnicity, sex, birth status, and residence status18.

In the SMU cohort, DKD was defined using the same eGFR threshold (< 60mL/min/1.73 m²) or 24-hour urinary protein excretion ≥ 0.5 g/24 h. This threshold is considered clinically equivalent to UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, as supported by multiple lines of evidence: Empirical studies (e.g., Zelmanovitz et al., 1998)19 demonstrate an excellent correlation (r = 0.95) between 24-hour urinary protein and albumin excretion, with a cutoff of 541 mg/24 h achieving 100% sensitivity for overt diabetic nephropathy.Chinese guidelines for diabetic kidney disease (2021)20 explicitly list 24-hour urinary protein ≥ 0.5 g as an alternative diagnostic criterion when UACR is unavailable.KDIGO 2024 guidelines21 maintain that ACR ≥ 30 mg/g corresponds to albumin excretion rate ≥ 30 mg/24 h, and since albumin is the predominant component of urinary protein in most kidney diseases, a total protein excretion ≥ 0.5 g/24 h inevitably reflects a substantial increase in albuminuria.

Covariates

Variables selected for adjustment included: age, sex (male or female), poverty‑to‑income ratio (PIR), smoking status (never, former, or current), hypertension, body mass index (BMI), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum uric acid (SUA), high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‑C), and low‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‑C). Hypertension was defined as a physician‑diagnosed history of hypertension or measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90mmHg22. Smoking status was categorized as never smoked (< 100 cigarettes in lifetime), former smoker (> 100 cigarettes previously but not currently smoking), or current smoker (> 100 cigarettes and still smoking).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Missing data were handled via multiple imputation using the mice package (version 3.15.0) with 5 imputations; predictive mean matching (PMM) was applied for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables. Due to the complexity of the sampling survey, weighted analyses were conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the NHANES database. For the NHANES cohort, all analyses accounted for the complex multistage cluster survey design by incorporating sample weights, stratification, and clustering variables as per the analytical guidelines. Weighted means (with standard errors) were calculated for continuous variables, and weighted percentages (with 95% confidence intervals) for categorical variables to ensure nationally representative estimates. Unweighted sample sizes (n) are provided in the tables for transparency. For the SMU cohort, standard descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, n (%)) were used.Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non‑normally distributed continuous data are expressed as median (P25, P75) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as number (percentage) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between the red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RA) and diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Three progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 – unadjusted; Model 2 – adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race, education level, smoking status); Model 3 – additionally adjusted for clinical indicators (BMI, blood pressure, lipid profile, renal function parameters, etc.). Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline differences between groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including the following covariates: gender, age, smoking status, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), hypertension, body mass index (BMI), serum uric acid, blood urea nitrogen, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, serum creatinine, and eGFR. RDW and albumin were not included in the model because they are integral components of the RA ratio used to define the exposure groups; including them would artificially balance the grouping criteria and contradict the study objective. We used 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper of 0.02 applied to the logit of the propensity score. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and Love plots were used to assess covariate balance before and after matching. Post-matching analyses were performed using conditional logistic regression or models with robust standard errors to account for the matched design.A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model with 3 knots was employed to examine the potential nonlinear relationship between RA and mortality risk.Covariates for multivariable adjustment were selected based on established risk factors for diabetic kidney disease (DKD) identified in prior epidemiological studies and clinical guidelines. These included demographic characteristics (age, gender), socioeconomic status (PIR), behavioral factors (smoking), clinical factors (hypertension, BMI), lipid profile (HDL, LDL), and kidney function markers (serum creatinine [Scr], blood urea nitrogen [BUN], eGFR). The inclusion of multiple renal markers was intended to comprehensively capture different dimensions of kidney function, as each reflects distinct physiological aspects (e.g., glomerular filtration, tubular function, muscle mass). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) in the SMU cohort and generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF) with the adjusted measure GVIF^ (1/ (2×Df)) in the NHANES cohort (to account for categorical variables), with values below 10 considered acceptable.Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group differences were compared with the log-rank test. Additionally, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality associated with RA levels. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; no evidence of violation was detected (global test p = 1.000; RA p = 0.959; Supplementary Fig. 3).Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential effect modifiers of the association between RA and DKD, and the heterogeneity of associations across subgroups, as well as interaction effects, were assessed.All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

This study included two independent cohorts: the NHANES cohort and the SMU cohort. In the NHANES cohort (Table 1), a total of 3,734 eligible diabetic patients were enrolled, comprising 1,780 females and 1,954 males, with a mean age of 58.86±13.86 years. The median level of the red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RA) was 3.38±0.51 dL/g . Compared with non-DKD patients, those with DKD showed a higher prevalence of hypertension, significantly elevated levels of RA, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (Scr), and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (all P < 0.05).

In the SMU cohort (Table 1), 393 diabetic patients were included, with 258 females and 135 males, a mean age of 55.21±11.43 years, and a mean RA level of 3.39±0.14dL/g . DKD patients similarly exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension, along with significantly higher RA, BUN, and Scr levels, and lower eGFR (all P < 0.05). Significant differences were also noted between groups in smoking history, hypertension, RA, BUN, Scr, and eGFR (all P < 0.05).

Within these cohorts, 1,508 and 216 patients were diagnosed with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in the NHANES and SMU cohorts, respectively.Additionally, statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, smoking history, hypertension, RA, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), albumin (Alb), uric acid (UA), BUN, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), Scr, and eGFR (all P < 0.05).

Logistic regression

As shown in Table 2, we employed multivariable logistic regression models to assess the associations of RA, RDW, ALB, and RA quartiles with DKD. In the NHANES cohort, RA was significantly and positively associated with the risk of DKD. The odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for RA across the three models in NHANES were 2.00 (1.63–2.45), 1.93 (1.59–2.34), and 1.46 (1.13–1.87), respectively. RDW showed an association in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.15–1.34), but this association was attenuated and became non-significant in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.99–1.15). ALB consistently demonstrated a protective effect, with an OR of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.39–0.83) in the fully adjusted model. Analysis by RA quartiles (RAQ) revealed a progressive increase in risk with higher quartiles. Participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 65% increased risk of DKD (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.12–2.43) even after full adjustment. In the SMU cohort, the association between RA and DKD remained significant after full adjustment (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.01–1.60). However, when RA was analyzed as quartiles, although a trend toward increased risk was observed in the higher quartiles, the association did not reach statistical significance in the fully adjusted model (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 0.94–3.13, P = 0.083). Notably, RDW did not show a statistically significant association in the fully adjusted models of either cohort.These results indicate that after adjusting for multiple potential confounders, RA remains independently associated with the risk of DKD in both cohorts when analyzed as a continuous variable. The quartile analysis in the SMU cohort showed a consistent trend but did not achieve statistical significance, which may be attributable to the smaller sample size and warrants further investigation in larger studies.

Sensitivity and multicollinearity analyses

To assess the robustness of the association between RA and DKD and to evaluate potential over-adjustment, we conducted sensitivity analyses by (1) excluding serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen from Model 3, and (2) additionally adjusting for HbA1c (Supplementary Table 7). In the NHANES cohort, the OR for RA changed minimally from 1.46 (95% CI: 1.13–1.87) in the original model to 1.33 (95% CI: 1.02–1.74) after excluding renal markers and 1.35 (95% CI: 1.03–1.77) after additional adjustment for HbA1c, with all estimates remaining statistically significant (all P < 0.05). In the SMU cohort, the association remained directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses, though the effect size attenuated (OR range: 1.10–1.18), likely attributable to the smaller sample size and higher missing rate for HbA1c (> 40%).

Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for the fully adjusted model in both cohorts (Supplementary Table 8). In the NHANES cohort, all adjusted generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF^ (1/ (2×Df))) were below the recommended threshold of 10, with the primary exposure variable RA showing a value of 1.06, indicating no concerning collinearity. As expected, serum creatinine and eGFR exhibited moderate collinearity (adjusted GVIF = 6.13 and 6.56, respectively), which is biologically inevitable given that eGFR is derived from serum creatinine. In the SMU cohort, all VIFs were below 5, with RA having a VIF of 1.31. These results confirm that multicollinearity does not compromise the stability or interpretability of our regression models.

We further assessed the incremental predictive value of RA for DKD by comparing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) between the base model (Model 3 without RA) and the full model (Model 3 with RA). As shown in Supplementary Table 9, the addition of RA resulted in a modest improvement in discrimination in both cohorts. In the NHANES cohort, the AUC increased from 0.821 to 0.822 (Δ AUC = + 0.002), while in the SMU cohort, the AUC increased from 0.807 to 0.819 (Δ AUC = + 0.012). These findings suggest that RA provides incremental predictive value beyond established risk factors for DKD risk stratification.

To further evaluate whether RA provides independent information beyond albumin alone, we included albumin in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) and assessed the interaction between RA and albumin (Supplementary Table 10). In the NHANES cohort, RA remained significantly associated with DKD after additional adjustment for albumin (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.20–2.09, P = 0.0014), confirming its independent contribution. In the SMU cohort, the association was attenuated and became non-significant (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.88–1.43, P = 0.368), likely attributable to the smaller sample size and more severe disease profile of hospitalized patients. Importantly, the interaction term between RA and albumin was non-significant in both cohorts (NHANES: P for interaction = 0.280; SMU: P for interaction = 0.236), indicating that the effect of RA does not vary across albumin levels and ruling out a simple scaling artifact.

Propensity score matching analysis

To further minimize confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed (Supplementary Tables 5–6). After matching, baseline characteristics between the high- and low-RA groups were substantially improved in both cohorts, as visualized in the Love plot (Supplementary Fig. 2)In the NHANES cohort, 1,419 matched pairs were retained from the original 3,734 participants (468 from the low-RA group and 428 from the high-RA group were excluded).Before matching, patients in the high-RA group (RA ≥ 3.33dL/g) were older, had a higher prevalence of hypertension, and exhibited significantly poorer renal function indicators compared to the low-RA group (RA < 3.33dL/g) (all P < 0.05). After PSM adjustment, all baseline characteristics were balanced between the two groups (all P > 0.05) except for RDW and albumin levels. An elevated RA (≥ 3.33dL/g) remained independently associated with DKD (OR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.38–1.88, p < 0.001).In the SMU cohort, 91 matched pairs were retained after matching (excluding 103 from the low-RA group and 108 from the high-RA group). Post-matching balance was substantially improved, although a few covariates—serum uric acid (SMD = 0.125), blood urea nitrogen (SMD = 0.188), serum creatinine (SMD = 0.173), and eGFR (SMD = 0.110)—showed SMDs slightly above 0.1. However, none of these variables demonstrated statistically significant differences between groups after matching (all P > 0.05). The residual imbalance is likely attributable to the relatively small sample size (N = 91 per group). As in the NHANES cohort, RDW and albumin remained significantly different (P < 0.001). Higher RA (≥ 3.23) was significantly associated with increased DKD risk after matching (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.38–1.80, P < 0.001) using conditional logistic regression.

The persistent differences in RDW and albumin suggest they may be independently associated with RA levels.

Prevalence of DKD across RA Quartiles in NHANES and SMU Cohorts

A comparative analysis of DKD prevalence across RA quartiles (RAQ) was conducted for both cohorts (Supplementary Table 2). A clear dose-response relationship was observed, with DKD prevalence increasing significantly across ascending RA quartiles (P for trend < 0.001 in both cohorts). NHANES and SMU demonstrated consistent trends, with the prevalence in the highest quartile (Q4) being 1.84-fold and 1.35-fold higher than in Q1, respectively. This consistency across independent cohorts indicates a stable positive association between RA levels and DKD risk. Notably, the DKD prevalence exceeded 50% in the highest RA quartile for both cohorts, a finding of substantial clinical concern. These data provide epidemiological evidence supporting the utility of RA for DKD risk stratification.

Survival analysis

A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to analyze the non-linear relationship between RA and mortality risk in DKD patients from the NHANES cohort (Fig. 2A). The results indicated a significant non-linear association (P for non-linearity < 0.001), with an optimal RA cutoff value of 3.33dL/g determined by the RCS model. Based on this cutoff, patients were stratified into a high-RA group (RA ≥ 3.33dL/g) and a low-RA group (RA < 3.33dL/g). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that the cumulative survival rate was significantly lower in the high-RA group compared to the low-RA group (log-rank test, P < 0.05), suggesting that elevated RA levels are significantly associated with poor prognosis in DKD patients.

All-cause mortality

Baseline characteristics

The most recent linked mortality data from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) extends through December 2019. Among the 3,734 diabetic patients included from the 2011–2018 NHANES cycles, 432 (11.6%) experienced all-cause mortality (Supplementary Table 3). Compared to survivors within the diabetic kidney disease (DKD) group, non-survivors showed significant differences in age, income-to-poverty ratio, race, education level, smoking status, hypertension, body mass index (BMI), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), serum uric acid (SUA), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) (all P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed for sex or hyperlipidemia (all P > 0.05).

Multivariable analysis

In this study, Kaplan-Meier curves were used to evaluate the prognostic significance of the red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RA) in diabetic patients (Supplementary Fig. 1). The results indicated that a higher RA level was associated with a significantly greater risk of all-cause mortality in patients with DKD (P < 0.05). Three Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to analyze the relationship between RA and all-cause mortality in DKD patients (Supplementary Table 4). In the unadjusted Model 1, higher RA levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (P < 0.05). In the fully adjusted Model 3, the hazard ratio (HR) for RA was 2.21 (95% CI: 1.84–2.64). When RA was categorized into quartiles, the results showed that being in the upper 50% (i.e., the 3rd and 4th quartiles) was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in DKD patients (P < 0.05).The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox models was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. No evidence of violation was detected (global test p = 1.000; RA p = 0.959; Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming that the hazard ratios can be interpreted as constant over the follow-up period.

Subgroup analysis

Stratified analyses were performed according to age, sex, smoking status, eGFR, ACR, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, with adjustments based on Model 3. The results revealed a significant association between RA and DKD across all strata of age and hypertension (all P < 0.05). For sex, the association was significant in males (P < 0.05) but did not reach statistical significance in females (P = 0.066). For smoking status, hyperlipidemia, eGFR, and ACR, the association was significant only in certain subgroups (P < 0.05). Interaction tests indicated no significant effect modification by age, sex, smoking status, eGFR, ACR, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia on the relationship between RA and DKD (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that the red blood cell distribution width to albumin ratio (RA) was significantly higher in participants with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) compared to those with diabetes alone. This association remained robust after comprehensive adjustment for multiple covariates. Although the link between individual components (RDW, albumin) and diabetic complications has been reported previously23, our findings are novel and significant in several key aspects. First, we characterized a non-linear relationship between RA and DKD, identifying an optimal clinical cutoff to enhance its utility in risk stratification.Notably, this cutoff of 3.33dL/g dL/g aligns with findings from a recent independent study by Zou et al.24 which analyzed NHANES data from 2005 to 2020 (N = 7,191) and also demonstrated a significant association between RA and DKD, with risk stratification patterns consistent with our threshold. Although Zou et al. reported a linear relationship after full adjustment, whereas we observed a non-linear association for all-cause mortality in DKD patients, this discrepancy likely reflects differences in the study outcome (prevalence vs. mortality) and the specific population examined. Together, these convergent results provide external validation for the clinical relevance of the RA cutoff. Second, by systematically collecting and analyzing demographic, behavioral, and extensive biochemical data within a multi-variable framework, we performed a more rigorous evaluation of RA ’s independent effect. Third, we demonstrated a significant association between elevated RA and increased all-cause mortality in DKD patients, and the consistency of this association across multiple subgroups strengthens the reliability of our results.

The pathogenesis of DKD is not fully elucidated, but inflammatory mechanisms have garnered increasing attention. Studies have shown that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α are involved in the development of DKD25. However, these specific markers are costly and complex to measure, limiting their clinical utility. Inflammation can promote erythrocyte heterogeneity by affecting erythropoiesis, red cell half-life, and deformability, leading to increased RDW26,27. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can reduce renal synthesis of erythropoietin (EPO), impair erythrocyte production and survival, and disrupt iron metabolism28. Albumin also plays a crucial role in modulating the inflammatory response29 and is a reliable prognostic indicator in critical illness. The rate of albumin synthesis is inversely correlated with the severity of inflammation30. Albumin can downregulate the expression of TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, CRP, and MMP-8, thereby mitigating the “inflammatory storm“31.The RA ratio, integrating both RDW and albumin, represents a novel, readily available, and composite inflammatory marker. Its specific role in DKD warrants further investigation but shows promise given its connection to the inflammatory pathways implicated in disease progression.

As an indicator of systemic inflammation, RA has been linked to various inflammatory conditions. For instance, Sheng P et al. found RA to be an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)32. Chen H et al., comparing multiple inflammatory-nutritional indices for diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), reported that RA demonstrated superior predictive performance33. Furthermore, Huang JC et al. observed that RA showed a stronger association and higher predictive accuracy for cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality compared to RDW alone34.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective observational design, a causal relationship between RA and DKD cannot be established. Prospective studies are needed to address this. Second, our analysis relied on a single blood measurement. Given the limited lifespan of blood cells, serial measurements might provide more accurate risk assessment than a one-time test. Third, although we adjusted for numerous potential confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors remains a concern. In the NHANES cohort, we were unable to adjust for C-reactive protein (due to cycle-specific missing data), iron studies, detailed medication use (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors), and direct nutritional indicators such as prealbumin. In the SMU cohort, HbA1c was missing in > 40% of patients, medication histories were not systematically recorded, inflammatory markers were measured only in a small subset, and liver function tests were incomplete; these limitations precluded more comprehensive adjustment. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses excluding renal function markers and additionally adjusting for HbA1c (in NHANES) yielded consistent estimates, supporting the robustness of our findings.Importantly, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our findings against potential over-adjustment bias. Formal multicollinearity diagnostics further confirmed that the observed associations were not inflated by collinearity among covariates (Supplementary Table 8).Fourth, this cohort study identifies associations but does not elucidate underlying mechanisms; further experimental or animal model studies are required. Finally, potential biases exist. In the NHANES cohort, recall bias is possible due to self-reported data. In the SMU clinical cohort, selection bias is likely, as hospitalized patients may represent more severe cases, potentially leading to different baseline health status and higher mortality rates compared to the population-based NHANES cohort. These differences between cohorts should be considered when interpreting the results.In conclusion, our study, utilizing both a nationally representative US survey and real-world clinical data from China with mutually consistent findings, suggests that RA is a significant and independent biomarker associated with DKD risk and prognosis. Future research involving larger, multi-ethnic, and prospective cohorts, as well as investigations into the mechanistic role of RA, is warranted to confirm these findings and explore clinical applications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a significant positive association between RA levels and the risk and poor prognosis of DKD in diabetic patients. Higher RA levels, particularly exceeding the threshold of 3.33dL/g, were linked to increased prevalence of DKD and worse clinical outcomes. These findings indicate the necessity of routinely monitoring RA in diabetic populations in clinical practice. Maintaining RA below the identified threshold may help reduce the incidence of DKD and improve patient prognosis. Furthermore, an RA level of 3.33dL/g may serve as an important prognostic cutoff to guide risk stratification and early intervention in diabetic patients.
