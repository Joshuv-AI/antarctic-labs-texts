Comparative Study of Medication Adherence and Glycemic Control in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Patients on Oral Hypoglycemics versus Insulin Therapy
Authors: Arpana Ashokbhai Patel, Rohan Mody, Jay Priteshkumar Shukla, Dhruv Chaudhari, Nirmit Udaybhai Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Adherence to medication is a critical factor that affects the glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and influences both short-term and long-term complications. The objective of the study was to assess the impact of medication adherence and glycemic control in patients with T2DM on oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs) and insulin.Materials and Methods:This 12-month prospective study involved 220 T2DM patients at a tertiary care hospital. The study cohort was divided into those on OHAs (n = 120) and insulin therapy (n = 100). Adherence was measured by the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale, and glycemic control was measured by levels of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).Results:The average age of the participants was 55.2 ± 11.8 years, and males were overrepresented (60%). Adherence was high among 68% of patients receiving OHAs and 52% of those receiving insulin (P = 0.02). The average HbA1c was lower in the OHA group (7.4% ±1.1%) than in the insulin group (8.2% ± 1.4%, P < 0.001). Glycemic control was also better in patients with high adherence (HbA1c 7.1% ± 0.9%) than low adherence (8.6% ± 1.3%, P < 0.001). Complexity of treatment, dose frequency, and injection fear were some factors that affected adherence in the insulin group.Conclusion:This study demonstrates that adherence is higher in patients treated with OHA and that it is associated with better glycemic control. Interventions to improve adherence in the insulin group are crucial for better management of diabetes.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disease resulting in chronic hyperglycemia due to insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction.[1] It represents a significant global health burden, with a growing incidence and complications, including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, and retinopathy.[2]

Good glycemic control is crucial to prevent complications, and drug therapy is the mainstay of treatment. Treatment options include the use of oral hypoglycemic agents (OHAs) or insulin, depending on the stage of the disease.[3] Although insulin is a highly effective medication for glycemic control, it can be burdensome due to injection pain, the risk of hypoglycemia, and complex dosage instructions.[4]

Adherence to medication is crucial to the success of the treatment of T2DM. Nonadherence is associated with suboptimal glycemic control, higher rates of hospitalization, and increased healthcare expenditure.[5] The research has demonstrated different levels of medication adherence in patients receiving oral medications versus insulin.[6]

While effective therapies are available, there is a lack of research on the relative patterns of adherence and associated glycemic control in these two patient groups, especially in clinical practice.[7]

As a consequence, this study sought to compare medication adherence and glycemic control in T2DM patients on oral hypoglycemic medications versus insulin.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corps, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as percentages. Independent t-test and Chi-square test were used for comparisons. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The mean age of participants was 55.2 ± 11.8 years. Males constituted 60% (n = 132), and females constituted 40% (n = 88). The mean duration of diabetes was 8.1 ± 3.6 years.

Medication adherence

High adherence was significantly higher in the OHA group (68%) compared to the insulin group (52%) (P = 0.02).

Glycemic Control and Adherence

Patients with high adherence had significantly better glycemic control compared to those with low adherence.

DISCUSSION

This study compared medication adherence and blood glucose levels in patients with T2DM receiving OHAs versus insulin. The study found that medication adherence was significantly greater in the OHA group than in the insulin group.

This finding is consistent with other studies that have shown higher adherence with oral medications compared to injectables, which are often complex and associated with discomfort.[1] Insulin, while highly effective, is associated with several obstacles, including fear of injections, complicated dose regimens, and the risk of hypoglycemia.[2]

The lower HbA1c levels in the OHA group indicate improved glycemic control, possibly due to higher adherence. Previous studies have also highlighted the association between adherence and glycemic control.[3]

It was also shown that patients with high adherence achieved better glycemic control than those with low adherence (P < 0.001). This highlights the role of adherence as a critical factor in the success of therapy for diabetes.[4]

Patients in the insulin group had higher HbA1c despite strong medication. This could be related to reduced adherence and the complexity of insulin therapy. Earlier studies have shown that adherence problems can offset the advantages of insulin treatment.[5]

Further, the longer duration of diabetes in the insulin group may have led to poorer control, as the progression of diabetes requires the use of insulin and is associated with decreased β-cell function.[6]

The results highlight the importance of diabetes education, counselling, and minimalist insulin regimens to improve adherence, especially in the insulin group. Insulin pens, continuous glucose monitoring, and diabetes education may improve adherence.[7]

CONCLUSION

The study demonstrates that medication adherence is significantly higher among patients on OHAs compared to those on insulin therapy and is strongly associated with better glycemic control. Insulin-treated patients exhibited lower adherence and poorer glycemic outcomes, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to improve adherence. Enhancing patient education, simplifying treatment regimens, and strengthening support systems are essential to optimize diabetes management and improve clinical outcomes.
