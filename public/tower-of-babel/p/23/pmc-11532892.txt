Introduction

Emphysematous cystitis (EC) is a rare and serious form of urinary tract infection characterized by the presence of gas within the bladder wall and lumen. EC is frequently observed in elderly diabetic women, with diabetes being a significant risk factor. This condition can be potentially life-threatening, requiring prompt evaluation and management. Risk factors include glycosuria, neurogenic bladders, recurrent urinary tract infections, urinary stasis and obstruction, and other pathological conditions. The presence of air in the urinary tract and bladder can result from various causes such as instrumentation, fistulas, tissue infarction, or the infection itself. Clinical presentations vary from patient to patient, ranging from incidental findings on imaging to severe sepsis. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are crucial to address this unique and potentially life-threatening urinary tract condition [2,3,6].

Case presentation

We present the case of a 55-year-old woman with a history of type 1 diabetes treated with insulin since childhood, a lumbar disc herniation surgery five years ago, and well-controlled hypertension with ACE inhibitors. She was admitted to the emergency department following deterioration in her overall well-being. The patient was confused with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 14. Her family reported a history of nausea, vomiting, right lumbar pain, concentrated bad-smelling urine, and micturition burn during the last 7 days. The patient had not experienced fever or chills.

On clinical examination, her temperature was 37.5 °C, heart rate was 80 beats/min, and blood pressure was 130/90 mmHg. Blood tests revealed elevated WBC 15,000/mm3, a CRP over 300 mg/L, hyperglycemia 3,70 g/L. Renal function was normal, and there were no electrolyte disturbances. An initial abdominal X-ray showed air content in the vesical area (Fig. 1) and subsequent CT scan revealed complete dissection of the vesical wall due to emphysema, along with the presence of gas in the right renal pelvis system and the ureters (Fig. 2). Blood cultures returned negative.

An abdominal X-ray revealing the presence of gas taking the shape of the bladder Compartment(arrows).

(A,B) CT scan images showing the presence of gas within the bladder wall, completely dissecting its contours (A,B), with the additional observation of gas in the right kidney(A).

The patient underwent urinary drainage with a simple bladder catheter, which yielded 700 cc of cloudy urine with an important amount of gas. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy has been initiated (Ceftriaxone, metronidazole, amikacin) and later adjusted based on urine culture results Indicating the presence of multI-sensitive Klebsiella pneumoniae.

The patient showed consciousness clinical improvement, and inflammatory parameters got normalized, although the CRP took longer to return to normal levels.

The patient took a 14 days' treatment as recommended and was referred after to an endocrinologist for a better following up of her diabetes.

Discussion

Emphysematous cystitis is a relatively rare condition caused by gas-producing microbes, with only 135 cases reported in the English-language literature prior to 2006. A literature review conducted a search of PubMed and Ichushi-Web (The Japan Medical Abstracts Society) for case reports of emphysematous cystitis written in English or Japanese between 2007 and 2013 and identified a total of 102 cases reported.

It's characterized by the presence of gas in the bladder lumen and, more importantly, within the bladder wall. Whether it is type 1 or type 2, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus is the most common predisposing condition found in over 70 % of cases, particularly among female subjects. A systematic review of 135 cases of emphysematous cystitis demonstrated that 64 % of the subjects were women, 67 % of them having diabetes. Escherichia coli (E. coli) was the most isolated pathogen in the same study [3].

Other contributing factors include all immunosuppression states, obstructive pathologies of the urinary tract, whether anatomical, organic, or functional (neurogenic bladder). Clinical manifestations vary considerably from one patient to another, ranging from completely asymptomatic forms (incidental radiological discovery) to systemic failure.

When symptomatic, emphysematous cystitis causes abdominal pain in 80 % of cases, it may be associated with purulent urine and systemic signs such as fever and altered general condition. Pneumaturia, whose exact mechanism is not entirely clear, is a very specific sign especially found during bladder catheterization, it is present in approximately 70 % of cases and may be, more or less, associated with lower urinary tract signs [4,5].

Apart from pneumaturia, all other clinical signs are nonspecific, necessitating radiological investigations for diagnosis. Although the use of simple radiographic images or ultrasound is possible, computed tomography is the gold standard for a positive diagnosis. It allows visualization of gas collection within the bladder, highlights air bubbles in the bladder wall, and enables the detection of other pathological conditions such as rectovesical fistulas that present with similar clinical features [3].

Blood analyses often show a biological inflammatory syndrome with some increased inflammatory parameters such as white blood cells and CRP. It is also useful to assess electrolyte imbalances and renal function, along with obtaining a urine culture to isolate the responsible pathogen and tailor subsequent therapy.

The choice of antibiotic therapy is crucial for achieving remission, and given the bacterial epidemiology dominated by gram-negative bacilli, antibiotherapy should be based on third-generation cephalosporins or quinolones, with or without an aminoglycoside. It should be administered intravenously initially and then adjusted based on the results of bacterial culture.

This antibiotic therapy should be systematically combined with effective urinary drainage and correction of risk factors such as glycemic control [5,6].

The promptness of intervention and effective antibiotic therapy mean that symptoms improve more or less rapidly (within 4 days), and biological parameters return to normal values within the same timeframe, giving this relatively rare and potentially life-threatening condition a favorable prognosis [4].

Conclusion

It is evident that emphysematous cystitis is a rare and concerning pathology that requires the full attention of the physician to achieve a diagnosis as quickly as possible. This timing is critical for prognosis and enables complete remission.
