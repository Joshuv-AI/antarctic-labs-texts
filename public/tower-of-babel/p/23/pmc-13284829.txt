Cleaning Methods and Microbial Safety of Child Feeding Equipment: A Systematic Review
Authors: Rehnuma Haque, Syeda Nurunnahar, Md Shariful Islam, Tasmin Sayeed, Ahsan Habib, Henrik Salje, Peter J. Winch, Robert Dreibelbis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobial contamination of child feeding equipment (CFE) includes feeding bottles, teats, and breast pumps. This contamination is a recognized pathway for enteric pathogen exposure during early childhood, particularly during the complementary feeding period (6–24 months). Inadequate or inconsistent cleaning of CFE has been associated with increased diarrheal risk. Many cleaning approaches are practiced globally. However, comparative evidence on their effectiveness remains fragmented. This systematic review (CRD420251058230) aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and efficacy of commonly used CFE cleaning methods. We included studies that assessed microbial contamination on CFE. Each study compared at least one cleaning method with another method or a control. We included all epidemiological study designs conducted globally. PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase were searched from January 1970 to January 2025. Two reviewers independently screened 18,415 records and included 17 studies. Risk of bias was assessed by ROBINS‐E and ROBINS‐I. Feeding bottles and teats were the most frequently assessed equipment. Rinsing with water alone was consistently ineffective. Washing with soap and brushing achieved greater contamination reduction than washing without brushing. Chlorine‐based disinfection substantially reduced contamination, with effectiveness varying by concentration and contact time. Sterilization by boiling or steaming, particularly when preceded by mechanical washing, achieved near‐complete microbial elimination. The evidence base was limited by the small number of experimental studies. Study quality was variable, and outcomes were heterogeneous. These limitations emphasize the need for more rigorous and context‐specific research to inform effective, scalable cleaning interventions.

Introduction

Complementary feeding is initiated at 6 months alongside continued breastfeeding. This period represents a pivotal phase for infant growth, cognitive development, and immune system maturation (Webber et al. 2021). During this period, caregivers frequently feed their children with breastmilk. Formula‐feeding equipment, such as bottles, nipples, breast pumps, pacifiers, and preparation utensils are commonly used. Poorly cleaned child feeding equipment and unsafe preparation environments can contaminate infant feeds. This contamination increases pathogen exposure (FAO/WHO 2007; Moloney et al. 1987; Sanidad & Zeng 2020) and contributes to diarrheal disease. Diarrhea remains the second leading cause of death among children under 5 years of age. It contributes to approximately 525,000 annual fatalities globally (UNICEF 2021; WHO 2025). In South Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa, compromised weaning hygiene is common. It is associated with 30%–50% of diarrheal episodes among infants (Higgins et al. 2024). Evidence suggested substantial levels of enteric pathogens in weaning utensils, primarily attributable to inadequate cleaning practices, compromised water sources, and insufficient sterilization (Ghosh et al. 2020; Henry et al. 1990; Islam et al. 2012; Moloney et al. 1987; Sanidad & Zeng 2020; Sobhan et al. 2024). Therefore, microbial contamination of CFE is a globally documented concern (Kosek et al. 2003; Ngure et al. 2014). As contaminated utensils are linked to the transmission of diarrhoeagenic pathogens and are associated with a major health burden among young children.

According to a recent meta‐analysis, E. coli was found in 58% of CFE samples in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), especially in infants who were bottle‐fed (Zenebe et al. 2020). A study in an Indian pediatric hospital reported that 92% of the bottles were infected with bacteria, including Staphylococcus, Klebsiella, E. coli, and Salmonella (Mohamed et al. 2023). In Pakistan, contaminated bottle contents are linked with more than half of all cases of children admitted to the hospital for diarrhea (Ayaz et al. 2020). In a childcare centre in Singapore, children's bottles presented an average concentration of 3.4 × 10⁴ CFU/mL, nearly 70 times above the safety limit. However, existing reviews were conducted some time ago and focus on a limited set of aspects, indicating scope for a robust synthesis that integrates both laboratory evidence and real‐world effectiveness across the full spectrum of breastmilk‐ and formula‐feeding equipment. Despite the seriousness of this public health issue, current CFE cleaning procedures such as boiling, rinsing, soap washing, chemical disinfection, and sterilization remain inconsistently practiced and their efficacy or effectiveness is insufficiently evaluated (Gilks et al. 2007; Price et al. 2006; Renfrew et al. 2008).

Therefore, this review fills that gap by synthesizing global evidence on rinsing, washing, disinfecting, and sterilizing practices to inform updated, context‐appropriate guidance aligned with broader global health priorities. This systematic review had two objectives: (i) to evaluate the efficacy and effectiveness of various cleaning techniques used for breastmilk and formula‐feeding equipment in diverse global contexts, and (ii) to compile and synthesize evidence regarding microbial contamination levels associated with these cleaning methods.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the methodological standards outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Cumpston et al. 2019) and reported following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al. 2021) presented in Supporting Information S1: Table 2.

The review has been registered with PROSPERO #CRD420251058230 (Haque et al. 2025). A search was conducted in three electronic databases using the time parameters without publication date: Web of Science, PubMed, and Embase, and screened studies on Covidence software (Covidence systematic review software 2024). A comprehensive search strategy was implemented for each database with relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. The reference lists of the initially retrieved papers were checked for backward citation chasing. The complete search strategies for different database searches are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table 1.

Inclusion Criteria and Selection

This systematic review's inclusion criteria allow multiple domains of microbial contamination in CFE used by children under two, including bottles, nipples, pacifiers, blenders, and breast pumps. Although many feeding tools (e.g., cups, plates, bowls, spoons, breast suction devices) are theoretically vulnerable to contamination, no eligible studies assessing these items were found. We included studies with information across diverse settings, including home, laboratory and hospital settings. Studies were also classified according to contamination type as either natural contamination, reflecting routine use without experimental inoculation, or artificial/standardized contamination, in which equipment was deliberately inoculated with specific microorganisms under controlled conditions to assess cleaning or sterilization efficacy. Included studies were further categorized by infant population and feeding context. Household and community‐based studies primarily involved healthy term infants and routine feeding practices, whereas studies in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), milk bank, or other institutional settings involved preterm, low birth weight, medically fragile infants, or donor milk preparation. We evaluated interventions involving diverse cleaning methods, including rinsing, washing, disinfecting, and sterilizing techniques. These methods include mechanical methods (e.g., scrubbing with brushes), chemical methods (e.g., soap, various rinses, sterilizing solutions, chlorine disinfection), and thermal methods (e.g., boiling, steam, microwave, electric sterilization), as well as drying and soaking. We systematically identified eligible studies by removing duplicates, screening titles and abstracts, and conducting full‐text reviews. Two independent reviewers performed initial title and abstract screening, reached a consensus, or consulted a third reviewer for disagreements. The full texts were then assessed against the selection criteria.

Data Extraction

Key data were extracted into an Excel sheet via a structured framework by two independent reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer. The extracted information is provided in Supporting Information S1: Table 3.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the included studies for the risk of bias (ROB) with appropriate tools based on study design; the Cochrane ROB 2.0 tool for randomized control trial (RCTs), the ROBIN‐I tool for nonrandomized intervention studies and the ROBIN‐E tool was applied to nonrandomized studies of exposures (Higgins et al. 2024; J. A. Sterne et al. 2016; J. A. C. Sterne et al. 2019). Before the full assessment, both reviewers pretested the ROB tool on several included papers to ensure consistent interpretation and data extraction, with discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. Risk‐of‐bias assessments were conducted for each study across all relevant domains and accompanied by justifications for each judgment. All results were tabulated, and summary figures were prepared to display domain‐level and overall ROB across studies. Non‐randomized and household‐based studies were interpreted with caution due to the predominance of moderate to serious confounding. Risk‐of‐bias judgments were made at the domain level, with ROBINS‐I and ROBINS‐E assessing seven domains, including confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results, while RoB 2 assessed five domains for RCTs‐ randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Overall risk‐of‐bias for each study was determined based on the highest‐risk domain. In ROBINS‐I, domains were rated as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk, while in ROBINS‐E, ratings were low, some concerns, high, or very high risk. By default, the worst‐rated domain defined the overall study rating; however, when multiple domains were rated as moderate or high risk, the overall judgment could be upgraded to account for cumulative bias. This approach ensured that overall risk reflected both the single most problematic domain and the combined influence of multiple sources of bias. Domain‐level judgments were also used to inform the likely direction of bias and potential threat to study conclusions. Domain‐level justifications and overall risk assessments were tabulated, and summary plots were generated to visualize domain‐specific and overall risk across all studies (Supporting Information S1: Tables 4–5, Supporting Information S1: Figures 1–4).

Data Synthesis

A quantitative meta‐analysis was not possible because each study had a different design or reported on different outcomes, and many lacked quantitative outcome measures. Therefore, a structured narrative synthesis was employed to evaluate the strength of evidence, considering the ROB and consistency. The study findings were analyzed and interpreted to enable relevant comparisons across different cleaning, sterilizing, and drying processes for child feeding equipment aimed at reducing microbial contamination.

Ethical and Reporting Considerations

This systematic review was conducted using previously published data and therefore did not require ethical approval or participant consent. The review was performed and reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database.

Results

Included Studies

After conducting the preliminary search across the three databases, we identified 18,415 potentially eligible articles. After title and abstract screening and removal of duplicates via Covidence 97 articles were retained for full‐text screening. At full‐text screening, 17 articles met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). We have listed all excluded full‐text articles and the reasons for their exclusion in Supporting Information S1: table 6.

PRISMA flow diagram.

The included studies were conducted in 15 countries, encompassing various income levels as categorized by the World Bank for the 2024 fiscal year (World Bank 2024). Eleven studies originated from high‐income countries, three from upper‐middle‐income countries, four from lower‐middle‐income countries and one from a lower‐income country. Eight studies were from domestic real‐world household/community settings, six from laboratory‐controlled settings, and three from hospital/milk bank/institutional settings. Of the included studies, 6 were experimental (including one RCT) and the remaining 11 were non‐experimental (cross‐sectional, quasi‐experimental, longitudinal, or descriptive). The review included 12 studies on bottle cleaning, 9 on teat cleaning, and 4 on breast pump decontamination, and only 2 studies examining pacifiers (n = 1) and blenders (n = 1) presented in Table 3.

Target Infant Populations

Most studies (n = 11, 65%) involved healthy term infants in household/community settings. Four studies (24%) focused on preterm or medically fragile infants (NICU and milk banks). The remaining two (12%) were equipment‐focused (blenders; pacifiers and teats).

Microbial Contamination Status

For outcome measurements, all microbiological contamination results are reported using standardized units originally reported in the included articles. Culture‐based bacterial counts are expressed as colony‐forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL), while most probable number (MPN/mL) is used exclusively for samples analyzed using probabilistic enumeration methods. CFUs were the most frequently used metric (88%), followed by concentration levels (53%), and prevalence data (41%) (Table 3). In terms of microbial outcome, gram‐negative bacteria were reported in 76% of the studies, gram‐positive bacteria in 47%, other reported indicators organism counts including total coliform count and aerobic plate count in 41%, and fungi in 12%.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk‐of‐bias was evaluated in 17 studies: 10 non‐randomized intervention studies (ROBINS‐I), 6 non‐randomized exposure studies (ROBINS‐E), and 1 randomized trial (RoB 2). Across designs, confounding (D1) and selection of participants (D2) were the dominant concerns; measurement and reporting domains (D6–D7) were largely low risk.

ROBINS‐I studies: Confounding (D1) was the main issue: 6 of 10 studies rated serious (Anderton & Aidoo 1991; Carré et al. 2018; Elegbe 1982; Ghuliani & Kaul 1995; Ma et al. 2009; Millar et al. 2021) and 3 moderate (Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Marege et al. 2023; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Selection (D2) showed 4 serious (Anderton & Aidoo 1991; Biela & McGill 1985; Rigourd et al. 2021; Rowan & Anderson 1998) and 6 moderate concerns (Carré et al. 2018; Ghuliani & Kaul 1995; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2009; Marege et al. 2023; Millar et al. 2021). Deviations from intended interventions (D4) were serious in two studies (Rigourd et al. 2021; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Missing outcome data (D5) raised moderate concerns in three studies (Biela & McGill 1985; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Overall ROBINS‐I ratings: 7 serious (Anderton & Aidoo 1991; Carré et al. 2018; Elegbe 1982; Ghuliani & Kaul 1995; Ma et al. 2009; Millar et al. 2021; Rigourd et al. 2021) and 3 moderate (Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Marege et al. 2023; Rowan & Anderson 1998).

ROBINS‐E studies: Again, confounding (D1) dominated 5 of 6 studies rated high (Carré et al. 2018; Elegbe 1982; Ghuliani & Kaul 1995; Gibson et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2009) and 1 as some concerns (Marege et al. 2023). Other domains (D2–D7) were mostly low risk, with missing data (D5) showing moderate concerns in two studies (Gibson et al. 2017; Marege et al. 2023). Overall: 5 serious/high and 1 moderate (Marege et al. 2023).

Randomized trial RoB 2: The single RCT (Flores‐Antón et al. 2019) had low risk for the randomization process (D1) and low risk for outcome measurement (D4), but some concerns for deviations from intended interventions (D2) due to incomplete blinding, and high risk for missing outcome data (D3) and selection of reported results (D5) because the trial was stopped early. Overall RoB 2 classification is high risk.

Overall, across 17 studies, confounding (D1) affected 12 and selection (D2) was the next most frequent problem, particularly in ROBINS‐I; missing data (D5) and deviations (D4) caused moderate–serious concerns in a minority. These biases, mainly uncontrolled baseline differences, likely biased effectiveness estimates; specific uncontrolled confounders included utensil material (Marege et al. 2023; Rowan & Anderson 1998), washing frequency (Biela & McGill 1985; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023), and storage conditions (Anderton & Aidoo 1991; Flores‐Antón et al. 2019). Overall evidence quality is therefore moderate–low, and findings should be interpreted cautiously; full ROBINS‐I/E data and plots are in Supporting Information S1: Tables 4–5 and Supporting Information S1: Figures 1–4. Evaluation of Cleaning Methods

Rinsing and Washing

Two experimental studies assessed rinsing and washing techniques (Ma et al. 2009; Rothstein et al. 2019). Rinsing alone, regardless of water temperature, was found to be ineffective, with neither study endorsing it as a standalone cleaning disinfection method. (Ma et al. 2009; Rothstein et al. 2019). In contrast, the low‐risk experimental study by Rothstein et al. (2019), washing with detergents and brushing substantially reduced microbial contamination compared with no cleaning. Thirty seconds of brushing was sufficient to lower microbial loads to 2.3 CFU/100 mL (SD: 1.7), and 60 s provided greater reductions to 1 CFU/100 mL (SD: 2), both within safe levels < 5 CFU/100 mL (Rothstein et al. 2019). Washing was also reported as more effective than only rinsing. Similarly, the high‐risk experimental study by Ma et al. (2009) found that the rinse‐brush‐soapy‐rinse (RbsR) method outperformed double rinsing, particularly under high contamination conditions. A brief pre‐rinse improved subsequent cleaning even when exposure times were short (Ma et al. 2009). Taken together, these studies demonstrate that mechanical cleaning specifically brushing with detergents is the critical first step in microbial removal.

Disinfecting

Four studies assessed the effectiveness of chemical disinfectants, including chlorine, hypochlorite, and saltwater (Elegbe 1982; Ma et al. 2009; Rigourd et al. 2021; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Compared with no treatment, disinfection was found to be consistently more effective. Ma et al. (2009), a quasi‐experimental laboratory‐based study with moderate ROB, demonstrated that chlorine‐based disinfection at concentrations of 50–200 ppm resulted in variable results depending on the exposure time and technique: vertical submersion for 30 min achieved complete bacterial elimination in all the tested bottles, whereas shorter exposures or placing bottles horizontally in solution were less effective (Ma et al. 2009). Rigourd et al. (2021) further reported that chlorine‐based disinfection reduced contamination to 2% contamination rate (Rigourd et al. 2021). However, Elegbe 1982), in a cross‐sectional household study with high ROB, found that the addition of detergent combined with saltwater was more effective (3.7%) than the addition of Milton solution, which reduced contamination to (25.6%). These findings indicate that chemical disinfection effectiveness depends on concentration, contact time, and technique, and performs optimally when integrated within a broader multistep cleaning process.

Sterilizing

Nine studies evaluated sterilization methods for child feeding equipment (Anderton & Aidoo 1991; Biela & McGill 1985; Flores‐Antón et al. 2019; Gerber Berliner and Karolus 1983; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Marege et al. 2023; Millar et al. 2021; Redmond et al. 2009; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Six of these were laboratory‐based experimental studies with low‐to‐moderate ROB compared with no treatment, heat‐based methods, particularly steam and boiling (n = 6), resulted in nearly total pathogen removal (Anderson & Gatherer 1970; Biela & McGill 1985; Flores‐Antón et al. 2019; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Millar et al. 2021; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Anderson and Gatherer 1970) reported that hypochlorite disinfection reduced contamination rates (70–7% ≤ 5 CFU) compared with boiling alone (Anderson & Gatherer 1970). Rigourd et al. (2021) also reported that boiling reduced contamination to 2%, a level comparable to that of sterile hospital conditions (7%) (Rigourd et al. 2021). Flores‐Antón et al. (2019), a low‐risk hospital‐based RCT, further showed that Steam treatment reduced gram‐negative bacteria from 37% to ≤ 5% and Enterobacteriaceae from 22.8% to 1.3% (p < 0.001) confirming high effectiveness under controlled conditions. (Flores‐Antón et al. 2019; Redmond et al. 2009). Studies that compared sterilization with other methods reported that steam and microwave sterilization achieved greater microbial reductions than chemical disinfectants did (Rowan & Anderson 1998), and autoclaving prevented regrowth more reliably than either boiling or chemical methods did (Anderton & Aidoo 1991). This study was unique in evaluating regrowth as an outcome and explicitly measured microbial regrowth over time following cleaning and sterilization of blenders, showing that while hypochlorite initially eliminated Klebsiella aerogenes, bacterial populations rebounded to 10⁴–10⁹ CFU in glass and metal and 10⁵–10⁷ CFU in plastic by 24 h, whereas autoclaving completely prevented regrowth. Rowan and Anderson 1998) and Gerber et al. (1983) also reported bacterial counts at multiple time points, highlighting that incomplete cleaning or delayed sterilization can allow microbial proliferation (Gerber et al. 1983; Rowan & Anderson 1998). Several studies indicated that sterilization outcomes were enhanced when equipment had undergone prior mechanical cleaning, consistent with findings reported in the Rinsing and Washing subsection. Brushing before sterilization also influenced outcomes, as no detectable B. cereus (< 1.0 log₁₀ CFU/mL) was detected for up to 18 h. Equipment that was brushed before boiling or autoclaving remained free of contamination, whereas skipping brushing left 5–7 log₁₀ CFU/mL after treatment (Rowan & Anderson 1998). Boiling with prior brushing further suppressed Bacillus cereus regrowth for at least 18 h, reducing bacterial loads to < 2 log₁₀ CFU/mL (Biela & McGill 1985; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Marege et al. 2023).

Two studies reported that, compared with washing alone, microwave sterilization reduced contamination by 18%, although spores sometimes persisted (Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Redmond et al. 2009). Chemical disinfection with hypochlorite eliminates bacteria in bottles but is less effective for teats (Gerber et al. 1983; Rowan & Anderson 1998).

Discussion

This systematic review provides available evidence on the efficacy and real‑world effectiveness of cleaning methods for CFE and the extent of microbial contamination following their use.

Across the 17 included studies, the most consistent finding was that a multistep process combining mechanical cleaning using detergent and brushing followed by thermal sterilization typically boiling or steaming achieved the greatest reduction in microbial contamination. Thermal sterilization was most commonly implemented through boiling or steaming. This result aligns with earlier laboratory‐based research demonstrating the temperature sensitivity of common enteric pathogens. Reductions in contamination following steam or boiling treatment in clinical environments (Flores‐Antón et al. 2019) were comparable to reductions observed in household settings where boiling was performed correctly (Marege et al. 2023). Studies consistently showed that brushing with detergent before disinfection improved microbial reduction, whereas chemical disinfectants applied without prior brushing or with insufficient exposure time were less reliable (Flores‐Antón et al. 2019; Marege et al. 2023). Laboratory studies indicate that biofilms readily develop on plastic bottles and nipples. Biofilm formation can reduce washing effectiveness by up to 40%. (Mouafo et al. 2022). In contrast, stainless steel demonstrates lower microbial adhesion compared with plastic (Global Hygiene Council 2024). Notably, laboratory experiments demonstrate that only autoclaving achieves true sterilization by eliminating both vegetative cells and heat‐tolerant survivors. Autoclaving was also the only method shown to prevent bacterial regrowth during post‐cleaning storage (Anderton & Aidoo 1991). Taken together, these findings indicate that the most reliable approach in domestic settings is multistep mechanical cleaning using detergent and brushing followed by boiling or steaming. In hospital and laboratory environments, sterilization through autoclaving remains essential where available. Poor adherence was observed in both high‐income and low‑ and middle‑income settings, including studies where sterilization equipment or guidance was readily available (Carré et al. 2018; Gibson et al. 2017; Jacob 1989). The drivers of non‐compliance remain poorly understood. This gap is noteworthy given the strong and repeated association between contaminated CFE and diarrheal illness in infants (Ayaz et al. 2020; Zenebe et al. 2020). The evidence base also remains narrow in scope. Bottles and teats were well represented in the literature. In contrast, research on pacifiers, blenders, and breast pumps was sparse.

To date, one randomized controlled trial has been conducted (Flores‐Antón et al. 2019). Most other studies adopted observational designs. Outcome measurement was also highly variable. Studies differed in microbiological techniques, reporting units, and contamination thresholds. This variability limited comparability and prevented quantitative synthesis. Furthermore, most experimental studies were conducted in HIC laboratory settings. Effectiveness studies conducted in LMIC household environments where diarrheal burden is greatest remain scarce. This imbalance mirrors observations from Renfrew et al. 2008) and Gilks et al. 2007), who noted that existing evidence does not sufficiently reflect contemporary domestic environments or resource‐limited settings (Gilks et al. 2007; Renfrew et al. 2008). The present review confirms that environmental constraints, fuel access, water quality, and material type continue to influence both contamination risk and cleaning feasibility. Evidence regarding rinsing and washing is derived primarily from controlled laboratory‐based experimental studies, including one low‐risk and one high‐risk study. While mechanical washing with detergent demonstrates consistent microbial reduction under experimental conditions, the absence of high‐quality household or clinical studies limits generalizability. Certainty in the effectiveness of only rinsing is low, whereas certainty in detergent‐based mechanical washing under controlled conditions is moderate. The evidence for chemical disinfection is heterogeneous across settings. Laboratory and hospital‐based studies with low‐to‐moderate ROB generally demonstrated substantial microbial reduction, particularly with optimized chlorine exposure. However, household‐based cross‐sectional evidence was subject to serious confounding and selection bias, reducing confidence in real‐world effectiveness estimates. Overall certainty for chlorine disinfection is moderate in controlled settings but low in household contexts. In contrast to rinsing and chemical disinfection, sterilization methods particularly steam, boiling, and autoclaving are supported predominantly by laboratory‐based experimental studies and one low‐risk hospital‐based randomized trial. These studies consistently demonstrated high levels of microbial inactivation, with autoclaving providing the most reliable prevention of regrowth. Given the predominance of low‐to‐moderate ROB studies in controlled environments, overall certainty for heat‐based sterilization under laboratory and clinical conditions is high. However, evidence from routine household implementation remains limited. Across methods, the strength of evidence varied considerably by study design and setting. Laboratory and clinical studies generally demonstrated lower ROB and more consistent microbial reduction, particularly for heat‐based sterilization. In contrast, household‐based observational studies were more frequently affected by confounding and selection bias, limiting certainty regarding real‐world effectiveness. These methodological differences were considered in interpreting the comparative effectiveness of cleaning strategies. Overall, heat‐based sterilization particularly steam and boiling achieved the greatest microbial reduction. Microwave and chemical methods were effective alternatives but showed greater variability in outcomes. Heat‐based sterilization methods are supported by the most consistent and lowest‐risk evidence, whereas rinsing and household‐based chemical disinfection are limited by moderate to serious confounding. Risk‐of‐bias judgments reflect ROB 2.0 for the single randomized controlled trial and ROBINS‐I/ROBINS‐E assessments for non‐randomized studies. Confounding (Domain 1) and participant selection bias were the most frequent concerns, particularly in household‐based observational studies.

A key methodological distinction was the difference between natural and artificial contamination. Natural contamination studies reflect real‐world effectiveness and capture variability related to caregiver practices, environmental hygiene, and feed handling. In contrast, laboratory inoculation studies provide controlled estimates of cleaning efficacy but may not fully represent routine household exposure levels. Consequently, findings across these two study types can not be interpreted as directly comparable but rather as complementary evidence on effectiveness versus efficacy.

Our review highlights that microbiological risk and cleaning requirements differ substantially between healthy term infants and preterm or sick infants. Household and community studies (Anderson & Gatherer 1970; Biela & McGill 1985; Elegbe 1982; Gerber et al. 1983; Ghuliani & Kaul 1995; Gibson et al. 2017; Ma et al. 2009; Marege et al. 2023; Redmond et al. 2009; Rothstein et al. 2019; Rowan & Anderson 1998) involved feeding bottles, formula preparation, and standard sterilization techniques applicable to home environments. NICU and milk bank studies (Carré et al. 2018; Flores‐Antón et al. 2019; Laforce‐Lavoie et al. 2023; Rigourd et al. 2021) focused on preterm or medically fragile infants, using multi‐component breast pump kits, expressed milk, and strict sterilization protocols due to their higher infection risk. In contrast, (Anderton & Aidoo 1970) and (Millar et al. 2021) examined hospital or laboratory equipment under controlled conditions, not specifically for NICU or preterm populations. These differences underscore the importance of stratifying studies by infant population and feeding context when interpreting findings. Risk mitigation strategies, microbial thresholds, and cleaning practices suitable for home bottle feeding may not be directly generalizable to preterm or NICU populations, and vice versa.

Limitations include potential selection bias from database restrictions. Substantial methodological heterogeneity—including differences in inoculum levels, cleaning protocols, and outcome measures—further limited comparability. Many key studies are dated (1960s–1990s). Several included studies conducted between 1970 and 1985 employed microbiological methods that predate contemporary WHO and ISO standards for microbial enumeration and detection. These studies primarily relied on culture‐based techniques, selective plating, and colony counting approaches with higher detection thresholds and limited sensitivity compared to modern methods, which now incorporate standardized protocols, improved selective media, and molecular confirmation. As a result, microbial counts, detection limits, and reported contamination rates from earlier investigations may not be directly comparable with more recent studies. While the direction of effect particularly the superiority of heat‐based sterilization remains biologically plausible and consistent across decades, uncertainty arises regarding the precision and absolute magnitude of reported reductions. Consequently, findings from older studies were interpreted with caution and weighed alongside more recent low‐risk experimental and clinical evidence when assessing overall certainty and generalizability to current practice. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in microbial thresholds, sampling timing, and reporting units, limiting cross‐study comparability. Additionally, variations in incubation times, culture media composition, and reporting units (e.g., CFU per area, volume, or gram) further limit cross‐study comparability.

Although heat‐based sterilization demonstrated the highest certainty of microbial inactivation under controlled conditions, feasibility varies substantially across settings. In high‐income countries (HICs), access to electricity, microwaves, steam sterilizers, and safe piped water generally facilitates implementation of boiling or steam‐based sterilization. However, adherence to recommended contact times, complete submersion, and adequate drying remains inconsistent, and improper storage following sterilization may permit recontamination. Even in hospital settings, lapses in drying, assembly, or pump storage have been associated with contamination. In contrast, in low‐ and LMICs, additional structural constraints may limit implementation. Fuel availability and cost can restrict routine boiling, particularly where households rely on biomass fuels. Water quality is also a critical determinant, as contaminated rinse water may reintroduce pathogens following cleaning. Chemical disinfection requires accurate dilution and sufficient contact time, which may be difficult to standardize in domestic environments. Safe drying surfaces and hygienic storage conditions are often limited, increasing the risk of post‐cleaning contamination. Furthermore, caregiver workload, time constraints, and limited risk perception may affect adherence to multi‐step cleaning protocols. These contextual differences suggest that while steam and boiling are microbiologically effective, real‐world effectiveness depends heavily on environmental infrastructure, caregiver behavior, and sustained implementation support. Future interventions should integrate behavior change strategies, simplified protocols, and context‐appropriate technologies to improve adherence and reduce contamination risk across diverse settings.

Feasibility and Implementation

Results of older included studies (1970–1985) do not provide adequate evidence for choice of effective methods under ideal conditions because their microbiological techniques do not align with current WHO/ISO standards, introducing moderate to high ROB. However, they point to methods that might be further evaluated for application in low‐income households or humanitarian settings where there is no good access to fuel or clean water, such as cleaning procedures based on solar disinfection, chlorine tablets with prolonged contact time, or ash‐assisted scrubbing. Notably, high‐income country studies are mostly laboratory‐based, whereas LMICs studies are rooted in real‐life household settings and more frequently evaluate bottles, teats, pacifiers, and blenders, with higher contamination observed on them. Given the serious confounding and selection bias across household studies, these older studies remain strictly hypothesis‐generating and cannot support any confident recommendation.

Feasibility differs across settings, but evidence is too biased to guide implementation. In HIC lab‐based studies, heat‐based methods appear theoretically feasible, yet real‐world adherence and contamination are rarely reported. In LMIC/LIC household studies, barriers include limited fuel for boiling, shortened contact time, unsafe drying/storage, and poor behavior change sustainability—but these observational studies suffer serious confounding and selection bias (households' self‐select methods). Heat‐based sterilization achieves greatest microbial reduction under controlled conditions, but real‐world effectiveness in low‐resource settings is uncertain. Chemical methods show greater variability with no rigorous unbiased comparisons. Household studies report high contamination on pacifiers and blenders (rarely examined in HIC lab research), though the number of investigations is small and ROB high. Thus, no feasible method can be recommended with confidence; identified barriers and contamination patterns only indicate priorities for future bias‐reduced research. These findings have important implications for public health policy and practice. First, guidance should clearly specify a multistep approach incorporating brushing with detergent followed by sterilization. Consistent with the Results, reliance on one method or single‐step washing is not supported by the available evidence (Marege et al. 2023; Mouafo et al. 2022). Second, there is a need for standardized, evidence‑based cleaning guidelines specifically tailored to CFE used during complementary feeding. Current WHO and UNICEF hygiene recommendations acknowledge contamination risk (FAO/WHO 2007; Carré et al. 2018). However, they do not provide detailed operational instructions for caregiver settings. This omission is notable given evidence that biofilm formation and material composition influence cleaning efficacy (Global Hygiene Council 2024; Mouafo et al. 2022). Policies must therefore move beyond technical recommendations. They should explicitly address feasibility across diverse economic and demographic contexts. In relation to the original research questions, this review adds evidence on the wide variety of utensils used to feed children in the complementary feeding period across different settings. Laboratory‐based studies have primarily in contrast, household studies more frequently evaluated bottles and teats. Higher contamination levels were observed on pacifiers and blenders in some studies, although the number of investigations assessing these items remains limited.

Conclusion

Future research should therefore prioritise field‑based evaluations of cleaning and sterilization methods in household settings. Boiling appears to be the most feasible thermal method in low‐resource contexts. However, it remains insufficiently validated under routine real‐world conditions. Additional priorities include mixed‑methods research to identify behavioural and infrastructural barriers to adherence. Standardized microbiological outcomes are needed to enable pooled analyses. Pragmatic trials of scalable interventions such as behavior‐change strategies, low‐resource sterilization tools, and caregiver support models should also be conducted. Finally, while contamination of CFE has been extensively documented, limited studies directly link contamination levels to clinical illness outcomes. Few studies assess the durability of hygiene improvements over time, representing a critical evidence gap for policy translation.
