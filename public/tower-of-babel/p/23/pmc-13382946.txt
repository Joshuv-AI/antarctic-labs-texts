Study on C–N coupling reactions and catalytic mechanisms of modified boron nitride for urea synthesis: from nitrogen and carbon monoxide
Authors: Shuxin Li, Yu Zhang, Tianxing Wu, Miaomiao Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urea is a key chemical for agriculture and industry, yet its conventional synthesis is energy-intensive and environmentally unfriendly. Electrocatalytic C–N coupling offers a sustainable alternative for urea production under mild conditions; however, significant challenges remain. Herein, density functional theory (DFT) is employed to investigate Fe- and Co-decorated defective boron nitride (BN) monolayers with mono-vacancies (VB, VN) and divacancies (VBB, VBN) as electrocatalysts. Their structural stability, activation of N2, corresponding reaction mechanism and selectivity for urea synthesis are comprehensively analyzed. Among the studied systems, Fe-VBN exhibits the lowest thermodynamic limiting potential of −0.413 V. In contrast, despite a compromised limiting potential of −0.715 V, the FeCo-VBB shows superior thermodynamic selectivity. This study provides insights into the C–N coupling mechanism on defective BN materials and may inform the rational design of electrocatalysts for urea synthesis.

Introduction

Urea, which is essential to agricultural productivity, has become the most widely used nitrogen fertilizer in the world.1 It also functions as a necessary chemical intermediary, allowing for its extensive use through chemical reactions in industries including fuel cells, textiles, healthcare, and animal husbandry.2–4 The traditional industrial synthesis of urea employs the Bosch–Meiser process, using ammonia and carbon dioxide as raw materials through high-pressure, high-temperature reactions.5 The needed ammonia gas is mostly produced by the Haber–Bosch process,6 which is energy intensive and environment unfriendly, consuming more than 2% of the global yearly energy supply and producing enormous carbon emissions.7 Therefore, it is necessary to search for environmentally friendly and resource-efficient urea production systems. A common area of research has been the electrocatalytic C–N coupling process.

Electrocatalytic C–N coupling for urea synthesis, a unique green manufacturing technique, is distinguished by moderate reaction conditions (ambient temperature and pressure or near-ambient conditions),8 low energy consumption and the possibility of using renewable energy sources. However, there are drawbacks to this strategy as well, such as an unclear reaction mechanism, low conversion rate, and poor catalyst stability.9 To overcome these obstacles and improve the catalytic activity, selectivity, and general efficiency of electrocatalytic urea synthesis, creative approaches and sophisticated catalyst designs are needed. Numerous potential catalysts have been proposed theoretically and experimentally in recent years. Jiao et al. explored the potential of the (101) surface of molybdenum phosphide (MoP) for the electrocatalytic production of urea from CO2 and N2, exhibiting a urea formation rate of 12.4 µg h−1 mg−1 and a faradaic efficiency of 36.5% at a potential of −0.35 V (vs. reversible hydrogen electrode).10 Using DFT calculations, Liu et al. discovered that vanadium-based catalyst (V2N6) anchored on defective graphene could activate N2 efficiently, exhibiting exceptional intrinsic activity for urea synthesis with a limiting potential of −0.26 V, while significantly inhibiting the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and N2 reduction reaction (NRR).11 Zhu et al. found that monolayer CuB12 exhibited optimal catalytic activity based on first-principles calculations, hence refining the reaction mechanism for CO2 and N2 synthesis of urea.12 Three two-dimensional metal borides like Mo2B2, Ti2B2, and Cr2B2 MBenes, were screened by Zhu et al. to examine their electrochemical urea synthesis mechanisms and electrochemical activity. All three MBenes exhibited excellent intrinsic surface activity, with limiting potentials ranging from −0.49 to −0.65 V, and significantly suppressed competing NRR to NH3.13

Boron nitride (BN), a two-dimensional layered material with a wide bandgap of 6–6.8 eV,14 was also proposed as potential catalyst for urea synthesis due to its many excellent physical and chemical properties.15 The research has revealed that introducing defects into BN nanosheets can cause the creation of a porous structure, enhancing their chemical reactivity,16 and that several active sites could be activated by anchoring of metal atoms. Especially, the transition metal atoms are preferred due to their low costs and corresponding strong catalyst stability. For instance, Zhao et al. concluded that single Mo embedded BN nanosheets with B vacancies function as extremely effective and stable electrocatalysts for nitrogen fixation with an overpotential of only 0.19 V.17 Ru–Ti@VB-BN could accomplish extremely effective NRR through an enzymatic process at a low limiting potential of −0.40 V.18 Ma et al. screened the V@BN catalyst, which exhibits an overpotential of 0.25 V and effectively suppresses the competitive HER.19 Diatomic catalysts have also been developed to enhance the activity of single-atom catalysts by offering diverse active sites and enabling synergistic interactions between adjacent atoms.20–23 Recently, a carbon-based catalyst with diatomic N–Fe–Co–O sites has been synthesized for electrocatalytic oxygen reduction and evolution.24 However, the study of diatomic catalysts for urea synthesis based on defective BN is still in its early stages.

Herein, we use density functional theory (DFT) calculations to examine the potential of transition metal atoms (Fe, Co) loaded defective BN as catalysts for urea production. In our work, the catalytic performance of Fe and/or Co-loaded defective BN with mono-vacancy (VB, VN) and divacancy (VBB, VBN) is studied. Using CO and N2 as the carbon and nitrogen source to urea synthesis, we systematically evaluated the catalysts' stability, activity and selectivity towards urea synthesis. The FeCo-VBB shows the strongest adsorption and activation of N2, and the best selectivity toward urea synthesis. Whereas, Fe-VBN shows the lowest limiting potential −0.413 V.

Computational details

Calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)25 based on spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) with van der Waals corrections.26 Electron-ion interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method27 with an energy cutoff of 500 eV. The exchange–correlation interaction was considered using the Perdew–Burke–Ernsthof (PBE) functional28 within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). Although the GGA+U approach is often employed to improve the description of localized d states in transition-metal systems,29 the calculated energetics are sensitive to the choice of the U parameter.30 For the surface catalytic systems investigated here, the local electronic environment differs substantially from that of the bulk phase, making the transferability of U values uncertain. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that PBE and PBE+U calculations generally yield the same reaction trends.31,32 Therefore, the PBE functional was adopted in this work. A 5 × 5 × 1 supercell of BN monolayer containing 25 B and 25 N atoms has been adopted to generate vacancies and to accommodate considered M atoms (M = Fe, Co),17,33 with a defect concentration of 4 percent. All the atoms were fully optimized without any constraints. The convergence criteria were set at 10−5 eV for total energy change and 0.02 eV Å−1 for the maximum forces on each atom, respectively. A z-direction vacuum space of 20 Å was set to minimize the interactions between periodic images. The Grimme's semiempirical DFT-D3 method of dispersion correction was utilized for properly describing the van der Waals (vdW) interactions between reaction intermediates and catalysts.34 For geometry relaxation, the Brillouin zone was sampled by 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-meshes. The ab initio molecular dynamic (AIMD) simulations were performed within the NVT statistical framework. The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for each elementary step was obtained by the following equation1ΔG = ΔE + ΔEZPE − TΔS,where ΔE, ΔEZPE, and ΔS are the reaction energy from the DFT calculation, the changes in zero-point energy and entropy, respectively. T is the temperature (T = 298.15 K). The free energy diagram of urea synthesis was obtained using the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) model.35 Solvation effects were not included in the present work because our test calculations indicate that they do not alter the overall trends or qualitative conclusions. In particular, for the final desorption step, the inclusion of solvation corrections further facilitates urea desorption, leading to an even more favorable desorption process (see Fig. S4).

Results and discussion

Structure, stability, and properties of BN

Before studying the catalytic properties of Fe and/or Co-loaded BN, the structures of pristine BN and defective BN with mono-vacancy and divacancy, were first optimized. For mono-vacancy defects, both B and N vacancy (VB, VN) were considered, whereas for divacancy defects, B–B and B–N vacancy (VBB, VBN) were examined. Due to the unfavorable interactions, the transition metal atoms cannot form stable chemisorption bonds with the B atoms adjacent to N vacancies. Therefore, only BN with VB, VBB and VBN were taken into account in the subsequent study. Our computational results revealed that the distances of B–N (dB–N), N–N (dN–N) and B–B (dB–B) in pristine BN were 1.45 Å, 2.512 Å, and 2.512 Å, respectively, and the hexagonal structure produced by B and N atoms exhibited a bond angle of 120°, as shown in Fig. 1(a). With one B vacancy, as shown in Fig. 1(b), the dB–B neighboring the vacancy decreased from 2.512 Å to 2.492 Å. The angle by N–B–N around the B vacancies increased to 123.725°, which is attributed to the repulsive force generated by the negative charge of the N atoms, leading to the increase in the bond angle. The angle by B–N–B around the B vacancies decreased to 118.600°. In the case of divacancy, defective BN exhibits hexagonal structural deformations of varying shapes, as seen in Fig. 1(c) and (d). Compared to the case of mono-vacancy, the presence of divacancy induces more severe deformation in BN. After relaxation, dB–B, dB–N, and dN–N exhibit a variety of values, suggesting that the degree of deformation induced by di-vacancies varies depending on the location of the vacancy.

Relaxed structure of BN monolayer with (a) no vacancy, (b) B vacancy, (c) double B vacancies and (d) B and N vacancies.

The density of states (DOS) of defective BN was computed to investigate the influence of these defects on the electronic structure. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the calculated bandgap of pristine BN is 4.6 eV, consistent with previous theoretical reports.36 However, the calculated band gap is systematically underestimated by the GGA-PBE functional due to the inherent limitations of semilocal exchange–correlation approximations.37 As the appearance of vacancy, additional states emerged within the bandgap, as shown in Fig. 2(b)–(d). The reduction of bandgap and increase of states near the Fermi level could enhance the conductivity and thereby promote the chemical reactions.

Total and projected DOS of BN monolayer with (a) no vacancy, (b) B vacancy, (c) two B vacancies and (d) B and N vacancies.

The next stage is to anchor metal atoms onto defective BN. For B, BN and BB vacancy, single Fe and Co vacancy-loaded configurations (Fe-VB, Co-VB, Fe-VBN, Co-VBN, Fe-VBB and Co-VBB) were considered, while for B divacancy, FeCo vacancy-loaded configuration (FeCo-VBB) was considered, as seen in Fig. 3. Considering that vacancy-containing BN structures coexisting with isolated transition metal impurities have a closer connection with experimentally accessible systems, related configurations (Fe + VB, Co + VB, Fe + VN, and Fe + VN) are also considered (see Fig. S1). After relaxation, the metal atoms protrude from the plane to variable degrees, generating a wrinkled structure. It should be emphasized that the catalyst configurations investigated herein are treated as idealized local models to elucidate the intrinsic reaction mechanisms of urea electrosynthesis. Realistically, anchor of dual atoms on perfectly ordered double vacancies (FeCo-VBB) poses significant experimental challenges due to elevated energetic barriers. Nevertheless, prior studies have validated the feasibility of stabilizing dual-atom centers on defect-rich supports.38,39 For instance, Zhang et al. successfully fabricated asymmetric Ru2/SiO2@BN dimers via high-temperature calcination and ozone oxidation.40

With the models successfully constructed, the thermodynamic and electrochemical stability of those configurations were evaluated by comparing the binding energy (Eb) with the corresponding cohesive energy of the metal atoms (Ecoh), as well as by dissolution potential (Udiss). The calculation formula is:2Eb = (EM/BN − EBN − EM),3Udiss = U0diss − Eb/ne,where, EM/BN and EBN represent the total energy of defective BN with and without anchored metal atom, while EM is the total energy of a metal atom in the most stable bulk structure. The standard dissolution potential of bulk metal is denoted by U0diss, and the number of electrons in the dissolution is denoted by n.41 In general, catalysts with Eb < 0, Udiss > 0 and Eb < Ecoh are considered thermodynamically and electrochemically stable.42 As displayed in Tables 1 and 2, for all the Fe and Co vacancy-loaded catalysts, the calculated dissolution potentials are all greater than 0, while the binding energies are all less than 0 and less than Ecoh, indicating high thermodynamical and electrochemical stability for all configurations. Whereas, for Fe + VB, Co + VB, Fe + VN, and Fe + VN, the metal atoms either move to the vacancy (in case of anchor on N site with one B vacancy) or have much higher binding energies (see Table S1), which don't satisfy the criterion of Eb < Ecoh. Therefore, these models were no longer considered in the subsequent analysis. In addition. AIMD simulations were performed for those configurations to evaluate their thermal stability. Both mono-vacancy and divacancy catalysts retained structural integrity after 10 ps of keeping at 500 K, demonstrating excellent thermal stability, as shown in Fig. S2.

Nitrogen adsorption on the catalysts

The electrolytic synthesis of urea is primarily a six-electron transfer process (reaction equation: N2 + CO2 + 6H+ + 6e− = CO(NH2)2 + H2O), which involves the production and conversion of several intermediates, making the process relatively complex. In this work, N2 is used as the nitrogen source of urea synthesis and the effective adsorption of N2 on the catalyst surface is the crucial first step toward urea production. Both the end-on and side-on adsorption configurations of N2 have been considered.

For single Fe-VB, end-on adsorption is energetically favorable, with a negative adsorption energy of −0.436 eV, whereas side-on adsorption shows a positive adsorption energy of 0.121 eV. Upon adsorption, the N2 bond length increases to 1.133 Å in the end-on configuration and to 1.157 Å in the side-on configuration. While, for single Co-VB, the initial side-on N2 adsorption configuration turns to end-on configuration after structural optimization with an adsorption energy of −0.056 eV and a N–N bond length elongation to 1.129 Å. Our studies confirm that the side-on adsorption mode of N2 enables better orbital overlap with bimetallic catalyst sites, thereby enhancing catalytic activity. Effective adsorption of N2 on FeCo-VBB is indicated by the ΔG value of 0.254 eV. As shown in Fig. S3, the bond length of N2 correspondingly increases to 1.165 Å. Single Fe-VBN and Co-VBN both show negative adsorption energies (−0.148 eV and −0.160 eV) for end-on N2 adsorptions, with the N–N bond lengths elongation to 1.138 Å and 1.127 Å. Whereas, both single Fe-VBB and Co-VBB show positive adsorption energies (0.016 eV and 0.377 eV) for end-on N2 adsorptions with bond length of 1.12 Å and 1.123 Å. Their initial side-on N2 adsorption configurations also turn to end-on configurations. Based on the comparisons of the N2 bond length elongations, FeCo-VBB achieves the most effective activation of N2.

In order to gain a deeper understanding of the activation mechanism of N2, an electronic structure analysis of N2 adsorption was performed. As shown in Fig. 5, upon N2 adsorption, both charge accumulation and depletion zones simultaneously emerge between metal atoms and N2 molecules, which can be explained by the electron donation-feedback mechanism, i.e., metal atoms provide vacant d orbitals to accept lone pair electrons from N2 molecules, while simultaneously feeding electrons from their occupied d orbitals back to the anti-bonding orbitals of N2, thereby weakening the N–N.43 Subsequently, integral crystal orbital hamilton population (ICOHP) analysis (see Fig. 5) and PDOS (as shown in Fig. 6) were performed.44 The partial overlap of the metal-d orbitals with the N2-2p orbitals indicates the presence of bonding interactions between the metal and nitrogen atoms. The d orbitals of the metal accept lone pair electrons from N2, resulting in the emergence of new d state peaks at the Fermi level. Reaction mechanism

The dominant mechanism may differ in different catalyst systems. For co-reduction of CO2 and N2, both gases first adsorb onto the catalyst surface, where CO2 reduces to the CO intermediate, while N2 adsorbs laterally to form the N <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N intermediate. The key NCON intermediate is generated via C–N coupling between these two intermediates, and it then proceeds through four stages of PCET. Hydrogenation proceeds via either a distal or alternating pathway to generate urea.46 The CO2 and NO3− system comprises NO3− undergoing multi-step reduction to create NO intermediates (NO3− → NO2 → NO), while CO2 is reduced to CO intermediates. Then, either CO couples with NO to form the first C–N bond (rate-determining step), or CO2 couples with NO2 at an earlier stage to generate the *CO2NO2 intermediate. This intermediate prevents excessive CO2 reduction to CO while hindering byproduct formation, significantly boosting urea selectivity.47 The CO2 and NO2− systems involve the two-step reduction of NO2− to yield the NH intermediate (NO2− → NO → NH), while CO2 generates CO or COOH intermediates depending on the surface charge properties of the catalyst. Then C–N coupling takes place between NH and CO or COOH.48 In the CO2 and NO system, NO is first reduced to form the NH2 intermediate (NO → NHO → NH2OH → NH2), while CO2 is reduced to form the CO intermediate. C–N coupling on catalyst surface to form the CONH2 intermediate, which subsequently combines with another NH2 to yield NH2CONH2.49

CO is selected as the carbon source for urea synthesis to avoid the challenging conversion of CO2 to CO, preventing carbonate formation from CO2, and enhancing catalytic efficiency.50 Therefore, the NCON and the CO mechanism are the two reaction mechanisms that were investigated in this paper. The CO mechanism involves a four-step proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway followed by coupling with CO. The NCON mechanism entails N2 coupling with CO to form the key intermediate NCON, followed by a four-step proton-coupled electron transfer pathway. The hydrogenation step can be further categorized into alternating hydrogenation and distal hydrogenation. The alternating hydrogenation step is “N2 → N2H → NHNH → NH2NH → NH2NH2”. The distal hydrogenation step is as follows: “N2 → N2H → N2H2 → NHNH2 → NH2NH2”. Additional mixed pathways are (1) “N2 → N2H → N2H2 → NCONH2 → NHCONH2 → NH2CONH2” and (2) “N2 → N2H → NHNH → NHCONH → NH2CONH → NH2CONH2”, where after two hydrogenation steps it combines with CO first, then finishes the remaining processes to synthesize urea. The possible reaction pathways, as illustrated in Fig. 7 and 8, were analyzed, and urea synthesis performance was evaluated based on the NCON and CO mechanism.

To begin, we studied the reaction pathway of Fe-VB with initial end-on N2 adsorption. Compared to NCON mechanism, the reaction is thermodynamically more favorable to synthesis urea via CO mechanism. The reaction proceeds preferentially through a mixed pathway initiated by PCET to form *N2H (ΔG = 1.342 eV), followed by alternating hydrogenation steps, and ultimately coupling to CO to form NH2CONH2 (ΔG = −1.731 eV). It is clear that the most likely reaction pathway is “N2 → N2H → N2H2 → NCONH2 → NHCONH2 → NH2CONH2” (mixed pathway 1). The initial PCET step is the potential limiting step with Ulimiting = −1.342 V (calculated as). The low desorption energy (0.03 eV) of the formed urea indicates that active site can be regenerated under reaction conditions. In the case of side-on N2 adsorption on Fe-VB, the initial PCET barrier decreases to 0.845 eV, which is less than the 1.847 eV required for coupling with CO. Compared to the alternating pathway, the distal hydrogenation pathway is thermodynamically more favorable. The following reaction tends to couple with CO to synthesis urea, leading to a lower limiting potential of −0.845 V. The subsequent reactions also follow mixed pathway 1, where urea desorption only requires an energy of 0.056 eV. Therefore, the urea synthesis on Fe-VB will follow mixed pathway 1 with a limiting potential of −0.845 V.

For Co-VB with end-on N2 adsorption, the first PCET step remains energetically demanding (ΔG = 1.724 eV), but the subsequent coupling with CO (ΔG = −1.224 eV) is strongly exergonic. In contrast, 2.717 eV is needed for the first step via the NCON mechanism. Though spontaneous desorption of urea can occur with an energy release of 0.297 eV, the whole pathway converges to the mixed pathway 1 with a disadvantageous limiting potential of −1.724 V.

Notably, Fe-VBN with end-on N2 adsorption demonstrates the most thermodynamically favorable energetics among all systems. The initial hydrogenation step requires only 0.413 eV, representing the lowest energy barrier among all investigated catalysts. After two successive distal PCET steps, the adsorbed *N2H2 intermediate preferentially couples with CO, accompanied by a favorable free energy change of −1.696 eV. So the reaction also follows a mixed pathway 1 “N2 → N2H → N2H2 → NCONH2 → NHCONH2 → NH2CONH2” with a remarkably low limiting potential of −0.413 V. At this potential, the final desorption of urea occurs spontaneously, as shown in Fig. 9(c).

As for the Co-VBN with end-on adsorbed N2, the reaction is also more inclined to synthesis urea via CO mechanism. The ΔG for the first PCET step is 0.826 eV. Subsequent reactions are inclined to follow the distal pathway, where the *N2H2 is more favorably with CO to form NCONH2 with the ΔG value of −1.926 eV. Therefore, the Co-VBN catalyst also tends to synthesize urea via the mixed pathway 1 with a limiting potentials of −0.826 V.

For the FeCo-VBB diatomic catalyst on which N2 adsorbs with side-on mode, we find that N2 prefers to react with H first, as the ΔG for the initial PCET step is 0.715 eV. Whereas, it requires 0.93 eV for the first step via the NCON mechanism. During the second PCET step, the reaction prefers to follow the alternating pathway. After completing the two-step PCET process, *NHNH intermediate forms and comparison of the ΔG for subsequent CO coupling (−1.983 eV) with that of the third PCET step (−0.052 eV) indicates that the reaction prefers the mixed pathway 2, i.e. “N2 → N2H → NHNH → NHCONH → NH2CONH → NH2CONH2”. The initial hydrogenation step of N2 on FeCo-VBB is the potential limiting step with Ulimiting = −0.715 V.

For Fe-VBB with end-on N2 adsorption, in contrast with the NCON mechanism, urea formation following the CO mechanism possesses better thermodynamic feasibility. The first PCET step remains energetically demanding (ΔG = 1.165 eV) Compared to a third-step PCET to form *N2H3 (with ΔG being −0.277 eV), the coupling of *N2H2 with CO, which releases an energy of −1.047 eV, is more advantageous. Urea synthesis of Fe-VBB tends to occur via mixed pathway 1. The free energy value for urea desorption reaches 0.503 eV, which is relatively higher than that of other catalysts.

In the case of Co-VBB with end-on N2 adsorption mode, the limiting potential is −1.121 V. Following two consecutive alternativing PCET processes, CO preferentially combines with *N2H2 intermediate. The most likely reaction pathway is the mixed pathway 2, in which the urea desorption energy is also relatively high.

In summary, the CO mechanism is thermodynamically much more favorable than the NCON mechanism for urea synthesis on all the studied catalysts, as shown in Fig. 9. Fe-VBN has the lowest limiting potential of −0.413 V via mixed pathway 1 and FeCo-VBB also exhibits good catalytic performance, characterized by a limiting potential of −0.715 V via mixed pathway 2. These results not only clarify the preferred reaction pathways under varied N2 adsorption modes but also provide a thermodynamic rationale for catalyst design toward efficient urea electrosynthesis.

Electrocatalytic selectivity: competition with HER

The hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)51 is thought to be one of the main competing reactions that lower the faradaic efficiency of urea production since both processes utilize protons in water solutions (H+ or H2O can accept electrons to form H2). HER will consume protons, and the resulting hydrogen gas may occupy the active sites of the catalyst, inhibiting the adsorption and activation of N2 and CO and reducing the efficiency of urea synthesis. We initially evaluated the interaction between hydrogen and the active sites. As shown in Fig. S5, for Fe-VB, Co-VB, FeCo-VBB and Fe-VBB N2 adsorption is stronger than hydrogen adsorption (*H). While, for Fe-VBN, Co-VBN and Co-VBB, N2 adsorption is weaker than *H. Although *H binds strongly, under conditions of sufficient N2 pressure, adsorption of nitrogen competes with hydrogen. Importantly, the potential required to drive hydrogen evolution is more negative than that needed for nitrogen adsorption. Therefore, by carefully controlling the applied potential, hydrogen evolution can be suppressed, enabling selective urea synthesis to proceed efficiently.

Electrocatalytic selectivity: competition with NRR

The nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR), which involves the electrocatalytic conversion of N2 to NH3, constitutes the primary competing pathway in urea electrosynthesis. To evaluate the selectivity of the catalysts toward urea formation over NRR, the reaction energy difference between the C–N coupling step and the further hydrogenation step toward NRR was quantified as ΔG(C–N coupling) − ΔG(NRR). A more negative energy differences indicates a better selectivity toward urea production. As shown in Fig. 10, the results indicate that all catalysts effectively suppress the competing NRR pathway. Among them, the FeCo-VBB exhibits the highest selectivity for urea formation, followed by Fe-VBN.

Conclusions

By performing DFT computations, Fe- and Co-decorated defective BN monolayers have been investigated as electrocatalysts for urea synthesis. The structural stability of the catalysts, N2 adsorption and activation, underlying reaction mechanisms were systematically investigated, alongside the competitive interactions among HER, NRR, and urea synthesis. Fe-VBN exhibits the lowest thermodynamical limiting potential of −0.413 V along the optimal urea synthesis pathway. Whereas, FeCo-VBB, with a compromised limiting of −0.715 V, achieves the highest selectivity for urea production. These findings provide fundamental insights into the electrocatalytic C–N coupling mechanism and offer a theoretical basis for the rational design of bimetallic catalysts supported on defective two-dimensional materials. While Fe/Co-decorated defective BN monolayers demonstrate promising potential for urea synthesis, some limitations of the present theoretical model should also be acknowledged. The proposed catalytic systems are constructed based on idealized structural models and thermodynamic evaluations under simplified conditions. The effects of electrolyte environment, applied potential, and kinetic barriers require further investigation to establish a more comprehensive understanding of the catalytic process. Future studies integrating advanced computational methods, realistic electrochemical simulations, and experimental verification will be essential for validating these theoretical predictions and guiding the development of efficient defect-engineered single- and dual-atom catalysts for sustainable urea production.
