EasyVoid: A Handheld Coandă Effect Uroflowmeter for Accurate and Portable Measurement of Urinary Flow Dynamics
Authors: Ray Chang, Yu-Chen Chen, Hao-Wei Chen, Ruei-Hong Zeng, Manu Prakash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Diagnosis of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) often relies on subjective questionnaires, conventional uroflowmetry or invasive urodynamic study, each with inherent limitations.Methods and procedures: This work presents EasyVoid, a novel handheld device that employs the Coandă effect to enable non-invasive and quantitative measurement of urinary flow. EasyVoid uses a passive fluidic mechanism in which the urinary stream adheres to a curved surface and transfers angular momentum to a helical rotor, allowing direct estimation of flow rate and volume without pressure sensors or weighing systems.Results: EasyVoid simultaneously records key voiding parameters, including flow rate, flow pattern, voiding time, and voided volume, with high temporal precision. In controlled bench-top trials using simulated urine events, measurements obtained from EasyVoid showed strong agreement with standard uroflowmetry across multiple flow patterns.Conclusion: These results demonstrate the potential of EasyVoid for portable and quantitative assessment of urinary flow dynamics, offering a promising solution for remote urinary health monitoring.

Clinical and Translational Impact—EasyVoid demonstrates the potential for portable, quantitative assessment of urinary flow to support future home-based care and longitudinal monitoring of lower urinary tract symptoms. Category: Early/Pre-Clinical Research.

Introduction

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) affect more than half of adults, with prevalence increasing in older populations [1], [2]. LUTS encompass a range of abnormal voiding patterns, including incomplete bladder emptying, nocturia, urinary frequency, difficulty in micturition, and weak urinary stream [3]. These symptoms are especially common in middle-aged and elderly individuals and significantly reduce quality of life [4]. A recent global review estimated that 2.3 billion people were affected by LUTS, nearly 45.8% of the world’s population, which reflects an 18.4% increase since 2008 [5], [6]. In the United States alone, the annual economic burden of LUTS has been estimated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\$}$\end{document}1.9 billion [7]. Although typically not life-threatening, LUTS are frequently under-recognized and inadequately treated, which amplifies both their clinical and societal impact.

Currently, the diagnosis of LUTS still relies heavily on patients’ self-awareness and their willingness to seek consultation with urologists. In outpatient clinic, urologists typically use standardized questionnaires such as the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and IPSS-Quality of Life (IPSS-QoL) [8] to assess the patients’ urinary status. Although IPSS is the most widely used tool worldwide, many older patients struggle to provide accurate responses, which highlights the limitations of subjective reporting. To improve patient comprehension, the Visual Analogue Uroflowmetry Score (VAUS) was developed, which uses a graphical scale of voiding severity to help patients describe their urinary experience [9]. While this approach improves ease of use compared to IPSS, both IPSS and VAUS remain subjective and may not capture true voiding patterns. For this reason, urologists must complement these methods with objective assessments, such as uroflowmetry, to obtain more reliable evaluations [3].

Uroflowmetry remains the standard clinical tool for objectively assessing lower urinary tract function by recording urine volume and flow rate during voiding [3], [10]. It is widely used across the lifespan, in both adult and pediatric populations. In adults, uroflowmetry assists in the evaluation of bladder outlet obstruction, benign prostatic enlargement, and urethral strictures. In children, the 2025 European Association of Urology (EAU) Paediatric Urology Guidelines recommend uroflow assessment in daytime lower urinary tract dysfunction, dysfunctional voiding, neurogenic bladder, and during follow-up after hypospadias repair, where obstructive flow patterns are frequently observed [11]. Parameters measured include maximum urinary flow rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document}), average urinary flow rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\text {ave}}$\end{document}), voided volume, voiding time, and flow pattern, which are crucial for differentiating underlying functional and anatomical abnormalities [3]. Uroflowmetry also informs clinical decision-making by quantifying disease severity and guiding treatment strategies [12], including medication choice and surgical planning. In a typical examination, the patient voids into a specialized container that measures urine weight or volume over time, generating a flow curve.

Despite its established utility, the test has several notable limitations. As a Class I medical device, it must be performed in a clinical setting and requires interpretation by medical professionals, which restricts accessibility and convenience. Patients are required to void on command into a collection apparatus, a process that many find uncomfortable or difficult to complete. In one survey, 58% of patients reported difficulty urinating during testing, citing embarrassment or stress as key factors [13]. Anxiety or misalignment with the device can also lead to invalid data, and one study reported that 63% of uroflowmetry results were uninterpretable [14]. Repeating the test requires waiting for the next voiding opportunity, prolonging evaluation and reducing compliance. Moreover, the equipment is bulky and immobile, occupying significant clinic space and increasing labor and maintenance costs. These challenges limit test reliability and fail to capture the variability of daily voiding behavior, underscoring the need for a more flexible, accessible, and clinically reliable approach.

Sound-based uroflowmetry (SUF) (or impact-acoustic method) represents one of the earliest attempts to bring uroflowmetry beyond the clinic. This approach estimates urinary flow by analyzing acoustic signatures generated when the urine stream impacts a surface, typically the water in a toilet bowl. These signals, captured through built-in or external microphones, are processed to extract flow parameters such as voiding duration, flow rate, and total volume. Early validations by Krhut et al. [15] demonstrated that acoustic data could reliably estimate voiding time and average flow rate in healthy men, findings later confirmed in women by Gärtner et al. [16]. Zvarova et al. [17] further proposed that sound features could correlate with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), suggesting potential diagnostic applications. Recent work has focused on improving accuracy and portability through mobile and AI-based platforms. El Helou et al. [18] introduced a smartphone-based system that recorded voiding sounds and produced flow estimates moderately correlated with standard uroflowmetry. Alvarez et al. [19] applied supervised learning to predict instantaneous flow rates from segmented sound data, achieving low prediction errors across phone models. Lee et al. [20] advanced this concept with a deep-learning framework trained on thousands of recordings, accurately reconstructing flow curves and detecting abnormal patterns. Dawidek et al. [21] clinically evaluated a mobile SUF app in men with LUTS and reported acceptable agreement with clinical uroflowmeters for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} and voiding time. Despite these advances, SUF remains limited by protocol heterogeneity, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Microphone type, placement, and distance from the toilet vary widely across studies, as do toilet geometries and ambient conditions, leading to inconsistent acoustic calibration and reduced generalizability. These dependencies make SUF sensitive to environmental noise and setup variation, restricting its clinical reproducibility and scalability for standardized home use.

Beyond sound-based methods, other portable and home-based systems have been developed to improve convenience and enable remote urinary monitoring. Rattanasomrerk et al. [22] created a weight-based flow measurement device integrated with an NB-IoT module for real-time data transmission to healthcare providers. The system enabled remote tracking of parameters such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} and voided volume, improving clinical monitoring. Triwiyanto et al. [23] designed an IoT-enabled urine flowmeter using an ESP32 microcontroller and load-cell sensor to measure flow rate and display flow curves via a web interface. Arjona et al. [24] introduced UroSound, a smartwatch-based platform that non-invasively detects flow acoustics and estimates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} and voided volume, providing a wearable alternative to smartphone recording. Chen et al. [25] developed a thermal imaging system that evaluates the urination angle and trajectory in real time, offering visual feedback on flow characteristics. While these innovations have improved the portability and user accessibility of uroflowmetry, each remains constrained by indirect measurement, calibration complexity, or dependence on controlled environments. Moreover, the lack of standardized recording protocols continues to hinder reproducibility and cross-study comparison. Collectively, these limitations highlight an unmet need for a compact, physics-based device capable of directly and quantitatively measuring urinary flow without reliance on environmental inference.

In this work, we present EasyVoid, a portable uroflowmeter that employs the Coandă effect to quantify urinary flow dynamics outside clinical settings. The Coandă effect describes how a jet stream adheres to a nearby curved surface, enabling passive guidance and momentum transfer without obstructing flow [26]. In EasyVoid, the urinary stream follows the curvature of a helical rotor through a narrow gap, transferring angular momentum that drives rotor rotation. This passive mechanism eliminates the need for pressure sensors or mass-based measurement while preserving accuracy and reliability. Using simulated voiding events across three prototypes, we calibrated the rotor–flow relationship, reconstructed representative flow patterns, and compared quantitative parameters with standard uroflowmetry. The results demonstrate strong correlation between EasyVoid and standard measurements. We further analyzed urologist interpretations of flow patterns using confusion matrix and calculated agreement statistics with Cohen’s and Fleiss’ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\kappa $\end{document}, establishing the diagnostic consistency of EasyVoid relative to standard uroflowmetry.

EasyVoid Platform

Device Design and Assembly

The EasyVoid device consists of five primary mechanical modules arranged sequentially: a wide-mouth funnel, an upper cap, a cylindrical barrel containing a chiral rotor, a lower cap, and a urine exhaust outlet. (Fig. 1) As urine flows through the funnel and into the cylindrical chamber, the Coandă effect guides the stream along the rotor surface, generating a tangential force that induces rotor rotation. This spinning motion is transduced into an electrical signal by a set of embedded magnets and a Hall effect sensor mounted on the outer casing. The rotor is suspended along a central axis using two axial pins protruding from the top and bottom caps. Pairs of flat washers and O-rings are used at each interface to define axial spacing. Note that the use of Coandă effect to guide the stream between the rotor surface and the inner surface of the barrel is essential for reliable operation in a portable uroflowmeter. Unlike conventional axial turbine flowmeters, which assume fully enclosed pipe flow, or paddle-wheel sensors that require the jet cross-section to be comparable to blade size, urine flow is a thin and unconstrained free-stream jet. In a standard fan-type or paddle-wheel geometry, such a narrow jet can easily adhere to the barrel wall and bypass the rotor entirely, particularly in patients with weak or obstructed streams. In contrast, the Coandă-guided helical rotor creates a narrow annular gap between the rotor and barrel wall that guides the jet to remain attached to the curved surface and follow a spiral path. This configuration helps the fluid to interact with the rotor even when the jet is weak, enabling more consistent capture of angular momentum under realistic voiding conditions.

In our prototype implementation, we repurposed a commercially available water flow indicator (S erounder, ASIN B088DB25V2), which contains a freely rotating dual helical blade rotor. This rotor assembly was integrated with a custom 3D-printed wide-mouth funnel and a slanted urine exhaust outlet to form a complete handheld device. To enable rotational sensing, four neodymium (N52) cylindrical magnets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1\times 1$\end{document} mm) were affixed to the rotor using superglue, with two magnets mounted symmetrically on each helical blade. The polarity of the magnets was verified using a Gaussmeter to ensure an antiphase configuration (i.e., one side with the north pole facing outward and the opposite side with the south pole facing outward) to maximize signal contrast as the rotor spins.

At the junction between the urine collection cone and the rotor chamber, the device incorporates three evenly spaced horizontal spokes. These structural elements serve a dual purpose: mechanical support and passive flow conditioning. As fluid exits the collection cone, the spokes disrupt coherent jet structures and redistribute the incoming flow prior to interaction with the rotor. This design attenuates sensitivity to upstream jet angle, trajectory, and tangential velocity components, promoting a more controlled and repeatable momentum exchange at the rotor surface. While this flow conditioning does not eliminate dependence on inlet velocity conditions, it reduces the influence of directional and transient flow components, thereby improving robustness of rotor response under varied inflow geometries.

To reduce frictional losses and stick–slip behavior in the rotor assembly, the rotor is mechanically isolated from the housing using two low-friction polymer washers placed between the rotor and the cap. In addition, both the washer interfaces and the rotor contact surfaces are treated with a dry polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) coating to minimize static friction and reduce startup torque. This design choice aims to improve sensitivity during low-flow and intermittently varying flow regimes, where inertial and frictional effects are most pronounced.

A linear analog Hall effect sensor (49E LM393) was mounted externally to detect magnetic field fluctuations during rotation. The sensor was positioned on the barrel wall at the same axial height as the embedded magnets to ensure optimal magnetic signal detection during rotor spin. The analog output from the sensor was sampled by an Arduino Nano ESP32 microcontroller, which wirelessly transmitted the signal via Bluetooth to a smartphone using the nRF Connect interface. The nominal sampling rate was set to 500 Hz; however, due to inherent limitations of the system, the actual sampling frequency varied slightly between trials. Accurate time information was reconstructed from timestamped data to determine the effective sampling frequency for each recording. The system was powered by a compact battery module.

Signal Processing

During preprocessing, the raw data were treated as uniformly sampled at a nominal rate of 500 Hz for the purpose of digital filtering. This assumption was used solely to define the filter cutoff and does not affect subsequent time reconstruction. Because the physical rotor dynamics are band-limited to frequencies well below the filter cutoff (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lesssim 6$\end{document} Hz), small variations in the true sampling interval do not materially influence the filtered signal. Actual timestamps were used downstream to assign time and compute the effective sampling frequency for numerical differentiation.

The raw voltage signal from the Hall effect sensor was first re-centered to have zero mean and passed through a 20th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 49 Hz cutoff frequency, selected to retain the rotor’s physical dynamics while removing high-frequency noise. Spurious peaks were algorithmically removed - these included unidirectional peaks (i.e., deflections that lacked a zero crossing) and peaks whose magnitude was less than 45% of the full signal range relative to baseline (Fig. 2A). Urine Flow Rate Calibration

To calibrate flow detection in EasyVoid, we used water as a surrogate fluid to simulate urination under controlled flow rates. A 3 L reservoir bucket supplied water to a speed-variable peristaltic pump (Longer Pump BT600LJ with YZ\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $||25$\end{document} pump head, maximum flow rate: 4140 mL/min or 69 mL/s, 1–600 rpm; #18 tubing, ID: 8.0 mm). Steady flows were generated at discrete rates from 20 to 320 rpm in 10 rpm increments, corresponding to approximately 2–35 mL/s. The tubing outlet was fixed with tape at an appropriate height to direct water simultaneously through EasyVoid and a standard clinical uroflowmetry device (MMS Flowmaster, Medical Measurement Systems) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

EasyVoid was mounted in a cardboard support with a cutout matched to the funnel diameter, secured with tape to maintain a horizontal orientation (0°) or tilted at controlled angles of 5°, 10°, and 15°. The support board rested between a fixed-height surface and an adjustable tripod platform, which allowed fine control of tilt. Angles were confirmed using a smartphone inclinometer application (Protractor). At each flow rate setting, the peristaltic pump was activated for 30 s, and water passed sequentially through the EasyVoid funnel and sensor before collecting in the MMS Flowmaster gravimetric measurement bucket. EasyVoid signals were transmitted via Bluetooth to a smartphone using the nRF Connect application, while the Flowmaster simultaneously recorded volumetric flow rate (mL/s) through its proprietary software (version FR-2.7i, FM-2.7i, DB-2.7h).

For each flow rate and angle condition, three repeated measurements were performed. This data collection protocol was carried out at tilt angles of 0° and repeated for three independent EasyVoid prototypes. At peristaltic pump rate of 150 rpm and 250 rpm, data were collected at four tilt angles (0°, 5°, 10°, 15°) and repeated for three independent EasyVoid prototypes. The average angular velocity of the rotor (rad/s) was computed over the time when the flow reaches plateau using the Hilbert-transform-based signal processing pipeline and compared against the corresponding average volumetric flow rate during plateau recorded by the Flowmaster. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, the measured angular velocity was highly repeatable across trials under identical flow and tilt conditions, but a systematic decrease was observed when the tilt exceeded 10°. Therefore, all subsequent measurements were performed at a fixed tilt angle of 0° to ensure maximum accuracy and consistency.

Urine Flow Rate Reconstruction Validation

To validate urine flow rate reconstruction in realistic clinical scenarios, we tested EasyVoid across physiologically representative voiding patterns generated using the same speed-variable peristaltic pump described above. Under the guidance of a licensed urologist, the pump was programmed to produce known flow patterns including bell-shaped, bell-with-tail, interrupted, plateau, and small-volume profiles. A total of 45 trials were conducted across three EasyVoid prototypes, with each structured waveform (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n=5$\end{document} waveform types \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\times 9$\end{document} trials) distributed evenly across the devices.

The five clinically relevant flow curves and patterns used in this study are defined as follows:

Bell-Shaped

A normal voiding profile where flow increases smoothly to a peak (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document}) and then gradually decreases. This pattern is typically observed in healthy individuals with good bladder contractility and no obstruction. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} is usually > 15 mL/s, and total voided volume > 150 mL.

Bell-with-Tail

Similar to the bell shape but with a prolonged descending phase or secondary flow after brief interruption. It may indicate delayed bladder emptying or residual urine, even when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} remains > 15 mL/s.

Plateau

Characterized by a low, flat flow rate maintained over time without a distinct peak. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document} is typically < 15 mL/s. This pattern may suggest bladder outlet obstruction or increased urethral resistance.

Interrupted (Intermittent, Fractionated)

Flow pattern exhibits multiple peaks separated by zero-flow intervals, indicating flow interruption. The maximum flow rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q_{\max }$\end{document}) is generally less than 15 mL/s and may reflect detrusor underactivity or functional obstruction.

Small-Volume

Defined by a total voided volume < 150 mL, with variable waveform shape. This may occur due to insufficient bladder filling and is generally not used for diagnostic interpretation without retesting.

Although five canonical flow phenotypes were included in the present validation framework, additional clinically relevant patterns exist. In particular, the staccato pattern—typically associated with dysfunctional voiding and characterized by high-frequency oscillatory flow without complete interruption—was not included in this simulation set. Because staccato flow arises from dynamic neuromuscular sphincter contraction, its simulation requires rapid impedance modulation rather than flow-rate modulation. Thus it cannot be faithfully reproduced using the current pump-based mechanical system.

During each trial, flow was simultaneously recorded using EasyVoid and a clinical uroflowmetry device (MMS Flowmaster), which served as the ground-truth reference. All measurements were conducted using water as the working fluid. EasyVoid’s reconstructed flow rate traces were time-aligned to the uroflowmeter output. From each trace, clinically relevant parameters were extracted, including maximum flow rate, total voided volume (via time integration using trapezoidal rule), voiding time, and average flow rate.

For each trial, these parameters were compared to those reported by the uroflowmetry system to assess accuracy. Additionally, the overall shape of reconstructed waveforms was visually evaluated against the ground-truth traces, confirming EasyVoid’s ability to capture characteristic flow dynamics across a range of voiding patterns. All ground-truth waveform classifications were determined from the pump control signal and independently validated via real-time visualization on the MMS software.

To quantify agreement between reconstructed and reference uroflow curves beyond scalar parameters, we evaluated pointwise and alignment-aware error metrics. For each trial, EasyVoid and standard uroflow signals were first interpolated onto a common uniform time grid spanning their overlapping duration. Whole-curve mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) were computed directly from these aligned signals. To account for temporal misalignment between curves, we further computed DTW-aligned MAE and RMSE using constrained dynamic time warping. Constrained DTW alignment permits limited nonlinear time adjustments while preserving amplitude differences [27]. To ensure physiologically plausible alignment, warping was restricted using a Sakoe–Chiba band corresponding to ±1.5 s. After determining the optimal warping path, MAE and RMSE were calculated along this path, providing error metrics that account for temporal misalignment without distorting flow magnitudes. These metrics are reported in units of mL/s and used to assess waveform-level agreement.

The curves (90 curves in total) generated by standard uroflowmetry and EasyVoid were further randomized, relabeled, and evaluated by urologists. Six independent board-certified urologists were asked to classify each curve into one of five predefined categories: bell, bell-with-tail, interrupted, plateau, or small volume. The study followed a single-blind design, in which the urologists were blinded to the ground truth while the data analyst was aware of it. Agreement between EasyVoid and standard uroflowmetry interpretations was summarized using a confusion matrix. Inter-rater reliability among the six urologists for all 90 curves was quantified using Fleiss’ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\kappa $\end{document} statistic.

Waveform-Level Agreement

In addition to parameter-level accuracy, we evaluated agreement of entire uroflow curves using quantitative error metrics. Across 45 trials, the raw whole-curve error between EasyVoid and standard uroflowmetry yielded MAE = 2.16 mL/s (SD = 0.80 mL/s) and RMSE = 2.68 mL/s (SD = 1.05 mL/s). When temporal misalignment was accounted for using constrained DTW alignment, errors decreased substantially, with DTW-aligned MAE = 0.92 mL/s (SD = 0.56 mL/s) and DTW-aligned RMSE = 1.54 mL/s (SD = 0.78 mL/s). These results demonstrate that the primary discrepancies between the two systems arise from temporal misalignment rather than amplitude distortion. After alignment, average amplitude differences are well below 2 mL/s, indicating close agreement at the waveform level.

Discussion

This study establishes the feasibility of EasyVoid as a portable and accurate uroflowmetry system for measuring urinary flow outside clinical settings. The device employs a passive rotor-based sensing mechanism driven by the Coandă effect to translate urine flow into rotational motion, enabling quantitative assessment of flow rate, voided volume, and voiding pattern without pumps, pressure sensors, or electrical contacts. This purely fluidic approach simplifies device design, reduces cost, and minimizes sources of mechanical failure. By removing the need for controlled environments or specialized training, EasyVoid enables real-world testing conditions that better reflect habitual voiding behavior. Future iterations will also require dedicated hygiene-oriented design features to support routine cleaning and infection control in home settings.

EasyVoid accurately quantifies key urodynamic parameters, including maximum flow rate, voided volume, voiding time, and average flow rate. Comparison with Conventional and Sound-Based Uroflowmetry: Toward Naturalistic and Scalable Uroflowmetry

Conventional gravimetric uroflowmetry remains the clinical gold standard for quantifying urination dynamics, yet its implementation requires complete urine collection to infer flow from mass change. This design inherently affects patient’s experience and modifies the act of voiding. Patients must direct urine into a collection vessel instead of a toilet, which introduces hesitation, alters flow behavior, and affects the accuracy of measurements. The method also imposes logistical and hygienic burdens, as the containers must be cleaned, disinfected, and handled under infection-control protocols, all of which increase healthcare staff workload and risk of contamination.

Sound-based uroflowmetry (SUF) has thus emerged as a non-contact alternative. Leverages common devices such as smartphones or smartwatches to capture acoustic signatures of urination and estimates flow by training models on reference datasets. Despite its potential, SUF faces substantial limitations, as its accuracy depends on precise calibration and strict experimental control (Supplementary Table S1). Studies often require specified microphone distances, angles, toilet setups, bathroom geometries, and specific water levels [15], [18], [19]. Even minor deviations in distance or ambient noise degrade performance. These constraints restrict reproducibility and make calibration non-transferable between homes, limiting real-world scalability. Reported correlations also vary widely, with correlation coefficients between 0.38 and 0.9 across studies (Supplementary Table S2), reflecting the fragility of purely acoustic inference.

Besides, there is currently no consensus on how SUF protocols should be standardized. Studies differ widely even on simple parameters such as the required water level in the toilet, ranging from 100 mL to 3L [15], [18], [19]. This heterogeneity makes it impossible to generalize findings or build universally applicable models. Moreover, as voiding is often triggered by urgency, it is impractical to expect patients with urinary dysfunction to pause, prepare a specific environment, and align their actions with a laboratory-grade protocol before every voiding event. These procedural demands undermine the core goal of unobtrusive and naturalistic monitoring, and severely hinder the deployment of SUF in home settings.

EasyVoid departs from both approaches. Its rotor-based sensing captures the mechanical interaction between urine stream and the Coandă-guided rotor surface, generating a direct physical signal independent of environmental acoustics. Unlike SUF, which infers flow from indirect spectral features, EasyVoid directly captures flow events through momentum transfer, making it robust, repeatable, and immune to environmental noise or room acoustics. The device is compatible with any standard toilet without toilet-specific calibration, electronics exposure, collection apparatus, or any special setup. Because the patient can void naturally, the data reflect habitual patterns rather than constrained laboratory behavior. The absence of protocol-specific variables removes the main source of inter-study variability seen in SUF.

This shift from indirect acoustic inference to direct hydrodynamic measurement defines a new category of uroflowmetry which is naturalistic, portable, and scalable. Cost considerations further highlight this distinction. Whereas conventional gravimetric systems require dedicated clinical hardware costing thousands of dollars, the EasyVoid prototype relies entirely on commodity electronics with a bill of materials of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\$}$\end{document}60–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\$}$\end{document}90 USD (Supplementary Table S3), enabling a fundamentally more accessible and scalable deployment model. EasyVoid preserves key quantitative flow metrics traditionally obtained with gravimetric systems under controlled conditions, while matching or exceeding the correlation metrics of recent SUF platforms. Its performance demonstrates that a simple passive fluidic sensor can achieve clinically relevant precision under non-laboratory constraints, supporting its potential for practical home-based urinary diagnostics and long-term bladder health tracking.

Engineering Principles and Design Tradeoffs

Operating Principle and Design Space

The core of EasyVoid’s sensing mechanism relies on a rotor system that converts the momentum of the urine jet into a measurable rotational signal. The urine stream attaches to the curved surface of a helical rotor through the Coandă effect, which causes the flow to remain guided along the rotor’s surface rather than detaching prematurely. As the fluid moves through the narrow annular gap between the rotor and barrel wall, it transfers angular momentum to the blades, and the reaction torque drives rotation. A pair of small embedded magnets and a Hall effect sensor positioned outside the barrel detect the rotor’s angular velocity in real time, providing a robust, contact-free readout of flow rate. This physical coupling between jet adhesion and momentum transfer enables EasyVoid to function as a true flow-through system, unlike gravimetric or acoustic methods that depend on urine collection or indirect signal inference.

The rotor geometry defines the operating window of sensitivity and stability. A helical structure sustains the Coandă effect along a spiral trajectory, allowing the flow to remain attached even when it does not completely fill the barrel cross-section. If the helical angle is too shallow, the stream travels axially and detaches from the surface; if too steep, curvature becomes insufficient to generate the lateral forces and spin the rotor. Similarly, the clearance between rotor and barrel determines the balance between adhesion and drag. A large gap risks detachment during low-flow states, while an overly narrow gap increases viscous losses and reduces rotational responsiveness. These observations define a theoretical design space that future optimization can explore systematically through controlled experiments and fluid dynamic modeling.

Dynamic Response and Hysteresis

As a passive, momentum-driven mechanical sensor, the EasyVoid rotor exhibits dynamic response characteristics governed by the balance between flow-induced torque, inertia, and friction. These dynamics define the temporal bandwidth of the system and shape how rapid flow transitions are represented in the reconstructed uroflow curves.

Rotor inertia contributes to finite response time during flow acceleration and deceleration. Our data indicate that the most pronounced deviations from reference measurements arise in low-speed regimes, such as plateau and interrupted voiding patterns, where the hydrodynamic torque generated by the urine stream can become comparable to static friction and bearing losses. Under these conditions, the rotor does not necessarily rotate smoothly and may instead transition between intermittent motion and temporary arrest. This stick–slip behavior produces short-timescale fluctuations in angular velocity, which can manifest as local over- or under-estimation of instantaneous flow rate.

The rotor response also exhibits hysteresis. During rapidly increasing flow, inertial effects can lead to transient overshoot, whereas during flow deceleration the rotor may continue rotating briefly due to stored angular momentum. This asymmetric response is most evident in interrupted and plateau-type voiding patterns, where frequent transitions between zero and nonzero flow amplify dynamic lag. These behaviors reflect intrinsic properties of passive mechanical transducers rather than artifacts of signal processing or electronic acquisition.

These dynamic characteristics motivate specific design tradeoffs and guide future engineering refinements. Reduced rotor moment of inertia, lower-friction bearing materials such as jewel bearings, improved manufacturing tolerances, and controlled surface wetting are expected to improve low-speed rotational stability. In addition, non-contact damping strategies such as eddy-current braking offer a promising route to achieve more symmetric and predictable transient response without introducing additional mechanical wear. Algorithmic compensation based on identified rotor dynamics may further improve point-wise temporal fidelity. Collectively, these strategies aim to refine the dynamic response while preserving the fundamental sensing principle based on Coandă-guided momentum transfer.

Usability Constraints on Tilt Sensitivity

Because EasyVoid operates by capturing the momentum of a gravity-driven free-stream urine jet, its performance is inherently influenced by device orientation relative to the vertical axis. Unlike enclosed pipe-flow sensors, where the flow direction is geometrically constrained, free-jet interception introduces a geometric dependence between jet trajectory, rotor alignment, and effective momentum transfer. As a result, tilt sensitivity represents an intrinsic tradeoff associated with open, free-stream flow measurement rather than a device-specific artifact.

Experimental characterization shows that reliable momentum capture and stable rotor engagement are maintained when the device is held within approximately ±10° of vertical orientation. Within this range, the jet trajectory remains sufficiently aligned with the Coandă-guided rotor surface to sustain continuous attachment and efficient angular momentum transfer. Beyond this range, misalignment between the incoming jet and the rotor geometry reduces effective coupling, leading to degraded rotational response. This behavior reflects hydrodynamic constraints of free-stream jet interception rather than limitations of sensing electronics or signal processing. In the present study, all measurements were conducted at 0° tilt to isolate intrinsic sensing performance under controlled conditions. This orientation represents the optimal configuration for evaluating the fundamental accuracy and repeatability of the rotor-based sensing mechanism.

These considerations also motivate usability-oriented design strategies. Future iterations will incorporate tilt-aware features, including onboard inertial sensing to detect excessive orientation deviation and provide user feedback, as well as mechanical and ergonomic refinements that naturally promote near-vertical alignment during voiding. Together, these strategies aim to improve robustness in real-world settings while preserving the advantages of a passive, free-stream measurement architecture.

Signal Processing Considerations

The signal-processing strategy in EasyVoid is designed to reflect the intrinsic bandwidth of the sensing mechanism rather than the raw bandwidth of the electronic readout. The Hall sensor voltage contains substantial high-frequency fluctuations arising from analog sensor noise and wireless data transmission, which do not correspond to meaningful fluid–mechanical information. In contrast, the underlying rotor dynamics are intrinsically low-frequency, with typical rotational frequencies on the order of only a few hertz even at maximum physiologically relevant flow rates. Accordingly, a 20th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 49 Hz cutoff was applied to suppress electronic noise while fully preserving the mechanical signal. High-frequency fluctuations beyond the mechanical response bandwidth of the rotor are therefore not resolved, as they do not correspond to physically meaningful flow dynamics in the present system. Consistent with this interpretation, the highest reconstruction fidelity occurs during high-flow bell-shaped patterns, whereas the largest errors appear at low rotor speeds where frictional and inertial effects dominate, indicating that the dominant performance constraints in the current system are primarily mechanical rather than electronic noise or signal processing.

Hygiene and Cleaning Considerations

The present engineering prototype was developed to evaluate sensing feasibility and does not yet incorporate dedicated infection-control features such as disposable liners or sealed fluid paths. As a result, considerations related to hygiene and cleaning are particularly important for any future home-use implementation. Routine cleaning will be necessary to prevent biofilm formation and accumulation of urinary residues during repeated use. Such deposits on the rotor surface or spindle can increase friction, alter surface wettability, and modify Coandă adhesion, potentially leading to changes in startup behavior and long-term calibration stability.

Based on preliminary laboratory experience, a two-level maintenance strategy is anticipated to be sufficient. For routine use, simple warm-water rinsing of the internal flow channel after each voiding session effectively removes soluble residues and prevents salt crystallization. Periodic daily disinfection using 3% hydrogen peroxide is proposed as a practical home-use approach to limit microbial growth and biofilm development [29]. Hydrogen peroxide is widely available, inexpensive, and commonly used for household disinfection. It provides broad antimicrobial activity while being generally compatible with the materials used in the current prototype, including stainless steel, common plastics, and certain elastomeric components [30]. Unlike bleach-based cleaners, dilute hydrogen peroxide decomposes into water and oxygen and does not leave persistent chemical residues that could affect surface wetting or sensor performance.

The specific procedures for rinsing, drying, and disinfection were not formally evaluated in the present study and therefore are not standardized here. Importantly, the current prototype was not designed for repeated clinical use without further engineering refinement. Future patient-ready versions of EasyVoid will incorporate hygiene-oriented design elements such as disposable urine-contact interfaces, sealed bearing systems, and geometries that promote complete drainage and rapid drying. These improvements will allow the development of validated cleaning protocols that maintain measurement accuracy while minimizing cross-contamination risk. Accordingly, the results presented in this work should be interpreted as a pre-clinical demonstration of sensing capability rather than a finalized medical device. Systematic studies will be required in future work to quantify how residue accumulation, cleaning frequency, and material selection influence long-term calibration stability and durability under real-world conditions.

EasyVoid’s ability to reconstruct these values with high accuracy and strong correlation to gold-standard devices supports its potential application in both screening and longitudinal follow-up assessment of such conditions.

Beyond single-event analysis, EasyVoid is designed to support mobile and real-world monitoring of urination over extended periods - an ability unattainable by conventional gravimetric or sound-based systems. Because EasyVoid is portable and operates independently of fixed toilet configurations, it has the potential to capture repeated voiding events in real-world settings and generate reliable 24-hour or 3-day bladder diaries [3], which are widely regarded as critical tools in the clinical management of complex urinary symptoms. Beyond flow quality, these longitudinal records provide additional insights into frequency, urgency, nocturia, and voided volume trends, which are critical pieces of information to assess overactive bladder, bladder pain syndrome, and cystitis. Physicians could, in future implementations, leverage such data to evaluate diurnal patterns, quantify treatment response, and personalize therapy based on each patient’s habitual voiding behavior. By enabling continuous, naturalistic data acquisition, EasyVoid defines a pathway toward bridging the gap between clinic-based diagnostics and home-based physiological monitoring, providing a dimension of insight that clinic-bound conventional tools simply cannot achieve.

EasyVoid’s portability and low cost further enhance accessibility. Standard uroflowmetry systems are large, immobile, and expensive, which restricts their use to specialized facilities. In contrast, EasyVoid offers a compact, affordable alternative that supports repeated testing at home or in outpatient settings. This combination of quantitative accuracy and longitudinal usability positions EasyVoid not only as a diagnostic instrument but also as a potential platform for precision monitoring and patient-centered management in urology.

Limitations and Future Work

Experimental Scope and Physiological Generalizability

While the results demonstrate the feasibility of EasyVoid as a flow-through uroflowmetry platform, several limitations related to experimental scope and implementation remain and will need to be addressed in future studies. The present study was designed as a controlled pre-clinical validation to establish the feasibility and intrinsic sensing performance of EasyVoid. Accordingly, all experiments were conducted using peristaltic pump–driven water under a fixed jet–cone geometry. While this configuration enables precise, repeatable control of flow rate and waveform shape, it does not fully replicate the variability of human voiding, where jet geometry, outlet conditions, and tangential momentum can vary substantially across individuals and voiding events.

Several aspects of the experimental design partially address these concerns. The pump-generated jet operates in a turbulent regime (Re \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx ~3$\end{document},200 under representative conditions), indicating that key turbulent flow characteristics relevant to physiological voiding are already captured at the benchtop level. In addition, the junction between the collection cone and the rotor incorporates three horizontal spokes that act as a passive flow-conditioning element, redistributing the incoming stream prior to rotor interaction and reducing sensitivity to upstream jet angle and trajectory. Nevertheless, validation using human voiding data remains essential to fully assess performance under physiological and pathological conditions and will be a critical focus of future work.

A related limitation is the use of water as a surrogate fluid. Urine differs from water in density, viscosity, surface tension, and biochemical composition, all of which may influence Coandă adhesion, rotor startup torque, and frictional behavior. Most of these differences are modest: urine density typically differs from water by less than 3% (specific gravity 1.005–1.030), and kinematic viscosity varies by less than 7% under physiological conditions [33]. Moreover, despite containing low concentrations of proteins and sediments, urine generally behaves as a Newtonian fluid [33]. On the contrary, surface tension exhibits a larger deviation from water and may affect wetting and film continuity, particularly at low flow rates. The differences in surface tension can thus potentially influence startup dynamics, stick–slip behavior, and hysteresis [34]. As a result, the present findings should be interpreted as a feasibility demonstration rather than a definitive characterization under physiological conditions. Future studies using synthetic and human urine will be required to fully assess these effects.

Unmodeled Voiding Phenotypes

An additional clinically relevant voiding phenotype not included in the present validation is the staccato pattern, which is typically associated with dysfunctional voiding and intermittent external sphincter contraction [11]. Unlike plateau flow caused by anatomical obstruction, staccato flow is characterized by high-frequency oscillations without complete interruption, reflecting neuromuscular discoordination.

Because our experimental framework relies on programmable pump-generated flow profiles, it cannot replicate dynamic sphincter-induced oscillatory behavior, which would require rapid impedance modulation rather than flow-rate modulation. The minor fluctuations observed in some plateau reconstructions are attributable to mechanical factors rather than physiological sphincter contraction. Future validation studies involving human subjects, particularly pediatric patients with dysfunctional voiding, will be required to determine the device’s ability to distinguish plateau obstruction from true staccato patterns.

Manufacturability and Calibration Variability

In the current study, each device was calibrated individually, reflecting variability inherent to hand-assembled prototypes. Factors such as manual rotor balancing and placement of the magnetic element introduce device-to-device differences that necessitate separate calibration curves and contribute to variability in fitted parameters and goodness-of-fit metrics. This represents a limitation of the present implementation. Note that, however, individual calibration is common in many clinical sensing modalities and does not inherently preclude scalability. A manufacturable design incorporating tighter tolerances, standardized assembly procedures, and integrated alignment features is expected to substantially reduce inter-device variability and enable more uniform calibration strategies.

Conclusion

EasyVoid establishes a new direction in uroflowmetry by demonstrating the feasibility of a timely, accurate, portable, and fully non-invasive system for evaluating lower urinary tract function. The present study is intended to establish engineering feasibility and clinical relevance, forming the basis for subsequent human validation studies currently in planning. Unlike conventional gravimetric systems that require complete urine collection or sound-based approaches that suffer from environmental acoustics and protocol-related variability, EasyVoid quantifies urinary flow directly through hydrodynamic interaction between the urine jet and a helical rotor. This flow-through design enables natural, real-time measurement without altering voiding behavior or requiring special toilet configurations.

Through mechanical sensing of flow-induced rotor motion, EasyVoid accurately measures key clinical parameters, such as maximum flow rate, voiding time, and voided volume, with strong correlation to clinical gold standard. The system performs reliably across diverse voiding patterns and settings, establishing the foundation for home-based monitoring, telemedicine integration, and extended bladder diary analysis. These capabilities enable longitudinal tracking of urinary function, allowing clinicians to identify trends, assess treatment response, and refine management strategies based on real-world data.

Moreover, EasyVoid’s scalability and ease of use position it as a practical tool with the potential to support early disease detection, medication titration, and real-time patient feedback. These capabilities may, in future clinical studies, improve adherence, reduce the delay in clinical interventions, and potentially prevent renal damage or the progression to surgical intervention. In doing so, EasyVoid has the potential to enhance the clinical workflow and contribute to cost-effective and personalized urologic care.

In summary, EasyVoid holds transformative potential in shifting uroflowmetry from a clinic-bound, episodic procedure to a patient-centric, continuous, and data-rich paradigm, ultimately empowering both clinicians and patients in the management of lower urinary tract health.
