Redefining the association of triglycerides with all-cause and Cardiovascular Disease Mortality in the Chinese population: a nationwide, population-based, prospective cohort study of 3.8 million Chinese adults
Authors: Guangyu Li, Linkang He, Xueke Bai, Shi Chen, Hao Dai, Guangda He, Yang Yang, Xiaoyan Zhang, Jianlan Cui, Xi Li, Chris Packard, Jan Boren, Liang Chen, Shengshou Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCurrent international guidelines on dyslipidemia were formulated based on the research performed mostly in White populations. However, whether the association of triglycerides (TGs) with cardiovascular disease (CVD) differs between Chinese and White populations is unknown.Methods3,789,025 participants from the China Health Evaluation And risk Reduction through nationwide Teamwork (ChinaHEART) were evaluated for the association between TGs and all-cause and CVD mortality with Cox regression models. The findings were tested for sex-specific differences and contrasted with another comparison cohort of 434,919 UK Biobank participants.Findings92,888 Chinese adults died during a median follow-up of 4.6 years. All-cause and CVD mortality initially increased with TG elevation abruptly and plateaued in Chinese men, while U-shaped associations were seen in Chinese women. In contrast, CVD risk increased incrementally with TGs in White people. Hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) were 1.02 (0.97–1.07), 1.05 (1.00–1.11), 1.07 (1.02–1.22), and 1.19 (1.13–1.24) from TG quintile 2 to 5 for White men, and 1.14 (1.08–1.21), 1.17 (1.11–1.24), 1.26 (1.18–1.33), and 1.41 (1.33–1.50) for White women, respectively. The association with CVD mortality was stronger in Chinese men who were lean, normotensive, and smoking but in Chinese women with diabetes.InterpretationThe association of TGs with CVD was different for the White and the Chinese populations. That mortality risk elevated in conventionally low-to-moderate TG range implicates that more aggressive intervention may be merited for Chinese and other East Asian populations.FundingThe National High Level Hospital Clinical Research Funding (2023-GSP-ZD-2, 2023-GSP-RC-01), the 10.13039/501100005045Ministry of Finance, and the 10.13039/501100018684National Health Commission of China.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We initially performed literature search on PubMed using the term triglyceride, after excluding “triglyceride-glucose index” OR “TyG”, combined with other terms cardiovascular disease OR CVD, over last 10 years from June 5th, 2025. A total of 13,636 results were found. We further limited our search to the cohort studies conducted in the Chinese general population by adding more terms: China AND general population AND cohort study, in which a total of 314 results were found. The majority of large-scale studies examining the relationship of TGs with CVD were mainly conducted in White populations, such as the Framingham Heart Study, the Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, the National Health And Nutrition Examination Survey, and the UK Biobank. Current international guidelines for dyslipidemia management and TG control were formed based on the findings drawn from White populations, which was adopted almost globally. However, they may not fit East Asian populations, especially the Chinese population which bears a disproportionally intense CVD burden. It has long been known that the CVD epidemiology is different in Asians and White populations and that hypertriglyceridemia and ischemic stroke (IS) are more common in East Asians. However, the limited studies conducted in East Asians were small in sample sizes and inconsistent in findings. Whether the association of TGs with CVD varied across different populations and whether TG elevation contributed to the disproportionally high IS burden in East Asia have not been investigated explicitly.

Added value of this study

We leveraged a nationwide, population-based, prospective cohort with 3.8 million Chinese adults to evaluate the association of TGs with all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. We also drew a comparison cohort from the UK Biobank project to assess the potential disparities in Chinese and White populations and potential sex-specific variations in the association between TGs and the mortality outcomes. First, we found that the association with IS mortality was the most evident in Chinese men, with no comparable associations being observed in Chinese women or in White people. Secondly, with TGs truncated above 200 mg/dL, the association with IS mortality resembles a close-to-linear curve in Chinese men, whereas the association with IHD risk was more significant for White people. Thirdly, the relationship of TGs with CVD mortality was evident in Chinese men with a lower metabolic risk profile, especially those who were lean and normotensive. In contrast, the association was remarkable for Chinese women with diabetes. Lastly, smoking had synergistic effects with TGs on CVD, especially for IS mortality in Chinese men, but quitting smoking seemed to alleviate such a synergistic effect.

Implications of all the available evidence

To our knowledge, our study was the largest cohort study examining the relationship between plasma TGs and CVD in Chinese general population, with the findings compared against another cohort of White people and examined for sex differences. The finding that TGs were associated with IS mortality particularly in Chinese men indicated that the pathological relationship between lipids and CVD varied across populational groups. Moreover, the finding that TG elevation within conventionally low-to-moderate range was also associated with increased IS mortality validated that Chinese men are extremely sensitive to any levels of TG elevation, and therefore more aggressive TG-lowering intervention may be merited for both Chinese men and other East Asian populations. Furthermore, TG management for low-risk men (normotensive and normal body mass) and more targeted TG intervention for high-risk women (especially those with diabetes) may be more effective. Lastly, promoting lifestyle modification along with TG intervention, particularly smoking cessation, is of great importance.

Introduction

Elevated plasma triglycerides (TGs) are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), as shown in previous population-based cohort studies.1, 2, 3, 4 Additional genetic evidence of Mendelian randomization studies validated their robust association with certain CVD outcomes, especially myocardial infarction (MI) and coronary heart disease (CHD).5, 6, 7, 8 Although low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) has been recommended as the primary intervention target for patients at risk of CVD, a residual risk remains after achieving optimal LDL-C levels with statin therapies, partially attributed to elevated TGs.9, 10, 11, 12 Hence, TGs have emerged as a potential intervention target to further decrease the residual cardiovascular risk.

Currently, most studies assessing the association of TGs with CVD were conducted in White populations, which laid the basis of current guidelines for TG management.13, 14, 15 These prevention strategies and treatment paradigms have been adopted almost globally based on the presumption that their relationship is uniform across different populations. However, it has long been known that the prevalence of hypertriglyceridemia16,17 is higher in East Asians and the pattern of CVD epidemiology is different from White populations.18,19 Hence, the relationship of TGs with CVD could be different for East Asian populations from the others, which has yet to be explicitly appreciated.

In previous cohort studies, the dose-dependent association of TGs with various CVD outcomes has been identified in East Asian populations.20, 21, 22, 23 However, these studies were relatively small in sample size and low in population diversity, compromising their generalizability to a broader range of East Asian individuals and their comparability with non-Asian populations. In addition, CVD mortality burden has been disproportionally intense in East Asia, especially in the Chinese population,24 but previous studies paid less attention to the association of TGs with mortality, with their findings inconsistent and the evidence drawn from East Asian populations lacking.25 Besides, sex-specific variations in dyslipidemia patterns and CVD risk have been implicated,26 but they have not been thoroughly evaluated in either Chinese or other East Asian populations.

Hence, by exploring a nationwide, population-based, prospective cohort with 3.8 million Chinese adults, this study aimed to evaluate the relationship of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality for the Chinese population, with the results tested for sex-dependent differences and contrasted with a White population. Our study would provide in-depth insights into different associations of TGs with CVD across various populational groups and inform more precise TG management.

Methods

Study design and population

An observational cohort was formed based on the China Health Evaluation And risk Reduction through nationwide Teamwork (ChinaHEART) project, formerly called China Patient-centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events Million Persons Project (China PEACE MPP).27, 28, 29, 30, 31 It is a nationwide, population-based cohort study with continuous enrollment since 2014, aiming to identify major CVD risk factors and novel intervention targets for Chinese general population.32 During the study period, 353 study sites across 31 provinces of mainland China were established, from which 4,404,586 participants aged 35–75 years old who had resided in the community for at least 6 months in the past 12 months were recruited. After excluding the participants with site-level measurement problems (n = 25,334), who were non-fasted (n = 524,906), and who missed the blood lipid measurements (n = 65,321), totally 3,789,025 participants were included for the final analysis (Figure S1), with a median follow-up of 4.6 years. They were stratified into 3 groups—secondary prevention group, primary prevention group, and low-risk group—according to their CVD risks, and none of them received any additional interventions (Supplementary Methods). The ChinaHEART project has been approved by the central ethics committee of Fuwai Hospital (2014-574) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02536456), with all participants provided written informed consents by enrollment.

A comparison cohort was formed based on the UK Biobank (UKB), which is a population-based, prospective cohort study with over 500,000 White participants aged from 40 to 69 years old who were enrolled from 2006 to 2010.33 A total of 434,919 participants were finally included in the present analysis. The exclusion criteria and overview of the comparison cohort were detailed in Supplementary Methods. UKB has been approved by the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (reference number: 11/NW/0382), with all its participants provided written informed consents. The present study was performed under the application number of 87841.

We acknowledged that the term “sex” carries a broad range of definitions, and we used it to refer to the “sex given at birth” in our study. “Chinese”, “Asian”, and “White” were defined based on self-reported race/ethnicity categories, and we acknowledged that ethnicities are sociocultural constructs instead of biological traits. The finding presentation was adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.34

Data collection, covariates, and definitions

At the initial visit, trained personnel conducted in-person interviews with every participant to gather their demographic information (e.g. education level, annual household income, health insurance, and marital status), lifestyles (e.g. smoking and alcohol drinking), medical history (e.g. diabetes mellitus [DM], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], and cancer), and medication use (e.g., lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic medications) (DM refers to both types of diabetes). Participants would also undergo standardized laboratory biochemical tests and anthropometric measurements. Details of baseline examinations and measurements for the ChinaHEART participants were presented in Supplementary Methods.

Study outcomes

In the present study, International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) was adopted to define the causes of death. Specifically, all-cause mortality and CVD mortality (I00–I99) were considered as primary outcomes, with CVD mortality subtypes as secondary outcomes, including ischemic heart disease (IHD) mortality (I20–I25), stroke mortality (I60–I63), and ischemic stroke (IS) mortality (I63). Mortality data came from National Mortality Surveillance System and Vital Registration of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. These records were from local healthcare institutions across the country and annually checked against with residential and health insurance information to ensure the accuracy and timeliness. Details about matching these outcomes in the comparison cohort based on UKB were presented in Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of participants were displayed by TG quintiles and by biological sexes. Categorical variables were characterized by counts and percentages, and continuous variables were presented by mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR).

Adjusted incidence rates were calculated with g-computation with standard parametric regression models35 (Supplementary Methods). Association of TGs with mortality outcomes was assessed using Cox proportional hazard regression models, and the proportional hazards (PH) assumptions were examined using the plot of Schoenfeld residual tests, in which no violations were observed.

Adjustment was made for sex (for analyses involving all participants only), age, education level, DM, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mmHg, LDL-C, smoking, alcohol drinking, overweight or obesity, lipid-lowering drugs, and history of CVD. No significant multicollinearity was found among the variables in regression models in multicollinearity tests with variance inflation factors (VIF) (Table S1). More specifically, on continuous scales, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 pre-defined knots (5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentile in default) were adopted to evaluate their nonlinear relationship. On ordinal scales, multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to examine their association across 5 TG quintiles. The TG quintiles in which the lowest risk fell in on continuous scales were selected as reference groups for men and women, respectively, which ensured that they represent the empirically lowest risk point and thus more conservative estimations of any high-risk quintiles. Since most participants had TGs ≤200 mg/dL, we further truncated TGs above this level and estimated the risks of CVD mortality and its subtypes per 30 mg/dL increment of TGs in this range for both grasping details of the change of risk and avoiding overfitting of the trend. The findings were compared with the comparison cohort formed based on UKB (Supplementary Methods).

The relationship of TGs with CVD mortality was examined across different subgroups and strata. First, the interaction between continuous TGs with covariates on CVD mortality was tested, with p values for interaction estimated by multiplicative interaction effects. For interaction terms with significant interaction effects, further forest plots were graphed for evaluating the disparities between binary groups. Secondly, given that TG elevation is a major determinant of metabolic syndrome (MetS),36 the relationship of TGs with CVD mortality was assessed throughout the strata by the number of metabolic disorders (Supplementary Methods). Their association was also examined across the three CVD risk stratifications as mentioned above. Thirdly, the effect modification mediated by smoking on CVD mortality and its subtypes was further analyzed by stratifying them into 4 groups by smoking status (i.e., current smoker versus non-smokers) and TG levels (i.e., ≤quintile 1 versus > quintile 1), with their HRs and 95% CIs calculated by Cox models. It was repeated for previous smokers in place of current smokers to determine whether quitting smoking could alter the effect modification by smoking.

To validate the robustness of our primary findings, we performed further sensitivity analyses and complementary analyses: (1) using nonlinear splines in alternative to RCS to assess the nonlinear relationship of TGs with mortality outcomes; (2) replotting RCS after excluding participants with cancer, COPD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), history of CVD, taking lipid-lowering treatments, either with at least one of these comorbidities or taking lipid-lowering drugs, and being followed for less than 3 years; (3) Fine–Gray competing risk models; (4) Population Attributable Fractions (PAF) for the proportions of mortality attributable to TG elevation.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.1.2 with packages “survminer”, “rms”, “SmoothHR”, “cmprsk”, and “AF”. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study included 3,789,025 ChinaHEART participants, comprising 1,517,326 men (40%; mean [SD] age: 56.6 [10.1] years) and 2,271,699 women (60%; mean [SD] age: 55.7 [9.8] years). Baseline characteristics of included participants were displayed in Table 1 and Table S2 that no differences from all ChinaHEART participants were observed (Table S3). Demographically, the residential distribution was uniform (rural areas: 60.3%; South China: 59.7%). Most participants had health insurance (97.7%) and were married (93.0%), but only a small proportion of them had had college-level or above education (7.8%) or annual household income >50,000 yuan (18.6%). As for lifestyle, the prevalence of current smokers and alcohol drinkers was significantly higher in men (45.9% men and 2.1% women currently smoked; 46.0% men and 8.5% women currently drank). As for medical history, metabolic disorders (e.g. DM, obesity or overweight) were more common than chronic, life-threatening comorbidities (e.g. cancer, COPD). The median TG level for women was 121 mg/dL, which was slightly higher for men (116 mg/dL). To compare with White individuals, a comparable cohort based on the UK Biobank was formed (n = 434,919). Baseline characteristics of the UK Biobank cohort were characterized in Table S4. Association of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality in the Chinese population

We assessed the association of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality in the Chinese population. For the total population, TGs demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with all-cause mortality and CVD mortality, with the mortality risks initially increased abruptly as TGs elevated and levelled off at higher TGs (Fig. 1). In Chinese men, a similar trajectory was found, with all-cause and CVD mortality increased sharply with TG escalation initially, followed by a plateau (Fig. 1). Compared to the reference group, the HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02–1.07), 1.03 (1.00–1.05), 1.02 (0.99–1.05), and 0.98 (0.96–1.01) for quintile 2 to quintile 5, respectively. Corresponding HRs for CVD mortality were 1.10 (1.06–1.14), 1.13 (1.09–1.18), 1.18 (1.13–1.23), and 1.15 (1.10–1.20), respectively (Figure S3A). In Chinese women, U-shaped associations of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality were observed (Fig. 1). Compared with the reference group, the HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.06 (1.02–1.09), 1.06 (1.03–1.10), 1.02 (0.99–1.05), and 1.04 (1.01–1.07) from the lowest to the highest quintiles. Association of TGs with CVD and its subtypes in Chinese and White populations

In Chinese women, the association of TGs with CVD mortality and its subtypes was less evident than in Chinese men and was only significant at relatively higher TG levels (Fig. 1). A similar trend was seen when evaluating their association by TG quintiles on ordinal scales (Figure S3B). However, in White women, both the association with IS and IHD risk were significant, and the association with IHD risk was slightly stronger (Figure S4B). Interaction of TGs with metabolic disorders and smoking

We evaluated the interaction effects of covariates on the association of TGs with CVD mortality. Metabolic disorders (DM, hypertension, and overweight or obesity) and lifestyle (smoking) had different effects with TGs on CVD mortality in men and women (Table S7). Subgroup analysis showed that the association was more robust in normotensive, lean, and currently smoking men but was more pronounced in women with DM (Fig. 3). We further examined the association of TGs with CVD mortality in men and women stratified by the number of metabolic disorders. The association was stronger in men with fewer metabolic disorders but more evident in women with more metabolic disorders (Figure S5). Sensitivity analyses and complementary analyses

First, nonlinear splines and change point detection confirmed the non-linear association of TGs with all-cause, CVD, and cause-specific mortality as shown in the RCS (Figure S8). Secondly, after excluding the participants with cancers (Figure S9), CKD (Figure S10), COPD (Figure S11), history of CVD (Figure S12), and who were receiving lipid-lowering treatments (Figure S13), respectively, the replotted RCS curves resembled the findings of main analysis. Similarly, after excluding the participants with at least one of these comorbidities or taking lipid-lowering drugs, no significant variations were observed from the main analysis (Figure S14). Moreover, after excluding the participants who have been followed for less than 3 years, the findings were consistent with the main analysis (Figure S15). No impacts of competing outcomes using Fine–Gray models was determined (Table S8). PAF indicates that lowering TGs to the lowest quintile, the estimated 5-year risks for all-cause mortality, CVD mortality, stroke mortality, IS mortality, and IHD mortality would decrease by 1.63% (p = 0.020), 11.09% (p < 0.005), 10.92% (p < 0.005), 17.00% (p < 0.005), and 8.92% (p < 0.005%) for Chinese men. No significant attributable risks for TGs were found in Chinese women (Table S9).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this nationwide, population-based, prospective cohort study, 3,789,025 Chinese people with a median follow-up of 4.6 years were assessed for the association of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality. First, we determined nonlinear associations of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality in Chinese population, in which mortality risks elevated abruptly with TG elevation in the low-to-moderate range for men, in contrast to a U-shaped association observed in women. Secondly, by comparing with a White population, we uncovered a remarkable variation in the association of TGs with CVD mortality, in which pronounced association with IS mortality was found particularly in Chinese men but more robust relationship with IHD risk in White people. Moreover, we found that the association of TGs with CVD mortality was incrementally stronger in Chinese men with lower metabolic risk profiles but in Chinese women with DM. Last but not the least, we revealed the synergy of smoking with TGs on CVD mortality in Chinese men. In summary, by leveraging a mega cohort of the Chinese general population and comparing findings with another cohort of White people, the present study provided a clear demonstration that the pathological relationship of TGs with atherosclerosis can be highly varied across different populations. It underscores the need of redefining hypertriglyceridemia in Chinese and modifying management strategies in accordance.

Potential explanations of principal findings

Current international guidelines for TG control generally recommend an optimal TG below 100–150 mg/dL, and TGs under this level were considered unremarkable for CVD prevention.13, 14, 15 The latest Chinese guidelines for dyslipidemia adopted a similar criterion for TG intervention.37 However, our study demonstrated that all-cause mortality and CVD mortality increased abruptly with TG elevation within the conventionally low-to-moderate range in Chinese men, which may indicate the early onset of metabolic disorders triggered by modest TG elevation even within the guideline-defined normal range, such as insulin resistance and atherogenic blood lipid profile.38 TGs are carried in blood by triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs), including chylomicrons, very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), and their remnants.39 VLDL1 is one of the two major species of VLDL, whose secretion is correlated with TG availability and regulated by insulin. Previous studies have demonstrated the “metabolic channeling” in the VLDL-LDL delipidation cascade, in which LDL particles transformed from VLDL1 are slower in decay rates, which are also major sources for highly atherogenic small dense LDL (sdLDL) particles.40 Insulin resistance signaled by modest TG elevation may also increase the levels of slowly catabolized and atherogenic lipid products, thus promoting the development of atherosclerosis and possibly predisposing mortality. On the other hand, the plateau followed by the abrupt increase of mortality risk may be attributed to the saturation effect, where additional metabolic damage confers diminishing incremental risk.

In contrast, U-shaped associations between TGs and all-cause and CVD mortality were observed in Chinese women. While the increased risks associated with lower TGs might be influenced by reversed causality, such as malnutrition and poor wellness, such a variation may indicate uneven responses to TG elevation in men and women, possibly due to the protective effects of estrogens. Earlier studies validated the anti-inflammatory functions of estrogens—downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibition of the adhesion, infiltration, and chemotaxis of immune cells in injured arteries.41,42 Recent studies further revealed that estrogens can prohibit the LDL transcytosis through coronary arterial walls,43 and could have a similar effect on TRL remnants. In a recent study based on the Italian population, lower-than guideline-defined cutoff values for hypertriglyceridemia was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which were later validated to be discriminative of CVD risk in multivariable-adjusted Cox models.44 Similarly, in another study integrating the data from population-based studies and randomized controlled trials (RCT), TG elevation across the full range was identified to be associated with increased all-cause and CVD mortality risk,4 which resembled the major findings in our study. However, no significant sex-specific differences were observed in these non-Asian studies, and further studies are merited for validating our finding and whether it only applied to East Asians.

In addition, our findings further implicated a remarkable variation in the relationship of TGs with CVD between East Asian and White populations. While the association with IS mortality was more robust for Chinese men, the relationship with IHD risk seemed to be more pronounced for White individuals. Such a finding carries particular importance in light of the epidemiologic pattern that the prevalence and the incidence of IS are higher in the East Asian populations.18,19 Although lacking direct evidence, previous population-based studies have provided implications. In the Copenhagen population study, although TGs were associated with IS risk, their association only became noteworthy at relatively higher TGs.2 In another pooled analysis of Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration, the association with IS lost significance when TGs were below 150 mg/dL.45 In contrast, in a meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies based on Asians, TGs between 50 and 150 mg/dL was shown to be associated with IS risk.46 Although the underlying mechanisms have not been fully elucidated, genetic factors might play a crucial role, with the genetic variants regulating TG levels being causally related to higher MI and CHD risk in White people5, 6, 7, 8 and with stroke risk in Chinese and Asian populations.47, 48, 49 Although cross-ancestry studies have located common genetic variants accounting for strokes,50,51 future studies looking for those specifically predisposing Asians to a disproportionally higher IS risk are needed, which would shed light on the underlying pathophysiology and potentially lead to the development of novel therapeutics.

When investigating the association of TGs with CVD mortality across different subgroups and strata, we found that this association was more pronounced in men with lower metabolic risks, especially those who were lean and normotensive. It may reflect the presence of unrecognized and subclinical metabolic dysfunction that is not captured by conventional risk factors, such as BMI and blood pressure.52 In East Asian populations, even individuals with a lean phenotype can accumulate visceral adiposity, which is metabolically active and may predispose metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance, low-grade inflammation, and atherogenic dyslipidemia.53 In contrast, we found that DM had a most significant interaction with TGs on CVD mortality in Chinese women. Although dysfunctional glucose and lipids metabolism together contributed to endothelial injury,54 previous studies have shown that DM conferred women with a higher risk of CVD.55 On the other hand, the loss of cardiovascular protection effects of estrogens may be present in women with DM, resulting in an elevated risk of CVD mortality.56,57 These findings underscored the role of TGs as an early indicator of metabolic dysfunction, which could be used as a more nuanced risk assessment tool for subclinical metabolic disorders and thus CVD risk.

The observed synergistic effect of smoking with TGs on CVD reinforced the compounded risks exerted by dyslipidemia and poor lifestyles. On one hand, such a synergistic effect could be due to the aggravating effect of smoking on lipid metabolism58,59 that exacerbates atherogenesis. On the other hand, both TGs and smoking have been shown to be able to cause inflammations in the endothelium,60,61 and the accumulated inflammatory burdens could promote the development of CVD events and predispose cardiovascular fatality. Although additional mechanistic studies are warranted to uncover the interaction between smoking and TG elevation in contributing to CVD, smoking cessation could be potentially beneficial for people with elevated TGs. As shown in our findings, the compounded effect of smoking with TGs on CVD were attenuated in those who had quitted smoking. For this reason, given that smoking cessation is a relatively attainable behavior, implanting lifestyle modifications along with TG intervention could be meaningful, despite that additional evidence is warranted to validate its real-world effectiveness and efficacy.

Clinical implications for TG management

The present analysis provided important insights into the precise management of TGs. First, our findings demonstrated that the relationship of TGs with all-cause and CVD mortality is different in East Asian and White populations. Chinese men are particularly susceptible to TG elevation within 150 mg/dL, which falls in the normal range as defined by current international guidelines. On one hand, it indicates that more intense TG intervention may be merited for both Chinese and East Asian men, but specific intervention goals should be ascertained by further clinical research. On the other hand, given that many randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating TG-lowering treatments among people with TGs >150 mg/dL failed to exemplify cardiovascular benefits,62, 63, 64, 65 our study provided evidence for future RCTs to incorporate subjects with conventionally normal TGs for a holistic assessment of their clinical utility. Secondly, the association of TGs and CVD mortality was more robust in the Chinese men with a lower CVD risk profile. It underscores that the significance of TGs as an early predictor of cardiovascular risk has been underestimated, and TGs should be granted greater weight in early detection and prevention of CVD. Last but not the least, smoking may have a synergistic effect with hypertriglyceridemia on CVD, reinforcing the significance of lifestyle modifications alongside TG-lowering interventions to further reduce the CVD mortality in both Chinese population and East Asian populations.

Limitations

Our study also has several limitations. First, it is an observational study from which we could not infer causality. Further causal inference studies are necessary to validate our findings. Secondly, we were unable to adjust certain variables, such as diets, sleep patterns, physical exercises, and other components of the Life's Essential 8 for CVD Prevention.66 Further studies are merited to evaluate the effects of these variables with TGs on CVD and mortality outcomes. Moreover, we used fasting TGs rather than non-fasting TGs used in increasingly more population-based cohort studies. Although using fasting TGs could avoid potential influences of diets on blood lipid level turbulence, non-fasting TGs have been more broadly adopted in large population-based cohort studies for convenience, closeness to physiological states, and equivalence with fasting TGs in CVD risk assessment.67 Additional research could compare the association of TGs with different CVD outcomes with fasting and non-fasting TGs. Also, the median follow-up time was relatively short that may prevent our study from obtaining potential time-dependent findings. Nonetheless, given a relatively low loss-of-follow-up rate in our cohort and the consistency with previous findings, the credibility of our findings is expected to be high.

Conclusion

TG is a significant risk factor for IS and CVD mortality Chinese men. The association is evident at ‘low-normal’ plasma TG levels and in those who are lean and generally have a lower risk profile with the exception of a positive interaction with smoking. These findings reinforced the necessity of redefining hypertriglyceridemia in the Chinese population and administering more aggressive TG interventions for Chinese and other Asian populations to further reduce the residual CVD risk.
