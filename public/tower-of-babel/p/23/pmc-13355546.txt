Reporting of second-generation p-values, Bayes factors and fragility indices in trials enhances assessment of clinical importance beyond p-values: a meta-epidemiologic study
Authors: Tonya M Esterhuizen, Lawrence Mbuagbaw, Nadia Rehman, Nathan Yanwou, Devron J Swaby, Esme Kittle, Johann-Christoph Licht, Lehana Thabane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFrequentist methods of statistical inference in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) may inadequately reflect clinical importance of results. We assessed whether three complementary approaches validly assess clinical importance and their impact on original conclusions.MethodsIn a sample of published superiority RCTs of health interventions, second-generation p-values, Bayes factors, and fragility indices were calculated for primary outcome results. Clinical importance was classified using effect size and confidence intervals relative to the reported minimum important difference. Agreement with clinical importance was measured by Cohen's Kappa estimates, positive predictive values (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV).ResultsIn the sample of 269 trials, second-generation p-values, Bayes factors and reverse fragility indices increased with decreasing clinical importance, while fragility indices decreased with decreasing clinical importance.Second-generation p-values showed strong alignment with clinical importance derived from a common minimum important difference. Among statistically significant studies, Bayes factor and fragility index showed poor agreement but high (>96%) PPV and low (<25%) NPV, while in not statistically significant studies, Bayes factor failed to identify clinically important trials but identified all non-clinically important trials. Reverse fragility index showed moderate agreement and yielded a PPV of 94.1% and a NPV of 46.5%.Original trial conclusions changed in 31.6% of trials using second-generation p-values, in 20.9% using Bayes factors, and in 57% of trials using fragility indices.DiscussionAnalysis plans and results interpretations should include quantitative assessments of clinical importance using second-generation p-values or Bayes factors, with robustness assessments using fragility indices.Keywords: Second-generation p-value, Bayes factor, Fragility index, Clinical importance, Statistical significance, p-values, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Statistical methods and paradigms in use today evolved from the work of statisticians prior to 1900, and continue to evolve [1]. However, no method should be thought of as unassailable. Kennedy-Shaffer (2019) reviews the history of p-values and hypothesis testing, acknowledging that while Ronald Fisher was not the originator of p-values and hypothesis testing, his work formalized the procedures and made statistical testing accessible to experimental researchers [1]. Fisher's rationale for suggesting p < 0.05 as a convenient cut point to reject the null hypothesis and judge whether results might be significant was based on a distance of approximately two standard deviations from the mean in a time when computers were not available, however, is still used as a threshold today [1,2]. The Neyman-Pearson framework for hypothesis testing challenged Fisher's views by presenting two opposing hypotheses, a null and alternative hypothesis, with fixed error rates. The null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) framework used today is a hybrid of the two opposing viewpoints (Fisher's and the Neyman-Pearson framework) [1].

Ongoing misconceptions about p-values and NHST and their possible role in the research replication crisis prompted the American Statistical Association (ASA) to publish a policy statement in 2016 summarizing the limitations of this approach and clarifying the appropriate use and interpretation of the p-value and statistical significance. They concluded that valid study design and conduct, paired with sound statistical thinking and reporting were more important than a single metric [3]. Propositions for statistical reform followed, ranging from a ban on p-values [4], to lowering, redefining or removing p-value thresholds [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10], to p-value functions which remove the need for dichotomisation by showing p-values and confidence intervals for every possible parameter value at difference confidence levels in the form of a curve [11]. A decade later, reform has been slow. Nevertheless, most statisticians agree that p-values, accompanied by effect estimates and associated confidence intervals (CI), remain central to frequentist analysis and reporting, particularly in well-designed clinical trials, provided their limitations are acknowledged [12] [13].

One of the limitations discussed in the ASA's statement was that p-values do not measure effect sizes nor importance of the findings [3]. Reporting guidelines for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) such as the CONSORT statement promote transparency, requiring details on assumptions made in sample size calculations, reporting of results in terms of estimated relative and absolute effect sizes and their CIs, numbers needed to treat or harm (NNT or NNH), and discussion of limitations [14]. However, guidelines fall short of emphasising discussion of the clinical importance of the findings. Relying solely on statistical significance to guide treatment decisions can result in misinterpretation and potentially harmful clinical consequences. Even reporting of a CI without interpretation of whether it spans a clinically important effect size is not helpful [15]. The minimum important difference (MID) represents the smallest change in any outcome that patients perceive as beneficial and would compel a change in management of their care [16]. In RCTs, the MID often informs sample size calculations as the target difference to detect, and should also be used to assess the clinical importance of results [17]. Man-Son-Hing et al. propose a framework combining the MID, effect estimate and CI limits to produce a classification of results as definitely, probably, possibly or definitely not clinically important [18]. Furthermore, interpretation of results becomes problematic if there is disparity between clinical importance and statistical significance. We previously showed that this is the case in around 20% of health intervention trials published between 2018 and 2022 and indexed in PubMed [19].

Real-world relevance of trial results can be communicated by supplementing p-values with complementary metrics [20] [21] [22]. Such metrics should be intuitive, simple to calculate and interpret, and feasible for use by researchers without advanced statistical training. Given the misuse of p-values, supplementary metrics which do not yield yet another p-value are particularly appealing. Additionally, being able to produce a quantitative value which explicitly incorporates the MID to assess clinical importance has advantages over the broad categorization of clinical importance used by Man-Son-Hing et al. [18] by providing a finer measurement of the degree of clinical importance or unimportance. Among the metrics specifically purported to address the shortcomings of traditional frequentist analysis and directly incorporate MIDs are second-generation p-values (SGPV) [23] and Bayes factor (BF), while the fragility index (FI) quantifies the robustness of trial results. Description and interpretation of supplementary metrics.

This study assessed agreement between three selected complementary metrics – SGPV, BF and FI – and clinical importance and p-values for primary outcomes of published RCTs of health interventions. We additionally evaluated the potential impact of these metrics on original trial conclusions by estimating the proportion of studies whose conclusions might change when these metrics are considered.

Methods

Study selection

We previously carried out a methodological study to assess the prevalence of disparity between statistical and clinical importance in published RCTs [19], the methods for which are detailed in the published protocol [33]. From the original sample of 500 studies, we selected a sub-sample of studies with binary or continuous primary outcomes, which reported the MID (usually as an assumption used in sample size calculation for the trial) as well as an effect estimate and CI of the primary outcome.

Clinical importance

We assessed the clinical importance of individual study primary outcome results as per Man-Son-Hing et al. [18] using the reported MID, effect size and CI reported for the primary outcome. Clinical importance was classified as either definitely, probably, possibly or definitely not clinically important, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [18].

Combinations of clinical importance and statistical significance [18].

Complementary metrics

Metrics were calculated for one primary outcome per study. Table 1 provides detail on how each metric was calculated and the information required in each case. SGPVs were calculated using the sgpv R package [23] using MID values reported by the studies as the lower (-MID) and upper (+MID) bounds of the interval null hypothesis. BFs were calculated with WINPEPI version 11.0, a freeware suite of programs for epidemiologists [28]. The MID reported in the sample size calculation of the study was used as the pre-study likelihood estimate or prior as suggested by Jakobsen (2014) for incorporating a prior based on evidence or anticipated realistic intervention effects defined prior to study results being known [34]. Fragility index (FI) and continuous fragility index (CFI) were calculated for statistically significant primary outcomes for binary (FI) and continuous (CFI) dependent variables, respectively. For FI, we used the Fragility Index Calculator from ClinCalc's website [35], and for CFI, data were simulated from summary statistics, with a tolerance of 0.1 and 5 iterations as described in Caldwell et al. [31] using a web-based calculator [36]. Outcomes reported as medians were excluded in the CFI calculation since the algorithm was designed for a parametric t-test. Reverse FI (RFI) was calculated for not statistically significant studies using the fragilityindex R package for binary outcomes [37].

Statistical analysis

Trial results were stratified by statistical significance and clinical importance, and metrics were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges, graphically depicted with box-and-whisker plots. Binary variables based on cut points which indicated clinical importance were created from the continuous metrics. SGPVs under 1 were classified as clinical importance, as were BFs of less than 0.1 [34]. As no previous cut points exist for FI, the study data were used to derive a cut point using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The FI or CFI was used as the test value, against a “true state” of clinical importance (consisting of definite, probable or possible clinical importance) of the study result. The cut point with the highest Youden's index, a summary statistic for diagnostic test accuracy calculated as (sensitivity + specificity)-1 [38], was selected. This process was repeated separately for RFI, using definitely not clinically important as the “true state”. Agreement between alternative metrics and clinical importance was assessed by Cohen's Kappa statistics and their 95% CIs. Convergent validity between reported p-values and metric values was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 30. Reporting of results followed a modified PRISMA checklist for methodological studies [39].

Results

Study characteristics

Two hundred and sixty-nine studies were included in the analysis. Primary outcome variables were binary in 132 (49.1%) and continuous in 137 (50.9%) studies. Statistically significant intervention effects were reported in 122 (45.4%) studies. Of the 269 studies, there were 260 p-values reported from the original analysis of the primary outcome. We were able to calculate 269 SGPVs (100%), 254 Bayes factors (94%), and 160 Fragility Indexes (60%), as shown in Fig. 2.

Flow chart showing number of studies used for each metric.

Complementary metrics and clinical importance

SGPV, BF and RFI increased with decreasing clinical importance, while FI in statistically significant studies decreased with decreasing clinical importance (Table S2, and Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Studies with definite clinical importance all had SGPVs of 0, low BFs and high FIs. Probably and possibly clinically important studies had median SGPVs of 0.38 and 0.72 respectively, with values of the former category tending to be below 0.5 and the latter above 0.5, with a few exceptions (Fig. 3). BF values of the probably and possibly clinically important categories were also distinguishable from each other in median values, with slight overlap of their inter-quartile ranges (Fig. 4). Similarly, for FI, studies with probable clinical importance were less fragile than those with possible clinical importance (Fig. 5). In contrast, those studies classified as definitely not clinically important mostly had SGPVs of 1, with only 25 (22%) of these studies having SGPVs which were less than 1 (Fig. 3). This group had high BFs on average (Fig. 4). They were also fragile, with a median FI of 4 (Fig. 5). The median FI of the total 100 statistically significant studies was 7 (IQR 2-16), while the median RFI of the 60 not statistically significant trials was 5 (IQR 3-7).

The ROC analysis derived optimal FI and RFI cut point was 6. FIs above 6 indicated more robust studies. In statistically significant studies, the area under the curve was 0.711 (p < 0.001) and the Youden's Index was 42.1% with corresponding sensitivities and specificities of 56.3% and 84.6% respectively. In not statistically significant studies, the AUC was 0.748 (p < 0.001), Youden's index was 39.6%, and sensitivity and specificity were 58.3% and 85.7%.

Statistically significant studies

SGPV showed perfect agreement with clinical importance (Kappa estimate = 1), while agreement was poor for BF, (Kappa estimate = 0.22; 95% CI 0.09 to 0.35) and FI (Kappa estimate = 0.20; 95%CI 0.06 – 0.33). SGPVs demonstrated 100% positive (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV). PPVs of BF and FI were 96.7% and 96.2% respectively, while their NPVs were low at 24.5% and 22.9% respectively.

Not statistically significant studies

SGPV showed good agreement with clinical importance, with a Kappa estimate of 0.71 (95% CI 0.60 – 0.82), identifying all clinically important studies (NPV 100%) but misclassifying some non-clinically important ones (PPV 70%). All BF classifications in this group were non-clinically important, thus Kappa could not be calculated. BF did not identify any of the clinically important studies as such, with a PPV of 0%, and a NPV of only 66%. The agreement for RFI was modest (Kappa estimate = 0.36; 95% CI 0.17 to 0.54). with a PPV of 94.1% for detecting non-clinical importance in robust studies and an NPV of 46.5% for clinical importance in fragile studies.

Convergent validity between p-values and complementary metrics

Table 3 shows that in statistically significant studies, p-values were weakly positively correlated with SGPVs (rho = 0.429), strongly positively with Bayes factors (rho = 0.862), and moderately negatively with FI (rho = −0.664). SGPVs were moderately positively correlated with BF (rho = 0.547) and moderately negatively correlated with FI (rho = −0.514), while BF and FI were also moderately negatively correlated (rho = −0.595).

Assessment of the impact of using complementary metrics on original conclusions

Table 4 shows the number of trials and overall percentage classified by original trial conclusion by SGPV, BF and FI/RFI. If SGPVs had been used to assess clinical importance of trial results, 15 trials which originally concluded statistical significance would have not concluded clinical importance. Additionally, 70 trials which originally concluded lack of statistical significance would have changed their conclusion to clinical importance of the effect. Thus, a total of 85 (31.6%) of trials could change their conclusions based on clinical importance measured by SGPVs.

Discussion

This study empirically evaluated three complementary metrics for interpreting RCT results relative to clinical importance. Because both SGPVs and clinical importance are calculated using the MID and the confidence intervals around the effect estimate, it was expected that SGPVs would demonstrate strong agreement with clinical importance and consistently identify clinically meaningful effects, regardless of statistical significance. Thus, this study did not attempt to validate the SGPV. Importantly, the information provided by the actual quantitative SGPV value over and above knowing whether a result is clinically important or not, is the proportion of the hypotheses supported by the study results which are also not clinically important [23]. Thus, it is a quantification of the clinical importance, rather than a categorization. Unlike p-values, the SGPV is not a tail area probability, and is more intuitive to understand than a p-value [23]. SGPVs thus provide a transparent and formal implementation of interval null hypothesis reasoning. Where the SGPVs did not align with clinical importance in this study, such as the misclassification of non-clinically important results from not statistically significant studies using SGPVs could be due to some CIs being wider than the interval null hypothesis range, conditions under which the SGPV tends to be difficult to interpret [40]. Additionally, we classified SGPVs of between 0 and 1 as clinically important, when this category was originally interpreted by Blume (2019) as inconclusive [23].

BF and FI correctly detected clinical importance in statistically significant studies, with PPVs of almost 100%. The high prevalence of clinical importance in this group contributed to the high PPVs, and in environments where the clinical importance prevalence may be lower, the PPV of these metrics will decrease. The high prevalence of clinical importance had the opposite effect on NPV percentages for BF and FI within the statistically significant studies, with very low values resulting from 40 to 37 false negative classifications, respectively. Some of the inaccuracies in these metrics for detecting clinical importance might be due to the cut points used in this study, especially since the cut point of 6 for FI was derived using the sample data and not externally validated. Additionally, in this study we used the MID as the prior when calculating BF. This might differ from a Bayesian prior distribution, potentially affecting the calculated BFs.

Although the predictive value of BF in not statistically significant studies was low, 100% of the non-clinically important studies were detected by a BF above 0.1. Our findings about the usefulness of BF to confirm null results are corroborated by a reanalysis of addiction RCTs, which examined 75 effect sizes from 12 published trials [41]. They found that only 20% of not statistically significant findings supported no effect, and concluded that researchers should avoid statements of no effect in not statistically significant trials unless BF is < 0.3 [41]. Hoekstra et al. assessed the strength of evidence for the absence of effect by calculating BF in 43 not statistically significant trials published in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2015 [42]. They found a range of BFs providing from weak to very strong evidence for the null hypothesis, concluding BF is a useful tool in quantifying the strength of evidence for the absence of effect, which large p-values are unable to do [42].

The FI and CFI increased as the clinical importance of the trial decreased in statistically significant studies, indicating that trial fragility can provide an indication of clinical importance. Most notable was the decrease in median FI from 25 in studies which were definitely clinically important, to 8 in probably clinically important studies. The median FI for statistically significant studies of 7 (IQR 2-16) was similar in other meta-epidemiological studies, with a 2014 review of published trials finding a median FI of 8, with a quarter of the sample having FIs of less than 3 [30]. A meta-analysis of 27 RCTs in patients with anterior shoulder instabilities found a median CFI of 8.2 (IQR 17.2) [43]. A slightly higher median FI of 12 was reported in a meta-analysis of Cochrane systematic reviews with binary outcomes, with one third being under 5 [44], and a relatively high median FI of 26 was found in heart failure trials [45]. However, mortality outcomes in 46 peri-operative trials have been shown to be extremely fragile, with a median of 2 [46]. FI is strongly correlated with the p-value and thus with trial sample size, and should not be interpreted as the strength of association [47], therefore FIs should be primarily interpreted for robustness of findings rather than to assess clinical importance, although our findings of alignment with clinical importance warrant further study. Further limitations of the FI are that it only applies to two group studies with equal randomization and is not yet able to process non-normally distributed outcomes summarized and compared as medians and interquartile ranges [30].

In a previous study which calculated RFI in 167 published not statistically significant trials, the median RFI was 8 (IQR 5-13), and the RFI increased as the trial p-value decreased, with a Spearman's correlation coefficient of 0.54 [32]. Our results using 60 trials show an even lower median RFI of 5 (IQR 3-7), and a similar correlation coefficient of 0.55 with p-values. RFI should be used to understand the robustness of not statistically significant effects, especially in marginally non-significant trials, to avoid possible type II errors [32].

Although we created binary inferences based on cut-points to assess predictive values of the metrics, we do not support doing this in practice. Similar to the advice to not dichotomise p-values or use the words “statistically significant” [8] [21] [22], clinical importance should also be reported and interpreted on a continuum.

Key limitations

The findings of this descriptive empirical study are from one sample of trials published between 2018 and 2022 and might not replicate in other samples. Performance of the metrics under different conditions should be tested in simulation studies. Furthermore, classification of clinical importance and calculation of SGPVs and BFs requires a MID to be specified. In this study, where a MID was not specifically stated as such, we used the difference to detect which was reported in the sample size calculation of the trial results publication as the MID. Our objective was to show how a pre-specified important difference can be incorporated into SGPV and BF calculations to align these metrics with clinical importance derived from the same pre-specified important difference, whatever the source or validity of that value. In practice, the MID would have to be well justified by the investigators of the trial if used in this way. We acknowledge that there might not be established MIDs for many outcomes, in which circumstances it might not be possible to calculate SGPV or BF.

Additionally, although it was not practical for us to perform sensitivity analyses in this study when reporting BFs for a large number of studies, we suggest that when reporting Bayes Factors to interpret results for individual studies, researchers should conduct and report sensitivity analyses, for example, by varying the prior between 0 and the MID.

Implications

Using p-values alone in a single trial to conclude the effectiveness of an intervention is poor practice. We agree with others that studies should be designed and interpreted using careful clinical, epidemiological and evidence-based reasoning [48]. Within the frequentist framework of analysis, p-values, effect estimates, confidence intervals, NNT and NNH should still be reported as per CONSORT guidelines [14]. Additionally, we propose that careful assessment of the clinical importance of the results should be pre-planned in the trial protocol and statistical analysis plan. SGPVs should be calculated for all primary outcomes regardless of the statistical significance of results. Non-clinical importance of results can be confirmed using BF for results with large p-values. Finally, sensitivity analysis should include FI or RFI as appropriate to assess the robustness of the results. Many researchers and statisticians are unfamiliar with these metrics; thus, courses and seminars should be designed to introduce these concepts. As the field of statistics constantly evolves, so should the way we teach and practise.
