Emergency department preparedness and hospital disaster planning: lessons from disasters
Authors: Mehmet Murat Oktay, Bektaş Sarı, Kazım Ersin Altınsoy, İlker Akbaş, Erdal Yavuz, Gül Özlem Yıldırım
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarthquakes significantly affect public health, causing mass casualties and increased mortality risks. They can damage healthcare facilities and disrupt essential services like information, energy, and water supplies. This study aims to explore the experiences of emergency medicine specialists and healthcare personnel involved in earthquake response efforts to improve Hospital Disaster and Emergency Plans (HAPs).MethodsThis study used a phenomenological approach to explore the experiences of emergency medicine specialists and healthcare professionals after the earthquake. Eighteen participants were purposively selected, and data were collected through semi-structured interviews following ethical approval and consent, and analysed using MAXQDA software.ResultsSix themes and twenty-four categories were identified. Participants reported fear and anxiety during the earthquake, particularly regarding their children’s safety. Post-earthquake, concerns for family and patient overload were prevalent. Issues related to HAPs included staff feeling like disaster victims, poor protocol implementation, disorganisation, and unclear job roles. After the earthquake, patients were transferred to emergency departments, facing challenges with hygiene, sanitation, heating, and power.ConclusionsIt is crucial to build hospitals and emergency departments that can withstand disasters and adhere to strict seismic codes and standards. Hospitals must adopt functional, context-specific disaster plans that recognize healthcare workers as disaster victims, ensure basic needs and family support, and embed role-based drills into routine practice to ensure staff understand their roles in the HAPs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01514-x.Keywords: Emergency department, Emergency medicine, Disaster medicine, Hospital disaster plans, Disaster management

Background

Earthquakes, classified as external disasters, cause significant destruction in residential areas and infrastructure. These disasters profoundly impact public health due to mass casualties and the risk of death. In addition to structural damage to hospital buildings, non-structural damage, such as information, energy, and water outages, can occur. Such situations create a complex disaster scenario where internal disasters compound external ones [1]. Unfortunately, despite these challenges, healthcare facilities are expected to maintain or even enhance their capacity to provide uninterrupted health services [2]. In fact, in such extraordinary circumstances, the appropriate approach is to consider hospitals as disaster victims and healthcare workers as disaster survivors [3]. Hospitals and Emergency Departments (EDs) are the primary centres to minimize mortality during any disaster. EDs, as the first point of care for patients and casualties, are disproportionately impacted by extraordinary situations [4]. The rising demand for health services in disasters undermines the operational capacity of healthcare facilities, jeopardizes patient safety, and necessitates that EDs play a central role in addressing the crisis [2, 5, 6]. Due to chaotic working conditions, emergency medicine specialists are the most efficient physicians to provide service during any disaster [4].

Although earthquakes cannot be prevented, various measures can be implemented to reduce casualties and injuries. To do so, the Hospital Disaster and Emergency Plan (HAP) was developed according to the standards set by the WHO and its affiliated organizations. In Turkey, hospitals under the Ministry of Health are legally required to prepare these plans following international standards and review them annually [7, 8]. Regular training and drills are also conducted under the supervision of Provincial Health Directorates. Despite all these implementations, during the major Kahramanmaraş earthquake on February 6, 2023, 42 hospitals in the affected regions suffered moderate to severe damage, and 448 healthcare workers, including 101 physicians, lost their lives [9, 10]. Consequently, HAPs were either not implemented or proved insufficient in the region.

This study examines the experiences of emergency medicine specialists, healthcare professionals, and support services personnel working in the EDs during and after the earthquakes in the affected region. The aim is to analyse these experiences using qualitative interview techniques and contribute to the improvement of HAPs based on the findings. The study provides unique insights by discussing the verified competencies and shortcomings of disaster healthcare personnel, as well as the solutions they developed to address unforeseen problems. Additionally, given the region’s significant migrant and refugee population, the study highlights the need to consider vulnerable populations in disaster planning.

Materials and methods

Research design

This study involved professionals employed in the EDs of hospitals located in provinces affected by the February 6, 2023, earthquake in Gaziantep, Adıyaman, Kahramanmaraş, Kilis, and Malatya. In-depth interviews were conducted with 18 participants who volunteered to share detailed accounts of their experiences. The study utilized the phenomenological method, focusing on lived experiences to uncover the meaning of an event and how it was experienced by individuals [11]. In this type of research, maintaining neutrality, defining research questions, and establishing participant criteria hold significant importance [12]. The research questions for this study are presented below.

What were the problems and solutions encountered in the emergency departments after the earthquake?

To address these questions, semi-structured interview questions were prepared and utilized during the interviews.

Participants

The study participants included physicians, nurses, paramedics, and support staff (cleaning, security, and technical personnel) working in the EDs in the earthquake-affected cities during the February 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake. Eighteen participants were selected through purposive sampling. The sample size was determined when qualitative saturation was reached. Participants were contacted by telephone. The purposive sampling approach is frequently used in qualitative research to access individuals with relevant experience for in-depth insights [12, 13]. The first participant was reached by one of the authors working at the same hospital, and subsequent participants were recruited using the snowball method.

Data collection

The research data were collected through online interviews using a semi-structured questionnaire prepared by the authors and submitted as a supplementary document. The data collection process took place between July 2024 and November 2024. Prior to conducting the interviews, the researchers introduced themselves and explained the purpose of the research and the questions. Participants determined the date and time of the online meetings and attended them individually from their homes, workplaces, or outdoor environments. Before each interview, participants provided both written and verbal consent, granted permission for audio and video recordings, and received transcripts of the interviews afterwards.

The first author (PhD, male) conducted all the interviews, recorded the responses, and took notes. During the study, the researchers were employed as university faculty members and had previously published several articles using qualitative methodologies. Participants verified the accuracy of their responses, and the data were coded after each interview. Data collection continued until enough information was gathered to reach saturation, defined as the point at which codes began to repeat. No repeated interviews were conducted. The COREQ checklist was followed throughout the research process.

Data analysis

The data analysis was performed using MAXQDA20 qualitative research software. All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Participants were anonymized and identified using numbers instead of names. All coding was conducted based on semantic similarities and differences [12]. Once the data had been reviewed, two researchers reached a consensus on all codes. During the analysis, a coding table was created instead of a coding tree.

Reliability

Ensuring reliability in qualitative research is critically important and is closely linked to the researchers’ accuracy and competency. Prolonged interaction, expert review, and inclusion criteria are essential for maintaining reliability in qualitative studies [12–14]. Emphasis is placed on the meticulous application of inclusion criteria and expert opinions to ensure the validity and reliability of the results [15]. In this study, reliability was enhanced through inclusion criteria, prolonged interaction, and the incorporation of expert reviews.

The primary themes include earthquake experience, post-earthquake work processes, hospital disaster planning, evacuation and triage procedures, identified needs and problems, and participants’ overall statements.

Figure 1 presents codes associated with employees’ experiences during the earthquake, organized into four categories: emotions and feelings, behaviors, thoughts, and physical environment. Within the emotions and feelings category, the most frequently reported experiences included fear, panic, anxiety, and a state of shock. Common behavioral responses were waiting for the tremor to subside, protecting children, and seeking safe locations. Regarding thoughts, prevalent perceptions included the belief that the shaking persisted for an unusually long duration, fears of imminent death, and the sense that everything was ending. The physical environment was frequently characterized as catastrophic, with conditions dominated by dust and smoke.

Figure 2 presents the employees’ descriptions of their work environment following the earthquake. From their perspectives, the most frequently repeated code during the post-earthquake work process was “I thought about my family.” This was followed, in order of frequency, by the codes: patient overload, chaotic environment, active work, and concerns. Additionally, less frequently mentioned codes included: I left my family, a physically unsafe environment, panic, anxiety, and we were confused about what to do.

Figure 3 provides an overview of codes related to employees’ experiences with the HAP, categorized into operations, team care, leadership gaps, tactical issues, planning, and drills. In operations, standard codes included HAP remaining theoretical and issues with organization. The team care category identified employees as disaster victims. Leadership gaps showed equal representation across codes. In tactical issues, unclear job descriptions were significant. There were no dominant codes in planning. For drills, “drills not conducted” and “drills conducted” were similarly noted. Key codes highlighted include “employees as disaster victims,” “HAP not implemented,” “HAP theoretical,” “lack of organization,” and “unclear job descriptions.”

Employees’ experiences and observations related to the hospital disaster and emergency plan. (The thickness of the arrows indicates the intensity of each code)

Figure 4 presents employee statements regarding evacuation and triage. Employees consistently reported that patients awaiting examinations and those receiving outpatient treatment at clinics were transferred to EDs following the earthquake. They further noted that patient evacuations to EDs occurred without planning or coordination, while other clinics continued to admit patients. Most participants indicated that triage could not be performed in their emergency departments. In contrast, emergency medicine specialists and other healthcare professionals stated that triage was attempted but proved ineffective. Additionally, participants described significant disorder during triage, with inadequate record-keeping.

Discussion

Emergency Medicine Specialists (EMS) and other healthcare professionals in the EDs are the first to confront the aftermath of disasters, both during and immediately after the event, delivering the initial emergency response. Simultaneously, they are also victims of these disasters, effectively making them disaster survivors themselves [3]. Before their fear and anxiety caused by the disaster can subside, they may face horrifying scenes such as dead bodies, severely injured individuals, and patients who have lost limbs. Those working in the EDs are vulnerable to such occupational stressors that can negatively affect their mental health, putting them under even greater stress [16]. However, we know very little about the personal or professional consequences of disasters have on these individuals. Participants in our study described their emotions during the earthquake as predominantly feelings of fear, panic, anxiety, and shock (Fig. 1). Participant (P) 17 shared: “I sought refuge in a café. There, I had to assist an unconscious earthquake victim. That was when I realized I was a doctor. At that moment, in the trauma and shock, I was unaware of anything—my brain had shut down. Then I thought to myself, ‘I must go to the hospital.’

Similarly, a nurse who worked in the ED during the Canterbury earthquake in New Zealand described feeling fear as she approached the structurally damaged ED, calling it “the worst imaginable fear.” Another nurse from the same event stated that when the building shook, she thought she would die, especially since they were on the ground floor and would likely be the first to perish. She also recounted worrying about her family and being torn between saving herself or the patients, not knowing what to do at all [17]. In our study, similar emotions were reported by healthcare professionals, who feared that the EDs, located on the ground floor, might collapse under the weight of the entire hospital building. Participant 16 shared, “Even the slightest tremor made me question whether to treat the patient or evacuate. It was unbearable.” P4 expressed, “We were terrified, constantly worried about another earthquake. What if we could not save ourselves or the patients?” In contrast, P2 felt the ground floor emergency department was safer: “It made evacuation easier.” P15 recounted a harrowing experience: “They brought in a child in cardiac arrest, and while doing CPR, the ceiling collapsed. We could not escape due to heavy snowfall and extreme cold.”

Our findings demonstrate that during the earthquake, EMS and other healthcare professionals working in the EDs exhibited behaviors such as searching for a safe environment in a state of fear and panic (Fig. 1). Additionally, they experienced thoughts of death and feelings that everything had come to an end. Earthquakes are known to be traumatic events that can have significant psychological effects on individuals. Fear, stress, and anxiety related to earthquakes can lead to various mental health issues, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety disorders, depression, and substance abuse [18]. The full extent of the psychological impact of this disaster on the disaster-affected healthcare professionals in our country is not yet fully understood.

In the aftermath of the earthquake, ED staff faced hazardous conditions due to structural damage and increasing patient loads. During this chaos, healthcare professionals experienced overwhelming anxiety and fear for their families. Participant No. 5 recalled, “I crawled under the bed with my son and covered him with my body,” illustrating protective instincts. Participant No. 10 similarly said, “I went to my children and prepared to die with them.” Others reported intense stress while worrying about their families’ safety. Research supports these findings, indicating that staff presence during disasters often depends on securing childcare [3]. For instance, during Turkey’s 1999 earthquakes, many prioritized family-care over work. During the Gulf War, fears of chemical attacks led to decreased staff attendance [19]. Many staffing challenges originate from the need for childcare and eldercare [3].

Hospital Disaster and Emergency Plans (HAPs) include units like Employee Support and Dependent Support, aimed at addressing staff’s basic needs and specific requirements during crises. They also encompass strategies for reducing structural risks through resilient construction and disaster assessments. However, many hospitals faced significant damage during disasters, suggesting that HAPs may lack practicality. Study participants noted that HAPs often feel theoretical, with staff unfamiliar with their job descriptions. A post-Hurricane Katrina study highlighted discrepancies between hospital disaster plans and the realities encountered during the event [20]. Despite the presence of Severe Weather Response Plans (including hurricanes), which had been implemented and activated many times over the previous decade, the study, consistent with our findings, revealed that the existing plans were inadequate and not implemented in some areas. Richardson et al. noted that healthcare workers were aware of HAPs but were not familiar with them [17]. Recurring issues may be mitigated in revised hospital disaster plans through the implementation of interactive workshops and drills.

Drills are essential for enabling healthcare personnel to experience HAPs and prepare for disasters. The World Health Organization (WHO) considers preparedness and trained personnel to be effective factors in reducing disaster-related injuries [21]. Our study revealed that participants reported that mandatory drills in hospitals based on HAPs were either not conducted or of similar intensity (Fig. 3). P10 questioned the realism of these drills, stating: “We conducted a fire drill that didn’t go beyond lighting a fire in a barrel.” Other studies have shown that despite periodic drills, many healthcare workers remain unprepared to respond to disasters [22]. Our study also found that patient evacuations from the EDs were carried out without planning, coordination, or an effective triage system (Fig. 4). P11, an EMS, highlighted the lack of coordination during evacuations: “Suddenly, both the patients from the operating room and those from intensive care units were being sent to the ED.” P16 added: “We attempted to implement triage, but during the process, we ended up mixing intubated patients or those likely to be intubated with the other patients we had triaged. The administration had trouble directing the process.” Similar findings in other studies support our observations, showing that many hospitals are unprepared for evacuations during disasters and lack special triage methods to facilitate the selection process, leading to disorganized evacuations [1, 23, 24]. It is important to note that there is still no standardized guideline for hospital evacuations.

Hospitals frequently face operational difficulties and failures during large-scale disasters like earthquakes. Our study found issues related to basic needs such as access to drinking water and hot meals, as well as challenges in communication. However, participants emphasized more significant problems such as general hygiene issues, toilet hygiene, heating problems, and power outages (Fig. 5). P8 stated: “There was only one toilet for approximately 250–300 people. There was no facility to meet our personal needs.” P4 added: “There was a terrible smell from the toilets in the hospital.” Other studies align with our findings, highlighting frequent issues such as communication and power outages, water shortages and contamination, lack of access to hot food and supplies, poor hygiene, and resource allocation deficiencies [1, 3, 20, 25].

Participants in our study made several recommendations for the post-earthquake period, particularly emphasizing the need to improve the applicability and functionality of HAPs. They suggested conducting frequent and realistic drills and training sessions tailored to disaster scenarios. This recommendation is both logical and practical. Traditional drill methods, such as workshops and tabletop exercises, can improve disaster management skills. However, the use of simulation-based training with realistic scenarios is a newer and more effective approach in education [22]. Two participants (Nos. 12 and 15) reported fully implementing HAPs during the earthquake. Both were EMS who had conducted disaster drills in accordance with HAPs prior to the earthquake. Their hospitals sustained severe damage. Both participants were also academics, with one conducting disaster drills and the other focusing on CBRN (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear) scenarios before the earthquake. The findings are consistent with the literature, which shows that prior disaster experience and training drills improve disaster preparedness and compliance with HAPs [26].

Participants also reported that many disaster victims, left homeless after the earthquake, sought shelter in damaged hospitals (Fig. 6). P14 described this situation: “People came to the hospital not as patients or injured individuals but as disaster victims. They identified the hospital as a safe place to stay. At times, we had to walk through groups of people sheltering in the corridors. We waited in line with them for the toilet, food, or water. I can say that having non-patient disaster victims in the hospital exhausted us. It affected access to water, food, toilets, cleanliness, and hygiene. I can state this very clearly.” Another significant issue in the earthquake-affected region was the high concentration of Syrian refugees, primarily elderly individuals, children, and women, which complicated response efforts. An EMS researcher noted that many refugees overwhelmed the damaged emergency department (ED), unaware of the hospital’s condition. Due to cold weather and snow, addressing their basic needs became urgent. Hospital staff created makeshift shelters in the garden and provided warmth with fires. The presence of these refugees was a desperate choice, as they lacked local support networks, unlike the local population. Economic constraints likely made their homes more vulnerable to collapse, and research shows that low income and belonging to vulnerable groups make these populations disproportionately affected by disasters [27, 28].

In this study, the predominant theme identified was that healthcare workers were also victims of the disaster (Fig. 6). Unlike the public, EMS and healthcare professionals faced the added challenge of increased workloads while acting as both victims and responders. However, there is a lack of research specifically focusing on healthcare workers as disaster victims [3]. Considering studies that emphasize the sense of abandonment among medical personnel, it is vital to provide social and psychological support to these workers to ensure continuity of healthcare services during major disasters like earthquakes [29]. P7 described the emotional state as follows: “There were people working while crying. And believe me, the people there were trying to help others through tears.” During disasters, hospitals, EDs, and healthcare professionals must remain operational. Studies have highlighted that maintaining the capacity to deliver essential services during disasters hinges on recognizing healthcare facilities and staff as both victims and responders in disaster scenarios [30].

Implications for policy and practice

Disaster health policies must treat hospitals and healthcare workers as both responders and victims. Hospital Disaster and Emergency Plans should ensure operational continuity while supporting staff and their families with dependent care, mental health support, and local accommodation. Regulations must require HAPs to be context-specific and tested through realistic drills and clear roles, not just paperwork. HAPs should be practical, integrated into daily routines, and supported by regular department drills to clarify roles, command, and triage. Hospitals must secure essential water, sanitation, power, food, and communication to maintain care in crises. Planning should also address vulnerable populations to prevent hospitals from becoming unplanned shelters. After disasters, hospitals should provide psychological and organizational support to staff to preserve resilience and system function.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, healthcare workers’ reluctance to discuss the consequences of a major disaster and their desire to avoid recalling the events limited the number of participants. Second, administrative hesitations among participants represented another significant limitation. Finally, the majority of participants were physicians, which may have resulted in a professional imbalance compared to other participant groups.

Conclusion

During severe disasters such as earthquakes, a critical component of effective response to increased patient volume is the construction of hospitals and EDs capable of withstanding seismic events. Healthcare facilities should be strategically located and built to standards that address geological and seismological risks. Even if hospitals and the EDs remain structurally sound, EMS and other healthcare professionals working within these facilities may also be considered disaster victims, experiencing the full impact of the event. These healthcare workers should not be left to fend for themselves or subjected to feelings of abandonment. Hospital disaster and emergency plans (HAPs) should address the safety and daily needs of healthcare workers’ families, provide support for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and ensure access to hygienic toilets and secure food supplies. Additionally, these plans should prioritize protective measures for vulnerable populations, including refugees.

Disaster preparedness processes must comprehensively incorporate vulnerable populations, including them in case studies and drill scenarios. Although earthquakes are sudden and unexpected natural events, the availability of realistic and applicable HAPs and their proper implementation are vital for mitigating disaster impacts. For this purpose, HAPs should be prepared specifically for each hospital through participatory workshops that involve the opinions and suggestions of all healthcare staff. Disaster drills should be conducted in a realistic and simulated manner, with hospital staff writing and implementing scenarios specific to their departments. Every staff member should have a definitive role in these scenarios, making the HAP a part of their professional lives. While HAPs should be prepared in accordance with international standards, involving participants with prior disaster experience can provide significant contributions through knowledge transfer.
