Clinical validation of a deep learning-based approach for preoperative decision-making in implant size for total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Ki-Bong Park, Moo-Sub Kim, Do-Kun Yoon, Young Dae Jeon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopedic surgeons use manual measurements, acetate templating, and dedicated software to determine the appropriate implant size for total knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study aimed to use deep learning (DL) to assist in deciding the femoral and tibial implant sizes without manual manipulation and to evaluate the clinical validity of the DL decision by comparing it with conventional manual procedures.MethodsTwo types of DL were used to detect the femoral and tibial regions using the You Only Look Once algorithm model and to determine the implant size from the detected regions using convolutional neural network. An experienced surgeon predicted the implant size for 234 patient cases using manual procedures, and the DL model also predicted the implant sizes for the same cases.ResultsThe exact accuracies of the surgeon’s template were 61.54% and 68.38% for predicting femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively. Meanwhile, the proposed DL model reported exact accuracies of 89.32% and 90.60% for femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively. The accuracy ± 1 levels of the surgeon and proposed DL model were 97.44% and 97.86%, respectively, for the femoral implant size and 98.72% for both the surgeon and proposed DL model for the tibial implant size.ConclusionThe observed differences and higher agreement levels achieved by the proposed DL model demonstrate its potential as a valuable tool in preoperative decision-making for TKA. By providing accurate predictions of implant size, the proposed DL model has the potential to optimize implant selection, leading to improved surgical outcomes.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Implant size, Preoperative, Deep learning, Clinical validity

Background

Accurate implant sizing is crucial for achieving optimal joint function and stability in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Traditionally, preoperative decision-making for implant size selection has relied on manual measurements and subjective assessments by orthopedic surgeons [1–18]. Preoperative surgical planning for TKA involves the use of acetate template film or dedicated software to decide the appropriate femoral and tibial implant sizes [3–10]. This includes measurement of bone dimensions, assessment of joint space, and consideration of anatomical landmarks [9]. This approach is limited by interobserver and intraobserver variability, which can affect the accuracy and consistency of implant sizing [19–22]. Furthermore, templating requires a long duration owing to axis setup and multiple measurements [6, 9, 10]. Moreover, several studies were unable to obtain good exact accuracy (i.e., same predicted size and implant size used) for implant size [11–17]. Trickett et al. reported manual manipulation-based exact accuracy levels of 48.0% (femoral) and 55.0% (tibial) [6]. Specht et al. used a digital templating method with dedicated software. They reported exact accuracies of 48.0% and 52.0% for femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively [9]. McLawhorn et al. reported an overall exact accuracy of 57.0% for femoral and tibial implant sizes [2]. Meanwhile, Peek et al. reported exact accuracies of 71.0% and 60.0% in predicting femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively [13]. The highest exact accuracies were reported by Hernandez-Vaquero et al. [18]: 82.5% and 79.5% for femoral and tibial implants, respectively. Although several studies reported a relatively low accuracy for predicting implant size, accuracy ± 1, which is an accuracy level allowing ± 1 size error, showed high levels of accuracy ranging from 80.0 to 100.0% [21, 22].

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in leveraging artificial intelligence techniques to assist in preoperative decision-making processes in orthopedic surgery [21–25]. It has shown great promise in automating the preoperative decision-making process [26–35]. The non-linear regression machine learning (ML) model showed good performance in predicting implant size, with 82.2% and 85.0% for femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively [32, 33]. Another study that reported on ML-based prediction of implant size showed exact accuracies of 78.0% and 80.5% for femoral and tibial implant sizes, respectively [16]. However, these studies applied ML alone, without the use of medical images. In this study, computer vision-based deep neural networks using radiographs were used to detect the femoral and tibial regions and to automatically decide on both implant sizes using the seed image without any manual manipulation. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical validity of a deep learning (DL)-based decision for implant size by comparing it with the performance of a surgeon’s prediction by manual templating.

Methods

Data preparation

The institutional review board of Ulsan University Hospital approved all experimental protocols (approval number: 2022-10-030). The data preparation procedure in this study consisted of three sequential steps for application to the DL model. Data from 347 patients were collected, which included 1,056 radiographs and surgical records containing postoperative information on implant sizes. Postoperative information showed the actual implant size used in the patient. The first step involved drawing the bounding box on anteroposterior radiograph images, which are Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images, to reveal the regions of the femur and tibia individually, using MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) for object detection. Second, we cropped the region marked by the bounding box from the radiographic images to ensure a standardized size of 224 × 224 pixels for the convolutional neural network (CNN) [36, 37]. Third, we defined seven classes corresponding to the actual implant sizes (Triathlon®; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), and the cropped images were classified as the folder according to the implant size referred from the postoperative surgical records. Through data augmentation, we secured 46,400 image sets for training. The ratios for training, validation, and testing (evaluation metrics for training) were 60%, 20%, and 20%, respectively. We prepared the radiographs for 234 patient cases as the test group, along with their postoperative surgical records, which included information on implant sizes, to conduct the clinical validation of the proposed DL model. A demographic study of all patients was performed to identify patient characteristics (Table 1). A total of 234 patients underwent TKA. The proportions of men and women were 18.4% and 81.6%, respectively. The percentages of the surgical side were 3% on the left side and 45.7% on the right side. The mean age of the patients was 71.5 years, with maximum and minimum ages of 89 and 59 years, respectively.

Model implementation and training

The DL models were constructed using MATLAB. The proposed DL architecture has two parts for determining both implant sizes. The model in the first part performs object detection using the modified You Only Look Once (YOLO) v4 model [38, 39]. It detects the femoral and tibial regions as bounding boxes in anteroposterior radiography, which is used for TKA. We commenced training with a base learning rate of 0.001, along with a preset sequence of intervals to scale down this rate. The learning rate was reduced every 20 epochs, with each decrement multiplied by a factor of 0.5. The initial plan was to execute the algorithm for a maximum of 100 epochs. The minibatch size and L2 regularization were set to 4 and 0.0005, respectively. To enhance the model iteratively, we used an optimizer for adaptive moment estimation. The architecture of YOLO v4 was segmented into four distinct sections (input, backbone, neck, and head), and the complete architecture is presented in Fig. 1. The input part was tasked with processing to import image DICOM files and feeding data into the cross-stage partial connection DarkNet-53 (CSPDarkNet-53), which forms the backbone of the model [35, 37].

Detailed structure of the proposed deep learning model. The upper part shows the model structure for the femoral and tibial region detection using the model in the first part. The four parts of the model are the input, backbone (including the cross-stage partial connection DarkNet-53), neck (composed of the spatial pyramid pooling and path aggregation network), and head (containing You Only Look Once v3). The detected image by the bounding box area was cropped to be a seed image for the next model involving the convolutional neural network (CNN). After reshaping the cropped image, the CNN classified the implant size using the seed image. The surgeons progressed the templating for component using acetate film and measurement tools in image software

The model in the second part is a CNN architecture used to classify the implant size from the seed images. The seed image is the image cropped by the bounding box, which was appointed by the model in the first part [36, 37]. Because the image cropped by the bounding box can export an image format that does not match 224 × 224 × 1, the input part of the model in the second part has the logic of reshaping the format to 224 × 224 × 1. The model in the second part has a layer that includes the convolutional layer, batch normalization, and rectified linear unit, and there are seven layers before the fully connected, softmax, and classifier. The dimensions of the convolution and pooling kernels were 3 × 3 and 2 × 2, respectively (stride = 2). The stochastic gradient descent with momentum was used as the optimizer. The initial learning rate and momentum were set as 0.001 and 0.9, respectively. The maximum epoch and mini-batch sizes were selected as 20 and 64, respectively. The hardware specifications for the training models were 2.10 GHz dual Intel® Xeon® Silver processors with 128 GB RAM and two GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs with 24 GB GPU memory.

Statistical analyses and clinical validation

A dataset comprising 234 patients was used to evaluate the clinical validity of the proposed DL model in predicting both implant sizes. The proposed DL model successfully predicted the implant sizes for all cases within a negligible timeframe, making accurate measurements challenging. In contrast, one surgeon with > 8 years of experience in TKA conducted templating using a conventional method that involved manual measurements and the use of acetate film. Because the single person progressed the templating, there is lack of inter- and intra-observer variability indicators. Although this is one of the limitations in this study, the consistency of result could be maintained. These recorded measurements served as a reference for the subsequent comparison and evaluation of the proposed DL model against the conventional templating approach. To evaluate the clinical validity of the prediction results by the proposed DL, we analyzed the accuracy results and calculated Spearman’s rho after the normality test using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test between the two groups as postoperative records showing the actual implant size used versus the prediction by the proposed DL model as well as postoperative records versus the prediction of the surgeon through manual templating [26, 30].

Results

Evaluation metric for training

The three types of evaluation metrics for training are mean average precision, loss variation, and accuracy variation according to iterations. The mean average precision is the evaluation metric for object detection, and it was calculated by averaging the area under the curve of the precision-recall graph. Two DL models were used for object detection (femoral and tibial regions). The precision-recall graphs for both the femoral and tibial region detection are presented in Fig. 2a and d. The mean average precision values for the femoral and tibial region detection were 0.84 and 0.89, respectively.

Clinical validity and statistics

To evaluate the clinical validity of the decision-making ability of the proposed DL, the perfect agreement ratios (exact accuracy) of the implant size between the prediction by the surgeon and the actual size used and between the prediction by the DL and the actual size used are reported in Table 2. Moreover, accuracy ± 1, which allows ± 1 size error, is also presented. For femoral implant size, the exact accuracies of the surgeon and DL model were 61.54% and 89.32%, respectively. For the tibial implant size, the exact accuracies of the surgeon and DL model were 68.38% and 90.60%, respectively. The accuracy ± 1 levels for the femoral implant size were 97.44% (surgeon) and 97.86% (DL model), and the accuracy ± 1 for the tibial implant size was 98.72% (both surgeon and DL model).

Discussion

The performance of the proposed DL model was compared with that of an experienced surgeon who used conventional methods and manual measurements. Although minor differences were observed between the surgeon and the DL model in terms of accuracy ± 1, the exact accuracy rates for the femoral and tibial implant sizes indicate > 22% higher level of agreement between the DL predictions and the actual implant sizes used. To further investigate clinical validity, Spearman’s rho coefficients were calculated (Table 3). The rho value provides a measure of agreement, with higher values indicating greater similarity between the predicted and actual implant sizes.

This can enhance the overall surgical workflow and improve patient outcomes. Moreover, it can be trained on diverse datasets and can generalize well to unknown data. This adaptability is particularly advantageous when addressing variations in patient anatomy and implant design. The ability of the model to learn from a wide range of input data enables it to accommodate different patient populations and implant manufacturers, making it a versatile tool for predicting the implant size. Finally, DL-based prediction eliminates the need for manual measurement and assessment of the implant size, which can be time-consuming and prone to human error. By automating the process, the proposed DL models simplify and expedite the selection of appropriate implant sizes, reduce the burden on surgeons, and improve the overall efficiency of the surgical workflow. Even the robotic system for planning also showed similar trend with the proposed DL models [40, 41]. Moreover, the planning on CT-based 3D virtual models also showed high level accuracy [42]. However, the high accuracy was shown from the proposed DL models with minimization of manual manipulation.

However, this study has several limitations. As this study presents the prediction results for only one vendor’s model of various sizes, a general evaluation after training the datasets for various models is essential for application in the clinical field. Second, the manual result was progressed by the single surgeon. It can helpful to maintain the good consistency, however, it causes the lack of inter- and intra-observer variability indicators. Third, the outcomes of the surgeon may be sufficiently different depending on the skill level of the surgeon.

The proposed DL model has the potential to enhance surgical planning and optimize implant selection, leading to improved patient outcomes. Nevertheless, to overcome the limitations of this study, further research and validation using larger and more diverse datasets as well as the involvement of multiple surgeons would strengthen the generalizability and robustness of the findings.

Conclusion

By providing accurate predictions of implant size, the proposed DL model has the potential to enhance surgical planning and optimize implant selection, leading to improved patient outcomes.
