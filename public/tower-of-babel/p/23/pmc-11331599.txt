Preoperative diagnosis of cervical cystic lesions using magnetic resonance imaging: a retrospective study
Authors: Makiko Omi, Yumiko Oishi Tanaka, Nozomi Kurihara, Yuko Sugiyama, Akiko Tonooka, Motoko Kanno, Atsushi Fusegi, Yoichi Aoki, Sachiho Netsu, Akiko Abe, Terumi Tanigawa, Sanshiro Okamoto, Hidetaka Nomura, Hiroyuki Kanao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe conducted this study to clarify the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characteristics of lobular endocervical glandular hyperplasia (LEGH) and Nabothian cysts.MethodsThis study included 48 patients who underwent hysterectomy at our institution between 2016 and 2020 for suspected LEGH. Histopathological studies confirmed the presence of 25 Nabothian cysts and 23 cases of LEGH. We retrospectively analyzed five characteristic MRI findings: (1) located at the upper cervical canal, (2) positioned within the cervical stroma, (3) not circumscribing the cervical canal, (4) low- to iso-intensity on T1-weighted images (T1WI), and (5) “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern. We compared the diagnostic accuracy of these findings for LEGH and Nabothian cysts using sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values. Combinations of findings were also calculated.ResultsThe characteristics “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern, lesion not circumscribing the cervical canal, and low/iso-intensity on T1WI had a sensitivity and specificity greater than 50%. The sensitivity was 73.9% and specificity 84.0% when a combination of “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern and lesion not circumscribing the cervical canal was present.ConclusionThe coexistence of a “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern and not circumscribing the cervical canal was the most characteristic finding that distinguished LEGH from Nabothian cysts. When neither of these findings is present, Nabothian cyst can be suspected.

Background

Lobular endocervical glandular hyperplasia (LEGH) is a benign polycystic glandular proliferative lesion of the uterine cervix. Nucci and Mikami established the histological diagnostic criteria for LEGH in 1999 [1, 2]. Since, in some cases, LEGH can coexist with or be a precursor of adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) or gastric-type mucinous carcinoma (GAS), hysterectomy or careful follow-up is recommended [3–7]. LEGH commonly affects the higher part of the cervix; hence, the detection rates of abnormal cytology and histology are low [8]. Many studies have attempted to diagnose LEGH using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Multicystic lesions with a so-called “cosmos pattern” (a small cyst or solid parts surrounded by larger cysts) and their occurrence in the upper cervical canal are the two well-established characteristic MRI findings of LEGH [9].

Many diseases appear as cystic lesions in the uterine cervix [10]. Among them, the Nabothian cyst is commonly seen in patients of reproductive age and can be confused with LEGH. These cysts do not require treatment if asymptomatic; however, there have been reports of patients undergoing surgical intervention after receiving a misdiagnosis of LEGH based on MRI findings. A multicenter study conducted in Japan revealed that approximately 30% of patients who underwent surgery for suspected LEGH or GAS had benign glandular lesions [9]. A hysterectomy for a Nabothian cyst alone represents an aggressive treatment that will impose an undue physical and emotional burden on the patient.

Clarifying the MRI characteristics of LEGH and Nabothian cysts may help avoid unnecessary procedures. Therefore, in this study, we retrospectively reviewed and compared the records of patients with LEGH and Nabothian cysts who underwent hysterectomy for suspected LEGH.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We conducted a survey study by reviewing the records of MRI findings of patients with LEGH and Nabothian cysts who were treated at the Cancer Institute Hospital, Tokyo, Japan. Patients who underwent an MRI for cervical cysts at our institution between 2016 and 2020 were enrolled (Fig. 1). Patients were eligible if we could retrospectively collect their data from the medical/radiographic reports. We excluded patients (1) whose MRI scans were of too poor quality due to various reasons, such as large slice thickness/intersection gap or numerous motion artifacts or susceptibility artifacts from intestinal peristalsis; (2) who had a gynecological concurrent illness other than uterine fibroids; or (3) who did not undergo hysterectomy or conization. Therefore, 48 patients who had confirmed histopathological diagnosis were included in this study.Fig. 1Flowchart of the patient selection process

Flowchart of the patient selection process

We evaluated their medical records, including preoperative cervical cytology, MRI, and hematoxylin–eosin sections of the surgically resected uterus. Additional data, including follow-up period, age at the first visit, menopausal status, symptoms, and reason for consultation were also reviewed.

Patient characteristics

The median follow-up period (from the date of the first visit to the date of the hysterectomy) was 63.5 days (range: 10–4204). The median age of all patients at the first visit was 48 years (range: 39–74 years), 47 and 48 years for the patients with LEGH and Nabothian cysts, respectively (Table 1). Fourteen patients (29%) were post-menopausal; of these, six had LEGH and eight had Nabothian cysts. The most common reason for consultation was abnormal cytology (35%), followed by symptoms (23%), incidental findings on imaging (21%), and discovery at a health check-up (21%). Three-quarters of the patients were asymptomatic; the symptoms in the remaining patients included watery discharge (17%) and abnormal bleeding (8%). Patient characteristics

Pathology

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections of the hysterectomy specimens were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) and diagnosed based on the WHO classification findings, as “lobular growth of small- to medium-sized mucinous glands surrounding a central cleft.” If the results of the HE staining were morphologically ambiguous, HIK1083 immunostaining was performed. Eight patients with LEGH were confirmed to have gastric-type mucin with the aid of monoclonal antibody HIK1083 staining.

Cytology

Cytology samples were obtained from the cervical canal and portio vaginalis at the time of the initial visit. We used the Onest Cervical Brush® (Onest Medical) to obtain cervical canal specimens for cytology and J fit brush® (Stain Chemical) to obtain portio vaginalis samples. Papanicolaou staining was performed. Glandular cells were determined as being atypical when atypical endocervical cells with golden mucin were observed.

Results of histological and cytological studies

Histological studies confirmed 25 cases of Nabothian cysts (three with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia or carcinoma in situ) and 23 cases of LEGH (two with AIS and two with GAS). One patient with GAS was initially diagnosed with LEGH after conization, and GAS was identified 8 years later by hysterectomy. Atypical glandular cells were identified on cytology in 9/19 (47.4%) patients with LEGH alone; none were seen in patients with Nabothian cysts. Adenocarcinoma was observed in all four cases of LEGH coexisting with AIS or GAS (Table 2). MRI technique and analysis

MRI was performed using 1.5 T or 3 T superconducting units (Discovery MR75w GE Health Care, Chicago, IL; MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany; Titan 3 T, Canon Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; Ingenia Elition, Philips, Best, The Netherlands) with a 28-cm field of view, a 3–5-mm slice thickness, and a 0.6–1-mm intersection gap. Sagittal T1-weighted images (T1WI) were obtained with spin-echo sequences, and sagittal and axial T2-weighted images (T2WI) with turbo spin-echo sequences. Contrast-enhanced and fat-saturated T1WI were obtained by spectral pre-saturation inversion recovery. When a patient underwent more than two MRI assessments during the follow-up, the findings obtained just before the hysterectomy or conization were selected for review.

Evaluations were performed independently by an experienced radiologist (Y.O.T.) who was blinded to the patients’ background information. Suspicion of LEGH was based on the following imaging-based criteria (Figs. 2, 3, and 4): (1) the lesion covers the internal uterine os or the midline of the lesion is located at the upper half of the cervical canal, (2) the lesion reaches deeper than the epithelium and is mainly located within the cervical stroma, (3) the lesion does not circumscribe the cervical canal, (4) the majority of the cyst content appears low- to iso-intense, compared to the cervical wall, on T1WI, and (5) the lesion forms a “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern, with a “cosmos” pattern defined as small cysts (< 3 mm) surrounded by larger cysts and a “microcystic” pattern defined as a collection of small cysts (< 3 mm).Fig. 2Schema of the cystic lesion. Statistical analysis

We assessed how accurately each of the five MRI findings could be used to distinguish between LEGH and Nabothian cysts by comparing the sensitivity and specificity values. In addition, the combination of two MRI findings was also considered. The diagnosis “ + ”, which indicated that LEGH was suspected, was only made if both MRI findings were labeled as “ + ”; otherwise, the diagnosis “-” was made, indicating a suspected Nabothian cyst.

We considered all combinations of two of the five MRI findings, resulting in a total of 10 combinations.

We expected the specificity to improve when considering different combinations because the conditions for a “ + ” diagnosis were more stringent than those for a “ + ” diagnosis based on each MRI finding alone.

Sensitivity was defined as the percentage of patients with LEGH who had a “ + ” MRI finding. Specificity was defined as the percentage of patients with Nabothian cysts who had a “-” MRI finding. When both sensitivity and specificity were above 50%, LEGH and Nabothian cyst classification performance was determined to be better than random. Furthermore, we focused on specificity because we wanted to avoid misclassification of Nabothian cysts as LEGH. Consequently, we expected specificity to be as high as possible and sensitivity to be at least 50%. In addition to sensitivity and specificity, positive predictive values (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) were also calculated for reference. We also present the point estimates and 95% confidence intervals for these indices.

Results

The percentages of patients with each of the five MRI findings according to group are depicted in Table 3. Cysts were located at the upper cervical canal in 23/23 (100%) patients with LEGH and in 22/25 (88.0%) patients with Nabothian cysts. A “cosmos,” “microcystic,” or other pattern was observed in 16, five, and two patients with LEGH and in nine, three, and 13 patients with Nabothian cysts, respectively. The cysts were positioned within the cervical stroma in 17/23 (73.9%) patients with LEGH and in 20/25 (80.0%) with Nabothian cysts. The cyst did not circumscribe the cervical canal in 18/23 (78.3%) patients with LEGH and in 10/25 (40%) with Nabothian cysts. The cyst content was low- to iso-intense on T1WI in 16/23 (69.6%) patients with LEGH and in 11/25 (44%) patients with Nabothian cysts (Tables 3 and 4). Three out of the five MRI findings, that is, “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern, not circumscribing the cervical canal, and low- to iso-intensity on T1WI, had a sensitivity and specificity greater than 50%.

The results for the 10 combinations of the two MRI findings are shown in Table 4. Combination numbers 8, 9, and 10 showed a slight decrease in sensitivity, but a marked increase in specificity. Discussion

All patients in this study had a histologically proven disease, and the MRI findings were evaluated by specialists blinded to any other clinical information including symptoms and cytological findings. The sensitivity and specificity of the combination of “cosmos” or “microcystic” patterns and “lesion not circumscribing the cervical canal” were both high; therefore, when neither of these findings is present, a Nabothian cyst can be suspected. Although the sensitivity of the combination was slightly lower than that of each characteristic alone, the specificity was greatly improved by combining the two. In other words, their combination could reduce the probability of incorrectly diagnosing patients with LEGH.

Previous studies have shown that about 20% of the patients with endocervical adenocarcinoma also have LEGH, with the rate of malignant change reported at 1.4% [3, 5]. In our study, two patients additionally had AIS, one had GAS, and one diagnosed with LEGH by conization was found to also have GAS 8 years later during a total hysterectomy. LEGH and GAS are less likely to show abnormal cytology than squamous intraepithelial lesions because they are located in the upper cervical canal. Malignant changes may be detected by cyst enlargement and deteriorating cytology many years later; therefore, long-term follow-up using repeated MRI and cytological examinations is necessary [5, 11].

Nabothian cyst is a common finding that presents with dilated endocervical glands. It represents the accumulation of mucus by the closure of the cervical glands caused by hyperplasia of the squamous epithelium during the healing process of inflammation [12–14]. As the MRI appearance of benign cysts and GAS can be similar, unnecessary treatment due to misclassification has been reported [9]. Doi and Yamashita reported a characteristic MRI finding of adenoma malignum, a clustered cystic mass within the cervical wall [15, 16]. Since then, many cases with multiple cysts within the cervical wall have been surgically removed and diagnosed as pathologies other than LEGH or GAS, such as Nabothian cysts and tunnel clusters. If the diagnostic accuracy of MRI findings improves, unnecessary surgery for Nabothian cysts can be avoided, and necessary follow-up can be accurately planned.

A multicenter study conducted in Japan revealed that about 30% of the patients who underwent surgery for suspicion of GAS had benign glandular lesions [9]. Cervical pap smears of more than 60% of the patients with LEGH were negative for intraepithelial lesions or malignancy (NILM). At our institution, cytological findings of 10/23 (43.5%) patients with LEGH showed NILM, even when a fine dedicated brush was used to obtain endometrial and cervical canal specimens.

Takatsu et al. defined the “cosmos” pattern as the presence of small cysts or solid parts often surrounded by larger cysts within the cervical wall and found that 87% of the patients with LEGH showed this pattern [9]. Ohya et al. classified 28 LEGH cases into four patterns; the “cosmos” pattern was the most frequently observed at 71.4% (20/28 cases) [17]. Omori et al. divided the MRI findings of 23 cases of LEGH into two categories: 10 flower-type (“cosmos” pattern) and 13 raspberry-type (close aggregation of tiny cysts = “microcystic”) pattern, with the latter being more frequent. The raspberry-type pattern is commonly seen in post-menopausal women and coexists with AIS [18]. In our study, the “cosmos” pattern was found in 69.6% (16/23) of the patients with LEGH, lower than the rate predicted in previous reports. However, when we included the six cases demonstrating a “microcystic” pattern, we detected a sensitivity of 21/23 (91.3%). According to a previous report [17], patients with “microcystic” patterns tend to be older (median age: “microcystic” pattern, 61 years; “cosmos” pattern, 46.5 years) and post-menopausal (“microcystic” pattern, 3/5 [60%]; “cosmos” pattern, 1/16 [6%]), but our patients who also had AIS exhibited a “cosmos” pattern in the pre-menopausal state.

Nabothian cysts are usually clusters of small cysts (2–3 mm in diameter), but they can grow to 4 cm or more [13]. They typically present in a multicystic pattern with cysts of various sizes, a clear margin, and a single macrocyst or scattered smaller cysts [14]. In our study, scattered cysts of various sizes, observed in 13/25 cases (52.0%), were the most frequent finding, but the “cosmos” pattern was also observed in 9/25 cases (36.0%).

LEGH tends to be located in the upper cervical canal, mainly at the internal cervical os; the lower margin rarely crosses the squamocolumnar junction [8]. Nabothian cysts generally appear superficially at the squamocolumnar junction of the cervix, but multiple and large cysts can extend deep into the cervical stroma, making it challenging to distinguish them from LEGH [13]. In our study, all but three Nabothian cysts were located in the upper cervix. Although we expected most of the Nabothian cysts to be located in the endocervical epithelium, 20/25 (80.0%) Nabothian cysts and 17/23 (73.9%) LEGH lesions were observed in the cervical stroma, at roughly the same frequency. Most patients who underwent hysterectomy at our hospital had lesions located in the upper cervix and within the cervical stroma.

Nabothian cysts are filled with mucus, but they also contain proteinaceous material and neutrophils, presenting with varying intensities depending on the viscosity of the liquid. High viscosity and rich protein content lead to high intensity, and the liquid component causes hypo-intensity on T1WI. A previous study reported that all 19 cases of Nabothian cysts showed iso- or high-intensity [12]. On the other hand, LEGH cysts were filled with gastric mucin, and Ohya et al. reported that 15/19 (78.9%) cases of LEGH showed hypo- or iso-intensity on T1WI [17]. In our study, hypo- or iso-intensity was seen in 11/25 (44.0%) cases of Nabothian cysts and 16/23 (69.6%) cases of LEGH; notably, LEHG is presumed to have more water content than Nabothian cysts have.

We empirically speculated that LEGH does not circumscribe the cervical canal. Although histological examination did not reveal the reason for this observation in our study, we assumed that in LEGH, dendritic glandular ducts swell around glandular ducts that enter from one location, whereas in the case of Nabothian cysts, glandular ducts encroach into the cervical wall from multiple locations of the endocervical epithelium. As expected, this pattern was present in 10/25 (40.0%) of Nabothian cysts and 18/23 (78.3%) of LEGH cases.

The diagnosis of cervical cysts is difficult to make using MRI alone, regardless of the diagnostic accuracy of MRI. Therefore, a comprehensive diagnosis must be based on the presence of watery discharge and abnormal cervical cytology, which may be present in LEGH. The discharge must be examined and the upper cervix must be subjected to cytological studies. Although transvaginal ultrasonography is a less invasive and simpler method of examination, MRI has higher contrast resolution and can diagnose the anatomical location more accurately. As the MRI findings examined in this study only required T1WI and T2WI without dynamic or diffusion-weighted images, we believe that our approach can be integrated into clinical practice.

Our study has several limitations. First, this is a retrospective study involving a single institute. This resulted in a small sample size and potential selection bias. We hope that diagnostic accuracy can be improved by further studies with larger samples. Second, the MRI parameters varied across cases, as our institution employs multiple MRI devices supplied by various vendors. Among those parameters, varying slice thicknesses, for example, could have affected the evaluation of the distribution and patterns of the cysts. Despite these limitations, the strength of this study is the high diagnostic accuracy of the MRI readings and pathological diagnosis of cervical cysts, consistently performed by the same radiology and pathology specialists.

Conclusions

The combination of the “cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern and “ not circumscribing the cervical canal” yielded the best diagnosis results in our data evaluation. This combination improved the specificity, although it also reduced the sensitivity compared to the diagnoses made according to each MRI finding. This indicates that this combination improves the probability of correctly diagnosing Nabothian cysts, assuming that it does not also significantly decrease the probability of correctly diagnosing LEGH. “Cosmos” or “microcystic” pattern and a lesion that does not circumscribe the cervical canal were thus the characteristic findings that may help distinguish LEGH from Nabothian cysts. If neither of these findings is present, Nabothian cysts can be suspected.
