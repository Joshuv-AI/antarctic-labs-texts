Reclaiming Rights and Justice: Migrant Mobilization and Collective Resistance in Canada
Authors: Siyuan Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Migrant rights and justice movements in Canada have arisen within an increasingly hostile political and discursive environment marked by restrictive immigration policies, precarious labor regimes, and the racialized marginalization of migrant communities. Yet these conditions have also generated various forms of collective resistance that contest the exclusionary boundaries of citizenship, labor rights, and national belonging. Drawing on a case study of a migrant domestic workers’ organization and Migrant Spring rallies in Vancouver, this article examines how migrant workers and their allies organize under precarious conditions. The analysis shows that migrant activists transform structural marginalization into symbolic power through public protests, intersectional coalition-building, and media interventions. By challenging nationalist narratives that frame migrants as disposable labor or social burdens, these movements articulate a more inclusive and transformative vision of political belonging and social change.

Introduction

In recent years, migrant rights and justice movements in Canada have emerged amid an increasingly hostile political and discursive environment. Migrants remain structurally indispensable to the Canadian economy and society, especially in the sectors of healthcare, agriculture, food services, education, and caregiving. Yet, they have become targets of growing public resentment, political scapegoating, and exclusionary nationalist rhetoric. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated these contradictions. While the former Canadian Prime Minister Justin Trudeau and other political leaders publicly praised essential workers for sustaining society during the crisis, many of these workers, who are migrant laborers, refugees, and undocumented immigrants, remained precariously positioned within legal, economic, and social hierarchies. Rising housing costs, stretched public services, and post-pandemic economic insecurity have been widely discussed in public discourse as consequences of immigration, despite extensive evidence that these crises stem from long-term austerity measures, neoliberal policy failures, and corporate profiteering. Migrant workers, undocumented immigrants, and refugees thus occupy a profoundly contradictory position: they are vital to the functioning of key domains of the economy and everyday life, yet simultaneously treated as outsiders whose presence is conditional, temporary, or expendable.

This intensifying hostility toward migrants marks a critical shift in terrain of migration politics in Canada. Once framed predominantly through liberal multicultural or humanitarian narratives, debates about immigration have become increasingly polarized, echoing trends in the United States and Europe. Right-wing political actors, mainstream media commentators, and certain public voices have amplified narratives casting migrants as competitors for jobs, housing, and social services, fueling moral panics that legitimize exclusionary policies. In this environment, migrant rights and justice movements operate within a symbolic terrain in which migrants’ very presence must be justified publicly.

Critical scholarship on migration and labor has long emphasized that migrant precarity is structurally produced through state policies, employer practices, and nationalist ideologies that render migrant labor simultaneously indispensable and disposable (e.g., Choudry and Smith 2016; Chun 2016; Sharma 2006; Walia 2021). Building on this insight, this study examines how migrant rights and justice movements in Canada respond to these conditions through public protest, intersectional coalition-building, and strategic engagement with media. The Canadian case is particularly compelling. Despite its reputation as a welcoming immigrant society, Canada relies heavily on temporary and conditional migration programs that institutionalize precarity and stratify labor. These structural arrangements create fertile conditions for both exploitation and scapegoating, situating migrant rights and justice movements in a paradoxical environment where economic necessity intersects with social and political exclusion and precarity.

While this article broadly engages migrant rights and justice movements in Canada, it is empirically grounded in a case study of community-based organizing and the 2024 Migrant Spring mobilizations in Vancouver. This study examines how national-level discourses and campaigns are enacted within a specific local organizational context. This approach allows for a deeper analysis of grassroots organizing practices, while also acknowledging the limits of generalizability beyond this case.

Drawing on social movement theories, the study examines the formation of collective identities, which enable migrants to recognize their personal struggles as part of broader systemic injustices, and emphasizes the critical role of informal networks and grassroots organizations in sustaining mobilization among marginalized populations. It also highlights the importance of political and media opportunities, including crisis moments such as the COVID-19 pandemic, shifting public discourse, and moments of policy debate, which activists strategically leverage to advance claims.

The study employs a qualitative, multimethod case study approach that combines semi-structured interviews with migrant activists, participant observation at rallies and marches, and discourse analysis of advocacy materials, government documents, and alternative media. The study addresses four main research questions: How does community-based organizing among migrant domestic workers in Vancouver enable mobilization under conditions of precarious immigration status and racialized labor? How are collective identities and solidarities constructed across differentiated immigration statuses within this local context? What repertoires of contention and strategic practices characterize grassroots migrant activism during Migrant Spring 2024? How does migrant activism reconfigure dominant discourses of deservingness, citizenship, and national belonging?

Migrant Precarity and Resistance

The intersection of dynamics of capital accumulation, uneven development between countries in the Global North and Global South, regional wars and conflicts driven by imperialist expansion, and racialized and securitized regimes of the state border control all contribute to precarious conditions faced by underprivileged migrants, including migrant workers, refugees, and undocumented immigrants. These overlapping forces shape reasons for displacement and migration and migrants’ everyday experiences of labor exploitation, social exclusion, legal vulnerability, and political marginalization in receiving societies. The forces produce and sustain a global system in which migrant labor is both essential to capitalist economies and simultaneously rendered disposable and exploitable (Walia 2021).

The production of migrant precarity is closely tied to capitalist labor regimes that rely on flexible, low-cost, and easily replaceable workers. Migrant labor programs regulate mobility, restrict access to permanent residency, and limit family reunification, thereby institutionalizing temporary and contingent forms of labor. These arrangements function as mechanisms of labor discipline, enabling employers and states to manage workforce flexibility while minimizing social obligations. Such dynamics reflect what scholars describe as the restructuring of labor markets under neoliberal globalization, in which migration becomes a key strategy for managing labor shortages and suppressing wages (de Haas, Castles and Miller 2014). Historically, temporary migrant worker programs introduced in Western countries following the Second World War exemplify this logic. These programs recruited migrant workers for low-wage sectors such as agriculture, construction, manufacturing, and care work while limiting access to citizenship and social rights. France's postwar agricultural recruitment programs and similar initiatives across Europe institutionalized temporary migration regimes designed to maintain a disposable labor force (Décosse 2016; de Haas, Castles and Miller 2014). As Walia (2021, 8) observes, “while migrant workers are temporary, temporary migration is permanent,” underscoring how migration regimes produce enduring systems of labor control and stratified citizenship.

These dynamics are not limited to Western contexts. In rapidly developing economies, migrant labor has similarly become central to economic growth and urbanization. Singapore, Taiwan, and Gulf countries rely heavily on migrant workers in sectors such as construction, domestic work, and manufacturing (Cheng 2016; Chin 2019; Pande 2013). For underprivileged migrants, political and economic precarity are inextricably entangled, producing layered conditions of vulnerability and insecurity.

The mutual entanglement of economic and political precarity is particularly evident among refugees and undocumented migrants. The contemporary refugee crisis reflects broader structural forces, including global inequality, environmental degradation, and geopolitical conflicts (Khiabany 2016). However, refugees are frequently framed as humanitarian burdens or security threats, obscuring their incorporation into precarious labor markets. For example, refugees displaced by conflict in Libya have been absorbed into Italy's agricultural sector as low-wage labor (Pradella and Cillo 2021). Similarly, undocumented migrants are often portrayed as economic threats despite their central role in sustaining key sectors such as agriculture, construction, and care work (Becerra et al. 2012).

While structural precarity constrains migrant agency, migrants are not passive subjects of exploitation. Rather, precarity and resistance coexist in dynamic tension. Migrant resistance can take multiple forms, ranging from everyday acts of survival to collective mobilization and political activism. Everyday resistance plays a critical role in migrants’ responses to marginalization. For instance, migrants in post-Brexit Britain have confronted xenophobia through community organizing and interethnic solidarity initiatives (Sime et al. 2022).

Collective resistance is often facilitated through community organizations and advocacy networks. While traditional unions may struggle to organize precarious migrants due to legal barriers, employer control and fragmented workplaces, community-based organizing functions as an alternative to union-based mobilization. In Singapore, for example, advocacy organizations provide legal support, rights education, and social networks that enable migrant workers to challenge exploitation (Dencik and Wilkin 2015). In the United States, Syrian refugee communities have mobilized collectively to advocate for educational and social rights (Lew 2024). Community-based worker centers provide legal assistance, advocacy and political education to underprivileged immigrant workers (Fine 2006).

Digital media has also transformed migrant resistance by enabling new forms of collective action. Refugees in Finland have used social media to challenge negative stereotypes and assert their contributions to society (Nikunen 2019). Rural migrant workers in China have utilized digital platforms to document labor exploitation and organize strikes (Qiu 2016; Yin 2018). These digital practices extend opportunities for collective action beyond traditional organizational structures.

These dynamics illustrate that migrant precarity is not passively endured but actively negotiated. Migrants challenge exclusion through everyday resistance, community organizing, and collective mobilization. These practices reshape political identities and expand possibilities for solidarity across diverse migrant groups. In this sense, migrant precarity is not only a condition of vulnerability but also a catalyst for political mobilization and social transformation.

Migrant Labor, Precarious Status, and Racialized Capitalism in Canada

Canada's capitalist economy has been historically and structurally dependent on migrant labor. From the late nineteenth century to the present, migrant workers have been differentially incorporated into the labor market through regimes that racialize and gender labor in ways that render it both exploitable and disposable (Choudry and Smith 2016; Sharma 2006). Historical examples such as Chinese railway workers and Caribbean domestic workers demonstrate how migrant labor has been mobilized to meet capital's demands while being excluded from full political and social membership. Recent scholarship further conceptualizes this process through the lens of precarious immigration status. Goldring and Landolt (2013) argue that migrant legal status exists along a continuum, ranging from temporary workers to undocumented migrants, all of whom experience varying degrees of insecurity, limited rights, and vulnerability to deportation. Precarious immigration status functions as a mechanism of labor discipline, restricting workers’ mobility, suppressing collective organizing, and reinforcing employer power. Similarly, Vosko (2019) conceptualizes deportability as a key feature of contemporary migration regimes and argues that the constant threat of deportation shapes migrant workers’ everyday lives and constrains collective resistance.

Canada's Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP), established in the 1970s, institutionalized this system of differential inclusion. Programs such as the Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program (SAWP) and the Live-in Caregiver Program (LCP) tie workers’ legal status to employers, restricting mobility and reinforcing vulnerability (Sharma 2006; Thomas 2016). The LCP, formalized in 1992, significantly expanded Canada's reliance on migrant care labor, particularly racialized women from the Global South. While the program offered a conditional pathway to permanent residency, this pathway was structured through prolonged precarity, employer dependency, and heightened vulnerability to abuse (Koo and Hanley 2016; Tungohan 2023). For instance, Pratt's study on Filipino domestic workers in Canada demonstrates that these migrant workers navigate transnational family separation, emotional labor, and structural marginalization, illustrating the gendered and racialized nature of migrant care labor (Pratt 2012). These migration regimes operate within broader dynamics of racialized capitalism and social reproduction. Migrant labor is central to the reproduction of capitalist economies, particularly through care work, domestic labor, and other forms of socially reproductive labor that sustain households and communities (Chun 2009; Ferguson and McNally 2014).

When TFWP was established in the 1970s, nationalist discourse was mobilized to legitimize a “them versus us” binary between migrant and local workers (Sharma 2006). This framing positioned migrant labor as temporary, disposable, and external to the national community (Sharma 2006; Walia 2021). Such nationalist sentiment remains salient in the contemporary period. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, while the public widely celebrated essential workers as patriotic heroes, migrant workers were simultaneously positioned as foreign laborers rather than members of the national community, despite the fact that many essential services are performed by migrant workers (Yin 2024).

The contradiction between economic reliance on migrant labor and their political exclusion constitutes structural conditions that give rise to migrant workers’ resistance. In Canada, migrant workers’ resistance encompasses a mix of overt resistance and covert, everyday-based resistance. Visible forms of collective resistance include endeavors toward unionization, and participation in worker strikes, public protests, and rallies (Choudry and Thomas 2013; Cohen and Hjalmarson 2020; Russo 2011). Individual workers also engage in overt resistance by speaking to the media, participating in documentary films, and pursuing legal action against their employers (Cohen and Caxaj 2018). Yet, precarious immigration status and disadvantaged positions often pose formidable obstacles for migrant workers to engage in overt resistance. Under conditions of deportability and restricted labor rights, migrant workers’ resistance frequently takes subtle and informal forms, including temporal disruptions of work, collective pacing practices, and clandestine connections with migrant justice networks, particularly among migrant agricultural workers. These actions enable migrant workers to challenge exploitative labor conditions and mistreatment without jeopardizing their employment opportunities.

Over the decades, grassroots organizations and migrant advocacy groups play a critical mediating role in transforming individual grievances into collective resistance. By providing legal knowledge, organizational resources, and spaces for solidarity, migrant justice organizing often emerges outside traditional unions. (Encalada Grez 2006; Thomas 2016). Community-based organizing is particularly significant for migrant domestic workers, particularly due to the unique challenges they face, such as not sharing common workplaces. Unlike other labor sectors where workers may congregate in centralized locations, migrant domestic workers often operate within private homes, making traditional forms of unionization or workplace advocacy difficult. Community organizing thus becomes essential in creating networks of support and advocacy for these workers (Pratt and Migrante 2018; Tungohan 2023).

Decades of resistance by migrant communities have laid the foundation for the current migrant rights and justice movements in Canada. In this study, I draw upon the perspectives of social movement studies to analyze how these grassroots efforts have transformed into more intersectional movements that bridge labor and social justice struggles.

Four Key Elements of Social Movements

In exploring the process of contentious politics and social movements, scholars have identified four key elements, collective identities, informal networks, political opportunities, and media opportunities. These four elements interact in dynamic ways to shape the trajectory of social movements, providing the resources, support, and strategic openings essential for mobilization and resistance.

Collective identities play a crucial role in collective action, fostering a sense of belonging and commitment among individuals as they engage in the process of identification. Identification process in collective action is not necessarily determined by individuals’ social positions along gender, class, race, ethnicity, rather, it is more “based on shared orientations, values, attitudes, worldviews, and lifestyles, as well as on shared experiences of action” (della Porta and Diani 2020, 92). This view can avoid an essentialist understanding of the relationship between identities and collective action.

Informal networks are a pivotal characteristic of social movements. Informal networks refer to individuals, social groups, and organizations engaging in resource exchanges to achieve shared objectives while maintaining their autonomy and independence (Diani and Bison 2004; della Porta and Diani 2020). Informal networks involve three main aspects. First, individuals can participate in social movements without any formal affiliation or membership in social movement organizations (della Porta and Diani 2020). Second, networks matter in mobilizing individuals (Gould 2007). Preexisting social, political, religious, community networks facilitate mobilization of individuals in social movements (Kraamwinkel 2016; Wickham-Crowley 1992; Wills et al. 2009). Third, social groups and organizations form alliances during the development of social movements.

Political opportunity structure refers to the arrangement of political conditions, encompassing both formal and informal institutions, policies, and power dynamics, that influence the emergence, development, and outcomes of social movements within a specific political environment (Tarrow 1994/2022). The primary components of political opportunities include the level of acceptance of protest by those in authority, the extent to which formal political channels are accessible, and the presence of influential allies (della Porta and Diani 2020). The endurance of protest often depends on the degree of tolerance by authorities.

Media scholar has developed the concept of mediation opportunity structure to capture the relationship between media and social movements, including mainstream media representation of social movements and the uses of media and technologies within these movements (Cammaerts 2012). In the mass media age, mainstream media tend to underrepresent or misrepresent protests and social movements (Rucht 2004). In the era of Internet and social media, a cluster of studies has closely examined the adoption of digital technologies in resistance actions among migrant groups in various national and transnational contexts (Dencik and Wilkin 2015; Nikunen 2019; Yin 2018).

Drawing on these four perspectives, this study seeks to explore how participants in migrant rights and justice movements develop and negotiate collective identities, which social actors are involved in forming informal networks, what types of informal networks have been established, and the role of mainstream and social media in shaping the development of these movements.

Method

This study adopts a case study approach that integrates semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and discourse analysis to examine migrant justice organizing in a specific local context. The study is empirically grounded in a case study of Filipino domestic worker organizing in Vancouver, centered on the Vancouver Committee for Domestic Workers’ and Caregivers’ Rights (CDWCR). Established in 1992, CDWCR is a community-based organization that advocates for the rights of migrant domestic workers and caregivers and provides free legal, immigration, and settlement support. This research focuses on how national campaigns and discourses are enacted and experienced within a local organizational context. This approach enables in-depth analysis while acknowledging limits to generalizability beyond the case.

Migrant Rights Network (MRN) is treated as an important contextual actor, a nationwide alliance established in 2018 that connects more than thirty migrant justice organizations across Canada. The Network represents a wide range of migrant constituencies, including migrant workers, undocumented immigrants, refugees, international students, and care workers. It serves as a central coordinating body for cross-country mobilizations that challenge restrictive immigration policies and advocate for permanent resident status, full labor rights, and access to public services for all migrants.

A key site of analysis is Migrant Spring, a major national rally organized by the MRN, alongside other coordinated protests, marches, and actions held in March and September 2024. I conducted participant observation at Migrant Spring events in Vancouver in March 2024, focusing on speeches, protest practices, coalition-building, and interactions among participants. In Vancouver, these rallies drew hundreds of participants, including migrant workers, community organizations, and allied groups, providing an important site for examining the scale and visibility of local mobilization within a broader national campaign. These events provided an important empirical lens through which to examine how migrant movements construct collective identities, frame injustice, and mobilize symbolic power in public space.

To ground the analysis in migrants’ lived experiences and organizing practices, I conducted nine semi-structured interviews with activists and migrant domestic workers affiliated with the CDWCR. Participants were recruited through organizational networks and snowball sampling. While limited in number, this sample enables in-depth qualitative analysis of activist trajectories and organizing practices. Interviews were conducted between March and June 2024, allowing participants to reflect on both ongoing activism and recent mobilizations.

All interview participants are Filipino migrants aged between 40 and 60. Most participants were current or former migrant domestic workers under temporary foreign worker programs, with some transitioning to permanent residency or citizenship. All participants identified as women, reflecting the gendered composition of domestic work. The interviews explored participants’ pathways into activism, including their personal histories, motivations, experiences of precarity, and perceptions of risk, solidarity, and collective action. Interviews were conducted in a semistructured format to allow participants to reflect in depth on their activist trajectories while enabling comparative analysis across cases. To protect participants’ privacy and safety, all interviewees have been anonymized.

To analyze how migrant activism navigates political and media opportunity structures, the study employs critical discourse analysis of multiple types of texts produced by both state and non-state actors. The corpus includes: (1) advocacy materials produced by the Migrant Rights Network, such as calls to action, policy proposals, press releases, petitions, and campaign statements; (2) Canadian government immigration-related documents, including mandate letters, policy announcements, and public speeches; (3) alternative media sources; and (4) digital materials, including websites and social media posts from migrant justice organizations.

The discourse analysis focuses on how key concepts, including essential work, status, rights, justice, dignity, and belonging, are constructed, contested, and rearticulated across these texts. Particular attention is paid to the underlying ideologies embedded in state narratives and activist counter-discourses, as well as to how advocacy groups strategically mobilize language, symbolism, and digital platforms to amplify migrant voices and legitimize their claims. This approach allows for an examination of how migrant movements generate symbolic power by reframing stigmatized identities and exposing the contradictions of nationalist immigration regimes.

This research is informed by a reflexive awareness of the researcher's positionality. While I am not a member of the migrant domestic worker community, my sustained engagement with migrant justice advocacy and prior research on migrant labor has shaped both access to participants and interpretation of the data. I have also conducted research projects with local migrant worker NGOs in China and Canada, participating in community events and advocacy initiatives that provided insight into migrant workers’ lived experiences and grassroots organizing practices. At the same time, I remain attentive to power dynamics in knowledge production and the limitations of my positionality, seeking to foreground participants’ voices and experiences while adopting a reflexive approach to analyzing migrant activism under conditions of legal precarity, gendered labor, and social marginalization.

Becoming Migrant Activists Under Conditions of Nationalist Exclusion

The development of migrant rights and justice movements in Canada should be understood against the backdrop of nationalism as a governing logic that produces hierarchies of belonging. While the Canadian state relies heavily on migrant labor, humanitarian migration, and international education, it simultaneously constructs noncitizens as conditional, temporary, and external to the national community. Becoming a migrant activist is not merely an individual political choice but a collective response to nationalist boundary-making that differentiates “deserving” members of the nation from those deemed foreign, temporary, or expendable.

The Vancouver-based Migrant Spring mobilization shows that diverse migrant groups, including migrant workers, undocumented immigrants, refugees, and international students, came together under the unifying demand of Status for All. Rather than organizing around discrete legal categories, participants challenged nationalism itself by exposing how immigration status functions as a technology of exclusion. The coalition rejected the fragmentation imposed by state policy and asserted a shared political identity grounded in precarity, disposability, and unequal access to rights. In doing so, migrant activists reframed themselves not as passive beneficiaries of humanitarianism or temporary labor regimes, but as political actors contesting the very foundations of national belonging.

Although Migrant Spring discourse brings together diverse groups, including undocumented migrants, refugees, and international students, the experiences analyzed in this study are grounded primarily in migrant domestic workers from CDWCR. This focus reveals how broader migrant justice discourses are enacted and sustained through localized, community-based organizing. Unlike traditional unions, CDWCR operates as a community-based organization, a model particularly suited to migrant domestic workers who labor in private households and are frequently excluded from formal labor protections and collective bargaining frameworks. As Fine (2006) and Chun (2016) contend, worker centers and grassroots community organizations often emerge in sectors where unions face structural barriers to organizing, particularly among precarious immigrant workers dispersed across fragmented and informal workplaces. Domestic work exemplifies these conditions, as workers are isolated within private homes and subject to employer dependency and immigration precarity.

This organizational model is also shaped by the gendered nature of domestic work. Migrant domestic labor is embedded within social reproduction and care economies, where gendered expectations of emotional labor, caregiving, and obedience reinforce workers’ vulnerability and invisibility (Pratt 2012; Tungohan 2023). These gendered dynamics not only structure exploitation but also shape pathways into organizing. Community-based organizations like CDWCR provide collective spaces where migrant women can transform individualized experiences of marginalization into shared political grievances. Through advocacy, legal support, community-building, and political education, these organizations foster leadership development and collective agency among precarious immigrant workers (Chun 2016; Tungohan 2023). The long-term sustainability of CDWCR, established in 1992, challenges assumptions that precarious workers are difficult to organize and demonstrates the durability of community-based models in sustaining migrant activism over time.

The experiences of L and R illustrate how migrant activism is shaped by transnational trajectories that unsettle nationalist assumptions about political agency. Both women arrived in Canada as migrant domestic workers, yet their political consciousness and organizing skills were forged in the Philippines through decades of engagement in labor struggles, popular protests, and resistance to state violence. L and R's trajectories reveal the central role of Global South activists in shaping labor and migrant justice movements in Canada. Their prior experiences with repression, collective organizing, and state violence sharpened their understanding of nationalism as a tool of governance and exclusion. As L reflected, “It's not just domestic workers [being exploited] in one country, but many countries, Canada, the Philippines, Mexico, etc.” Her statement underscores how migrant activism is rooted in an awareness of global capitalism and nationalist regimes as interconnected systems.

When L co-founded CDWCR, she carried this transnational political sensibility into the Canadian context. Her insistence that “we must speak up” reflects a rejection of the nationalist expectation that migrants should remain grateful, silent, and compliant. Similarly, R's continued activism after obtaining Canadian citizenship challenges the notion that political engagement is tied to legal status alone. Her motivation, “it's concern for others,” signals a form of solidarity that exceeds individual mobility and instead targets the exclusionary structures that continue to marginalize non-status and temporary migrants.

For migrants without prior activist experience, becoming an activist often begins within community-based organizations that provide collective protection against the risks produced by nationalist immigration regimes. Migrant organizations such as CDWCR function as political incubators where migrant workers can reimagine themselves as rights-bearing subjects rather than temporary laborers. The slogan “If we are good enough to work, we are good enough to stay” directly confronts nationalist logics that justify inclusion solely on economic terms while denying political membership.

The narratives of M and F demonstrate how activism emerges through collective support in contexts shaped by fear, surveillance, and legal precarity. M described how participation in CDWCR transformed her sense of self: “When I am with our group… I don't feel scared.” Fear of deportation, employer retaliation, or immigration consequences is a central affective mechanism through which nationalism disciplines migrant subjects. As Vosko (2015) shows in the context of migrant agricultural workers, legal precarity actively suppresses collective action. Domestic workers articulated similar anxieties, particularly regarding permanent residency applications. Collective organizing, however, mitigates these fears by distributing risk and fostering a sense of shared struggle.

F's transformation from a hesitant worker to a public speaker at a rally illustrates how activist identities are cultivated through encouragement and recognition within the collective. Her struggle with language testing requirements highlights how nationalist immigration systems construct “merit” through racialized and classed benchmarks that disproportionately exclude migrant women. Speaking publicly against these constraints represents not only personal courage but also a challenge to the depoliticization of migrant suffering.

Migrant activism is not confined to formally recognized workers. Refugees, undocumented immigrants, and international students also experience nationalist exclusion, albeit through different policy regimes. Refugees, though discursively positioned as humanitarian subjects, face long-term marginalization, credential devaluation, and racialized surveillance. Undocumented immigrants, meanwhile, occupy the most extreme position of exclusion, rendered invisible and criminalized by the state. International students, while often framed as “ideal” migrants due to their economic contributions, are subjected to tuition exploitation, precarious work conditions, and uncertain pathways to permanent status.

The Status for All framework articulated during the Migrant Spring collapses these distinctions by identifying immigration status itself as the central axis of inequality. This approach challenges nationalist narratives that rank migrants according to productivity, vulnerability, or moral deservingness. Instead, it foregrounds shared experiences of exclusion and asserts a collective claim to rights, dignity, and political voice. Migrant activism thus becomes a site where fragmented migrant identities are reassembled into a broader political subject that contests the nation-state's authority to determine who belongs.

The formation of a collective migrant activist identity is neither linear nor uncontested. While many activists sustain their commitment beyond securing legal status, internal divisions persist. Y's frustration with former migrant workers who oppose expanded pathways to permanent residency reveals how nationalist logics can be internalized even within marginalized communities. The belief that others must “struggle as I did” reflects a scarcity model of belonging that mirrors state discourses of deservingness.

Nonetheless, the collective identity forged through migrant activism remains a powerful counter-narrative to nationalism. It is built not on citizenship or legal status, but on shared experiences of exclusion and a commitment to social justice that transcends individual advancement. Whether through transnational activist histories or first-time engagement in community organizing, migrant activists transform fear into political action and isolation into solidarity. In this sense, becoming a migrant activist is a process of politicization that exposes nationalism as both a material and ideological force. By challenging exclusionary borders, legal, symbolic, and social, migrant activists assert themselves as key actors in shaping more inclusive visions of belonging, rights, and justice in Canada.

Building Symbolic Power Through Inclusive Alliances and Intersectional Agendas

In Vancouver, Migrant Spring rallies drew hundreds of participants, while coordinated actions across multiple Canadian cities expanded the movement's reach. The Migrant Rights Network has secured endorsements from hundreds of organizations for key campaigns such as Status for All, indicating a broad coalition base. In addition to in-person mobilization, digital platforms—including organizational websites and social media—play a crucial role in disseminating calls to action, circulating petitions, and sustaining engagement. Together, these indicators suggest that migrant justice mobilization operates through both localized gatherings and expansive networked infrastructures.

Beyond NGOs and advocacy organizations of migrant workers, refugees, undocumented immigrants, and international students, Migrant Spring was also actively supported by Indigenous communities, the Palestinian diaspora, and LGBTQ groups. This broad coalition transformed the rally into a highly visible site where multiple marginalized constituencies articulated overlapping critiques of exclusion, dispossession, and inequality. The Migrant Spring exemplifies how migrant justice movements strategically mobilize inclusive alliances and intersectional agendas to generate what Jennifer Chun conceptualizes as symbolic power, which is the capacity of marginalized actors to gain public recognition and legitimacy for their struggles by rendering injustice visible in the public arena (Chun 2009).

Speeches delivered during the rally addressed a wide range of issues, including Canada's restrictive immigration regime, the complicity of the Philippine state in labor export, demands for decolonization and Indigenous sovereignty, opposition to imperialism, calls for refugee and undocumented migrant justice, solidarity with Palestine and demands for a ceasefire, LGBTQ rights, and the precarious conditions facing international students amid soaring tuition fees and housing costs. Rather than fragmenting the movement, this multiplicity of issues functioned as a symbolic leverage strategy, reframing migrant justice as inseparable from broader struggles against racial capitalism, settler colonialism, heteronormativity, and global inequality (Figure 1).

Through such intersectional framing, Migrant Spring directly contests nationalist discourses that construct migrants as outsiders, burdens, or temporary instruments of the nation-state. Nationalism, as enacted through immigration regimes, relies on symbolic boundaries that distinguish between those who belong and those who may be exploited without inclusion. In this context, migrant activism operates not only as a demand for policy change but as a struggle over meaning, recognition, and moral worth. Migrant Spring demonstrates that marginalized groups can generate political leverage by transforming stigmatized identities, such as temporary, foreign, or illegal, into morally legitimate political subjectivities. Through public protest, migrants assert their dignity and reframe themselves as rights-bearing members of a broader social and moral community.

The collective occupation of public space is central to this process. By gathering in visible, highly symbolic locations, migrant workers, undocumented immigrants, refugees, and international students disrupt dominant narratives that render them invisible or reduce them to individualized administrative cases. Rather than appearing as isolated problems to be managed through immigration control, they emerge collectively as political actors making claims to justice, equality, and belonging. In Chun's terms, symbolic power is produced through the public performance of injustice (Chun 2009). By narrating lived experiences of precarity, exploitation, and exclusion, migrants expose the moral contradictions of a system that depends on their labor while denying them recognition and rights. This visibility transforms vulnerability into a source of moral authority, allowing migrants to contest the legitimacy of nationalist exclusion itself.

This symbolic power is further consolidated through the organizing infrastructure of the Migrant Rights Network, which situates Migrant Spring within a broader repertoire of coordinated, cross-country actions. The Network's expansive advocacy agenda—demanding permanent resident status for all migrants, full labor rights and minimum wage protections, universal access to public services, Indigenous self-determination, and gender, racial, climate, and anti-Islamophobic justice—reflects a deliberately intersectional political vision. Rather than advancing migrant claims as exceptional or narrowly humanitarian, the Network situates migrant precarity within shared structural conditions produced by neoliberal austerity, racialized labor markets, settler colonial governance, and global inequality. This framing strengthens symbolic legitimacy by aligning migrant justice with widely resonant struggles for fairness, dignity, and collective well-being.

The movement's symbolic power is sustained not only through formal organizations but through dense informal networks of solidarity. The alliances forged through Migrant Spring extend beyond membership-based advocacy groups, enabling collaboration among grassroots organizations, community collectives, and unaffiliated individuals. These informal networks amplify migrant voices, broaden the audience for migrant justice claims, and deepen the movement's moral resonance. By explicitly linking migrant struggles to those of Indigenous peoples, Palestinians, LGBTQ communities, and students, the movement foregrounds the interconnectedness of oppression across lines of citizenship, race, gender, and sexuality. In doing so, it rejects nationalist logics that isolate migrants as a special-interest group and instead positions migrant justice as integral to broader struggles against systemic injustice.

This strategic deployment of symbolic power is also evident in the Migrant Rights Network's cross-country marches against racism and immigrant scapegoating. In September 2024, coordinated marches across nine Canadian cities confronted right-wing narratives that blame immigrants for housing shortages and rising living costs. The Network's call to action reframed these crises as outcomes of corporate greed, policy failures, and decades of austerity that have eroded public services for all (Migrant Rights Network 2024). By situating migrant injustice within a wider critique of neoliberal governance, the movement sought to expand its symbolic appeal beyond migrant communities and cultivate broader public identification with its demands. In this way, symbolic power operates not only to legitimize migrant claims but to rearticulate the boundaries of collective responsibility and solidarity in contemporary Canada.

These protest events also draw in individuals without formal organizational affiliations. For instance, rallies and demonstrations often attract ordinary people who willingly sign petitions in support of migrant workers’ rights. Such encounters illustrate how symbolic power operates through visibility and affect. Public demonstrations create moments where moral claims resonate with everyday experiences of insecurity and injustice, enabling the movement to convert sympathy into political support.

The inclusive alliances and intersectional agendas forged through Migrant Spring and the Migrant Rights Network demonstrate the ways that migrant justice movements in Canada deploy symbolic power to counter nationalist exclusion. By making migrant struggles visible, morally compelling, and interconnected with other social justice causes, these informal networks generate legitimacy for migrant claims and foster a shared sense of purpose among diverse marginalized groups. Symbolic power, in this sense, becomes a critical resource for sustaining migrant activism and advancing a broader project of social transformation that challenges the boundaries of citizenship, belonging, and justice.

Navigating Political and Media Opportunities

The COVID-19 pandemic marked a critical conjuncture in which existing contradictions within Canada's migration regime became highly visible. As the federal government repeatedly praised essential workers for sustaining the economy and social life during a moment of crisis, it inadvertently created a powerful discursive and political opportunity structure for migrant justice movements. The former Prime Minister Justin Trudeau's public recognition of essential labor, delivered through speeches, press conferences, and symbolic gestures, was framed in nationalist terms, celebrating sacrifice, resilience, and collective responsibility. Yet this rhetoric obscured a fundamental contradiction: many of the workers performing essential services were migrants, undocumented immigrants, refugees, and international students who remained excluded from full legal status, labor protections, and social rights. Migrant advocacy organizations seized this moment to expose the gap between symbolic inclusion and material exclusion, transforming state discourse into a site of contestation.

Rather than rejecting the category of essential worker, migrant activists strategically appropriated and reworked it. Through rallies, speeches, and public statements, they reframed essentiality as a moral and political claim to recognition, dignity, and belonging. The voices of undocumented workers at protests illustrate this process of discursive inversion, whereby a state-sanctioned label is repurposed to challenge the very structures that produced exclusion. As one undocumented construction worker declared, “We are all valuable … and we all deserve status.” Another emphasized the continuity of exploitation beyond the pandemic: “We were essential yesterday, we’re essential today” (Migrant Rights Network 2020). These claims contest nationalist logics that treat migrants as temporary economic inputs rather than social and political subjects. By asserting essentiality as enduring rather than exceptional, migrants destabilize the notion that rights can be selectively granted during crises and withdrawn afterward.

Between 2020 and 2024, the Migrant Rights Network translated this discursive leverage into sustained collective action. More than a dozen cross-country rallies, digital protests, and car caravans were organized to demand permanent resident status for all migrants, including undocumented workers, refugees, international students, and temporary foreign workers. These mobilizations directly responded to the federal government's failure to implement meaningful regularization despite public commitments. The former Prime Minister Trudeau's 2021 mandate letter to the Minister of Immigration outlined plans to expand pathways to permanent residency, yet the absence of concrete policy changes reinforced activists’ understanding that symbolic recognition alone does not produce structural transformation (Prime Minister of Canada 2021). As articulated in a Migrant Rights Network call-to-action, collective pressure, rather than benevolent state acknowledgment, was identified as the primary driver of political change (Migrant Rights Network 2022).

Migrant justice movements operate within and against existing political opportunity structures. While state discourse provided a momentary opening, it was migrant-led mobilization that sustained pressure over time, preventing essentiality from becoming an empty symbol. The proposal for a “Comprehensive and Inclusive Regularization Program,” endorsed by over 500 organizations, further illustrates how movements convert protest into policy-oriented demands (Migrant Rights Network 2023). This combination of disruptive action and institutional engagement reflects a strategic understanding of the state as both a site of exclusion and a terrain of struggle.

Media representation has been central to amplifying these claims and enhancing their legitimacy within the broader public sphere. During the pandemic, mainstream Canadian news media often framed migrant activism in relatively supportive terms, highlighting migrants’ contributions and the urgency of their demands (Yin 2024). Media visibility allowed migrants to contest stigmatizing narratives that depict them as threats to national cohesion or drains on public resources. As one activist noted, consistent media presence at rallies fostered a sense of validation and political momentum, reinforcing activists’ belief that their voices were being heard beyond movement circles.

At the same time, alternative and left-oriented media have played a crucial role in articulating critiques that extend beyond humanitarian appeals. Independent media, such as Spring, in their series of articles supporting migrant rallies and campaigns, situate migrant justice within a broader internationalist and anticapitalist framework, and explicitly link immigration control to racial capitalism, imperialism, and class exploitation (Spring Magazine 2026). By framing borders as tools used by elites to divide the working class and criminalize migrants, alternative media challenge nationalist common sense and advance a counter-hegemonic vision of solidarity. This ideological work is particularly significant in resisting right-wing narratives that scapegoat migrants for housing shortages, inflation, and declining public services. Instead, migrant justice movements redirect attention toward corporate greed, austerity policies, and structural inequalities that affect both migrant and non-migrant workers.

Digital media further extend these political and symbolic struggles by enabling coordination, documentation, and affective connection across space. Migrant organizations actively maintain websites, social media accounts, and digital archives to share information, mobilize supporters, and preserve the history of their campaigns. For migrant domestic workers, Facebook groups affiliated with organizations such as CDWCR serve as vital spaces of community-building, political education, and mutual care. These digital infrastructures allow migrants to overcome isolation, particularly for those working in private homes or precarious conditions, and to participate in collective action despite geographic and temporal constraints.

Digital archives also function as repositories of movement memory, transforming ephemeral protest into enduring political narratives. By documenting rallies, statements, and media coverage, migrant organizations assert the historical significance of their struggles and provide resources for future activism. In this sense, digital media do not merely supplement offline mobilization; they actively shape the temporal and spatial continuity of migrant justice movements.

Migrant activists navigate and reshape political, discursive, and media opportunity structures. By appropriating state rhetoric, leveraging media visibility, and combining grassroots protest with policy advocacy, migrant justice movements convert symbolic recognition into collective pressure. They expose the foundational contradiction of Canada's immigration regime: migrants are indispensable to the nation's economic and social reproduction, yet systematically denied full belonging. The reframing of essentiality thus becomes a strategic intervention that challenges nationalist boundaries of inclusion and advances a broader vision of justice grounded in dignity, equality, and solidarity.

Conclusion

Migrant rights and justice movements in this study are examined not simply as policy-oriented efforts but as broader struggles over recognition, dignity, and belonging, which unfolds within and contests the hegemonic regimes of nationalism, citizenship, and labor governance. At the same time, this analysis is grounded in a specific empirical case: Filipino domestic worker organizing in Vancouver. The findings therefore do not aim to represent the full diversity of migrant organizing across Canada, but rather to provide in-depth insight into how grassroots activism operates within a particular local and organizational context. Migrants’ collective actions challenge dominant narratives that frame them as temporary laborers, security threats, or social burdens, and instead assert their presence as rights-bearing political subjects embedded within wider communities of struggle. These movements unsettle the exclusionary logics through which political belonging is conventionally defined and governed.

Central to this process is the transformation of structural marginalization into political leverage. Migrant activists mobilize precarity as a basis for collective claims-making. Migrant activists mobilize what might otherwise be read as vulnerability, including undocumented status, precarity, racialization, or deportability, as a basis for collective claims-making. Through protest rallies, marches, public testimonies, and media interventions, injustice is not merely articulated but publicly performed. These actions function as symbolic arenas in which stigmatized identities are resignified. Terms such as undocumented, temporary, or foreign are reframed from markers of illegitimacy into claims of moral worth, social indispensability, and political entitlement. Symbolic power, in this sense, does not derive from formal institutional authority or access to policymaking channels, but from the capacity to render inequality visible, emotionally resonant, and politically compelling to broader publics.

The case of CDWCR highlights the critical role of community-based organizations in sustaining long-term activism among precarious workers, particularly in sectors where unionization is difficult. By foregrounding gendered labor, precarious immigration status, and grassroots organizing, this study demonstrates how local activism mediates broader structural conditions. Even in contexts where institutional responsiveness is limited or absent, symbolic struggles for visibility, legitimacy, and solidarity play a constitutive role in reshaping political imaginaries and expanding the boundaries of the possible. By altering how migrants are seen, heard, and understood, these struggles lay the groundwork for longer-term transformations in public discourse, alliance-building, and political subjectivity. They also facilitate the formation of durable solidarities across legal statuses and racialized identities and strengthen the infrastructural and moral foundations of future mobilization.

The Canadian case reveals the limits of liberal multicultural narratives that celebrate diversity and inclusion while simultaneously sustaining deeply unequal migration regimes structured by temporariness, exclusion, and differential rights. Migrant movements expose these contradictions by disclosing how multicultural recognition coexists with systemic exploitation and disposability. They compel a rethinking of inclusion that extends beyond formal citizenship and legal status toward a more substantive conception of social and political belonging. By foregrounding the interconnectedness of political-economic and social-cultural struggles, migrant rights and justice movements articulate a holistic and transformative vision of resistance to challenge the conventional compartmentalization of labor, immigration, racial justice, and feminist activism. Rather than treating these as discrete arenas of struggle, migrant movements illuminate their mutual constitution and open space for more unified, multifaceted strategies for achieving social justice.

This study focuses primarily on activist organizations and events in Vancouver. Future research could build on this analysis by examining migrant activism in other regional contexts and exploring internal tensions and negotiations within intersectional coalitions. Such inquiries would further illuminate the conditions under which migrant struggles translate into durable political change and the obstacles in sustaining broad-based alliances under conditions of structural inequalities.
