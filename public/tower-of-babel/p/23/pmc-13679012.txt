Preoperative natremia and AKI after cardiac surgery: an international multicenter prospective cohort study
Authors: Jose Maria Mora, Pablo Montero-López, Angel Candela, Esther Gómez, David Nagore, Jorge M Núñez-Córdoba, Gemma Echarri, Raquel Callejas, Marc Vives
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and goal of studyWhether preoperative natremia levels may play a role in the development of acute kidney injury (AKI) after cardiac surgery is uncertain. We conducted a cohort study to evaluate the influence of preoperative natremia on AKI in high-risk patients undergoing cardiac surgery.Materials and methodsThis multicenter prospective cohort study involved 14 British and Spanish hospitals, each under local ethics committee approval. The study population comprised all consecutive patients aged 18 years or older, with a Cleveland Clinic score ≥ 4, undergoing cardiac surgery from July to December 2017. The resultant 241 consecutive patients were classified according to tertiles of preoperative natremia levels (< 139, > 139–141, and > 141 mmol/L).Results and discussionThe AKIN criteria were used to define AKI. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate the adjusted association between preoperative natremia and AKI. A potential link was observed between sodium levels ≤ 139 mmol/L and an increased risk of moderate-to-severe AKI relative to levels above 141 mmol/L (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.02–3.95, p = 0.03), after multivariable adjustment for age and preoperative serum creatinine. No significant associations were observed between sodium levels and RRT or in-hospital mortality.ConclusionsThe results from this cohort of high-risk cardiac surgery patients lend tentative support to the hypothesis that preoperative natremia levels ≤ 139 mmol/L might be linked to a higher risk of moderate-to-severe AKI compared with levels above 141 mmol/L. These findings warrant further evaluation in a larger cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s44158-026-00436-1.Keywords: Natremia, Hyponatremia, Hypernatremia, Acute kidney injury, Cardiac surgery, Renal replacement therapy, Mortality

Introduction

Approximately 1.5 million cardiac surgeries are performed worldwide annually [1]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a significant issue in cardiac surgery because it is common (affecting 20–40% of patients), and is strongly associated with increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital and ICU stays, and higher healthcare costs [2]. Even mild AKI after cardiac surgery increases the risk of short- and long-term mortality, and patients who develop AKI are at elevated risk for progression to chronic kidney disease and need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), which carries a high mortality rate [2].

Preoperative dysnatremia might be related to AKI in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, and its presence increases the risk of adverse outcomes [3]. The prevalence of dysnatremia in the hospital setting ranges from 15 to 30%, but this range may vary in the context of cardiac surgery. However, data may be underestimated due to the lack of consensus on the definition of dysnatremia in multiple studies. Subsequently, it may be challenging to identify an optimal threshold for natremia in patients undergoing major surgery, such as cardiac surgery [4].

In this hypothesis, this study aims to assess preoperative natremia, including those within the normal range, to estimate the risk of AKI and to determine the natremia threshold that increases the risk of AKI.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This multicenter prospective cohort study at 14 Spanish and British cardiothoracic hospitals included all consecutive patients aged 18 years or older with a Cleveland Clinic score (CCS) ≥ 4 undergoing cardiac surgery between July and December 2017 (Fig. 1). The CCS is a well-validated score for identifying patients at risk of severe AKI after cardiac surgery. All cardiac surgery procedures (including major aortic surgery cases) were included except for heart transplantation and pericardiectomy. In addition, patients who were pregnant or had severe preoperative renal insufficiency (defined as need for RRT or estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2) were excluded.

Flow chart of study recruitment

This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board in Barcelona (Reference #EPA027/15) and by each local institution´s Ethics Committees, and informed consent was obtained from the patient or a substitute decision-maker before surgery. Clinical trial number: not applicable. The cohort was divided into three groups: patients with preoperative natremia below 139 mmol/L (n = 108), between 139 and 141 mmol/L (n = 62), and above 141 mmol/L (n = 71).

Definitions and measurements

The main objective of the study was to assess the incidence of AKI in patients undergoing cardiac surgery and its association with natremia levels. Natremia levels were categorized into tertiles based on their empirical distribution within the study population. Hyponatremia was defined as a natremia level ≤ 139 mmol/L, and hypernatremia as a natremia level > 141 mmol/L. The highest tertile (natremia level > 141 mmol/L) was selected as the reference category because it clinically represents the subgroup with the lowest theoretical risk of AKI, thereby facilitating a direct and intuitive interpretation of increased risk in the lower tertiles. The AKIN criteria were used to define AKI.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as means, standard deviations (SD), and percentages. A complete-case analysis was performed. The logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), adjusting for potential confounders including age, Cleveland Clinic score, vasopressor support (intraoperative norepinephrine), inotropic support (intraoperative dobutamine), aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative hemoglobin, and intraoperative transfusion requirements. Covariates included in the final multivariable model were selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance and evidence from previous studies identifying them as potential confounders of the association between preoperative serum sodium and postoperative AKI after cardiac surgery. Variable selection was therefore guided by clinical judgment and existing literature rather than solely by statistical significance in univariable analyses. A p-value < 0.05 (2-sided) was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Stata 19 (StataCorp. 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Population characteristics

Of the 261 patients with a CCS ≥ 4, those with missing data, which was restricted to preoperative natremia (6.5%) and left ventricular ejection fraction (1.1%), were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 241 patients for analysis (Fig. 1). The cohort presented a mean age of 69.6 years (SD: 11.2), with women accounting for approximately one-third of the sample. Most patients were hypertensive (> 80%), and half had a history of chronic kidney disease (CKD); 54.6% had preoperative CHF, and 20.4% had previous cardiac surgery. The mean serum creatinine was 1.5 mg/dl. The types of cardiac surgery were as follows: 25% underwent valve surgery, and 12% underwent CABG. Sixty-five percent of the surgeries were elective (Table 1).

Primary outcome

Moderate-to-severe AKI

When preoperative natremia levels were stratified (≤ 139, > 139–141, and > 141 mmol/L), there were notable differences in the incidence of moderate-to-severe AKI (38.0%, 25.8%, and 23.9%, respectively). Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that sodium levels ≤ 139 mmol/L were associated with a twofold increase in the risk of AKI compared with levels above 141 mmol/L (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.02–3.95, p = 0.03) after adjusting for age and preoperative serum creatinine. Controlling for other potential confounders did not substantially modify these results (Fig. 2). This association attenuated and lost statistical significance after adjustment for age, CCS, vasopressor support (intraoperative norepinephrine), inotropic support (intraoperative dobutamine), aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative hemoglobin, and intraoperative transfusion requirements (OR 1.67, 95% CI 0.79–3.54) (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. S1). The final model included 74 AKI events and 7 covariates, corresponding to approximately 10.5 events per variable.

Secondary outcomes: RRT and in-hospital mortality

Among the preoperative natremia levels groups (≤ 139, > 139–141, and > 141 mmol/L), we also observed differences in the incidence of RRT (19.4%, 8.1%, and 12.7%, respectively). Despite a higher crude incidence of TDE in the preoperative hyponatremia group with natremia levels ≤ 139 mmol/L compared with the reference category (natremia levels > 141 mmol/L), the association was not sustained following adjustment for age, CCS, vasopressor support (intraoperative norepinephrine), inotropic support (intraoperative dobutamine), aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative hemoglobin, and intraoperative transfusion requirements (OR 1.06, 95% CI 0.40–2.85) (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. S1).

When evaluating the incidence of in-hospital mortality among the different levels of preoperative natremia, the rates were 18.5% for the ≤ 139 mmol/L group, 12.9% for the > 139–141 mmol/L group, and 14.1% for the reference group (> 141 mmol/L). In the multivariable logistic regression model, the odds of in-hospital mortality were higher for patients with preoperative hyponatremia (≤ 139 mmol/L) compared with those above 141 mmol/L (OR 1.26, 95% CI 0.51–3.11) after adjustment for age, CCS, vasopressor support (intraoperative norepinephrine), inotropic support (intraoperative dobutamine), aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative hemoglobin, and intraoperative transfusión requirements. However, this association was not statistically significant (Table 4, Supplementary Fig. S1).

Discussion

Our findings suggest a trend linking lower natremia to an increased risk of AKI, although formal statistical significance was exclusively achieved in the model adjusted for age and creatinine. In the remaining configurations, the wider confidence intervals reflect limited statistical precision. Therefore, while the uniformity of the point estimates is noteworthy, these results must be interpreted with caution and validated in larger cohorts. The links between preoperative hyponatremia and a higher risk of RRT requirement or in-hospital mortality were not apparent, despite higher crude incidences of these events in the preoperative hyponatremia group (serum sodium levels ≤ 139 mmol/L) compared with the reference category (serum sodium levels > 141 mmol/L).

Our results are in line with previous studies [3, 5, 6]. Large cohort studies demonstrate that patients with preoperative hyponatremia have higher rates of postoperative AKI and RRT requirement, even after adjustment for comorbidities and procedural risk factors [3, 5].

Data from a retrospective cohort study on 16,238 cardiac surgery patients observed that preoperative hyponatremia (Na⁺ < 135 mEq/L) was independently associated with increased risk of postoperative AKI (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.20–1.93, p < 0.001) after multivariable adjustment [3]. Furthermore, in a retrospective cohort study on 1.008 patients with non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease undergoing cardiac surgery, it was observed that preoperative hyponatremia was independently associated with increased need for RRT (HR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1–1.7, p = 0.0008), and that decreasing preoperative sodium levels were proportionate to the incidence of RRT [5]. Another retrospective cohort study on 4370 cardiac surgery patients reported that preoperative hyponatremia was independently associated with increased risk of postoperative RRT (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.11–2.44, p = 0.013), as well as increased mortality and complications [6]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 observational studies (including cardiac surgery cohorts) found that preoperative hyponatremia was significantly associated with an increased odd of major complications, including postoperative AKI (adjusted OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.23–1.53) [7]. Furthermore, postoperative hyponatremia has also been associated with higher AKI, RRT, hospital length of stay, and mortality after cardiac surgery [8]. While hypernatremia may be more directly associated with AKI development in children, hyponatremia consistently worsens outcomes and prolongs recovery in those who develop AKI [9].

Our study contributes to the current knowledge by using alternative cutoff values for preoperative natremia (139 mmol/L versus 135 mmol/L). None of the studies mentioned analyzed the optimal preoperative sodium level in cardiac surgery to maintain patients within a safe threshold for AKI risk. In our cohort, a significant percentage of patients had hypertension, and up to 54.6% suffered from preoperative Congestive Heart Failure (CHF), suggesting a high rate of treatment with thiazide diuretics before surgery. In our study, a preoperative serum sodium level greater than 141 mmol/L defined a group of patients at lower risk of AKI. However, the optimal preoperative serum sodium level for reducing the risk of severe AKI requiring RRT seemed to be in an intermediate category (139–141 mmol/L).

These findings are of great importance as they suggest preoperative natremia levels at which patients are more vulnerable to renal complications or death.

Previous studies have focused on natremia levels above 135 mmol/L, assuming that these levels are protective. This assumption might be incorrect and may place patients with apparently normal natremia levels at risk. Because sodium balance is dynamic, even minor fluctuations within the low-normal range may compromise physiological homeostasis.

It remains unclear whether hyponatremia is a causative factor in AKI development or merely an early marker of AKI. A recent multicenter study involving 401 patients across 18 countries worldwide (29 centers) analyzed urinary sodium excretion and diuretic response in patients with heart failure [10]. This study found that a volume-depletion approach guided by natriuretic dynamics led to increased natriuresis and shorter hospital stays, underscoring the predictive value of sodium-balance control in patients with heart disease [10]. In contrast, our cohort maintained normal serum sodium levels, minimizing the influence of factors such as volume overload, vasopressin release, or aggressive diuretic use. This finding suggests that the mechanisms underlying AKI risk in hyponatremia may be linked to effects on renal tubular cells, such as osmolar stress-induced oxidative stress and impaired tubular function [11–13].

Current serum and urinary biomarkers for predicting AKI in critically ill patients are not standardized or widely used [14–16]. In contrast, natremia is readily available and reflects a dynamic aspect of the patient’s cardiorenal management. While markers like creatinine and cystatin C can indicate established injury, natremia may help identify patients at early risk, guiding interventions to prevent AKI onset.

This study’s strengths include its prospective design, focus on a real-world, high-risk cardiac surgery population, and detailed covariate analysis. Our international multicenter collaboration (including 14 cardiothoracic hospitals) helps ensure the generalizability of our results.

Our study’s limitations include the relatively small patient cohort, which limits statistical power for secondary outcomes such as renal replacement therapy and in-hospital mortality. Furthermore, due to these sample-size constraints and the limited number of events per predictor, the multivariable analyses may be susceptible to overfitting, warranting a conservative interpretation of the precision and robustness of the adjusted estimates. Therefore, non-significant findings should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out due to unmeasured covariates, including diuretic use, perfusion-related variables, and intraoperative fluid balance. However, we controlled for other key risk factors, and the homogeneity of our high-risk cohort could have reasonably mitigated the impact of unmeasured clinical variables. The observational design allows identification of associations but does not support causal inferences regarding sodium correction as a strategy for AKI prevention. No randomized controlled trials or interventional studies directly demonstrate that correcting preoperative hyponatremia prior to cardiac surgery reduces the incidence of postoperative acute kidney injury or improves outcomes. Despite adjusting for key risk factors, the possibility that hyponatremia functions as a marker of underlying disease severity, neurohormonal activation, fluid status, diuretic exposure, or chronic kidney disease could not be ruled out. The categorization of preoperative serum sodium into tertiles was data-driven and was intended to facilitate exploratory comparisons between groups rather than to define a biologically meaningful threshold. Therefore, the observed cut-off of 139 mmol/L should be interpreted with caution and not considered a definitive clinical threshold. Future studies should evaluate serum sodium as a continuous variable and explore potential non-linear associations to determine whether clinically relevant thresholds exist and to validate our findings in independent cohorts. Lastly, the absence of serial perioperative sodium measurements represents an additional limitation, as perioperative sodium fluctuations might have influenced renal outcomes.

Further research is required to assess whether interventions aimed at maintaining natremia above 139 mmol/L can effectively reduce AKI risk in critical care units.

Current international guidelines, including those from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons and the Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists, recommend routine screening for and correction of electrolyte disturbances, such as hyponatremia, as part of perioperative care bundles to reduce the risk of AKI. Still, these recommendations are based on expert consensus and observational data, not on direct evidence from interventional trials for sodium correction in the cardiac surgery population [17–19]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses in hospitalized adults suggest that correcting severe hyponatremia is associated with reduced mortality and improved outcomes. Still, these studies are not specific to cardiac surgery and do not provide randomized trial data on AKI prevention [20].

Similarly, observational data in general hospitalized populations indicate that inappropriate or overly rapid correction of hyponatremia, especially in the setting of AKI, may increase mortality risk, highlighting the need for careful management [21].

In conclusion, our study suggests that lower preoperative serum sodium concentrations may be associated with an increased risk of postoperative AKI in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The observed threshold of 139 mmol/L was derived from our study population and should be considered exploratory rather than a biologically or clinically established cutoff. Given that the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after full multivariable adjustment, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they support the hypothesis that preoperative serum sodium may be a clinically relevant marker warranting further investigation in larger prospective studies to determine whether clinically meaningful thresholds exist and whether serum sodium plays an independent role in AKI risk stratification.
