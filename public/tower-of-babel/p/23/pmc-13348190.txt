ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical strategies for recurrent lumbar disc herniation (ReLDH) range from repeat microdiscectomy (MD) to instrumented fusion (IF). Identifying predictive factors for surgical treatment could improve patient selection and outcomes. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of Modic changes (MC) on clinical outcomes, radiological progression, and re-operation rates in ReLDH. The secondary objective was to compare outcomes between MD and IF stratified by MC patterns to determine whether MC assessment can guide surgical decision-making.MethodsThis is a pre-planned subgroup analysis from a single-center retrospective cohort study of prospectively collected registry data. We analyzed 450 consecutive patients who underwent surgery for ReLDH (MD: n = 316, IF: n = 134) from 2004 to 2024. Radiological assessment included T1-and T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences analyzing presence and type of MC, disc height, and Pfirrmann grade of the affected segment, and spinopelvic parameters on standing radiographs. Persistent MC were defined as maintaining the same Modic type at both index and revision surgery (e.g., Type 1→Type 1). Patient-reported outcome was assessed using the Core Outcome Measures Index (COMI) and achievement of Minimal Clinical Important Change (MCIC ≥2.2 points) up to 5 years postoperatively. Re-operation rates, time to re-operation, and rates of subsequent re-revision surgery were analyzed for both surgical groups, with at least 5 years of follow-up.ResultsPatients with persistent MC Type 1 (n = 64; MD: 41, IF:23) demonstrated the shortest median time to revision (0.58 years) compared to no MC (1.03 years, p = 0.012). Persistent MC Type 1 demonstrated the highest re-revision rate among all MC patterns following MD (48.8%), which was dramatically reduced with IF (13.0%, p < 0.05), representing a 3.7-fold risk reduction. Overall re-revision rates were 36.7% for MD versus 15.7% for IF (p < 0.001). Persistent MC Type 1 pattern showed accelerated disc degeneration with progression to Pfirrmann grade 5 in 29.7% (vs 5.8% in no MC, p < 0.001) and severe disc height loss (>30% height loss) in 20.3%. While COMI outcomes showed no significant differences between surgical approaches at any timepoint, IF demonstrated numerically greater improvement at 2 years (5.3 vs 3.5 points, p = 0.692; derived ODI: 33.1 vs. 25.9). MCIC achievement in persistent MC Type 1 patients showed no significant differences at any time point: 3 months (65.0% IF vs 47.8% MD), 1 year (63.2% IF vs 64.7% MD), 2 years (76.9% IF vs 70.6% MD), and 5 years (66.7% IF vs 66.7% MD).ConclusionPersistent Type 1 MC represent a distinct high-risk phenotype in ReLDH characterized by early recurrence, elevated re-revision rates, and accelerated segmental degeneration, compared to other MC types. The 3.7-fold reduction in re-revision rates and superior functional outcomes with IF provide compelling evidence, that warrant consideration in surgical decision-making.Keywords: Modic changes, Recurrent lumbar disc herniation, Microdiscectomy, Instrumented fusion, Re-operation, Disc degeneration, Vertebral endplate

Introduction

Modic changes (MC) are defined by distinct vertebral endplate marrow signal changes on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and have become increasingly recognized as important contributors to the pathophysiology of chronic low back pain. Initially described by Modic et al. (1988a), these lesions are classified into three types based on their MRI characteristics: MC type I represent bone marrow edema and inflammation (hypointensity on T1-weighted images and hyperintensity on T2), type II lesions reflect fatty degeneration (hyperintensity on T1-and T2-weighted images), and type III lesions indicate subchondral bone sclerosis (hypointensity on T1-and on T2-weighted images) (Dudli et al., 2022).

Epidemiological studies demonstrate that Modic changes are prevalent among patients with degenerative disc disease (DDD), with reported prevalence rates ranging from 19% to 59%, predominantly affecting the L4–L5 and L5–S1 spinal segments (Albert and Manniche, 2007a; Braithwaite et al., 1998). Although MC type II lesions are frequently observed and may account for up to 90% of MC (Modic et al., 1988a; Schmid et al., 2004; Kuisma et al., 2006), type I lesions have been more strongly associated with increased pain intensity and disability (Yang et al., 2025). MC type I lesions are associated with active inflammatory processes with increased concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, and PGE2 in adjacent discs, and with more edema in the bone marrow. Ohtori et al. (2006) and Dudli et al. (2022) found that vertebral endplates in discogenic low back pain patients with MC type 1 exhibited increased nerve fiber density (PGP 9.5 immunoreactivity) and elevated tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) immunoreactive cells compared to no MC or MC type 2 endplates. This suggests active inflammation mediated by proinflammatory cytokines and nerve ingrowth with a higher nerve density in MC type 1 (Fields et al., 2014), as potential causes of discogenic low back pain in type 1, whereas type 2 and 3 lesions likely represent more quiescent stages of the degenerative process. Rannou et al. (2007) demonstrated significantly elevated serum levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), a systemic marker of low-grade inflammation, in patients exhibiting Modic type 1 changes compared to those with type 2 or no MC, but this could not be replicated by a more recent study (Schulze et al., 2024). These findings support the hypothesis that local inflammation contributes substantially to the clinical presentation of patients with MC type 1 lesions.

The prognostic significance of MC in surgery remains controversial, with studies reporting variable outcomes depending on MC type, surgical technique, and patient population (Lambrechts et al., 2023; Nian et al., 2023a), with meta-analyses suggesting that MC Type 1 specifically predicts worse outcomes after decompression procedures, while fusion may modify this relationship through mechanical stabilization and subsequent MC conversion (Nian et al., 2023a; Ohtori et al., 2010; Vital et al., 2003a; Esposito et al., 2006a). Recurrent lumbar disc herniation (ReLDH) presents a unique surgical challenge, with optimal management between repeat microdiscectomy (MD) and instrumented fusion (IF) (Fischer et al., 2026). While fusion demonstrates superior long-term durability with significantly lower re-operation rates over extended follow-up (Fischer et al., 2026), patient selection criteria remain poorly defined, with the lack of objective biomarkers for treatment success and decision. The presence of MC in revision settings may be particularly relevant given evidence of compromised endplate integrity and increased complications after MD in MC-positive patients (Kumarasamy et al., 2022).

Critical knowledge gaps persist and no study has examined whether MC could guide surgical selection in ReLDH. The specific impact of persistent Type 1 changes, those maintaining their inflammatory phenotype before the index and revision surgery, remains unexplored despite biological plausibility (Dudli et al., 2016a).

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of MC type and evolution on surgical outcomes in ReLDH, including clinical outcomes, radiological progression, and re-operation rates. As a secondary objective, we assessed whether MC patterns can guide surgical decision-making by comparing outcomes between MD and IF stratified by MC type and evolution. We hypothesized that persistent Type 1 changes represent a distinct high-risk phenotype, potentially establishing MC assessment as an objective radiological measurement for surgical decision-making in this challenging population.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This is a pre-planned subgroup analysis from our recently published cohort study comparing MD versus IF in 450 consecutive ReLDH patients (2004-2024) (Fischer et al., 2026). The current analysis specifically investigates the impact of MC on surgical outcomes, re-operation rates, and their evolution patterns between index and revision surgery. This retrospective study analyzed prospectively collected data from our local spine registry, in conjunction with the hospital's clinical information system. The registry documents both surgical data and patient-reported outcomes for all spinal procedures performed at our center using the EUROSPINE Spine Tango Registry framework (Mannion et al., 2009a). We identified patients with surgically treated ReLDH who underwent either repeat MD or IF, including monosegmental TLIF (Transforaminal Lumbar Interbody Fusion) and PLIF (Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion) over a 20-year time period from 01.01.2004 to 31.12.2023. All patients had previous MD and underwent MRI before revision and index surgery. ReLDH was defined as reherniation at the same spinal level (ipsilateral or contralateral). Exclusion criteria comprised ReLDH at other spinal levels, IF as index surgery, re-operations due to postoperative complications and for degenerative spinal stenosis or foraminal stenosis.

The study was approved by the cantonal ethics commission (BASEC, 2023-01683) with patient consent and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki II.

Baseline clinical assessment

Electronic patient charts were reviewed to extract baseline information including age, sex, BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, number of recurrences, time between the surgeries and surgery types. The predominant clinical presentation was systematically categorized into three distinct patterns: predominant low back pain, predominant radiating leg pain, or equivalent presence of both symptoms. Neurological deficits, complications, and subsequent procedures were documented throughout follow-up.

Radiological assessment

Radiological measurments were conducted using the Picture Archiving and Communications System (PACS) and digital picture analysis (JiveX DICOM viewer, VISUS Health IT GmbH, Bochum, Germany) by two spine surgeons with a minimum of three years of specialized experience in spine surgery and spinal imaging. Herniation type was classified by anatomical location on MRI. Disc height was measured in millimeters on T2-weighted median-sagittal MRI at the widest point of the disc space, perpendicular to the endplate. Disc degeneration was evaluated using the Pfirrmann grading system (Pfirrmann et al., 2001). Vertebral endplate changes were assessed using the Modic classification (Modic et al., 1988b) according to T1-and T2-weighted MRI sequences before the index and revision surgery and classified into the predominant type and distinct evolution patterns. Persistent MC were defined as maintaining the same Modic type (0, 1, 2, or 3) at both index and revision surgery (e.g., Type 1→Type 1). New onset MC were defined as progression from no MC (Type 0) at index surgery to any MC type at revision surgery. Progressive patterns were defined as transitions from one MC type to a numerically higher type between index and revision surgery (Type 0 → 1, Type 0 → 2, Type 1 → 2, Type 1 → 3, or Type 2 → 3). Regressive patterns were defined as transition to a numerically lower type (Type 2 → 1, Type 1 → 0 and Type 2 → 0).

Spinopelvic parameters were measured on preoperative standing lateral radiographs, including segmental lordosis (SL: angle between the superior endplate of the upper vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower vertebra at the affected level), total lumbar lordosis (LL: angle between the superior endplate of L1 and the superior endplate of S1), and pelvic incidence (PI).

COMI

Patients were requested to complete the Core Outcome Measures Index (COMI) questionnaire preoperatively and at 3 months and 1, 2, and 5 years after surgery (Mannion et al., 2009b). The COMI is a multidimensional index consisting of validated questions covering the domains of pain (leg/buttock and back pain intensity, 0 - 10 scale), function, symptom specific well-being, general quality of life, and social and work disability (each measured on a 0 - 5 scale). The final COMI score results in a value from 0 (excellent) - 10 (worst) (Mannion et al., 2009a, 2009b; Cômes et al., 2025), with a minimal clinically important change score (MCIC) for individual improvement of 2.2 points (Mannion et al., 2009b).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics, with categorical variables reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline differences between groups were analyzed using unpaired Student's t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. To reduce selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed based on age, sex, BMI, ASA score, Pfirrmann grade, Modic changes, and pre-revision COMI scores. Changes in COMI over time and between treatment groups were assessed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc comparisons. The proportion of patients achieving MCIC at follow-up was evaluated using logistic regression, with odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. Survival Kaplan-Meier curves were analyzed using pairwise log-rank testing with Bonferroni correction. COMI scores were converted to estimated ODI scores using the validated crosswalk by Mannion et al. (2022) to facilitate comparison with the wider literature; derived ODI scores are provided for descriptive purposes only. The following linear regression formulas were applied: for baseline scores, ODI = COMI × 7.13 − 4.20; for follow-up scores, ODI = COMI × 6.34 + 2.67.

Finally, uni- and multivariate logistic regression was conducted to identify potential risk factors associated with the outcome, with results presented as ORs and 95% CIs, and illustrated using a forest plot.

Results

Study population

450 patients who underwent revision spine surgery were analyzed, comprising 316 patients (70.2%) in the MD group and 134 patients (29.8%) in the IF group. The mean age of the overall cohort was 53.1 ± 15.0 years, with 254 patients (56.4%) being male. Patients in the IF group were significantly older than those in the MD group (58.7 ± 14.1 vs 50.7 ± 14.6 years, p < 0.001) and had a lower proportion of males (45.5% vs 61.1%, p = 0.002). Body mass index was similar between groups (26.4 ± 5.0 vs 25.8 ± 4.8 kg/m2, p = 0.24), as was the proportion of current smokers (35.8% vs 42.4%, p = 0.19). IF patients were significantly older (58.7 vs 50.7 years, p < 0.001), predominantly female (54.5% vs 38.9%, p = 0.002), and had higher ASA grades (p = 0.004). The distribution of MC at index surgery did not differ significantly between the MD and IF groups (p = 0.40). For details refer to Table 1.

Modic change distribution and evolution

MC prevalence increased significantly from 41.3% at index surgery to 53.8% at revision surgery (p < 0.001), representing a 12.5% absolute increase with unidirectional progression—56 patients (21.3% of those without initial MC) developed any new MC between index and revision surgery (McNemar's χ2 = 55.0, p < 0.001). MC Type 1 changes demonstrated the most dynamic increase between the surgeries (18.0% to 25.1%, p < 0.01), type 2 changes showed a non-significant increasing trend (22.2% to 27.1%, p = 0.089), and type 3 changes remained rare at both timepoints (1.1% to 1.6%, p = 0.561). Re-reoperation rates and time to revision

Analysis of time to revision surgery (Fig. 2) revealed that patients with persistent Type 1 MC had the shortest median time to revision at 0.58 years (interquartile range [IQR] 0.15-1.38 years), significantly shorter than persistent no MC (median 1.03 years, IQR 0.31-2.94 years, p = 0.012), persistent type 2 MC (median 1.13 years, IQR 0.33-4.22 years, p = 0.001), and progression from no MC to type 1 (median 1.39 years, IQR 0.44-3.05 years, p = 0.032). The 1-year revision-free survival rate for persistent type 1 was 37.5%, compared to 52.4% for persistent no Modic, 52.1% for persistent type 2, and 57.4% for progression from no Modic to type 1.

| Time to revision surgery by MC type Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that persistent type 1 Modic changes conferred the shortest time to revision, with a median time to revision of 0.58 years compared with 1.03 years for patients without MC. Pairwise log-rank testing with Bonferroni correction revealed that persistent type 1 changes differed significantly from all other evolution patterns (P < 0.05 for all comparisons).

Multivariate analysis revealed a significant overall surgical effect with an odds ratio of 0.30 (95% CI: 0.18-0.52, P < 0.001) favoring IF over repeated MD. However, interaction effects between MC patterns and surgery type were not statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons, including persistent Type 1 MC (interaction OR 0.31, corrected p = 0.735), persistent no MC (interaction OR 0.66, corrected p = 0.735), persistent type 2 MC (interaction OR 0.61, corrected p = 0.735), and progression from no MC to type 1 (interaction OR 0.58, corrected p = 0.735). These non-significant interaction terms indicate that while fusion provides superior outcomes overall, no specific Modic pattern demonstrated statistically greater benefit from fusion compared to other patterns.

Clinical outcomes

Despite the marked disparity in failure rates, both surgical approaches achieved significant functional improvement from baseline at all follow-up timepoints across all MC types (p < 0.001). Outcomes among patients with persistent Type 1 MC (n = 64, see Table 5), baseline COMI scores were similar between groups (MD: 7.8 ± 1.9, IF: 8.1 ± 1.8, p = 0.61). At 2 years post-revision, the IF group shows numerically greater mean improvement (5.3 ± 2.8 vs 3.5 ± 2.1 points) and higher MCIC achievement rates (76.9% vs 70.6%). However, these between-group differences did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons (FDR-corrected p > 0.05). This pattern of significant improvement from baseline (p < 0.001) with comparable between-group outcomes was consistent across all measured timepoints (3 months, 1 year, 2 years and 5 years), with MCIC achievement rates exceeding 60% by 1 year in both groups. Multivariate analysis revealed no significant associations between MC patterns and clinical outcomes after FDR correction (all p > 0.398). After correction for multiple comparisons, no significant differences in COMI scores or MCIC achievement were observed between surgical groups at any timepoint (all p > 0.05). Using a validated COMI-to-ODI crosswalk (Mannion et al., 2022), derived ODI scores were similar at baseline (IF: 53.6, MD: 51.4) and at 5 years (IF: 24.2, MD: 24.9), with lower scores in the IF group at 2 years (20.4 versus 25.5).

Radiological progression

Persistent Type 1 MC were associated with accelerated disc degeneration. Progression to Pfirrmann grade 5 occurred in 29.7% of persistent Type 1 MC patients versus 5.8% with persistent no MC (OR 6.83, p < 0.001). Severe disc height loss (>30% reduction) was observed in 20.3% of persistent Type 1 MC cases compared to 14.7% with persistent Type 2 and 8.8% with stable no MC (p < 0.05). Mean disc height loss (from index to revision) was −16.3% for persistent Type 1 changes versus −13.0% for persistent Type 2 and -11.7% for stable no MC patterns (p < 0.05). Any Pfirrmann grade progression occurred in 51.6% of persistent Type 1 patients compared to 35.4% and 35.4% in persistent Type 2 and persistent no MC groups, respectively (p < 0.05).

Follow-up

Two distinct follow-up endpoints were assessed. First, clinical and radiological follow-up for the re-operation endpoint was tracked through institutional registry and clinical records. Among patients requiring re-reoperation, 110 (80.3%) completed minimum 5-year follow-up through clinical (including COMI scores) and/or radiological assessments. Clinical and radiological follow-up yielded a mean follow-up of 6.1 years (median 4.2 years). Five-year follow-up rates were 78.4% (91/116) for MD (mean 7.2 years) and 90.5% (19/21) for IF (mean 7.5 years). Second, patient-reported outcomes (COMI) were collected at standardized intervals (preoperative, 3 months, 1, 2, and 5 years postoperatively). As inherent to a 20-year enrollment period (2004–2023), COMI completion rates decreased at later timepoints; sample sizes at each timepoint are reported in the corresponding tables.

Discussion

This first large-scale analysis of 450 patients establishes persistent Type 1 MC as a distinct high-risk phenotype in ReLDH, characterized by accelerated re-herniation (median 0.58 years), dramatically elevated re-revision rates following repeated MD (48.8%), and progressive disc degeneration compared to other MC types. The 3.7-fold reduction in re-revision risk with IF (13.0% vs 48.8%, p < 0.01) and the observation that 73% of MD failures in this cohort ultimately required fusion (Fischer et al., 2026), underscores the progressive nature of disc degeneration when inflammatory endplate changes persist despite surgical intervention. These findings address a critical gap in ReLDH management, where surgical selection criteria remain poorly defined.

Outcome

IF transformed outcomes in this high-risk population. Beyond the dramatic reduction in re-revision rates, IF demonstrated superior functional recovery compared to repeated MD with mean COMI improvement of 5.3 points versus 3.5 points at 2 years, and higher MCIC achievement rates across multiple timepoints (76.9% vs 70.6% at 2 years). Although multivariate analysis showed non-significant interaction terms (likely due to limited sample size, n = 64), the clinical magnitude remains compelling. Our findings align with and extend Udby et al.'s (Udby et al., 2025) observations. While they demonstrated that the extent of MC (≥25% vertebral involvement) predicted worse outcomes in lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) surgery, our study reveals that the persistence and type of MC may be even more important than extent alone. Their MCG classification system represents an important methodological advance in standardizing MC assessment, yet their cohort's composition—92% Type 2 changes with only 14 Type 1 patients—limits comparison. The discrepancy in Type 1 prevalence between studies (6.7% in Udby vs 14.3% persistent Type 1 in ours) likely reflects fundamental pathophysiological differences between primary LSS and ReLDH. LSS typically represents end-stage degenerative disease where inflammatory Type 1 changes have already converted to fatty Type 2 degeneration, whereas ReLDH involves acute-on-chronic mechanical failure where active inflammation persists. Watanabe et al. (2025) 10-year data showing a dramatic increase in MC prevalence from 37.1% preoperatively to 74.2% in LSS, demonstrating the progressive nature of these changes. Critically, their analysis revealed that preoperative MC Type 2 was associated with significantly inferior long-term outcomes, lower JOA scores (18.2 ± 4.9 vs 23.1 ± 4.2 for MC1, p = 0.02) and recovery rates (24.3% vs 55.3% for MC1) even after adjusting for age, sex, sagittal alignment, and disc degeneration severity. Their data provides important context for understanding our persistent Type 1 phenotype. While Watanabe found that 75% of MC Type 1 progressed to Type 2 and only 8% resolved over 10 years, our persistent Type 1 patients represent those in whom this natural progression failed to occur. This subset may represent a particularly severe form of disc degeneration that resists both natural resolution or surgical stabilization.

The differential treatment response observed in our study aligns with subgroup analyses from recent systematic reviews. Nian et al. (2023b) meta-analysis of 2299 patients with lumbar disc herniation undergoing discectomy found that Type 1 MC were associated with inferior ODI scores at 2 years (weighted mean difference 5.82, 95% CI 2.47-9.17). Kumarasamy et al. (2022) prospectively analyzed 309 consecutive patients undergoing primary MD, including 86 (27.8%) with preoperative MC (Type 1: n = 31, Type 2: n = 47, Type 3: n = 8). They found patients with any MC had significantly worse VAS back pain scores at all follow-up points: 3 months (3.4 ± 2.8 vs 2.1 ± 2.3), 6 months (3.1 ± 2.9 vs 1.8 ± 2.2), and 12 months (2.9 ± 3.0 vs 1.6 ± 2.1), all p = 0.001. ODI scores showed similar patterns. While this supports MC as a negative prognostic factor, our study demonstrates that appropriate surgical selection (IF for persistent Type 1) can overcome this challenge. Li et al. (2021) compared Percutaneous Transforaminal Endoscopic Discectomy (PTED) versus TLIF in 53 patients with Type 1 MC, showing superior TLIF outcomes for back pain (VAS 2.1 ± 0.8 vs 3.4 ± 1.2) and ODI (18.3 ± 5.7 vs 24.6 ± 7.2) at 12 months. Our extended 5-year follow-up captures late failures and demonstrates sustained fusion benefit.

The prognostic significance of MC on clinical outcomes has been explored in multiple studies (Udby et al., 2025; Minetama et al., 2022; Laustsen and Bech-Azeddine, 2016a; Ulrich et al., 2020). Most of these studies included patients with LSS and did not specifically define distinct MC types and found no association between preoperative MC and postoperative changes in PROMs (Laustsen and Bech-Azeddine, 2016a; Ulrich et al., 2020). Studies on total disc replacement have suggested that MC might be a prognostic factor for a positive outcome after disc replacement in isolated LBP cases (Laustsen and Bech-Azeddine, 2016b; Esposito et al., 2006b). The variation in spine phenotype classification, including MC definition and grading, has been speculated as the possible cause of the heterogeneous results on MC and PROMs (Herlin et al., 2018; Udby et al., 2022).

While COMI scores showed no statistically significant differences between treatment strategies, the numerical improvements favoring fusion at 2 years (5.3 vs 3.5 points) and MCIC achievement rates across timepoints suggest clinically meaningful differences that warrant consideration in surgical decision-making. Patients with persistent type 1 MC should be carefully informed of the possible reduced risk of improvement in function and higher risk of patient dissatisfaction at two-year follow-up and higher risk of re-operation in the long term.

Pathobiological mechanisms and clinical implications

The dynamic nature of MC described by Albert & Manniche (Albert and Manniche, 2007b), who followed 166 patients with lumbar disc herniation for 14 months, revealed that MC Type 1 prevalence increased from 9% to 29%. Notably, 43% of new Type 1 changes developed at the previously herniated level, with risk factors including age >40 years (OR 3.2) and previous disc herniation (OR 4.5). Our longitudinal data showing evolution from index to revision surgery reflects a dynamic evolution, with 12.4% of patients developing new MC between surgeries. However, our focus on persistent Type 1 MC, those maintaining their inflammatory phenotype despite initial surgery, identifies a particularly aggressive subset (14.3% of our cohort) that appears resistant to the natural resolution process.

The persistence of inflammatory changes between index and revision surgery in 14.3% of our cohort is less than population studies showing up to 46% of Type 1 MC remaining Type 1 at 4-year follow-up, identifying a subset that fails natural resolution where most Type 1 changes and only 7% convert to Type 2 (Jensen et al., 2009). This sub-population likely represents ongoing endplate microfractures with pro-inflammatory disc-marrow crosstalk by Dudli et al., 2016b, 2017. Our observation supports that IF interrupts this “vicious cycle” where inflammation weakens the endplate, leading to further segmental degeneration—evidenced by superior outcomes and reduced re-revision rates.

Our findings validate the conceptual framework linking MC Type 1 to further segmental degeneration and instability. Rahme & Moussa (Rahme and Moussa, 2008) characterized Type 1 as inflammatory edema associated with segmental instability, with excellent fusion outcomes and 75-100% conversion to Type 2. Vital et al. (2003b) confirmed universal conversion at 6 months post-fusion with clinical improvement paralleling radiographic changes. Thompson et al. (2009) documented that MC type 1 demonstrate a positive predictive value in provocative discography for pain response of 0.81 and specificity of 0.98. Consistent with this diagnostic accuracy, our extended follow-up demonstrates profound clinical implications: dramatically reduced re-operation rates (13.0% vs 48.8%) confirming fusion successfully addresses underlying progressive degeneration and instability.

Patients with persistent MC Type 1 should be counseled about the substantially higher re-operation risk with repeat MD and the potential benefits of fusion, though this must be balanced against fusion-related morbidity in individual decision-making.

Disc degeneration and temporal patterns

The median time to revision of 0.58 years for persistent Type 1 changes represents among the shortest intervals reported, contrasting with persistent no Modic (1.03 years, p = 0.012), suggesting faster mechanical insufficiency and degeneration. The accelerated disc degeneration, 29.7% progressing to Pfirrmann grade 5 (OR 6.83, p < 0.001), quantifies observations by Kerttula et al. (2012). They prospectively followed 54 sciatica patients for one year using serial MRI, measuring disc height, signal intensity, endplate defects, and Modic changes. Patients with MC Type 1 (n = 18) showed significantly greater disc height loss (−1.8 mm vs −0.3 mm) and increased endplate defects (67% vs 22%) compared to patients without MC. Our extension of their work to revision surgery demonstrates this accelerated degeneration has direct clinical consequences, contributing to the 48.8% re-revision rate with repeat MD.

Teichtahl et al. (2017) conducted a community-based longitudinal study of 144 participants without symptoms, performing MRI at baseline and 2-year follow-up. They found 18 incident Modic lesions in 12.5% of participants, with Type 2 being most common (n = 15). Incident Type 2 lesions were associated with severe baseline disc degeneration at multiple levels (OR 11.7, 95% CI 1.5-88.5). While their asymptomatic cohort showed relatively benign progression, our surgical population demonstrated more aggressive patterns, particularly in persistent Type 1 patients where 51.6% showed any Pfirrmann grade progression versus 35.4% in persistent Type 2 patients.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study's strengths include comprehensive outcome collection with both patient-reported measures and objective endpoints, large sample size (n = 450), detailed longitudinal MC assessment capturing dynamic changes over time and extended follow-up to 5 years in a homogeneous population.

Several limitations merit consideration. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, though the magnitude of observed differences suggests robust findings. The absence of randomization prevents definitive causal inference, though ethical considerations would complicate randomizing patients with demonstrated progressive segmental degeneration to repeat discectomy. Our single-center design may limit generalizability, though it ensures consistent surgical technique through a 20 years’ time frame.

Notably, multivariate analysis revealed non-significant interaction terms, indicating that while fusion provided superior outcomes overall, we could not demonstrate statistically greater benefit for any specific MC pattern compared to others—likely reflecting limited sample size rather than absence of effect.

While we focused on MC type, recent work by Udby et al., 2022, 2025 suggests that incorporating MC extent (MCG classification) might provide additional prognostic refinement. Future studies should evaluate both type and grade to optimize patient stratification.

Importantly, despite our extended follow-up period, patient attrition resulted in progressively smaller sample sizes at later time points. While we maintained adequate numbers for analysis at 2 years (141 MD, 96 IF with paired data), the 5-year follow-up cohort became reduced, limiting our ability to detect meaningful differences between surgical approaches at this time point. This attrition pattern, common in longitudinal spine studies, prevented robust statistical comparisons of long-term outcomes beyond 2 years, particularly in the persistent Type 1 subgroup where the initial cohort was already limited (n = 64).

Future research should prospectively validate our findings in larger multicenter cohorts with strategies to minimize long-term attrition. Investigation of biological markers that might identify persistent Type 1 patients at highest risk for failure could inform targeted therapies. Additionally, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing immediate fusion versus stepwise management for persistent Type 1 changes would inform healthcare policy, particularly given the high re-revision rates (48.8%) observed with repeat MD in this phenotype.

Conclusions

Persistent Type 1 MC represent a distinct high-risk phenotype in ReLDH characterized by early recurrence, elevated re-revision rates, and accelerated segmental degeneration, compared to other MC types. The 3.7-fold reduction in re-revision rates and superior functional outcomes with IF provide compelling evidence, that warrant consideration in surgical decision-making.
