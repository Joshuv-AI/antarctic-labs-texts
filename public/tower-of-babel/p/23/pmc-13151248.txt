Overall survival of oral cancer from the population-based cancer registries of the Konkan area, Maharashtra, India
Authors: Suyash Agate, Suvarna Patil, Monika Sarade, Deepali Lokhande, Sandip Bhojane, Sachin Angane, Dipak Das, Shripad Banavali, Pankaj Chaturvedi, Atul Budukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral cancer (C03-C06) is a major public health challenge in India, particularly in rural regions where tobacco consumption is widespread. Despite the high burden, literature on survival data from rural populations is scarce. This study aims to assess overall survival and to identify prognostic factors associated with survival of oral cancer (OC) patients, using data from rural Population-Based Cancer Registries (PBCRs) in the Konkan area of Maharashtra, India.MethodsThis study was conducted on OC cases registered in the Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg PBCRs from 2017 to 2019. Survival was calculated from the date of diagnosis, with follow-up until December 31, 2023, or the date of death. Observed survival (OS) was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and relative survival (RS) was calculated using the Pohar Perme method. Sociodemographic and treatment-related prognostic factors were assessed using the Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsOf the 656 OC patients, 245 patients (37.3%) were alive, while 411 (62.7%) had died by the end of follow-up period. The 5-year age-standardized relative survival (ASRS) for all ages was 38.01%. In multivariate analysis, no statistically significant differences in survival were observed across socio-demographic variables except income. Patients in the high-income group had better survival compared with those in the lower-income groups (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.35–0.88).The highest 5-year survival was observed among patients treated with surgery combined with radiotherapy (57.75%), followed by surgery alone (55.57%). In contrast, patients treated with chemotherapy alone had poor survival (10.37%), reflecting presentation at advanced stages or management with palliative intent. Patients who completed the prescribed treatment protocol had significantly better survival compared with those who received no treatment after diagnosis (HR: 5.74; 95% CI: 4.09–8.07) and those who did not complete the prescribed treatment protocol (HR: 2.54; 95% CI: 1.83–3.51).ConclusionOral cancer survival in Konkan area is lower than that reported in urban regions, owing to socioeconomic disadvantage, late diagnosis, and low treatment uptake. Strengthening early detection, ensuring timely access to care, and raising awareness about tobacco cessation services and government health schemes can improve survival.

Background

Oral cancer (OC) is a significant public health challenge in India. In 2024, an incidence of 1.56 million new cases was estimated, of which 141,342 (9%) were oral cancers (C03-C06). 80.1% of OC cases (113,249) occurred in males and 28,093 (19.9%) in females. The crude incidence rate (CIR) of OC is 10.0 per 100,000 population (males: 15.6 per 100,000; females: 4.1 per 100,000) [1]. One in 63 males and one in 250 females has a lifetime risk (0–74 years) of developing OC. Mortality accounted for 72,347 deaths (55,491 males and 16,856 females), corresponding to a crude mortality rate (CMR) of 5.1 per 100,000 population (males: 7.6 per 100,000; females: 2.4 per 100,000) [1].

The large burden of OC is predominantly due to the widespread use of tobacco in various forms. Chewing products such as gutka, khaini, mawa, and betel quid are prevalent. Smoking habits extend beyond conventional cigarettes, including bidis (hand-rolled cigarettes) [2]. Poor oral health and a diet deficient in essential nutrients are also risk factors [2]. Additionally, alcohol consumption with tobacco acts synergistically, further increasing the risk of malignant transformation [3, 4].

The coastal districts of Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg in the Konkan area of Maharashtra state have a high prevalence of tobacco consumption. As per the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019-21), tobacco consumption among individuals aged 15 years and above ranges from 26.8% to 31.3% in males and 11.8% to 18.4% in females in these two districts [5].

Population-Based Cancer Registries (PBCRs) are vital tools for assessing cancer incidence, mortality, geographical distribution, and survival rates in a defined population [6, 7]. Tata Memorial Centre (TMC), Mumbai, and Bhaktshreshtha Kamalakarpant Laxman Walawalkar (BKLW) Hospital, Dervan, Ratnagiri, collaborated to establish rural PBCRs to systematically monitor the cancer burden, examine incidence and mortality trends, and identify cancer patterns in the rural districts of Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg, Maharashtra, India [8, 9].

The PBCRs in the Konkan area have systematically documented not only the incidence and mortality burden, but also the survival status of the cancer patients in this rural region [8, 9]. Literature on OC survival in rural India is limited, with available evidence largely derived from selected population-based cancer registries reporting geographic disparities in survival outcomes [10]. In this paper, we provide survival rates for OC cases registered by the PBCRs of the Konkan area from 2017 to 2019.

Methods

Study design and population

This population-based survival study included OC cases registered between 2017 and 2019 in the Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg districts of the Konkan area of Maharashtra, India.

The Ratnagiri PBCR covers 9 talukas (sub-divisions of a district) with 1,537 villages, and the Sindhudurg PBCR covers 8 talukas with 755 villages. Both registries also cover the urban areas within these talukas [8, 9]. Figure 1 highlights the coverage areas of the PBCRs. During the study period (2017–2019), these registries covered an estimated population of approximately 2.27 million. Data was provided by the respective PBCRs maintained under the TMC. PBCR registered cases with a confirmed place of residence in the districts for at least one year were included.

The PBCRs use an active surveillance approach, with trained field staff assigned to designated talukas. Field staff regularly visit multiple data sources, including government and private hospitals, radiology centres, primary health centres, birth and death registration offices, non-governmental organizations, and village panchayats to collect information on new cancer cases and cancer-related deaths, and also interact with Anganwadi workers, Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) workers, and local physicians to facilitate case identification and follow-up. Based on information provided by patients or their caregivers, field staff contact the treating centres to collect clinical details. If medical records are unavailable, information is obtained through verbal autopsy from patients or their caregivers. Verification of the place of residence is carried out through home visits or phone calls. The senior staff of Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) regularly review all cases through periodic field audits to ensure data quality.

Each case was coded using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD-O-3) [11]. The data entry is done into the CanReg5 software developed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [12].

Variables collected & statistical analysis

The data included socio-demographic variables such as age at diagnosis, sex, education, place of residence, marital status, occupation, religion, and income. The treatment-related variables included the date of diagnosis, method of diagnosis, primary site, histology, stage, treatment modalities, and treatment completion status. Patients who left the prescribed treatment midway or whose treatment was ongoing before December 31, 2023, are grouped under ‘treatment not completed’. Patients who completed the prescribed treatment protocol are classified as ‘treatment completed.’ Patients who were prescribed a treatment protocol but did not undergo the recommended treatment are classified as receiving ‘no treatment’ under treatment modalities.

Follow-up details, including the date of last follow-up, vital status, and date of death, were collected by the PBCR field staff. The stage at diagnosis was not available for all the cases, limiting stage-specific survival analysis. This limitation is primarily because the PBCRs collect data from multiple sources, and many hospitals do not routinely provide staging details.

The date of diagnosis was defined as the start point for calculating survival, and the endpoint was either December 31, 2023, or the date of death. Survival time was calculated in years. Observed survival (OS) was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method [13]. Life tables for rural Maharashtra, which represented the general population, were used to calculate expected survival, matched for age, sex, and calendar period [14–17]. Relative survival (RS) was calculated using the Pohar Perme method [18]. Age-standardized estimates for RS were produced to allow reliable comparisons between populations and subgroups [19]. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the independent effect of socio-demographic and treatment variables on survival outcomes [20]. STATA 17 software was used for all statistical analyses (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) [21].

Results

A total of 679 OC cases were registered in the PBCRs of the Konkan area between 2017 and 2019. We excluded 23 cases (3.4%) due to incomplete follow-up information or diagnosis based solely on Death Certificate Only (DCO). Thus, 656 cases were included in the survival analysis, as summarised in Fig. 2. Out of 656 cases, 439 (66.9%) were from Ratnagiri, and 217 (33.1%) were from Sindhudurg district. The median age at diagnosis was 58 years (Interquartile range 48–66 years). Most individuals were married, identified as Hindu, and resided in rural areas. Median follow-up time was 30 months (Interquartile Range: 9.5–60.0 months).

Educational attainment was generally low, with 156 (23.8%) being illiterate (individuals who cannot read and write) and 276 (42.1%) having only primary education. 44.2% (290) of the cases were farmers, and 31.1% (204) were housewives. More than half (59.1%) belonged to the low-income group, earning less than ₹5000 (US$56.3) per month.

Squamous cell carcinoma was the predominant histological type, and 617 (94.1%) cases were microscopically confirmed. Surgery, either alone or in combination with radiotherapy, was the most frequently reported treatment modality. 444 cases (67.7%) completed the treatment. 78 (11.9%) patients did not complete the prescribed treatment protocol, and 104 (15.8%) received no treatment after diagnosis. At the end of the follow-up, 245 (37.3%) patients were alive, and 411 (62.7%) had died. Detailed distribution of sociodemographic and treatment-related characteristics is provided in Table 1.

Income (p = 0.012), Treatment modality (p < 0.01), and treatment completion (p < 0.001) were significant prognostic factors as per the multivariate analysis. As shown in Table 3, survival was better among patients who were in the higher income group with a monthly income of more than ₹10,000 (US$112.6) (RS:50.00%), compared to those who were in the low-income group earning less than ₹ 5000 (US$56.3) (RS: 35.73%) (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.35–0.88; p = 0.012).

Regarding treatment, patients who underwent surgery alone (RS: 55.57%) or surgery combined with radiotherapy (RS: 57.75%) had better survival outcomes. Those who received chemotherapy alone demonstrated the poorest survival (RS: 10.37%) (HR = 3.42; 95% CI: 2.12–5.51; p < 0.001), and patients receiving combined radiotherapy plus chemotherapy had moderate survival (RS: 27.00%) (HR: 2.79; 95% CI: 1.77–4.40; p < 0.001) among those who received treatment. In comparison, only 8% of those who did not receive any treatment survived for 5 years after diagnosis (HR 5.74, 95% CI 4.09–8.07, p < 0.001).

Those who completed their prescribed treatment had significantly improved survival (RS: 49.27%) compared to those did not complete the prescribed treatment protocol (RS: 15.49%) (HR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.83–3.51; p < 0.001).

Discussion

The rural districts in the Konkan area have a high prevalence of tobacco use [5], which consequently results in a high OC burden. This population-based survival study offers valuable insights into the survival outcomes of OC in these districts. The analysis is based on data from two rural coastal PBCRs, which have consistently demonstrated the capacity to collect high-quality cancer patient data [8, 9, 22].

Population-based cancer survival is a vital measure of the availability and accessibility of cancer care programs and infrastructure in an area. In the present study, the five-year ASRS for OC was 38.01%, which is consistent with a pooled analysis of ten Indian PBCRs, that reported an ASRS of 39.3% [10]. This homogeneity indicates that survival rates remain consistently low across India, revealing broader structural issues. In many Asian countries, including India, effective cancer care is hampered by various structural and socio-economic challenges. These include poverty, low literacy levels, delayed diagnosis due to advanced stage at presentation, restricted access to healthcare facilities, and insufficient treatment infrastructure, all of which contribute to poor treatment outcomes [23]. The Konkan area is no exception and faces similar challenges.

Our study found that individuals residing in urban areas have better survival compared to those in rural areas. This is consistent with findings from a pooled analysis of 10 Indian PBCRs [10]. Rural residence does not represent a single risk factor, but it appears to play a multifaceted role in cancer outcome disparities [24]. Low public awareness, self-neglect, resulting in delayed presentation, inadequate awareness among primary physicians, insufficient infrastructure, incomplete treatment, and the preference for alternative systems of medicine are frequent challenges prevalent in rural areas [25, 26]. Furthermore, patients from rural areas frequently have to travel to urban centres to receive cancer care, and a previous study reports that those who travel more than 50 miles are more likely to be diagnosed in advanced stages, face challenges in treatment adherence, and have poor prognosis and quality of life [27]. However, survival in the Konkan area was comparatively better than in other rural registries. For instance, the Muzaffarpur and Barshi registries reported lower five-year survival rates for OC [28, 29]. This relative improvement in the Konkan area may be attributed to better access to tertiary cancer care facilities such as BKLW Hospital (Dervan), TMC (Mumbai), Goa Medical College, and Kolhapur Cancer Centre [8, 9].

Western countries like those in Europe and the USA consistently report high oral cancer survival outcomes, ranging between approximately 69%-75% [30, 31]. High-income Asian countries have also demonstrated comparatively better survival rates, with Israel, Japan, and the Republic of Korea reporting five-year survival rates of around 60% [29, 32]. Our results, however, are more in line with survival rates from middle-income countries such as China, Malaysia, and Thailand, where the five-year relative survival rate ranges from 27.4% to 39.3% [29].

Income level was a significant determinant of survival in our study, with patients from higher-income groups demonstrating better survival compared to those from lower-income groups (RS: 35.73% vs. RS: 50.00%). This disparity may be attributed to the high cost of cancer treatment in India, where the majority of healthcare is delivered through private providers, often without sufficient insurance coverage or social security [33]. Consequently, patients admitted to private hospitals face higher rates of catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) and impoverishment compared to those treated in public facilities [34]. The financial burden of cancer is not limited to direct medical and treatment expenses. Patients and their families also face significant direct non-medical costs, such as transportation, accommodation, and food during treatment, with considerable indirect costs, including loss of income due to absence from work and the impact on family members who may need to forgo their own occupational or household responsibilities [35]. Additionally, individuals from socio-economically deprived neighborhoods are more likely to get diagnosed with advanced-stage disease and experience poor outcomes [36]. The combined effect of treatment-related expenses and socio-economic disadvantage influences cancer survival, often leading to treatment discontinuation, delayed care, and compromised survival outcomes.

Treatment modality is another major determinant of survival in our study. Patients who underwent a combination of surgery and radiotherapy demonstrated the highest five-year survival (RS: 57.75%), followed by those who received surgery alone (RS: 55.57%). In contrast, those treated with radiotherapy alone and chemotherapy alone had lower survival rates of 31.34% and 10.37%, respectively, likely reflecting that these patients presented with advanced-stage disease or were treated with palliative intent. Similar patterns have been reported in a Mongolian study, where surgery alone yielded a five-year survival of approximately 57.3%, while chemotherapy as a single modality treatment offered only limited benefits, with survival close to 20% [37]. Another study further supported this pattern, showing that patients treated exclusively with chemotherapy or radiotherapy had significantly poorer survival compared to those who underwent only surgery [38].

Furthermore, in our study, 64.1% of patients who received chemotherapy as a sole treatment modality did not complete the prescribed treatment protocol. This high rate of non-completion is likely attributable to side effects from chemotherapy, which are commonly seen in OC patients. Fatigue, drowsiness, disturbed sleep, memory problems, and distress are among the symptoms that interfere with daily functioning and compromise treatment adherence [39]. Patients experiencing greater cancer-related symptom severity and interference are more likely to drop out of the treatment, resulting in poor survival outcomes [39]. Further, it is possible that the patients who did not undergo surgery were diagnosed at an advanced stage, that did not allow tumour resection, and that the intent of treatment might have been palliative. However, as this was a population-based study, the availability of detailed clinical information was limited.

Finally, treatment completion was strongly associated with improved survival. Patients who completed treatment had a five-year RS of 49.27%, compared to 08.10% among those who did not receive treatment and 15.49% among those who did not complete the prescribed treatment. Delays in treatment initiation and financial barriers are key contributors to treatment non-completion.

Government-funded health schemes, such as the Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY) and the Mahatma Jyotirao Phule Jan Arogya Yojana (MJPJAY), have been shown to improve treatment completion rates without leading to impoverishment [40]. Evidence suggests that enrollment in PM-JAY or similar state-sponsored programs substantially reduces CHE and lowers the risk of impoverishment during hospitalization [34]. Moreover, patients enrolled in AB-PMJAY demonstrate improved access to timely cancer care, with initiation rates rising from 33% among non-enrolled individuals to 90% among those enrolled [41]. However, despite eligibility, a proportion of patients do not avail these schemes. Although PM-JAY has become a familiar name among the population, awareness regarding specific scheme facets, such as entitled benefits, empanelled hospitals, and covered services, remains limited [42]. Additional operational barriers, including delays in receiving beneficiary cards and treatment initiation, continued out-of-pocket expenditure on drugs and diagnostic investigations, co-payment demands by healthcare providers, and inadequate training of healthcare personnel in empanelled hospitals, may further hinder utilization [42]. Gaps in communication and implementation within the health system may contribute to eligible patients remaining inadequately informed about scheme eligibility and benefits. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of government health schemes in promoting treatment completion, thereby improving treatment outcomes and enhancing cancer survival. It is important that Governmental health departments and hospitals raise awareness of the health scheme available in their area to improve treatment uptake.

This study utilized data from the PBCRs of Ratnagiri and Sindhudurg, which follow standardized protocols and provide high-quality, representative data [8, 9, 22]. The population-based design ensures the applicability of findings, particularly for rural populations that are often underrepresented in survival studies. A notable limitation was the lack of staging information for a significant proportion of cases, limiting the ability to conduct stage-specific survival analysis. This limitation reflects a common challenge faced by the PBCRs that collect data from multiple sources, and many hospitals do not routinely provide staging data.

These findings demonstrate the urgent need for an integrated approach to control OC in rural India. Community-based early detection programs led by trained healthcare personnel are vital, especially for high-risk populations such as tobacco and betel quid users. The Early Detection Program (EDP) implemented in Sangrur, Punjab, offers an effective and practical approach by combining community-based health education, active involvement of local leaders, health workers, and easy access to diagnosis and treatment. When compared to routine cases attending the hospital, this approach demonstrated the down-staging of common cancers and improved treatment completion rates [43]. Interim findings from our ongoing oral cancer screening trial conducted in selected talukas of Ratnagiri suggest a 5-year survival of more than 80% among early-stage OC patients (A.M Budukh, personal communication).

Furthermore, targeted awareness campaigns delivered in regional languages through primary healthcare centres, dental outpatient departments, and community-based platforms led by ASHA and Auxiliary Nurse Midwife (ANM) workers can help improve recognition of early OC symptoms and encourage timely health-seeking behaviour. During registration at the hospital, IEC material about schemes should be provided in the case file. Integration of tobacco cessation counselling and referral to the National Tobacco Quit Line Service (NTQLS) within routine outpatient services and community screening programs can further facilitate positive behavioural change [44]. Linking cancer screening and diagnosis services with onsite counselling and facilitation for enrolment in government-funded schemes such as MJPJAY and AB-PMJAY at public and empanelled private facilities can help reduce barriers to care, encourage timely initiation of treatment, and reduce treatment dropouts.

Conclusion

We report the first population-based survival study of oral cancer in rural coastal Maharashtra. Socioeconomic factors and treatment completion were identified as major determinants of survival. Those who completed the prescribed treatment protocol have significantly improved survival. The five-year age-standardized relative survival is low. Enhancing survival requires the implementation of comprehensive public health strategies that focus on early detection, especially among the high-risk rural populations, where tobacco and betel quid use are widespread. These strategies should include culturally tailored awareness campaigns, improved access to tobacco cessation services, and strengthened referral and treatment infrastructure. Strengthening healthcare provider capacity and increasing awareness of government health schemes can further support timely diagnosis and help reduce financial barriers to care. A collaborative approach that engages health systems, policymakers, and local communities is essential to improving survival rates in rural India.
