Beyond Nerve Size: The Role of Fascicular Architecture in Differentiating Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP) From Other Demyelinating Polyneuropathies
Authors: Liene D Silva, Rodrigo T Brisson, Josevania F Arruda, Marco Antonio S Lima, Marcos R De Freitas, Rita de Cássia L Fernandes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNerve ultrasound contributes to the evaluation of inflammatory polyneuropathies, providing diagnostic support when clinical and electrodiagnostic (EDX) findings overlap. This study aims to evaluate cross-sectional area (CSA), echogenicity, and fascicular structure by ultrasound in patients with demyelinating polyneuropathy and identify features to differentiate chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) from other conditions.MethodsFifteen patients meeting EDX criteria for demyelinating polyneuropathy were included. Bilateral median and ulnar nerves were assessed at five segments. CSA and architectural features-including fascicular pattern and echogenicity-were evaluated visually and quantitatively using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).ResultsThe sample included 11 patients with CIDP and four with other demyelinating neuropathies (anti-MAG, multifocal motor neuropathy, and HIV-related vasculitic neuropathy). All patients exhibited nerve enlargement, particularly in proximal segments (axilla and humerus), with no significant CSA differences between groups (p > 0.05). However, CIDP patients showed a significantly higher number of nerve segments with loss of fascicular architecture (median 7.7 nerve segments) compared to other neuropathies (median 4.0 nerve segments; p = 0.033). An inverse correlation was observed between echogenicity and loss of fascicular pattern.ConclusionsAlthough no significant differences in nerve CSA were found between the CIDP and non-CIDP groups, internal fascicular architectural changes provide potential clues for distinguishing these conditions. Assessing nerve microstructure, rather than size alone, may offer valuable diagnostic support in neuromuscular clinics.

Introduction

Over the past decade, high-resolution ultrasound (HRUS) has contributed to the diagnosis of inflammatory polyneuropathies by enabling objective and reliable detection of peripheral nerve and root enlargement. This imaging pattern has proven to be a highly sensitive indicator of inflammatory neuropathies [1]. Consequently, in 2021, the Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) officially recognized this finding as a supportive diagnostic criterion [2].

However, nerve enlargement alone still lacks specificity, and diagnostic challenges are common, particularly in the evaluation of demyelinating polyneuropathies. Numerous conditions, including hereditary neuropathies, POEMS (Polyneuropathy, Organomegaly, Endocrinopathy, Monoclonal protein (M-protein), and Skin changes) syndrome, amyloidosis, lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy, IgM monoclonal gammopathy associated with anti-MAG antibodies, and neurolymphomatosis, may present with a polyneuropathy that fulfills electrophysiological criteria for demyelination and demonstrate peripheral nerve enlargement on HRUS [3,4].

Thus, the investigation of potential biomarkers for specific diseases represents a valuable element, as delayed diagnosis can lead to undertreatment and potential permanent disability [5]. Several studies have investigated HRUS findings to identify sonographic patterns specific to certain diseases [6-8]. In chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), nerve enlargement typically occurs in the proximal segments of the median nerve (MN) and ulnar nerve (UN), such as the axilla, brachial plexus, and cervical roots [6]. In Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1 (CMT1), thickening is diffuse along the nerves, a pattern also observed in approximately 37% of CIDP patients. In contrast, diffuse nerve enlargement is uncommon in multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN) and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) [7]. Nonetheless, the specific role of internal fascicular architecture in improving the differentiation between these overlapping conditions remains insufficiently explored.

Further studies have described structural alterations such as changes in echogenicity and fascicular architecture, which correlate well with specific neuropathies. The analysis of fascicular enlargement patterns appears to facilitate the differentiation between neuropathies. In CMT1, diffuse fascicular enlargement is observed, whereas MMN shows regional and heterogeneous involvement, with enlarged fascicles adjacent to normal ones in the same segment [8].

Recently, ultra-high-frequency ultrasound (UHF-US) has been applied to assess and compare nerve microstructures among dysimmune neuropathies. Although this technique reveals similar global patterns of alteration, it provides superior visualization of internal nerve architecture compared to conventional HRUS [9].

Despite these advances, distinguishing between specific demyelinating neuropathies remains difficult, as clinical presentation, electrophysiological findings, and CSF analysis often overlap. Based on these considerations, we studied a case series of patients with electrophysiological evidence of demyelinating polyneuropathy, aiming to characterize peripheral nerve sonographic features and identify abnormalities associated with specific disease entities.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This observational, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Neuromuscular Disease Outpatient Clinic of the Clementino Fraga Filho University Hospital (HUCFF/UFRJ) between September 2022 and August 2023. The primary aim was to examine the distinctive nature of HRUS findings in CIDP compared to other acquired demyelinating conditions.

Due to the rarity of these specific neuroimmunological conditions, a convenient sample size of 15 patients meeting the 2021 European Academy of Neurology (EAN)/PNS diagnostic criteria was included. To explore morphological differences, patients were categorized into two distinct groups. The first group consisted of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of CIDP (n = 11). The second group (n = 4) comprising “other demyelinating neuropathies” was intentionally designed as a heterogeneous comparative cohort pooling specific mimics: anti-MAG neuropathy (n = 2), MMN (n = 1), and HIV-related vasculitic neuropathy (n = 1). This heterogeneous design replicates a challenging real-world clinical scenario.

Inclusion criteria were a progressive sensory-motor polyneuropathy identified during neurological evaluation and a prior electroneuromyography showing a demyelinating pattern. Final diagnoses were confirmed through longitudinal clinical follow-up, nerve biopsy, specific laboratory testing (e.g., anti-MAG antibodies), or treatment response. Patients with no confirmed final diagnosis or those who did not meet complete neurophysiological demyelinating criteria were excluded. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (CAAE: 52240421.8.0000.5257), and all participants provided written informed consent.

High-resolution nerve ultrasound (HRUS)

Patients were examined by a single examiner using an HD11XE ultrasound system (Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) equipped with a 5-12 MHz linear transducer. The protocol followed standardized parameters described in the current consensus literature [10]. The cross-sectional area (CSA) of the MN and UN was measured along the inner margin of the hyperechoic epineural rim using the continuous manual tracing technique.

For each nerve segment, three consecutive CSA measurements were obtained, and the mean was used for analysis. Both upper limbs were examined at five standardized sites (Figure 1). All participants underwent electrodiagnosis (EDX) of both upper and lower limbs, but for the purpose of comparison with HRUS findings, only the MN and UN were analyzed, as these nerves can be visualized continuously along their course with ultrasound.

A qualitative evaluation of fascicular architecture was performed through visual assessment of each nerve. Nerves were categorized according to the presence of a fascicular appearance, defined as the ability to identify the outer boundaries of individual fascicles. The humeral and axillary segments were specifically selected for this analysis because, as we previously noted, given their larger CSA values, the qualitative evaluation of fascicular organization was easier.

The echogenicity was quantitatively analyzed using a semi-automated method implemented in the open-source software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), as previously described [11,12] and depicted in Figure 2. The region of interest (ROI) of each HRUS image was processed to calculate the percentage of white pixels within the ROI, representing the proportion of echogenic tissue (higher values indicating greater echogenicity).

Statistical analysis

The sample was characterized by measures of central tendency (median) and spread (interquartile range (IQR), minimum, and maximum values). Statistical analyses and graphs were performed using SPSS (version 31, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared between CIDP patients and other demyelinating neuropathies using the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normal variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test as necessary. Associations between continuous non-normally distributed variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

CSA: cross-sectional area

In the overall analysis, Friedman’s test revealed a significant global difference in CSA values across the five analyzed segments, indicating that nerve dimensions vary significantly depending on the anatomical site. To determine the direction of these variations, post hoc Wilcoxon pairwise tests with Bonferroni correction were performed (10 comparisons, significance threshold set at p < 0.005). These comparisons demonstrated that the proximal segments exhibited the largest dimensions: the humeral segment of both nerves presented significantly higher CSA values than the wrist (MN: p < 0.001; UN: p < 0.002) and forearm (MN: p < 0.001; UN: p < 0.002). Additionally, the axillary segment of the UN exhibited higher CSA values compared to the wrist (p < 0.001). Fascicular architecture and quantitative echogenicity

Qualitative analysis of nerve echotexture revealed significant morphological disorganization, fascicular size heterogeneity, and loss of the typical fascicular pattern in CIDP patients (Figure 4). The median number of abnormal nerve segments per patient was significantly higher in the CIDP group (median: 8; IQR: 3; range: 0-12) than in the other demyelinating neuropathy group (median: 4; IQR: 3; range: 3-6). A non-parametric analysis (Mann-Whitney U test) confirmed a statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.033).

(D) Vasculitic neuropathy (CSA 11 mm²), showing mild nerve enlargement and few abnormalities; (E) anti-MAG neuropathy (CSA 13 mm²), with modest CSA increase and relatively preserved fascicles; (F) multifocal motor neuropathy (CSA 15 mm²), showing limited enlargement and identifiable fascicular architecture. In (D-F), the lesser degree of enlargement and abnormalities allows partial preservation of fascicular architecture, unlike in CIDP.

Quantitative analysis via ImageJ revealed that nerves with "lost" fascicular architecture consistently showed lower echogenicity (lower percentage of white pixels) compared to nerves with preserved architecture. An inverse correlation was observed between the mean gray value and the visual loss of fascicular pattern (Spearman’s rho = -0.72; p < 0.01), suggesting that the loss of the typical "honeycomb" appearance is quantitatively linked to a more hypoechoic nerve profile.

Discussion

The present study reinforces the role of HRUS as a tool in the diagnostic workup of demyelinating polyneuropathies. While nerve enlargement is a well-established finding in CIDP [13], our results suggest that the internal morphology of the nerve-specifically the fascicular architecture-may aid in differentiating conditions that frequently overlap in clinical practice. The main HRUS alteration found in both CIDP and other demyelinating neuropathy patients was nerve enlargement, particularly in proximal segments. This finding appears to exhibit specificity for chronic inflammatory neuropathies and could help differentiate them from axonal neuropathies and motor neuron disease, as previously noted by other authors. Nonetheless, an isolated higher CSA is not specific to any single demyelinating chronic neuropathy [14,15].

This study characterized regional changes in nerve caliber beyond absolute localized values. Our data demonstrate consistent mean variations along the limb, supporting a proximal-to-distal CSA gradient. Conversely, the 95% CI for Cohen’s d exhibited a wider range. This broader interval for the effect size reflects the expected mathematical power limitations inherent to smaller sample sizes, where individual biological fluctuations heavily influence standardized metrics. While the physical magnitude of the nerve enlargement is documented by the raw metrics, the precision of the standardized effect size is expected to stabilize in larger confirmatory cohorts.

In CIDP, the pathologic changes underlying nerve enlargement include increased intraneural blood flow, edema, onion-bulb formations, and Schwann-cell proliferation resulting from repeated cycles of demyelination and remyelination. The accumulation of intraneural proteinaceous material secondary to nerve degeneration also contributes to nerve swelling [16]. Previous studies have identified characteristic HRUS morphological abnormalities in CIDP and proposed three classes of sonographic nerve changes, each correlating with symptom duration [17]. Class 1 is generally associated with symptoms lasting no more than 5-6 years, Class 2 with 3-10 years, and Class 3 with more than 7 years of disease [17]. These observations suggest a relationship between disease duration, the severity of pathologic nerve injury, and the sonographic patterns detected on HRUS.

Furthermore, the assessment of fascicle size using HRUS has previously been shown to help differentiate neuropathies [8]. Studies have demonstrated that patients with CMT1 typically exhibit diffuse fascicular enlargement, whereas MMN is characterized by focal fascicular enlargement adjacent to normal segments and a predominance of upper-limb involvement [8]. Although fascicle size was not quantitatively measured in our study, we observed that CIDP nerves display more pronounced morphological alterations on rapid visual inspection, particularly a greater disruption of the typical fascicular pattern when compared with other demyelinating neuropathies. In situations where electrophysiological findings overlap, this qualitative loss of fascicular architecture may therefore aid in distinguishing CIDP from other conditions.

The quantitative analysis using ImageJ provided an objective correlate to our visual observations. The inverse correlation between echogenicity and the loss of fascicular pattern confirms that as the nerve loses its internal structure, it becomes progressively more hypoechoic [12,13]. This objective measurement helps to overcome the subjectivity of visual assessment and could serve as a parameter for monitoring disease activity in future studies [14].

Unlike CIDP, vasculitic neuropathy is not primarily characterized by inflammatory processes that lead to significant nerve hypertrophy [18]. Instead, findings such as multifocal fiber loss, perineural thickening, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, neovascularization, active Wallerian degeneration, and immune deposits within epineural vessels have been described [18]. In anti-MAG neuropathy, sural nerve biopsy typically shows loss of myelinated fibers, thinning of myelin sheaths, and variable degrees of widely spaced myelin lamellae [19]. Although both conditions may exhibit inflammatory changes capable of producing demyelinating features on EDX and mild nerve enlargement on ultrasound, our observations suggest that the analysis of fascicular architecture and echogenicity can provide crucial support in distinguishing them from CIDP.

Regarding clinical correlations, the absence of a relationship between disease duration and nerve enlargement suggests that nerve hypertrophy may occur early in the disease process and persist even in chronic phases. This aligns with the concept that sonographic changes may reflect both active inflammation and permanent structural remodeling of the nerve.

Study limitations

Our study has some limitations, including the small sample size, which reflects the rarity of these conditions and the single-center nature of the study. Additionally, the qualitative assessment of fascicular architecture, although standardized, remains somewhat operator-dependent, highlighting the importance of developing more automated quantitative tools like the one used in our ImageJ analysis.

We acknowledge the biological and pathological heterogeneity within the non-CIDP group as a limitation of this study. Each of these disorders may possess distinct ultrasound signatures. However, our primary focus was to identify robust ultrasound features that remain specific to CIDP even when compared to a diverse pool of demyelinating conditions, reflecting the diagnostic challenges faced in clinical neurology.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that nerve enlargement (CSA) is a hallmark of demyelinating polyneuropathies, but it lacks the specificity required to differentiate CIDP from its clinical mimics. Beyond CSA measures, the observation of nerve architecture patterns-specifically the loss of fascicular architecture and changes in echogenicity-serves as a more discriminative diagnostic marker. Ultimately, this simple, fast, and cost-effective diagnostic method may thus contribute to improving diagnostic accuracy in situations where electrophysiological and clinical data overlap.
