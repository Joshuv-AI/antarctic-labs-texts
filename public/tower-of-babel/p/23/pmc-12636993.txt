Genomic Profiling of Intraocular Leiomyomas Reveals Recurrent Copy Number Alterations
Authors: Vivian Tang, Yubai Chou, Cuyan Demirkesen, Michele M. Bloomer, Joseph Brooks Crawford, Ahmet M. Sarici, Carol Shields, Ralph C. Eagle, Codrin E. Iacob, Walter Patrick Devine, Tatyana Milman, Melike Pekmezci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLeiomyomas are benign smooth muscle tumors that commonly present in the uterus, soft tissue, skin, and gastrointestinal tract but in rare cases can also arise within the eye. Notably, intraocular leiomyomas often show slightly different histopathologic and immunohistochemical features, referred to as mesectodermal morphology, given their presumed neural crest origin. Genetic and cytogenetic alterations of intraocular leiomyomas, as well as their association with various clinical and histopathologic features, have not been previously studied.MethodsWe identified eight patients diagnosed with intraocular leiomyoma and performed targeted next-generation sequencing, whole transcriptome RNA sequencing, and chromosomal copy number analysis on those with sufficient residual tissue.ResultsCopy number analysis demonstrated multiple, recurrent whole-chromosome losses (including losses of 1, 2, 3, 10, 15q, 22q) suggestive of near haploidization of the genome. DNA sequencing did not reveal any pathogenic single-nucleotide variants, deep deletions, amplifications, or structural rearrangements. Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing performed in a subset of cases did not show any pathogenic or likely pathogenic gene fusions. Tumors with mesenchymal versus mesectodermal morphology and those with or without histologically worrisome features did not show any differences in their copy number profile.ConclusionsGenetic and cytogenetic features of intraocular leiomyomas are different from the leiomyomas of uterine and deep soft tissues, as well as from uveal melanocytic tumors.Translational RelevanceRecurrent whole-chromosome losses in the absence of mutations or fusions are common among intraocular leiomyomas and may aid in the diagnosis.

Introduction

Leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle tumors most commonly encountered in the uterus, soft tissue, and gastrointestinal tract but very rarely present within the eye. Approximately 80 cases have been reported in the literature, and from these studies, intraocular leiomyomas have been observed to arise in the uveal tract from the smooth muscle of the ciliary body, the dilator and sphincter papillae muscles of the iris, and the smooth muscle of blood vessels in the choroid.1 Intraocular leiomyomas typically present as a slow-growing mass in the anterior uvea, associated with scleral thinning and ectasia, and, much less frequently, as an indolent mass in the choroid associated with retinal exudation and detachment. There is a slight predominance among females (approximately 3:2 male-to-female ratio). These tumors can present throughout life with a median age at diagnosis of 30.5 years (mean age at presentation 35.8 ± 17.4 years; range, 8–80 years).2 Importantly, they can be challenging to recognize clinically due to similarities with the more common uveal melanoma on clinical examination and ancillary imaging studies.2 Particularly in tumors with locally destructive growth, this often results in enucleation. Although the nature of enucleation precludes evaluation for local recurrence in certain cases, no cases of metastatic intraocular leiomyomas have been reported to date.

Histologically, the expression of smooth muscle markers usually informs the correct diagnosis of intraocular leiomyoma, although the presence of atypical features can make diagnosis less straightforward. In leiomyosarcoma elsewhere in the body, cytologic atypia, necrosis, mitotic activity, and invasion are conventionally accepted diagnostic features of malignancy, and there are reports of intraocular leiomyosarcoma in the literature using similar criteria.3,4 However, no established criteria for malignancy exist for intraocular leiomyoma. Furthermore, among the 80 tumors studied in recent series, there is no molecular characterization performed to date, which could refine our ability to accurately diagnose and, potentially, grade and prognosticate these tumors. To this end, we performed targeted next-generation DNA and whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing in a cohort of intraocular leiomyomas to determine whether any recurrent molecular alterations are seen across intraocular leiomyomas, whether any molecular signatures distinguish intraocular leiomyomas with worrisome features, and whether intraocular leiomyomas share any alterations in common with leiomyomas of other sites. We will also discuss clinical, histologic, and molecular features of an unusually large leiomyoma with worrisome histologic features (case 0) in more detail and compare with other cases.

Methods

This study was conducted as a retrospective study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, under the approval of the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) and Wills Eye Hospital (WEH) Institutional Review Boards. We searched the pathology archives of UCSF, WEH, and New York Eye and Ear Infirmary of Mount Sinai between 1994 and 2023 for intraocular leiomyomas diagnosed primarily or in consultation. Case 1 was previously reported in Tomar et al.2 Three pathologists (VT, TM, MP) reviewed all cases with available pathology material to reach a consensus diagnosis. Tumors were classified as mesenchymal (i.e., conventional), mesectodermal (i.e., neuroglial), or mixed based on morphologic features (Figs. 1A, 1B). Each tumor was assessed for the presence and extent of cytologic atypia, mitotic activity, necrosis, and invasive growth. Tumor epicenter was determined by the extent of involvement of the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. Involvement of adjacent structures (trabecular meshwork, sclera, etc.) was noted when present, Tumors with sufficient tissue for molecular analysis were included in the study. Clinical data, including tumor location, ultrasonographic features, surgical procedure, and follow-up, were obtained from medical records, and macroscopic features were obtained from pathology reports. Ultrasound and macroscopic measurements were recorded in millimeters and reported as median (range) and/or mean ± standard deviation (SD). Results of the immunohistochemistry performed at the time of original diagnosis were obtained from pathology reports. Additional immunohistochemistry for CD56 was performed in five cases, using routine techniques in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified clinical laboratory.

Targeted Next-Generation Sequencing

Tumor tissue (tumor proportion ranging from 85% to 95%) was selectively scraped from 10 to 18 unstained slides (cases 1–7) or punched from a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded block using a 2-mm biopsy punch (cat. 33-31-P/25; Integra Miltex Instruments, Princeton, NJ, USA) for case 0. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), yielding 8 to 120 ng/µL DNA. Two cases yielded less than 1 ng/µL DNA and are excluded from the study. Targeted next-generation sequencing was performed using the UCSF500 Cancer Panel as previously described.5 Capture-based next-generation DNA sequencing was performed using an assay that targets all coding exons of 529 cancer-related genes, select introns, and upstream regulatory regions of 47 genes to enable detection of structural variants, including gene fusions, and DNA segments at regular intervals along each chromosome to enable genome-wide copy number and zygosity analysis, with a total sequencing footprint of 1.5 Mb (see Supplementary Table S1 for list of genes targeted by the assay). Multiplex library preparation was performed using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's specifications. Hybrid capture of pooled libraries was performed using a custom oligonucleotide library (Nimblegen SeqCap EZ Choice). Captured libraries were sequenced as paired-end reads on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequence reads were mapped to the reference human genome build GRCh37 (hg19) using the Burrows–Wheeler aligner. Recalibration and deduplication of reads were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit. Coverage and sequencing statistics were determined using Picard CalculateHsMetrics and Picard CollectInsertSizeMetrics. Single-nucleotide variant and small insertion/deletion mutation calling was performed with FreeBayes, Unified Genotyper, and Pindel. Large insertion/deletion and structural alteration calling were performed with Delly. Variant annotation was performed with Annovar. Single-nucleotide variants, insertions/deletions, and structural variants were visualized and verified using Integrative Genome Viewer. Genome-wide copy number and zygosity analysis was performed by CNVkit and visualized using Nexus Copy Number (BioDiscovery, Hawthorne, CA, USA). In addition to gene-specific probes, a comprehensive custom set of baits spanning the entire genome with an average of 0.3 Mb distance was included in the capture, allowing for copy number and zygosity calls throughout the genome. According to the test performance data generated during initial clinical validation, for samples with at least 25% lesional cells, the sensitivity for detection of copy number changes is 100%, and the sensitivity and specificity for single-nucleotide variants and small indels (≤40 bp) are >99% and >98%, respectively, with mean targeted sequencing coverage of >500×, which equates to approximately 200 reads for both major and minor alleles.

Whole-Transcriptome RNA Sequencing

Tumor tissue was selectively scraped from unstained slides or punched from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks using biopsy punches (cat. 33-31-P/25; Integra Miltex Instruments). Total RNA was extracted using the truXTRAC FFPE Total NA Auto 96 Manual Kit (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA), and the quantity and quality of total RNA were assessed via Qubit and BioAnalyzer, respectively. Library preparation was performed using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (Roche) according to the manufacturer's specifications using 10 to 500 ng (optimally at least 250 ng) of total RNA. Libraries were pooled (six to nine libraries per pool) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 for a target of 50 million uniquely mapped reads. Sequence reads were mapped to the reference human genome build GRCh37 (hg19), and fusion variant calling was performed using a custom pipeline, including STAR-Fusion, Pizzly, Arriba, CICERO, EricScript, and FusionCatcher. All predicted fusions were manually reviewed by a board-certified molecular pathologist (WPD).

Patient Cohort Demographics and Clinical Data

We identified eight patients with available pathologic material, of whom four (50%) were female. Mean age at presentation was 42 ± 18.3 years, and median age was 38.5 years (range, 16–70). Visual acuity ranged from no light perception (in case 0) to 20/20. The right eye was affected in 6 (75%) patients. Four tumors (50%) were localized to the temporal, superotemporal, or superior quadrants; two (25%) were in the inferior or inferior-nasal quadrant; one (case 0, 12.5%) filled the entire globe; and specific location information was not available for one case. The exact epicenter of case 0 is difficult to discern due to its size, and initially, the possibility of a retinoblastoma was considered. All other tumors had a presumed epicenter in the ciliary body. B-scan ultrasound demonstrated a dome-shaped or round tumor with sonographic measurements ranging from 10 to 18 mm (median, 13 mm; mean ± SD, 13.6 ± 18.3 mm) in the greatest dimension for all cases except case 0, which filled the globe. Echogenicity varied from low internal reflectivity in two cases (25%), low to medium internal reflectivity in one (12.5%), medium internal reflectivity in four (50%), and was heterogeneous with central hypodense and focal hyperdense areas in case 0 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Case 0 additionally underwent magnetic resonance imaging, which showed heterogeneously hypo- and hyperintense intraocular mass in both T1- and T2-weighted images with near-diffuse contrast enhancement (Supplementary Figs. 1B, 1C). In this cohort, the tumors under 13 mm in the greatest sonographic dimension underwent partial lamellar scleroiridocyclectomy (4/8, 50%) with additional vitrectomy in case 2, and the remainder underwent enucleation. Follow-up ranged from 6 to 44 months, during which all eight patients (100%) were alive with no evidence of recurrence.

Macroscopic Features

On macroscopic examination, the mean basal diameter was 14.25 ± 8.15 mm (median, 10 mm; range, 9–32). Of five tumors that were transilluminated, two (40%) had a shadow, two (40%) had no shadow, and one (20%) had a light shadow, and in case 0, the globe did not transilluminate.

Histopathology

On microscopic evaluation, tumors were in the suprauveal space adjacent to the sclera in three cases and involved the uveal stroma in three cases. The small tumor in case 4 was entirely shelled out, suggestive of suprauveal location. Tumor in case 0 filled the entire globe (Fig. 3A), making the precise localization challenging, but the presence of scant choroid between the tumor and the sclera (Fig. 3B) and a thin layer of choriocapillaris between the tumor and retinal pigmented epithelium on the posterior aspect (Fig. 3C) raised the possibility of choroidal origin.

Upon masked review of the morphology, two tumors (25%) were predominantly spindled, with fascicular arrangements of elongated cells and blunt-ended nuclei, similar to features seen in soft tissue leiomyomas and considered to be of the conventional/mesenchymal type. Three tumors (32.5%), including case 0, demonstrated more neural or glial cytomorphology with round to ovoid nuclei embedded in a delicate, fibrillary matrix, considered to be of the mesectodermal type (Fig. 3E). The remaining three tumors (32.5%) contained a mixture of both morphologies. There was no obvious correlation between the morphologic pattern and the suprauveal or stromal location, or involvement of the ciliary body, choroid, or iris.

Atypical histologic features were defined as cytologic atypia, tumor-type necrosis, and any mitotic activity (>1 mitotic figure per 40 high-power fields). Although most tumors demonstrated some degree of cytologic atypia (at least rare atypical cells in 5/8, 62.5%), mitotic activity was identified in only two cases (25%) and necrosis in one case (12.5%). Case 0 was the only tumor with all three atypical features (Figs. 3D, 3F, 3G).

Immunohistochemistry

As previously reported in intraocular leiomyomas,2 tumors were positive for smooth muscle markers (⍺-smooth muscle actin [8/8, 100%], muscle-specific actin [MSA] [2/2, 100%], calponin [2/2, 100%], and caldesmon [1/1, 100%]); desmin was positive in one case and negative on another (1/2, 50%). Of neuroglial markers, only CD56 was consistently positive (5/5, 100%); S100 was weakly positive in two cases (2/6, 33%), and GFAP was negative (0/2, 0%). Leu7, a Schwannian marker, was negative (0/3, 0%). Melanocytic markers were uniformly negative when tested (HMB45 [0/5, 0%], MelanA [0/3, 0%], MITF [0/2, 0%]). Ki-67 labeling index was 2% or less among the cases stained (4/4). SMA and CD56 staining did not show variation among tumors with mesenchymal, mesectodermal, or mixed morphology. Case 0 showed strong staining with SMA, caldesmon, desmin, and CD56; showed weak wispy staining with S100 (Fig. 3H); and was negative for SOX10, MelanA, AE1/AE3, EMA, myogenin, GFAP, synaptophysin, and NSE. Ki-67 labeling index was 2% suggestive of low proliferative activity.

Molecular Characteristics

Two tumors (cases 4 and 5) were submitted for DNA extraction but did not yield sufficient DNA after library preparation. Six tumors with sufficient DNA underwent targeted next-generation sequencing, which showed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations or structural rearrangements despite adequate sequencing coverage and high tumor content (>85% for all cases). Copy number analysis did not show any high-level amplifications or focal deep deletions; however, there were recurrent copy number losses involving multiple whole chromosomes in all six tumors (Fig. 4). Tumors showed loss of 7 to 18 chromosomes (of 22 autosomes), with a median of 9 and a mean of 10.5 chromosomes. The most common chromosomal losses included chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 10, 15q, and 22q, identified in all tumors. Chromosomes 13q and 21q, as well as chromosomes 6 and 9, were additionally lost in a subset of cases (4/6, 66.7%, and 2/6, 33.3%, respectively). None of the tumors showed losses of chromosome 4, 8, or 17, and only case 6 showed copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity involving chromosome 17. Of note, case 0 with atypical histologic features did not have unique copy number variations; it showed 12 whole chromosome losses, but these were also seen in other tumors without atypical features.

Four cases with additional material (cases 0, 1, 3, and 6) underwent whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing and did not show any pathogenic or likely pathogenic structural rearrangements.

Case 0 additionally underwent genome-wide methylation analysis at the National Cancer Institute (NCI)/National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA) after DNA extraction and bisulfite conversion (EZ DNA Methylation kit; Zymo Research, Tustin, CA, USA) using the Infinium MethylationEPIC kit (Illumina), and it did not match any of the known central nervous system (CNS) tumors or soft tissue sarcomas, including leiomyosarcoma, in the existing classifier databases, including 11b6 and 12b6 of the DKFZ CNS Classifier (Heidelberg, Germany) and the NCI/Bethesda Classifier (Bethesda, MD, USA).

Discussion

Soft tissue and uterine smooth muscle tumors have established histopathologic criteria, which allow separation of these neoplasms into prognostically and therapeutically relevant categories, such as benign (leiomyoma), borderline (smooth muscle tumor of uncertain malignant potential, mitotically active leiomyoma), and malignant (leiomyosarcoma). While there are no existing histopathologic criteria for malignancy in intraocular smooth muscle tumors, some of these neoplasms demonstrate histologically worrisome features, which may make the diagnosis and management decisions less straightforward. Furthermore, no genetic or cytogenetic data on intraocular leiomyomas have been described in the literature thus far. In this study, we aimed to describe the genetic and cytogenetic features of intraocular leiomyomas, as well as assess whether there were any biologic differences correlating with the different tumor locations and histopathologic features.

Demographic features of the eight patients in our series are comparable with the literature reporting a median age of 30.5 years (range, 8–80) with a 63.7% female ratio.2 In our series, right-to-left eye ratio was 3:1, but no predilection for quadrant was observed. Similar to the literature, the ciliary body was the most common presumed epicenter for the intraocular leiomyomas in this study; however, our cohort was enriched for choroidal involvement and larger tumor size, given the requirement for sufficient residual tissue for molecular analysis. In our limited series, approximately half of the cases, specifically those less than 13 mm in the greatest dimension on ultrasound, underwent local excision with partial lamellar scleroiridocyclectomy. With the caveat that the remaining half of our cohort underwent enucleation, there was no evidence of recurrence, metastasis, or otherwise aggressive clinical behavior on follow-up, which is similar to the previously reported cases in the literature.2

Likewise, a spectrum of histopathologic features, including some worrisome for malignancy, was observed within our cohort. Cytologic atypia, beyond rare atypical cells, was seen in three (37.5%) cases, mitotic activity of more than a single mitotic figure per 40 high-power fields in two (25%), and tumor type necrosis in one (12.5%). Only case 0 demonstrated all three of these atypical features and underwent sequencing. Neural or glial morphology, referred as mesectodermal, is also a feature seemingly unique to intraocular leiomyomas and presumed to be attributed to the neural crest origin of the ciliary body from which some of these tumors arise.2 Six cases (75%) in this study demonstrated at least partial mesectodermal morphology with components of the fibrillar matrix and round ovoid glia-like cytology. However, this morphologic pattern did not correlate with ciliary body, iris or choroid location, suprauveal or stromal location, or CD56/S100 positivity.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to report recurrent copy number variation as a recurrent genetic alteration among intraocular leiomyomas. All cases in our cohort had fewer than 40 chromosomes, with monosomy of 7 to 12 autosomes, consistent with hypodiploidy, which is suggestive of near-haploidization of the genome. Losses of chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 10, 15q, and 22q were universally detected among the six tumors sequenced, with chromosome 6, 9, 13q, and 21q losses being also common in this cohort. Importantly, there were no chromosome losses that were unique to case 0 with worrisome histologic features, nor was there a difference in tumors with mesectodermal versus mesenchymal morphology.

Genome haploidization is not a common genetic phenomenon in tumors, but near-haploidization with or without subsequent genome doubling (endoreduplication) has been described in other tumors, such as giant cell glioblastoma,6 diffuse pleural mesothelioma,7 inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumor,8 and oncocytic adrenocortical, thyroid, and parathyroid tumors.9 Inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumors encompass cases previously diagnosed as “inflammatory leiomyosarcoma,” which were renamed, given that their immunophenotype conformed more to skeletal than smooth muscle differentiation.8 In addition, near-haploidization or significant hypodipolidy has been reported in subsets of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors10 and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia11 as a finding associated with worse outcomes.

Uterine leiomyomas may harbor mutations in MED12 and FH genes, as well as rearrangements involving HMGA2 and HMGA1 genes, among other less frequent alterations.12 Leiomyomas of the deep soft tissues also share some of these alterations with uterine leiomyomas.13 Uterine leiomyosarcomas often show more complex copy number profiles with frequent mutations in TP53, ATRX, PTEN, and RB1, although their presence currently is not considered diagnostic of sarcoma.14,15 Within the spectrum from benign to malignant, some of the uterine “leiomyomas with bizarre nuclei” and “smooth muscle tumor of uncertain malignant potential” may have either simple or complex genomic profiles and may also harbor mutations in TP53 and RB1.16 Elsewhere in the body, leiomyosarcomas, including soft tissue leiomyosarcomas, are known to show widespread copy number changes, including chromothripsis and whole-genome duplication, and harbor near-universal TP53 and RB1 alterations in addition to frequent ATRX alterations.14 All exons of TP53, RB1, ATRX, PTEN, MED12, FH, and HMGA2 are included in our panel, and none showed mutations in our cohort, suggesting that intraocular leiomyomas are genetically distinct from uterine and soft tissue neoplasms.

Primary intracranial smooth muscle neoplasms are extremely rare and most frequently include Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)–associated smooth muscle tumors, usually in the setting of HIV-associated immunosuppression.17 While the genetic features of primary intracranial smooth muscle tumors are not specifically studied, EBV-associated smooth muscle tumors were shown to have limited copy number changes similar to soft tissue leiomyomas; therefore, they are different from intraocular leiomyomas.18

Also, it is important to note that intraocular leiomyomas do not harbor mutations associated with uveal nevi, melanocytoma, and melanoma, including GNAQ, GNA11, CYSLTR2, PLCB4, BAP1, SF3B1, and EIF1AX, all of which are covered by this targeted assay.5,19,20 While uveal melanomas may harbor well-characterized somatic copy number alterations, including losses of 1p, 3, and 6q and gains of 6p and 8q, copy number variants are usually fewer, and most changes are at the chromosome arm level.19,20 In cases with ambiguous clinical features between intraocular leiomyoma and melanoma, molecular changes evaluated on fine-needle aspiration biopsy specimens or potentially cell-free DNA applications using aqueous humor can further aid in the diagnosis.

In summary, intraocular leiomyomas are defined by frequent whole-chromosome losses, leading to near-haploidization of the genome, without recurrent single-nucleotide variants, structural rearrangements, deep deletions, or high-level amplifications as evaluated on our targeted next-generation sequencing assay of 529 known cancer genes. Although reflex whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing and genome-wide methylation analysis were selectively pursued to identify potential structural rearrangements and epigenetic modifications, the presence of underlying novel driver mutations or alterations not targeted by the next-generation sequencing assay cannot be entirely ruled out. Nevertheless, there is no correlation between the histologic features and the number of chromosomal losses or the specific chromosomes that are deleted. None of the cases in this cohort show evidence of recurrence or metastasis, arguing against malignancy.
