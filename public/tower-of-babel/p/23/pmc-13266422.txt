Axon Diameter Mapping in the Living Human Brain with Ultra‐High‐Gradient Diffusion MRI at 500 mT/m Gradient Strength
Authors: Yixin Ma, Laleh Eskandarian, Kwok‐Shing Chan, Hansol Lee, Gabriel Ramos‐Llordén, Aneri Bhatt, Julianna Gerold, Mirsad Mahmutovic, Boris Keil, Hong‐Hsi Lee, Susie Y Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPushing the resolution limit of axon diameter mapping in the living human brain requires higher gradient strength than is currently available on most clinical MRI systems. The noninvasive quantification of axon diameter not only enables the exploration of axonal damage in a wide range of neurological disorders but also provides fundamental insights into axonal organization and conduction patterns of white matter tracts. The goal of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity of axon diameter mapping to small diameter axons using the next‐generation Connectome MRI scanner (Connectome 2.0), which features a maximum gradient strength of 500 mT/m and slew rate of 600 T/m/s, compared to the original Connectome 1.0 scanner with 300 mT/m gradient strength. We applied the AxCaliber‐SMT model to diffusion MRI data from 40 healthy adults, comprising 20 participants scanned on Connectome 1.0 before it was decommissioned and a separate cohort of 20 age‐ and sex‐matched participants scanned on Connectome 2.0. Our findings are based on group‐level comparisons between these two cohorts. The theoretical minimum detectable axon diameter was 2.5 μm on Connectome 2.0 and 3.6 μm on Connectome 1.0. The MR‐estimated axon diameter in the corticospinal tract on the Connectome 2.0 scanner was 2.66 ± 0.54 μm, significantly lower than 3.35 ± 1.00 μm on Connectome 1.0 (Welch's t‐test: p = 0.0110). Comparison was performed across independently acquired datasets from age‐ and sex‐matched individuals on different scanners; therefore, the observed group differences should be interpreted as strong and supportive, though not strictly causal, evidence of the proposed scanner capability. Furthermore, we examined 7 healthy adults for scan‐rescan repeatability and demonstrated that the voxel‐wise mean absolute difference in axon diameter estimates between scan and rescan decreased to 0.29 μm on the Connectome 2.0 (vs. 0.65 μm on the Connectome 1.0), indicating improved repeatability of the axon diameter estimates. These improvements are enabled not only by the higher gradient strength of Connectome 2.0, but also by the associated reduction in echo time and increase in SNR, which together enhance sensitivity to restricted diffusion and improve parameter reliability. Our findings highlight the importance of stronger and faster gradients for accurate and robust mapping of the axonal microstructure in the human brain.

Introduction

Axons are the fundamental unit of the brain's long‐range wiring, with diameter being the key biophysical determinant of conduction velocity, synchronizing communication across distributed neural circuits (Drakesmith et al. 2019; Horowitz et al. 2015; Hursh 1939; Mancini et al. 2021; Ritchie 1982; Skoven et al. 2023; Waxman 1980, 2006). Axon diameter is unevenly distributed across the white matter network, mirroring the speed demands of different pathways. Histological studies have shown that large‐diameter axons concentrate in fast motor pathways such as the corticospinal tract (Firmin et al. 2014; Saliani et al. 2017; Tomasi et al. 2012), while interhemispheric fibers in the corpus callosum contain mostly sub‐micron fibers (Aboitiz et al. 1992b, 1992a). Axon diameter changes systematically across the lifespan and in disease. Ultra‐high gradient diffusion MRI shows that apparent axon diameter increases during late childhood and puberty, paralleling hormone‐driven white‐matter growth (Genc et al. 2023). From mid‐adulthood onward, selective loss of thin fibers shifts the apparent diameter distribution toward larger values—a hypertrophic pattern first quantified stereologically (Marner et al. 2003) and later reproduced in vivo with MRI (Fan et al. 2019). Pathology follows similar trends: post‐mortem multiple‐sclerosis tissue reveals substantial axon loss in normal‐appearing white matter (DeLuca et al. 2004; Ganter et al. 1999), and high‐gradient MRI detects larger apparent axon diameters in lesions and the corpus callosum, correlating with clinical disability (Huang et al. 2019, 2016). These converging findings demonstrate the promise of axon diameter as a sensitive biomarker of white‐matter integrity across development, aging, and neurological disease.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) enables noninvasive evaluation of tissue microstructure by sensitizing MR signals to the Brownian motion of water molecules (Basser et al. 1994; Jensen et al. 2005; Stejskal and Tanner 1965). Early multi‐compartment models (Assaf and Basser 2005) moved beyond diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) by distinguishing restricted (intra‐axonal) from hindered (extra‐axonal) water and laid the foundation for axonal diameter mapping in highly aligned fiber bundles (Alexander et al. 2010; Assaf et al. 2008). To account for the non‐trivial fiber orientation dispersion in human white matter (Axer et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2019), powder‐averaging (Jespersen et al. 2013) and the spherical mean technique (SMT) (Kaden et al. 2016) perform directional averaging of diffusion MRI signals at each diffusion weighting b, yielding the well‐known 1/b scaling of the spherical mean signal (Jensen et al. 2016; Kroenke et al. 2004; McKinnon et al. 2017; Veraart et al. 2019). Deviations from this power‐law scaling at high b‐values provide signal contrast for axonal diameter estimation (Fan et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2022; Veraart et al. 2020).

Despite advances in biophysical modeling, axon diameter mapping in the living human brain has historically been constrained by the limited maximum gradient strengths G max on clinical MRI scanners (Huang et al. 2015; Nilsson et al. 2017). In theory, the resolution limit (the shortest detectable length scale) of axon diameter using diffusion MRI is proportional to G max −1/2, that is, the larger the gradient strength, the smaller the detectable axon diameter. Clinical MRI systems typically operate at gradient strengths of 40–80 mT/m, which can make it challenging to differentiate intra‐ from extra‐axonal water. Specialized high‐gradient MRI systems can detect smaller diameter axons (Abad et al. 2025; Dyrby et al. 2013; Foo et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2015, 2021; Ramos‐Llordén et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2024). Previous studies using the original Connectome MRI scanner equipped with gradient strengths up to 300 mT/m have demonstrated sensitivity to intra‐axonal water diffusion, allowing for the estimation of effective axon diameters down to approximately 3–4 μm (Fan et al. 2018, 2020, 2021; Huang et al. 2016) However, histology shows that most callosal axons are < 2 μm (Aboitiz et al. 1992a, 1992b), whereas dMRI with gradients ≤ 300 mT/m is largely insensitive to axons below 3.6 μm (theoretical resolution limit) and provides estimates that are weighted toward larger caliber axons in the distribution. This gap in sensitivity to smaller diameter axons has provided a key motivation for stronger gradients on human MRI systems.

We recently reported the development of the next‐generation Connectome MRI scanner (Connectome 2.0) equipped with ultra‐high gradient strengths of up to 500 mT/m and a slew rate of 600 T/m/s—the highest achieved to date for in vivo human imaging (Ramos‐Llordén et al. 2025). The stronger and faster gradients of Connectome 2.0 not only extend the maximum b‐values achievable for a given echo time but also allow shorter diffusion waveforms at the same diffusion weighting, reducing echo time (TE) and increasing signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) (Fan et al. 2018; Guérin et al. 2017). Higher b‐values boost the sensitivity of dMRI‐based axon diameter mapping to restricted diffusion in small diameter axons (Huang et al. 2015; Nilsson et al. 2017; Veraart et al. 2020), while shorter TE mitigates T 2‐related signal loss, particularly in deep white matter. Together, we hypothesized that these hardware advances (Huang et al. 2021; Mahmutovic et al. 2025; Müller et al. 2025; Ramos‐Llordén et al. 2025) would enhance the contrast between intra‐ and extra‐axonal signals, reduce noise‐induced bias, and ultimately improve both the accuracy and precision of in vivo axon diameter estimates, enabling more reliable characterization of smaller axon populations. Building on previous studies of scan‐rescan repeatability of axon diameter mapping on other high‐gradient systems (Abad et al. 2025; Dyrby et al. 2013; Fan et al. 2021; Veraart et al. 2021), we also sought to quantify the variance of axon diameter estimates using the Connectome 2.0 scanner, which is important for assessing the statistical power enabled by higher sensitivity measurements of axonal microstructure for related studies.

In this study, we investigated axonal diameter mapping in the living human brain on the Connectome 2.0 scanner. We assessed the accuracy and precision of axon diameter estimates by comparing data from 20 healthy subjects scanned on the Connectome 1.0 scanner and 20 demographically matched subjects scanned on Connectome 2.0 and compared the experimental results with theoretical predictions of the resolution limit at 300 mT/m versus 500 mT/m maximum gradient strength. Furthermore, we analyzed the reliability of axonal diameter estimates through scan‐rescan experiments in seven healthy subjects scanned on the Connectome 2.0 and compared the findings to comparable scan‐rescan experiments performed on the Connectome 1.0 (Fan et al. 2021). We systematically investigated the capability of the Connectome 2.0 to estimate small diameter axons in major white matter regions of interest (ROIs) and pathways. Our findings build on prior high‐gradient diffusion MRI research and demonstrate the potential of ultra‐high gradient diffusion MRI systems like the Connectome 2.0 to push the boundaries of mesoscale neuroimaging.

Methods

MRI Acquisition

20 healthy adult participants were scanned on the original Connectome 1.0 scanner (MAGNETOM Connectom, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), which was equipped with a maximum gradient strength of 300 mT/m and a slew rate of 200 T/m/s. The Connectome 1.0 system was subsequently decommissioned and replaced by the Connectome 2.0 scanner (MAGNETOM Connectom.X, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), which features a maximum gradient strength of 500 mT/m and a maximum slew rate of 600 T/m/s. 20 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy adults were independently recruited and underwent imaging on the Connectome 2.0 scanner. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Massachusetts General Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent before participating.

Both scanners employed harmonized diffusion MRI protocols based on the previously established TractCaliber MRI protocol (Huang et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2022). On Connectome 1.0, diffusion‐weighted imaging used b‐value shells of 50, 350, 800, 1500, 2400, 3450, 4750, 6000 s/mm2 acquired at Δ = 19 ms, and 200, 950, 2300, 4250, 6750, 9850, 13,500, 17, 800 s/mm2 acquired at Δ = 49 ms (pulse width δ = 8 ms); echo time TE = 77 ms. The same b‐value scheme was collected on Connectome 2.0 but with shorter diffusion times (the first Δ was shortened from 19 to 13 ms, and the second Δ was shortened from 49 to 30 ms), a narrower pulse width (δ = 6 ms), and a shorter echo time (TE = 54 ms). Diffusion‐encoding directions were 32 for shells with b ≤ 2300 s/mm2 and 64 for shells with b > 2300 s/mm2. The diffusion MRI acquisitions on both scanners used 2 mm isotropic resolution with partial Fourier factor = 6/8, GRAPPA = 2, and SMS = 2. Interspersed non‐diffusion‐weighted (b = 0 s/mm2) images were acquired every 16 diffusion‐weighted volumes and used to estimate and correct for bulk head motion.

For white matter segmentation, high‐resolution 3D T 1‐weighted anatomical scans were acquired. On Connectome 1.0, T 1‐weighted images were acquired using a multi‐echo MPRAGE (MEMPRAGE) sequence at 1.0 mm isotropic resolution with TR = 2530 ms, TEs = 1.15, 3.03, 4.89 and 6.75 ms, TI = 1100 ms, flip angle = 7°, and GRAPPA acceleration factor = 3. On Connectome 2.0, T 1‐weighted images were acquired using a magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE) sequence at 1.0 mm isotropic resolution with TR = 2500 ms, TE = 3.36 ms, TI = 1100 ms, flip angle = 8°, and GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2.

Age distributions were compared between the Connectome 1.0 (n = 20) and Connectome 2.0 (n = 20) cohorts using a two‐sided Welch's t‐test and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Sex proportions were compared with Fisher's exact test. All tests were two‐sided.

Image Preprocessing

Diffusion‐weighted images were preprocessed to correct for Gibbs ringing, susceptibility‐ and eddy‐current‐induced distortions, motion artifacts, and gradient nonlinearity distortion following the pipeline outlined in Figure S1. Gibbs ringing removal was applied based on the local subvoxel shifts (Kellner et al. 2016), and susceptibility and eddy current correction was performed using FSL's topup and eddy tools (Andersson and Sotiropoulos 2016). Gradient nonlinearity distortion correction was corrected using an in‐house MATLAB script (Fan et al. 2016; Jovicich et al. 2006) with the gradient spherical harmonics coefficients for the Connectome 1.0 and Connectome 2.0 gradient coils, respectively. The preprocessing steps were further refined using a custom script, which is available at https://github.com/yixinma9/diffusion_preproc_C2.

For qualitative assessment, diffusion‐weighted images were normalized by their corresponding b = 0 image for both the Connectome 1.0 and Connectome 2.0 scanners. For quantitative comparison, voxel‐wise temporal SNR was computed as the mean signal divided by its standard deviation across the first ten b = 0 volumes, and the reported SNR represents the average of voxel‐wise values across the whole brain.

AxCaliber‐SMT Model Fitting

Forward Model and Assumptions

In the AxCaliber‐SMT model, diffusion in white matter is modeled as the combination of restricted diffusion in cylinders for the intra‐axonal space, hindered diffusion of anisotropic Gaussian form for the extracellular space, and free diffusion of an isotropic compartment for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The axial diffusivity for both intra‐ and extra‐cellular space, D a and D eL, was set to 1.7 μm2/ms (Fan et al. 2016, 2021). The intrinsic diffusivity D 0 of intra‐axonal space was also set to 1.7 μm2/ms (Fan et al. 2016, 2021). The CSF diffusivity D csf was set to 3 μm2/ms (Veraart et al. 2020). We fitted the model to spherical mean signals factoring out the fiber orientation dispersion. The fitted parameters included axon diameter, restricted (intra‐axonal) volume fraction f r, isotropic volume fraction f csf, and extracellular radial diffusivity D eR.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo With Ensemble Sampler With Affine Invariance

We performed parameter estimation using the GACELLE (GPU‐Accelerated toolbox for high‐throughput multi‐dimensional quantitative parameter Estimation) framework (Chan et al. 2025), which implements an affine‐invariant ensemble sampler (Goodman and Weare 2010) for efficient Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) fitting. GACELLE is designed to handle large, multi‐dimensional diffusion MRI datasets by vectorizing computations and exploiting GPU acceleration, enabling orders‐of‐magnitude speed‐up compared to CPU‐based processing.

For each voxel within the brain mask, we initialized 50 walkers and ran 2000 iterations, discarding the first 20% of samples as burn‐in. Chains were thinned by a factor of 20, yielding 80 posterior samples per walker and a total of 4000 samples per voxel for parameter estimation. The stretch move step size was set to 2, and the posterior median across all samples was taken as the point estimate for each parameter. All computations were implemented in MATLAB, fully vectorized, and executed on an NVIDIA GPU, achieving a speed‐up of ~2210× relative to single‐core CPU execution. The GACELLE source code is available at: https://github.com/kschan0214/gacelle.

Group ROI‐Based Analysis

The MRI data of the 20 subjects scanned on Connectome 1.0 and 20 subjects scanned on Connectome 2.0 were used for ROI‐based analysis. We used FSL's “fsl_reg” to perform registration of each subject's FA map to the standard template space (FSL_HCP1065_FA_1mm.nii.gz). We then applied the resulting warp fields to the axon diameter and other metric maps using “applywarp”, bringing all data into the same standard space. We averaged axon diameter maps and restricted volume fraction maps across the 20 subjects scanned on Connectome 1.0 and 20 subjects scanned on Connectome 2.0, respectively.

ROI analyses were performed on both the axon diameter maps and the restricted volume fraction maps using the JHU white matter atlas (Hua et al. 2008; Mori et al. 2005; Oishi et al. 2010; Wakana et al. 2007). Specifically, the JHU‐ICBM‐labels‐1 mm atlas was used to examine the genu, body, and splenium of the corpus callosum, as it provides detailed subregional parcellations of this structure, while the JHU‐ICBM‐tracts‐maxprob‐thr50‐1 mm atlas was applied to analyze various major white matter tracts, given its tract‐based probabilistic delineations. For each subject, an ROI value at the subject level (i.e., the median metric within that region) was calculated. These values were then averaged across the study cohort, and the group mean ± standard deviation was computed for each ROI.

Group comparisons between Connectome 2.0 (n = 20) and Connectome 1.0 (n = 20) were performed using two‐sided Welch's t‐tests on participant‐level median values within each ROI or tract. For multiple comparisons, p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR procedure at q = 0.05. Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation for each group.

Tract‐Based Analysis

We applied a template‐based tractometry approach to characterize axon diameter variation along major white matter pathways (Yeatman et al. 2012). First, a deterministic fiber tract reconstruction was generated in the JHU/HCP group template (e.g., the HCP‐1065 template) using DSI Studio (Yeh et al. 2013). Given that each subject's axon diameter map had already been nonlinearly registered to this same template, axon diameter variation (profile) along each fiber tract was sampled on each individual's transformed axon diameter maps. For each tract of interest, including the corticospinal tract, inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and arcuate fasciculus, DSI Studio's “Tract Analysis” tool was used to normalize every streamline to 0%–100% of its length, then extract axon diameter values from each subject's registered map at evenly spaced nodes. The per‐subject “profile” of axon diameters spanning the entire tract was subsequently averaged across subjects to produce a group mean and standard deviation at each node. We highlighted both the overall shape of diameter changes along key fiber bundles and the inter‐subject variability in these profiles.

Scan‐Rescan Experiments

Image Acquisition

Healthy adults with normal cognitive function were recruited to undergo two imaging sessions on the same day using the Connectome 2.0 protocol described in Section 2.1, with a brief rest period outside the scanner between the initial scan and rescan. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in the study.

Image Processing

Preprocessing steps were identical to the common pipeline described in Section 2.2. Below we detail only the additional registration and repeatability procedures. Model fitting was conducted within the original native space for both the scan and rescan sessions.

We assessed repeatability within each subject in a symmetric “halfway” space equidistant between the scan and rescan diffusion spaces, following the scan‐rescan study on Connectome 1.0 (Fan et al. 2021; Greve and Fischl 2009). White matter ROIs were defined with the JHU‐ICBM DTI81 atlas (Hua et al. 2008) as in Section 2.6. For each subject, FA maps from both sessions were transformed to the halfway space for voxel‐wise repeatability analysis, then nonlinearly registered to the JHU‐ICBM 1 mm FA template space with FSL for intraclass correlation (ICC) analysis. The inverse mappings projected atlas ROIs to native diffusion space, and the same mappings were applied to FA, restricted volume fraction, and axon diameter maps.

Scan‐Rescan Analysis

Voxel‐wise repeatability was assessed by restricting analyses to white matter (FA > 0.2) and computing, for each participant, Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) and mean absolute error (MAE) between scan and rescan values. Improvements in r relative to previously published Connectome 1.0 results were tested with a two‐sided Fisher r‐to‐z transformation. Differences in subject‐level MAE between our Connectome 2.0 scan‐rescan dataset (n = 7) and the published Connectome 1.0 scan‐rescan cohort were evaluated with two‐sided Welch's t‐tests.

To assess the reliability of the metrics in capturing meaningful inter‐subject microstructural differences relative to intra‐subject variability, we computed the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for each ROI (Fan et al. 2021). The ICC was measured as follows:

ICC=Sinter2Sinter2+Sintra2, where Sinter2=Varim¯i=1N−1∑i=1Nm¯i−m̿i2, m̿i=1N∑i=1Nm¯i, and Sintra2=1N∑i=1N12mi,scan−m¯i2+mi,rescan−m¯i2. mi represents the metric value for subject i within a specific ROI. m¯i represents the average of the scan and rescan sessions. An ICC value approaching 1 indicates that the variability between subjects is substantially greater than the scan‐rescan variability within subjects. ICCs from Connectome 2.0 and Connectome 1.0 were compared across the 23 ROIs with a paired t‐test, and the proportion of ROIs achieving “good” reliability (ICC ≥ 0.75) was compared between scanners using a McNemar exact test.

Results

Participant Demographics

Single Scan Cohort Study

20 healthy participants (14 females; mean age: 34.8 ± 12.0 years, range: 22–69 years) were scanned on the Connectome 1.0 scanner. Following replacement of the system with the Connectome 2.0, an additional 20 age‐ and sex‐matched participants (14 females; mean age: 35.4 ± 11.3 years, range: 23–69 years) were imaged. As expected from the matching procedure, the groups show virtually identical age profiles (Welch's t = 0.16, p = 0.87; Kolmogorov–Smirnov D = 0.20, p = 0.83), ensuring that any scanner‐related differences were not confounded by age.

Scan‐Rescan Study

Seven participants (5 females; mean age: 28.1 ± 8.7 years, age range: 19–46 years) underwent the TractCaliber protocol twice on the Connectome 2.0 scanner within the same day.

Theoretical Resolution Limits and SNR

Theoretical calculation of the resolution limits for axon diameter mapping showed that the Connectome 2.0 scanner achieved higher sensitivity toward small diameter axons compared to Connectome 1.0 (Figure 1a). The theoretical minimum detectable diameters computed for these two scanners were dmin,SMT = 3.57 μm for Connectome 1.0 and 2.47 μm for Connectome 2.0 given the realistic SNR derived from the in vivo data. Connectome 2.0 provided higher temporal SNR (38.0) compared to Connectome 1.0 (17.9) (Figure 2).

Comparison of normalized diffusion‐weighted spherical mean signals at various b‐values acquired on Connectome 2.0 (C2) and Connectome 1.0 (C1) scanners. C1 suffered from a high noise floor, as shown in the higher intensity at the high b‐value. The SNR values of non‐diffusion‐weighted signals (b0) acquired on C2 and C1 are 38.0 and 17.9, respectively, indicating a 2.12 times higher SNR on C2.

Group‐Averaged Axon Diameter Maps

Figure 3a displays the group‐averaged axon diameter maps for Connectome 2.0 and Connectome 1.0 overlaid on anatomical reference images in the sagittal, coronal, and axial planes. Across all the tracts represented, Connectome 2.0 yielded consistently smaller estimates, particularly in the central white matter, with differences reaching statistical significance (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05). The distribution plots of axon diameters (Figure S3) across multiple white matter regions show that the Connectome 2.0 group consistently exhibited uniformly smaller values in these ROIs, with narrower spectra that suggest more precise estimation. In Figure 3b, a closer view along the corpus callosum highlights the results within the genu, body, and splenium for both groups. The results from Connectome 2.0 showed a pattern broadly consistent with the low–high–low trend in axon diameter values across the genu, body, and splenium reported in histology studies (Aboitiz et al. 1992a, 1992b), whereas this pattern was less apparent in the results from Connectome 1.0. The genu region exhibited the largest group difference, followed by the midbody and splenium.

We extracted median axon diameters from the major white matter tracts represented in the JHU white matter atlas. The bar chart in Figure 3c illustrates the mean ± standard deviation (over subjects) of the median axon diameter values across tracts (e.g., corticospinal tract (CST), forceps, inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculus (IFOF), and superior longitudinal fasciculus). Consistent with the corpus callosum patterns, the data acquired on Connectome 1.0 resulted in significantly higher axon diameter estimates (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05) in all tracts examined, often accompanied by greater variation across subjects, while the data acquired on Connectome 2.0 exhibited smaller axon diameter estimates with generally reduced between‐subject variability.

To mitigate potential cohort‐related confounds, we analyzed a subset of the 40 individuals scanned for this study, namely, three paired subjects scanned on both systems (Figure S4). Despite the limited sample size, consistent trends were observed at the individual‐subject level, supporting that the reported effects primarily reflect scanner‐dependent sensitivity rather than cohort differences. However, because two distinct cohorts overall were acquired on the two scanners, residual cohort‐specific effects cannot be entirely excluded. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted as convergent and highly suggestive evidence, supported by simulations and additional analyses, rather than a definitive causal demonstration attributable solely to scanner differences.

Group‐Averaged Restricted Volume Fraction Maps

Figure S5a shows the cohort‐averaged restricted volume fraction maps for the 20 Connectome 2.0 participants (top row) and the 20 Connectome 1.0 participants (bottom row). Restricted fraction in white matter in Connectome 2.0 was visibly smaller than in Connectome 1.0. Figure S5b summarizes this observation quantitatively: for 12 major tracts extracted with the JHU atlas, Connectome 2.0 produced systematically lower median restricted volume fraction values than Connectome 1.0, for example, the largest percentage change in restricted volume fraction was observed in the right corticospinal tract, decreasing from 0.76 on Connectome 1.0 to 0.69 on Connectome 2.0 (Δf r = −0.07, −9.2%). The differences reached statistical significance (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05) in a total of seven tracts: left and right anterior thalamic radiation, left and right corticospinal tract, forceps major, left inferior frontooccipital fasciculus, and left superior longitudinal fasciculus (marked with *). Besides a downward shift of the mean, Connectome 2.0 also reduced between‐subject dispersion (averaged standard deviation of 0.05 vs. 0.07 on Connectome 1.0).

Figure S6 explores whether the shorter echo time (TE = 54 ms) used by Connectome 2.0 could by itself explain the lower restricted volume fraction values. We simulated T 2‐weighted signals for a three‐compartment model (T 2,restricted ~ 100 ms, T 2,extra ~ 50 ms, T 2,CSF = 2000 ms) across a physiologic range of intrinsic (T2‐independent) restricted volume fraction (0.30–0.60) and a CSF volume fraction of 0.10. The simulated results revealed that decreasing TE from 77 ms (Connectome 1.0) to 54 ms (Connectome 2.0) decreased the T 2‐weighted restricted volume fraction by up to 3%–6%. The reduction in restricted volume fraction fr observed with Connectome 2.0 is only partly explained by T2‐weighting effects. In our simulations, using tract‐specific intra‐ and extra‐axonal T2 values for the CST (T 2,restricted/T 2,extra = 107/44 ms in posterior limb of internal capsule) (McKinnon and Jensen 2019; Veraart et al. 2018) yields a predicted decrease (up to 6%), consistent with the trend of in vivo observations (e.g., 9.2% in the right CST in Figure S5, cf. simulations in Figure S6). This demonstrates that tract‐specific intra‐axonal T2 values, together with the shorter TE on Connectome 2.0, partially account for the observed reductions of fr in major white matter tracts like the CST.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a systematic assessment of the resolution limit and scan‐rescan repeatability of axon diameter mapping using the original Connectome MRI scanner equipped with 300 mT/m maximum gradient strength and the next‐generation Connectome 2.0 MRI scanner equipped with 500 mT/m maximum gradient strength. Group‐level comparisons between demographically matched groups of healthy adults scanned on each scanner revealed consistently smaller and more anatomically plausible axon diameter estimates on Connectome 2.0, while scan‐rescan analyses confirmed greater repeatability, indicating improved precision and reliability. As high‐gradient systems with ≥ 200 mT/m gradient strengths become increasingly available through commercial dissemination, our findings support the growing feasibility of axon diameter mapping as a noninvasive tool for probing brain microstructural tissue organization. Our study affirms the capability of strong gradients and the concomitant improvement in SNR provided by reductions in echo time and high‐density parallel receive arrays to offer substantial reductions in axon diameter estimates and improve the reliability of this demanding microstructural measurement.

Comparison of Results With Other High‐Performance Gradient Systems

The development of high‐performance gradient systems with gradient strengths in the 200–300 mT/m range (Abad et al. 2025; Fan et al. 2020; Foo et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2015; Tian et al. 2022) has demonstrated that stronger gradients provide greater sensitivity to intra‐axonal diffusion, increasing the effective dynamic range to probe smaller axon diameters. Our findings using 500 mT/m extend the findings of previously published work by empirically quantifying the improvement in axon diameter sensitivity, demonstrating good agreement with the theoretically predicted resolution limit in capturing a greater proportion of small diameter axons within the distribution expected in human white matter.

To compare explicitly against other comparable high‐performance gradient systems, the original MAGNUS scanner equipped with 200 mT/m maximum gradient strength (Abad et al. 2025; Foo et al. 2020) was able to quantify axon diameters in the major white tracts at a scale comparable with results reported on the Connectome 1.0 (~300 mT/m) scanner (Fan et al. 2021; Tian et al. 2022). However, axons below 4 μm in diameter remained challenging to quantify. In comparison, our study showed that Connectome 2.0's 500 mT/m gradients can improve the range of estimated axonal diameters down to ~2.5 μm (Figure 1a).

Reduced Bias in Axon Diameter Estimates

Our results show that the Connectome 2.0 scanner systematically probes smaller axon diameters compared to Connectome 1.0. The higher maximum gradient strength and increased SNR on Connectome 2.0 jointly lowered the detectable axon size predicted by Equation (1), enhancing sensitivity to small caliber axons. We observed that the theoretical resolution limit dropped from 3.6 μm on Connectome 1.0 protocol (300 mT/m) to 2.5 μm on Connectome 2.0 protocol (500 mT/m), in line with theoretical predictions that 400–500 mT/m gradients are required to detect sub‐3 μm axons with pulsed‐gradient spin‐echo waveforms (Huang et al. 2021; Nilsson et al. 2017). Consistent with the expected Gmax−1/2 scaling of the theoretical resolution limit, the ratio between Connectome 2.0 and Connectome 1.0 (2.47 μm/3.57 μm ~ 0.69) is comparable to the simplified prediction (500/300)−1/2 ~ 0.78. The remaining discrepancy likely reflects additional contributions from differences in SNR and pulse duration, which jointly influence the effective resolution limit beyond gradient strength alone.

In experiments, we observed that the Connectome 2.0 data yielded smaller axon diameter estimates that were more consistent with histology (Aboitiz et al. 1992b, 1992a), for example, in the corpus callosum (Figure 3b). Connectome 1.0 produced larger and more heterogeneous axon diameter estimates, indicative of greater bias and lower precision, whereas Connectome 2.0 yielded smaller, more spatially uniform profiles consistent with reduced bias and higher precision (Figure 4). These theoretical and empirical comparisons indicate that stronger gradients, faster slew rates, and optimized pulse sequences using the newest generation of head‐only gradient systems such as Connectome 2.0 may effectively push the resolution limit to detect smaller axons. Furthermore, noise propagation analyses with synthetic Gamma‐distributed axon diameters confirmed that the Connectome 1.0 protocol showed reliable axon diameter estimates above 3.6 μm, whereas the Connectome 2.0 protocol showed reliable estimates above 2.5 μm, cross‐validating the theoretical resolution limit calculation (Figure S2).

The estimated effective axon diameter deff is a single voxel‐wise value determined by the ratio of different moments of the axon diameter distribution. Its sensitivity is limited by the local detection threshold (resolution limit in Equation 1), which depends on spatially varying SNR and maximum gradient strength, and pulse sequence parameters. Compared with Connectome 1.0, the resolution limit map on Connectome 2.0 shows diameters that are relatively smaller and more homogenous (Figure S8). Furthermore, the resolution limit maps on both Connectome 2.0 and 1.0 have a radial gradient (increasing from cortical surface to deep brain region), which is distinct from the in vivo observation of an anterior‐to‐posterior gradient of axon diameter estimates from both Connectome 2.0 and Connectome 1.0. Therefore, the axon diameter maps estimated on Connectome 2.0 and 1.0 more likely reflect the true biological variations in axon diameter rather than the spatially varying resolution limit driven by SNR and maximum gradient strength.

Gradient nonlinearity can introduce spatial variations in both b‐values and gradient directions, which may affect axon diameter estimates using the spherical mean technique. To quantify this effect, we performed voxel‐wise calculations of the direction‐dependent biases in b‐values and gradient directions following (Bammer et al. 2003) based on the known nonlinearity of the Connectome 2.0 gradient coil and conducted noise‐propagation simulations. Our results indicate that neglecting voxel‐wise b‐table biases has minimal impact on the estimated diameters (Figure S9). These findings suggest that, while gradient nonlinearity should be considered in high‐gradient systems, the SMT‐based estimates in this study are robust.

Enhanced Scan‐Rescan Reliability With Ultra‐High Gradients

For measurement repeatability, previous multi‐subject scan‐rescan studies on high‐performance gradient systems (Abad et al. 2025; Dyrby et al. 2013; Fan et al. 2021; Veraart et al. 2021) found that axon diameter metrics are inherently more variable than standard tensor metrics, such as mean diffusivity and FA. Similar to the previous Connectome 1.0 study (Fan et al. 2021), we observed comparable voxel‐wise scan‐rescan correlations (r ≈ 0.5–0.6) for axon diameter estimates on the Connectome 2.0 scanner; however, the absolute deviation was markedly reduced from 0.65 μm with Connectome 1.0 to 0.29 μm with Connectome 2.0, representing a reduction of more than half, from 0.65 to 0.29 μm. By comparison, the theoretical resolution limit improved from 3.6 to 2.5 μm, a relative change of less than 50%. We postulate that the improved scan‐rescan reliability observed with Connectome 2.0 reflects the combined effects of greater diffusion sensitivity to small diameter axons provided by strong gradients (Abad et al. 2025; Fan et al. 2020; Foo et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2022) and increased SNR, which are intrinsically coupled. For a fixed maximum b‐value, stronger gradients enable shorter diffusion times and pulse widths and subsequently reduce TE, thereby limiting T2 decay and increasing effective SNR. While both higher SNR and stronger gradients improve parameter stability according to the resolution limit (Equation 1), the primary driver is the increased gradient strength, which enhances sensitivity to restricted diffusion and indirectly boosts SNR through TE shortening. Together, these effects reduce parameter uncertainty and improve the robustness of model fitting, leading to improved scan‐rescan reliability.

Agreement With Histology‐Derived Trends

As in prior high‐gradient dMRI work (Fan et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2016, 2019), we examined axon diameter estimates in the corpus callosum, a canonical region for assessing in vivo axon diameter measurements against ex vivo histology. Multiple histological studies (Aboitiz et al. 1992b, 1992a; Liewald et al. 2014; Tomasi et al. 2012) have noted that the body of the corpus callosum has the largest proportion of large‐caliber axons, while the genu and splenium each contain a mixture of small‐to‐medium axons. Our observations on Connectome 2.0 captured these subtle variations (i.e., axon diameter following low‐high‐low trend in genu, body, and splenium at the 2–3 μm range), aligning with known histological patterns (Aboitiz et al. 1992b, 1992a).

Nonetheless, direct quantitative matching to histological absolute values remains challenging due to histology's own biases (e.g., tissue shrinkage, potential sampling differences in postmortem tissue) and the fact that diffusion MRI measures an “effective diameter” heavily weighted by the tail of axon diameter distribution. In addition, complicated axonal shapes can further confound the axon diameter estimates, such as axonal undulation (micro‐dispersion along each axon) and beading (along‐axon caliber variation), especially in pathological brain tissue (Brabec et al. 2020; Fieremans et al. 2016; Lee, Jespersen, et al. 2020; Lee, Papaioannou, et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2024; Nilsson et al. 2012). While Connectome 2.0 narrows the gap between in vivo MRI estimates and typical ex vivo histological references, careful interpretation remains essential.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

A limitation of this study is the use of two datasets acquired in different subject cohorts, as the same individuals were not scanned on both Connectome 1.0 and Connectome 2.0 due to the time interval elapsed between decommissioning and upgrade of the Connectome 1.0 system. Consequently, group‐level comparisons may reflect between‐group differences at the individual level as well as scanner effects, rather than purely within‐subject changes. We sought to minimize this limitation by age‐matching the cohorts, ensuring all participants were cognitively healthy, and by recruiting 20 subjects per group, providing a sufficiently large sample to help reduce variance from cohort differences. While residual effects cannot be fully excluded, we directly compared the results for three participants who were scanned on both scanners (Figure S4); these paired data were directionally consistent with the larger groups of individuals scanned on each scanner (Figure 3c), supporting the robustness of the observed scanner‐related differences. The paired‐subject analysis in Figure S4 provides critical supporting evidence demonstrating consistent within‐subject behavior across scanners. Although limited in size, this analysis reduces the likelihood that the main findings arise solely from unmeasured cohort effects and strengthens the interpretation that the observed differences reflect genuine differences in scanner capabilities.

Ultra‐high‐performance gradient MRI systems like Connectome 2.0 are not currently widely available, yet access to such systems as shared scientific resources can help to advance fundamental research using these cutting‐edge technologies. Furthermore, the findings may be translated to high‐performance gradient systems with G max up to 200 mT/m (e.g., Siemens Cima.X, GE MAGNUS), which are now commercially available for clinical use with a growing number of installations worldwide. In this study, we focused on the application of the pulsed‐gradient sequence for axon diameter mapping. Incorporating other diffusion waveforms such as oscillating gradients may help to estimate axon diameters more efficiently (Alderson et al. 2025; Drobnjak et al. 2016; Harkins et al. 2021; Kakkar et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2016). Beyond oscillating gradient sequences, future developments may also benefit from combining ultra‐strong gradient systems with advanced biophysical models that explicitly account for time‐dependent extra‐axonal diffusion (Burcaw et al. 2015; De Santis et al. 2016; Fieremans et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2018) and the effect of axonal beading and undulation on ADM (Lee, Jespersen, et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2024), which could further improve microstructural specificity and parameter interpretability. The AxCaliber‐SMT model employed here assumes perfectly straight cylinders with no undulations or beading; the simple cylinder model was validated in a taxon phantom in prior high‐gradient dMRI work (Fan et al. 2018). Apparent changes in dMRI‐derived axon diameter in pathological tissue should be interpreted with caution, as axonal beading, undulation or model degeneracy can bias the estimates (Lee et al. 2024; Paquette et al. 2020). Finally, we assumed Gaussian diffusion in the extracellular space and ignored its diffusion time‐dependence, which has been observed in the human brain (Burcaw et al. 2015; Fieremans et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2018; Lee, Papaioannou, et al. 2020) and may have an effect on axon diameter mapping (De Santis et al. 2016).

Conclusions

Ultra‐high gradient diffusion MRI using the next‐generation Connectome MRI scanner delivers a substantive step forward for in vivo axon diameter mapping. By pairing stronger gradients with higher SNR, the Connectome 2.0 system increases the sensitivity of axon diameter mapping to small caliber fibers and stabilizes estimates across sessions. Relative to the original Connectome MRI scanner, the estimated diameters are smaller and more anatomically plausible, show better agreement with histology‐derived trends, and exhibit improved scan‐rescan precision, together indicating more reliable microstructural measurements. With clinically available high‐gradient systems coming online, our findings on Connectome 2.0 pave the way toward clinically implementable axon diameter mapping for detecting white matter microstructural alterations in development, aging, and disease.

Figure S11: Normalized spherical mean signal (data points) averaged in the corticospinal tract of one subject scanned on Connectome 2.0. At high b‐values (> 6 ms/μm2), normalized spherical mean signal linearly scaled with 1/b, demonstrating a characteristic signature of the stick model for intra‐axonal space. The fitted curves had a negative intercept on the y‐axis in b→∞ limit. This slight deviation of the 1/b scaling yielded a finite estimation of axon diameter.
