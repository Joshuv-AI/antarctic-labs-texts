Catalytic Autoxidation for Depolymerization of Multilayer Plastic Films
Authors: Ciaran W Lahive, Amy A Cuthbertson, Anna N Walsh, Sydney E Reiber, Hannah M Alt, Brenna A Black, Christopher Botello, Jason S DesVeaux, Spencer Lask, Clarissa Lincoln, William E Michener, Joel Miscall, Kelsey J Ramirez, Lisa M Stanley, Sean P Woodworth, Shannon S Stahl, Gregg T Beckham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecycling multilayer plastic films is challenged by a diversity of polymers, prompting development of new recycling methods. For the depolymerization of mixed polymers like those in multilayer films, metal‐catalyzed autoxidation offers a versatile chemical recycling method to deconstruct multiple polymers to useful oxygenates. Here, we demonstrate that catalytic autoxidation is effective for depolymerizing multilayer films across diverse chemistries. We investigated conditions for a model polyethylene substrate using a Co, Mn, and Br cocatalyst system, achieving full carbon closure with oxygenated small molecules contributing up to 48 mol% carbon. Subsequently, we characterized product distributions for several common polymers used in multilayer films using high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and developed analytical methods to quantify the resulting complex product streams. Optimized conditions for polyethylene were applied to 11 multilayer plastic films containing 10 different polymers, including films with nonpolymeric potential disrupters like aluminum foil and titanium dioxide, showing that catalytic autoxidation is effective across a broad range of polymer types and is resistant to disrupters and additives. The generation of CO2 in these reactions overall suggests that both reaction engineering and modifications to the reaction conditions will be required to achieve higher yields of soluble oxygenated products.Keywords: chemical recycling, nylons, mixed plastic waste, polyolefins

Introduction

The complexity of today's plastic waste is incompatible with existing recycling technologies [1, 2]. Multilayer plastic films, which are prevalent in food packaging and account for 26% of the flexible packaging market by mass globally [3], are a primary example of the complexity of plastic waste. Multilayer plastic films can contain a variety of polymer types, additives, adhesives, and barrier materials such as metal foil. Currently, no mainstream technology exists to recycle multilayer films, and nearly all end up in landfills or leak into the environment [4]. As a result, new technologies are emerging to address complex materials like multilayer films, including solvent dissolution‐based technologies [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], pyrolysis [2, 10], as well as chemical deconstruction and catalytic upgrading [11, 12, 13, 14].

Catalytic depolymerization into valuable small molecules is a promising approach to plastic waste valorization [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18], and foundational work by Partenheimer to deconstruct several polymers based on the Mid‐Century process established a foundation for oxidative deconstruction of plastic waste [19]. Building on this precedent, we developed a two‐stage catalytic autoxidation and biological funneling process to deconstruct and convert mixed plastics to value‐added products [14]. In that work, the autoxidation reaction was catalyzed by Co and Mn salts with N‐hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) as a radical initiator to deconstruct polystyrene (PS) to benzoic acid (63 mol% of initial carbon), poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) to terephthalic acid (68 mol% of initial carbon), and high‐density polyethylene (HDPE) to dicarboxylic acids (DCAs; 34 mol% of initial carbon). Other research groups have explored similar reactions, including PE deconstruction followed by fungal upgrading of the reaction products [20], and deconstruction of mixed plastics with sodium bromide (NaBr) as a radical initiator in ambient reaction conditions [21].

The effectiveness of autoxidation on multiple polymers suggests that this approach merits investigation for application to complex multicomponent materials, such as multilayer films. Moreover, in our previous work, we optimized reaction conditions for HDPE autoxidation, which resulted in 34% of the molecular carbon converted into DCAs with a series of lactone acids (LAs). DCAs are valuable product streams with many aliphatic DCAs finding applications in the food, beverage, and polymer industries, as components of hydraulic fluids, and as intermediates in the manufacture of agricultural and pharmaceutical chemicals and dyes. Many of these applications require specific molecules rather than mixtures and, accordingly, will require the development of separation technologies to facilitate isolation and utilization [22]. In lieu of a separation strategy, there is substantial research in the direct utilization of mixed DCA streams for the oxidation of polyolefins via diverse methods including biological funneling [23] and polymerization to access polyesters [24]. Moreover, while full product characterization was outside the scope of the previous work, developing methods to identify and quantify products from HDPE autoxidation and other polymers is essential to maximizing yields of desirable products and further optimizing the process for application to real plastic waste streams.

In this work, we further developed the catalytic autoxidation process for the deconstruction of several single polymers and mixed‐polymer substrates into well‐characterized products. First, we improved the efficiency of the process by optimizing reaction conditions to maximize product yields from HDPE using NaBr to replace the organic initiator NHPI. We then applied these conditions to several polymers relevant to multilayer film packaging, including HDPE, ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH), ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA), polypropylene (PP), nylon 6, and nylon 6,6. Next, we comprehensively characterized the resulting product streams using high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and developed analytical methods to quantify these complex mixtures of small molecules and gaseous products. Finally, we applied autoxidation catalysis to 11 post‐industrial multilayer films with known formulations. The resulting process effectively converts a suite of common plastic waste components into oxygenated molecular products with potential for subsequent valorization and highlights that further efforts should be focused on minimizing gaseous product generation, potentially through tuning catalyst systems and reaction engineering.

Results

Development of Autoxidation Catalysis for High‐Density Polyethylene

An initial objective of this work was to transition the autoxidation chemistry from the use of NHPI to a bromide source, specifically NaBr, in line with the p‐xylene oxidation to terephthalic acid [25]. Bromide is less expensive and more stable than NHPI, significantly improving its practical utility [26]. We used the reaction conditions developed by Sullivan et al. [14] and a standard HDPE substrate (Alfa Aesar, Supporting Information Table S2) as a starting point for this investigation. Specifically, conditions of 9.7 wt% Co(OAc)2, 9.5 wt% Mn(OAc)2, 8 bar O2, 72 bar N2, 20 mL acetic acid, 160°C, and 1.6 wt% solids loading were used to conduct time‐course reactions replacing the original 22 wt% NHPI with 0.25 and 0.5 wt% NaBr (Supporting Information Text S1). We chose a solids loading of 1.6 wt% to avoid oxygen limitations, and we elected to keep the solids loading constant throughout this work to simplify trying to understand the effects of catalyst, oxygen, water, reaction time, and temperature. Catalyst loadings reflect their wt% with respect to polymer mass in the reactions, while polymer wt% is defined with respect to the total weight of the solvent used in the reaction. We determined the initial carbon content based on the carbon wt% of HDPE obtained from elemental analysis (Supporting Information Text S2). The yields of C4 to C22 aliphatic DCAs, the primary HDPE autoxidation products using this catalyst system (Figure 1A), were measured by ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography–triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐QqQ‐MS) operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to monitor reaction progress. These time‐course experiments (Figure 1B, Supporting Information Figure S1A) demonstrated that 0.5 wt% NaBr in place of NHPI resulted in DCA yields between 41 and 48 mol% carbon between 2 and 3.5 h of reaction time. These results compare favorably with the ∼34 mol% carbon yields utilizing the NHPI initiator. During the first 30 min (including heating time), minimal product formation was observed, and between 2 and 3.5 h, the distribution of DCAs shifts from C10 to C22 to shorter‐chain C4 to C6 DCAs, suggesting that the conversion of long‐chain to shorter‐chain DCAs occurs after production of additional long‐chain DCAs slowed. The likely reason for NaBr to exhibit improved product yield relative to NHPI is likely attributed to the stability of the Br radical generation cycle at high temperatures, relative to the thermal instability of NHPI above 80°C [27]. The greater stability of the Br radical generation cycle may allow for more sustained radical production throughout the oxidation reaction as opposed to the front‐loading of radical generation in the early stages of the reaction prior to substantial NHPI degradation at reaction temperatures of 160°C. We hypothesize that the sustained radical production offered by using NaBr over NHPI provides greater control and thus higher performance.

With 0.5 wt% NaBr identified as a starting point, Co and Mn loadings were next investigated in isolation (holding one constant while varying the other) (Supporting Information Figures S1B–C) and together (Figure 1C). This investigation did not reveal any improvement in overall DCA yields; however, Co and Mn loading could be reduced by over 50% (9.7/9.5–4.0/3.9 wt%), with DCA yields largely unaffected. This loading was used going forward. Next, the effects of water and O2 loading were explored (Figure 1D–E). The addition of water to the reactions (1 and 5 vol%) had a limited effect, with increased DCA yields in the C10–C22 range, indicative of a slowed reaction. Overall, DCA yields were unaffected until >10 vol% water was added to the reaction, with 25 vol% H2O addition reducing DCA yields to 20 mol% carbon and 50 vol% H2O addition quenching the reaction entirely, resulting in visible residual polymer. At moderate loadings (25 vol%), H2O is likely inhibiting the reaction, while at higher loadings, poor dissolution of HDPE in the reaction mixture may also contribute to the lack of reactivity. Oxygen exerted an especially strong influence over the reaction, as reducing the O2 loading to below 2 bar resulted in minimal reaction. DCA yields were reduced at 4 and 6 bar, while 8 bar, our standard condition, produced the highest DCA yields. Increasing the O2 loading beyond 8 bar was not possible due to safety considerations (Supporting Information S1).

With data from the Co, Mn, O2, and H2O experiments in hand, the loading of NaBr was revisited (Figure 1F) by varying NaBr loadings while holding other parameters constant. With 3 h of reaction time, it was found that NaBr loadings of below 0.5 wt% reduced overall DCA yields. Increasing the loading above 0.5 wt% resulted in slightly reduced yields of DCAs, with the C10–C22 DCAs making up only 1–2 mol% of the total carbon yield, suggesting increased radical generation rates with higher NaBr loadings driving C–C bond cleavage. Finally, the reaction temperature was examined between 100°C and 200°C (Figure 1G). It was found that at 100°C and 120°C, the reaction was negligible, likely due to poor dissolution of HDPE at these temperatures. At 140°C, the reaction resulted in moderate DCA yields with preferential C10–C22 DCA production. Above 160°C, the reaction produced moderate DCA yields with >1 mol% C10–C22 DCAs, indicating a high degree of reactivity. Taken together, these investigations identified a set of optimal reaction conditions that we chose to apply to a selection of multilayer films and multilayer film‐relevant single‐polymer materials (4.0 wt% Co(OAc)2, 3.9 wt% Mn(OAc)2, 0.5 wt% NaBr, 8 bar O2, 72 bar N2, 20 mL acetic acid, 160°C, 1.6 wt% solids loading, 3 h).

Analytical Method Development for Comprehensive Characterization of Deconstruction Products

Previous work identified and characterized some of the primary product series being produced from the oxidative deconstruction of plastics. Here, we aimed to close the carbon mass balance and characterize as many of the deconstruction products as possible, encompassing the starting materials (Supporting Information Tables S1–S3), mixtures of soluble small molecules formed during oxidation, and gaseous products (Figure 2).

(A) Multilayer films were oxidized in a Parr batch reactor using acetic acid, Co, Mn, and NaBr at 160°C and 80 bar for 3 h, which resulted in a mixture of gases, soluble and nonsoluble products, solid residual polymeric material, and nonsoluble inorganic components. We analyzed these mixtures to distinguish acetic acid oxidation and close the carbon mass balance (B) by conducting experiments with known amounts of 13C isotopically‐labeled acetic acid and collecting the headspace for gas analysis using IRMS and GC–MS/Polyarc‐FID. The remaining solid fractions were weighed and analyzed using ICP‐OES as well as GPC. The dissolved material was characterized using LC‐ and GC‐HRMS, and products were quantified using LC‐MS and GC–MS/FID. Created in part with BioRender.com.

The multilayer film starting materials were characterized using pyrolysis–gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (PyGC‐MS) for organic additives (Supporting Information Figures S2–S12, Tables S4–S9), inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES) for inorganic additives (Supporting Information Tables S10–S11). Prior to ICP‐OES analysis, samples were digested using fluoroboric acid rather than hydrofluoric acid, which exhibits higher dissolution efficiency for refractory compounds such as TiO2 and silica; therefore, the reported concentrations for some elements may be underestimated. Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen (CHN) analysis was used to determine carbon content of the starting material for mass balance calculations (Supporting Information Tables S2–S3). The total amount of carbon from the polymer substrate introduced to the reactor was calculated by multiplying the polymer mass used in a reaction by the wt% of carbon measured with CHN analysis (Supporting Information Text S2).

Previously unidentified soluble products (i.e., molecules beyond DCAs and LAs) were analyzed using liquid chromatography with HRMS (LC‐HRMS) and silylation derivatization with N, O‐bistrifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) followed by gas chromatography with HRMS (GC‐HRMS) (Figure 2). LC‐HRMS and GC‐HRMS data, including formula assignment, mass accuracy, and retention time, are presented in the supplemental Excel file for some initial experiments run using NHPI as an initiator with multiple substrates. Once identified, reaction conditions using NaBr were applied, and we quantified small‐molecule products using multiple methods, including silylation followed by GC–MS coupled to a flame ionization detection (GC–MS/FID) and UHPLC‐QqQ‐MS. Product information, including chemical structures and quantification methods, can be found in Supporting Information Tables S13–S17 and corresponding GC–MS/FID chromatograms in Supporting Information Figures S13–S14 for PE, PP, nylon 6, and nylon 6,6 deconstructed using the aforementioned HDPE‐optimized conditions with NaBr.

Gaseous products CO and CO2 produced from reactions carried out on HDPE and PP were analyzed by collecting the reactor headspace into gas bags and subsequently analyzing them with GC–MS coupled to a Polyarc flame ionization detector (FID) (GC–MS/Polyarc‐FID). Due to the reactivity of the solvent acetic acid under these reaction conditions,28 it was necessary to differentiate CO2 formed from the oxidation of acetic acid from that formed from polymer oxidation.

We conducted experiments using spiked amounts of 13C isotopically‐labeled acetic acid and monitored the production of 13CO2 via isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS). The CO concentration from acetic acid oxidation was estimated based on the ratio of CO to CO2 in blank control reactions without polymer, with the assumption that the ratio would be consistent with that of reactions carried out with polymer present. The headspace was sampled at high pressure (>70 bar), where models like Henry's law no longer apply due to the deviations of ideal gas behaviors at high pressures. Therefore, we modeled the concentration of CO and CO2 in the liquid phase at the sampling pressure using an extension of the Soave–Redlich–Kwong equation of state (POLYSRK) using Aspen Plus V14 (Supporting Information Figure S15, Table S18), which compared favorably against published experimental results [28, 29]. Using this model, it was estimated that 28%–30% of the total CO2 and 3%–4% of the total CO were in the liquid phase at the pressure and temperature at which sampling was conducted. Total CO and CO2 were calculated as a sum of the quantified headspace concentrations and the modeled concentration in the liquid phase.

Finally, post‐reaction liquid and precipitated solids were weighed and analyzed using ICP‐OES to account for additives such as TiO2 (Supporting Information Table S12) and gel permeation chromatograph (GPC) to account for nonreactive polymers such as PET.

Application of Developed Reaction Conditions to New Polymers and Characterization of Products

Small‐molecule analysis of the HDPE dissolved products via HRMS revealed a complex mixture of oxidized products (Scheme 1). LC‐HRMS and GC‐HRMS analyses detected 397 and 85 unique molecular formulas, respectively (Excel file). The difference in assigned formulas is due to the greater sensitivity of the LC‐HRMS method as well as difficulty in assigning formulas on derivatized compounds with small precursor fragments using GC‐HRMS. Based on retention times and mass differences of CH2, we identified 29 potential series: C x H y O (three series), C x H y O2 (five series), C x H y O3 (four series), C x H y O4 (six series), C x H y O5 (four series), C x H y O6 (six series), and C x H y O7 (one series). Structural identification of several of these molecules indicates that different series are composed of several functional groups expected from alkane oxidation, including double bonds, alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, and cyclic ethers [30]. The most stable and prevalent species were C4–C22 DCAs (34.1 mol% of total carbon mass) and C5–C20 LAs (4.8 mol% of total carbon mass). In addition, we observed C4–C8 unsaturated DCAs (0.6 mol% of total carbon mass), C5–C17 fatty acids (FAs) (1.1 mol% of total carbon mass), and C2–C7 hydroxy acids (HAs) (0.2 mol% of total carbon mass) (Scheme 1). For tentatively identified compounds, we only included the 27 compounds that we could assign a molecular formula for. A total of 91 compounds were used for mass balance equations. Total carbon closure from small molecules was 43.1 ± 0.2 mol%. To estimate the total carbon from deconstructed HDPE in the liquid phase, we dried the post‐reaction liquid of acetic acid and conducted CHN analysis. The resulting material was ∼58 wt% carbon (Figure 4A, red line).

Following method development on HDPE, we tested the reaction conditions identified above with additional polymers common in multilayer films, including different types of PE: low‐density PE (LDPE), four types of maleic anhydride grafted linear low‐density PE (LLDPE 1–4), and a very low‐density PE (VLDPE). All PE types were fully solubilized after the reaction and produced similar quantities of dissolved small molecules to the HDPE substrate used for method optimization (Figure 3A). The overall carbon mass balance, including DCA yields, LA yields, and other soluble species, was comparable to HDPE in all cases.

The optimized reaction conditions demonstrated variable effectiveness for other polymer types. For EVA (n = 2) and EVOH (n = 1), the polymers were fully in solution after the reaction, and dissolved products were similar for both polymer types, with 19 mol%–23 mol% and 21 mol% of the initial carbon mass accounted for, respectively (Figure 3). For EVA (n = 2) and EVOH, DCAs were 13.2 mol%–16.2 mol% and 13.6 mol%, respectively, of the initial carbon mass, LAs represented 3.4 mol% across all samples, and other small molecules represented ≈2.1 mol%–2.3 mol% and 3.8 mol%, respectively.

PP autoxidation yielded 441 compounds, of which 32 could be assigned a chemical formula and used in mass balance calculations (Scheme 1, Figure 3C, Excel file). Based on LC‐HRMS retention time and CH2 mass differences, we identified 19 potential homologous series: C x H y O (one series), C x H y O2 (four series), C x H y O3 (two series), C x H y O4 (five series), C x H y O5 (four series), and C x H y O6 (three series). Among the C x H y O4 series, we identified three methylated diacid series that were confirmed using one representative standard (Scheme 1). We also tentatively identified four series via both LC‐HRMS and GC‐HRMS, plus an additional five series found via LC‐HRMS only. Several of these series are likely related to the other diacid series, but full structural analysis was not completed for these compounds. For PP deconstructed utilizing NaBr, triplicate reactions showed that only 8.6 ± 0.2 mol% of the initial carbon mass was converted to soluble products after oxidation under conditions optimized for HDPE (Figure 3C). DCAs and LAs were not observed, as expected based on differences in the molecular structures of PE and PP. No solids remained after the reaction, indicating that all other products were in the gas phase. We also conducted a series of shorter PP oxidation reactions including 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h, with small molecules accounting for 6.1 mol%, 8.5 mol%, and 8.8 mol% of the initial carbon as measured by CHN, respectively. Previous studies have shown that acetic acid is a major product of polypropylene deconstruction [21]. Quantification of acetic acid generation would require isotopically–labeled acetic acid experiments, and due to the scale at which we were conducting our study (20 mL of acetic acid per reaction), we did not carry out this study. Our carbon closure for the oxidation of PP is therefore likely an underestimate.

Nylons generated comparable or higher quantities of dissolved small molecules relative to PE (Scheme 1 and Figure 3D), with nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 yielding 45 mol% and 78 mol% small molecules, respectively, relative to the initial carbon mass. Product analysis showed that autoxidation of nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 yielded many of the same compounds and series as PE, with the addition of several nitrogen‐containing compounds. C4‐C6 DCAs were the most abundant compound class for both types of nylon, at 13.4 mol% for nylon 6 and 28.2 mol% for nylon 6,6. LAs, HAs, and FAs were present, but substantially less abundant than DCAs, at 0.4 mol%, 0.7 mol%, and 0.2 mol%, respectively, for nylon 6, and 0.5 mol%, 0.3 mol%, and 0.3 mol% for nylon 6,6, respectively. We tentatively identified six additional non‐nitrogenous compounds, but their overall contribution to the carbon balance was small, at 2.3 mol% for nylon 6 and 1.6 mol% for nylon 6,6. Among nitrogen‐containing compounds, acetamide was the most abundant for both nylon 6 and nylon 6,6, accounting for 6.7 mol% and 10.0 mol%, respectively, of the total carbon. Succinimide was also relatively abundant, at 1.1 mol% in both nylon 6 and nylon 6,6. In addition, 6‐acetamidohexanoic acid comprised 5.1 mol% of the carbon mass for nylon 6, although it was <0.1 mol% for nylon 6,6. A minor nitrogen‐containing series dioxopyrrolidinyl acids (DOPAs) was confirmed with standards, and C6‐C11 DOPAs accounted for 3.3 mol% of the carbon mass for nylon 6 and 0.9 mol% for nylon 6,6. Finally, several tentatively identified compounds accounted for relatively high proportions of the carbon mass, but were not verified with standards. They were C5H5NO (2.3 mol% for nylon 6 and 1.9 mol% for nylon 6,6), C4H3NO2 (3.7 mol% for nylon 6 and 3.5 mol% for nylon 6,6), C6H9NO2 (3.4 mol% for nylon 6 and 17.4 mol% for nylon 6,6), C6H10N2O (1.0 mol% for nylon 6 and 9.1 mol% for nylon 6,6), and C10H15NO (1.8 mol% for nylon 6 and 0.1 mol% for nylon 6,6). Structural analysis was conducted for C4H3NO2 and C6H9NO2 with suggested structures in Supporting Information Figures S16–S17. Other structures and isomers are also possible for these compounds. Tentatively identified compounds have high associated error in carbon mass balance calculations, as they are semiquantified using a proxy FID calibration with the DCA of corresponding carbon number (Supporting Information Table S16 and Text S5), and results should be interpreted with caution. Both polyethylene and nylon produce DCAs under oxidative depolymerization conditions; therefore, DCAs detected in mixed‐polymer films could not be uniquely assigned to a single polymer source. Nylon‐derived products were identified only through nitrogen‐containing compounds and prior confirmation of nylon presence. Consequently, reported DCAs may reflect contributions from both polymers.

For HDPE and PP, measured concentrations of CO and CO2 were sufficient to close the mass balances for both. Our oxidation of HDPE 35 ± 4 mol% of the initial carbon mass was converted to CO2, and 32 ± 8 mol% was converted to CO (Figure 3A). Overall, these gas measurements combined with the dissolved small molecule measurements, accounted for 110 ± 4 mol% of the initial carbon mass. The carbon mass balance over 100% could be due to several factors including standard analytical error or unaccounted for acetic acid oxidation. For PP, 42 ± 2 mol% of the initial carbon mass was converted to CO2 and 36 ± 1 mol% was converted to CO (Figure 3C), and combined with the dissolved small molecules, our measurements accounted for 87 ± 1 mol% of the initial carbon mass.

There are several compounds that were observed that are likely formed during acetic acid oxidation, including glycolic acid acetate and bromoacetic acid [31]. Glycolic acid acetate was quantified and formed at higher concentrations in the PP mixtures in comparison to PE and nylon mixtures. This is likely because as polypropylene was overoxidized, acetic acid was oxidized at higher rates, forming higher concentrations of glycolic acid acetate. While bromoacetic acid was not quantified, it appeared to be low in concentration. Other compounds that can form from acetic acid oxidation include 2‐butenedioic acid (both stereoisomers, fumaric and maleic acid), glycolic acid (C2 HA), oxalic acid, and butanedioic acid (in small concentrations) [31]. Many of these compounds are also observed in HDPE and PP oxidation (butanedioic acid, butenedioic acids, and glycolic acid), and although the contribution from acetic acid in these experiments was not accounted for, it is unlikely to markedly impact the carbon yields calculated. We also observed several compounds in an NHPI blank control reaction with no polymer added (Supporting Information Table S19), including glycolic acid acetate and butanedioic acid.

Application of Optimized Reaction Conditions to Multilayer Films and Characterization of Products

Optimized reaction conditions for HDPE and the quantitative analytical methods for the product streams of PE, PP, nylon 6, and nylon 6,6 were then applied to 11 different multilayer films of known formulation (Supporting Information Table S3). The 11 multilayer films were sourced from a packaging company and encompass a range of realistic compositions, for which information and pictures are provided in Supporting Information Table S3. The reaction product mixtures could be grouped into four categories based on their post‐reaction states: fully soluble (film‐1, film‐6, and film‐7), partially soluble with unreacted polymer (film‐2 and film‐3), partially soluble with unreacted inorganic materials (film‐8, film‐9, and film‐10), and partially soluble with both unreacted polymer and inorganic materials (film‐4, film‐5, and film‐11) (Figure 4 , Supporting Information Figures S18–S19). As with the individual polymers, CHN analysis was used to determine the carbon content for carbon mass balance calculations by multiplying the polymer mass used in the reaction by its carbon mass percentage (Supporting Information Table S3), and elemental analysis was conducted to assess inorganic concentrations in the films (Supporting Information Table S11).

The multilayer films largely yielded product mixtures consistent with those from their individual components, with most unreactive polymers and inorganic additives recoverable as solids (Figure 4). Elemental analysis was conducted using ICP‐OES on all post‐reaction liquids, while some post‐reaction solids were collected and analyzed for a subset of materials (film‐2 to film‐4, film‐11) (Supporting Information Table S12). Gaseous products were not measured but are assumed to comprise a majority of the remainder of the carbon balance not accounted for by solids or dissolved small molecules, based on data obtained for HDPE and PP.

The fully soluble multilayer films (film‐1, film‐6, and film‐7) generated product mixtures similar in content and proportion to those generated from the individual polymers of which they were comprised. For example, film‐1 generated 46.1 mol% total carbon mass of quantifiable dissolved small molecules. Film‐1 is predominantly PE (∼60% of total film thickness) and nylon 6 (∼25% of total film thickness), with linear low‐density polyethylene graft maleic anhydride tie layers (9%) and EVOH (5%) accounting for the remainder (Figure 4). We estimate that PE‐derived dissolved small molecules represent 35.7 mol% of the initial carbon mass, while nylon‐derived small molecules represent 10.5 mol%. DCAs were a major component of the dissolved product pool, comprising 27.9 mol% of the starting carbon mass. LAs, HAs, and FAs were present in smaller quantities, at 3.8 mol%, 0.4 mol%, and 1.6 mol%, respectively. The remaining non‐nitrogen‐containing small molecules accounted for a total of 1.4 mol% of the initial carbon mass. Several nitrogen‐containing compounds were also identified, with acetamide accounting for 1.8 mol% of the initial carbon mass. The remaining nitrogen‐containing compounds comprised 8.7 mol% of the initial carbon mass. Further discussion on film‐6 and film‐7 can be found in Supporting Information Text S3.

The presence of PET in film‐2 and film‐3 did not hinder the oxidative deconstruction of the other polymers, and the residual PET was recoverable via filtration (Supporting Information Figure S19). Film‐2, containing 18% PET and other polymers including PE, EVOH, EVA, PP, and polyurethane adhesive, generated 27.9 mol% dissolved compounds, including DCAs (21.4 mol%), LAs (3.8 mol%), FAs (0.8 mol%), and HAs (0.2 mol%). Film‐3, primarily PET (89%), with small amounts of PE, EVOH, and EVA, yielded 5.2 mol% dissolved compounds, including DCAs (4.4 mol%) and LAs (0.4 mol%). Nitrogen‐containing compounds were not detected in the post‐reaction liquids of either film. GPC analysis of the recovered solids confirmed the presence of unreacted PET in both films (Supporting Information Table S20), although the degree of possible PET degradation was not explored.

The multilayer films containing inorganic additives also did not behave substantially differently than their individual components, with film‐8, film‐9, and film‐10 containing 1.2–1.8 wt% Ti, likely as TiO2, and film‐9 also contained Al (0.3 wt%), likely an aluminum vapor deposition layer. Film‐8, primarily PP with added TiO2 (92%), produced 6.7 mol% dissolved small molecules. Film‐9, composed of 81% PP, 14% chitosan, and 5% acrylate adhesive, generated 7.4 mol% dissolved small molecules. Other non‐nitrogen‐containing compounds comprised 1.7 mol% of the initial carbon mass, while acetamide and other nitrogen‐containing compounds each comprised an additional 0.1 mol% of the initial carbon mass. Film‐10 (92% PP) produced 6.5 mol% dissolved compounds. Nitrogen‐containing compounds were not detected, although caprolactam was detected in the PyGC‐MS analysis of the starting material of film‐10, likely from a polyurethane adhesive layer. Aluminum foil and TiO2 precipitated out of solution, but Al and Ti were in low concentration in the post‐reaction liquid (Supporting Information Table S12), and solids could not be individually recovered (Supporting Information Figure S18).

Finally, the multilayer films containing both unreactive polymers and inorganic additives (films‐4, film‐5, and film‐11) behaved as expected. Film‐4, composed of 44% PE, 23% aluminum foil, 16% PET, 13% LDPE with TiO2, and 4% ethylene acrylic acid copolymer, produced 37.7 mol% dissolved compounds, including DCAs (30.3 mol%), LAs (4.7 mol%), FAs (1.0 mol%), and HAs (0.2 mol%), with no nitrogen‐containing compounds detected. As with film‐2, GPC confirmed the presence of PET in the solid reaction residue. Film‐4 contained significant amounts of Al (1.1 wt%) and Ti (0.6 wt%), with some of these metals recovered in the post‐reaction solids (0.4 wt% Al, 0.1 wt% Fe, and 0.4 wt% Ti). These results indicate that our reaction was not significantly affected by the presence of either unreactive polymers or disrupters like Al or Ti. Similar results were observed for film‐5 and film‐11 (Supporting Information Text S3). Film‐4 showed an apparent recovery of >100 wt% (Figure 4B), possibly due to residual acetic acid entrained in the precipitate, oxygen uptake during autoxidation, and possible retention of inorganic catalyst salts.

Discussion

Development of Reaction Conditions

Several reaction parameters were investigated for the oxidative deconstruction of HDPE, including the catalyst loading, temperature, time, water content, and oxygen availability. Replacing 22 wt% NHPI with 0.5 wt% NaBr produced comparable product yields for DCAs, with the possible benefit of decreasing costs due to the potential recoverability of NaBr. Results from this study indicate that Co and Mn loading could be reduced by over 50%, and that the reaction also tolerates water up to 10 vol%, without affecting yields. Determining good water tolerance could point to the potential for the reuse of solvents without energy‐intensive and costly water‐removal processes. Oxygen availability plays a significant role in maximizing product yields. While improving oxygen availability will play a major role in future development, higher oxygen loading was not explored in this study due to a limit of 8 bar in available batch reactor systems. Ultimately, this work suggests that the investigated conditions required to initiate the breakdown of HDPE were excessively oxidizing, converting produced products to CO2/CO.

Developing a Method for Comprehensive Characterization of Deconstruction Products

The analysis pipeline utilizing derivatization with GC‐HRMS and LC‐HRMS was productive in identifying oxygenated products formed by the autoxidation of a variety of polymer types. Furthermore, the quantification and semiquantification of tentatively identified compounds using derivatization and GC–MS/FID were used to distinguish major and minor products to prioritize for further structural analysis. Tentatively identified compounds, which were semiquantified with proxy calibrations, have a high associated error, and future efforts should include confirming these tentatively identified compounds with standards. Many other compounds were observed, but formula assignment was not possible (Supporting Information Table S16) and therefore could not be included in carbon mass balance calculations.

Application of Optimized Reaction Conditions to New Polymers and Characterization of Products

All types of PE analyzed in this study (HDPE, LLDPE, LLDPE grafted with maleic anhydride, and VLDPE) all had similar yields for small molecules indicating that differences in PE polymer structure (e.g., density and linearity) had little impact on yields. Functional polymers with an ethylene backbone, like EVOH and EVA, had lower yields than PE. EVA, containing 25%–40% vinyl acetate, is prone to oxidation due to the presence of an ester bond contributing to a loss of carbon. EVOH, with 29% ethylene, is destabilized by its alcohol functionality, leading to overoxidation and gas formation. These characteristics might account for the lower carbon yields from EVA and EVOH.

As expected, PP had significantly lower small molecule product yields than other polymers, likely due to the methyl groups, which activate the hydrogen atom on the tertiary carbon, making PP less chemically resistant to oxidation [32, 33]. It is likely that optimizing for PP with less harsh conditions would result in higher small molecule product yields for PP. Previous research by Zhao et al. provided evidence of the formation of acetic acid from PP [21], and acetic acid is likely a missing major product in our work presented here. These types of reactions would require the use of fully deuterated acetic acid in the reaction vessel, which we plan to pursue in future research.

Nylon 6 and nylon 6,6 had relatively high small molecule product yields (45 mol% and 78 mol%, respectively); however, the tentatively identified compounds (23 mol% and 38 mol% carbon, respectively) have high associated error. We also observed many different nitrogenous ring structures, which are more resistant to oxidation because the electron‐donating nature of nitrogen delocalizes electron density and stabilizes the molecules [34]. The formation of these compounds may explain the higher carbon yields.

Our approach to analyze the formation of gases CO and CO2 distinguishes between acetic acid and polymer oxidation using an isotopically–labeled solvent. These techniques can be labor‐intensive and introduce a significant amount of experimental error. Future methodologies might include online gas analysis, validation of the liquid phase partitioning models, and experiments with isotopically–labeled polymer. Future work will include gas analysis for other polymer types beyond just PE and PP.

Application of Optimized Reaction Conditions to Multilayer Films and Characterization of Products

Carbon mass balance of multilayer films was similar to the monolithic substrates, and product yields were not heavily impacted by the presence of disrupters, such as inorganic additives, including high concentrations of aluminum and TiO2. Multilayer plastic films composed primarily of PE with fully soluble reactive polymers produced relatively high small molecule carbon yields, while multilayer plastic films with PET had lower yields due to the precipitation of the PET. Polymers that were primarily PP had low yields, likely due to the overoxidation of PP, as indicated by the monolithic PP experiments. These experiments demonstrate that autoxidation is possible for complex multilayer films with a variety of polymer types, while highlighting the challenges of working with multilayer films and the scale of the analytical task.

Conclusion

Current management strategies for complex multilayer films include landfilling and incineration, but there are other promising technologies in development, including solvent separation techniques [9]. Results from this study indicate that catalytic autoxidation is a promising technology for handling complex multilayer film waste, but that carbon closure of soluble small molecule products is still being developed. Reactor design, including flow‐through or continuous reactors, might increase oxygen availability and decrease reaction time, minimizing CO and CO2 production while improving soluble small molecule yields. Product mixtures of this complexity could be further valorized through biological funneling as seen in previous work [14].

Small Molecule Analysis

Gas Chromatography–High‐Resolution Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Nonderivatized samples were diluted volumetrically 10x into 50:50 ethyl acetate and hexane. Samples were also sequentially derivatized for nontarget analysis of poorly ionizable compounds containing carbonyls using PFBHA and BSTFA as follows. A 5 mg/mL solution of PFBHA was made in 50:50 acetonitrile and water. A 100 µL of 5 mg/mL PFBHA solution was added to 1 mL of sample in a GC vial. Samples were then capped and vortexed for 30 s and allowed to react at room temperature for 24 h. The samples were then concentrated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen. Once dry, the samples were reconstituted in 500 µL of 50:50 dichloromethane and hexane. A 100 µL of pure BSTFA was added, and samples were capped and vortexed for 10 s. The samples were placed in a heating block at 60°C to react for 40 min. Samples were then cooled to room temperature before analysis.

Derivatized and nonderivatized samples were analyzed using a high‐resolution gas chromatography time‐of‐flight mass spectrometer LECO GC‐TOF (St. Joseph, MI) operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at a full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) resolution of 25,000. Mass acquisition was operated in full scan mode from 33 to 530 m/z. Samples were injected by splitless injection (1 µL) onto a Restek Rxi‐5 MS column (30 m x 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm, Bellefonte, PA). The inlet temperature was 250°C, with an oven ramp temperature beginning at 35°C, held for 4 min, and then increased to 280°C at a rate of 9°C/min and held for 20 min. The transfer line temperature was 280°C, with an ion source temperature of 225°C at 70 eV emission current. Data analysis was conducted using LECO ChromaTOF software.

Liquid Chromatography–High‐Resolution Mass Spectrometry Analysis

The samples were diluted 10 µL into 990 µL of 50:50 (v/v) ultrapure water:methanol solution prior to injection. Analysis was performed on a Acquity liquid chromatography system (UPLC H‐class, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA) using a Kinetex C18 1.3 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) at a constant flow rate of 0.45 mL/min, an injection volume of 2 µL, and column oven temperature of 35°C. The mobile phase solvents, which consisted of eluent (A) ultrapure water modified with 0.1% formic acid (v/v), and eluent (B) acetonitrile also modified with 0.1% formic acid (v/v), were used as follows: 0–2 min hold at 99% A and then decreases to 1% A over the next 20 min, followed by a 5‐min equilibration time for a total run time of 27 min.

Compounds eluting from the column were ionized by electrospray negative‐ and positive‐ion mode ionization using 1.9 kV or 3 kV spray voltage, respectively, on a Synapt G2‐Si mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). The desolvation temperature was set to 550°C, while the source temperature was set to 150°C for both ionization modes. Ions were scanned between a m/z range of 50–1200 in resolution mode, and data was collected in profile. Nitrogen was used as the desolvation gas, sweep gas, and nebulizer gas, set at 1000 L/h, 10 L/h, and 6.5 L/h, respectively, for both ionization modes. Mass calibration was performed prior to each data set, and additionally as needed if the LockMass drifted more than 30 ppm mass error. The LockMass reference mass calibration solution consisted of Leucine‐enkephalin at 100 ng/mL in water/acetonitrile 50/50 (v/v) was infused 5 µL/min, and data was collected at intervals of 0.5 min.

After UPLC‐HRMS collection, data was first adjusted for the mass drift of Leucine‐enkephalin (continuous LockMass correction process; resolution 50,000 FWHM, Np multiplier 1.0, LockMass 554.2615 Da, and a LockMass window of +/‐ 0.2 Da) and converted to centroid (automatic peak detection process under automatic default parameters) using Waters MassLynx v4.1. Following this, the data was processed using Waters Progenesis QI v2.1to identify analytes from high‐resolution mass spectral data according to molecular formula, mass error, and retention time. Waters Progenesis QI was also used to subtract a solvent blank of LC‐MS system‐induced background ions and ions occurring from a blank Parr reaction of only solvents and the initiator (NHPI).

Quantification Methods

Derivatization with BSTFA and GC–MS/FID

Deconstructed polymer mixtures in acetic acid were dried completely under a gentle stream of nitrogen. These were reconstituted in 1:1 volume sample to BSTFA (with 1% TMCS) and brought up to 1 mL with either dichloromethane or tetrahydrofuran. Samples were then spiked with 10 μL of an internal standard (o‐terphenyl) and surrogate (hexanedioic acid‐d4) mixture. Sample vials were capped and vortexed, then added to a heating block set at 70°C, and allowed to react for 20 min. Calibration curves were constructed between 1 and 200 mg/L using the same procedure. Linear or quadratic curve fits were R2 > 0.994 with a minimum of 5 data points.

Samples were analyzed using an Agilent 8890 GC coupled to a 5877 MSD (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and an FID (GC–MS/FID) using a HP‐5 30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 µm column (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). This instrument has a capillary flow technology (CFT) plate that splits the sample flow into two detectors, a mass spectrometry detector (MSD), and a FID at a 1:4 split ratio. Samples were injected at 1 µL into the inlet port at 280°C in splitless mode. The oven ramp temperature started at 60°C, was held for 2 min, and then ramped at 3°C/min to 300°C and held for 5 min. The transfer line temperature to the MSD was 325°C. The MSD was in EI mode at 70 eV, with the MS source temperature at 230°C and the quadrupole temperature at 150°C. The MSD was run in full scan mode with m/z range of 33–600. The FID detector temperature was held at 315°C.

Compounds that were quantified using effective carbon number (ECN) (Supporting Information Text S5) were calculated following Supporting Information equations S2–S5. Compound information including retention time, quantifier ion (m/z), and monitored ion for compounds semiquantified using ECN by FID can be found on Supporting Information Tables S14–S15.

UHPLC‐QqQ‐MS

DCAs (C3–C22) were measured using previously established methods [14]. Briefly, post‐reaction liquid samples were diluted in tetrahydrofuran and then analyzed by UHPLC‐QqQ‐MS (Agilent 1290 UHPLC coupled to Agilent 6470 triple quadrupole MS, St. Louis, MO, USA) run in MRM mode. Chromatography was achieved using a Phenomenex C18(2)‐HST column and a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in methanol. The MS was operated in electrospray ionization (ESI) in negative mode. Compounds were quantified using MRM with internal standard calibration curves. Calibration curves had a coefficient of determination R2 > 0.995 with a quadratic curve fit. MRM transitions can be found on Supporting Information Table S17.

Gas Analysis

Reaction headspace was collected from the Parr reactors using gas bags and analyzed using an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph 5975C mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a Polyarc (Activated Research Company, Eden Prairie, MN) FID (GC–MS/Polyarc‐FID) equipped with an HP‐PLOT Q 30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.20 µm column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Gas was manually injected using a Hamilton gas‐tight syringe at 250 µL into the inlet at 250°C at a split ratio of 5:1. The oven program was as follows: 35°C for 5 min, 30°C/min to 70°C, and 10°C/min to 250°C. The mass spectrometer detector transfer line temperature was 270°C, the source was 230°C in EI mode at 70 eV, and the quadrupole was 150°C. The MS was run in scan mode from m/z 10 to 200. The FID detector was set to 270°C with H2 flow at 30 mL/min, air flow at 350 mL/min, and makeup flow at 20 mL/min. A one‐point calibration was used with a gas mixture from Airgas (Radnor, PA) of CO and CO2, using the Polyarc‐FID signal. CO and CO2 were run at 5.0 mol% and 5.1 mol% in the calibration mix, with sample concentrations calculated as a ratio of peak areas to the calibration. Calibration verifications were run presample and after sample injection, and the percent difference for calibration verifications for each gas was <20%.

All quantitative data analyses, including GC–MS/FID, UHPLC‐QqQ‐MS, and GC–MS/Polyarc‐FID, were completed using MassHunter Quantitative Analysis software.

Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometry for 13CO2 Analysis

For experiments using acetic acid‐13C2, gas‐tight 12‐mL soda glass vials from EA consumables LLC (product # E2852, Marlton, NJ) were vacuum evacuated and then filled with 13 mL of sample from the gas bags with headspace collected from the Parr reactors. Vials were then shipped to the UC‐Davis Stable Isotope Facility for 13CO2 analysis using IRMS.

Gas Solubility Estimations

Aspen Plus V14 was used to model gas phase solubility of CO2, CO, O2, and N2 in a mixture of water, acetic acid, and oxidation products. The mixture feed composition, temperature, and volume were determined from experimental results, and the POLYSRK equation of state method was used to model the vapor–liquid equilibrium.

Organic Additives Analysis Using PyGC‐MS

PyGC‐MS was conducted using a microfurnace PY‐2020iS/Rx‐3050TR pyrolyzer (Frontier Laboratories, Koriyama, Japan) coupled to a GC–MS/FID (Agilent 7890B/5977A MSD) with an Ultra‐Alloy‐5 capillary column (Frontier Laboratories, Japan). Approximately 1–5 mg of the multilayer film was loaded into deactivated stainless‐steel cups, then automatically dropped into the pyrolysis zone set to 350°C, then vapors were transferred via He carrier gas at 54 mL/min, and then injected onto the GC–MS/FID inlet with a liquid nitrogen trap. The GC oven was programmed as follows: hold at 40°C for 2 min, then ramp to 300°C at a rate of 10 C/min, and hold for 5 min. Data processing was conducted on MassHunter Qualitative Analysis.

CHN Analysis

The multilayer films were analyzed for carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen using previously established methodology [35]. Briefly described, ≈100 mg of multilayer film was analyzed using a LECO CHN 628 (LECO Corporation; St. Joseph, MI). Details on these procedures can be found in Supporting Information Text S6.

Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy

Samples were prepared via microwave‐assisted digestion using a Milestone Ultrawave Single Reaction Chamber Microwave Digester with a 15‐vial rack. Aluminum (Al), calcium (Ca), cobalt (Co), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), sodium (Na), nickel (Ni), phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), antimony (Sb), titanium (Ti), strontium (Sr), zinc (Zn), and zirconium (Zr) were measured using and Agilent 5100 ICP‐OES. Digestion method protocol and instrumental conditions can be found in Supporting Information Text S7.

Gel Permeation Chromatography

GPC analysis was conducted using previously established methods [35] with the following procedural changes made. First, samples were dissolved in hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) and filtered to remove insoluble layers. The filtrate was dried under nitrogen at ambient temperature, dried further in a vacuum oven at 40°C overnight, and then reconstituted in HFIP. The system flow rate was 0.35 mL/min. The PET literature value for differential refraction of index (dn/dc) was used for all calculations. Details on these procedures can be found in Supporting Information Text S8.

We also provide a GC‐MS Agilent library that can be accessed using Agilent Library Editor software, that will include the spectra of trimethylsilyl (TMS) derivatives of compounds confirmed with standards, tentatively identified compounds with formula assignments and compounds that were observed but do not have a formula assignment. Raw data used in this study can be found in an online database with Open Science Framework (osf.io) under the project name “Catalytic autoxidation for depolymerization of multilayer plastic films.”
