Age-Specific Efficacy of MiSight Dual-Focus Soft Contact Lenses and Orthokeratology Lenses for Myopia Control in Pediatric Patients: A Retrospective Comparative Study
Authors: Hye Jin Hong, Dong Wook Kim, Hui June Kim, Younghee Kim, Jiwon Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To assess age-specific effectiveness of dual-focus soft contact lenses (MiSight) and orthokeratology (Ortho-K) in slowing myopia progression over 18 months in pediatric patients, emphasizing axial length (AL) changes.Methods:Retrospective analysis of 239 eyes from 123 myopic pediatric patients (aged 8–18 years) (MiSight: 137 eyes; Ortho-K: 102 eyes), stratified by age (younger: ≤10 vs. older: >10 years). Primary outcome was AL elongation measured by IOL Master. Secondary outcomes included spherical equivalent (SE), keratometry, and cylinder power (CYL) through autorefraction. Orthokeratology measurements were taken during treatment without washout, limiting direct refractive comparisons. Linear mixed models were used for intragroup changes, and general linear model adjustment was applied for baseline imbalances in AL and CYL.Results:Despite significant baseline imbalances (MiSight group had longer AL and more myopic SE), GLM-adjusted analysis showed modestly lower overall annualized AL elongation with MiSight (0.20 mm/year) compared with Ortho-K (0.24 mm/year). In older children (older than 10 years), MiSight was associated with lower AL progression, particularly between 6 to 12 months (0.07 vs. 0.11 mm/6–months, P=0.012). In younger children (10 years and younger), adjusted AL elongation rates were comparable between groups. MiSight maintained relatively stable refractive parameters, while Ortho-K induced corneal flattening but progressive worsening of CYL.Conclusions:This age-stratified retrospective comparison demonstrated modestly lower axial elongation with MiSight after adjustment for baseline differences, particularly in older children (older than 10 years). These findings suggest potential age-dependent differences in treatment response between the two modalities and highlight the importance of individualized myopia management strategies.

The global rise in myopia prevalence poses a significant public health challenge, with projections indicating that by 2050, nearly five billion people—half the world's population—will be myopic, including 10% with high myopia, thereby increasing risks of retinal detachment, glaucoma, myopic maculopathy, or other ocular complications that can lead to vision loss.1 In South Korea, where 96.5% of 19–year-old men in Seoul are myopic, driven by urban lifestyles and intensive near-work, the burden is particularly acute.2 The increasing incidence of high myopia heightens the risk of severe complications, such as myopic choroidal neovascularization.3 In children, myopia impairs learning, emotional well-being, and physical activity, necessitating early interventions to mitigate axial length (AL) elongation and irreversible vision loss.3,4

Myopia control strategies include pharmacological, spectacle lenses, and rigid or soft contact lens-based approaches. Atropine, introduced in the 19th century, slows progression but requires additional optical correction to achieve optimal visual acuity.5 The Low-Concentration Atropine for Myopia Progression trial in young patients aged 8 to 13 explored low-concentration atropine, reporting a 0.27 to 0.42 diopters (D) annual reduction in spherical equivalent (SE) progression when compared with a matched control group.6 Orthokeratology (Ortho-K), Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for temporary myopia correction in 1994 and overnight wear in 2002,7,8 reshapes the cornea to induce peripheral myopic defocus, with studies showing a 0.36 to 0.63 mm AL suppression in treatment versus (vs.) control.9 However, its efficacy varies, it lacks FDA approval specifically for myopia control, and overnight wear poses infection risks.10 MiSight lenses, FDA-approved in 2019 as a dual-focus soft contact lens for both myopia correction and control,10 are daily disposable soft lenses with a concentric ring design, achieving a 0.24 D/year SE reduction and 0.09 to 0.14 mm/year AL suppression vs. control over 3 years.11 Unlike Ortho-K, MiSight is a daily disposable soft contact lens that offers reduced infection risk and enhanced comfort. However, it incurs higher ongoing replacement costs compared with reusable Ortho-K lenses.

Comparative studies highlight MiSight's consistent efficacy in reducing myopia progression and stabilizing SE,11,12 while Ortho-K shows early AL suppression that may plateau or diminish slightly over extended treatment periods.9 However, age-stratified analyses are rare, missing developmental differences. Research by Zhang et al. (2023) and Liang et al. (2024) revealed faster AL elongation in younger children and astigmatism's varying impact on myopia progression and treatment response across different school-age phases (e.g., elementary versus secondary school years),13,14 reflecting differential axial growth rates around age 10 (accelerated elongation in children 10 years and younger, slowing thereafter).2,9,14

This study evaluated the age-specific efficacy of MiSight dual-focus soft contact lenses versus orthokeratology in slowing myopia progression over 18 months in 123 pediatric patients, stratified by age (younger: ≤10 years versus older: >10 years), with AL as the primary outcome. By directly comparing these two commonly used modalities, this study seeks to contribute to the limited age-stratified evidence in pediatric myopia control.

METHODS

Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective cohort study evaluated the efficacy of MiSight daily disposable soft contact lenses and Ortho-K lenses in controlling myopia progression among pediatric patients. Conducted at Fatima Eye Clinic in the Republic of Korea from March 2022 to May 2025, the study included only patients with complete follow-up data at baseline and 6, 12, and 18 months to ensure balanced data sets for longitudinal analysis. The protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Ministry of Health and Welfare (https://public.irb.or.kr; P01-202509-01-003). As a retrospective analysis of deidentified clinical records, informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional policy.

Patients and Stratification

Data from 239 eyes of 123 myopic children (aged 8–18 years) were analyzed (7 eyes were excluded from the initial 246 eyes: five due to incomplete follow-up data at any of the scheduled visits and three fellow eyes with emmetropia not requiring treatment). Patients were assigned to MiSight (aged 8–18 years, n=137 eyes) or Ortho-K (aged 8–15 years, n=102 eyes) based on clinical judgment, parental preference, and suitability (e.g., astigmatism levels). In this study, “pediatric patients” and “children” are used interchangeably to refer to individuals aged 8 to 18 years. Age stratification used a 10–year cutoff: younger patients (children 10 years and younger) and older patients (children older than 10 years), based on evidence of differential AL growth rates around this threshold (e.g., accelerated elongation in children 10 years and younger, slowing thereafter).2,9,14 Inclusion criteria included an SE of −0.75 D to −7.5 D and astigmatism not exceeding−1.5 D (cylinder power [CYL]). Exclusion criteria included prior exposure to myopia control interventions (e.g., low-dose atropine eye drops, multifocal spectacles, or other contact lens therapies). Myopia control spectacles were not commercially available in Korea at the time of the study. Coexisting ocular pathologies (e.g., congenital cataract, glaucoma, strabismus, keratoconus, or retinal disorders), history of ocular surgery or trauma, and incomplete medical records—specifically, missing data from any scheduled follow-up visit (baseline, 6, 12, and 18 months).

Interventions

Participants in the MiSight group wore daily disposable soft contact lenses (omafilcon A material; CooperVision, Inc) featuring a dual-focus design with concentric rings providing +2.0 D of peripheral myopic defocus to reduce hyperopic defocus signals in the peripheral retina while maintaining central acuity. The MiSight group participants were recommended to use the lenses for a minimum of 10 hr per day and take a rest one day per week, wearing spectacles instead.

Participants in the Ortho-K lenses group used spherical four-zone lenses (α ORTHO-K, Menicon Co, Selangor, Japan). A properly fitting lens was chosen based on the fluorescein pattern observed under the slitlamp microscope and corneal topography. The Ortho-K participants were recommended to wear the lenses for at least 8 hr every night.

Clinical Examinations and Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was alteration in AL from baseline to 18 months, enabling direct comparison between MiSight and Ortho-K groups for myopia control efficacy. Axial length was assessed with noncontact optical biometry (IOL Master 700; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany), acquiring ≥3 scans per eye and averaging those with signal strength >2.0. Secondary outcomes included changes in SE, flat (K1) and steep (K2) keratometry, and CYL. Spherical equivalent and CYL were measured through autorefraction (KR-800 Auto Kerato-Refractometer; Topcon Corp, Japan) and subjective refraction with the mean of three consecutive readings recorded. In our clinic, MiSight and Ortho-K were prescribed in an approximately 6:4 ratio. All autorefractions were performed without cycloplegia, which may limit the reliability of SE as a secondary outcome; this is acknowledged as a limitation of the retrospective design. Spherical equivalent was calculated at the spherical power plus 1/2 CYL. Keratometry (K1/K2) was derived from anterior corneal curvature maps through Scheimpflug imaging (Pentacam HR; Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Germany), focusing on the 3–mm central zone. In the Ortho-K group, all measurements were obtained without a rebound period (i.e., during ongoing lens wear).

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and SPSS (version 27.0). Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively, with between-group differences assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables. Longitudinal changes in primary (AL) and secondary (SE, K1, K2, CYL) outcomes were analyzed separately within each lens group (MiSight or Ortho-K) using linear mixed models (LMMs) to account for repeated measures within eyes and subjects. LMM analyses were stratified by age group [younger vs. older] based on the age of 10 at 6−month intervals, with comparisons limited to pre– to post–18-month changes and post–6- to post–18-month changes, respectively. Models included fixed effects for time (baseline, 6, 12, 18 months) and time × age group interactions, with random intercepts for subjects and eyes to handle clustering. Baseline differences (e.g., AL and SE) were included as covariates. Data were presented as means±SD for continuous variables.

Between-group comparisons of postinterventive AL elongation were performed using independent t-tests (unadjusted) and general linear model (GLM) with baseline AL and CYL as covariates to address substantial baseline imbalances. The GLM-adjusted least-squares means±standard errors are the primary results for intergroup comparisons. All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Demographics and Baseline Parameters

This study included 239 eyes from 123 pediatric patients with myopia aged 8 to 18 years, with 137 eyes (71 patients; 8−18 years) in the MiSight group and 102 eyes (52 patients; 8−15 years) in the Ortho-K group. Table 1 presents baseline demographics and ocular measurements. MiSight patients had a mean age of 12.58±2.34 years, female predominance (47/71 patients, 66%), baseline AL of 25.17±0.87 mm, SE of −4.30±1.60 D, and CYL of −0.89±0.59 D, with modest age-related increases in AL and CYL. Orthokeratology patients had a mean age of 10.77±1.50 years, near-balanced sex (25/52 patients, 48% female), baseline AL of 24.32±0.70 mm, SE of −2.46±0.90 D, and CYL of −0.41±0.32 D, with minimal age-related variations.

Between-Group Comparisons

Overall, the MiSight group presented baseline disadvantages across key parameters, with significantly older age (12.58±2.34 vs. 10.77±1.50 years; difference +1.81 years), longer AL (25.17±0.87 vs. 24.32±0.70 mm; difference +0.85 mm), more myopic SE (−4.30±1.60 vs. −2.46±0.90 D; difference −1.84 D), and greater CYL (−0.89±0.59 vs. −0.41±0.32 D; difference −0.48 D) (all P<0.0001), with similar K1 (P=0.8476) and slightly flatter K2 (P=0.0317); gender ratio was more female-skewed (P=0.0068).

In younger patients (10 years and younger), age was equivalent (9.47±0.83 vs. 9.48±0.59 years; difference −0.01 years; P=0.9863), but MiSight showed more myopic SE (−4.36±1.45 vs. −2.26±0.79 D; difference −2.10 D; P<0.0001), longer AL (24.78±0.69 vs. 24.15±0.69 mm; difference +0.63 mm; P=0.0002), slightly flatter K1 (difference −0.29 D; P=0.028), similar K2 (P=0.3410), and greater CYL (−0.64±0.48 vs. −0.37±0.30 D; P=0.5723); sex was balanced (P=0.5671).

In older patients (older than 10 years), MiSight patients were older (13.43±1.82 vs. 11.96±0.98 years; difference +1.47 years; P<0.0001), with more myopic SE (−4.29±1.64 vs. −2.65±0.96 D; difference −1.64 D; P<0.0001), longer AL (25.28±0.88 vs. 24.48±0.68 mm; difference +0.80 mm; P<0.0001), flatter K1 (P<0.0001), similar K2 (P=0.2176), and greater CYL (−0.96±0.60 vs. −0.44±0.33 D; P=0.7281); sex trended female-skewed (P=0.114).

These baseline differences likely reflect practitioner bias in lens selection, whereby younger children were more frequently assigned to Ortho-K under the assumption that they may have difficulty handling soft contact lenses.

Time-dependent Myopia Control: Retardation of Axial Length Growth

Axial length elongation was tracked over 18 months, with age-stratified trajectories in Table 2 and intergroup contrasts in Figure 1 (unadjusted in A/B; GLM-adjusted in C/D). Unadjusted analyses showed numerically lower AL progression with MiSight in older and overall groups. After GLM adjustment for baseline AL and CYL (the primary baseline imbalances, with AL as the strongest predictor of future elongation and CYL as the most significant group difference), the patterns remained consistent with key nuances; younger rates comparable at both intervals (6–12 m adjusted, P=0.2109; 12–18 m, P=0.8274); older patients showed significantly lower AL elongation with MiSight at 6 to 12 m (0.07 vs. 0.11 mm, P=0.012) but convergence at 12 to 18 m (0.10 vs. 0.10 mm, P=0.9898); overall, the advantage was significant only at 6 to 12 m (P=0.0303) with convergence thereafter (P=0.8065). Annualized adjusted AL elongation was 0.44 vs. 0.36 mm/year (younger), 0.18 vs. 0.21 mm/year (older), and 0.20 vs. 0.24 mm/year (overall). These findings indicate that MiSight provides an early (first-year) advantage in AL control for older children and overall after baseline correction, with rates converging later; younger patients exhibited comparable progression regardless of lens type.

Within-Group Longitudinal Axial Length Changes by Age Subgroups

MiSight showed no significant temporal changes in AL from baseline to 18 months (younger P=0.3389; older P=0.3759; overall P=0.1430, all P>0.05), indicating consistent elongation control, with uniform progression from 6 to 18 months (all aP>0.05), reflecting sustained stability postintervention without age-dependent differences (Table 2). Orthokeratology displayed significant changes from baseline to 18 months in younger (P=0.0460) and overall (P=0.0172) groups, but not in older (P=0.4051), suggesting greater early variability in younger patients; uniform progression from 6 to 18 months (all aP>0.05) confirmed maintained effects. Both groups exhibited greater AL elongation in younger patients (nearly twice that of older patients), but MiSight showed consistent control across age groups, whereas Ortho-K displayed more variability in younger patients, influencing overall trends.

Between-Group Comparisons of Postintervention Axial Length Progression

After GLM adjustment for baseline AL and CYL, MiSight showed modestly lower AL elongation (annualized 0.20 mm/year) compared with Ortho-K (annualized 0.24 mm/year). In younger patients (10 years and younger), annualized adjusted rates remained comparable without significant difference between lenses (0.44 vs. 0.36 mm/year). In older patients (older than 10 years), MiSight demonstrated modestly lower progression (0.18 vs. 0.21 mm/year), primarily driven by the early 6 to 12 month period where the difference remained statistically significant even after adjustment (0.07 vs. 0.11 mm/6 months, P=0.012). These patterns were consistent with the unadjusted analyses (Fig. 1C,D) despite the greater baseline myopia in the MiSight groups (Table 1).

Table 3 summarizes age-stratified changes in refractive parameters (SE, K1, K2, CYL), with overall changes from baseline to 18 months (P-values) and post–6-month uniformity (aP-values). In the MiSight group, SE, K1, and K2 showed gradual increases from baseline to 18 months (P>0.05 for intragroup variations) and stability from 6 to 18 months (all aP>0.05), without notable age-dependent differences, indicating minimal impact on refractive profiles across the study period. Overall, post–6-month SE progression was −0.42 D (6–12 months) and −0.19 D (12–18 months) (annualized −0.61 D/year), indicating moderate myopia control through sustained peripheral defocus. K1 and K2 changes were minimal (semiannual fluctuations <0.22 D postintervention), consistent with the lens's mechanism without corneal alteration (P>0.05 overall; aP>0.05 after 6 months), and no marked age-related differences. For CYL, overall changes showed variability from baseline to 18 months (P=0.0010), but post–6-month stability was observed (all aP>0.05), with greater variability in older patients (6–12 months: −0.14 D vs. +0.06 D; 12–18 months: −0.08 D vs. −0.13 D) (annualized −0.22 vs. −0.07 D/year), indicating age-related astigmatic shifts.

In the Ortho-K group, SE displayed nonuniform changes from baseline to 18 months (P<0.0001), with initial hyperopic shift (+1.55 D at 6 months) followed by stability (all aP>0.05) and no age-dependent variations (younger: +1.42 D; older: +1.67 D at 6 months). Similarly, K1 and K2 showed initial flattening (overall −1.89 D for K1; −2.22 D for K2 at 6 months) followed by stability (all aP>0.05), indicating maintained fitting; however, these SE changes should be interpreted cautiously as they are partly attributable to the corneal flattening induced by the Ortho-K lenses themselves rather than solely to axial myopia control. For CYL, overall changes showed variability from baseline to 18 months (P=0.0085), with initial progression (−0.22 D at 6 months; ∼54% increase from baseline) persisting over time, and greater variability in younger patients (6–12 months: −0.04 D vs. +0.06 D; 12–18 months: −0.10 D vs. −0.02 D), suggesting age-related astigmatism risks.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study compared the age-specific efficacy of MiSight dual-focus soft contact lenses and Ortho-K for myopia control over 18 months in pediatric patients, with AL elongation as the primary outcome. Despite marked baseline imbalances (MiSight group older and more myopic), GLM adjustment for AL and CYL confirmed modestly lower AL elongation with MiSight overall and in older children. These baseline imbalances indicate that patients with more severe myopia, higher astigmatism, and older age were more likely to select MiSight lenses, potentially affecting subsequent myopia control efficacy comparisons.

Axial length elongation is a key biomarker for myopia progression and future complications such as retinal detachment and myopic choroidal neovascularization.3,13 In untreated myopic Asian children, annual AL growth typically ranges 0.30 to 0.40 mm/year, with faster rates in younger individuals (∼0.40–0.50 mm/year) slowing in older ones (∼0.20–0.30 mm/year), reflecting prepubertal acceleration that tapers with age, as shown in longitudinal studies.2,9,14 Our age-stratified analysis extends these data. After GLM adjustment, annualized AL elongation was modestly lower with MiSight overall (0.20 vs. 0.24 mm/year) and in older children (older than 10 years; 0.18 vs. 0.21 mm/year), with the advantage most evident in the first 6 to 12 months (0.07 vs. 0.11 mm/6 months, P=0.012). In younger patients (10 years and younger), adjusted rates were comparable between groups (0.44 vs. 0.36 mm/year). Although myopia progression is typically faster in younger children, older MiSight patients showed modestly lower absolute AL elongation than younger ones (015 vs. 0.31 mm/year unadjusted). This pattern is consistent with practitioner selection bias (older, more myopic children preferentially fitted with MiSight) and with sustained efficacy of dual-focus lenses across age groups, as demonstrated in the 3–year MiSight randomized controlled trial (RCT) and subsequent analyses.11

These absolute rates align with effective control reported in prior trials (MiSight 0.10–0.20 mm/year; Ortho-K 0.15–0.25 mm/year), confirming both modalities provide meaningful suppression relative to untreated benchmarks.2,9,14 Systematic reviews of multiple RCTs have shown consistent AL suppression with both modalities,15 while long-term Ortho-K studies have reported sustained effects over 5 years.16 The early convergence after 12 months and attenuation of differences after baseline adjustment highlight the value of statistical correction in comparative myopia research.

Refractive parameters in MiSight remained stable, with negligible changes in keratometry (K1/K2), and CYL, consistent with its peripheral defocus mechanism without corneal remodeling.11 By contrast, Ortho-K produce initial SE improvement (+1.55 D at 6 months) but persistent CYL worsening, reflecting known risks of corneal reshaping.9,17,18 This variability aligns with reports of induced astigmatism an altered corneal biomechanics in Ortho-K users.17,18 Direct refractive comparisons between groups were not performed due to the absence of washout in the Ortho-K arm, limiting interpretations to within-group trends.

Limitations of this study are as follows. First, Ortho-K's nonwashout measurements may inflate apparent SE benefits, reflecting clinical practice but limiting direct refractive comparisons; future studies should include washout periods. Second, the retrospective single-center design and Korean cohort restrict generalizability, despite alignment with international RCTs.11,15 Propensity score matching or prospective multicenter trials would strengthen baseline balance. Third, the 10–year age stratification, while evidence-based, could benefit from more granular analyses. Finally, untracked compliance and the 18–month horizon limit long-term inferences. Even after rigorous GLM adjustment for baseline AL and CYL—the two most critical confounders—residual confounding cannot be fully excluded due to the retrospective design.

In conclusion, this age-stratified retrospective comparison revealed modest differences in AL control between MiSight dual-focus soft contact lenses and Ortho-K, with the pattern varying by patient age. The findings highlight potential age-dependent responses to the two modalities and underscore the value of individualized lens selection in pediatric myopia management.
