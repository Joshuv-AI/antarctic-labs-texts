A two-stage sequential optimization framework for bioenergy and solar energy development
Authors: Chih-Chun Kung, Juan Li, Shan-Shan Kung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTaiwan imports most of its energy and suffers from energy insecurity. Bioenergy has been promoted but grows slowly; thus, the government has decided to develop more renewable energy possibilities. This study outlines a two-stage sequential optimization framework in which the first stage optimizes the social welfare of the agricultural and bioenergy sectors, and the second stage incorporates the land-use change from bioenergy development to examine the regional solar energy capacity. The results show biopower production ranges from 5,816 to 1,640 GWh and 4.9 to 504 million liters for biofuel, with an emission offset ranging from 0.35 to 3.49 million tons. Changes in emission prices cause a technological transition and substantial land transfer. Solar energy could provide 811 to 1,041 GWh, offsetting up to 1.13 million tons of emissions. Collectively, agrivoltaic programs provide up to 6,855 GWh per year, but land-use transfer is a challenge for long-term solar energy programs.

Introduction

The world is facing unprecedented climate impacts, which would result in substantial and irreversible environmental problems significantly influencing human well-being, such as sea level rise, desertification, and increased probability of hurricanes and typhoons.1,2 Climate change is believed to be caused by the extensive use of fossil fuels that emit greenhouse gases (GHG). Because it is impossible to stop using energy on which all human activities rely, it is crucial to replace fossil energy with clean and sustainable energy sources that mitigate climate change and retain energy security.3,4,5 Renewable energy development has been widely examined, including bioenergy,6,7,8 solar energy,9,10,11 wind farm,12,13 municipal solid wastes,3,14 geothermal,15,16 and tidal energy.17,18 These studies have provided insightful concerns and constructive approaches to the energy potential and cost-effective possibilities. However, resource availability and capital accessibility vary across countries. Thus, analyzing a specific technology may not truly reflect a country’s net renewable energy potential. Therefore, we need to examine further the integration of renewable energy systems with the availability of securing funds, and identify the effects of such integrated renewable energy systems.19,20

Taiwan, a small island in the West Pacific Ocean, has a population of 23 million, and its GDP per capita is ranked 14th globally. Since Taiwan has no energy stock, it imports more than 99% of its energy.21 Such a high dependence on foreign energy would make it vulnerable and fragile to international conflicts and price distortions, reducing social welfare considerably.22 Thus, the administrative authority of Taiwan has been looking for alternative energy sources that are reliable, sustainable, and economically feasible.23 However, the government has not paid great attention to the development of the renewable energy industry in the past decades for several reasons. First, large-scale renewable energy development is not presented because the fossil energy supply is stable, and which renewable energy source would be helpful is under debate. Second, the high-tech industry structure of Taiwan requires a highly reliable energy supply. For example, the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company is the leading company in Taiwan, and it consumes more than 27 billion kWh per year (i.e., about 8% of Taiwan’s total energy). Therefore, the government’s priority is ensuring a sufficient and reliable energy supply to this company. Third, Taiwan has not had stringent environmental regulations in the past decades. The “Greenhouse Gas Emission and Management Act” was effective in 2015 and prohibited the use of fossil energy since then. Only after the promulgation of such policies did the development of renewable energy in Taiwan attract much attention.

Bioenergy is produced by carbohydrate-rich feedstocks such as corn, sweet potatoes, sorghum, and woody materials.6,24 Therefore, it is fascinating to the authority because one-third of the total cropland is idle without appropriate rotation. The government has spent billions of dollars subsidizing idle land to support the farmers. If cropland can be utilized to produce energy crops, farmers’ revenue and domestic energy supply will increase simultaneously. Thus, how to develop bioenergy has been an exciting and attractive issue in Taiwan since 2022. Bioenergy can be briefly categorized into liquid energy (i.e., biofuel) and electrical energy (i.e., biopower), and each type requires different production technologies and processing facilities. Due to the context of food-energy conflicts, cellulosic technologies utilizing crop residuals19,25,26 have been widely considered in recent years.

Solar energy is another possibility that the authority has considered. Unlike bioenergy, whose input is supplied stably, solar energy relies more on the climate conditions, and its power potential is highly correlated with regional radiation strength.9,27 Since solar energy has a desirable energy conversion rate and can be installed in different sizes, making its investments more flexible,28,29 it has been widely applied in many countries. The solar radiation in Taiwan is 1,366 W/m2, and Chang30 estimates that large-scale solar energy development is crucial to improving Taiwan’s energy security. Kung et al.31 also show that solar energy can provide up to 10,633 GWh annually, with a budget constraint of 0.1% GDP.

Both bioenergy and solar energy are valuable. However, competition across resources and technology would inevitably occur for a country or region to develop a large-scale renewable energy industry. For example, the government recently claimed that solar energy programs should be installed in active cropland, and such an integration can make land use more efficient. However, this approach has been severely criticized because it becomes challenging for farmers to crop commodities and for energy suppliers to operate the system. Therefore, some scholars suggest that solar energy systems should be installed in idle cropland. Additionally, if we examine bioenergy and solar energy programs separately, the aggregate energy potential may result in a double-counting problem.

A reasonable approach to investigating net bioenergy and solar energy potential is to examine them simultaneously, and the solar energy potential should be estimated based on the residual land. The reason is simple: if we allow inexpensive solar energy to compete freely with food and energy crops, the entire agricultural system may collapse because income from the most fertile land is incomparable to solar energy sales. Therefore, to resolve the double-counting problem and simultaneously consider food security and renewable energy production, we propose a two-stage sequential optimization model to adequately accommodate the land resources in the different renewable energy developments. The process is delineated as follows:(1)This study fills this gap by linking alternative land uses to examine the renewable energy production, emission consequences, capital accessibility, and financing possibilities of the integrated frame of bioenergy and solar energy development. In the first stage, we determine the efficient bioenergy production with inputs from energy crops and crop residuals, given the fixed agricultural resources and commodities’ elasticities, quantities, and prices. Therefore, we can verify the land requirement for agricultural commodities and energy crops and the land transferred from agricultural to non-agricultural uses. We need to identify the land use transfer because solar energy development will occupy the land for at least 15 to 20 years. It implies that we cannot crop this land in the first year, install solar panels next year, and crop it in the third year. The land available for solar energy development must thus be used for non-agricultural purposes and will be occupied thereafter. At this stage, the welfare of the agricultural and bioenergy sector is maximized, and we will identify the results regarding biofuel output, land-use transfer, resource allocation, emission offset, and technological transition.(2)In the second stage, we can model the solar power potential on non-agricultural land and further verify the associated economic and social components, such as total financial requirements, renewable energy production, technology transition, regional land-use transfer, efficient resource allocations, and climate change mitigation possibilities. Because these steps collectively require the non-transferable properties of land resources and capital among renewable energy possibilities, we can then adequately investigate efficient bioenergy and solar energy production with a sequential optimizing framework.

This study fills this gap by linking alternative land uses to examine the renewable energy production, emission consequences, capital accessibility, and financing possibilities of the integrated frame of bioenergy and solar energy development. However, there are also limitations associated with this framework. First, because the agricultural system must be sustained, bioenergy derived from the farming sector is indirectly assumed to be more critical than solar energy. The solar energy can be optimized only after the bioenergy production is optimal. Second, the land-use change across cities is highly dependent on the market conditions, and because the solar capacity cannot be freely switched, an assumption about the use of non-agricultural cropland should be made for the solar energy programs.

The study provides several insightful contributions by integrating bioenergy and solar energy systems. First, the results identify the optimal development strategy of an integrated renewable energy system that accommodates bioenergy and solar energy. Previous studies that analyze a specific renewable technology independently24,32,33 and the results possibly understate or overstate the energy potential of that technology if the competitive inputs, such as land and capital, are highly constrained. With the proposal of a sequential programming model that relates the competitive factor to different renewable energy optimization problems, this work demonstrates a feasible approach reflecting true renewable energy potential. The results would be beneficial for establishing and reforming renewable energy policies. Second, this study shows that the budget constraints of different renewable technologies depend on their energy outputs and are highly correlated with the stability of expected outputs. Some studies34,35,36 show that borrowing rate is a crucial factor affecting renewable energy production, and a low-cost green financing source would effectively reduce the investment and operating risks of the firms. Our results would be a valuable addition to this work by exploring the capital dependence on alternative production possibilities, which could help the decision-makers complete the green finance mechanism and make the size of the green capital market more efficient.

Research background

Bioenergy technology

Bioenergy can be produced either in liquid form, such as bioethanol and biodiesel, to fuel mobiles and trucks, or in electrical form, such as electricity that industries and households can consume. Depending on the forms of bioenergy, the production and processing technologies could vary significantly,37,38,39 and their energy output and emission offset capability are also highly varied.40,41,42 Biofuel can be directly produced from energy crops, but this process would decrease food commodities in the international market,6 resulting in a well-known phenomenon called food-energy conflict. A more advanced technology known as the cellulosic process, which is less dependent on the carbohydrate content, is thus widely examined and utilized because crop and forestry residuals can now be appropriately integrated with the bioenergy production.19,26

Pyrolysis converts biomass into biogas, biooil, and biochar without hydrogen.43,44 Each output can be refined and upgraded for energy source or stored as a means of carbon sequestration.45,46,47 In recent years, a pyrolysis processing mode called torrefaction has attracted significant attention because it converts more than 85% of biomass into solid charcoal, which can be used as a soil amendment to sequester carbon, improve soil quality, and retain soil humidity.48,49,50 Because bioenergy is highly related to the agricultural sector, Alexander et al.51 point out that the potential change in land use should be a crucial and essential topic for bioenergy analysis.51

Progress of solar energy

Solar energy is a popular renewable energy technology because it uses solar radiation as the sole input. The solar panel can be installed at any desired size, making the investment flexible and affordable to less wealthy countries.52,53,54 Because Taiwan has high sunny hours and average radiation, the administrative authority of Taiwan has proposed large-scale development of solar energy in recent years.55 The installed solar energy capacity has grown from 231 MW in 2012 to more than 880 MW in 2015, and is about 12,400 MW in 2023.56

Crystalline silicon (c-Si) and Cadmium Telluride thin-film (CdTe) are standard solar cell modules. Technologies for both modules are mature, and because many components are designed and manufactured in Taiwan, employing these systems can reduce total investment cost. In general, the production cost of a c-Si module is higher57 than that of a CdTe module, which has moved to the Vapor Transport Deposition (VTD) process and Close-Spaced-Sublimation (CSS),28 making it more economically feasible than the c-Si module. Nevertheless, the energy conversion rate is another crucial determinant factor we must consider.58,59

The CdTe module has a lower production cost, but does not guarantee that CdTe should dominate the c-Si system, especially when useful life and degradation rates are considered.60 Benda61 shows that the crystalline structure62 of the c-Si module makes it useful for up to 30 years, and such a long useful life can lead to a low depreciated annualized investment. However, the First Solar28 indicates that the useful life of the thin-film module has a similar lifetime; thus, the CdTe module also achieves a low annualized cost. Solar collectors’ conversion rate has improved from 7.5 to 10% in the last decade to more than 20% in recent years.63,64,65 In 2023, the laboratory experiments report that the conversion efficiency of c-Si is 27.42% and 19.9% for CdTe.29 The degradation rate of CdTe is 1.7%,52,66 and Cambell67 shows that the annual degradation rate of c-Si is between 0.5% and 1%, and such a low degradation rate could promise a stable energy output during its lifetime.68,69 Thus, even though the CdTe module has a lower production cost, the low degradation rate benefits the c-Si module and makes it more economically attractive.

While numerous bioenergy and solar studies exist, Taiwan must explore an applicable development pattern21,31,70 based on its regional characteristics, such as biomass types,19 land utilization modes,51 and received solar radiation.9,60 Tso et al.71 and Chen et al.22 have conducted various input-output efficiency studies of energy crops and demonstrate that high yields of sweet potatoes could make it the best energy crop for Taiwan’s bioenergy development. However, Kung and McCarl20 show that while Taiwan has great bioenergy potential, bioenergy production cost is susceptible to the hauling distance and crop density. Chen et al.49 further point out that bioenergy output can significantly deviate from the predetermined goals without a properly designed emission trade system. Similar situations occur in the solar energy optimization.72 Because of solar panels’ long expected useful life, removing a less efficient mode would be very costly. A prudent decision on a large-scale solar energy system is required in the face of rapid improvements in the energy conversion efficiency of alternative panel systems.

Figure 1B shows biofuel production in the face of alternative gasoline prices. As a substitute for gasoline, biofuel production will increase when the gasoline price increases. Additionally, because green bonds reduce the production cost of biofuel, the lower the borrowing rate, the lower the production cost facing the suppliers, and thus, the more gains would be received by biofuel producers. Since production generally has decreasing returns to scale, the more production there is, the more it costs to produce the additional unit of biofuel. The green bond market alleviates this situation, and thus, we can see that total biofuel production at a borrowing rate of 4.5% is greater than at higher borrowing rates. This result is insightful: an effective green bond market can sustain the biofuel industry. Under such circumstances, total biofuel production may expand up to 668–751 million liters.

Here, we examine how biopower production is affected by electricity and gasoline prices and compare how green bond applications benefit the biopower industry. Figure 2A shows the biopower production under different electricity prices. Generally, biopower production increases when electricity prices rise, regardless of the production cost. However, the result indicates that biopower production has no distinctive change under various borrowing rates. It is an interesting finding, and we must discuss what caused this result. One possible explanation is that biopower production is relatively cheaper than biofuel, and competitive pyrolysis technologies would select the most profitable production techniques. In other words, every unit of electricity that is produced has already covered its production cost, and the lower the borrowing rate, the more the cost savings can be obtained by the supplier.

Similarly, Figure 2B shows that gasoline prices generally do not influence biopower production. Thus, at each borrowing rate, the biopower production remains constant. However, this does not mean green bond applications do not impact biopower production. While biopower production under different borrowing rates seems to be the same, the net production cost should differ. Therefore, the green finance market allows firms to produce a given quantity of biopower at an even lower price. This saving in production cost benefits the biopower industry and can be considered a social gain.

Figures 1 and 2 show that biofuel and biopower production are relatively stable when energy prices change, and the green bond markets improve the competitive power of bioenergy. However, this may not be the case when they face variations in emission prices. When emission offset generates values, the bioenergy value is a sum of energy sales and emission trade minus production costs. Because the offset capability of each bioenergy technology differs substantially, as indicated in Table 1, changes in emission prices may induce a technological transition that maximizes the value attached to bioenergy output.

Figure 3 presents the bioenergy production strategy under various emission prices. Because biochar is an essential source of offsetting emissions, its production is also displayed. Figure 3A indicates that biopower production could decrease substantially when emission prices increase. At low emission prices, energy sales constitute the primary revenue source, and thus, technologies such as fast pyrolysis and slow pyrolysis, yielding the most biopower, would dominate. However, at higher emission prices, biochar that sequesters carbon becomes valuable, and thus the firms will utilize torrefaction that maximizes the biochar output. Therefore, when emission prices increase, biopower produced from fast pyrolysis sharply decreases from 5,525 GWh to 853 GWh (i.e., an 84.57% reduction). Figure 3B shows the production of biochar. At a low emission price, energy is more valuable than emission offset, and thus, biochar production is only 258,000 tons. When the emission price reaches NT$50 per ton, biochar becomes valuable, and its production quickly increases to 7.95 million tons. Biochar production could reach 8.73 million tons at an even higher price. Based on Figures 3A and 3B, the emission price is the primary driving factor of the technology transition. The result is interesting because it indicates that the goal of renewable energy maximization may deviate when the emission price fluctuates. This result can be interpreted by Figure 3C. At a low emission price, the bioenergy producers align with energy maximization. However, when the emission price increases, the energy supplier will produce more biochar to sequester carbon. Under such circumstances, the production strategy is closer to the goal of climate change mitigation.

Figure 3D indicates that biofuel will be phased out at high emission prices. In the face of emission price variations, a decrease in borrowing rate does not benefit biofuel production. This is because the offset capability of biofuel is relatively low, and thus, its production will not make attractive economic returns such as pyrolysis. Hence, even if the green bond market can substantially decrease biofuel production cost, the firms will still choose biopower technologies that significantly reduce emissions. The results show that the several percent reduction in production cost is outweighed by the value attached to the emission offset. Therefore, while a decrease in borrowing rates does benefit biofuel in terms of cost savings, it cannot make biofuel an attractive possibility at high emission prices.

While the net solar energy output does not vary significantly among scenarios, the distribution of solar energy does change considerably. Even though the net energy output is at the same level, the investment decision would behave differently. Since the solar energy programs involve huge capital investment and the solar radiation received in each city differs, there is merit in exploring the investment and installation capacity so that the decision-makers can find the most appropriate allocation of capital. The results indicate that the solar energy output differs substantially across cities and will also vary considerably in different market conditions.

Unlike bioenergy, borrowing rates can substantially influence solar energy development. For example, in the changing electricity price scenarios with a borrowing rate of 7.5%, Taoyuan and Hualien cities will generate about 368 and 178 GWh, respectively. However, when borrowing rates decline, output in Taoyuan will dramatically decrease to 26 GWh, implying that an appropriate installation capacity in Taoyuan should decrease. Conversely, production in Ilan will expand from 69 to 215 GWh, indicating that Ilan should receive more capacity at a low borrowing rate. The results are insightful because they show a possible separation between bioenergy and solar energy programs. Although the energy output among scenarios does not change considerably, the government should not focus on the cities that have substantially engaged in energy crops; instead, solar energy capacity should be placed in cities that have released substantial non-cropland.

One crucial question is to decide where to install. Table 4 indicates that the energy prices, emission prices, and borrowing rates will affect the capacity allocation and output distribution. Since the solar panels are fixed on the site for 15 to 20 years, the government must verify the market conditions before the systems are eventually deployed. In addition, because the market conditions, such as the energy and emission prices, can fluctuate quickly and frequently, the government must maintain a desirable market condition to encourage installation efficiency. Otherwise, it is unlikely to achieve optimal solar energy development.

Biopower production increases to 5,814 GWh at high electricity prices and decreases to 5,525 GWh and 1,640 GWh under variations in gasoline and emission prices, respectively. A reduction in the borrowing rate would benefit, improving biopower production by approximately 2.68%. However, increased emission prices will offset such a benefit because energy suppliers will quickly switch from energy-oriented pyrolysis to offset-oriented torrefaction. Under such circumstances, biopower production will sharply decline, and biochar production will increase.

Biochar production is generally negatively related to biopower production because biochar has a greater sequestration capability than biopower. The higher the emission price, the more biochar will be produced to realize environmental gains. Thus, when emission prices increase, net biochar production increases from about 0.27 million tons to more than 8.64 million tons, and the emission offset of bioresource expands from 0.368 million tons to about 3.38 million tons. The results show that biochar’s increased environmental value can recover a biopower loss. With green bond applications, benefits from both energy and environmental sides (i.e., biopower and biochar sequestration) can be further improved.

Unlike the fluctuation of bioenergy, solar energy production is relatively stable under various energy and emission prices. However, because solar panels are subject to being fixed in specific sites in the long run, careful consideration of capacity design is required to achieve efficiency.

Discussion

The study has evaluated the bioenergy potential, given the sustainable development of Taiwan’s agriculture. Based on the results, some insights merit more discussion. First, because bioenergy is more profitable than many conventional commodities, its production can be sustained in most cases. However, depending on market prices of energy and emissions, bioenergy would be produced from different technologies. For example, a reduction of biofuel may be offset by an increase of biopower in the face of high electricity prices, thereby maintaining a certain level of total bioenergy output. Nevertheless, such an output transfer may not be perfectly realized due to facility constraints. For example, Karima et al.74 show that the production patterns differ across bioenergy technologies, and Fei et al.73 point out that the equipment designed for fast pyrolysis may not be easily switched to gasification and torrefaction. Thus, some types of bioenergy may eventually be interrupted under significant price fluctuations, and the total bioenergy output would deviate from expectations. Second, while green bonds can benefit bioenergy producers by reducing production costs and operating risks, the government pays for the price because the interest payment attached to the low-cost bond will be eligible for tax cuts, implying a potential social loss. Therefore, even if supportive policies could stimulate green development, the administrative authority must carefully evaluate specific green projects’ environmental and economic benefits.

The results indicate that a potential land-use change could occur under sustainable agriculture and bioenergy development. Two insights derived from the findings could be further discussed. First, because some biomass is used for bioenergy production, the well-informed farmers will consider this production possibility and adjust their cropping decisions accordingly, resulting in the land-use change. Nevertheless, while it is adequate to assume that farmers have access to information about commodity prices and policies in modern society, they generally crop based on their own experience and expectations rather than using an economic theory that optimizes net incomes. Because cropland in Taiwan is privately owned and the government cannot enforce the specific cropping decisions for the land, the results of this study can only be used as a guide for the total land-use change under optimal development of agriculture and bioenergy. Second, many economic and environmental factors besides bioenergy accommodation could affect land-use change. For example, Syed et al.75 point out that climate change can substantially affect land productivity, and the benefits from biochar may not recover such a loss in land fertility. Therefore, more studies regarding economic and environmental uncertainty may be necessary.

The results indicate that solar energy can be valuable to Taiwan’s renewable energy development. However, whether such a design could optimize solar energy production while maintaining sustainable agriculture and bioenergy should be further discussed. First, the size of Taiwan’s green bond market is a crucial factor affecting solar energy development. Based on the public data of the Taiwan Security Exchange Corporation,76 the total green bond issuance is generally less than $700 million per year, and only a portion of the capital is issued for green energy projects. Kung et al.36 also point out that the green market in Taiwan is too small to support efficient renewable energy development. Therefore, if there is little access to the green bond market, the financing costs and operating risks facing the firms would increase, thereby reducing the investment in solar energy.

Second, the stability of the green finance policy is crucial. An unstable green finance policy may make the predetermined development schedule inefficient. For example, Xu and Xu77 indicate that green finance policies can substantially affect the allocation of green resources, and Cojoianu et al.78 show that a market with frequently changing finance policies will discourage the green industry. Thus, if the green finance policy fluctuates frequently, the solar energy programs may not achieve their optimal. Because solar development encourages the local economy and power supply, the elected officers always try to promulgate financing policies to support local industries. Since the officers are frequently in and out, regional equity concerns generally make Taiwan’s green finance policy unstable, reducing development efficiency. Notably, such fluctuation in green finance policies not only discourages solar energy development but also deters investment in other green projects such as bioenergy.

Policy implications

Bioenergy development alters the land-use patterns available for large-scale solar energy programs. Thus, this study proposes a sequential framework that successfully links bioenergy-driven land transfer to solar potential, revealing key interactions between energy prices, emission prices, finance, and technology choice. Some insightful results are explored, and each could provide helpful information to decision-makers in formulating and adjusting policies.

The study has some insightful findings that can be used in policy formulation.(1)Effects of technological transition. The result shows that technological transitions would inversely fluctuate bioenergy output and offset emissions. However, because the emission price is primarily determined by the market and the government has little control over it,79,80 its fluctuation is inevitable. Some government interventions should take place to align the proposal goal (i.e., bioenergy maximization or offset maximization). For example, if the goal of Taiwan is to improve energy structure and security, then maximizing renewable energy output would be the priority. Thus, energy suppliers must be provided with certain subsidies or tax cuts to encourage renewable energy output.(2)Promotion of green finance markets. We show that the green bond application benefits both the bioenergy and solar energy industries in terms of investment cost and operating risks. However, Table 1 shows that the green bond market in Taiwan is insufficient to satisfy the needs of renewable energy programs.36,76 The administrative authority of Taiwan should further promote the size of the green finance market to minimize the social losses due to lost opportunities.(3)Capacity distribution and long-term possibilities. Solar energy programs are generally applied based on current land distribution. Once the capacity is designed and installed, there is no chance of altering it in the next 15 to 20 years. Thus, the land use determined in the short run will fix the land use in the long run. Because Taiwan has a small territory and land resources are ultimately valuable, a city suitable for solar energy panels may benefit in the short term but eventually be hurt in the future. Thus, by considering short-term benefits and possible long-term lost opportunities, the government should be careful in placing the largest solar energy capacity in a few cities. If this is the case, then a prudent analysis of land-use change must be conducted, and stable market conditions must be maintained.

The first key finding of this study is that while bioenergy production would vary substantially when emission prices change, a loss in biofuel and biopower production would be compensated by the increase in carbon sequestration attached to biochar. At an emission price of $200 per ton, biopower decreases from 5,810 to 1,640 GWh. However, biochar production and net emission offset will increase by 8.37 million tons and 3.01 million tons, respectively. Technology transition results in this change.

The second key finding of this study is that the solar energy programs on non-cropland could stabilize Taiwan’s renewable energy industry. Variations in energy and emission prices could fluctuate bioenergy production, but net solar energy is relatively stable. Thus, when torrefaction replaces fast and slow pyrolysis and bioenergy production declines, solar energy could still provide up to 1,142 GWh and sequester 1.39 million tons of CO2 emissions.

Third, land-use transfer provides land for solar energy programs, but the installation capacity is not uniformly distributed across cities. In addition, when bioenergy development is simultaneously embedded in the agricultural sector, non-cropland distribution will vary substantially across regions when market conditions change. At the beginning stage, a small installation size will not cause problems; however, it may still reduce development efficiency in the long run.

Limitations of the study

This study has proposed a sequential mathematical programming model to evaluate the development of sustainable agriculture, bioenergy, and solar energy in Taiwan. While the results indicate that Taiwan can improve its energy security while sustaining agricultural practices, several insights regarding the analytical approach merit more discussion. First, the sequential optimization framework separates development stages, and the results of the first stage (i.e., agriculture and bioenergy) will significantly affect the results in the second development stage (i.e., solar energy). The approach specified in the first stage is adequate as delineated in many economic and policy analysis.8,81,82 In the first optimization, the agricultural practices and bioenergy are simultaneously examined, and the equilibrium indicates that we cannot increase one production without reducing the production of the other. However, the decisions made in the second stage have no backward effects, and it is developed based on a given result. Such a modeling framework is due to the concern of food security. According to the Taiwan Ministry of Agriculture,83 the food self-sufficiency rate in Taiwan is a mere 30.3%, the lowest in the past 18 years. Because food is the most fundamental resource for sustaining human life, this study makes it a priority and should be sustained in the first place. However, such a modeling process would subsequently make the solar energy secondary: its development scale is solely dependent on the first stage.

Second, bioenergy and solar energy development efficiency is generally hard to evaluate due to climate conditions. For example, a cumulated precipitation in July 2025 is reported to be 666 to 735 mm in many southern cities,84 resulting in an NT$1.89 billion loss of agricultural commodities and lost harvest for approximately 19,188 ha of cropland.85 Additionally, approximately 150,000 solar panels are also destroyed by Typhoon Danas.86 Because climate disasters such as typhoons and mudslides frequently occur in Taiwan, an appropriately designed economic model cannot accurately predict or estimate Taiwan’s renewable energy potential and development efficiency. While defects are embedded in this approach, this study provides a pilot analysis integrating development strategies with different priorities.

This study also has some shortcomings. It separates bioenergy and solar energy production and allows solar energy development to depend on the land transfer caused by bioenergy. This assumption may be valid from the food security and energy security viewpoint, but it also makes the development of solar energy programs highly reliable for bioenergy development. The administrative authority of Taiwan recently proposed the agrivoltaic program that directly covers the solar panels on the top of crop fields to relax the land resource constraints. However, this application may change the temperature of the covered field and subsequently alter crop yields. Farmers and energy suppliers also claim that it will make the cropping activities and the operation and maintenance of energy suppliers more difficult. Therefore, some scholars suggest solar energy systems should be installed in idle cropland rather than on active cropland. However, the existence of solar panels may prevent cold storms from destroying the crops. This proposal is still under debate because the exact impact of this approach is unrevealed. It provides another viewpoint for researchers to modify the integrated renewable energy development framework.

Method details

Bioenergy development depends on agricultural outputs. Thus, it is necessary to optimize the production of agricultural commodities and energy crops simultaneously to investigate efficient bioenergy paths. Suppose the sum of net gains received by the agricultural and bioenergy markets is maximized. In that case, we can expect the agricultural sector’s social welfare to be maximized simultaneously. Samuelson87 proposes a mathematical programming approach to find multiple market equilibria. This framework has been demonstrated to be valid in many environmental and resource allocation studies, including food security,88 climate risk assessment,89,90 climate impacts on agriculture,91 adaptive and mitigative approaches,92 biofuel analysis,22 and dynamic performance of biochar.93 Because this study links the land transfer to examine efficient bioenergy and solar energy development, we have to evaluate the equilibrium of the agricultural sector and determine the production of energy crops and bioenergy technologies before solar energy programs are put in effect. “Model formulation for bioenergy” delineates the agricultural system and bioenergy possibilities, and “Model formulation for solar energy programs” uses the results of land transfer and proposes the solar energy optimization model.

Model formulation for bioenergy

The equilibrium of the agricultural sector is determined by multiple factors such as domestic consumption, crop yields, subsidy policies, and international trade. Since bioenergy is produced to fuel vehicles and machinery, it can be considered a secondary commodity, such as soybean oil and feedstuffs used to feed animals. Thus, we can simultaneously accommodate bioenergy production into the agricultural sector and determine its optimal output.

Subsidy is another crucial factor to be included because the government usually utilizes different subsidy policies to sustain outputs and farmers’ income.
