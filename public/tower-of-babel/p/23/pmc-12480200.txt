Health literacy strengths and challenges of Australian smokers: a cluster analysis
Authors: Cheryn Coleman, Satish Melwani, Stuart G Ferguson, Rosie Nash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmoking tobacco continues to be a leading cause of death and disease. This study aimed to provide health literacy profiles which include strengths and challenges of Australian adults who smoke, to identify their needs and inform future smoking interventions. We conducted a cross-sectional survey using the Health Literacy Questionnaire to measure and describe the health literacy profiles. A total of 563 Australian adults who smoke completed the questionnaire. Data were analysed using hierarchical cluster analysis with Ward’s method for linkage. Ten clusters were identified as the optimal cluster solution. These clusters had distinct health literacy profiles with diverse needs and demographic characteristics. Most participants self-reported strengths in understanding health information well enough to know what to do, but identified challenges in navigating the healthcare system. This cluster analysis revealed that Australian adults who smoke have varying health literacy competencies and needs. These findings can be used to educate stakeholders and to inform tailored interventions to support people who smoke to quit smoking.Keywords: health literacy, public health, smoking, tailored intervention, tobacco smoking

To our knowledge, this is the first time that the health literacy profiles of Australian smokers have been described.

INTRODUCTION

In 2022, there were 1.245 billion people in the world, aged 15 years or older, who used tobacco products. An estimated 995 million of these tobacco users, were smoking tobacco products on a current basis (World Health Organization 2024). While the number of people who are smoking is decreasing as a proportion of the population, there are more smokers in the world today than ever before (Reitsma et al. 2021). Insights into tobacco use in Australia in 2022 and 2023 found that about 1.8 million (8.3%) people were daily smokers (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2024). Overwhelmingly, the burden of disease and death caused by tobacco can be attributed to smoking. From the available evidence, tobacco smoking has been linked with chronic diseases—cancers, respiratory diseases, cardiovascular disease, metabolic diseases—and premature death (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2014). Therefore, it is vital for the health of smokers and for general population health, to reduce the number of people who smoke (García-Gómez et al. 2019).

Research has identified that behavioural interventions—such as group behaviour therapy, individual behavioural counselling and telephone counselling—and pharmacotherapy, including bupropion and nicotine replacement therapy, are the most effective interventions for smoking cessation (Lemmens et al. 2008). Most smokers who attempt to quit however, do not use these evidence-based approaches (VanFrank et al. 2024), hampering their likelihood of quitting successfully. There is clear evidence that an individual’s ability to make informed health decisions is influenced by their health literacy (Berkman et al. 2010). The World Health Organization has broadly defined the health literacy of an individual as ‘people’s knowledge, confidence and comfort—which accumulate through daily activities and social interactions and across generations—to access, understand, appraise, remember and use information about health and health care, for the health and well-being of themselves and those around them’ (World Health Organization 2022). Addressing health literacy needs can help individuals make better decisions about their health, wellbeing and health care (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care 2014).

The association between health literacy and smoking behaviour has been explored in the existing literature, with most researchers using health literacy tools that provide an overall score and health literacy classification (Stewart et al. 2013, 2014, Hoover et al. 2015, Lin et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023). These studies have divided participants into low or high levels of health literacy (Stewart et al. 2013, 2014, Hoover et al. 2015) or assessed the adequacy of the participants’ health literacy (Lin et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023) and tested the association of this measurement with smoking behaviours. For example, a meta-analysis by Li et al. (2022) and a study by Hoover et al. (2015) both found that individuals with inadequate or low health literacy were more likely to be current smokers. Smokers with lower health literacy were also found to have higher levels of nicotine dependence (Stewart et al. 2013) and more likely to relapse following smoking cessation treatment (Stewart et al. 2014). Simply knowing that people have low or inadequate health literacy, however, is not sufficient to develop solutions to address the health literacy needs of these individuals (Bakker et al. 2021). Rather than focusing solely on gaps or comparing one group’s health literacy to another’s, current research is shifting towards a strengths-based, solution-focused approach. This perspective emphasizes the skills, knowledge, and resources that individuals and communities already possess—and explores how these assets can be leveraged to improve health outcomes (Osborne et al. 2022).

The Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ), a multidimensional health literacy profile tool, has been developed to detect the health literacy competencies or needs of individuals across nine independent scales of health literacy (Osborne et al. 2013). These scales capture the lived experience of individuals when accessing, understanding, and utilizing health information and services to enhance their health (Osborne et al. 2013, Hawkins et al. 2017). The nine HLQ scales remain separate, and can be used to generate profiles that highlight health literacy strengths and challenges (Beauchamp et al. 2017). These profiles can be used to understand and tailor solutions to meet individual health literacy needs (Hawkins et al. 2017). This paper is part of broader programme of research and presents the first of six steps in the Ophelia (Optimizing Health Literacy and Access) approach to developing health literacy interventions—the needs assessment (Batterham et al. 2014).

Recent insights have shown that health literacy is a multidimensional concept (Buchbinder et al. 2011), and that individuals can have health literacy strengths and challenges that influence their engagement with health information and services (Dodson et al. 2015). For example, cluster analysis has been carried out by Aaby et al. (2020) in patients with chronic health conditions such as cardiovascular disease, pregnant women and mothers (Melwani et al. 2023), Australians experiencing inequity (Cheng et al. 2024) and Tasmanians representing a regional population with known educational attainment challenges (Spencer et al. 2024). This cluster analysis technique provides greater insight than can be achieved by examining HLQ averages.

The HLQ has previously been used to assess the health literacy of Australians, including smokers, as part of the 2018 National Health Survey: Health Literacy Survey (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2018). However, this HLQ data were analysed using descriptive statistics, which were reported as mean scale scores for the sample (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2018). Recent public health research is increasingly using cluster analysis to analyse HLQ data, to provide more detailed insights into health literacy patterns among sub-populations (Bakker et al. 2021, Cheng et al. 2024, Melwani et al. 2024). To the best of our knowledge, no other study has analysed HLQ data using cluster analysis to measure and describe the health literacy profiles of Australian smokers. This research contributes novel insights into the health literacy strengths and challenges of Australian smokers. Hence, this study aimed to provide detailed health literacy profiles (strengths and needs) of Australian smokers. These could be used in future research, to inform the design of smoking cessation interventions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants and procedure

This study was conducted using a cross-sectional survey design. Participation was limited to current smokers, defined as someone who smokes cigarettes every day or some days, who were at least 18 years old and a resident of Australia. Participants were recruited in June 2024, using a convenience sampling approach (Liamputtong 2017) via Pureprofile, an online panel provider consisting of people who have pre-registered their interest in completing surveys. Panel members who had previously indicated that they were a smoker and an Australian resident were invited to complete an online questionnaire. Screening questions were presented to panel members to confirm their eligibility. Eligible respondents were provided with information about the study and asked to confirm their consent before completing the questionnaire. Participants who completed the questionnaire were provided compensation by Pureprofile in recognition of their participation. While there are no strict minimum sample size requirements for cluster analysis of the HLQ data, previous research has shown that reliable and meaningful results can be achieved with fewer than 500 participants (Beauchamp et al. 2020, Melwani et al. 2024, Spencer et al. 2024). The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at the University of Tasmania (approval number: H0029737).

Measures

Participants completed a series of questions to gather basic demographic characteristics. Information gathered included their age, gender, Indigenous status, educational attainment, employment status, income, geographic location, living alone, speaking English at home and smoking characteristics (including the length of time they had been a smoker, frequency of smoking, and intention to quit).

The 44-item HLQ, a questionnaire developed using a grounded validity-driven approach with robust psychometric properties (Comparative Fit Index = 0.936, Tucker-Lewis Index = 0.930, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation = 0.076, and Weighted Root Mean Square Residual = 1.698) (Osborne et al. 2013), was used to measure and describe the health literacy profiles of the participants. The items within the HLQ represent nine distinct scales of health literacy (Osborne et al. 2013) (Table 1). The HLQ does not provide a single overall mean score; instead, it offers a separate mean score for each scale. Scales 1–5 are scored using a 4-point scale: strongly disagree [1], disagree [2], agree [3], and strongly agree [4]. Scales 6–9 are scored using a 5-point scale: cannot do or always difficult [1], very difficult [2], quite difficult [3], easy [4], and very easy [5].

Data analysis

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Both the demographic and HLQ data were analysed using a cluster analysis method. Cluster analysis is an analytical approach that helps study multivariate data and identify groups of similar observations (Everitt et al. 2011). The technique used in this study was hierarchical cluster analysis with Ward's method for linkage (Hair et al. 2014, Murtagh and Legendre 2014). This method treats clustering as an analysis of variance, merging clusters that exhibit minimum within-cluster variance (Hair et al. 2014).

The cluster analysis approach was guided by the Ophelia process (Batterham et al. 2014, Beauchamp et al. 2017); as such in this analysis, we also incorporated demographic characteristics for each cluster along with the nine scale scores to identify subgroups. The demographic characteristics provide context for each cluster and help to determine optimal number of cluster solutions (Cheng et al. 2024). This cluster analysis approach has successfully been used in other studies to identify and generate health literacy profiles experiencing similar health literacy strengths and challenges across the nine HLQ scales (Cheng et al. 2024, Melwani et al. 2024, Spencer et al. 2024). Demographics included for this analysis were age, gender, smoking status, intention to quit, education, employment status, weekly income, area of residence (metropolitan or regional/remote areas) identified using the Modified Monash Model (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care 2021), Indigenous status and living status (alone or living with someone).

There are no standard procedures for determining and validating the number of cluster solutions (Hair et al. 2014) and most recommended approaches in the literature are not applicable, as the current method involves both cluster analysis variables and related demographic variables. However, following the Ophelia process (Batterham et al. 2014, Beauchamp et al. 2017), the selection of optimal clustering solutions was guided by examining the standard deviation (SD) of the scores and the demographic data associated with each cluster. Based on criteria established in previous Ophelia-focused studies, an SD >0.6 in one or more HLQ scales indicates significant variation within the cluster (Bakker et al. 2021, Cheng et al. 2024, Melwani et al. 2024). It is important to note that SDs tend to be naturally higher for clusters with a small number of individuals, so this measure should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, small clusters are included in the Ophelia process as they can help to identify hard-to-reach populations (Douglas et al. 2025). To determine the optimal number of clusters, 3–16 cluster solutions were generated. Each cluster split was examined to assess whether the new clusters exhibited different health literacy patterns compared to the parent cluster. A key consideration was whether any single cluster was sufficiently homogeneous to represent a group with relatively uniform health literacy strengths and challenges, enabling meaningful action to support this group. For instance, if a cluster showed high variance in any individual scale scores, meaning it included individuals with both very high and very low scores, a decision needed to be made on whether meaningful action could be taken for this cluster or if splitting the cluster would yield more substantively meaningful results (Cheng et al. 2024). Additionally, two groups with similar health literacy profiles but different demographic characteristics may require distinct solutions to address their challenges. Therefore, examining the demographics of the clusters is informative when designing solutions that promote health equity (Batterham et al. 2014, Cheng et al. 2020).

S.M. conducted the cluster analysis and initially reviewed the clusters with C.C. to identify which solutions best represented the optimal clusters to represent the sample. The distinction between clusters with high score variance was based on the authors’ informal observations, particularly in relation to the demographic characteristics associated with each cluster. The final cluster solutions were reviewed and discussed with other authors. The clusters are presented as means and SD for each of the scale score within each cluster, along with information about the demographic characteristics specific to each cluster.

RESULTS

A total of 563 participants completed the questionnaire. Table 2 displays the demographic characteristics of the study sample. The mean age was 47 years, and just over half the participants identified as female. Most participants spoke English at home, lived in a metropolitan location and were currently employed; approximately half were university educated. The average number of years of smoking was 20.7 years, with approximately half of participants reporting that they smoked every day. With the exception of education, the demographic characteristics of the sample were representative of Australian smokers more broadly (Table 2).

However, 10 clusters were identified as the optimal cluster solution, based on the SD estimation, demographic characteristics and the distribution of health literacy scores. Cluster A: people who self-report strengths across all health literacy scales

Cluster A represents 9.77% (n = 55) of the sample. This cluster had the highest mean scores for seven of the nine HLQ scales, when compared to the other nine clusters. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 3.71, SD = 0.39) and the lowest value was in Scale 5 (appraisal of health information) (mean = 3.60, SD = 0.34). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 4.69, SD = 0.34) and the lowest value was in Scale 8 (ability to find good health information) (mean = 4.59, SD = 0.40).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 47.6 years, had on average been smoking for about 20 years, and had the highest proportion of participants who were not seriously considering quitting smoking, along with the second lowest motivation score to quit (5.7, where 0 was no motivation to quit and 10 was completely motivated). Cluster A also represented the highest average income compared to the other clusters.

Cluster B: people with strengths across most health literacy scales, but face challenges in managing their health and critically appraising health information

Cluster B represents 6.57% (n = 37) of the sample. This cluster had the highest mean scores for the remaining two (Scale 6 and 9) of the nine HLQ scales, when compared to the other nine clusters. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 3.61, SD = 0.36) and the lowest value was in Scale 3 (actively managing my health) (mean = 2.57, SD = 0.39). For Scales 6–9, the highest scores were in Scale 6 (ability to actively engage with healthcare providers) (mean = 4.75, SD = 0.33) and Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 4.75, SD = 0.31) and the lowest value was in Scale 8 (ability to find good health information) (mean = 4.43, SD = 0.56).

Participants in this cluster had the highest mean age of 57.7 years, had on average been smoking for the longest period of time, roughly 33 years, had the highest proportion of participants who smoked 11 or more cigarettes per day, as well as the highest proportion of participants who lived in regional/rural/remote locations. Compared to the other nine clusters, Cluster B had the lowest proportion of participants who were university educated or who were in current employment.

Cluster C: people who have a good understanding of health information and are confident engaging with healthcare providers, but may lack social support and do not feel supported by their healthcare providers

Cluster C, the largest cluster, represents 28.24% (n = 159) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 3.08, SD = 0.30) and the lowest value was in Scale 4 (social support for health) (mean = 2.93, SD = 0.29). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 4.14, SD = 0.37) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.91, SD = 0.30).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 47.9 years, had on average been smoking approximately 19 years, and had the second lowest proportion of participants who smoked every day, or who smoked 11 or more cigarettes per day. This cluster was motivated to quit, with an average motivation to quit score of 6.8, the second highest score when compared to the other clusters.

Cluster D: people who have good social support and appraisal skills, but may encounter challenges in navigating the healthcare system and finding good information

Cluster D represents 4.97% (n = 28) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 3.45, SD = 0.34) and the lowest value was in Scale 3 (actively managing my health) (mean = 2.99, SD = 0.53). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 3.69, SD = 0.45) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.55, SD = 0.29).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 43.8 years, had the lowest proportion of participants who smoked every day and who smoked 11 or more cigarettes per day, and had the highest average score for motivation to quit. This cluster had the lowest proportion of participants who reported living alone, the equal lowest (with Cluster G and Cluster J) proportion of participants living in regional/rural/remote locations, and the lowest proportion of participants who speak English at home. Among the 10 clusters, Cluster D had the highest proportion of participants who were currently employed.

Cluster E: people who are confident engaging with healthcare providers and understanding health information, but may face challenges in actively managing their health and appraising health information

Cluster E represents 12.08% (n = 68) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 3.01, SD = 0.27) and the lowest value was in Scale 5 (appraisal of health information) (mean = 2.40, SD = 0.40). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 4.10, SD = 0.37) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.75, SD = 0.32).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 55.0 years. Only Cluster B had a higher mean age of participants. On average, participants in this cluster had been smoking for 32 years, with 72% of participants, the highest proportion for all clusters, smoking every day. This cluster had the highest proportion (97%) of participants who speak English at home.

Cluster F: people who lack social support and support from their healthcare providers and experience challenges with navigating the healthcare system

Cluster F, the second largest cluster, represents 19.36% (n = 109) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 5 (appraisal of health information) (mean = 2.63, SD = 0.34) and the lowest value was in Scale 2 (having sufficient information to manage my health) (mean = 2.54, SD = 0.33). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 3.69, SD = 0.46) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.30, SD = 0.36).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 42.1 years. Only Cluster J had a lower mean age of participants. On average, participants in this cluster had been smoking for 17 years, with 69% of the participants smoking 10 or less cigarettes per day. This cluster had the highest proportion of participants who were university educated.

Cluster G: people that have sufficient information to manage their health but face challenges in navigating the healthcare system and engaging with healthcare providers

Cluster G represents 7.46% (n = 42) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 2 (having sufficient information to manage my health) (mean = 2.98, SD = 0.16) and the lowest value was in Scale 4 (social support for health) (mean = 2.71, SD = 0.38). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 3.21, SD = 0.46) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.02, SD = 0.34).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 42.4 years and had on average been smoking for 16 years. The majority of participants (93%) lived in metropolitan areas and on average had the second highest weekly income of all the clusters.

Cluster H: people who experience challenges with navigating the healthcare system, do not feel understood or supported by their healthcare provider and have more challenges with social support for health

Cluster H, the smallest cluster, represents 1.95% (n = 11) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 2 (having sufficient information to manage my health) (mean = 2.48, SD = 0.41) and the lowest value was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 1.66, SD = 0.45). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 4.15, SD = 0.42) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 3.03, SD = 0.64).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 48.7 years and had on average been smoking for 26 years. Cluster H had the highest proportion of participants that identified as male and the highest proportion of participants that reported living alone. This cluster had 64% of participants speak English at home, the second lowest among the clusters. More than half the participants, the highest proportion of all the clusters, were seriously thinking of quitting within the next year.

Cluster I: people experiencing challenges across most scales, particularly navigating the healthcare system and finding good health information

Cluster I represents 7.10% (n = 40) of the sample. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 1 (feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers) (mean = 2.62, SD = 0.60) and the lowest value was in Scale 3 (actively managing my health) (mean = 2.25, SD = 0.46). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 2.78, SD = 0.46) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 2.52, SD = 0.39).

Participants in this cluster had a mean age of 42.9 years and on average had been smoking for 18 years. More than half of the participants smoked 11 or more cigarettes per day, the second highest proportion of all the clusters. Compared to the other nine clusters, Cluster I also had the second highest proportion of participants that identified as male, reported living alone and were Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. Most participants (65%) in this cluster were not university educated.

Cluster J: people who encounter challenges across all health literacy scales

Cluster J, the second smallest cluster, represents 2.49% (n = 14) of the sample. This cluster had the lowest mean scores for all HLQ scales, when compared to the other nine clusters. For Scales 1–5, the highest score was in Scale 5 (appraisal of health information) (mean = 1.79, SD = 0.49) and the lowest value was in Scale 4 (social support for health) (mean = 1.59, SD = 0.45). For Scales 6–9, the highest score was in Scale 9 (understand health information well enough to know what to do) (mean = 2.16, SD = 0.93) and the lowest value was in Scale 7 (navigating the healthcare system) (mean = 1.75, SD = 0.55).

Participants in this cluster had the lowest mean age of 35.1 years, on average had been smoking for 12 years, the fewest of all the clusters, and had the lowest average score for motivation to quit. Cluster J had the highest proportion of participants that identified as female and the highest proportion of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander participants. This cluster had the equal lowest (with Cluster D and Cluster G) proportion of participants living in regional/rural/remote locations and the lowest average weekly income compared to the other nine clusters.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a detailed picture of the health literacy strengths and needs of Australian smokers using a multidimensional health literacy tool, the HLQ. Through cluster analysis, 10 clusters with distinct health literacy profiles and demographic characteristics were identified. These health literacy profiles reveal that Australian adults who smoke have varying health literacy competencies and needs. This knowledge extends our understanding of health literacy for this subgroup beyond an average score. Future smoking cessation interventions can therefore be tailored to meet the health literacy needs of the individuals represented in the profiles.

A recent scoping review (Coleman et al. 2024) identified the barriers to smoking interventions experienced by all stakeholders (smokers, service providers and policymakers). These barriers can be combined with the insights from the cluster analysis to develop interventions that are tailored to the individual and designed to overcome the barriers. Some of the barriers identified in the international literature are also identified as challenges in the profiles (e.g. access and service navigation challenges).

The findings from the cluster analysis revealed that not all smokers experience health literacy challenges. Cluster A, for example, self-reported health literacy strengths across all nine HLQ scales, while they still choose to smoke and have little intention of quitting. This result highlights the complexity of health literacy and why it has previously been described as a concept that is content- and context-specific (Sørensen et al. 2019). Furthermore, it has been argued that ‘an individual’s health literacy may vary depending upon the medical problem being treated, the health care provider, and the system providing care’ (Baker 2006). In other words, health literacy is multidimensional. Beyond that this research highlights there are social, commercial and many other determinants that influence a smoker's behaviour. The way individuals and services interact has evolved and interventions need to be co-designed to meet the diverse health literacy strengths and needs of smokers. This cluster of smokers, are in paid employment with good earning capacity, aligning with a recent study by Aw et al. (2024) which found that smoking is not limited to those who are unemployed or uneducated. It will be important to isolate which incentives will work best in this group and what their smoking cessation preferences include.

In contrast to Cluster A, Cluster J represents a group of individuals who experience the most challenges. Significant health literacy challenges were identified for the participants in Cluster J. This cluster had a higher representation of younger, female adults, who identified as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, spoke a language other than English at home, were not university educated and had a lower income. Participants in Cluster J self-reported challenges across all nine scales of the HLQ, particularly in relation to feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers, social support for health, ability to actively engage with healthcare providers and navigating the health system. This data points to these smokers, having both individual and organizational health literacy challenges (Jessup et al. 2018 ). Focusing on individual and organizational change alone is insufficient to address such significant health literacy challenges, rather it calls for a systems approach to meet these needs (Sørensen et al. 2021). This reinforces the importance of the public health approach that calls for simultaneous focus on policy, service and individual level considerations (Van Den Broucke 2014). A systems approach would integrate health literacy into all policies and programmes within schools, workplaces, social services, healthcare and other relevant systems (Bröder et al. 2018). This approach has been shown to create a health environment that promotes equitable, safe and quality health care (Vellar et al. 2017). Using a systems approach could make it easier for smokers, particularly in this cluster, to access the evidence-based cessation services they need to stop smoking.

While there were very distinct health literacy patterns for the 10 clusters identified in this study, the scale that most often received the highest score for Scales 6–9, was Scale 9—understand health information well enough to know what to do. All 10 clusters, self-reported strengths in this health literacy scale. This result implies that smokers, are able to understand written health information, or instructions regarding treatments or medications (Osborne et al. 2013). In other words, they possess basic/functional health literacy skills that enable them to function effectively in everyday situations (Osborne et al. 2013). This is an asset that health professionals and health promotion services should consider in the design of their treatment or promotion interventions (Osborne et al. 2022, World Health Organization 2022).

Conversely, the scale that most often received the lowest score for Scales 6–9, was Scale 7—navigating the healthcare system. Eight of the 10 clusters (Clusters C, D, E, F, G, H, I, and J) representing 84% (471 participants) of the study sample, self-reported having challenges with this scale. This finding suggests that smokers are unable to navigate the healthcare system to meet their health needs and have limited understanding of the resources available to them (Osborne et al. 2013). Previous research confirms this finding, indicating that smokers have poor knowledge and thusly underuse the cessation supports available to them to quit smoking (Bryant et al. 2011). This insight highlights the support smokers need to find, understand and use evidence-based smoking cessation interventions when attempting to quit. Given this healthcare system navigation and access challenge, smoking cessation services and efforts could be provided through non-traditional settings such as libraries, neighbourhood houses, sporting clubs and other venues more easily accessible to the community (Jenkins et al. 2023). Consistent with a systems approach, it has the potential to bring together multiple settings to advance health promotion and health literacy development (Jenkins et al. 2023).

Recommendations and future research

This research highlights the importance of considering the social determinants of health that influence smoking behaviour, such as health literacy and other social demographic factors, when designing interventions. As part of a broader programme of research, these findings are the first step toward the co-design of smoking cessation interventions with key stakeholders. Future research according to the Ophelia process would involve developing data informed vignettes or short stories, using the identified health literacy profiles, to illustrate the lived experience of smokers experiencing diverse health literacy strengths and challenges. These vignettes would then be presented to key stakeholders, including service providers, health professionals, policy makers and smokers, at idea generation workshops, to co-design context-specific practical solutions grounded in local knowledge and expertise (Batterham et al. 2014, Cheng et al. 2020).

Strengths and limitations

The use of cluster analysis in this study offers new insights into the varied health literacy profiles of Australian smokers. These findings enhance our understanding of this subgroup and can be used to co-design tailored smoking interventions. Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the use of self-reported data may have introduced bias and inaccuracies. Secondly, the sample was drawn from an incentivised research panel, which may have contributed to the absence of missing data and potentially caused selection bias. While the sample appears broadly reasonable, it should be noted that participants were more highly educated than is typical for the smoking population, which may impact generalisability. However, the HLQ mean scores closely align with smoking population norms, suggesting that this may not significantly impact the findings. Furthermore, the survey was conducted online and only in English, which may have led to the underrepresentation of smokers with limited internet access or digital literacy, as well as those who speak a language other than English. Additionally, the analysis was conducted using Ward’s hierarchical cluster analysis, a method that involves some researcher subjectivity. To mitigate this, two researchers (S.M. and C.C.) reviewed the data and jointly identified the most meaningful number of cluster solutions. The cluster analysis results were shared with the other authors (S.G.F. and R.N.) for sense checking/review and a comprehensive discussion.

CONCLUSION

This study provides detailed health literacy profiles of Australian adults who smoke. The cluster analysis has shown the diversity of health literacy profiles among Australian smokers. Furthermore, each individual cluster has unique health literacy competencies and challenges. By targeting the unique strengths and needs of different clusters, we believe that smoking cessation programmes can be more effective and efficient, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes. Key stakeholders can use the health literacy profiles identified in this research to generate tailored smoking cessation interventions that meet the needs and preferences of smokers. Tailored interventions will support smokers to overcome the barriers that are currently present at a policy, service and individual level.
