Exploring the role of PD-1 as a marker in drug-refractory epilepsy and its potential indication for valproic acid treatment
Authors: Hong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe immune checkpoint pathway PD-1/PD-L, known for its role in immunosuppression via regulatory T cells (Tregs) and inflammatory signaling, is implicated in the neuroinflammation of drug-refractory epilepsy (DRE). Given the emerging importance of immune dysregulation in epilepsy, identifying immune-related biomarkers may improve diagnosis and guide treatment strategies.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the potential of PD-1 as a diagnostic biomarker in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma and to investigate whether the therapeutic effect of valproic acid (VPA) in intractable status epilepticus (ISE) is mediated through immunomodulation.MethodsA cohort of 74 patients with DRE (46 with partial seizures (PS) and 28 with ISE) and 25 healthy controls was enrolled. PD-1 levels in CSF and plasma were quantified by flow cytometry and ELISA. In a VPA-treatment sub-study of 25 ISE patients, serial samples were analyzed for PD-1+CD4+CD25high Tregs and cytokine (IL-10/IL-6) levels at baseline (d0) and after 48 h (d3). VPA concentrations were determined by LC-MS/MS.ResultsSignificantly elevated PD-1 levels were observed in both plasma and CSF of epilepsy patients compared to controls, with the highest levels in the ISE subgroup. IL-10 and IL-6 levels were also elevated in intractable epilepsy (IE) patients compared to controls. Critically, clinical improvement was associated with a reduction in PD-1+ Tregs at d3, whereas VPA concentrations did not correlate with treatment response. These findings reveal a compartmentalized immune response, with peripheral immunosuppression and localized CNS immune activation in IE.ConclusionThese findings identify PD-1 as a promising immune-inflammatory biomarker for DRE and its severe forms. Moreover, they suggest that the efficacy of VPA in ISE is mediated through the modulation of the PD-1 pathway and related cytokines, rather than a direct concentration-dependent pharmacological effect.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a prevalent chronic neurological condition, affecting more than 70 million individuals globally (Thijs et al., 2019), with an estimated incidence of 56.78 per 100,000 person-years (Bastos and Cross, 2020; Ioannou et al., 2022; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2012). The underlying mechanisms of epilepsy remain incompletely understood, with approximately 60% of cases classified as idiopathic (GBD, 2016, 2019). Furthermore, despite the availability of numerous antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), about one third of patients develop drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), a phenomenon known as intractable epilepsy (IE) (World Health Organization, 2019; O'Connell et al., 2017), which is frequently compounded by psychiatric comorbidities, social stigma, and a substantial economic burden (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2012; Löscher et al., 2020; Foster et al., 2020; Laxer et al., 2014). Notably, the epidemiology and etiology of DRE differ between pediatric and adult populations: developmental and genetic etiologies are more common in children, while structural and acquired causes predominate in adults (Wirrell et al., 2022). Intractable status epilepticus (ISE), a severe and potentially life-threatening form of IE (Perucca et al., 2020), occurs in nearly 40% of patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2012). Current therapeutic strategies for IE include optimization of polypharmacy with newer-generation AEDs, non-pharmacological approaches such as the ketogenic diet, neuromodulation techniques (e.g., vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation), and surgical resection of epileptogenic foci when feasible. However, these interventions are not universally effective, may be associated with significant side effects, and are often inaccessible or unsuitable for many patients (Gakharia et al., 2022; De Caro et al., 2019; Citraro et al., 2021). The diagnosis of IE remains challenging, partly due to the limited sensitivity of electroencephalography, frequently resulting in underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. Therefore, there is a pressing need to identify reliable biomarkers and develop novel therapeutic strategies for this patient population.

Accordingly, major research efforts are directed towards elucidating the mechanisms of IE and developing novel therapies. Despite this focus, the considerable heterogeneity in etiology, pathogenesis, clinical presentation, and prognosis among epilepsy syndromes poses a fundamental challenge (Perucca et al., 2020). Emerging evidence now underscores the critical role of non-neural mechanisms, specifically neuroimmune and inflammatory pathways, in both epileptogenesis and seizure recurrence (Gakharia et al., 2022; De Caro et al., 2019; Citraro et al., 2021; Lindefeldt et al., 2019; Vezzani et al., 2011, 2016; Fabene et al., 2013; Zattoni et al., 2011). Both systemic infections and sterile inflammation can activate immune-mediated processes that facilitate the development and persistence of epilepsy (Wang et al., 2025; Steriade et al., 2021; Palomino-Antolin et al., 2022; Mishra et al., 2025). Autoimmune encephalitis, for instance, is increasingly recognized as a significant etiology of seizure disorders (Ngugi et al., 2013; Greco et al., 2016). These observations underscore the importance of immune dysregulation in IE pathophysiology.

The programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) pathway serves as a key immune checkpoint, maintaining self-tolerance and modulating inflammatory responses through several mechanisms, including inhibition of T-cell receptor signaling and induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Ishida et al., 1992; Boussiotis, 2016; Okazaki and Honjo, 2007). Given the established roles of both innate immunity (mediating rapid but non-specific inflammatory responses through microglia, macrophages, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6) and adaptive immunity (involving antigen-specific T and B cell responses that contribute to autoimmunity and sustained neuroinflammation in epilepsy) (Duthoit et al., 2005), the immunomodulatory function of PD-1 suggests that it could serve as a biomarker and potential immunotherapeutic target in this context. Valproic acid (VPA) is widely used in the management of status epilepticus owing to its favorable safety profile and minimal impact on cardiorespiratory function (Leu et al., 2017; Soria-Castro et al., 2020; Jin and Guo, 2016; Xie et al., 2018; Czapinski and Terezynski, 1998; Uberall et al., 2000; Limdi et al., 2005). Beyond its conventional antiepileptic properties, VPA has been shown to exert immunomodulatory effects in several disease contexts, including cancer and multiple sclerosis, by modulating immunoglobulin production and T-cell differentiation (Azuchi et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2021; Long et al., 2015; Li et al., 2013). Nevertheless, whether VPA influences PD-1 signaling in epilepsy remains unexplored.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that PD-1 could serve as a diagnostic biomarker in both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma of patients with IE, and that the therapeutic efficacy of VPA in ISE may involve modulation of the PD-1 pathway and related immune components. To test this, we quantified PD-1 and IL-10/IL-6 levels in CSF and plasma from IE patients and controls, and further analyzed dynamic changes in ISE patients following 48 h of VPA treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement and study participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No: [2021] Ethical Review [Clinical Research] No. 162). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to enrollment.

Patients with DRE, including those with partial seizures (PS) and ISE, were recruited from the Epilepsy Clinic, Department of Neurology, and Neurological Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Diagnosis and classification of epilepsy were confirmed by two independent neurologists based on the diagnostic criteria established by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) (Kwan et al., 2010; Trinka et al., 2015). All patients underwent comprehensive evaluation, including medical history review, neurological and psychiatric examination, electroencephalography (EEG), and cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to exclude structural abnormalities. IE was defined according to the ILAE consensus proposal (Kwan et al., 2010; Trinka et al., 2015) as a seizure history of at least two years, failure to achieve seizure control with at least three appropriately dosed AEDs, and absence of identifiable structural brain lesions. Non-intractable epilepsy patients were defined as those who achieved seizure freedom with standard AED regimens.

Control participants were recruited from individuals presenting with mild dizziness or headache, who had no history of epilepsy, other central nervous system disorders, psychiatric comorbidities, or use of central-acting medications. Neurological diseases were ruled out through clinical examination, cranial MRI, and CSF analysis. Control groups were age- and sex-matched to the respective patient cohorts.

Exclusion criteria included: psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (pseudo-seizures), secondary epilepsy due to progressive neurological disorders, history of active infection within the past month (including Hepatitis B Virus, HIV, tuberculosis, or syphilis), current or previous malignancy, receipt of immunosuppressive therapy, autoimmune diseases, or chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease).

As outlined in Fig. 1, 238 patients were screened for eligibility. After exclusions (n=128), 110 participants were enrolled. Thirty participants were assigned to the control group, while the remaining 80, comprising the IE cohort, were stratified by seizure severity into a PS group (n=52) and an ISE group (n=31). During the trial, 11 participants were lost to follow-up. The primary reasons were as follows: 5 cases withdrew consent due to personal reasons, 4 were lost to follow-up due to relocation, and 2 were hospitalized due to comorbid conditions. Additionally, 3 cases were excluded due to medication switch following an inadequate response to VPA. Notably, no serious adverse events related to VPA treatment were reported among all patients lost to follow-up. The final analysis thus included 25 controls and 74 intervention participants (46 PS, 28 ISE).Fig. 1Participants flow chart.Fig. 1

VPA treatment protocol for ISE

Patients diagnosed with ISE received standardized VPA therapy over 48 h. Treatment was initiated with an intravenous loading dose of 10–15 mg/kg, followed by continuous maintenance infusion at 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/h via a microinfusion pump. This dosing regimen was selected based on established clinical guidelines for the management of status epilepticus and previously published pharmacokinetic studies demonstrating that steady-state plasma concentrations are achieved within 24-48 h (Hopp and Vossler, 2025; Soni et al., 2025; BMJ Best Practice, 2025; American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP), 2024). The 48-h time point (d3) was chosen to assess early immunomodulatory effects while minimizing confounding factors from prolonged hospitalization or additional interventions. Seizure control time, defined as the duration from treatment initiation to the cessation of clinical and electrographic seizures, was recorded. Patients were continuously monitored for adverse effects and therapeutic response, with no concurrent administration of other long-term AEDs or non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., ketogenic diet, vagus nerve stimulation) during the 48-h study period.

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood (5 mL) was collected into K3EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer®, Becton Dickinson, USA) from all participants. For the ISE subgroup, CSF (2 mL) was obtained via lumbar puncture. For control participants, CSF samples were also collected when clinically indicated for diagnostic purposes (e.g., to rule out meningitis or encephalitis) and when patients provided additional consent for research use. Samples were collected at two time points: baseline (d0, within 24 h post-seizure control) and post-treatment (d3, after 48 h of VPA therapy). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated within 4 h of collection using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation for immediate flow cytometric analysis. Plasma and CSF supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent batch analysis.

Flow cytometry

Freshly isolated PBMCs were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium. Cell viability, assessed by trypan blue exclusion, exceeded 95% in all samples. Cells were stained with a pre-optimized antibody cocktail containing anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD25-PE, and anti-PD-1-APC (all from BioLegend, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing, data acquisition was performed on a BD FACS Calibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). A minimum of 50,000 events were collected within the CD4+ T lymphocyte gate. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1, BD Biosciences).

Cytokine measurement by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Concentrations of IL-10 and IL-6 in plasma and CSF were quantified using commercial double-antibody sandwich ELISA kits (Boster Bioengineering, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. These cytokines were selected based on their established roles in the PD-1/PD-L pathway and their opposing biological functions in inflammation: IL-10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine produced by Tregs and is known to be regulated by PD-1 signaling, while IL-6 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a central role in neuroinflammation and has been implicated in seizure pathophysiology (Mishra et al., 2025; Boussiotis, 2016; Dingledine et al., 2024). The simultaneous measurement of these cytokines allows investigation of the balance between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory responses in the context of PD-1-mediated immunomodulation. Although other cytokines such as IL-1β are also important in epilepsy, we focused on IL-10 and IL-6 to specifically examine this immunoregulatory axis. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve. All samples were assayed in duplicate. Outliers were identified using the Grubbs' test and were only excluded when clearly attributable to technical error (e.g., sample hemolysis); no such exclusions were necessary in the final dataset.

Quantification of VPA by LC-MS/MS

VPA concentrations in plasma and CSF were determined using a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method. Analysis was performed on a SCIEX 4500MD system (SCIEX, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Kinetex C18 column (3 × 30 mm, 2.6 μm) with a gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Mass transitions were monitored in multiple reaction monitoring mode. The assay was validated for linearity, precision, accuracy, and specificity according to international guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for two groups, and one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple groups. For comparisons between two related samples (e.g., pre- and post-treatment in the ISE subgroup), the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson's rank correlation coefficient. Outliers were not excluded from the analysis unless attributable to experimental error. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated PD-1 expression and altered T cell profiles in peripheral blood of IE patients

To investigate immune alterations in the peripheral compartment of IE patients, flow cytometry was employed to quantify immune cell populations in the plasma of study participants. Analysis revealed significant differences between IE patients and controls. Specifically, the percentage of CD4+ T cells was significantly reduced in IE patients, with the most substantial decrease observed in the ISE subgroup (all P < 0.001). Similarly, CD4+CD25+ T cell percentages were markedly lower in IE plasma, showing the greatest reduction in the PS subgroup (all P < 0.001). To assess the inflammatory milieu in the peripheral blood, ELISA was used to measure plasma concentrations of IL-10 and IL-6. Both cytokines exhibited increasing trends across IE groups relative to controls. IL-10 levels rose significantly with seizure severity, reaching peak concentrations in the ISE subgroup (all P < 0.001). Although IL-6 levels were significantly elevated in IE patients compared to controls (P < 0.001), no statistically significant difference was detected between the PS and ISE subgroups (Fig. 2B).

Correlation analyses revealed several significant associations within IE plasma groups. In the PS subgroup, disease duration showed a positive correlation with the percentage of PD-1+CD4+CD25low Tregs (Pearson's r = 0.1856, P < 0.01). A similar positive correlation was observed in the ISE subgroup between disease duration and PD-1+CD4+CD25low Treg percentage (Pearson's r = 0.1695, P < 0.05), as well as between CD4+ T cell percentage and patient age (Pearson's r = 0.1869, P < 0.05). Conversely, a negative correlation was identified between IL-6 levels and seizure frequency in the ISE subgroup (Pearson's r = -0.4655, P < 0.05). No significant correlations were found with gender across any analyzed parameters (Fig. 2C).

Central nervous system immune activation in ISE patients

To determine whether immune alterations extend to the central compartment, flow cytometry analysis of CSF was performed. In contrast to the peripheral blood, the CSF compartment exhibited a distinct immune profile. The PD-1+CD4+CD25high Treg cell counts were significantly elevated in the ISE subgroup compared to controls (P < 0.001). Consistent with localized CNS inflammation, ELISA measurements demonstrated elevated cytokine levels in the ISE subgroup. Both IL-10 and IL-6 concentrations were significantly increased in ISE patients compared to controls (IL-10: P < 0.001; IL-6: P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

Correlation analysis identified several significant associations with age. Positive correlations were observed between age and PD-1+CD4+CD25low Treg percentage (Pearson's r = 0.393, P < 0.05), as well as between age and CD4+CD25high Treg percentage (Pearson's r = 0.457, P < 0.05). Conversely, a negative correlation was found between age and CD4+ T cell percentage (Pearson's r = -0.471, P < 0.05). No significant correlations were detected with gender, seizure frequency, or disease duration for any of the analyzed parameters (Fig. 3C).

VPA treatment modulates PD-1 expression and IL-10 levels in ISE patients

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of VPA, we analyzed dynamic changes in immune parameters following 48 h of VPA treatment. VPA treatment induced significant alterations in PD-1 expression and cytokine profiles. After 48 h of VPA administration (d3), plasma PD-1 expression on both CD4+CD25high and CD4+CD25low Treg subsets decreased significantly compared to d0 (P < 0.001; Fig. 4A). To assess the clinical relevance of these immunological changes, we analyzed correlations between treatment response and immune parameters. Analysis of treatment response revealed significant negative correlations between seizure control time and immunologic parameters. The seizure control time correlated negatively with both CD4+CD25+ Treg levels (Pearson's r = -0.384, P < 0.05) and IL-10 levels (Pearson's r = -0.427, P < 0.05) measured at d3. The seizure control time correlated negatively with CD4+CD25+ Treg levels (Pearson's r = -0.5644, P < 0.01) measured in CSF at d0. The seizure control time correlated positively with CD4+CD25high Treg levels (Pearson's r = 0.4007, P < 0.05) measured in CSF at d0.

To determine whether VPA's effects were concentration-dependent, we analyzed VPA pharmacokinetics. Pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrated that VPA concentrations in CSF were significantly lower than in plasma, with no notable changes observed between d0 and d3. Furthermore, no correlation was found between either plasma or CSF VPA concentrations and seizure control time (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4C and D), suggesting that the therapeutic effects are mediated through immunomodulation rather than direct concentration-dependent mechanisms.

Discussion

This study provides evidence for a compartmentalized immune response in drug-refractory epilepsy, characterized by peripheral immunosuppression and localized CNS immune activation, and demonstrates that the therapeutic efficacy of VPA in ISE is associated with immunomodulation of the PD-1/IL-10 axis rather than direct concentration-dependent effects.

The concept of immune compartmentalization emerged as a central theme from our data: The CD4+ T-cell and PD-1+CD4+CD25low percentages were reduced in peripheral blood, but PD-1+CD4+CD25low percentages were increased in CSF. PD-1 expression on CD4+CD25high Tregs was elevated in both compartments, particularly in ISE, and correlated with increased IL-10 levels. The peripheral blood exhibits a phenotype of “immune silence” or systemic immunosuppression, potentially attributable to T cell exhaustion, apoptosis, or migration to the central nervous system (CNS). In contrast, the CSF compartment is characterized by “locally confined immune activation.” This site represents the “front line” of disease activity, populated by activated yet precisely regulated immune cells.

The selection of IL-10 and IL-6 for this study was guided by their opposing roles in inflammation and their relevance to the PD-1 pathway. IL-10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine produced by Tregs and is known to be regulated by PD-1 signaling, while IL-6 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a central role in neuroinflammation and has been implicated in seizure pathophysiology (Opp et al., 1995; Espinosa and Bermudez-Rattoni, 2001; Lehtimaki et al., 2004). The simultaneous elevation of both cytokines in IE patients—IL-10 increasing progressively with seizure severity and IL-6 elevated but not differing between PS and ISE subgroups—suggests a complex immunoregulatory environment. The marked increase in IL-10, which decreased following seizure control, suggests a compensatory anti-inflammatory response aimed at limiting pro-inflammatory cytokine-mediated damage. However, the concurrent elevation of IL-6 indicates that this compensatory mechanism may be insufficient to fully control inflammation. The high PD-1 expression on Tregs may reflect an exhausted or dysfunctional phenotype, limiting their suppressive capacity despite numerical expansion.

Based on these observations, we propose a model of compartmentalized immunopathology in IE: within the CNS, the PD-1/IL-10 axis collectively establishes a “protective immunosuppressive barrier” designed to limit inflammatory damage and protect neural tissue. However, this same suppressive mechanism may concurrently impede the effective clearance of inflammatory mediators, potentially contributing to disease chronicity. While this model provides a conceptual framework for understanding our findings, we acknowledge that it is primarily based on the observation of increased IL-10, a single anti-inflammatory cytokine, alongside PD-1 upregulation. Other anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., TGF-β, IL-35) and immunoregulatory cell types (e.g., Bregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells) may also contribute to the establishment of this immunosuppressive milieu and should be investigated in future studies. Moreover, the functional consequences of elevated PD-1 on Tregs—whether it represents an exhausted, hyper-suppressive, or dysfunctional phenotype—remain to be fully elucidated through mechanistic in vitro studies.

The immunomodulatory effects of VPA observed in this study are consistent with its known mechanisms of action. VPA modulates immune function by inhibiting excessive T-cell proliferation and differentiation, promoting apoptosis of activated T cells, and regulating cytokine production through inhibition of histone deacetylase (HDAC) and glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3) (Azuchi et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2012). It also dampens dendritic cell responsiveness and influences T-cell differentiation (Leu et al., 2017; Shiah et al., 2005). The results suggest that VPA modulates inflammation by altering the capacity of dendritic cells to polarize distinct T lymphocyte responses, thereby offering a complementary immunoregulatory strategy for treating inflammation-related diseases. These findings indicate that VPA exerts a significant influence on the immune system (Hulkkonen et al., 2004; Ichiyama et al., 2000). Our results demonstrate that VPA treatment in ISE patients reduced PD-1 expression on CD4+CD25high and CD4+CD25low Treg cells in peripheral blood, paralleled by a decrease in IL-10 levels. High post-treatment (d3) IL-10 levels correlated negatively with time to seizure control. These findings suggest that VPA may alleviate seizures by attenuating PD-1-mediated immunosuppression, inhibiting IL-10 production, and enhancing effector and memory T-cell activation, thereby improving the clearance of inflammatory mediators.

Notably, VPA plasma concentrations reached steady state rapidly, with no significant difference between d0 and d3, and no correlation was found between plasma and CSF VPA levels or between drug concentration and seizure control time. Instead, PD-1 expression emerged as a more relevant biomarker for treatment response, supporting our hypothesis that VPA's efficacy in ISE is mediated through immunomodulation rather than direct concentration-dependent effects.

Interestingly, we observed correlations between age and certain CSF immune parameters in the ISE group. Positive correlations of age with PD-1+CD4+CD25low Treg and CD4+CD25high Treg percentages, and a negative correlation with CD4+ T cell percentage, suggest that age-related immunosenescence might influence the local CNS immune landscape in the context of severe epilepsy. This finding underscores the need to consider age as a biological variable in future studies of epilepsy immunopathology.

This model raises critical translational questions. Can PD-1+CD4+CD25low cells or PD-1+ Tregs in the CSF serve as biomarkers to distinguish IE from other encephalitides or to assess disease activity, thereby offering diagnostic utility? Furthermore, it underscores the complexity of immune checkpoint inhibitor (e.g., anti-PD-1 antibody) therapy. While such treatment may “liberate” suppressed effector T cells, it could simultaneously undermine the protective immunosuppressive function of Tregs, carrying the potential risk of exacerbating neuroinflammation. Future therapeutic strategies must, therefore, aim for the precise targeting of specific cellular subsets to achieve clinical benefit without compromising this delicate balance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study did not explore the mechanistic basis of PD-1 regulation in epilepsy. Second, CSF samples were not available for the PS group, limiting central immune profiling. Third, the sample size was constrained by the difficulty of obtaining CSF from IE patients and controls. Fourth, other inflammatory and oxidative stress markers were not assessed. Fifth, the impact of seizure control on PD-1 levels could not be fully analyzed due to uncertain baseline seizure frequencies. Future large-scale, longitudinal studies with comprehensive immune profiling are needed to clarify the role of PD-1 and related pathways in epilepsy and to evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of antiepileptic drugs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study defines a compartmentalized immunopathology in IE, characterized by peripheral immunosuppression and localized CNS activation. We mechanistically link this to PD-1+ T-cellsubsets specifically, PD-1+CD4+CD25low cells in CSF and PD-1high Tregs across compartments—which collectively establish an IL-10-enriched immunosuppressive barrier. However, given that this model is primarily based on IL-10 as a single anti-inflammatory mediator, further studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore additional regulatory mechanisms. Critically, we demonstrate that VPA exerts part of its seizure-control effect by attenuating this PD-1/IL-10 axis and restoring T-cell function, independent of its pharmacokinetics. These findings nominate PD-1 as a compelling biomarker and therapeutic target, while underscoring the necessity for precise immunomodulatory strategies in IE that can disrupt pathological suppression without erasing protective neural mechanisms.
