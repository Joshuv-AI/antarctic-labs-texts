Physics-inspired enhancement framework for industrial digital radiography based on radiation–matter interaction modeling
Authors: Fayu Chen, Guancheng Lu, Wei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial digital radiography images are degraded by scattering that obscures defects, and conventional enhancement methods often sacrifice either noise suppression or detail preservation. This work presents a physics-inspired, annotation-free framework that explicitly separates the scattering component from the detected intensity through a radiation–matter interaction model. A multistage pipeline consisting of attenuation estimation, spatially adaptive scatter modeling, residual scatter removal, edge sharpening, and local contrast enhancement recovers the direct transmission signal. Evaluated on 60 industrial weld radiographs (ship plates, boilers, oil pipelines), the proposed method performs favorably against global histogram equalization, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization, a discrete wavelet transform approach, and a generic convolutional neural network baseline (serving as a lower-bound reference). It achieves the highest average contrast-to-noise ratio among the compared methods (up to 2.94), the lowest naturalness image quality evaluator scores (down to 4.67), and the lowest blind/referenceless image spatial quality evaluator scores (down to 22.37). Ablation studies verify incremental contributions of each stage, while sensitivity analyses on an independent test set and cross-condition validation confirm stable performance under parameter perturbations and across weld categories. The empirically calibrated coefficients are not derived from first principles, and cross-dataset generalization remains a necessary future investigation. The proposed framework provides a new detail-enhancement solution that significantly improves defect visibility and structural fidelity for industrial nondestructive evaluation using X-ray imaging. Introduction

Industrial digital radiography (DR) is an indispensable nondestructive evaluation (NDE) modality in high-stakes manufacturing sectors, including aerospace, automotive, and nuclear energy industries. DR images are conventionally archived in the 16‑bit grayscale digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format, thereby offering a vast dynamic range spanning 0–65,535 and are capable of encoding minute material discontinuities. Paradoxically, this dynamic range poses considerable challenges for defect analysis, as critical indicators, such as micron-scale cracks, voids, or inclusions, frequently manifest as exceedingly faint intensity perturbations superimposed on complex, noise-laden backgrounds. Conventional image enhancement techniques routinely prove inadequate in this context, as they fail to simultaneously achieve effective noise attenuation and faithfully preserve diagnostically relevant details, a trade‑off that becomes critically limiting when confronting subtle, low‑contrast defect signatures.

A review of the established enhancement methodologies reveals persistent and fundamental limitations1. Global histogram equalization (HE), despite its simplicity, often results in excessive noise amplification and global contrast distortion, thereby obliterating the intrinsic intensity relationships essential for material discrimination2,3. Although contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) mitigates some of these drawbacks by operating on localized tiles, it remains susceptible to the generation of block artifacts and exhibits pronounced sensitivity to parameter selection, necessitating careful, often subjective tuning that lacks generalizability across disparate imaging conditions4. Transform-domain approaches, such as those predicated on the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), afford multi-scale analytical capabilities yet introduce distinct limitations: their performance is inextricably linked to the selection of the mother wavelet, and the associated thresholding and reconstruction processes can introduce ringing or checkerboard artifacts, potentially distorting fine structural boundaries5,6.

Recently, deep learning techniques, particularly those leveraging Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have been applied to image enhancement tasks7,8. Despite their capacity to learn intricate mappings, these data-driven approaches face several critical challenges in the industrial DR domain1,7,9. First, they exhibit a strong dependency on copious volumes of high‑quality, accurately annotated training data, the procurement of which is exceptionally costly and labor‑intensive for specialized industrial inspection scenarios9–11. Second, they are prone to generating physically implausible hallucinations or artifacts, particularly when presented with features underrepresented in the training corpus. Most significantly, such models typically operate as "black boxes," devoid of physical interpretability7,12,13.

CNN-based approaches have demonstrated considerable efficacy in processing electron signal imaging modalities, including backscattered electron images for phase segmentation in cementitious materials14 and the microstructural characterization of geopolymer systems15. However, the performance of such methods in the present context of high‑dynamic‑range industrial DR imagery is likely to be comparatively diminished for several substantive reasons. First, the scattering-induced degradation in DR images adheres to well-defined physical principles, specifically the Compton and Rayleigh scattering mechanisms governed by photon–matter interaction dynamics, which purely data-driven models cannot readily capture without explicit physical constraints. Second, the 16‑bit dynamic range and subtlety of defect contrasts in industrial DR imagery diverge markedly from the 8‑bit natural image domain for which many CNN architectures are originally optimized. Third, the scarcity of large-scale annotated industrial DR datasets limits the generalization capacity of such models, particularly when confronted with rare defect morphologies. Fourth, the inherent stochastic nature of X-ray scattering produces spatially correlated noise patterns that differ fundamentally from the additive white Gaussian noise assumptions underpinning many denoising networks. This absence of a transparent nexus between the enhancement process and the underlying X-ray physics undermines user trust and impedes diagnostic reasoning, which is paramount for safety–critical inspections.

Thus, a discernible research gap persists: there is a pressing need for an enhancement framework that can robustly resolve the intrinsic conflict between noise reduction and detail enhancement in high-bit-depth DR imagery without relying on extensively annotated datasets16–19. An ideal solution should be firmly grounded in the physical principles of radiographic imaging to ensure interpretability and reliability while maintaining computational efficiency and adaptability to the substantial variability inherent in industrial components and defect morphologies11,20–22.

To address these limitations, this study proposes a novel physics-inspired enhancement strategy based on a radiation–matter interaction model. Its core principle is to estimate and separate the scattering contribution from the total detected intensity, thereby recovering and enhancing the direct-transmission signal. The principal contributions of this study are threefold. First, a physically motivated scattering estimation and suppression model that directly targets the dominant source of contrast degradation in industrial X-ray imaging is introduced. Its multiplicative structure follows from the radiative transfer equation under the single-scattering approximation, whereas the numerical coefficients are calibrated within physically constrained bounds. These coefficients were not derived from first principles but were selected via a systematic grid search on an independent validation subset, with search ranges informed by physical scaling arguments and prior Monte Carlo studies. Therefore, the model is best characterized as physics-inspired, and its interpretability lies in the explicit separation of the scattering and transmission components rather than in the exact numerical values of its coefficients. Cross-dataset validation was not performed and remains necessary for future investigations. Second, a Gaussian-free multistage processing architecture preserves the high-frequency details that are often blurred by conventional scale-space methods. Third, a robust, interpretable, and annotation-free solution was validated on challenging real-world industrial DR images, providing a reliable tool for practical NDE. For full transparency, the model parameters are explicitly classified in Section "Scatter Matrix estimation method" into three categories: (i) physically derived parameters that follow directly from the Beer–Lambert law; (ii) physically bounded parameters whose admissible ranges are determined by the scaling analysis of the underlying physics; and (iii) fine-tuned parameters selected within these physical bounds using a systematic grid search on a validation subset. The fine-tuned parameters were empirically determined, and their generalizability beyond the tested weld categories and imaging conditions requires future cross-dataset validation.

Related work

Prior studies on X-ray image enhancement and scattering suppression are critically reviewed. Existing methods are surveyed and categorized, their limitations are examined, and the contribution of the proposed framework is positioned accordingly.

Survey of prior work in x‑ray image enhancement and scattering suppression

A substantial body of research has addressed the challenges of X-ray image enhancement and scattering suppression, including conventional image processing, physics-inspired modeling, and modern data-driven techniques.

Histogram‑based methods

Global HE and its adaptive variant, CLAHE, have been extensively applied to radiographic image enhancement. Although computationally efficient, these methods often amplify noise and introduce unnatural contrast distortions, particularly in high-dynamic-range images, where defect contrasts are inherently subtle. Approaches combining guided filters with Lipschitz-constrained CLAHE have been proposed to improve detail rendition; however, even refined histogram-based techniques require careful parameter tuning to mitigate artifact introduction. The fundamental limitation of such methods lies in their purely intensity-driven nature, which disregards the underlying physical processes governing image formation.

Transform‑domain methods

Techniques utilizing the DWT afford multi-scale analysis capabilities and have been employed for detail preservation in X-ray imaging. Wavelet‑domain feature compensation networks for sparse‑view X-ray computed laminography illustrate the continued relevance of wavelet‑based approaches while highlighting their inherent dependence on appropriate mother wavelet selection and thresholding parameters. Reconstruction artifacts, including ringing and blocking effects, remain a persistent concern with these methods. Other multi-scale frameworks, such as those based on contourlets or shearlets, have been explored in medical imaging contexts but have seen limited adoption in industrial DR because of their computational overhead and parameter sensitivity.

Physics‑based scattering correction

In the domain of scattering suppression, physics‑based methodologies have been proposed to explicitly model the physical processes underlying image degradation. Comprehensive reviews of X-ray scatter correction methods for digital radiographic imaging catalogue various approaches, including anti-scatter grids, post-processing algorithms, and model-based corrections. These methods are based on the understanding that the detected intensity comprises both a direct transmission component governed by the Beer–Lambert attenuation law and a scattering component arising from Compton and Rayleigh interactions. Hybrid approaches integrating deep learning with wavelet decomposition for X-ray scatter artifact correction demonstrate the potential of combining physical insights with data-driven techniques. However, such hybrid approaches still require representative training data, limiting their applicability in scenarios in which annotated datasets are scarce. Image reconstruction methods based on adaptive-weighted bilateral relative total variation for computed laminography further exemplify the trend toward physics-informed regularization in radiographic image reconstruction.

Deep‑learning‑based enhancement

Deep‑learning‑based approaches have garnered considerable attention for X‑ray image enhancement tasks. CNN-based models have been proposed for denoising and super-resolution of radiographic images. Improved KBNet architectures for industrial digital radiographic image denoising have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance while acknowledging their dependency on curated training datasets. Latent space diffusion models for digital radiography image super-resolution enhancement achieve impressive generative capabilities but entail substantial computational overhead and training data requirements. Recently, multi‑scale feature fusion‑enhanced intelligent defect detection systems for X‑ray weld images employing CNN–Transformer hybrid architectures have illustrated the trend toward attention‑augmented models for industrial inspection tasks. Recent publications from 2024 and 2025 have further advanced this field. Fine-grained tiny defect detection frameworks for spiral welds that combine semantic discrimination and contrast transformation have been proposed. Practical pose estimation methods for industrial X-ray radiography based on deep neural networks and local template matching have been developed. Comprehensive reviews of artificial-intelligence-assisted nondestructive testing (NDT) of welded joints have been compiled. Investigations into resolution enhancement through extended image restoration methods in Bragg-coherent X-ray diffraction imaging have been conducted. Notably, novel hybrid YOLO approaches incorporating dual-layer templates and attention mechanisms for precise paper defect detection illustrate the efficacy of attention-augmented architectures for surface defect identification23. In a related vein, multimodal fusion techniques have been explored for defect detection applications, wherein optimized LiDAR and camera data are integrated to enhance the detection accuracy24. Attention mechanisms have been successfully integrated into object detection frameworks for diverse applications, including anti-bird collision systems25. Other relevant contributions include the application of adaptive Fourier decomposition and deep learning techniques for spirometry interpretation26 and the development of large language models for intelligent recommendation systems27. Although these studies do not directly address DR image enhancement, they exemplify broader trends in the integration of domain‑specific knowledge with advanced computational methods, which is a philosophy that aligns with the physics‑inspired approach proposed herein.

Limitations of prior work and positioning of the proposed method

Despite the advances achieved by purely data-driven, physics-based, and hybrid enhancement methods, several notable limitations persist. Purely data-driven approaches—including CNNs, transformers, and diffusion models—require large annotated datasets and offer limited physical interpretability, rendering them unsuitable for safety–critical industrial applications where transparency and reliability are paramount. Although interpretable, physics-based methods rely on simplified assumptions or empirical parameterizations that restrict generalization across diverse imaging conditions. Moreover, most existing techniques are optimized for 8-bit natural images or specific medical imaging contexts and do not transfer to the high-dynamic-range, low-contrast regime characteristic of industrial digital radiography. Although hybrid approaches are designed to bridge this gap, they remain dependent on training data and thus inherit similar constraints.

The proposed method differs from prior work in the following respects. First, it explicitly models the scattering component from radiation–matter interaction principles, thereby addressing a principal source of contrast degradation, a characteristic shared with physics-based correction but realized without requiring annotated data. Second, it eliminates the need for annotated datasets, thereby avoiding a key limitation of deep learning methods. Third, it adopts a Gaussian-free multistage architecture designed to preserve high-frequency details that are often attenuated by conventional scale-space techniques, which distinguishes it from typical histogram-based and transform domain approaches. Fourth, it provides physical interpretability through the explicit estimation and separation of scattering and direct transmission components, a feature that is not available in standard black-box deep-learning models. Fifth, its parameters are explicitly categorized as physically derived, physically bounded, or empirically fine-tuned; the fine-tuned parameters are determined using a systematic grid search within physically constrained ranges, maintaining a connection to the underlying physics while acknowledging the empirical aspect of coefficient selection. Sixth, unlike many recent transformer- and diffusion-based enhancement models that typically rely on GPU acceleration, the proposed method can perform inference using only standard CPU resources, thereby facilitating its deployment in industrial settings. Consequently, the proposed framework offers a complementary alternative to conventional image processing and deep learning paradigms, seeking to balance performance, interpretability, and practical deployability of the models.

Proposed method

The proposed physics‑inspired framework integrates physical imaging principles with digital processing in a multistage pipeline: inversion of the Beer–Lambert law to obtain attenuation coefficients, estimation of relative material thickness, construction of a spatially varying scatter model, recovery of the direct transmission matrix, residual-scatter removal, gradient-based edge sharpening, adaptive S-curve contrast enhancement within local neighborhoods, and final normalization to a 16‑bit unsigned integer format.

Principles of proposed method

The proposed method is based on a multistage enhancement framework that integrates physical imaging models with digital image processing. Its central principle lies in the estimation and separation of the scattering component from the total intensity image received by the detector, followed by the restoration and enhancement of the direct transmission component. The grid search is guided by a composite objective that directly reflects the dual aim of the scattering correction: to enhance the visibility of defects while preserving the perceptual naturalness of the radiographic image. This objective maximizes the mean contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and simultaneously penalizes the mean naturalness image quality evaluator (NIQE). The CNR quantifies the signal strength of a defect relative to the local background noise, thereby capturing the defect conspicuity. The NIQE is a no-reference perceptual metric that measures the deviation of an image’s statistical regularities from those observed in natural undistorted images, with a lower NIQE indicating higher perceptual naturalness. These two components are balanced by attaching a relative weight of 0.2 to the NIQE penalty term. The formal definitions of these metrics, their detailed computation, and the rationale for adopting no-reference measures over full-reference alternatives, such as PSNR or SSIM, are presented in Section "Evaluation metrics". By using these quantitative, task-aware metrics as the optimization criterion, parameter selection is anchored to the practical objectives of the enhancement pipeline rather than being an arbitrary or purely heuristic choice. Critically, the 20-image validation subset employed for this calibration was completely disjoint from the 60-image test set used for all reported performance evaluations, thereby eliminating any risk of data leakage between parameter tuning and final assessment.

In summary, all model coefficients are either physically derived, physically bounded, or fine-tuned strictly within the physically justifiable ranges. The fine-tuning process is transparent, reproducible, and driven by explicit image quality objectives that are defined and justified within the manuscript. Nonetheless, it is acknowledged that the empirical nature of the coefficient calibration limits the demonstrated optimality to the weld categories examined in this study; generalization to substantially different materials or imaging conditions would require a comparable calibration protocol on representative data.

Direct transmission matrix estimation method

The scattering component was subtracted from the total intensity to obtain the direct transmission component. The objective was to recover the direct transmission information that reflects the true attenuation characteristics of the object as follows:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(x,y)={I}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x,y)-{I}_{\mathrm{scatter}}(x,y)\end{array}$$\end{document}

To maintain physical plausibility, the following constraints were applied:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(x,y)=max(0.1\cdot {I}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x,y),min({I}_{\mathrm{norm}}(x,y),{I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(x,y)))\end{array}$$\end{document}where the constant 0.1 is a lower bound that ensures that the direct transmission is not less than 10% of the total intensity, thereby avoiding information loss from over-subtraction. This value is based on the physical consideration that the direct transmission component should not be excessively small. This formula achieves an initial separation of the scattering and direct components based on a scattering model with constraints that ensure physical plausibility.

Residual scattering removal method

This stage removes residual scattering noise or "white fog," from the direct transmission matrix based on the local standard deviation. The objective was to eliminate residual scattering and enhance the image clarity. The window size for local statistics was selected through a systematic sensitivity analysis on the validation subset (20 images), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w\in \{\mathrm{3,5},\mathrm{7,9}\}$$\end{document} were evaluated. The 5× 5 window was chosen as it provided the best compromise between statistical robustness and spatial localization. Specifically, 5× 5 yielded the highest average CNR (2.81) compared with 3× 3 (2.74), 7× 7 (2.78), and 9× 9 (2.72), while maintaining a processing time below 2.5 s per image.

First, for each pixel (x,y), the standard deviation of the intensity values within its 5 × 5 neighborhood is calculated as follows:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{\overline{I}}_{{\mathrm{transmission}},\Omega }(x,y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in {\Omega}_{5\times 5}} {I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(i)\end{array}$$\end{document}where n denotes the number of pixels in the neighborhood (n=25 for a 5 × 5 window), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{I}}_{{\mathrm{transmission}},\Omega }(x,y)$$\end{document} denotes the mean attenuation of the neighborhood. Then,17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{\mathrm{local}}(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in {\Omega}_{5\times 5}} ({I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(i)-{\overline{I}}_{{\mathrm{transmission}},\Omega }(x,y){)}^{2}}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Subsequently, a dehazing operation was performed:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{cleaned}}(x,y)={I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}(x,y)\cdot exp(-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{local}}(x,y))\end{array}$$\end{document}

This operation is derived from a statistical model of the residual error. After the initial scatter subtraction, residual scatter manifests as a spatially correlated noise component whose local variance is proportional to its mean intensity, a characteristic of Poisson–Gaussian mixed noise models encountered in X‑ray detectors. Under this heteroscedastic noise assumption, the local standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\mathrm{local}}(x,y)$$\end{document} serves as a proxy for the residual scatter intensity. The exponential decay operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{local}})$$\end{document} constitutes the maximum‑likelihood solution for suppressing a noise component whose log‑likelihood is proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{local}}\cdot {I}_{\mathrm{transmission}}$$\end{document} under a simplifying uniform prior, analogous to a Bayesian MMSE estimator for signal‑dependent noise where the exponential form emerges naturally from the Poisson likelihood.

Edge sharpness enhancement method

The edge clarity at the material interfaces was enhanced by gradient magnitude weighting. The objective was to sharpen the edges and improve the visibility of the structural contours. First, the gradient magnitude is calculated as follows:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}M(x,y)=\sqrt{{G}_{x}(x,y{)}^{2}+{G}_{y}(x,y{)}^{2}}\end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{x}(x,y)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{y}(x,y)$$\end{document} denote the horizontal and vertical gradient components, respectively, computed using a 3 × 3 Sobel operator. To ensure that the gradient magnitude is dimensionally and dynamically commensurate with the intensity image regardless of the absolute gradient scale, a global normalization is first applied to the gradient map:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}\tilde{M}(x,y)=M(x,y)\cdot \frac{{\sigma}_{{I}_{\mathrm{cleaned}}}}{{\sigma}_{M}+\epsilon }\end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{{I}_{\mathrm{cleaned}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{M}$$\end{document} denote the global standard deviations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I}_{\mathrm{cleaned}}$$\end{document} and M, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon ={10}^{-6}$$\end{document} prevents division by zero. Edge enhancement is then performed as follows:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{edge}}(x,y)=(1-\alpha )\cdot {I}_{\mathrm{cleaned}}(x,y)+\alpha \cdot \tilde{M}(x,y)\end{array}$$\end{document}where α denotes the edge enhancement factor, set to 0.1 based on classical unsharp masking theory, where the weight corresponds to the fraction of high‑frequency detail added to the original image. This fixed value avoids artifacts while providing perceptible edge accentuation, and the prior normalization ensures that the weight is meaningful across different contrast ranges.

Detail contrast enhancement method

The local detail contrast was enhanced using an adaptive S curve. The objective was to expand the local grayscale dynamic range and highlight the subtle details. For each pixel, a normalized value is computed within its 5 × 5 neighborhood (the choice of this window size is justified in Section "Residual scattering removal method" through a systematic sensitivity analysis) as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}v(x,y)=\frac{{I}_{\mathrm{edge}}(x,y)-{I}_{min}(x,y)}{{I}_{max}(x,y)-{I}_{min}(x,y)+\epsilon }\end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{n}}(x,y)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}(x,y)$$\end{document} denote the minimum and maximum intensities in the 5 × 5 neighborhood centered at (x,y), respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon ={10}^{-6}$$\end{document} prevents division by zero. An S-curve transformation is applied as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{v}{\prime}(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+{e}^{-k\cdot (v(x,y)-0.5)}}\end{array}$$\end{document}where k denotes the detail contrast enhancement factor, with an empirically recommended value of 20, selected from the range10,30 based on validation performance to ensure noticeable enhancement without saturation artifacts. The transformed value is mapped back to the original range as follows:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{enhanced}}(x,y)={I}_{min}(x,y)+{v}{\prime}(x,y)\cdot ({I}_{max}(x,y)-{I}_{min}(x,y))\end{array}$$\end{document}

The following blending operation was performed:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{I}_{\mathrm{detail}}(x,y)=0.5\cdot {I}_{\mathrm{edge}}(x,y)+0.5\cdot {I}_{\mathrm{enhanced}}(x,y)\end{array}$$\end{document}

The S curve stretches the local grayscale range to enhance the detail contrast, whereas equal-weight blending maintains an overall natural appearance and prevents over-enhancement artifacts.

The experimental framework encompasses the dataset, the comparative methods, and the evaluation metrics. Comparative methods and parameters

A rigorous comparative framework was established to evaluate the performance of the proposed method by incorporating representative mathematical methodologies and a deep learning baseline for image enhancement to ensure a comprehensive assessment. Four conceptually diverse techniques were selected as benchmarks: a generic CNN-based model28, a DWT-based approach29, CLAHE30, and global HE31. All methods were executed under standardized conditions using default or widely cited parameter configurations from the literature to guarantee typical and reproducible performance.

The generic CNN-based method employed was adapted from a denoising CNN originally designed for grayscale image enhancement. The network architecture comprised 17 convolutional layers with 64 feature maps per layer, each employing 3 × 3 convolutional kernels, followed by batch normalization and rectified linear unit activation functions. This architecture was adapted from generic CNN-based image enhancement designs and is distinct from hybrid transformer-CNN models such as DPFINet28. Citation28 is retained solely to acknowledge prior work on deep-learning-based image enhancement and does not imply the direct adoption of that specific model. The network was trained on a dataset of 1,202 images, partitioned into 70% for training (841 images), 15% for validation (180 images), and 15% for testing (181 images). The dataset encompasses diverse industrial DR scenarios, including weld inspections, casting evaluations, and composite material assessments. All images were preprocessed to a standardized dimension of 512 × 512 using bicubic interpolation and normalized to the range [0, 1] before training. The network was trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10^−4, batch size of 16, and mean squared error loss function. Training was performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU with an average training duration of approximately 4.2 h. During inference, the input images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels, processed by the network, and then resized back to their original dimensions. Because this generic architecture was originally developed for 8‑bit natural images and is applied here without architectural adaptation to 16‑bit DR data, its performance should be regarded as a lower-bound reference for deep learning methods on this task, rather than as an optimized or state-of-the-art solution. Comparisons with domain-adapted architectures specifically designed for industrial DR (e.g., improved KBNet, transformer-based models) are deferred to future work.

The DWT-based method uses a three-level Daubechies‑4 decomposition and applies soft thresholding to the detail coefficients, with the threshold at each level based on the MAD of the coefficients at that level. A uniform reconstruction gain factor of 1.5 was applied to enhance the contrast of the reconstructed image. The CLAHE was configured with a clip limit of 2.25 and a tile grid size of 8 × 8 pixels. These parameters were selected based on common practices in the radiographic image enhancement literature. Global HE was applied directly to the 16‑bit intensity data without modification using the standard cumulative distribution function mapping. For the proposed method, the edge enhancement factor α was set to 0.1, and the detail contrast enhancement factor k was set to 20. The other parameters are specified in Section "Proposed method".

Evaluation metrics

Enhancement performance was quantified using three no-reference metrics—CNR, NIQE, and BRISQUE—which capture defect detectability, perceptual naturalness, and overall image quality, along with SF improvement as a complementary measure of detail enhancement. No‑reference metrics are suitable because full‑reference metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM) penalize effective enhancement by rewarding similarity to the unenhanced original. These metrics are established in the image quality assessment literature and have been validated for enhancement evaluation in X‑ray CT reconstruction32, physics‑based medical X‑ray enhancement33, classical sharpening studies34,35, and biometric hand‑ and finger‑vein recognition36.

The CNR is a standard metric in radiographic imaging that quantifies the detectability of a feature concerning its surroundings. It is defined as27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{C}}{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{|{\mu}_{\mathrm{defect}}-{\mu}_{\mathrm{background}}|}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{background}}}\end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu}_{\mathrm{defect}}$$\end{document} denotes the mean pixel intensity within a manually annotated defect region of interest, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu}_{\mathrm{background}}$$\end{document} denotes the mean intensity of a neighboring background region of interest of equal size, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\mathrm{background}}$$\end{document} denotes the standard deviation of the background region of interest. For each test image, the CNR values were computed for three representative defect regions and averaged to obtain a per-image score. Higher values indicate improved conspicuity and enhanced defect detectability.

The NIQE is a no-reference image quality metric that evaluates the naturalness of an image by measuring deviations from the statistical regularities observed in natural scenes. It operates by extracting quality-aware features from image blocks at multiple scales, fitting a multivariate Gaussian model to these features, and computing the distance between the feature distribution of the test image and that of a pristine natural image corpus. Lower NIQE values indicate higher perceptual naturalness and fewer distortions.

The BRISQUE is a no-reference image quality metric that assesses perceptual quality by comparing an image with a default model derived from natural scenes with similar distortions. It extracts natural scene statistics in the spatial domain and employs a support vector regressor trained on human opinion scores to generate quality scores. The score typically ranges from 0 to 100, with lower values indicating better perceptual quality. Recent improvements to the BRISQUE framework have been proposed that incorporated frequency-domain features derived from the modulation transfer function, demonstrating an enhanced correlation with perceptual quality in benchmark databases37.

The SF was retained as a complementary metric to quantify the overall activity and textural richness of the enhanced images. The SF measures the intensity variations across spatial domains and serves as an indicator of image sharpness and detail. Parameter sensitivity and cross‑condition validation

To address concerns regarding the empirical selection of the model parameters and their generalization across different imaging conditions, a two‑pronged sensitivity analysis was conducted.

First, a standard sensitivity analysis evaluated the impact of variations in the key empirical parameters on the enhancement performance, as measured by CNR and NIQE. The parameters examined included the scattering factor coefficients (Eqs. 6–8), the exponential decay coefficient (Eq. 12), the thickness exponent (Eq. 13), and the clipping bounds for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{s}$$\end{document} (Eq. 11). For each parameter, the value was varied within ± 30% of its nominal setting, while holding all other parameters constant. To avoid circular validation, this assessment was conducted on the entire independent test set of 60 images; the nominal parameter values were previously selected using a separate validation subset of 20 images (Section "Scatter Matrix estimation method") that does not overlap with the test set. It must be noted that the sensitivity analysis demonstrates only that the model performance is not brittle with respect to parameter choice within the examined perturbation range; it does not establish that the parameters possess intrinsic physical meaning.

Second, to evaluate cross‑condition generalization within the available data, the 60 test images were partitioned into three groups according to weld type: ship plate (20 images, thickness ~ 33 mm), boiler (20 images, thickness ~ 38 mm), and oil pipeline (20 images, thickness ~ 26 mm). These groups represent substantially different material thicknesses and imaging geometries. The proposed method, with the same fixed parameter set (no re‑tuning), was applied independently to each group, and the average CNR and NIQE were computed. The results showed that CNR varied from 2.71 (boiler) to 2.94 (oil pipeline), and NIQE from 4.67 (oil pipeline) to 5.03 (boiler), indicating that the performance remained consistently high across the three distinct conditions, with relative variations of less than 8% for CNR and less than 7% for NIQE. This cross‑group stability suggests that the empirically selected parameters are not over‑fitted to a single weld type within the examined corpus; however, it does not constitute validation across different materials, imaging systems, or external datasets.

Discussion

This discussion critically interprets the experimental findings and situates the physics-inspired framework within industrial radiographic image enhancement. The core innovation, empirical validation, performance hierarchy, limitations, and future directions are examined in sequence.

Core innovation and paradigm

The proposed paradigm explicitly models and subtracts the scattering component, which is the principal physical source of contrast degradation, using a radiation–matter interaction model, thereby departing from purely data-driven and conventional signal-processing strategies. The numerical coefficients of the scatter model were empirically calibrated rather than derived from first principles. Therefore, the interpretability stems from the explicit decomposition of the detected signal into physically meaningful scattering and transmission constituents, rather than from the fundamental physical significance of the coefficients. Sensitivity and cross-condition analyses confirmed operational stability; however, the model remained a physics-inspired engineering approximation. To address the inherent conflict between noise suppression and detail preservation, the multiplicative scattering model recovers the direct transmission signal through global statistics, local attenuation, and thickness factors, providing a physically interpretable enhancement pathway. The multistage architecture preserves high-frequency details that Gaussian-smoothing-based scale-space methods often compromise. Categorizing parameters as physically derived, physically bounded, or fine-tuned ensures transparency, and cross-condition validation demonstrates that fine-tuned values are not over-fitted to a single weld type. This integration of physical grounding and tailored processing yields a transparent framework suited for safety–critical inspection tasks, where interpretability is paramount.

Empirical validation

Systematic experiments on 60 industrial DR images from three weld inspection scenarios validated this method. It consistently outperformed HE, CLAHE, a DWT‑based method, and a generic CNN baseline across CNR, NIQE, BRISQUE, and SF metrics, achieving the highest average CNR (2.71–2.94) and the lowest NIQE (4.67–5.03), and BRISQUE (22.37–24.86). The modest but consistent improvement over the generic CNN (CNR gains of 3.9–5.0%) demonstrates that the physics‑inspired framework can match and slightly surpass a well‑trained generic deep‑learning model while offering interpretability and annotation‑free operation. An ablation study identified the scatter estimation stage as the largest contributor. Sensitivity analysis and cross-condition validation confirmed robustness: all parameters exhibited less than 8% CNR variation across a ± 30% perturbation range and across the examined weld categories.

Performance hierarchy and advantages

A clear performance hierarchy emerged: HE yielded the lowest metrics, followed by CLAHE (modest gains, noticeable artifacts), the DWT‑based method (intermediate performance, wavelet‑specific artifacts), the generic CNN (strong but a lower‑bound reference), and the proposed method, which achieved the highest scores across all the metrics. The proposed method provided a small but consistent improvement over the generic CNN and substantial gains over conventional techniques. Its principal advantages are physical interpretability, independence from large annotated datasets, computational efficiency (2.34 s per 2048 × 1800 image on a standard CPU), and enhanced subtle defect visibility without severe artifacts.

Limitations and scope

Several limitations of the present study are acknowledged.(i)The numerical coefficients of the scatter model were calibrated empirically and validated exclusively on the weld datasets examined herein; their transferability to dissimilar materials (e.g., aluminum alloys and composites) and to different imaging configurations therefore requires external cross‑dataset verification.(ii)The model rests on the assumptions of material homogeneity and predominant single scattering; consequently, its fidelity is likely to deteriorate when applied to highly heterogeneous composites or regimes dominated by multiple scattering.(iii)A fixed 5 × 5 neighborhood was employed throughout for residual scatter removal and local contrast enhancement; adaptive window sizing, governed by the image resolution and noise characteristics, could yield further refinement.(iv)NIQE and BRISQUE, which are trained on natural image statistics, may incompletely capture the quality attributes of industrial digital radiography images; therefore, task‑specific metrics directly aligned with defect detectability are warranted.(v)The generic CNN baseline, adapted from an 8‑bit natural‑image architecture, constitutes only a lower performance bound; comparisons with state-of-the-art domain-adapted models, such as transformer-based and diffusion-based architectures, have been deferred to future investigations.

Future directions

Future work should prioritize a refined scattering model, potentially incorporating iterative or deep‑learning‑augmented components, to accommodate complex multi‑material geometries and polychromatic effects. Systematic cross‑dataset validation on external radiographs with diverse materials and acquisition parameters is essential to assess parameter transferability. A comparative evaluation against advanced domain-adapted deep architectures will clarify the trade-offs between physics-inspired and data-driven paradigms. Integrating physics-based stages as differentiable layers within deep networks offers a route to combine interpretability with representational power. Extending the scattering separation principle to modalities such as X-ray CT and ultrasonic imaging, where an analogous superposition of scatter occurs, constitutes another promising research direction. Finally, adaptive parameter selection driven by real-time image content analysis enhances automation and deployability by eliminating manual calibration.

Conclusion

This study presents a physics-inspired enhancement framework for industrial digital radiography. The proposed method is grounded in radiation–matter interaction, where the scattering component is explicitly modeled and subtracted from the detected intensity to recover the direct transmission signal. The scatter model employs a physically motivated multiplicative form; its coefficients are empirically calibrated within physically justified bounds and explicitly treated as fine-tuned parameters rather than first-principles constants. The architecture comprises a Gaussian-free multistage pipeline comprising attenuation estimation, scattering modeling, residual removal, edge sharpening, and adaptive detail contrast enhancement. The parameters are categorized as physically derived, physically bounded, or fine-tuned, clarifying their origins and limitations.

The method was validated on 60 industrial DR images from ship plate, boiler, and oil pipeline weld inspection. Compared with conventional, transform-domain, and generic deep learning benchmarks, the method attained the highest average CNR (up to 2.94), lowest NIQE (4.67), lowest BRISQUE (22.37), improved spatial frequency, and lowest metric variances. Ablation experiments confirmed the contribution of each stage, and the scattering estimation stage produced the largest performance gain. Parameter sensitivity analysis on the independent test set and cross-condition validation across the three weld categories indicated that the performance varied by less than 8% under parameter perturbations and across conditions. It is acknowledged that this analysis does not confer physical significance on the empirical parameters and that external cross-dataset validation is required to assess broader generalizability.

This study makes three contributions. First, a physically motivated scattering estimation and suppression model is introduced to address contrast degradation, featuring explicit parameter categorization, cross-condition validation, and transparent acknowledgement of empirical coefficient selection. Second, a Gaussian-free processing pipeline was established to preserve high-frequency details. Third, an interpretable, annotation-free solution was validated using challenging industrial DR images. The framework provides a tool for enhancing defect detectability in nondestructive evaluation, linking physical interpretability with image-enhancement performance. Future work will extend the framework to more diverse materials and imaging conditions, include systematic cross-dataset validation, and conduct comparative evaluations against emerging transformer- and diffusion-based enhancement approaches.
