Association between vitamin K intake and periodontitis in American elderly population: a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data
Authors: Long Zhu, Si Liang, Yang Yang, Ziqi Su, Lijia Ou, Weimeng Zhang, Binjie Liu, Ousheng Liu, Hui Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn increasing amount of data has been acquired on the close relationship between Vitamin K intake and the progression of periodontitis. The purpose of this study was to estimate whether Vitamin K intake is associated with periodontitis among older adults in America using information from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis of data on periodontal indices and vitamin K intake among American adults aged 60 years and older in the 2009–2014 NHANES database was extracted. Periodontitis was defined according to CDC/AAP criteria, classification of periodontitis and severity of periodontitis according to the degree and amount of attachment loss in the interproximal site, and vitamin K intake was categorized using optimal cutoff value. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to investigate the association between vitamin K intake and the risk of periodontitis.ResultsBased on the information analysis of 2303 participants who met the inclusion criteria, the risk of periodontitis was lower in the elderly population with higher vitamin K intake. Especially, a statistically significant reduction in the prevalence of periodontitis was observed in elderly populations with vitamin K intake greater than 89.51 mg/d (OR = 0.648,95%Cl = 0.502–0.837, P value = 0.001) compared to those with vitamin K intake less than 89.51 mg/d. Subgroup analyses revealed that this association was more prominent in elderly people aged 60–69 years and 70–79 years, the female/male population, the Non-Hispanic White population, those with a poverty index of 1.3–3.5, education level over high school grad/GED, non-smokers, 1–5 drinks/month, non-alcohol drinkers, overweight, normal blood pressure and without diabetes.ConclusionOur research found a strong relationship between vitamin K intake and periodontitis among elderly people in the United States, suggesting vitamin K intake is a possible risk factor and may be a novel idea for the diagnosis and treatment of periodontitis.Keywords: Periodontitis, Vitamin k, American, Elderly population, NHANES

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic infectious inflammatory disease characterized by progressive loss of periodontal ligament, gingival tissue and supporting alveolar bone, which in severe cases can lead to loosening and loss of teeth [21]. An epidemiologic study has shown that nearly 2/3 of the elderly population in the United States is afflicted with moderate to severe periodontitis, and severe tooth loss resulting from periodontitis peaks at about 65 years of age [42]. In the elderly, periodontitis occurs mainly as a consequence of alterations in the periodontal flora, and is influenced by other factors such as immune factors, systemic diseases, decline in mastication, decreased nutrient intake, and reduction in the function of the stem cells of the alveolar bone and periodontal ligament, contributing to progressive gum recession, attachment loss, and alveolar bone resorption, and ultimately result in tooth loss [36]. Furthermore, loss of teeth can negatively affect chewing function and aesthetics, impairing the quality of life of elderly patients [35]. The tissue damage of periodontitis is mainly caused by the increased host immune inflammatory response [9, 14, 34], and the imbalance of vitamin intake may be one of the factors. In recent years, with changes in the global environment and diet, scholars have begun to pay attention to the intake and content levels of vitamins in the human body and their impact on chronic diseases, including the role of vitamins in periodontitis [26, 44, 50].

Vitamin K, a cofactor for the γ-glutamyl carboxylase enzyme, is necessary for post-translational protein modification and plays a vital role in blood coagulation, osteogenesis and anti-inflammation [28]. In the dental perioperative period, performing surgical procedures such as implant surgery and periodontal flap surgery in patients receiving vitamin K inhibitor therapy significantly increases the risk of postoperative hemorrhage [22]. More importantly, vitamin K is not only originally recognized as a factor required for normal blood coagulation, but its role in bone metabolism and anti-inflammation is now receiving increasing attention [1, 6, 10, 20, 43]. Researchers have found that it promotes aspects of alveolar bone metabolism and is involved in the regulation of calcium phosphate synthesis and the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells. Vitamin K can provide a possible for serving as a potential preventive and therapeutic approach to anti-infection and anti-inflammatory conditions in the development of periodontitis. Growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6), a vitamin K-dependent activation of inflammatory regulatory proteins, (Vitamin K-dependent actions of Gas6.) can inhibit the expression of inflammatory factors stimulated by Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (P. gingivalis-LPS)(Liu et al., [24]. Meanwhile, Gas6 expression was also decreased in inflammatory periodontal tissues as well as in P. gingivalis-LPS stimulated cells [46]. In addition, some researchers have found that Gas6 and AXL receptor tyrosine kinase (AXL) activate the Gas6/AXL axis and inhibit the expression of P. gingivalis-LPS-induced inflammatory cytokines in human periodontal ligament cells (hPDLCs)(Zhang et al., [49]. Although the role of vitamin K intake has been explored in the development of periodontitis, the association between vitamin K intake and periodontists is still unclear in cross-sectional study. Herein, we conducted a cross-sectional investigation based on the demographic information dataset from NHANES database from 2009 to 2014.

Methods

Study participants

All data used in our study were derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Conducted under the guidance of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), NHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional study designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. population using a complex, multistage, probability sampling design. All surveys were authorized by the NCHS Ethics Review Board prior to implementation, and all participants provided signed informed consent. The cross-sectional study data were obtained from the NHANES database from 2009 to 2014. In total, we extracted data from 30,468 subjects and screened them. Subsequently, we processed the data according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) NHANES subjects above 60 years of age; (2) participants whose levels of vitamin K intake were measured in the NHANES; (3) NHANES participants who underwent a comprehensive periodontal health examination; and (4) NHANES participants with full covariate data. Based on this, we eliminated all unsuitable subjects, yielding a final sample of 2303. (Fig. 1)

The flowchart of the selection process from NHANES (2009–2014)

Exposure variable

We collected elders above 60 years old into three groups: “60–69 years old”, “70–79 years old” and “80 years and above”. Vitamin K intake was obtained from the total dietary nutrient intake table for two days and the mean values were taken for analysis, data on vitamin K intake were obtained from the What We Eat in America (WWEIA) information-gathering program, a collaboration between the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS), which collected information on the type and quantity of foods and beverages (including all types of water) consumed by participants in the 24 h (midnight to midnight) prior to interview, and estimated intakes of energy, nutrients, and other food components from these foods and beverages. The type and amount of food and beverages (including all types of water) consumed by participants in the 24 h (midnight to midnight) prior to the interview were captured, and the intake of energy, nutrients, and other food components from these foods and beverages were estimated. For subjects with missing data on vitamin K intake on the second day, we took the vitamin K intake on the first day and utilized it for the analysis. The receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve was used to determine the optimal cut-off value for the vitamin K intake level.

Periodontal examination

Patients with periodontitis were screened and classified according to the periodontitis classification criteria as defined in the “CDC/AAP Definition of Periodontitis Case Surveillance.” There is an exhaustive categorization of the different levels of periodontitis in the criteria. Mild periodontitis was characterized by a loss of attachment on two or more interproximal sites ≥ 3 mm and two or more interproximal sites that are not in the same tooth position with a probing depth of ≥ 4 mm or an interproximal site with a probing depth of ≥ 5 mm. Moderate periodontitis was characterized by a loss of attachment ≥ 4 mm on two or more interproximal sites or a probing depth of ≥ 5 mm on two or more interproximal sites that are not in the same tooth position. Severe periodontitis was characterized by a loss of attachment ≥ 6 mm on two or more interproximal sites and a probing depth of ≥ 5 mm on one or more interproximal sites. The control group is defined as periodontally healthy subjects who do not satisfy the aforementioned criteria for determining mild, moderate, or severe periodontitis. Following this criterion, we categorized the participants according to the severity of periodontitis.

Covariates

We collected information about other subjects and filtered them for relevant covariates. These covariates involved gender, race (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other/multiracial), education (Less Than 9th Grade, 9–11th Grade, High School Grad/GED, Some College or AA degree, College Graduate or above), and body mass index(BMI)、blood glucose、blood pressure、smoking status (Current Smoker, Former Smoker, Never Smoker), drinking status (1–5 drinks/month, 5–10 drinks/month, ≥ 10 drinks/month, Non-drinker), and poverty index (< 1.3, 1.3–3.5, ≥ 3.5). Diabetes was categorized according to whether or not one had been diagnosed by a doctor. Diabetic patients normally have a fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a two-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, a random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, and a positive oral glucose tolerance test. According to the criteria provided by the World Health Organization (WHO), we classified BMI and blood pressure. BMI is classified into four categories: Underweight: < 18.5 kg/m2; Normal: ≥ 18.5, < 25 kg/m2; Overweight: ≥ 25, < 30 kg/m2; and Obese: ≥ 30 kg/m2. Blood pressure (BP) was graded according to the range of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure(DBP): Hypotension: SBP < 90 mmHg, DBP < 60 mm Hg; Normal BP: SBP 90–120 mm Hg, DBP 60–80 mm Hg; Elevated BP: SBP 120–140 mm Hg, DBP 80–90 mm Hg; Hypertension Stage 1: SBP140–160 mm Hg, DBP 90–100 mm Hg; Hypertension Stage 2: SBP 160–180 mm Hg, DBP 100–110 mm Hg; Hypertension Stage 3: SBP ≥ 180 mm Hg, DBP ≥ 110 mm Hg.

Statistical analyses

Our analyses in this research are based on the sample weights suggested by NHANES analysis guidelines. The three-year weights data were calculated using 1/3 of the total vitamin K intake weights. Meanwhile, the baseline descriptive statistics in this study were calculated by vitamin K intake. Normal distribution variables were tested by the one-way ANOVA. Non-normal distribution variables were evaluated by the Kruskal - Wallis test. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. Multivariate regression was used to analyze the relationship between vitamin K intake and periodontitis in the elderly population. In addition, stratified analyses of exposure factors and different populations (race, blood pressure, drinking status, etc.) were conducted. To further exclude the effect of covariates, we applied the following three models: Crude model, an unadjusted model in which variables were not adjusted; Model 1, adjusted for age, gender, and race; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, blood pressure, education level, drinking status, smoking status, diabetes, BMI, and poverty index. In addition, we utilized a restricted cubic spline (RCS), a usual way of fitting a nonlinear relationship, to fit the relationship between vitamin K intake and periodontitis. Data were processed using MS in conjunction with the NHANES (2009–2014) database and R (version 4.2.3) software to generate results. We defined P < 0.05 as a statistically significant difference.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 30,468 subjects were included in this study, and 2303 subjects were finally analyzed after condition selection. The optimal cutoff value of vitamin K intake was 89.51 mg/day according to the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC = 0.574, 95%CI: 0.550–0.598, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2), which was used for subsequent analysis. A total of 1149 women and 1154 men were investigated in this research, with the largest number of elderly people aged 60–69 years (n = 1334). Most of the subjects (n = 1366) had some degree of periodontal disease, with moderate periodontitis being the most common (n = 1087). Meanwhile, we found that vitamin K intake level was strongly correlated with the progression of periodontitis among older adults in America (P < 0.001). Participants with vitamin K intakes greater than 89.51 mg/d were less likely to develop periodontitis than those with vitamin K intakes less than 89.51 mg/d. Compared with the non-periodontitis group, the prevalence of severe periodontitis was higher in males (40.4% vs. 65.69%; p = 0.001), non-Hispanic blacks (3.83% vs. 18.80%; p < 0.001), Mexican Americans (1.93% vs. 8.77%; p < 0.001), and other Hispanics (2.09% vs. 4.81%; p < 0.001), those with less than high school grad (less than 9th grade, 2.12% vs. 8.45%; p < 0.001; 9–11th grade, 5.92% vs. 15.33%; p < 0.001; high school grad/GED, 17.61% vs. 33.91%; p < 0.001), PIR < 1.3 (7.67% vs. 24.53%; p < 0.001), and those who smoked (3.90% vs. 24.57%; p < 0.001) and had diabetes (13.60% vs. 21.22%; p = 0.012). Furthermore, vitamin K intake and the prevalence of periodontitis in different subgroups of the elderly Americans were evaluated using multivariate regression analysis (Table 3). Compared to people with vitamin K ≤ 89.51 mg/d, vitamin K intake > 89.51 mg/d was negatively related to the prevalence of periodontitis in those who were older adults aged 60–69 years old (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.739, 95% CI: 0.576–0.947, p = 0.017) and 70–79 (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.534, 95% CI: 0.370–0.772, p = 0.001). In addition, this difference was found among males (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.662, 95% CI: 0.506–0.867, p = 0.003), females (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.672, 95% CI: 0.523–0.864, p = 0.002), and the non-Hispanic whites population (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.552, 95% CI: 0.430–0.710, p < 0.001). Similarly significant results were found among those with higher levels of education (some college or AA degree, OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.617, 95% CI: 0.445–0.854, p = 0.004; college graduate or above, OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.606, 95% CI: 0.426–0.863, p = 0.006). This conclusion was much more pronounced among those who had better lifestyle habits or were in better health (1–5 alcohol drinks per month (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.725, 95% CI: 0.557–0.944, p = 0.017); no alcohol consumption (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.587, 95% CI: 0.423–0.816, p = 0.001); never smoked (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.622, 95%CI: 0.485–0.799, p < 0.001); normal blood pressure (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.520, 95% CI: 0.388–0.695, p < 0.001); no diabetic (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.624, 95% CI: 0.508–0.768, p < 0.001)). Finally, the prevalence of periodontitis was also lower in poverty index of 1.3–3.5 (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.571, 95% CI: 0.429–0.761, p < 0.001) and overweight (OR ≥ 89.51 mg/d vs. < 89.51 mg/d = 0.602, 95% CI: 0.442–0.820, p = 0.001) populations with high vitamin K intake. In Fig. 3, we found the restricted cubic splines (RCS) between vitamin K intake and reduced prevalence of periodontitis in the elderly population of Americans based on the crude model, Model 1, and Model 2. The red curve fits the RCS curve between vitamin K intake and risk of periodontitis, and the gray line (OR = 1) is a decomposition of the high and low risks of periodontitis in the model. The light blue area indicates its 95% confidence interval. More importantly, the RCS curve for vitamin K changes from above to below the gray line at an intake of approximately 90 mg/d, suggesting that vitamin K intake is a cut-off point where the risk of periodontitis decreases in older adults who consume more than 90 mg/d per day, and that this nonlinear relationship is significant (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study conducts a cross-sectional survey utilizing the demographic information dataset from the NHANES database, covering the years 2009 to 2014. The relationship between vitamin K intake and periodontitis among the elderly population in the United States was assessed by collecting data on probing depth, attachment loss at six sites, and vitamin K intake over two days for individuals aged 60 years and older. The findings indicate that elderly individuals with a daily vitamin K intake exceeding 89.51 milligrams are less likely to experience periodontitis compared to those whose intake is below this threshold. Among seniors who meet or exceed the recommended daily intake of vitamin K (greater than 89.51 milligrams), non-smokers, non-drinkers, and non-diabetic patients exhibited a significantly lower incidence of periodontitis relative to their counterparts with lower vitamin K consumption. To evaluate these correlations, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed; additionally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were utilized to further investigate the nonlinear relationship between vitamin K intake and periodontitis. These results suggest a strong association between vitamin K consumption and periodontitis prevalence, highlighting the importance of nutrient intake in preventing and managing this condition among older adults.

As is well known, periodontitis is more prevalent in older adults than in young adults. The increasing prevalence of periodontitis in the elderly population is related to the decline of their mobility, degenerative changes, or vision loss, which leads to poor oral hygiene maintenance and loss of appetite. Meanwhile, due to a decrease in the number of teeth and impaired chewing ability, elderly people have weakened digestive and absorption functions, leading to imbalanced nutrient intake and malnutrition. Particularly, elderly people tend to consume soft or finely processed foods, which typically have higher levels of fat and energy, and lower levels of micronutrients such as vitamins and minerals. It may be one of the important reasons for the low intake of vitamin K in elderly people and the high incidence of periodontitis. Additionally, according to the National Institutes of Health’s 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the recommended daily intake of vitamin K for females aged 51 + is 90 mg, while the recommended daily intake for males aged 51 + is 120 mg. Predictably, vitamin K intake in the elderly population below 89.51 mg/d does not meet the recommended dose, and insufficient vitamin K intake may be closely related to the increased incidence of periodontitis.

In 1935, Danish scientists Dam et al. reported that vitamin K has a coagulation function, also known as coagulation vitamin [8]. It is an essential vitamin for the synthesis of all four coagulation proteins in the liver [27]. A clinical study found that patients taking vitamin K inhibitors have a significantly increased risk of bleeding after undergoing periodontal flap surgery, tooth extraction, or implant surgery [22]. On the other hand, vitamin K can act on osteoblasts, promote bone tissue calcification, such as osteocalcin, proteins, etc., all of which require vitamin K to exert their effects, while inhibiting osteoclasts and avoiding bone resorption. In bone remodeling, low expression of vitamin K affects the expression of osteocalcin (ucOC) carboxylation, osteoprotegerin, and pyridine cross-linking [15, 25, 31]. In the absence of vitamin K, the carboxylation of osteocalcin is reduced, and its binding with calcium and hydroxyapatite is inhibited. Inhibiting osteoprotegerin promotes the differentiation of osteoclast precursors into mature osteoblasts, thereby inhibiting bone remodeling. Researchers have found that vitamin K2 can significantly promote osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs (periodontal ligament stem cells) by regulating the Wnt/β - catenin pathway [6].In recent years, as an antioxidant and recessive anti-inflammatory agent, vitamin K has attracted extensive attention of researchers in osteoporosis [33], diabetes [19], cardiovascular disease [17], Alzheimer’s disease [37], cancer [4] and other chronic diseases [16]. Its antioxidant properties are mainly achieved through the uptake of reactive oxygen species, restriction of intracellular free radical accumulation, and inhibition of 12 lipoxygenase activation, thereby reducing oxidative cell damage and cell death [39]. Therefore, vitamin K may play a crucial role in the occurrence and development of periodontitis through its hemostatic, osteogenic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory functions.

Table 3 demonstrates a significant correlation between vitamin K intake and the development of periodontitis in populations that are non-diabetic, non-smoking, and non-drinking. Recent research indicates that individuals with high vitamin K intake exhibit decreased blood glucose levels, increased insulin production indices, and reduced glycated hemoglobin A1c [47]. These findings suggest that vitamin K consumption may aid in blood sugar regulation, thereby mitigating the progression of periodontitis [48]. Vitamin K also serves as a cofactor for vitamin K γ-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX), which is crucial for regulating the post-translational γ-carboxylation of specific glutamic acid residues in vitamin K-dependent proteins. This process has been associated with a reduction in the incidence rate of type 2 diabetes. Following an increase in advanced glycation end products (AGEs), there was a notable rise in the levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and PGE2 within the gingival crevicular fluid of diabetic patients; this elevation may contribute to the onset of periodontitis [19]. Furthermore, some researchers have observed that diabetic rats with bone defects treated with vitamin K2 exhibited enhanced guided bone regeneration (GBR) healing within their bone tissue defects. This finding highlights the clinical significance of vitamin K in promoting periodontal bone tissue regeneration [10]. Additionally, studies have shown that vitamin K2 can promote osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) in vitro via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thus playing a vital role in alveolar bone protection [6]. Regarding the association between smoking and periodontitis, it has been established that smoking negatively regulates host immune cells, such as neutrophils, thereby increasing susceptibility to periodontitis [38]. A previous cohort study involving 1,332 Japanese males without periodontitis found that after four years, the probability of developing periodontitis with tooth loss was significantly lower in the non-smoking group compared to their current smoking counterparts [32]. Cross-sectional studies conducted on the adult population in the United States have also revealed that individuals who are non-smokers or have quit smoking exhibit a markedly lower likelihood of having periodontitis than those who are current smokers [40]. Furthermore, alcohol consumption is associated with an increased incidence of periodontitis in prior cross-sectional studies [13]. In a controlled study involving 1,285 participants, researchers discovered that an increase in alcohol intake moderately elevated the risk of developing periodontitis [2]. Additionally, alcohol consumption may promote osteoclastogenesis and exacerbate the progression of periodontitis [7]. As age increases, so does the risk of developing periodontitis; this underscores the necessity for further research efforts aimed at preventing and treating this condition among elderly populations [23]. The likelihood of moderate to severe periodontitis is significantly heightened in male diabetic patients; however, no such correlation has been observed among females [48]. A simulation experiment conducted on monkeys revealed significant differences in gene expression between male subjects during both progression and remission stages of periodontitis. This finding suggests that gender may serve as a biological variable influencing outcomes related to periodontal disease [11]. Previous investigations have indicated disparities among various ethnic groups concerning prevalence rates; specifically, Hispanics exhibit a higher prevalence of periodontitis, whereas non-Hispanic whites demonstrate the lowest probability of experiencing this condition [12]. In a population cohort study involving 10,000 subjects, researchers identified a significant correlation between educational attainment and incidence rates of periodontitis [45]. A systematic review analyzing clinical data from 37 recent articles has established a positive correlation between periodontitis and obesity [18]. Furthermore, two meta-analyses have shown that the risk of developing periodontitis increases with rising body mass index (BMI) [30, 41]. In studies encompassing a total of 9,779 participants, researchers found a significant association between income levels and the prevalence of periodontitis [3], while Mendelian randomization experiments further substantiated that income is an important factor influencing the occurrence of periodontitis [5]. Regarding the relationship between periodontitis and hypertension, meta-analytic findings indicate that moderate to severe periodontitis is associated with an increased risk for hypertension. Prospective studies have confirmed that a diagnosis of periodontitis elevates the likelihood of developing hypertension in affected individuals. Subsequent interventional studies revealed that following periodontal treatment in twelve patients suffering from both periodontitis and hypertension, there was a notable reduction in blood pressure. These results underscore the connection between the onset of periodontitis and hypertension [29].

Our study explored the positive correlation between vitamin K intake and the incidence of periodontitis, that is, the incidence of periodontitis decreased with the increase of vitamin K intake. However, there are certain limitations in our research. Firstly, as the NHANES database only includes periodontal examination data from 2009 to 2014, the sample data obtained is not large enough to reflect the changes in these statistical data over the past decade. Secondly, we only studied the nutritional intake for two 24-hour periods, and the nutritional intake for two days may not fully represent the nutritional intake of the population over a long period of time. In terms of dietary assessment based on individual reports, the calculation of vitamin K may be biased due to memory bias and inaccurate assessment of food types and weights. Based on these findings, future studies will focus on the impact of vitamin K supplements on the incidence and severity of periodontitis in elderly populations, and further utilizing animal models of periodontitis and controlled clinical trials for validation.

Conclusion

According to the NHANES database from 2009 to 2014, we found that there was a strong positive association between vitamin K intake and the prevalence of periodontitis in the American elderly population. Older adults above the recommended daily intake of vitamin K, especially non-smokers, non-drinkers, and non-diabetics, were significantly less likely to develop periodontitis than those with lower vitamin K intake. Supplementation of adequate vitamin K intake can be effective in preventing the development of periodontitis in the elderly population. Further exploration is needed to confirm the underlying mechanism of the influence of unbalanced vitamin K intake on periodontitis prevalence in the future.
