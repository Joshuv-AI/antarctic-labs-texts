Maternal prenatal systemic inflammation indexes predicts premature neonatal respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Mengqing Weng, Jie Wang, Jingfeng Yin, Liufang He, Han Yang, Huimin He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) is one of the leading causes of neonatal mortality in low-income countries. It is caused by a lack of surface-active substances in the lungs, and the maternal inflammatory response plays an important role in the formation of surface-active substances in the fetal lungs. We aimed to investigate the correlation between maternal prenatal systemic inflammatory indices and respiratory distress syndrome in preterm neonates. This is a retrospective case–control study that collected data from all patients who delivered between 28 and 36 weeks of gestation at Longhua District People's Hospital in Shenzhen City and whose infants were admitted to the neonatal unit, newborns with respiratory distress syndrome were in the experimental group (NRDS group), and newborns without NRDS were in the control group (non-NRDS group). To minimize the effect of confounders on the results, propensity score matching was performed on baseline characteristics. Totally, 524 patients were included (93 in the NRDS group and 431 in the non-NRDS group), and 71 matched pairs (142 patients). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) and neutrophil lymphocyte to platelet ratio (NLPR) were higher in the NRDS group than in the non-NRDS group (p < 0.05). The ROC curves of NLR, dNLR, SII, SIRI, AISI and NLPR for the diagnosis of NRDS were plotted, and it was found that the combined diagnostic efficacy of these six systemic inflammatory markers was better (AUC: 0.643, P = 0.003). Patients were divided into two groups based on the cut-off values determined from the ROC curves, and analysis using binary regression models revealed that SII ≥ 1199.94 (OR, 2.554; 95% CI 1.245–5.239, P = 0.011) and NLPR ≥ 0.0239 (OR, 2.175; 95% CI 1.061–4.459, P = 0.034) were independent risk factors predicting NRDS. Maternal prenatal SII ≥ 1199.94 and NLPR ≥ 0.0239 are independent risk factors for NRDS, and clinicians may be used to prevent neonatal respiratory distress in advance to reduce the incidence of NRDS.Keywords: Respiratory distress syndrome, Premature birth, Systemic inflammation indexes, Neutrophil lymphocyte to platelet ratio, Systemic immune-inflammation indexSubject terms: Respiratory distress syndrome, Paediatric research, Inflammation

Introduction

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) accounts for 45% of the leading causes of premature neonatal death in low-income countries and is one of the most common problems of preterm birth1 which is due to a deficiency of pulmonary surface active substances. More than 90% of NRDS occur in preterm infants2. The main manifestations are shortness of breath, expiratory moaning, cyanosis and chest wall recessions (suprasternal, intercostal and subcostal) which in severe cases can manifest as hypopnea, irregular breathing, and apnea and is one of the leading causes of neonatal death3. In the recovery period of this disease, approximately 30–50% of infants appear patent ductus arteriosus, and heart failure and pulmonary edema can occur when the shunt volume is large4. Recovery from NRDS depends on its severity, which in turn is influenced by pregnancy (e.g., pre-eclampsia, in vitro fertilization, etc.) and birth weight5–7. The European consensus guidelines for the management of respiratory distress syndrome also indicate that interventions to improve outcomes and prevent RDS should be started before birth8 .

Usually, the diagnosis of NRDS is based on clinical presentation, chest X-ray (CXR) and arterial blood gas results. However, CXR exposes the newborn to ionizing radiation with potential adverse effects9. Systemic inflammatory indices have recently gained attention because they can predict the prognosis and prognostic significance of various diseases10 by calculating complete blood counts, such as neoplasms11,12, missed abortions13, and diabetes14. Meanwhile, it has been shown that inflammatory mediators can lead to alterations in lung surfactant production and type II epithelial cell function. Currently, studies on the prediction of NRDS are mainly focused on ultrasound15,16. However, some studies have shown that maternal whole blood counts and inflammatory markers in umbilical cord blood can be used to predict NRDS. For example, in the studies of Otsubo et al. the inflammatory markers interleukin-6, interleukin-8, monocyte chemotactic protein-1, macrophage inflammatory protein-1α and high-sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) in cord blood were found to be useful in predicting NRDS17–19, and in the studies of Dundar et al. platelet in the complete blood count of preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) were helpful in predicting NRDS20,21. However, research on the relationship between the maternal systemic inflammation index and NRDS in preterm delivery is still lacking. Therefore, the aim of this study was to estimate the predictive value of the maternal systemic inflammatory index in preterm births on NRDS using propensity score-matched (PSM) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

Discussion

In the PSM study, we utilized the systemic inflammatory index calculated from a simple and noninvasive complete blood count to predict NRDS. Before PSM analysis, the cesarean section rate was higher in the NRDS group22,23 than in the non-NRDS group; the PPROM rate24,25and GA8 were lower in the NRDS group than in the non-NRDS group, which was consistent with previous studies. Our obstetricians use dexamethasone to promote fetal lung maturation in all patients with premature rupture of membranes (PROM) before 34+6 weeks26, which led to the conclusion that the rate of preterm rupture of membranes was lower in the NRDS group than in the non-NRDS group. Therefore, in this study, the cesarean section rate, PPROM rate, GA and BW-for-GA of patients in the NRDS group and non-NRDS group were matched to ensure the quality of the study. After matching, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of appellate indexes. In terms of inflammatory indexes, NLR, dNLR, SII, SIRI, AISI, and NLPR were significantly higher in the NRDS group than in the non-NRDS group.

NRDS is caused by decreased content of pulmonary surfactant (PS)27, which increases alveolar surface tension, decreases functional residual capacity (FRC) at end-expiration, and tends to atrophy the alveoli. Pulmonary surface active substance refers to a phospholipid-protein complex synthesized and secreted by type II alveolar epithelial cells, the main components of which are dipalmitoyl lecithin and surface active substance binding protein28,29. Inflammatory mediators can lead to alterations in the synthesis, secretion and recycling of pulmonary surfactants, and the function of type II epithelial cells may also be altered. In premature infants in low-income countries, the most common cause of death is respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)30, and preterm infants with RDS are three times more likely to die than those without RDS31. Therefore, it is important to predict the disease progression in NRDS based on systemic inflammatory indicators to prevent and improve the prognosis in advance.

SII and NLPR have been reported to play an important role in many gestational and neonatal disorders, such as hyperemesis gravidarum (HEG)32, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE)33, and combined adverse neonatal outcomes (including at least one case of NRDS, neonatal sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis, and congenital pneumonia)34. Several studies have shown that neutrophil abnormalities, lymphocyte abnormalities, or platelet abnormalities can lead to alveolar injury35–37. On the other hand,SII and NLPR were calculated from neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, reflecting the role of the simultaneous presence of all of these variables. NRDS is due to a deficiency of type II alveolar epithelial cell synthesis complexes38, however, there is a close association between the mother and fetus during pregnancy, and therefore, maternal SII and NLPR levels should correlate with infant lung surface active substances.

We found that SII ≥ 1199.94 and NLPR ≥ 0.0239 were independent risk factors for predicting NRDS. This is consistent with past studies39, but in that study it was the SII in the first hour of birth in preterm infants that was used to predict NRDS, and the cut-off value of 78.2 in that study was smaller than the threshold determined in this study. This can be due to the fact that the neutrophil count in neonates is smaller than in adults and the units are different.In addition, in order to further analyze the relationship between systemic inflammatory indicators and NRDS, the diagnostic efficacy of NRDS was also analyzed by combining NLR, dNLR, SII, SIRI, AISI and NLPR. It was found that the diagnostic efficacy of the combined indexes was better, so in practical clinical work, NRDS should be predicted based on the combined systemic inflammatory indexes. Surfactant therapy improves survival in NRDS and reduces the incidence of pneumothorax8. It has been shown that predicting surfactant deficiency before an infant's illness worsens may enable infants on continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) to receive surfactant therapy earlier, thereby reducing the need for mechanical ventilation (MV) and improving prognosis40,41. Obtaining prenatal indicators of systemic inflammation is both simple and noninvasive, and may provide an alternative approach to the early diagnosis of RDS.

There are also some limitations in this study. First, this study is a single center study, indicating that the overall evidence is insufficient. Second, this study did not include potential external factors that may influence NRDS, such as mothers' history of smoking and alcohol consumption. Third, this study was retrospective, meaning that information obtained from medical records was not sufficient to rule out potential bias. Finally, we took patients before delivery of the complete blood count, but there will be changes during pregnancy in patients with inflammatory indicators, so we still need a prospective study design to collect the data of each pregnancy period, observe its change and forecast.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results suggest that maternal prenatal NLR, dNLR, SII, SIRI, AISI, and NLPR are new predictors of NRDS, and clinicians can use them to prevent neonatal respiratory distress in advance and thus reduce the incidence of NRDS. Meanwhile, their combined predictive efficacy is better. When SII ≥ 1199.94 and NLPR ≥ 0.0239, they are independent risk factors for NRDS. However, given the limitations of this study, prospective and multicenter studies are needed to further clarify the role of maternal SII and NLPR in NRDS.

Materials and methods

Study ethics and design

This is a retrospective case–control study conducted at the Longhua District People's Hospital in Shenzhen, China. The study population was all patients (1040 total) who delivered between 28 and 36 weeks of gestation and whose infants were admitted to the neonatal department. The data collection period was from January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2022. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Longhua People's Hospital (Longhua District People’s Hospital Ethical Review (Research) [2023] No. (007)).The requirement for informed con-sent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of Longhua People's Hospital due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Definitions

The diagnosis of NRDS is based on progressive exacerbation of dyspnea in infants within the first 6–12 h of life, the need for oxygen to maintain a PaO2 > 50 mm Hg, and lung X-rays that typically show low lung volumes and the typical diffuse reticular granular ground glass appearance with air bronchogram42. Infants with weights between the 10th and 90th percentiles of average birth weight for the same gestational age were defined as appropriate for gestational age (AGA) infants, below the 10th percentile were defined as small for gestational age (SGA) infants, and above the 90th percentile were defined as large for gestational age (LGA) infants43.

Patient inclusion and study groups

Patients who delivered between 28 and 36 weeks of gestation and whose infants had NRDS were included in the study group, and those without NRDS were included in the control group. The inclusion criteria wereas follows: singleton pregnancy, non-in vitro fertilization, absence of infectious or chronic inflammatory diseases, absence of hematological abnormalities, absence of chromosomal and genetic abnormalities, and complete data. Exclusion criteria for maternity: genitourinary tract infection during pregnancy, infectious and parasitic diseases during pregnancy, respiratory diseases during pregnancy, gastrointestinal diseases during pregnancy, malignancy, any invasive procedure (such as amniocentesis)20,21. Exclusion criteria for neonates: transient tachypnoea (wet lung), interstitial emphysema (existing independently), pulmonary hemorrhage, congenital viral diseases, bacterial sepsis, infection with congenital infectious and parasitic diseases (Fig. 2).

Data exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Data were obtained from the electronic medical records system of Shenzhen Longhua District People's Hospital. Baseline data included maternal age, gestational age (GA), blood type, number of pregnancies, number of deliveries, history of miscarriage, placental disorders, placental abruption, pre-eclampsia, cesarean section, PPROM, neonatal sex, birth weight-for-gestational age (BW-for-GA), 1-min Apgar score, 5-min Apgar score, 10-min Apgar score. Laboratory tests included the following parameters of complete blood counts used in this study: white blood cell count (WBC), absolute neutrophil count (Neut#), absolute lymphocyte count (Lym#), absolute monocyte count (Mono#), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), and platelet distribution width (PDW). Systemic inflammatory indices include44–47: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = absolute neutrophil count/absolute lymphocyte count, derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR) = absolute neutrophil count/(white blood cell count-absolute neutrophil count), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) = absolute monocyte count/absolute lymphocyte count, blood platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = platelet count/absolute lymphocyte count, neutrophils lymphocyte to platelet ratio (NLPR) = absolute neutrophil count/(absolute lymphocyte count * platelet count), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) = (absolute neutrophil count * platelet count)/absolute lymphocyte count, systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) = (absolute neutrophil count * absolute monocyte count)/absolute lymphocyte count, Aggregate Index of Systemic Inflammation (AISI) = (absolute neutrophil count * platelet count * absolute monocyte count)/absolute lymphocyte count.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 24.0 (IBM-SPSS, Chicago, USA). Data were matched for propensity score in a 1:1 case ratio. The NRDS and non-NRDS groups were matched for PPROM, GA, BW-for-GA and cesarean section. Quantitative data were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. If the data were normal, they were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Conversely, it was expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Qualitative data were expressed as percentages (%). For comparison of continuous variables, independent samples Student's t-test (normal distribution) and Mann–Whitney u-test (non-normal distribution) were used; for comparison of categorical variables, Pearson X2 test was used. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to estimate the optimal threshold, and the ability of each systemic inflammation index to identify NRDS was assessed by comparing the area under the curve (AUC) of ROC. Binary logistic regression analysis (backward: LR method) was used to analyze independent risk factors for predicting NRDS by including variables that were significant and uncorrelated in univariate analysis in the model. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
