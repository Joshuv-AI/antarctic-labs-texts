Intercropping between persimmon and apple enriched the leaf endophytes and rhizosphere communities against apple root rot
Authors: Haichun Cun, Pengbo He, Ayesha Ahmed, Pengfei He, Yixin Wu, Yingjie Jiang, Hui Zhang, Guowen Tang, Baohua Kong, Sahar A Youssef, Shahzad Munir, Yueqiu He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRoot rot disease has seriously restricted the development of the apple industry Worldwide. After being infected by pathogens, the microbial communities of rhizosphere soil and leaves may change. We found that apple and persimmon intercropping can reduce the occurrence of apple root rot achieving a relative control efficacy of 80.3% following persimmon intercropping.ResultsHere, we investigate the effect of intercropping with persimmon tree on rhizosphere microbiome and leaf endophytes. For this, we tested the rhizosphere soil and leaves of healthy and infected apple trees during intercropping. The soil and leaves of healthy and infected root rot apple trees, persimmon trees, and intercropping were collected (during sampling time, apple trees were 5 years old, and persimmon trees were 3 years old). The high-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina Miseq platform to analyze the leaf endophytic bacterial and soil microbial communities. We further revealed that infection by root rot pathogens severely impacts the synergistic effects between endophytes and the composition of rhizosphere microbial communities. The intercropping between apple and persimmon trees can modulate apple leaf endophytes and rhizosphere microbial communities. Importantly, the relative abundance of Pantoea and Serratia increased significantly in the leaves of apple trees affected by root rot. We argue that bacteria such as Pantoea and Serratia are conducive to the occurrence of root rot. Notably, Bacillus and Trichoderma were significantly reduced in the rhizosphere of apples with root rot trees; however, persimmon trees and apple trees can maintain the relative abundance of beneficial species or improve the abundance compared with healthy apple trees.ConclusionsThe overall study concludes that intercropping apples and persimmons maintains the beneficial microbial balance and plant growth and helps to prevent dysbiosis caused by pathogens. Based on this shift in microbiome balance, a management strategy for apple root rot is discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07338-4.Keywords: Dysbiosis, Endophyte, Intercropping, Rhizosphere, Root rot

Introduction

Apple root rot is one of the major threats to apple trees that are attacked by several pathogenic fungi. These known and emerging pathogens have seriously affected trees normal growth and development, significantly reducing yield and quality. Such alarming pathogens responsible mainly include Rosellinia necatrix [1], Sclerotium rolfsii [2], Helicobasidium mompa [3], Fusarium spp [4]. and Armillaria mellea [5]. Phytopythium vexans was novel pathogenic fungus that has recently emerged in plants of the Rosaceae family [6]. Apple root rot has become an extreme disease of apples in recent years, with the continuous expansion of apple cultivation areas and deterioration of the orchard’s ecological environment. The apple orchard needs a lot of manpower and material resources, water and fertilizer management, pesticide control, and replanting to reduce the losses, however, apple root rot still cannot be effectively controlled [7].

Currently, the traditional root rot prevention and control measures are chemical fungicides [8] and resistant rootstocks [9]. The fungicides commonly used in rotation to mitigate apple root rot include hymexazol, thiophanate-methyl, and carbendazim. Although the use of chemical pesticides is convenient and fast, it has environmental consequences in the form of residues, limited function of potential microbes, and pathogen resistance [10]. Experiments found that a higher degree of interference of chemical pesticides with pathogenic fungi results in a slower recover ability of soil microbiome. The response of soil microorganisms to a range of chemical pesticides is variable, ranging from unfavourable to the death of beneficial microbes [11]. At cellular and biochemical levels, pesticides destroy the morphology, ultrastructure, viability/cellular permeability, and many biochemical reactions, including protein profiles of soil bacteria. Several classes of pesticides also disturb the molecular interaction between crops and their symbionts, impeding the overall useful biological processes. Due to the complexity of the etiology of root rot and difficulty in observing the phenotype of rootstock resistance, limited progress has been observed in using apple rootstock resistance to apple root rot [12]. For instance, using chemical pesticides and rootstocks wasn’t effective in reducing the dysbiosis caused by apple root rot.

The next frontier is to use endophytic bacteria and rhizosphere microbes that could play an important role in plant growth, nutrition, and health. The changes in community structure and composition could reflect the health status of plants [13]. Reducing beneficial soil microbiota could lead to the accumulation of pathogens in the rhizosphere, further promoting soil-borne disease spread in crops via negative plant-soil feedback over the long term [14]. Increasing plant diversity in ecosystems can enhance the stability of ecosystem functionality and avoid large-scale damage caused by biotic or abiotic stresses [15]. Moreover, monoculture is unfavorable for controlling pests and diseases, especially soil-borne diseases in orchards. Thus, intercropping may effectively reduce the occurrence of crop diseases, and many of the successful cases of disease prevention through intercropping have been observed [16, 17]. Intercropping of maize and pepper can effectively reduce the occurrence of Phytophthora pathogen[18]. The intercropping of cotton with onion or garlic could effectively inhibit the occurrence of cotton Verticillium wilt [19]. Planting in the alleyways and planting rootstocks genetically dissimilar to the previously planted rootstock can benefit tree establishment [20]. This provides us with a new approach for preventing apple root rot through intercropping with apple trees to provide resistance to pathogens and increase plant health. In the early stages, apple root rot is difficult to detect. Changes in above-ground plant parts, such as leaf wilting and yellowing, are most easily observed. Investigating bacterial community structure in leaves related to apple root rot occurrence provides a theoretical basis for a comprehensive analysis of apple root rot causes and mechanisms.

In 2021, after farmers planted persimmon trees in apple orchards afflicted by root rot, they observed a decrease in the incidence of apple root rot. Following this serendipitous discovery, an intercropping system of persimmon and apple trees was adopted, leading to subsequent investigations into the effects of intercropping on leaf and rhizosphere microbial communities. It was found that there was a significant improvement in apple tree growth after the diverse planting of apple and persimmon trees. During the same period, plots with solely planted apple trees exhibited a 30% mortality rate due to apple root rot disease. In contrast, plots with intercropped persimmon trees showed a mortality rate of 5%, achieving a relative control efficacy of 80.3% following persimmon intercropping (data not published). The quality of healthy fruit was maintained, and root rot incidence significantly decreased. Based on this, we hypothesize that persimmon and apple tree intercropping positively affected apple leaf endophytic bacteria and rhizosphere bacteria or fungi. The potential beneficial bacteria groups could greatly support reducing the pathogen and increasing plant health. This may provide a theoretical basis for an in-depth study of the causes of apple root rot and screening of effective growth-promoting bacteria.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The study (2016–2021) used Red Fuji Apple (Malus x domestica) trees planted in the Year 2016, followed by planting Jiro Persimmon (Diospyros kaki ‘Jiro’) trees in the Year 2018 in the same field to study the variation of microbiome during intercropping. Certified plants were obtained from the Agricultural and Rural Bureau of Kunming Xishan District, Kunming 650,188, China, under the supervision of Dr. Hui Zhang, in charge of the study area. The soil and leaves of healthy and infecting root rot apple tree, persimmon tree, and intercropping trees were collected during the Year 2021 (during sampling time, apple trees were 5 years old, and persimmon trees were 3 years old) in the Apple Base of Kunming Dingshun Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd., Tuanjie Township, Kunming City, Yunnan Province, China (N: 25°04′43.90″, E༚102°30′54.21). The figure of the experimental design was presented in Fig. 1.

The figure of the experimental design

The experimental plot of persimmon trees intercropped with apple trees was 7,000 ha with 1,100 trees, while the area of apple trees in monoculture was 27,000 ha. The intercropping method between persimmon and apple trees was in rows. All apple trees, irrespective of their disease status, were sourced from a single orchard for the intercropping experimental design. Diseased trees were identified based on yellowing and withering above-ground parts. After excavating the soil, root decay was observed, while healthy trees had normal above-ground and root systems. Sampling points for healthy trees were at least 200 m away from diseased trees to minimize contamination. The orchard was planted at a spacing of 1.5 m by 3 m, resulting in a planting density of 148 trees per hectare. Soil was collected from the area surrounding the base of the plant at a depth of 20 cm within a radius of 10 cm. Samples were taken from east, south, west, and north directions, mixed and returned to the laboratory for further analysis. Soil samples were collected from the intersection of the rhizospheres of apple and persimmon trees. The areas where the fine roots of the two tree types crossed were identified, and soil samples around these roots were collected for laboratory analysis. Three replicates were sampled for each group of samples and transported back to the laboratory at 0 °C, then quickly transferred to − 80 °C refrigerator for storage.

The samples in dry ice were stored and sent to the sequencing company to detect the diversity of endophytic bacteria in leaves HAL (leaves of healthy apple), DAL (leaves of root rot apple), PL (leaves of persimmon tree), ApL (apple leaves of intercropping apple and persimmon trees), and PaL (persimmon leaves of intercropping apple and persimmon trees), rhizosphere soil bacteria as HASB (healthy apple tree rhizosphere soil for bacteria), DASB (root rot apple tree rhizosphere soil for bacteria), PSB (persimmon tree rhizosphere soil for bacteria), and APSB (the cross-rhizosphere soil of apple and persimmon), and fungi HASF (healthy apple tree rhizosphere soil for fungi), DASF (root rot apple tree rhizosphere soil for fungi), PSF (persimmon tree rhizosphere soil for fungi), and APSF (the cross-rhizosphere soil of apple and persimmon) (Table 1).

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Leaf DNA extraction and PCR amplification

The leaves (five treatments, three replicates) were surface sterilized with 75% alcohol for 6 minutes, then rinsing with sterile water 6 times. The 100 µL of sterile water for the last rinsing was applied evenly on the Luria Bertani (LB) agar plate to detect whether the surface is disinfected and clean from contamination. Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from leaf samples using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [21]. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., USA) and kept at −80 ℃ prior to further use. The V5-V7 regions of the bacteria 16S rRNA gene of each sample were amplified twice. The PCR reactions were first conducted with primers 799 F (5’-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3’) and 1392R (5’-ACGGGCGGTGTGTRC-3’) by thermocycler PCR system (GeneAmp 9700, ABI, USA). Subsequently, the initial PCR products were used as templates for the second amplification with primers 799 F (5’-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3’) and 1193R (5’-ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC-3’) [22]. The PCR reaction mixture including 4 µL 5 × FastPfu buffer, 2 µL 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL FastPfu polymerase, 0.2 µL BSA, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH2O to a final volume of 20 µL. PCR amplification cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by cycles of denaturing at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s, one round of primers had 27 cycles, two rounds of primers had 13 cycles, and extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s, and single extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min, and end at 4 ℃. All samples were amplified in triplicate. The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA).

Soil DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from four groups, including HAS, DAS, PS, and APS samples, using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., USA) and kept at −80 ℃ prior to further use. The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the rhizosphere soil bacterial 16 S rRNA gene were amplified with primer pairs 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R(5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) [23]by an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (ABI, CA, USA). The hypervariable region of the rhizosphere fungi ITS1 gene were amplified with primer pairs ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’) [24]. The PCR reaction mixture of rhizosphere soil bacteria was prepared same Like 2.2.1. The PCR reaction mixture of rhizosphere soil fungi includes 2 µL 10 × buffer, 2 µL 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL each primer (5 µM), 0.2 µL Taq polymerase, 0.2 µL BSA, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH2O to a final volume of 20 µL. PCR amplification cycling conditions of soil bacteria were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturing at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s and extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s, and single extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min, and end at 4 ℃. The PCR reaction of the soil fungi hhas 27 cycles. All samples were amplified in triplicate. The PCR product was extracted from 2% agarose gel and further purification was performed as mentioned above.

Illumina Miseq sequencing

Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocols by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw sequencing reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Accession Number: SRP484895, SRP484894, SRP484893).

Data processing

Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed using an in-house perl script, and then quality-filtered by fastp version 0.19.6 [25] and merged by FLASH version 1.2.7 [26] with the following criteria: (1) the 300 bp reads were truncated at any site receiving an average quality score of < 20 over a 50 bp sliding window, and the truncated reads shorter than 50 bp were discarded, reads containing ambiguous characters were also discarded; (2) only overlapping sequences longer than 10 bp were assembled according to their overlapped sequence. The Maximum mismatch ratio of overlapping region is 0.2. Reads that could not be assembled were discarded; (3) samples were distinguished according to the barcode and primers, and the sequence direction was adjusted, exact barcode Matching, 2 nucleotide mismatches in primer matching. Then, the optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1 [27] with 97% sequence similarity level. The most abundant sequence for each OTU was selected as a representative sequence. The OTU table was manually filtered, i.e., chloroplast and mitochondria sequences in all samples were removed. To minimize the effects of sequencing depth on alpha and beta diversity measures, the sequences from each sample were rarefied, which still yielded an average Good’s coverage of 99.09%, respectively. The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier version 2.2 [28] against the 16 S rRNA or ITS gene database using a confidence threshold of 70%. Finally, the community composition of each sample was counted at different taxonomic levels.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analysis of the leaf/soil microbiota was carried out using the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Based on the OTUs information, rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices including observed OTUs, Chao1 richness, Shannon index and Good’s coverage were calculated with Mothur v1.30.1 [29]. Group differences in alpha diversity were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The similarity among the microbial communities in different samples was determined by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity using Vegan v2.5-3 package. R language tools (version 3.3.1) was used for statistical analysis, and venn diagram, and heatmap figures. The species-level hierarchical clustering method is based on the average. The Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test was used to assess the percentage of variation explained by the treatment along with its statistical significance using Vegan v2.5-3 package.

Results

Quality control and analysis of sequencing data

According to the 16 S amplicon sequencing results, a total of 612,606 high-quality bacterial reads were obtained from all leaf samples (Table 1). After removing the low-quality and plant-derived reads, sample leveling was performed by the minimum number of sample sequences, 17,825 reads per sample were retained for subsequent analysis (Figure. 2 A). The remaining reads (267,375) were clustered into 415 endophytic bacterial OTUs at ≥ 97% sequence identity. We used amplicon sequencing of Marker genes to analyze the rhizosphere microbiota of apple and persimmon plants. We obtained 772,169 16 S rRNA gene reads and 841,011 internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) reads. After removing the low-quality and plant-derived reads, sample leveling was performed by the minimum number of sample sequences. A total of 32,316 and 59,637 reads per sample were retained for subsequent analysis (Figure. 2B, C). The remaining reads (387,792 and 715,644) were clustered into 3685 bacterial OTUs and 2187 fungi OTUs at ≥ 97% sequence identity. The rarefaction curves showed that the sequencing work was relatively comprehensive in covering the microbial diversity, as the curves tended to approach saturation, indicating that the selected sequence data adequately reflected the microbial abundance of these samples (Figure. 2 A, C).

Rarefaction curves of the OTU number at 97% similarity for different samples and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity analysis of microbial communities. A, D Endophytic bacteria B, E The bacterial microbiome in the rhizosphere C, F The fungal microbiome in the rhizosphere

On the other hand, the distributions of microbial composition in different apple and persimmon samples were detected on different taxonomy levels, and other diversity indices, including Shannon, Simpson, Ace, and Chao, were applied to estimate the microbial community complexity. All library coverage is above 98.14% (Table S1). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis revealed significant differences (p < 0.007, p < 0.001) in the composition of microbial communities in leaf or soil rhizosphere samples (Figure. 2D, F). The results indicate that apple plants actively recruit distinct microbial communities to colonize the rhizosphere and leaves under healthy, diseased, and intercropped conditions.

Shared and unique microbial communities present during intercropping

Venn diagram was used to identify the common and unique OTUs among different samples. The OTU abundance of HAL (279) was highest, the secondary was PL (167) and PaL (165), last but one was ApL (136), DAL has least OTU abundance. Five (5) leaves samples shared 60 same OTUs in terms of endophytic bacteria, but harbored 121, 17, 37, 16, and 33 unique OTUs respectively (Figure. 3 A). The OTU abundance of HASB, DASB, PSB, and APSB were 2987, 2691, 3050, and 2735 respectively. Four (4) rhizosphere soils shared 1822 same OTUs in terms of bacteria, but harbored 79, 156, 88, and 66 unique OTUs respectively (Figure. 3B). The OTU abundance of HASF, DASF, PSF, and APSF were 1064, 1193, 1210, and 1266 respectively. In terms of fungi, 4 rhizosphere soils shared 1822 for bacteria same OTUs, but harbored 161, 288, 191, and 234 unique OTUs respectively (Figure. 3 C).

Comparison of community’s differences at phylum level

The predominant bacterial components of samples from different treatments were similar (Figure. 4). The phyla showing a relative abundance exceeding 1% were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes in 5 leaves samples (Figure. 4 A). Proteobacteria was the predominant phylum, representing more than 89% of the total. Firmicutes (1.89%) and Bacteroidetes (0.28%) were the lowest in DAL leaves compared with other leaves samples, while the proportion of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria in DAL leaves was similar to that of HAL and ApL leaves (Figure. 4B). Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, Firmicutes, Gemmatimonadota, Bacteroidota, Myxococcota, and Patescibacteria showed a relative abundance higher than 1% in the 4 rhizosphere soils (Figure. 4 C). The proportion of Acidobacteriota and unclassified species in DASB was less than half compared to the other samples. In contrast, the crossed rhizosphere soil in APSB exhibited the highest proportion. In contrast, the three phyla Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Chloroflexi were slightly higher in DASB than in the remaining samples (Figure. 4D). The fungal phyla with relative abundance higher than 1% were Ascomycota, Mortierellomycota, Rozellomycota, and Basidiomycota (Figure. 4E). Ascomycota was the dominant phylum of all soil samples, accounting for more than 68%. The proportion of Mortierellomycota in DASF was less than half that of the other soil samples, while the proportion of Basidiomycota and unclassified species was higher than in the other soil samples. The proportion of Rozellomycota in HASF (13.18) and APSF(8.03) rhizosphere soil was higher than DASF(4.50) (Figure. 4 F).

Discussion

Here, we undertook a major effort to investigate control measures of apple root rot through microbiome manipulation involving the intercropping of persimmon and apple. We used high-throughput sequencing technology to analyze the effects of persimmon and apple cross planting on apple root rot from the perspective of microbial composition and diversity. Previously, the most important measures included reasonable crop rotation through an intercropping system, scientific and reasonable fertilization, soil disinfection, and disease-resistant varieties [30]. Extensive use of such methods could be time-consuming, laborious, and expensive. They might be helpful but have limited efficacy and are not sustainable long-term. Importantly, biological control has significant attention in crop disease prevention and control, because of its low toxicity and an environmentally friendly approach [31].

Bacteria or fungi play an important role in soil ecosystems and are critical for soil nutrient composition and recycling [29, 32]. In agriculture, chemical pesticides are widely used to control pests and diseases. However, the excessive use of pesticides can lead to pesticide residues and soil pollution, which may adversely affect the soil microbial community, thus affecting crop health and forming a vicious cycle. Studies have shown that pesticide application can reduce rice bacterial diversity and richness [32], and the application of dazomet and mancozeb can change the structure and diversity of the soil microbial community [33]. The richness and diversity of endophytic bacteria in apple leaves and rhizosphere soil microorganisms by intercropping apple and persimmon trees was higher than that of apple with root rot plants. The richness and diversity of rhizosphere soil fungi in healthy apples was lower than in apples with root rot. In contrast, the richness and diversity of endophytic bacteria and rhizosphere soil bacteria in healthy apples were higher than in apples with root rot. During millions of years of evolution, pathogens have been plants most intimate interacting partners. When apple trees are infected with root rot pathogens, the plants lose their resistance, providing opportunities for pathogenic opportunists. Moreover, the primary pathogens responsible for apple root rot are predominantly pathogenic fungi, which results in a negative correlation between increased fungal diversity in the rhizosphere of diseased plants and plant health. This phenomenon has also been substantiated in the study of strawberry root rot [34].

The accidental discovery of intercropping apple and persimmon trees can reduce the occurrence of root rot, which not only reduces the cost of pesticides but also gradually restores the soil microbial balance. In addition, some studies have shown that the bacterial richness and diversity of apple and other Rosaceae rhizosphere soil were significantly reduced after infection with pear fire blight [35]. The fungal diversity and richness of rhizosphere soil with apple root rot were significantly higher than those of healthy apples, as reported in our study [36]. Besides, Onwona-Kwakye [37] found that Enterobacter, Aeromonas, Comamonas, Stenotrophomonas, Bordetella, and Staphylococcus were reduced in rhizosphere soil after applying pesticide in paddy fields; in contrast, increased abundance of Domibacillus, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus was observed. Intercropping with persimmon trees altered the microbial communities on apple leaves and rhizosphere, and obtained several significant differences in microorganisms. In particular, the difference between diversified apple planting and diseased plants provides a theoretical basis for future studies on pesticide-resistant bacteria and research ideas for subsequent studies. Some studies have also shown that plant diversity enhances soil fungal diversity and resistance to plant invasion. The higher plant diversity results in a higher diversity of fungal pathogens, but the infection rate of pathogenic fungi [38] is lower.

Modern agriculture is basically a single planting mode, especially orchards, which results in high yield, but is usually more susceptible to diseases, pests, and the climate [39]. Increasing the plant diversity of the ecosystem can enhance the stability of the ecosystem and avoid consequences caused by biotic or abiotic stress, such as reducing the occurrence of diseases, such as wind-borne (rice blast) [40] and soil-borne disease (pepper blight) [18]. The imbalance between leaf regions and microbiota could destroy plant health. High humidity readily induces numerous phyllosphere diseases but exhibits humidity-dependent dyshomeostasis of the endophytic commensal bacterial community in the Phyllosphere [41]. Intercropping with aromatic plants (Ocimum basilicum, Tr1; Satureja hortensis, Tr2; Ageratum houstonianum, Tr3) has different effects on the diversity, structure, composition, and function of soil microbial communities in a pear orchard [42]. Alfalfa and mulberry intercropping improved the bacterial richness and diversity in rhizosphere soil [43]. Little research can explain how the plant microbiota and soil-borne diseases during intercropping are related. For the first time, the impact of apple and persimmon tree diversity on apple root rot was examined in this study from the standpoint of microbial composition and diversity in the rhizosphere or leaf. Although some dominating flora can be greatly impacted, the dominant flora remains similar following intercropping or single cropping. The relationship between affected flora and disease has not been resolved, but intercropping can affect the functional groups in the soil and microbes associated with the nitrogen cycle [42, 43]. The phyllosphere bacterial community in apple is mainly composed of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Acidobacteria, Tenericute, and Chloroflexi [44]. The bacterial community structure in the apple rhizosphere consists primarily of five dominant groups: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Chloroflexi, Acidobacteria, and Bacteroidetes [45, 46]. The predominant phylum of apple rhizosphere fungal community is mainly composed of Ascomycota, Zygomycota, Basidiomycota, Ciliophora, Glomeromycota, and Chytridiomycota [47].

Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are important biological resources for carbohydrate degradation, and simple or complex carbohydrates can produce many metabolites under anaerobic conditions [48, 49], including pyruvate or acetyl-CoA. The relative abundance of Acidobacteria is positively correlated with soil total nitrogen content [50] and can be used as an indicator of a poor soil environment [51]. Therefore, a high relative abundance of Acidobacteria is frequently interpreted as an indicator of a less fertile, less productive, or “poorer” soil environment from an agricultural productivity perspective. The lower abundance of this group indicated the higher soil quality [52]. In this study, the dominant phylum of endophytic bacteria was similar, but the relative abundance of some phyla changed. Firmicutes (1.89%) and Bacteroidetes (0.28%) in diseased apple leaves were in the lowest proportion than healthy or intercropping apple leaves. The dominant phylum of soil bacteria in the rhizosphere was similar as that observed Acidobacteria (5.97%) in the rhizosphere soil of apple root rot, less than half of the other samples (Figure. 4). Therefore, it is speculated that the carbohydrate degradation microbes in the healthy apple leaves are higher compared to diseased leaves. The proportion of Mortierellomycota and Rozellomycota in the rhizosphere soil with apple root rot was lower than healthy and intercropping apple rhizosphere soil. In comparison, the proportion of Basidiomycota was higher than other soil samples. The phylum Mortierellomycota contains some fungal populations with phosphate-solubilizing properties, which can dissolve phosphorus elements that are difficult to use in soil [53]. Rozellomycota was affiliated with the Cryptomycota, and this phylum’s function was unclear. Basidiomycota is the highest class of fungi, with about 25,000 known species containing both beneficial fungi (such as saprophytic fungi that can decompose plant residues) and some harmful fungi (Armillariella tabescens and other fungus that cause root rot). Ascomycota, which accounts for more than 60% of the population, also contains beneficial and harmful fungi. The diversity of fungal species is not necessarily a determinant of disease suppression, but it maintains the balance of fungal species under appropriately dominant species.

Given that their increased presence in the leaves is viewed as detrimental and may contribute to the disease, Pantoea and Serratia are most likely functioning as dysbiosis-associated taxa during apple root rot. Pantoea and Serratia are identified as leaf endophytic bacteria whose populations bloom during disease and are considered detrimental, contributing to or being strong indicators of the dysbiosis associated with root rot infection. The possible reason could be their pathogenic potential, which causes the microbial imbalance in the host [54]. Further, Streptomyces and Bacillus have been widely studied as potential microbes with good biological control potential. In this study, the relative abundance of Streptomyces and Bacillus was significantly higher in intercropping apple planting than in the tree with root rot. Then, they may biocontrol action against the fungi responsible for root rot. This provides a theoretical basis for the future application of beneficial microorganisms to adjust the balance of soil microbes to achieve disease prevention and growth promotion. The disease fungus, for example, Fusarium spp [55]. was not significantly different between the treatments, nor was it a dominant genus, especially in the rhizosphere soil.

In conclusion, we suggest that crop rotation positively affects the native microbiome and brings potential benefits to plant health. Here, intercropping diverse fruits (persimmon and apple) could maintain the normal microbial balance and reduce the incidence of the apple rot pathogen. This microbial balance (both for bacteria and fungi) shifts towards a positive trend during intercropping, protecting the host plant from several biotic and abiotic factors, and could serve as a positive indicator for plant health. Beneficial groups such as Streptomyces and Bacillus in healthy apple leaves and rhizosphere indicated the microbial balance during intercropping. Finally, we showed that the rhizosphere microbes in the apple orchard did not completely lose their balance compared to the leaf microbiome. Still, it should be checked regularly to avoid future loss of beneficial microbial diversity.
