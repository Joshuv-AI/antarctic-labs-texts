Quantitative phosphoproteomic approach to unveil potential protein biomarkers linked to high meat quality in ducks
Authors: Weiran Huo, Mengzhen Wang, Yunfan Han, Kaiqi Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thus far, potential biomarkers for assessing the quality of duck meat remain poorly characterized. However, with the advancement of omics technologies, protein post-translational modifications have been discovered as potential biomarkers of meat quality. In this study, high-quality (HQ) and normal-quality (NQ) meats were investigated from 65-day-old Liancheng White ducks and 37-day-old Cherry Valley ducks, and quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis was performed. The HQ duck meat was tender and mainly had a high content of intramuscular fat and protein. Moreover, A total of 1594 phosphopeptides corresponding to 892 phosphoproteins were identified, of which 170 up-regulated and 100 down-regulated phosphopeptides in HQ meat by quantitative phosphoproteomic. The majority of these differentially phosphorylated proteins were involved in carbohydrate metabolism, muscle fibers, and tight/adherens junctions, especially glycolytic enzymes. Furthermore, the phosphorylation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and phosphoglucomutase 1 may serve as potential candidate biomarkers for intramuscular fat content and tenderness in ducks, respectively. These data provide a phosphoproteomic perspective for biomarkers linked to high meat quality. This study provides a new strategy to improve the quality of duck meat.

Introduction

The production of duck meat, which is the second largest poultry product, was estimated at approximately 4,460,226 tons according to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization in 2017. China has the largest duck meat consumption worldwide, accounting for approximately 70% of the total consumption (Zeng et al., 2016). Meat quality is one of the most important economic traits of duck, affecting consumers’ acceptability and preference (England et al., 2013; Naveen et al., 2016). Different duck breeds or strains have different meat qualities and flavors. Traditionally, slow-growing ducks have an inferior growth rate with a poor feed conversion rate and are reared for a longer period as compared to fast-growing breeds, but the meat of slow-growing ducks has unique meat flavor and texture (Kwon et al., 2014). In the Chinese duck industry, fast-growing ducks at 28 to 45 days old are usually sold as conventional meat products. In contrast, the slow-growing ducks are usually slaughtered at a more mature age and are widely used in the production of high-quality (HQ) meat in China. However, accurately assessing and distinguishing HQ and normal-quality (NQ) meat, has become one of the most challenging problems in the meat industry. To date, conventional methods have been used to assess meat quality, such as colorimetry, texture analysis, and pH measurements; however, conventional methods are unable to determine the mechanisms that influence meat quality changes or predict meat quality (Huang et al., 2020). Meanwhile, many studies have used proteomics to distinguish and predict meat quality; however, these studies lack an across-the-board systematic summary of the biological mechanisms underlying meat quality traits (Wei et al., 2019). In this case, researchers have paid a lot of attention to interpreting the biological mechanisms underlying meat quality traits and to discovering biomarkers that evaluate and predict meat quality traits.

With the rapid development of omics techniques, the analysis of post-translational modifications of proteins has been adopted to discover potential biomarkers of meat quality. Liu et al. (2018) found that the level of phosphorylated phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1) showed diverse changes in high and low caprine meat quality. Furthermore, Silva et al. (2019) stated that the phosphorylation of PGM1 was negatively correlated with the Warner-Bratzler shear force in Nellore (Bos taurus indicus), suggesting that PGM1 could be used as a biomarker of meat tenderness. Hence, identifying potential phosphoprotein biomarkers can not only evaluate meat quality but also provide a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in high meat quality. Phosphoproteomic research has successfully linked biomarkers to meat quality in livestock and fish meat, such as pork (Li et al., 2015), sheep (Li et al., 2017), bulls and steers (Silva et al., 2019), and grass carp (Chen et al., 2020). However, there is still a lack of information on phosphorylated protein biomarkers linked to meat quality.

In the present study, the meat quality of Liancheng White (slow-growing) ducks and Cherry Valley (fast-growing) ducks at their respective marketable ages were characterized and compared, and their muscle fiber characteristics were determined. Quantitative MS-based phosphoproteomic analysis was performed to decipher the phosphoprotein as a potential candidate biomarker linked to HQ meat. The data provide a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in high meat quality, as well as a new strategy for evaluating and predicting the quality of duck meat.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The use of animals was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the School of Animal Science and Technology, Henan Normal University (Permit Number: HNSD-2024-07-21). Fifteen 37-day-old female fast-growing ducks (Cherry Valley ducks, SM3 heavy hybrid) and fifteen 65-day-old female slow-growing ducks (Liancheng White ducks) were obtained from LiHua Farming Co., Jiangsu, China. Animals were fasted for 12 h before slaughter, with free access to water. The birds were electronically stunned in a water bath (240 mA, 120 V, 5 s) and killed by neck cutting. The breast muscles (M. pectoralis major) were excised from the carcass. The samples were cooled at 4°C, packaged with plastic wraps, and transferred to the laboratory; 24 h post-mortem, samples (0.5 × 0.5 × 1.0 cm3) were cut and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for microstructural observation. Samples for protein extraction (1 g for each measurement) were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. The remaining samples were stored at 4°C to retain meat quality.

Meat quality measurements

For phenotypic measurements, nine ducks were randomly selected from the fifteen birds in each group. Shear force was evaluated in accordance with the method of Baublits et al. (2005). Water-holding capacity was evaluated using the pressing loss, as described by Joo (2018). The protein and intramuscular fat contents were analysed using FoodScan™ Analyser (Delta 320, Mettler-Toledo Group) via the methods described by Song et al. (2017). Our previous study describes these methods in detail (Huo et al., 2021). All traits were measured in triplicate, and then mean values were calculated.

Histology and immunohistochemistry analysis

Muscle samples were embedded in paraffin wax and sectioned into 5 µm. The slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, according to routine protocols. Approximately 200 fibres in each muscle sample section were counted. Muscle fibre characteristics were determined as previously described (Huo et al., 2021). Immunohistochemistry staining was performed as Kim and Yang (2016). Anti-fast myosin skeletal heavy chain (1:1,000, #ab51263; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-slow myosin skeletal heavy chain (1:4,000, #ab11083; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibodies were used.

Phosphoproteomic analysis

Nine duck samples in each group were chosen. Three individuals were pooled in each replicate of different groups to reduce the individual variation. Each group contained three biological replicates to reduce the individual variation. Protein extraction was performed as previously described (Mi et al., 2019). The supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C. Protein digestion was performed as previously described (Weng et al., 2021). Subsequently, 100 μg of peptide was labelled using Tandem Mass Tag 6-plex Labeling Kits and Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The phosphopeptides were enriched using TiO2 beads (batch mode) in accordance with Larsen et al. (2005). These enriched phosphopeptides were pooled and loaded onto a C18-reverse phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Easy Column). The peptide fractions were then analysed using an Easy nLC (Proxeon Biosystems, now Thermo Fisher Scientific). The method of phosphopeptide enrichment and LC-MS/MS analysis was that of our previous study (Weng et al., 2021).

The raw MS/MS data were analysed using MaxQuant engine (Max-Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Germany; version 1.5.5.1) against the Uniprot database: database, uniprot_Anas_platyrhynchos_44952_20190909. The quantification was based on our previous study (Weng et al., 2021). Bioinformatic analyses, such as cluster analysis, GO and KEGG annotation, were performed. The parameters used for the quantification of the MS/MS data and the software used for bioinformatics Nanalysis was listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical software (version 17.0, SPSS, Chicago IL, USA) was used for the data analysis. The results are expressed as means ± standard error (SE) of the mean. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to visualise the relationships between the differential abundance of phosphoproteins and phenotypes of duck meat quality. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05 and < 0.01.

Results

The breast meat of Liancheng White ducks showed better quality in terms of protein and intramuscular fat contents compared with that of Cherry Valley ducks (P < 0.01). The Cherry Valley ducks had a greater shear force than the Liancheng White ducks (P < 0.01). Next, the myofiber characteristics (area, diameter, density, and fibre type composition) of breast muscles in the HQ and NQ groups were investigated. The breast muscles consisted of one isoform, type II, in the HQ and NQ groups (Fig. 1B). The mean diameter, area and density were significantly different between the HQ and NQ groups (Fig. 1C). Phosphoprotein identification

To characterise the regulatory mechanism of improved meat quality in ducks, we employed quantitative phosphoproteomics to identify phosphorylated proteins (Fig. 2A). Based on the data, we identified 1594 unique phosphopeptides (2580 phosphosites) corresponding to 892 phosphoproteins (Fig. 2B). Of the identified phosphoproteins, 72.56% had molecular weights in the range of 10–150 kDa (1873) (Fig. 2C). Further analysis of the identified phosphorylation amino acids revealed that serine was the most frequently phosphorylated amino acid (74.5%), followed by threonine (18.14%) and tyrosine (7.36%) (Fig. 2C). In addition, 54.55% of the 892 phosphoproteins contained more than one distinct phosphorylation residue (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, the MEME software was used for the motif analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, 11 putative phosphorylation motifs were identified, including a proline-directed kinase substrate motif (pSP, pYXXP, and RXXpSP) and a basophilic kinase motif (LxRxxpS). The data shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 suggest that these motif sites may play a role in the glycolysis pathway.Fig. 2Phosphoproteome of HQ and NQ muscle meat. Phosphoproteins quantification

Of the 892 phosphoproteins, the phosphoproteins with a fold change >1.2 or <0.833 (HQ meat group/NQ meat group) and a P-value < 0.05, were determined to be differentially expressed phosphopeptides (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B, 270 phosphopeptides with varied abundance were verified, consisting of 100 phosphopeptides which were down-regulated (corresponding to 85 phosphoproteins) and 170 which were up-regulated (corresponding to 157 phosphoproteins). In addition, 270 significantly different phosphopeptides were subjected to hierarchical cluster analysis (Fig. 3C). All 270 phosphopeptides which displayed significant differences in phosphorylation levels were subjected to hierarchical clustering analysis. The colour distributions of the three biological replicates were similar in each group, and the two groups represented the characteristic patterns of phosphorylation. High levels of phosphorylation in the HQ muscle group were often associated with normal levels of phosphorylation in the NQ muscle group, and vice versa. Functional enrichment analysis

To further explore the impact of molecular mechanisms of differentially expressed phosphoproteins in the determination of duck meat quality, GO enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed. These differentially expressed phosphoproteins were classified into three categories based on the GO annotations (Fig. 4A). With regard to the biological process, differentially expressed phosphoproteins are proteins that primarily participate in the negative regulation of molecular function and metabolic processes. In the cellular component group, the differentially expressed phosphoproteins were mainly distributed in the myosin complex, axon part, and actin filament. Molecular function analysis indicated that binding and enzymatic catalytic activities were the major functions.Fig. 4GO (A) and pathway enrichment (B) analysis (Top20).Fig 4 dummy alt text

Based on a pathway enrichment analysis, these differentially expressed phosphoproteins were enriched in 172 pathways, including 15 pathways that were significantly enriched such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the HIF-1 signalling pathway (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). The most significant enrichment pathway was glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, where nine differentially expressed phosphoproteins were enriched (Supplementary Fig. 3). The results of the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the quality of duck meat is probably primarily influenced by carbohydrate metabolism.

To illustrate the assumed function and relationship between these differentially expressed phosphoproteins, protein-protein interaction networks for all differentially expressed phosphoproteins were constructed. Fig. 5 shows information about the clusters identified, such as carbohydrate metabolism, muscle fiber, and tight junctions. The majority of the cluster is that of the muscle fiber, and the cluster with the closest interactions is that of the carbohydrate metabolism-related phosphoproteins. It was speculated that the quality of duck meat may be associated with phosphoproteins, and muscle fiber- and carbohydrate metabolism-related phosphoproteins play major roles in this interaction.Fig. 5Protein–protein interaction networks.Fig 5 dummy alt text

Correlations between differentially abundant phosphoproteins and duck meat quality

According to the functional enrichment analysis, 38 key phosphoproteins were selected as key phosphoproteins related to meat quality, including 5 tight/adherens junction-, 15 carbohydrate metabolism-, and 18 muscle fiber-related proteins. Correlation analyses revealed 113 trends (P < 0.05) and 72 significant correlations (P < 0.01) between phosphoprotein abundance in muscle samples and meat quality traits (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 2). Glycolysis-related proteins were negatively correlated with the protein and intramuscular fat contents, but positively correlated with shear force, pressing loss, and fiber diameter. Tight/adherens junction-related proteins were positively correlated with the protein and intramuscular fat contents, but negatively correlated with shear force, pressing loss, and fiber diameter. Muscle fiber-related proteins, including myosin 3 (positions 1081, 1447, 1546, 1214, 1105, 1418, 1569, and 1027), titin (positions 32225, 18658, 24733, 14897, 20847, 21530, 20635, 19323, 27233, 25814, 19265, 28217, and 21657), tropomyosin 1 (position 161), myomesin 2 (positions 728 and 725), LIM domain binding 3 (position 220, 316), myosin heavy chain, skeletal muscle, adult (position 70), and actinin alpha 1 (positions 60, 415, 414, 599, 224, 45 and 37) were negatively correlated with the protein and intramuscular fat contents, but positively correlated with shear force, pressing loss, and fiber diameter. However, others, such as myosin 3 (position 233), titin (positions 1939, 31893, 17303, 32013, and 13467), and tropomyosin 1 (position 161) showed the opposite trend. Discussion

Duck meat is the second most widely consumed poultry meat worldwide after chicken, especially in China and developing countries (Wijaya et al., 2015). More than 30 native duck breeds are found in China. The proximate composition and physical properties are related to meat quality and vary with breed. Compared with slow-growing duck breeds, fast-growing duck breeds exhibit faster growth rates and higher feed conversion efficiency (usually slaughtered between 28 and 45 days of age). In contrast, slow-growing breeds grow more slowly and have lower feed conversion ratios (usually slaughtered at a more mature age), yet they are often favored for their superior meat quality. In the present study, Liancheng White (slow-growing) ducks and Cherry Valley (fast-growing) ducks were selected at 65 and 37 days of age, respectively, at which point each had attained its typical market body weight. Their proximate composition and physical properties were investigated. Under this market-age comparison, the slow-growing Liancheng White ducks showed higher protein and intramuscular fat content and lower shear force than Cherry Valley ducks. Thus, the Liancheng White ducks were assigned to the HQ group, and the Cherry Valley ducks were assigned to the NQ group.

Skeletal muscle is the main component of meat, and several researchers have confirmed that muscle fiber characteristics have been widely recognized as key determinants of meat quantity and quality (Weng et al., 2021). In the present study, no difference in muscle fiber types was found; the breast muscle in two duck breeds is entirely composed of fast-twitch fibers, which consistent with the previous studies (Huo et al., 2021). Therefore, we concluded that muscle fiber development, rather than fiber type, contributed to the observed variations in meat quality. This conclusion is supported by our finding that significant differences in fiber diameter, fiber density, and fiber area were observed between the HQ and NQ groups. However, the regulatory mechanisms of muscle development and meat quality differences in HQ and NQ groups have not been well characterized.

The effect of protein phosphorylation on meat quality was recently tested (D'Alessandro and Zolla, 2013). The mechanisms by how protein phosphorylation at specific sites regulates meat quality have been demonstrated (Liu et al., 2018). In this study, MS-based quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis was performed to investigate the possible molecular mechanisms by which protein phosphorylation at specific sites were related to meat quality and provides useful strategies to ensure HQ meat though the application of phosphoprotein biomarkers.

Glycolysis plays a critical role in meat quality as it is directly or indirectly associated with meat quality traits such as drip loss, pH, and tenderness (Chen et al., 2019). Huang et al. (2011) reported that specific phosphorylation levels of phosphoproteins were associated with glycolysis by regulating the activity or stability of glycolytic enzymes, such as glycogen phosphorylase and pyruvate kinase. In the present study, HQ muscle showed significantly down-regulated phosphorylation of glycolytic enzymes, such as PGM1, phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK1), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Consequently, we hypothesized that phosphorylation levels of these enzymes correlate with glycolytic rates during the early postmortem stage in HQ and NQ meat. Actually, it was reported that GAPDH phosphorylation can contribute to the enhancement of GAPDH activation of GAPDH was up-regulated when the GAPDH phosphorylation was increased (Baba et al., 2010). Silva et al. (2019) declared that PGM1 enzymatic activity was significantly increased after phosphorylation. According to Chen et al. (2019), a high level of phosphorylated glycolytic enzymes resulted in a high rate of lactate formation and a rapid pH decline in ovine muscle. Therefore, we inferred that glycolytic enzymes activation were up-regulated with an increase in its phosphorylation, an excessively rapid postmortem glycolysis leads to swift lactic acid accumulation and a sharp pH decline. This precipitous pH drop can cause myofibrillar protein denaturation, reduced water-holding capacity, and structural shrinkage—effects that are generally unfavorable for meat tenderization (Huff-Lonergan and Lonergan, 2005). Therefore, we propose that the down‑regulated phosphorylation of glycolytic enzymes in HQ meat suggests a moderated glycolytic rate, a milder acidification and a more gradual pH decline, thereby reducing shear force and enhancing tenderness, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. To predict meat quality, phosphoproteins involved in glycolysis were regarded as potential candidate biomarkers. In the glycolysis pathway, PGM1 is the key player in catalysing the reversible interconversion of glucose 6-phosphate and glucose 1-phosphate, which plays a critical role in energy metabolism (Anderson et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2018). In the present study, down-regulated phosphorylation of PGM1 phosphoproteins was observed in HQ meat, which positively correlated with shear force. Thus, the phosphorylation of PGM1 was closely related to tenderness, and may serve as a potential candidate biomarker for tenderness in ducks.

The intramuscular fat content is a major meat quality trait that plays an important role in quality grading and carcass pricing (Scollan et al., 2017). A previous study investigated glucose as the main carbon source for the de novo fatty acid synthesis of intramuscular fat (Smith and Crouse, 1984). Moreover, the triglyceride synthesis relies on glycerol-3P produced by glycolysis (Nye et al., 2008). In the present study, the proteins involved in carbohydrate metabolism, especially the glycolysis process, were identified to be the largest group of proteins with significantly changed phosphorylation levels in muscles of different intramuscular fat content. Thus, it can be speculated that the phosphorylation of glycolytic enzyme might play essential roles in intramuscular fat deposition through regulating of glycolysis. Another study on steaks has indicated that the greater glycolysis along with reduced oxidative phosphorylation enhances the synthesis of glycerol-3P and fatty acids from glucose, supporting the higher intramuscular fat content (Silva et al., 2019). However, the effect of protein phosphorylation at the specific sites, which were identified to be color-related in this study, on its structure, function and activity is still unclear and further research was needed. In addition, phosphoproteome analysis indicated that GAPDH is a key intramuscular fat-related enzyme, which acts at the junction of oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, and fatty acid metabolism (Mráček et al., 2013). The phosphorylation level of GAPDH differed significantly between the HQ and NQ groups. There is a significant negative correlation between GAPDH phosphoproteins and the intramuscular fat content. Thus, GAPDH, as a phosphoprotein, represents a potential candidate biomarker for intramuscular fat content of ducks, although further functional validation is warranted.

Phosphoproteins involved in cell adhesion, including catenin (cadherin-associated protein) delta 1 (CTNND1), and tight junction protein 1 (TJP1), exhibited higher phosphorylation levels in HQ meats than in NQ. Moreover, the post-mortem degradation of focal adhesion molecules impacts the generation of drip channels, which could facilitate drip loss and affect meat quality (Zhang et al., 2006). TJP1, a member of the tight junction protein, is responsible for the protein networks between actin and integral tight junction proteins (Itoh et al., 1999; Tuomi et al., 2009). CTNND1 is a substrate of the oncogenic tyrosine kinase Src, and CTNND1 is defined as a component of the adherens junction complex that includes beta-catenin (Liao et al., 2017; Shibamoto et al., 1995). The extracellular domain of cadherin participates in Ca2+-dependent, homophilic cell-to-cell adhesion, and the cytoplasmic domain is associated with a family of proteins called catenins (Kemler, 1992; Takeichi, 1991). The phosphorylation state of β-catenin is crucial for its association with cadherin and, thus, the association of cadherin with the cytoskeleton (Balsamo et al., 1998). Phosphorylation of tyrosine 654 residue reduces the affinity of β-catenin for cadherin, potentially disrupting their association (Piedra et al., 2001; Roura et al., 1999). These results were similar to those of the present study, which showed negative correlations between the phosphorylation of tyrosine 551 and pressing loss. In duck muscle, phosphorylation changes of adhesion-related proteins would also contribute to the drip formation between cell membranes and the cell cytoskeleton; thus, a higher level of phosphorylation could increase drip. However, the pressing loss measured in the HQ and NQ groups, showed no difference between the groups. In subsequent studies, we will further validate the function of those selected key phosphoproteins at specific sites. The corresponding kinases and phosphatases will be screened to assess their effects on phosphorylation levels and meat quality, both in vitro and in vivo. We will further attempt to modulate phosphorylation levels through targeted injection of relevant kinases or phosphatases into ducklings, and evaluate the resulting effects on meat quality traits at market age.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that slow-growing ducks had mainly high contents of protein and intramuscular fat, and their meat was tender. The phosphorylation of some glycolytic enzymes at specific sites might play a crucial role in the quality of duck meat through the regulation of glycolysis. In addition, we observed that phosphorylation of GAPDH may serve as a potential candidate biomarker for intramuscular fat content, while phosphorylated PGM1 may similarly serve as a potential candidate biomarker for tenderness in duck meat. Overall, our findings provide a better understanding of the mechanism by which protein phosphorylation at specific sites regulates meat quality and provide a new strategy to ensure HQ meat through the application of phosphoprotein biomarkers.
