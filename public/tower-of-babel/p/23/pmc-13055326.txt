Feasibility and early outcomes of a multi-site TeleHospitalist admissions programme: staff and patient perspectives from a large health system
Authors: Ngoc-Anh Nguyen, Sarah Sossong, Farhan Ishaq, Henry Ellison, Grace Lee, Lindsay Randle, Melissa Gomez, Jannika Machnik, Sarah Pletcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaffing shortages contribute to admission delays, fragmented care and hospitalist burnout. Although telemedicine has been widely adopted in critical care and subspecialties, its use in general inpatient admissions remains understudied. This study evaluated the feasibility, operational performance and early perceptions of a TeleHospitalist admissions programme designed to address nocturnal staffing shortages in a large academic health system.MethodsIn October 2024, Houston Methodist launched a nocturnal TeleHospitalist programme. The programme provided centralised coverage via audio-video technology, operating from 18:00 to 02:00 initially and expanding to 18:00–06:00. Adult patients admitted from the emergency department (ED) between October 2024 and August 2025 and clinical staff involved in admission workflows, were surveyed. Operational data on admission timeliness were extracted from the Epic electronic medical record, and patient and staff experiences were evaluated through structured surveys and qualitative feedback.ResultsOver 10 months, 1575 TeleHospitalist encounters were completed (median age 65 years (IQR 49 to 75); 56% were ≥65 years). Median hospital length of stay was 38.5 hours (IQR 17.3 to 66.3), and median ED arrival-to-admission time was 7.3 hours (IQR 4.7 to 12.8). Among 311 patient respondents (19.7%; median age 66.5 years (IQR 52.3 to 75.6); 5% were ≥65 years), satisfaction scores were high (median 4 (IQR 3 to 5). Staff responses (n=29) demonstrated strong endorsement: 86% favoured continuation, 79% reported comfort with the technology and 75% rated admission processes as better than traditional workflows.ConclusionsA system-wide TeleHospitalist admissions programme was feasible and well accepted by patients and staff. Older adults reported positive experiences, suggesting age is not a barrier to virtual inpatient care. Early findings indicate improved timeliness, communication and workflow efficiency while mitigating overnight staffing gaps. Future studies will evaluate long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness and strategies to enhance privacy and patient engagement.

Introduction

Evolution of the hospitalist model

The hospitalist model, introduced by Wachter and Goldman in 1996,1 has become a cornerstone of inpatient care in the USA. Hospitalists, physicians whose primary responsibility is the medical care of hospitalised patients, have been associated with improved outcomes and enhanced care coordination.2 3 Since its inception, hospital medicine has become one of the fastest-growing specialties, with over 80% of hospitals employing hospitalists and more than 70% of hospitalised patients receiving care from hospitalist teams.4 5 Studies consistently show that hospitalist care is linked to improved guideline adherence,6 reduced length of stay,7 lower costs,8 more timely admissions,9 fewer adverse events,10 reduced 30-day readmissions,11 fewer intensive care unit (ICU) transfers12 and better outcomes for common conditions such as heart failure, pneumonia and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.13

Ongoing staffing challenges

Despite these achievements, the hospitalist field faces persistent workforce challenges. Staffing shortages, particularly overnight, strain continuity and capacity. The widely adopted ‘7-on/7-off’ schedule, once attractive for work-life balance, now contributes to burnout and is increasingly misaligned with newer generations of physicians seeking flexibility and autonomy. Nocturnist recruitment remains especially difficult due to circadian disruption, childcare demands and lifestyle constraints. However, nearly half of all hospitalist admissions (approximately 48.6%) occur at night,12 creating a persistent mismatch between demand and available coverage.

Variability in staffing models

Hospitals have experimented with multiple staffing strategies. Some designate ‘admitters’ for peak periods or rotate nocturnist duties. Others use closed systems, in which a single group manages all admissions, promoting standardisation but limiting flexibility.13 In contrast, open systems allow multiple groups to share responsibility, increasing adaptability but risking inconsistency and fragmented care.14 15 Understaffing contributes to delays, burnout and turnover,1618 while overstaffing during off-peak hours undermines financial efficiency.13 19 20 In decentralised or hybrid models, diffuse oversight may further contribute to variability in quality and performance across sites.21 22

Emergence of TeleHospitalist models

To address these operational gaps, health systems have begun piloting virtual or ‘TeleHospitalist’ programmes. These models leverage audio-video technology to centralise after-hours coverage, enabling remote physicians to perform admissions across multiple hospitals in real time. By decoupling physical presence from clinical capacity, TeleHospitalist programmes offer a scalable solution to staffing variability, potentially reducing delays and improving throughput.

Workforce adaptability and retention

Beyond operational advantages, virtual models broaden the hospitalist workforce. They support more flexible and sustainable scheduling arrangements, appealing to clinicians for whom traditional nocturnist work is less feasible, such as parents with caregiving responsibilities, newly trained attendings seeking supplemental shifts, seasonal remote workers, travel-based professionals and semiretired clinicians. By accommodating these preferences, TeleHospitalist programmes may help retain skilled clinicians who might otherwise leave the workforce, strengthening resilience and continuity of care amid widespread burnout.

Impact of COVID-19 and remaining gaps

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of TeleHospitalist programmes by normalising virtual workflows, demonstrating adaptability to remote interactions and expanding reimbursement pathways. Early implementations focused on rural or under-resourced facilities lacking on-site night coverage. More recently, large health systems have adopted centralised overnight models to mitigate staffing shortages and improve efficiency.

Despite growing interest, published evidence on TeleHospitalist implementation remains limited, particularly within large, multi-site systems. Key questions persist regarding patient and staff acceptance, workflow integration and operational impact. This report evaluates these early implementation outcomes, operational performance and perceptions from both patients and staff across a multi-hospital health system.

Methods

Study design

This study follows the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence V.2.0 guidelines. It was a quality improvement (QI) initiative designed to evaluate early implementation outcomes, operational feasibility and user experience associated with a nocturnal TeleHospitalist programme. A mixed-methods design was employed, integrating descriptive analysis of quantitative operational data and survey results with qualitative feedback from patients and clinical staff.

Setting and programme context

Houston Methodist is a multi-hospital academic health system comprising fully employed, hybrid and private-practice physician groups across academic and community-based sites. These arrangements introduce significant variability in admission workflows, clinical staffing and resource allocation.

Prior to implementation, the system frequently encountered overnight volume surges that could not be dynamically managed due to fragmented, site-specific staffing structures. The decentralised coverage model and lack of centralised oversight hindered the system’s ability to level the load across hospitals. As a result, patients waiting in the emergency department (ED) often experienced prolonged treatment gaps before inpatient placement. Once transferred to the floor, they were frequently received by day-shift hospitalists with no prior involvement in their care and no established plan, exacerbating downstream delays in clinical decision-making.

Efforts to expand overnight coverage were constrained by recruitment limitations and staffing shortages. These structural barriers underscored the need for an innovative, scalable solution that could transcend site-specific constraints. The patient population requiring overnight admission typically included high-acuity conditions such as stroke, sepsis, heart failure and metabolic emergencies, where delays in hospitalist evaluation could affect outcomes.

Programme implementation

In October 2024, Houston Methodist launched a nocturnal TeleHospitalist programme designed to centralise after-hours admissions, reduce site-level variability and improve responsiveness to overnight demand. The programme initially operated from 18:00 to 02:00, Monday through Friday, at Houston Methodist Main and three freestanding emergency care centres (ECCs): Pearland, Voss and Kirby ECCs. It expanded to 18:00–06:00, seven nights per week and added Houston Methodist Baytown in May 2025.

The TeleHospitalist admissions model leveraged the care.ai platform (Orange County, Florida, USA), which integrates real-time audio-video communication, remote physical exam tools and digital ‘knock’ room-entry signalling. Licensed hospitalists, credentialed across all participating sites, conducted remote admissions in real time, collaborating with ED physicians, bedside nurses and bed coordinators to evaluate patients, initiate treatment plans and place orders. TeleHospitalists were responsible for managing patients throughout their shift, including all communication, documentation and handoffs; formal sign-out to in-person teams ensured continuity of care.

Programme implementation was led by a multidisciplinary team of leaders in hospital medicine, emergency medicine, operations and QI. Key activities included an analysis of historical ED admission volumes and surge patterns to guide site selection; development of standardised workflows for consult initiation, escalation and cross-coverage; deployment of endpoint technology and clinical documentation infrastructure and training for both virtual and on-site staff. The team also established operational metrics to monitor timeliness, safety and responsiveness, ensuring the programme’s performance could be evaluated in real time. Clinical selection criteria emphasised institutional familiarity, high-quality performance and aptitude for virtual care. Remote physicians were onboarded with standards for professionalism, camera-enabled assessments, synchronous communication and appropriate physical workspaces to promote patient trust and engagement.

Throughout the rollout, frontline feedback was incorporated to refine workflows and address operational barriers. Patient experience surveys began at launch (October 2024), and a comprehensive staff survey followed in July 2025 after expansion. While early feedback was favourable, findings were primarily subjective; future evaluation efforts will incorporate objective metrics such as time to medication administration, documentation timeliness and throughput.

Initial staffing consisted of moonlighting physicians with full-time clinical roles elsewhere. While this allowed rapid deployment, it introduced variability in continuity and limited the clinician pool. The first full-time hospitalist was hired in November 2024 and in May 2025, the model transitioned to a core team of two full-time TeleHospitalists providing dedicated overnight coverage (18:00–06:00) on a week-on/week-off basis. Recruitment for two additional full-time hires was underway by mid-2025 to support expansion and scheduling stability.

QI framework

This initiative was implemented using a structured QI methodology consistent with the Plan-Do-Study-Act framework. Prior to implementation, formal process mapping was created to analyse baseline overnight admission workflows and identify key stakeholder roles, escalation pathways, documentation and handoff processes.

Each physician hired into the programme in a full-time capacity underwent multiple structured reviews of clinical documentation and billing practices to ensure baseline completeness, accuracy and accordance with institution standards.

Following implementation, ongoing monitoring through formal internal auditing, survey feedback and operational metrics supported programme refinement and adaptation, which led to multiple actionable process improvements.

Patient survey

Patient experience with the TeleHospitalist programme was evaluated through a post-consultation survey administered in real time at the bedside or electronically after discharge via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) (online supplemental multimedia Appendix 1; TeleHospitalist Patient Experience Survey). Eligible participants were adult patients admitted from the ED and evaluated by a TeleHospitalist between October 2024 and August 2025. The instrument consisted of six 5-point Likert-scale items and one open-ended question addressing satisfaction, timeliness, comfort with the platform and overall impressions. A total of 1575 patients met eligibility criteria, and 311 responses (19.7%) were received as of 31 August 2025. Response rates and thematic findings are reported in the Results section.

Staff surveys

A comprehensive staff survey was administered in July 2025 via SurveyMonkey (online supplemental multimedia Appendix 2; TeleHospitalist Staff Experience Survey, 2025) to frontline stakeholders across all participating sites, including ED nurses, ED physicians, inpatient providers and care coordinators. This 12-item instrument included Likert-scale items, comparative questions about virtual versus in-person care, a Net Promoter Score question and an open-ended feedback field.

29 staff members completed the survey, representing ED registered nurses (RNs) (62%, n=18), ED physicians (17%, n=5), ED charge nurses or managers (10%, n=3), TeleHospitalists (10%, n=3) and admitting providers (3%, n=1). Most responses were from Houston Methodist Hospital Main (76%, n=22), with additional participation from Pearland ECC (17%, n=5), Kirby ECC (14%, n=4), Baytown Hospital (10%, n=3) and Voss ECC (3%, n=1). Participation was voluntary, no incentives were offered and no identifiable data were collected.

Data sources and analytical approach

Quantitative data included patient demographic information, operational metrics (eg, time to consult, note completion time and length of stay) and structured survey responses from patients and staff. Operational data were extracted from the Epic electronic medical record (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, Wisconsin, USA) and the care.ai platform and summarised monthly by site; the admission note timestamps served as a proxy for the timing of TeleHospitalist consults.

Survey instruments were developed by the research team based on institutional experience and reviewed for face validity. Open-ended and qualitative responses were coded by two independent reviewers using an inductive thematic framework, with discrepancies being resolved by consensus. Descriptive statistics were used for means and medians, and Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied for median comparisons. Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio (V.2025.09.0; Posit Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). The study was determined to be exempt by the Houston Methodist Institutional Review Board. All data were collected and analysed retrospectively as part of a routine programme evaluation (figure 2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the initial design or reporting of this study. However, their feedback obtained through structured post-admission surveys directly informed iterative programme adjustments, including modifications to privacy options and communication processes within the TeleHospitalist workflow.

Results

This section presents early findings from the first 10 months of the TeleHospitalist programme, integrating operational metrics with patient and staff perspectives to assess feasibility, value and implementation outcomes. A total of 1575 TeleHospitalist encounters were documented across participating sites as of 31 August 2025.

Patient population and demographic context

Between October 2024 and August 2025, 1575 patients were admitted through the TeleHospitalist programme following ED evaluation. The median age of the admitted cohort was 65 years (IQR 49 to 75), with over half (55%) aged 65 years or older. Adults aged 30–64 years comprised the remaining 45%. This age distribution reflects a typical adult inpatient population and underscores the relevance of TeleHospitalist services for older adults, who are particularly vulnerable to delays in overnight care and whose needs are often time-sensitive.

Clinical case mix and acuity

Between October 2024 and August 2025, the most common admission diagnoses were dehydration and hepatic encephalopathy, each 3.0% (47 cases). This was followed by hypokalaemia (2.5%), hyponatraemia (2.0%) and shortness of breath (2.0%). Together, these conditions represent the leading diagnoses observed in the cohort. These findings illustrate the model’s application to a diverse adult population with common acute medical conditions.

Patient admission metrics

Across 1575 encounters, the median hospital length of stay was 38.5 hours (IQR 17.3 to 66.3). The median time from ED arrival to inpatient admission was 7.3 hours (IQR 4.7 to 12.8). Operationally, the median interval from bed order to patient assignment was 41.3 min (IQR 21.7 to 74.8) and from assignment to service start was 55.9 min (IQR 14.9 to 127.7). These results demonstrate that virtual admission workflows were integrated efficiently, without prolonging throughput times across participating sites. Patient survey results

Of the 1575 patients admitted through the TeleHospitalist programme, 311 (19.7%) completed the post-admission survey. The six-item instrument (5-point Likert scale; 1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree) assessed patient satisfaction, perceived quality, comfort with virtual admission and related domains. Across all six items, the median response was 4 (IQR 3 to 5), indicating favourable ratings across every domain (table 1). All six survey domains demonstrated significantly positive ratings (p<0.001). Response distributions did not vary meaningfully by encounter hour (online supplemental multimedia Appendix 5A), and overall satisfaction scores remained consistently high throughout all night-shift hours (online supplemental multimedia Appendix 5B).

Of the 311 respondents, most described the virtual admission process as timely and easy to navigate, frequently citing faster access to physicians and earlier symptom management as key advantages. A smaller subset raised concerns about privacy, particularly when assessments occurred in shared or hallway spaces or about the perceived impersonality of virtual encounters. These findings suggest that environmental and communication factors play important roles in shaping virtual care experiences.

Of the 311 patients who completed the survey, 49 provided open-text comments. Open-ended responses (n=49) were thematically coded into five positive and five negative domains. The mean respondent age was 63.3±16.7 years (median 66.5 (IQR 52.3 to 75.6)); 56% were aged 65 years or older.

Many patients expressed conditional acceptance of the virtual model, preferring in-person care in general but valuing the virtual encounter when it enabled faster evaluation and responsive communication (see online supplemental multimedia Appendix 3).

Staff experience: qualitative insights

In parallel with structured surveys, informal qualitative feedback was collected throughout late 2024 during implementation meetings, operational walk-throughs and ad hoc staff interactions. These early comments, primarily from ED nurses, TeleHospitalists and physician leaders, offered valuable insights into the model’s perceived accessibility, efficiency and integration within existing workflows.

Key themes from frontline staff included improved accessibility, faster order placement and greater operational efficiency. Staff described the virtual physicians as ‘easy to reach’, and several nurses noted that orders were placed more quickly and patient movement between rooms was streamlined. Participants consistently expressed strong support for expanding coverage, emphasising the need for ‘24/7 service’ and identifying an initial learning curve as nurses adapted to staying in rooms during virtual rounding.

Physician leaders echoed these sentiments, describing the initial rollout as ‘an overall success’ and advocating for broader adoption, including full overnight and potentially 24/7 coverage. While these early impressions were largely positive, they likely reflected a small and self-selected group of participants. Nonetheless, the feedback informed iterative workflow refinements and supported leadership decisions to expand programme scope and staffing. These insights highlight both perceived strengths and operational considerations across key domains such as communication, role clarity and virtual team integration (see online supplemental multimedia Appendix 4).

Staff survey results (quantitative)

In July 2025, a 12-item staff survey was distributed to a broad group of frontline stakeholders, including TeleHospitalists and ED physicians, nurses and charge nurses/managers, to assess three key domains: (1) experience with the virtual admission process; (2) comparative perceptions of virtual versus in-person care and (3) overall endorsement of the programme, including the Net Promoter Score (NPS) (online supplemental multimedia Appendix 2).

Overall programme support and satisfaction

Staff demonstrated strong support for continuing the TeleHospitalist programme, with 86% expressing either support (41%, n=12) or strong support (45%, n=13) for programme continuation. Only 14% (n=4) were neutral, and no respondents opposed continuation. This high level of endorsement was further reflected in the NPS of +7, with 28% of respondents classified as promoters (scores 9–10), 52% as passives (scores 7–8) and 21% as detractors (scores 0–6).

Technology comfort and workflow integration

Staff reported high comfort levels with virtual care technology, with 79% indicating they felt ‘comfortable’ (43%, n=12) or ‘very comfortable’ (36%, n=10) using the video platform and electronic systems. Only 4% (n=1) reported feeling ‘uncomfortable’, and 14% (n=4) were neutral.

Communication effectiveness

When comparing virtual workflows to in-person admissions, 42% (n=12) of staff reported that communication was better than in traditional workflows (21% ‘slightly better’, 21% ‘much better’). Half (50%, n=14) felt communication was ‘about the same’, and 7% (n=2) found it ‘slightly worse’; none rated communication as ‘much worse’.

Impact on admission process

Staff perceived significant improvements in the admissions process compared with in-person workflows. 75% (n=21) indicated that the telehealth model had a positive impact (36% ‘much better’, 39% ‘slightly better’). The remaining 25% (n=7) rated the impact as ‘about the same’, with no respondents rating the virtual model as worse than traditional approaches.

Care quality and timeliness

When rating the timeliness and appropriateness of care for patients admitted through the programme, 72% (n=21) of staff provided positive ratings (17% ‘excellent’, 55% ‘good’). 24% (n=7) were neutral, and only one respondent (3%) rated care quality as ‘very poor’.

Patient understanding and acceptance

Regarding patient understanding of TeleHospitalist benefits, 65% (n=19) of staff felt patients had at least moderate understanding (48% ‘moderately well’, 17% ‘very well’). However, 17% (n=5) felt patients had ‘little’ or ‘no understanding’ of the benefits, and 17% (n=5) were unsure.

Workflow challenges

When asked about recurring workflow challenges, 48% (n=14) reported none, 28% (n=8) identified ongoing issues and 24% (n=7) were unsure. Commonly cited challenges included unclear roles, delayed handoffs and technical difficulties. Taken together, staff feedback reflected both operational advantages and recurring implementation challenges associated with the TeleHospitalist model (table 2).

Discussion

Patient perspectives

Survey responses suggested a broadly positive reception across domains, with the highest ratings for comfort interacting with virtual physicians. Patients frequently cited faster access to care and earlier pain management as meaningful benefits. The predominance of older adults among participants suggests that the model aligns well with populations most affected by overnight care delays.

Qualitative comments revealed important nuances. Some patients were uncomfortable with virtual assessments conducted in hallways or shared spaces, raising concerns about privacy or personal connection. In response to this feedback, we introduced the option for patients to transfer audio from the care.ai platform to their personal devices via Bluetooth, allowing for a more private and individualised interaction. Others questioned the thoroughness of remote evaluations. Still, many patients accepted the virtual format when it was framed as a way to receive care more quickly, particularly for pain or prolonged ED waits. These findings underscore the importance of setting expectations at the point of care, clearly communicating the TeleHospitalist’s role in admission and follow-up and ensuring privacy safeguards to optimise acceptance.

Staff perspectives

Staff feedback aligned with patient perceptions, highlighting faster care initiation, greater provider accessibility and improved continuity. Nurses found TeleHospitalists more accessible, while physician leaders endorsed broader adoption. Survey results showed strong support: 86% favoured continuation (NPS +7), 79% reported comfort with technology and most rated communication as equal to or better than in-person workflows. Admission processes were viewed favourably by 75%. Key challenges included privacy (62%), technical issues (48%) and perceived loss of personalisation (45%).

Clinical context and operational evolution

Consistent with national data, adults admitted to the hospital from the ED are typically older than those discharged without admission, and the likelihood of admission rises steeply with age.23 Given this age distribution, our cohort reflects the expected demographic of ED admissions, suggesting that TeleHospitalist programmes are feasible and well-received among older adults. The programme served a variety of high-acuity conditions for which the timeliness of care is crucial. Frequent encounters with electrolyte disturbances and dehydration underscored the value of early intervention. During the first year, a major structural evolution, from moonlighting physicians to a dedicated team of full-time TeleHospitalists, addressed variability in coverage, engagement and workflow consistency. The move to a week-on/week-off schedule facilitated closer integration with ED teams and more consistent clinical handoffs, strengthening operational stability.

Limitations

This evaluation has several limitations. The patient survey response rate (19.74%) raises the possibility of response bias. Surveys were not formally validated, and response timing varied across sites, possibly limiting generalisability. Staff survey timing after programme maturation may have captured more favourable impressions than would have been seen earlier. Additionally, as a quality-improvement initiative rather than a prospective study, data collection was not standardised across sites. These constraints may limit definitive conclusions but provide important real-world insights into feasibility and perceived value.

Broader implications

This evaluation adds to the emerging literature on virtual inpatient care by demonstrating feasibility in a large academic health system and highlighting alignment between patient and staff perceptions of timeliness benefits. The convergence of these perspectives suggests that TeleHospitalist programmes may effectively mitigate overnight admission delays while maintaining quality and satisfaction.

Equally important are the design features that shape success. Private consultation spaces, proactive patient communication, reliable technology infrastructure and dedicated physician staffing emerged as key determinants of acceptance. Variability in adoption across sites underscores the need for local adaptation of workflows and culture.

Organisations pursuing similar models should recognise that virtual care is not merely a technical solution but a redesign of clinical workflows and relationships. Structured feedback loops with frontline staff and patients are critical for iterative improvement. Attention to privacy, communication and clinician engagement will be essential.

Conclusions

The early implementation of Houston Methodist’s nocturnal TeleHospitalist programme demonstrates that TeleHospitalist coverage is operationally feasible and well accepted by patients and staff. Strong staff support, positive patient satisfaction and perceived improvements in timeliness suggest that virtual models can address overnight staffing challenges while maintaining quality of care. While definitive evidence of clinical impact awaits outcome-based evaluation, these early findings indicate that TeleHospitalist programmes represent a promising strategy to enhance efficiency and access in acute care. Future studies should incorporate objective outcome metrics and identify operational factors that predict successful implementation and sustainability.
