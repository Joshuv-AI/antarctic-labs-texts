Anatomic Reinsertion of Isolated Proximal Semimembranosus Tendon Avulsion in Athletic Population: A Technical Note
Authors: Nicolas Lefèvre, Ali Alayane, Mohamad K Moussa, Yoann Bohu, Alain Meyer, Alexandre Hardy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute hamstring tears are common athletic injuries. Most of these injuries involve muscle belly strain or affect the myotendinous junction. These lesions can often be managed nonoperatively, resulting in a satisfactory functional outcome after an appropriate rehabilitation program. However, acute proximal hamstring avulsion injury occurs less commonly, and early surgical reinsertion is recommended to avoid long-term functional disabilities and to allow a successful return to sport. In this article, we describe a step-by-step surgical technique for reinsertion of proximal isolated semimembranosus tendon avulsion in the athletic population, highlighting the significance of the preoperative radiologic cobra-sign diagnosis and its intraoperative manifestation in this rare injury.

The hamstring group involves 3 muscles: biceps femoris, semimembranosus (SM), and semitendinosus.1 The SM muscle originates from the superolateral part of the ischial tuberosity, while the semitendinosus and the long head of the biceps femoris form the conjoint tendon, originating from the inferomedial part of the ischial tuberosity. The main function of these muscles is to provide hip joint extension and knee joint flexion.2 Hamstring muscle belly strains and myotendinous junction lesions represent the most common form of hamstring muscle athletic injury.3 Although rare, proximal hamstring avulsion injury (PHAI) is commonly seen in the athletic population.3 The two primary types of PHAI are injuries sustained during high-speed running and those occurring during stretching exercises performed in extreme joint positions.4,5

The SM muscle is the largest hamstring muscle,6 and an isolated SM injury is associated with greater tendon retraction compared with avulsion of the conjoint tendon,7 potentially impacting the overall performance of athletes. Recently, the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–identified “cobra sign” was described and was reported to be a highly accurate and specific diagnostic indicator for isolated SM avulsion, with reasonable sensitivity.8 In addition, this sign was correlated to an overestimation of tendon retraction in comparison to the actual surgical findings. Nonoperative treatment is recommended for a single-tendon tear without retraction9; however, early diagnosis and surgical reinsertion of isolated proximal SM avulsion injury in high-level athletic patients are recommended to allow early rehabilitation and a return to the preinjury level.

In this article, we describe anatomic reinsertion of an isolated proximal SM tendon avulsion at its ischial tuberosity footprint in the high-level athletic population. Pearls and pitfalls are presented in Table 1. Advantages and disadvantages are described in Table 2.

Surgical Technique

Patient Setup

Under general or spinal anesthesia, the patient is placed in the prone position with all bony prominences well padded. The knee is positioned at 50° of flexion on a leg holder aiming to decrease the tension on the hamstring muscles. Standard scrubbing and draping of the leg are performed, allowing free knee range of motion during the operation.

Surgical Approach

SM tendon retraction is approximated preoperatively on a coronal T2-weighted MRI slice (Fig 1). The ischial tuberosity is palpated and marked, and the gluteal skin crease is marked. A transverse 8- to 10-cm skin incision is performed at the gluteal crease, passing through the marked ischial tuberosity. Alternatively, a vertical skin incision can be made starting from the marked ischial tuberosity at the gluteal crease (Fig 2). The choice between transverse and vertical incisions depends on the clinical scenario. A transverse incision is commonly used; however, a vertical incision is recommended in cases of chronic SM avulsion (≥3 weeks) or significant SM tendon retraction (≥6 cm). The vertical approach allows better tendon identification and scar tissue dissection, as well as protection of the sciatic nerve (Fig 2). Moreover, it is important to identify and protect the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh during the surgical procedure.

Subgluteal Approach

Subcutaneous dissection and hemostasis are obtained using an electrocautery device until the inferior border of the gluteus maximus muscle is exposed. Blunt dissection is performed to create a subgluteal plane; then, the gluteus maximus muscle is retracted superiorly to improve visualization of the hamstring fascia. The hamstring fascia is incised longitudinally in line with the hamstring’s fibers, and the hematoma is evacuated, facilitating the identification of the retracted SM tendon, its retrieval, and its debridement to viable tissue (Fig 3). In chronic cases, SM tendon identification becomes more challenging, and meticulous dissection through scar tissue is needed to identify and protect the sciatic nerve before proceeding to full SM tendon liberation.

Intraoperative photograph showing a vertical approach to the left posterior thigh, with identification of the retracted semimembranosus stump after lateral retraction of the semitendinosus muscle. (SM, semimembranosus tendon.)

Identification of Cobra Sign and De-plication

Identification of the cobra sign involves locating the false stump, characterized by a fibrotic mass at the junction between the folded segment and the remaining tendon (Fig 3). This fibrosis, particularly in chronic cases, hides the surgical cobra sign (Fig 4), necessitating its release to accurately unfold and delineate the retracted tendon structure. The surgeon methodically releases this fibrotic tissue, facilitating careful unfolding of the retracted tendon. This release provides the extra length needed for the restoration of the tendon to its anatomic footprint and reinsertion (Fig 5).

Ischial Tuberosity Preparation

A curved retractor is placed under the conjoint tendon within the obturator foramen, aiming to lift this tendon and expose the lateral facet of the ischial tuberosity. This retractor is maintained by the MedEnvision Gripper (with MedEnvision EsySuit drapes), allowing stable retractor positioning on the operating room table. The lateral aspect of the ischial tuberosity is cleaned and prepared using a Farabeuf rasp, starting at the medial side and moving toward the lateral side. Two bioabsorbable suture anchors (Lupine loop; DePuy Mitek) are placed on the lateral facet of the ischial tuberosity in a double-row configuration (Fig 6). A cannulated drill bit is recommended for anchor preparation to avoid sciatic nerve injury.

(B) The second strand of each anchor is passed through the tendon, allowing reduction of the tendon to its footprint as it smoothly slides into the anchor.

SM Tendon Preparation and Reinsertion

The tendon is sutured with running locking suture (Krackow stitch) using 1 strand from each anchor (Fig 6). The second strand of each anchor is passed in the tendon, allowing reduction of the tendon to its footprint as it smoothly slides into the anchor (Fig 7). All sutures are then tied. Simple sutures are used to close the interval between the SM muscle and the conjoint biceps femoris and semitendinosus muscles. Identification and protection of the sciatic nerve are recommended throughout the procedure, especially in chronic cases (≥3 weeks).

Intraoperative photograph of the left thigh showing the semimembranosus (SM) tendon insertion after restoration of the appropriate tendon length.

Postoperative Protocol

Postoperatively, a hinged knee brace is used for knee immobilization at 45° of flexion to reduce the tension on the hamstring muscles. Subsequently, knee flexion is gradually reduced between 4 and 6 weeks until full knee extension is achieved. Immediate postoperative partial weight-bearing using crutches is allowed.

Rehabilitation is started after 6 weeks. It includes active quadriceps strengthening exercises and closed-chain exercises for the hamstring muscles. The patient can gradually return to sport, starting with non-pivoting sports at 12 weeks, followed by pivoting sports at 4 to 6 months.

Discussion

PHAI is frequently observed in athletes.3 Nonoperative treatment is recommended for a single-tendon tear with or without retraction or a multi-tendon tear with retraction of less than 2 cm.8 However, nonoperative treatment of PHAI is associated with chronic pain, challenging rehabilitation, and poor functional outcomes in the athletic population.1 Surgical reinsertion of the hamstring tendon can be performed using either open or endoscopic techniques.10,11 Isolated proximal SM tendon avulsion represents a partial PHAI subgroup that necessitates early diagnosis (≤3 weeks) and anatomic surgical reinsertion at the ischial tuberosity footprint, allowing rapid rehabilitation and return to the patient’s preinjury level of sporting activity.12

This article provides a detailed open surgical technique for isolated proximal reinsertion of SM avulsion (Video 1). Preoperative recognition of the cobra sign on T2-weighted MRI slices was identified as a specific radiologic landmark for this injury.8 Early radiologic identification of this partial PHAI subtype provides a preoperative estimation of SM retraction, thereby helping in the planning of the surgical approach. In addition, it is crucial to recognize the importance of meticulous dissection and liberation of scar tissue to unfold the retracted tendon. This approach provides an adequate tendon length and anatomic reinsertion without tension on the ischial tuberosity, consequently reducing the need for allograft augmentation.

The horizontal approach through the gluteal crease is commonly used, providing an esthetic benefit. However, if SM tendon retraction exceeds 6 cm, a vertical approach is recommended.13 The subgluteal approach is essential to improve visualization and protection of the sciatic nerve. Early diagnosis and surgical treatment are essential to obtain easy access to the SM muscle and tendon, which lies medial to the conjoint tendon, and thereby reinsert it on its anatomic footprint just lateral to the conjoint tendon insertion on the ischial tuberosity.

The main risk associated with our technique is sciatic nerve injury. This complication can be prevented by performing meticulous dissection of scar tissue surrounding the SM tendon with identification and protection of the sciatic nerve.

We believe that our technique offers a safe open surgical approach for isolated SM tendon reinsertion in the athletic population, with a relatively rapid learning curve. Early diagnosis and surgical repair of isolated SM tendon avulsion allow rapid rehabilitation and return to the preinjury athletic level.
