Lanthanide Atoms Induce Strong Graphene Sheet Distortion When Adsorbed on Stone–Wales Defects
Authors: Vladimir A. Basiuk, Yifan Wu, Oleg V. Prezhdo, Elena V. Basiuk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Local curvature in graphene can enhance its reactivity and catalytic activity and can be induced by the adsorption of certain chemical species. By employing periodic density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we demonstrate that significant local curvature can be systematically observed when lanthanide atoms (the full series from La to Lu) are adsorbed on the Stone–Wales (SW) defect in graphene, contrary to that in defect-free graphene. Despite the typical high coordination numbers of lanthanide species, their hapticity is always η2 (and not η5, η6, or η7), where Ln atoms are adsorbed on the (7,7) junction, forming relatively short Ln···C separations. Contrary to the pristine graphene, the SW region undergoes considerable distortion and results in much stronger Ln bonding. The positive charge acquired by Ln atoms upon adsorption on SW is approximately 1.5 times larger than that on defect-free graphene. The high visibility of electron-rich lanthanide species in scanning tunneling microscopy images provides a means to locate SW defects in graphene samples experimentally.

The graphene out-of-plane distortion1−17 (termed as curvature, corrugations, ripples, waves, warps, wrinkles, etc.) is a very interesting aspect of the entire science of graphene, having important implications for its fundamental properties and versatile applications. The existence of local curvature was shown to enhance graphene sheet reactivity and catalytic activity,1,3,5,6,8,9,11,12,15 which is especially attractive and useful for such green energy-related processes such as hydrogen adsorption2,12,15 and evolution,5,8,16 oxygen evolution, and oxygen reduction reactions.17 In addition to the innate curvature formed in the process of graphene growth,2,3,8,13−15,17 curvature can be induced mechanically1,9,11 or by means of adsorption of certain chemical species.4 At the same time, one should emphasize the role of density functional theory (DFT) studies in this particular area,1−3,5−13,16 as well as in the closely related area of adsorption of different chemical species on graphene-based surfaces.18−22 In turn, one of the most appealing and, at the same time, computationally challenging cases is the adsorption of lanthanide species.23−31

Our interest in the latter area32−36 is rather multifaceted. It comprises a merely methodological aspect as the use of thermal smearing to facilitate self-consistent field (SCF) convergence for particularly difficult open-shell systems,32 comparison of the adsorption behavior for the (when possible) full series of lanthanide atoms34,35 and Ln3+ ions,33 and the effect of structural defects on Ln–graphene interaction parameters,36 as well as the Ln adsorption-induced distortion of a graphene sheet model.33,35,36 The bending observed for defect-free graphene can be rather strong, but only in the case of a relatively small supercoronene cluster model,33,35 whereas no such effect was evident in the periodic calculations.34 These findings might imply that the existence and magnitude of the distortion depend on the size of graphene structures and the proximity of the metal to graphene sheet edges. Namely, the quasi-infinite or simply very large sheets are too rigid to undergo bending, whereas this might become tangible for relatively small graphene quantum dots, especially closer to their edges. In the present context, one should mention another observation: while the adsorption of lanthanum and lutetium atoms and trications on pristine graphene cluster models causes no or minor bending,33,35,36 it becomes very strong after the introduction of a Stone–Wales (SW) defect.36 This reminds one of the behavior of the SW defect-containing models noncovalently interacting with such large molecules as free-base phthalocyanine,37 its 3d metal,37 yttrium,38 and some lanthanide (e.g., gadolinium) complexes.39 Which, naturally, leads to the question: how would the quasi-infinite SW defect-containing graphene behave upon adsorption of Ln species?

In the present report, we addressed the latter question by employing periodic DFT calculations for the full series of lanthanide atoms. The model for SW defect-containing graphene (SWG; Figure 1a) was generated from the frequently used hexagonal supercell for defect-free (pristine) graphene,32,34 which has 32 carbon atoms.

However, the rotation of one C–C bond to yield a SW defect changes the hexagonal symmetry of the lattice to primitive of C-centered monoclinic; the lattice parameters and cell angles are specified in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. The computational methodology included Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) GGA functional40 complemented with the dispersion correction introduced by Grimme41 (commonly referred to as PBE-D2), a plane wave basis set and ultrasoft pseudopotentials as implemented in the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP),42 which is the same computational technique as the one used in the previous reports.32,34 Due to a typical high degeneracy of the f element-containing systems, it was impossible to complete computations without the use of thermal smearing. At the same time, the target smearing value was low enough, 0.02721 eV (equivalent to 0.001 Ha and temperature of 315.8 K). In addition, whenever possible, it was reduced to 0.00272 eV (equivalent to 0.0001 Ha and temperature of 31.6 K): as it was demonstrated previously,32 both values guarantee convergent computational results.

The results were analyzed in terms of the adsorption site geometry, interaction strength, charge, and spin of Ln atoms. In turn, to characterize the changes in the adsorption site geometry, we monitored the dC–C bond length in the (7,7) junction of the SW defect and the dihedral angle θ as explained in Figure 1c, as well as the shortest distances dLn···C: the corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 1. First of all, one should mention that the dC–C bond length and θ angle in SWG in the absence of any adsorbed species are 1.337 Å and 179.7°, respectively. The coordination pattern for all Ln atoms is the same, η2 (Figure 1c), with almost equivalent dLn···C distances. The shortest ones of 2.307 and 2.314 Å were obtained for SWG+Dy, and the longest ones were 2.481 and 2.487 Å for SWG+Nd. On the other hand, the dC–C bond length of the (7,7) junction increases compared to the one of 1.337 Å in the isolated SWG but varies very insignificantly depending on the Ln atom, between 1.414 Å for Sm and 1.423 Å for Er. The dihedral angle θ values range from 140.9° in SWG+Tb to 149.7° in SWG+Sm (Table 1).

In the case of the θ angle, there is no straightforward trend, which would be, for example, linear or close-to-linear: such a behavior would match the known effect of lanthanide contraction. Instead, the θ values first increase from La to Sm, then fall down to Tb, and then increase again up to Lu (Figure 2b). The dLn···C approaches behave in a qualitatively similar way, though the maximum and minimum do not coincide with those found for θ angles. Both plots exhibit a reverse sigmoid-like shape, where the central part (the most dramatic decrease of both dLn···C and θ values) corresponds to the highest total number of unpaired electrons, between Eu (seven) and Gd (eight). Taking into account that both elements have seven 4f electrons, such a sharp change must be attributed to the presence of one 5d electron in the Gd atom. Interestingly, the behavior of the other two lanthanides having one 5d electron does not exhibit a dramatic deviation with respect to that of their neighbor: namely, Ce compared to Pr and Lu compared to Yb.

Another aspect of interest is how the bonding strength evolves throughout the entire Ln series compared to the one reported previously for the periodic defect-free graphene systems.34 The interaction energies calculated directly from the absolute energies for isolated Ln atoms and the SWG model (Table S2 of the Supporting Information) according to formula 1 are referred to as ΔEfull:1

One can say that the shape of the ΔEfull(Ln) curve (Figure 2a; gray solid line) matches the general features of the ΔE-versus-Ln interrelation:34 in particular, an increase to the first broad maximum (at Pr–Eu in the present case and at Nd–Eu in ref (34)), then a decrease to a broad minimum (at Gd–Ho in the present case and at Gd–Dy in ref (34)), followed by a sharp increase to another, even higher maximum (at Er in the present case and at Tm in ref (34)), and a final decrease to Lu. On the other hand, this behavior is very different from the calculation results obtained on the supercoronene cluster model (G) for graphene interacting with Ln atoms,35 where a sharp maximum is observed for Gd. However, the common feature of the present case and the latter is a strong distortion of the graphene model induced by the adsorption of neutral lanthanide species (a similar phenomenon was observed for the adsorption of Ln3+ ions on the same supercoronene cluster33).

The observation, which made us undertake a deeper analysis of the energies of SWG+Ln complex formation, was an anomalous fact observed previously35 that the ΔE value (referred to as ΔEfull in the present work) for Gd+G turned out to be positive 16.4 kcal/mol. In other words, interpreted as it is, it meant repulsion between the Gd atom and the supercoronene model: this did not make any sense, especially taking into account that the strongest distortion of the latter was found precisely for the Gd+G complex. The interpretation we offered33,35 is that any distortion (i.e., elongation/shortening of C–C bonds and C–C–C angle bending) of the normally planar polycyclic aromatic backbone composed of hexagonal rings logically causes an increase in its energy. This increase (denoted as ΔEdist) can be quantified by calculating the difference between the entire energy of the totally optimized (relaxed) isolated graphene model and the single-point energy of the (distorted to a variable degree) model retrieved from the optimized lanthanide+graphene complex (that is, after removing the Ln atom35 or ion33). As one can see from Figure 2a, the shape of the ΔEbond(Ln) curve (black solid line) closely follows that of the ΔEfull(Ln) one, but the former curve is found to be lower by roughly 20 kcal/mol, due to the distortion component. More specifically, the ΔEdist values comprise a rather narrow range from 17.1 kcal/mol for SWG+Sm to 27.3 kcal/mol for SWG+Tb (Table 1). For comparison, for the Ln complexes with a supercoronene model,35 it was from 0.5 (Yb; no tangible distortion) to 20.9 kcal/mol (Gd; the strongest distortion): note that Yb and Gd represent two extremes with zero and eight (maximum possible) unpaired electrons, though as a whole the ΔEdist(Ln) plot exhibited a relatively random distribution of data points (similarly to the changes in ΔE and ΔEbond). For SWG+Ln complexes, the ΔEdist(Ln) curve behaves more smoothly compared to the ΔEfull(Ln) and ΔEbond(Ln) plots. Most importantly, there is an evident reverse correlation of the ΔEdist values with the θ angles, with higher distortion energy matching smaller θ angles (minimal for SWG+Tb; Figure 2b) and vice versa (in particular, the smallest ΔEdist and the highest θ value for SWG+Sm). Both curves have an inflection between Eu and Gd. One should note also that, regardless of whether the ΔEfull or ΔEbond values are considered, the Ln bonding is considerably stronger on SWG than on the G model (especially for the central lanthanides Gd–Dy).

When analyzing the charge of adsorbed Ln atoms (the values in Table 1), we compared the data obtained here to the values reported for the Ln complexes with the defect-free periodic graphene model.34 (A comparison with the case of supercoronene model35 would not be particularly valid, since its electronic properties are strongly altered by the presence of 18 peripheral hydrogen atoms.) The general appearance of the plot of charge versus Ln shown in Figure 2c (red data set) is similar to the pattern described in ref (34). For SWG+Ln complexes, it is almost a plateau from La to Yb, where the charge fluctuates between 1.63 e for Gd and 1.98 e for Tm and then finally dramatically drops as low as 0.77 e for Lu. On the defect-free periodic graphene,34 the Ln charge varied relatively insignificantly until Tm, between 1.09 e (Nd and Sm) and 1.35 e (Ho) and then decreased to 0.75 e (Yb) and finally to 0.43 e (Lu). Therefore, despite the general similarity between the two plots, one can see very large differences in the degree of charge transfer from lanthanide atoms to graphene: the one observed in the case of SWG is approximately 1.5 times larger than that for the defect-free model. An explanation is a higher electrophilicity of the shorter C=C bond connecting two pentagonal rings (or (7,7) junction of the SW defect; the calculated bond length of 1.337 Å) compared to that of the bonds in a purely hexagonal network (the calculated bond length of 1.421–1.422 Å):34 a typical illustration of such a difference is the one observed between (6,6) and (5,6) junctions in the common fullerene C60.

Another important electronic parameter, closely related to the Ln charge, is the Ln spin as well as its distribution within SWG+Ln complexes. In principle, the Ln spin values in the complexes closely reflect the formal spins for isolated lanthanide atoms (Table 1), with three clear exceptions of SWG+La (0.11 ℏ/2), SWG+Tb (7.07 ℏ/2), and SWG+Er (0.12 ℏ/2): the isolated atoms must have 1, 5, and 2 unpaired electrons, respectively. The corresponding curve (Figure 2c; blue data set) almost uniformly rises until the maximum value of 7.80 ℏ/2 for SWG+Gd and then begins to fall. The difference from the case of defect-free graphene34 consists mainly of the behavior after Gd. Namely, the maximum is observed for Tb (9.85 ℏ/2), and the curve shape for the series from Er to Lu is inverted so that zero or close-to-zero spin values are not found for Er and Yb (Table 1) but for their later neighbors Tm and Lu.

When summarizing the spin changes upon interaction with the SWG model, one can see that they can be both positive (gain) and negative (depletion), compared to the spin of isolated lanthanide atoms. The first trend is more common: it is observed for nine elements (Nd–Eu, Tb–Ho, Tm, and Lu), spanning from a negligible gain of 0.01 ℏ/2 (Lu) to a very large one of 2.07 ℏ/2 (Tb). The spin depletion was found for five lanthanides (La–Pr, Gd, and Er), but its span is almost equally broad: from as little as 0.01 ℏ/2 (Pr) to 1.88 ℏ/2 (Er). No change was observed for Yb only (zero spin); this is the only lanthanide atom that does not have unpaired electrons due to its closed-shell electron configuration [Xe]4f146s2.

The most striking differences can be seen in the spin density plots. The one corresponding to the SWG+Ln complexes is shown in Figure 3.

One should point out a series of important differences between these plots and the ones reported for the Ln complexes with the periodic defect-free graphene model34 (the isosurfaces were obtained at the same 0.05 au values). First, the shape of the electron cloud differs for all lanthanides except for Eu, and the spin direction (up or down) in most cases does not coincide either. Second, as already follows from the numerical spin values discussed in the previous paragraph, no spin density can be visualized for La (spin of 0.11 ℏ/2) and Yb (zero). Nevertheless, SWG+Er exhibits rather extended isosurfaces, even though the spin of the Er atom in the complex was calculated to be very small (almost equal to that of La atom in SWG+La), 0.12 ℏ/2: this seeming contradiction can be explained by the simultaneous presence of spin-up and spin-down lobes of approximately the same size, which compensate for each other. The third, and perhaps the most important distinctive feature of SWG+Ln complexes, is that in none of them can spin density be detected on the carbon atoms: in the defect-free models, such a situation was observed for eight elements (from Pr to Sm and from Er to Lu). For the remaining seven species, small but clearly distinguishable spin density lobes did appear on the C atoms: for La, Eu, Gd, and Tb, on the remote carbons only, and for Ce, Dy, and Ho, both on remote carbon atoms and on the C atoms of the Ln-coordinated hexagonal ring. The Mulliken population analysis, however, shows that the carbon atoms of the SWG model do acquire some spin, but its value rarely exceeds 0.01 ℏ/2; thus, it cannot be visualized on the isosurfaces plotted at 0.05 au, whereas in the case of the Ln complexes with defect-free graphene,34 the spin transfer to some C atoms reached 0.05–0.06 ℏ/2.

One of the most important and frequently analyzed electronic characteristics of graphene-related materials, including those doped with lanthanide species,23−27 is the density of states (DOS) plots. At the same time, since the main aspect of interest is the conductivity properties of the latter (as well as many other) materials, the range of energies analyzed does not extend very far from the Fermi level, comprised at most from −10 to 5 eV.23 Since, like in the previous study of G+Ln complexes,34 we were interested also in the behavior of 5s and 5p innermost shells, we considered the full energy range yielded by the CASTEP calculations (using the ultrasoft pseudopotentials), going beyond −50 eV. The DOS plots (Figures 4 and S1) for different SWG+Ln complexes, as could be expected, exhibit individual distinctive features, being in many regards similar to those of their G+Ln analogues.34

Full-range partial DOS plots for the Ln components of representative SWG+Ln complexes (Ln = La, Ce, Nd, Sm, Gd, Tb, Ho, Er, Tm, and Yb). The Fermi energy is set to zero (vertical dashed line). α and β denote spin-up and spin-down electrons.

The bands due to SWG naturally dominate and change insignificantly after Ln adsorption (Figure S1), so we focused on the contribution of lanthanide atoms (Figure 4). There are no 4f bands in the case of La, since these electrons are absent in this element. For most lanthanides, the 4f peaks are found in both the valence and conduction zones. The exceptions are Eu and Gd with a half-filled 4f shell, as well as Yb and Lu with a totally filled 4f shell, for which the 4f peaks are observed at about −1.8, −9.5, −0.6 and −6.7 eV, respectively. For comparison, for the light lanthanides embedded in graphene (by means of the substitution of one carbon atom), the locations of 4f orbitals are away from Fermi level gradually to lower energies from Ce to Gd,23 whereas the band behavior for the Ln complexes with pristine graphene34 is very similar to the present one. The most deeply lying 4f peaks (due to α-electrons) were found in the case of Tb: between −8.8 and −13.5 eV.

Another similarity with G+Ln complexes34 can be seen for the 5d electrons: the main density is observed in the conduction zone (up to roughly 4 eV; for Eu and Gd, up to 5 eV), with only traces in the valence zone, down to ca. −6 eV. On the contrary, for the same G+Ln complexes,34 we observed that the most significant changes in energy occur for the 5s and 5p shells. Namely, for G+La, 5s electrons of La appear at −34.5 eV for both spin-up and spin-down orientations, whereas for G+Ce, they are clearly resolved at −35.0 and −33.8 eV, respectively. From Pr to Tb, both α and β components gradually shift to higher energies, and the separation between them increases as well. Finally, the energies stabilize on average around 50.0 eV, but the separation between spin-up and spin-down states gradually vanishes, so that in the case of G+Yb they have the same energy of −52.0 eV. Comparison with the DOS data for isolated lanthanide atoms showed that the effect of complexation with graphene can be from zero (Yb) to 2 eV (Tb), even though 5s is a deeply lying shell. In the case of the SWG+Ln complexes presented here, the general trend is similar (Figures 4 and S1); we analyzed it in more detail. The energies of the 5s α- and β-electrons retrieved from the partial DOS plots for Ln components are presented in Figure S2, along with the corresponding plot. First, one should note that the energy of the α-electrons in most cases is lower than that of β-electrons; the exceptions are the complexes of La, Sm, Eu, Dy, and Er. Second, the α- and β-electron energies totally or almost coincide not only for La and Yb (as was the case for G+Ln complexes34) but also for the Er complex (which also exhibited an anomalously low spin of 0.12 ℏ/2 on the metal atom; Table 1 and Figure 2c). For the entire energy range of the 5s electrons, it spans from −33.3 (β-electrons of Ce) down to −52.1 eV (both α- and β-electrons of Yb), which is essentially the same as in the case of G+Ln complexes.34 Another close similarity (with minor differences) between G+Ln34 and SWG+Ln can be observed in the behavior of the 5p electrons.

In conclusion, the adsorption behavior of lanthanide atoms on Stone–Wales defect-containing graphene exhibits a series of distinctive features compared to defect-free graphene.34 Despite the typical high coordination numbers of lanthanide species, the hapticity for all Ln atoms on SWG is η2 and not η5 (coordination to pentagons), η6 (to hexagons34), or η7 (to heptagons): Ln atoms are adsorbed on the (7,7) junction due to its higher electrophilicity, forming much shorter Ln···C separations than those in the case of G+Ln complexes. The SW defect undergoes a strong distortion, which is not observed for the complexes with defect-free G (but only in the case of their analogues with the supercoronene cluster model33,35). Regardless of whether the ΔEfull or ΔEbond values are considered, the Ln adsorption is considerably stronger on SWG than on G34 (especially for the central lanthanides Gd–Dy). The positive charge acquired by Ln atoms upon adsorption on SWG is approximately 1.5 times larger than that in the case of defect-free G. Several practical implications of the calculation results can be suggested. Since local curvature can enhance reactivity and catalytic activity of graphene sheets1,3,5,6,8,9,11,12,15,43−46 and because SW defects are common in real-world graphene, the generation of curvature by adsorption of Ln species provides a relatively simple way to enhance chemical activity. Further, due to a high visibility of electron-rich lanthanide species in scanning tunneling microscopy images, the location of adsorbed Ln species may be used to identify SW defects in graphene samples.
