Considerations for Lifetime Management After TAVR or SAVR: When Bigger or Higher May Not Be Better
Authors: Deven R Rajagopal, Vinod H Thourani, Pradeep K Yadav
Published: 2025

Over the past 2 decades, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the treatment of aortic stenosis (AS), propelled by rapid device innovation and compelling clinical data. Landmark randomized trials show that TAVR is noninferior or superior to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) across risk categories at 1 year, and up to 5 years in low-risk patients. In low-risk patients, PARTNER 3 and Evolut Low Risk trials reported no significant difference in rates of death, stroke, and valve performance at 5 years.1,2 Yet long-term durability, particularly beyond 10 years, remains uncertain, leaving its role in younger patients (<65 years) unsettled. Notably, both PARTNER 3 (mean age 72 years) and Evolut Low Risk (mean age 73 years) enrolled very few patients <65 years (∼7 and 6%, respectively).3 Despite this lack of evidence, TAVR use in patients under 65 years has risen sharply in the United States.4 These realities underscore the need for a lifetime management approach: carefully weighing TAVR versus SAVR (bioprosthetic or mechanical) as the index therapy.

In this issue of the journal, Sheth and colleagues provide important insights by analyzing patients from 4 high-volume TAVR centers and one high-volume SAVR center. The study included 245 patients in 4 groups: 117 with SAPIEN 3 implanted at ≤90% “aortic depth” (more ventricular), 66 at >90% “aortic depth” (more aortic), 31 with SAVR, and 31 with SAVR plus aortic root enlargement (ARE). All patients underwent post-treatment computed tomography. Future TAVR was classified as feasible if 1) the neo-skirt was below both coronaries or 2) the neo-skirt-to-coronary distance (VTC) was ≥4 mm and the neo-skirt-to-sino-tubular junction (VTSTJ) was ≥2 mm. By structuring the comparison this way, the investigators addressed 2 questions: 1) To what extent does the depth of TAVR implantation affect the feasibility of future TAVR? and 2) Does SAVR, with or without ARE, as the initial strategy improve the feasibility of future TAVR?

The results were striking. As expected, more ventricular TAVR implantation (≤90% aortic depth) yielded far greater feasibility of future TAVR than more aortic implantation (>90% aortic depth): feasibility was 90.6 vs 71.2%. Despite the theoretical benefit of leaflet excision, feasibility after SAVR was 77.2%, not meaningfully different from TAVR with more aortic implantation (71.2%). Most unexpectedly, SAVR with ARE (71.0%) provided no additional benefit to SAVR alone in terms of TAVR in SAVR in relationship to the coronary arteries and was similar to a TAVR with an aortic implantation (71.2%).

Why? Most contemporary SAVRs are implanted supra-annularly, effectively reducing coronary height relative to the functional annulus, which is often the key limitation for future TAVR. In this study, while annulus-to-coronary distances were unchanged after TAVR, it fell substantially after SAVR (left main 15.4 → 9.2 mm; right coronary artery 17.9 → 10.2 mm) and even more after SAVR + ARE (left main 13.9 → 4.8 mm; right coronary artery 15.9 → 4.8 mm). Although there are advantages of the ARE by enlarging the noncoronary sinus dimensions, allowing a larger surgical prosthesis implantation, this may lead to tilting the valve toward the left sinus to accommodate upsizing.

These data challenge 2 concepts regarding implantation for TAVR and SAVR. First, higher (aortic) implantation with a balloon expandable valve is best for decreasing permanent pacemaker implantation, but based on this current series may lead to less chance for a TAVR in TAVR in 29% of patients. Second, that SAVR, particularly SAVR plus ARE, may not allow TAVR in SAVR in up to 29% of patients and depending on the sinus size should not be considered an automatic future TAVR. Although SAVR plus ARE reduces the incidence of patient-prosthesis mismatch compared to standard SAVR, TAVR also often achieves larger effective orifice areas in small annuli. Even in experienced hands, more than half of SAVR + ARE recipients receive ≤23 mm valves and roughly one-quarter receive 19–21 mm valves.5 Most such patients could be candidates for a 23 mm balloon-expandable TAVR (e.g., SAPIEN 3 Resilia), with an effective orifice area ∼ 1.8 cm2—comparable to 23-mm surgical valves.6 Although long-term durability remains a concern, the NOTION trial reported no difference at 10 years in bioprosthetic valve failure or reintervention and less bioprosthetic valve dysfunction after TAVR, largely due to lower patient-prosthesis mismatch.7

The choice of TAVR and SAVR remains an important conversation in shared decision-making with patients with severe AS. With over 100,000 TAVR implants in the United States last year, this remains the dominant form of therapy for AS worldwide. Current U.S. guidelines recommend surgery in those less than 65 years of age and mechanical AVR in patients <50 years and a life expectancy of greater than 20 years.8 In those with bicuspid AS with heavy raphe or subannular calcification, SAVR is preferred, but TAVR is reasonable when these features are absent.9,10 Other “hostile” anatomies that increase the risk of annular rupture or coronary obstruction may also favor SAVR.

So what does this all mean for lifetime management? This paper highlights that SAVR with or without ARE does not improve the feasibility of valve-in-valve; conversely, improving feasibility with TAVR requires a lower implant (more ventricular than 90/10). Lifetime planning requires meticulous preprocedural computed tomography analysis and, increasingly, advanced virtual modeling. While tools such as 3Mensio remain widely used, they are labor-intensive and have significant limitations. By contrast, dedicated simulation platforms such as DASI Simulations or FeOps provide highly sensitive and specific predictions, enabling physicians to weigh trade-offs in annular size, coronary risk, and future valve-in-valve interventions with unprecedented clarity.11 In particular, simulations can quantify the risk of annular rupture or coronary obstruction, and test the effect of deployment strategy or leaflet modification. This is particularly relevant given the growing experience with leaflet-modification strategies (e.g., ShortCut device or the Excision Medical platform), which might further expand the patient population suitable for TAVR.12,13 Simulation may also guide adoption of next-generation technologies, including balloon-expandable TAVRs that offer commissural alignment, shorter frame size, no foreshortening, and more predictable geometry.14

We congratulate the authors on an excellent submission that questions the notion that surgery, particularly with root enlargement, or TAVR implantation that is more aortic guarantees freedom for future TAVR. In reality, it may constrain tomorrow’s choices. With advanced simulation and imaging now available, lifetime management must abandon tradition and adopt precision. Bigger or higher may not always be better, and the first valve should open the door to every option ahead—not close it.
