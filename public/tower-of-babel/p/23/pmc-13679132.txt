ZRANB1-mediated EZH2 stabilization drives S100B/S100A4 transcriptional activation and corneal neovascularization in alkali burns
Authors: Fangyuan Liu, Ying Xiao, Yeping Huang, Yang Liu, Zhiyou Peng, Yulin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlkali burn is one of the main causes of corneal injury. Corneal neovascularization caused by alkali burns aggravates corneal damage, resulting in aberrant vascular proliferation and consequent visual impairment. The present study investigated the role of enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) in alkali burn‑induced corneal neovascularization. Through integrated bioinformatics and experimental approaches, we identified S100B, S100A4 and PI3K/AKT pathway activation as key drivers of alkali-induced corneal injury. In corneal alkali burn mice, knockdown of S100B or S100A4 attenuated corneal neovascularization and suppressed PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway. EZH2 was upregulated in alkali-burn models and transcriptionally activated S100B and S100A4 via direct promoter binding. The deubiquitinase ZRANB1 was identified to stabilize EZH2 protein through deubiquitination, thereby amplifying its transcriptional activity. In alkali-injured human corneal epithelial cells, EZH2 overexpression enhanced cell viability, angiogenic capacity, migration and invasion ability, which was reversed by S100B or S100A4 knockdown. In vivo, EZH2 overexpression exacerbated PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway activation and corneal neovascularization via regulating S100B or S100A4. Collectively, this study demonstrated that ZRANB1-mediated stabilization of EZH2 drove pathological corneal angiogenesis in corneal alkali burns by activating S100B/S100A4-PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a axis. Introduction

Cornea, an avascular and transparent tissue at the anterior segment of the eye, serves as a crucial barrier against external damage [1]. Corneal diseases are among the main causes of blindness, with approximately 4.6 and 23 million patients worldwide suffering from bilateral and unilateral corneal blindness [2]. Epidemiological data from Shandong Province of China, identify corneal pathologies as the third most prevalent cause of visual impairment and blindness [3]. Chemical exposure, particularly alkaline burns, constitutes a major etiology of corneal damage [4], with alkali-induced injuries frequently leading to corneal neovascularization and subsequent vision loss [5]. Corneal alkali burns remain a clinically significant blinding trauma whose pathogenesis requires further elucidation. Excessive inflammatory responses and pathological angiogenesis have been established as key contributors to tissue damage [6, 7]. Current therapeutic strategies include butyrate-mediated acceleration of wound healing through NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition [8] and epigallocatechin gallate’s anti-angiogenic effects in rodent models [9].

Corneal neovascularization occurs when the balance between angiogenic and antiangiogenic factors is tipped toward angiogenic molecules. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), one of the most important mediators of angiogenesis, is upregulated during neovascularization [10]. Anti-VEGF therapy happens to be an effective treatment strategy by directly inhibiting angiogenesis at the molecular level. While anti-VEGF therapies like bevacizumab demonstrate initial efficacy in reducing corneal neovascularization [11], their long-term application faces limitations including adverse effects. Alternative approaches targeting mTOR-mediated angiogenesis regulation [12], netrin-1-induced VEGF/PEDF balance restoration [13], or GLS1 inhibition-mediated macrophage polarization modulation [14]. However, the complete mechanisms of governing corneal neovascularization are not fully understood.

Emerging evidence implicates epigenetic regulation in corneal pathophysiology. Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), the catalytic subunit of Polycomb repressive complex 2, mediates transcriptional silencing through histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) [15]. Beyond its canonical repressive function, EZH2 exhibits context-dependent transcriptional activation capabilities [16, 17]. In ocular systems, EZH2 regulates retinal development [18] and diabetic retinal angiogenesis [19], while its inhibition attenuates corneal neovascularization through FoxO3a-dependent mechanisms [20]. The calcium-binding proteins S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B) and S100 calcium-binding protein A4 (S100A4), members of the S100 family, participate in diverse cellular processes including inflammation and wound healing [21]. Given their marked upregulation in our single-cell RNA-seq analysis of alkali-injured corneas and the prediction by ENCODE database that both genes are transcriptionally regulated by EZH2, S100B and S100A4 emerged as compelling candidates for further investigation. Consistent with this rationale, S100A4 silencing has been shown to inhibit alkali burn-induced corneal neovascularization in vivo [22] and promote corneal repair via PI3K/AKT/mTOR suppression [23], while S100B is known to promote cellular proliferation and angiogenic mediator release [21]. However, the regulatory interplay between EZH2 and S100 proteins in corneal neovascularization remains unexplored.

Reported studies have emphasized the importance of post-translational modifications, particularly ubiquitination, in regulating protein stability and function in corneal pathophysiology [24, 25]. Ubiquitination, a reversible process mediated by E3 ligases and deubiquitinases, plays a pivotal role in modulating key signaling molecules involved in inflammation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [26, 27]. It has been reported that in cancer contexts, EZH2 protein stability and activity are regulated by ubiquitination [28, 29]. However, whether ubiquitination-mediated regulation of EZH2 occurs in corneal injury remains unclear. To investigate the possibility that EZH2 protein stability might be modulated by deubiquitination in corneal injury, we utilized the UbiBrowser 2.0 database to predict potential deubiquitinases interacting with EZH2 [30]. This bioinformatic screening provided a set of candidate enzymes (ubiquitin‑specific peptidase 21 (USP21), USP36, USP44, USP7, zinc finger RANBP2‑type containing 1 (ZRANB1)), prompting us to further examine their roles and expression in alkali-induced corneal injury.

Through integrated bioinformatic and experimental approaches, this investigation revealed a multi-layered regulatory mechanism underlying corneal neovascularization in alkali burn models. We first demonstrated that EZH2 transcriptionally activated both S100B and S100A4 expression through direct promoter binding, which in turn drove PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway activation to promote pathological angiogenesis. Furthermore, we identified that EZH2 protein stability was regulated by post-translational modification, with ZRANB1-mediated deubiquitination playing a pivotal role in stabilizing EZH2 protein in alkali-injured corneas, thereby amplifying its transcriptional activity. These findings established that ZRANB1-mediated deubiquitination stabilized EZH2, which transcriptionally upregulated S100B and S100A4 to activate the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway, ultimately driving pathological neovascularization in corneal alkali burns. This integrated regulatory framework positions EZH2 as a promising therapeutic target for corneal alkali injuries.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The corneal alkali burn-related single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE275678 (containing one NaOH-induced model sample and one uninjured control sample) was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Data analysis was performed using the R package Seurat, with stringent quality control criteria applied for cell filtering (nFeature_RNA > 500; nCount_RNA > 1000; nFeature_RNA < 7000; nCount_RNA < 70000; Percent_mt < 15). Following quality control, data were normalized using the NormalizeData function. Highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function with the variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) method, and the top 3000 variable genes were selected for downstream analysis. Data were then scaled using the ScaleData function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the RunPCA function, and batch effects were corrected using the Harmony R package. Dimensional reduction visualization was conducted using the RunTSNE and RunUMAP functions for TSNE and UMAP projections, respectively. For UMAP visualization, parameters were set as spread = 10 and min.dist = 0.5 to optimize cluster separation. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function with thresholds of |log fold change| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.001, with results visualized through volcano plots generated by the ggplot2 R package. The distribution patterns of S100B and S100A4 genes across experimental groups were visualized using the scCustomize R package. Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using clusterProfiler. Based on KEGG results, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was specifically performed for the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Finally, pathway activity scoring of PI3K-AKT signaling was assessed using the AUCell package. The AUCell_buildRankings and AUCell_calcAUC functions were employed for this calculation, and results were visualized through dimensional reduction plots and boxplots.

Cell culture

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) were purchased from Qingqi Biotechnology (#BFN60803901, Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in high‑glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. To establish an in vitro alkali injury model, HCECs were seeded to achieve 70–80% confluence and then treated with 0.05 mol/L NaOH in the culture medium (containing 10% FBS) for 24 h [31]. Under these conditions, the final pH of the culture medium was approximately 9.0. For experiments investigating EZH2 ubiquitination, HCECs treated with or without NaOH were administrated the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Functional assays were performed following the 24 h NaOH exposure period.

Cell transfection

Gene silencing was achieved using sequence-specific si-RNAs targeting S100B, S100A4, EZH2, or ZRANB1. For EZH2 overexpression, HCECs were transfected with pcDNA3.1-EZH2 (EZH2-OE), with empty pcDNA3.1 vector serving as the negative control (Vector). In the functional rescue experiments, HCECs were co‑transfected with EZH2‑OE and si‑S100B or si‑S100A4. All transfections were performed using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (#11668500, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral infections were conducted with polybrene (#E1299, Selleck, Houston, USA) to enhance transduction efficiency. All the gene vectors were obtained from Gene pharm (Shanghai, China). The complete sequences of all si-RNA and sh-RNA oligonucleotides are listed in Table 1. For experiments involving ZRANB1 mutants, full-length human ZRANB1 and its OTU domain catalytically inactive mutant were subcloned into the pBabe-SFB vector using the Gateway system (Invitrogen) and transfected in 293T cells to evaluate its effect on EZH2 binding and stability. For all transfection experiments, HCECs were seeded and allowed to reach 70–80% confluence prior to transfection. At 24–48 h post-transfection, cells were subjected to NaOH treatment as described above. The ZRANB1-overexpressing lentiviral plasmid (pLV3-CMV-ZRANB1(human)-3×FLAG-CopGFP-Puro, #P85362) was purchased from Miaoling Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, Hubei, China).

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–7 weeks age, 20–25 g weight) were purchased from the Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China). The mice were placed in controlled environments (12-h light/dark cycle; 20–26 °C; 40–60% humidity) and had free access to bacteria-free water and food. All animal housing and experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines formulated by the Animal Experimental Committee of the Animal Experimental Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (CDYFY-IACUC-202407QR201).

Corneal alkali burn model induction and treatment

To establish the corneal alkali burn model, 6-7-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital. Gene expression modulation was achieved through subconjunctival administration of lentiviral constructs (LV-sh-S100B, LV-sh-S100A4, or LV-EZH2) or 3-Deazaneplanocin A (DZNeP) (Selleck) on day 7 post-arrival, two days prior to alkali injury. The right cornea was subjected to alkali injury by applying a sterile 2.5-mm diameter filter paper soaked with 1 M NaOH for 30 s, followed by immediate removal and thorough irrigation with 10 mL of sterile saline. The left eye served as an untreated internal control. Corneal neovascularization was quantitatively assessed on day 7 post-injury using slit-lamp microscopy [32]. The area of corneal neovascularization (CoNV) was calculated using the formula: Area = (C/12) × 3.14 × [R² − (R − L)²], where C represents the number of clock hours of neovascularization observed, R is the corneal radius, and L is the maximum vessel length extending from the limbus. Corneal tissues were harvested at specified time points (days 1, 3, 7, and 14 post-alkali injury) for subsequent experimental analyses.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Invitrogen (#15596018, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instruction and was reverse transcribed by using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (#RR037Q, Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Real time PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (#RR820Q, Takara) on a Real time PCR system (#ABI7900HT, Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, California). For quantification of gene expression, the 2−ΔΔCt method was used. GAPDH expression was used for normalization. The primers were listed in Table 2.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from corneal tissues or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentration were quantified using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were denatured by boiling, separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-S100B (#15146-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-S100A4 (#16105-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-p-P13K (#ab182651, 1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-P13K (#ab76234, 1:1000; Abcam), anti-p-AKT (#ab38449, 1:1000; Abcam), anti-AKT (#ab8805, 1:1000; Abcam), anti-p-FoxO3a (#9466S, 1:1000; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-FoxO3a (#2497T, 1:1000; Cell Signaling), anti-VEGF (#81323-2-RR, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-USP21 (#17856-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-USP36 (#14783-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-USP44 (#A08401-2, 1:1000; Boster, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-USP7 (#26948-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-ZRANB1 (#27050-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), and anti-β-actin (#66009-1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with species-matched horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (#AB0102 or #AB0101, 1:5000; Abways, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate. For VEGF detection, protein samples were prepared under non-reducing conditions using native lysis buffer (#R0030, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 4× non-reducing loading buffer (#P1017, Solarbio) without boiling, to preserve the biologically active dimeric form.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse corneal tissues were immediately harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding. Serial 4-µm sections were prepared using a microtome (Leica, Germany). After standard deparaffinization through xylene and graded ethanol series, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin (5 min) and eosin (2 min) according to the instructions of reagent kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, #C0105S). Histopathological evaluation was performed using microscopy (Leica, Germany).

Immunofluorescence assay

For single‑label immunofluorescence in corneal tissues, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X‑100 and blocking with 3% BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against CD31 (#BA1346, Boster). Immunoreactivity was detected using Cy3‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (#A0516, Beyotime). For double‑label immunofluorescence, HCECs or mouse corneal sections were processed as follows. Cells were seeded and treated as indicated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‑100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X‑100, and blocking with 3% BSA. Both cells and sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ZRANB1 (#DF14929, Affinity Biosciences, Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) and EZH2 (#66476‑1‑Ig, Proteintech), followed by FITC‑conjugated anti‑mouse IgG (#A0568, Beyotime) and Cy3‑conjugated anti‑rabbit IgG (#A0516, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C (cells) or room temperature (sections). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. All images were captured using a fluorescence microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded sections corneal tissues were initially deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded alcohol solutions. Following natural cooling and rinsing with PBS, sections were treated with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 10 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating sections with 5% BSA. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody VEGF (#PA1080, 1:200; Boster) or EZH2 (#A19577, 1:200; ABclonal, Woburn, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. DAB (#G1212-200T, Service bio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used for color development, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, these sections were observed with a microscope (Leica, Germany). The percentage of VEGF expression was quantified by Image J software.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was detected using the CCK-8 kit (#40203ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, HCECs were seeded in 96 well plates and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and the plates were further incubated for 4 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (#Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Matrigel tube formation assay

Given that HCECs are the primary cell type injured by alkali burns and a major source of pro-angiogenic factors, their tube formation capacity was evaluated using a Matrigel-based tube formation assay. HCECs were first treated with 0.05 mol/L NaOH in complete culture medium for 24 h as described above. Following treatment, cells were harvested by trypsinization, and viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion; only cell suspensions with > 95% viability were used for seeding. 250 µL Matrigel (#356234, Corning, NY, USA) was polymerized in pre-cooled 24-well plates at 37 °C for 1 h. A total of 2 × 105 treated HCECs were then seeded onto the Matrigel matrix in complete culture medium without NaOH. After incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the formation of cellular tubes was visualized using an inverted microscope and quantified using Image J software.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration capacity was assessed using a standardized scratch wound assay. Cells were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching 95% confluence, after which a uniform wound was created in each well using a 200 µL pipette tip. Following three washes with PBS, fresh medium with the indicated treatment was added. Wound closure was monitored at 0 h and 24 h post-scratch using an inverted microscope. The width of the wounds was quantified using Image software.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion was evaluated using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers. Briefly, cells were seeded in the upper chamber pre-coated with Matrigel (#356234, Corning), while the lower chamber contained complete medium with 10% FBS as chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation, non-invading cells and the Matrigel layer on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. The invaded cells on the membrane underside were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images were captured using an inverted microscope without cutting the membrane, and the number of invaded cells was quantified using ImageJ software. This assay models the capacity of HCECs to penetrate the damaged basement membrane and invade the corneal stroma following alkali injury.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assay was carried out with the method in this article [33]. Briefly, the S100B and S100A4 promoter regions (–1500 to + 50 bp relative to the TSS) were amplified from HCEC genomic DNA by high-fidelity PCR using the following primers: S100B forward 5’-CGGGGTACCCCACCTCTACCCCCGTGGAGTT-3’ (KpnI) and reverse 5’-CCCAAGCTTTCTTTCCAAAGGCTGTTTTAACGGC-3’ (HindIII); S100A4 forward 5’-CGGGGTACCTGTGTGCGTGGGTGTGCATGGCAC-3’ (KpnI) and reverse 5’-CCCAAGCTTGTATCAAACATAACACAGTTTAT-3’ (HindIII). PCR products were digested with KpnI and HindIII and ligated into the pGL3-Basic vector. The wild-type S100B or S100A4 promoter sequence was cloned into the luciferase pGL3-Basic vector to generate the reporter construct. For S100B, the promoter region spanning 1–1430 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) was cloned (genomic coordinates: chr21:46,597,239–46,598,669, negative strand; RefSeq transcript: NM_006272.3; HGNC:10500; Ensembl: ENSR00000215611). For S100A4, the region spanning 1–1427 bp upstream of the TSS was used (chr1:153,544,292–153,545,719, positive strand; RefSeq transcript: NM_002961.3; HGNC:10494; Ensembl: ENSR00000149161). HCECs were co-transfected with S100B reporter or S100A4 reporter, along with pcDNA-EZH2 or si-EZH2 using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (#L3000015, Invitrogen). After 48 h, the firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using Dual-Lumi™ Luciferase kit (Beyotime). The EZH2 binding sites within the S100B and S100A4 promoters were predicted using the JASPAR database and validated by public ChIP-seq data: S100B (CGCGGCTGCAGGATCCTTGCCTCCAACG) and S100A4 (GTGACTGAATTTGAGCTCTCCAGGTGC). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange kit (Agilent) with pGL3-S100B-WT and pGL3-S100A4-WT as templates. Mutated plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and designated pGL3-S100B-Mut and pGL3-S100A4-Mut.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The interaction between EZH2 and S100B or S100A4 promoter was examined using a standardized ChIP protocol [34]. The chromatin or DNA-protein complex was isolated using Chromatin Extraction Kit (#ab117152, Abcam), followed by immunoprecipitation with anti-EZH2 antibody and the One Step ChIP Kit (#ab117138, Abcam). qRT-PCR was performed to detect the ChIP signal and enrichment of target gene was analyzed. The following specific primers were used in the ChIP-qPCR analysis: S100B forword sequence: 5’-CACATGATTGGATCTGGTGGCTGT-3’, S100B reverse sequence: 5’-GCCTTTCTGTGAAAGGGGTG-3’; S100A4 forword sequence: 5’-GGCATCTATCAGTGGGGTGG-3’, S100A4 reverse sequence: 5’-GGCAGTGTCTGACCCATTCA-3’.

Co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP) assay

To detect protein interactions, endogenous Co‑IP was performed in NaOH‑treated or untreated HCECs using anti‑EZH2 antibody (#66476‑1‑Ig, Proteintech) to capture endogenous EZH2, with normal IgG as a negative control. For exogenous Co‑IP, 293T cells were co‑transfected with plasmids encoding EZH2 with an HA tag and deubiquitinases (ZRANB1, USP21, USP7). In mapping assays, ZRANB1 wild‑type or its catalytically inactive mutant was expressed in 293T cells and immunoprecipitated using anti‑HA antibody to assess binding to EZH2. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA kit. Co‑IP was performed with Protein A + G Magnetic Beads (#P2179S, Beyotime). After overnight incubation at 4 °C and washing, bound proteins were eluted. Eluates and inputs were analyzed by Western blot using antibodies against EZH2, USP21, USP7, ZRANB1, or HA.

Ubiquitination assay

To evaluate EZH2 ubiquitination, 293T cells (for exogenous analysis) or HCECs (for endogenous analysis) were treated with MG132. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor. EZH2 was immunoprecipitated using anti‑HA antibody as described in the Co‑IP assay, with normal IgG as a negative control. Immunoprecipitated complexes were washed and eluted, followed by Western blot analysis using anti‑ubiquitin antibody to detect ubiquitinated EZH2. Total EZH2 levelin inputs were also analyzed.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

Treated HCECs were incubated in medium containing 100 µg/mL CHX (#HY‑12320, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Cells were harvested at 0, 3, 6 and 12 h post‑CHX treatment. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor and quantified using a BCA assay kit. Western blot analysis was performed as described above to detect EZH2 protein level at each time point.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Two-tailed Student’s t test were used for comparing two variables. One-way ANOVA test was used for multiple variable comparison. All values are presented as mean ± standard deviation. P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each assay was performed for 3 times.

Results

S100B and S100A4 were upregulated in alkali-burned corneas and their knockdown attenuated corneal neovascularization

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis was performed on dataset GSE275678 containing both alkali-burned and uninjured control samples. Following stringent quality control filtering, a total of 14,559 high-quality single cells were obtained, comprising 8,031 cells from the alkali burn group (NaOH-treated) and 6,528 cells from the uninjured control group (Fig. S1A). Dimensional reduction visualization using TSNE and UMAP revealed partially overlapping yet distinguishable distribution patterns between the two groups in transcriptomic space (Fig. S1B), indicating significant transcriptomic remodeling induced by alkali burns. Differential expression analysis identified significant upregulation of S100B and S100A4 in the alkali burn group (Fig. S1C-D). S100B and S100A4 were selected for functional validation given their established roles in corneal pathophysiology—S100A4 silencing inhibits corneal neovascularization in alkali burn models [22, 23] and S100B promotes cellular proliferation and pro-angiogenic factor release [21]. Spatial expression mapping further demonstrated distinct enrichment patterns of these two genes in specific cell subpopulations (Fig. S1E), while violin plots clearly illustrated their significant upregulation in the alkali burn group (Fig. S1F-G). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of DEGs in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. S1H). GSEA analysis further confirmed specific activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway in the alkali burn group (Fig. S1I). AUCell scoring system evaluation revealed significantly enhanced PI3K-AKT pathway activity in specific cell subpopulations of the alkali burn group (Fig. S1J-K). These findings collectively suggested that S100B, S100A4 and PI3K-AKT pathway might play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of corneal alkali burns.

To validate the expression of S100B and S100A4 in a corneal alkali burn model in vivo, an alkali-induced corneal injury model was established by treating the right corneas of C57BL/6 mice with alkali treatment (AT). A schematic of the experimental timeline is shown in Fig. 1A. qRT-PCR and Western blot assays demonstrated significantly higher levels of S100A4 and S100B in corneal tissues of the AT group compared to the Control group at 1, 3, 7 and14 days post-injury (Fig. 1B, S2A). For investigating the roles of S100B and S100A4 in corneal alkali burns, mice received subconjunctival injections of efficiency-verified lentiviral sh-S100B or sh-S100A4 prior to model establishment (Fig. S2B-E). Slit-lamp examination revealed that while Control + LV-sh-S100B and Control + LV-sh-S100A4 groups showed no significant morphological differences compared to Control + LV-sh-NC, the AT + LV-sh-NC group developed marked corneal neovascularization. Importantly, this neovascularization was significantly attenuated in both AT + LV-sh-S100B and AT + LV-sh-S100A4 groups (Fig. 1C-D). Histopathological analysis through H&E staining confirmed these observations, showing severe corneal edema and thickening in AT + LV-sh-NC corneas that was substantially reduced with S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, CD31 immunofluorescence quantification revealed extensive neovascular networks in AT + LV-sh-NC corneas that were significantly diminished following S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 1F). Correspondingly, immunohistochemical analysis showed dramatically elevated VEGF expression in AT + LV-sh-NC corneas that was substantially reduced in both knockdown groups (Fig. 1G). These results indicated that S100B and S100A4 played crucial roles in mediating alkali burn-induced corneal neovascularization.

S100B and S100A4 knockdown inhibited the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling pathway activation in corneal alkali burns

Previous studies have showed that S100A4 silencing confers a promising effect on wound healing of alkali-burned cornea by blocking the PI3K/AKT pathway while FoxO3a is a downstream effector of PI3K/AKT pathway [23, 35]. Our findings demonstrated that S100B and S100A4 regulated the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling pathway in corneal alkali burn models. In the C57BL/6 mouse model, alkali burn significantly increased phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT and FoxO3a in corneal tissues, while knockdown of either S100B or S100A4 effectively suppressed this activation (Fig. 2A). In vitro studies using HCECs transfected with si‑S100B or si‑S100A4 (knockdown efficiency validated in Fig. S3A-D) further confirmed these observations. In 0.05 mol/L NaOH-treated HCECs, S100B or S100A4 knockdown similarly significantly decreased the phosphorylation level of PI3K, AKT and FoxO3a (Fig. 2B). These results suggested that S100B and S100A4 were key regulators of the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling pathway in corneal alkali burn models.

EZH2 was upregulated and promoted S100B/S100A4 transcriptional activation in alkali-burned cornea model

It has been demonstrated that the inhibition of EZH2 alleviates corneal angiogenesis by inhibiting PI3K/Akt/FoxO3a signaling pathway [20]. Intersection of ENCODE-predicted EZH2 target genes with differentially upregulated genes from the GSE275678 dataset identified S100B and S100A4 as candidate EZH2 transcriptional targets (Fig. S4A). In C57BL/6 mice, both mRNA and protein levels of EZH2 were significantly elevated in AT group corneas compared to controls, showing progressive increase from day 1 to day 14 post-injury (Fig. 3A, S4B). In addition, immunofluorescence staining confirmed predominant nuclear localization of EZH2 in corneal tissues, with markedly stronger staining intensity in AT group than control group (Fig. 3B-C). Consistent with in vivo findings, HCECs treated with 0.05 mol/L NaOH exhibited significantly higher EZH2 expression at both mRNA and protein levels compared to untreated controls (Fig. 3D-E). These results suggested that EZH2 might be involved in the development of alkali burn-induced injury.

EZH2 protein has been shown to exert its biological activity as a transcription repressor [17]. This study demonstrated that EZH2 directly modulated S100B and S100A4 expression in both normal and alkali-injured HCECs. In untreated HCECs, EZH2 overexpression significantly increased S100B and S100A4 mRNA and protein levels while EZH2 knockdown decreased these levels (Fig. 3F-G). Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay showed that EZH2 overexpression increased the promoter activities of S100B and S100A4 in untreated HCECs, while EZH2 knockdown decreased their activities (Fig. 3H). Mutation of the EZH2 binding sites abolished these effects, confirming direct regulation through the identified promoter elements (Fig. 3I). ChIP assay verified direct binding of EZH2 to the promoters of both S100B and S100A4 under normal conditions (Fig. 3J). NaOH stimulation significantly increased S100B and S100A4 mRNA and protein levels in untreated HCECs (Fig. 3K-M). Remarkably, in NaOH-treated HCECs, EZH2 overexpression further amplified alkali-induced S100B and S100A4 upregulation at the mRNA and protein levels, whereas EZH2 knockdown attenuated this effect (Fig. 3K-M). Consistently, EZH2 also potentiated S100B and S100A4 promoter activation in NaOH-stimulated HCECs (Fig. 3N) while maintaining direct binding to their promoters (Fig. 3O). Moreover, significant H3K27me3 enrichment was observed at both the S100B and S100A4 promoter regions (Fig. S4C), suggesting that canonical EZH2 methyltransferase activity may contribute to the regulation of these loci. These findings indicated that EZH2 promoted the expression of S100B and S100A4 through transcriptional regulation.

ZRANB1 deubiquitinated and stabilized EZH2 to amplify its transcriptional and pro‑angiogenic functions in alkali‑injured corneas

To investigate the potential ubiquitination-mediated regulation of EZH2 in corneal alkali injury, potential deubiquitinases interacting with EZH2 were screened using the UbiBrowser 2.0 database, revealing several candidates including USP21, USP36, USP44, USP7, and ZRANB1 (Fig. S5). Western blot analysis of candidate deubiquitinases in NaOH‑treated HCECs revealed that ZRANB1 expression was the most significantly upregulated among the tested enzymes (Fig. 4A). Co‑IP assay confirmed endogenous interactions between EZH2 and three deubiquitinases (ZRANB1, USP21, and USP7) in HCECs (Fig. 4B), which were further validated by exogenous Co‑IP in 293T cells (Fig. 4C). Immunofluorescence staining showed ZRANB1 was distributed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, while EZH2 was predominantly localized in the nucleus, and NaOH treatment enhanced their protein expression (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, in 293T cells, ZRANB1 wild‑type bound to and stabilized EZH2 protein expression, whereas its catalytically inactive mutant did not bind to EZH2 and failed to maintain EZH2 protein stability (Fig. 4E). ZRANB1 overexpression significantly increased EZH2 protein level in 293T cells treated with either DMSO or MG132 (Fig. 4F). Importantly, ubiquitination assay demonstrated that ZRANB1 overexpression reduced exogenous EZH2 ubiquitination, while ZRANB1 knockdown increased endogenous EZH2 ubiquitination (Fig. 4G‑H). In vivo, ZRANB1 protein was significantly upregulated in alkali‑burned corneas (Fig. 4I), and its co‑localization with EZH2 was enhanced (Fig. 4J). These data collectively indicated that ZRANB1 acted as a deubiquitinase that stabilized EZH2 by reducing its ubiquitination.

EZH2 enhanced the angiogenic capacity of HCECs via S100B/S100A4-PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway

The angiogenic process is closely linked to the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of vascular endothelial cells [36]. Compared to the Con + si-NC group, NaOH + si-NC cells exhibited significantly increased VEGF expression (Fig. 6A), enhanced cell viability (Fig. 6B), greater tube formation (Fig. 6C), stronger migration (Fig. 6D), and greater invasion capacity (Fig. 6E). However, these NaOH-induced pro-angiogenic effects were attenuated by either S100B or S100A4 knockdown, but were enhanced by EZH2 overexpression. Notably, the promoting effects of EZH2 overexpression on VEGF expression, cell viability, tube formation, and migratory capacity in NaOH-stimulated HCECs were partially reversed by either S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 6A‑E). At the signaling level, S100B or S100A4 knockdown suppressed the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT and FoxO3a in NaOH-stimulated HCECs, while EZH2 overexpression enhanced this phosphorylation (Fig. 6F). Importantly, S100B or S100A4 knockdown partially reversed the enhanced phosphorylation induced by EZH2 overexpression. In parallel, ELISA analysis confirmed that secreted VEGF-A level increased more rapidly in NaOH-treated HCECs than in controls over 12, 24, and 48 h (Fig. S7), further supporting alkali-induced activation of pro-angiogenic factor secretion. Furthermore, under non-alkali conditions, S100B or S100A4 knockdown similarly reversed EZH2 overexpression-induced VEGF expression, cell viability, tube formation, migration and invasion (Fig. S8A-E). These findings indicated that EZH2 regulated S100B/S100A4-PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling to influence the angiogenic capacity of HCECs.

EZH2 promoted the corneal neovascularization via upregulating S100B or S100A4

To determine the role of EZH2 in corneal neovascularization in vivo, the expression of EZH2, S100B and S100A4 in conjunctival tissues of alkali-burned mice was modulated through lentiviral injection or DZNeP treatment. Slit-lamp examination revealed that while the AT group developed significant corneal neovascularization compared to controls, both EZH2 inhibition and knockdown of S100B or S100A4 markedly reduced corneal neovascularization. Conversely, EZH2 overexpression exacerbated corneal neovascularization, which was reversed by concurrent sh-S100B/sh-S100A4 (Fig. 7A). Histopathological analysis showed that AT group corneas exhibited severe edema and thickening, which were alleviated by DZNeP, S100B knockdown or S100A4 knockdown but worsened by EZH2 overexpression. Importantly, EZH2 overexpression induced-pathological deterioration in alkali-burned corneas was partially alleviated by concurrent S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 7B). CD31 immunofluorescence confirmed these findings, with AT + LV-EZH2 showing extensive neovascular networks that were diminished upon S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 7C-D). Correspondingly, VEGF expression followed the same pattern. EZH2 overexpression-driven VEGF upregulation was abolished by S100B or S100A4 knockdown (Fig. 7E). These findings indicated that EZH2 drove corneal neovascularization in alkali-burned mice through upregulation of S100B or S100A4.

Discussion

This study mainly focused on the roles of EZH2 in corneal alkali burn. This work demonstrated that EZH2 expression was significantly upregulated in alkali-burned corneas and predominantly localized in the nucleus, where it transcriptionally activated S100B and S100A4 through direct promoter binding. This activation in turn promoted PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway activation and subsequent corneal neovascularization. Furthermore, we identified that the deubiquitinase ZRANB1 was upregulated upon corneal alkali injury, interacted with EZH2, and enhanced its protein stability by reducing ubiquitination. Together, these findings revealed the integrated ZRANB1-EZH2-S100B/S100A4-PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a axis and provided novel therapeutic targets for corneal alkali burns.

Epigenetic mechanisms modulate gene expression and function without altering the base sequence of DNA [37]. EZH2 exerts its transcriptional regulatory function primarily through mediating H3K27me3 [38]. Previous study demonstrated that EZH2 was necessary in constraining the expression of potent developmental regulators to maintain retinal progenitor proliferation, as well as regulating the timing of late differentiation [39]. EZH2 inactivation repressed H3K27me3 expression at promoter of pro-angiogenic genes and impaired angiogenic abilities of the microvascular endothelial cell in diabetes mellitus [15]. Moreover, inhibition of EZH2 alleviated corneal angiogenesis by inhibiting FoxO3a-dependent ROS production through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [20]. Consistent with previous literature, this study confirmed that EZH2 inhibition attenuated corneal neovascularization in alkali-burned mice. However, the upstream regulators of EZH2 stability and its downstream transcriptional targets in this context remain poorly defined. More importantly, we made the novel discovery that EZH2’s pathogenic role in corneal alkali burns depended on its transcriptional regulation of S100 proteins. These findings align with recent reports demonstrating EZH2’s methyltransferase activity-independent transcriptional activation function [16]. Notably, corneal alkali burn is a complex pathological stimulus that likely activates multiple parallel pathways. The additive effects of alkali stimulation and EZH2 overexpression on S100B/S100A4 expression suggest that EZH2 functions as a critical but not exclusive regulator within this signaling network.

The PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling pathway represents one of the most crucial intracellular pathways regulating cell proliferation and differentiation [40, 41]. Recent studies have confirmed that PRMT1 promotes corneal neovascularization through PI3K/AKT activation [42], while PI3K/AKT-mediated FOXO3a phosphorylation significantly influences angiogenesis [43]. Our current study demonstrated that alkali burns markedly enhanced PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a pathway activation in corneal tissues, consistent with findings reported by Wan et al. [20]. Moving beyond confirmation of known pathway activation, we revealed the novel mechanism whereby EZH2 activated this pathway through transcriptional regulation of S100 proteins. These results not only validated the role of PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a signaling in corneal neovascularization but also uncovered a previously unrecognized upstream regulatory mechanism of this pathway. Notably, our data showed increased VEGF expression in alkali-burned corneas and enhanced VEGF secretion in NaOH-treated HCECs. These observations are mechanistically supported by previous findings that FoxO3a functions as a transcriptional repressor by directly binding to the VEGF promoter, and its phosphorylation-mediated cytoplasmic retention relieves this suppression, thereby promoting VEGF transcription [44–46]. This provides a potential mechanistic explanation for the VEGF upregulation observed in our alkali burn model.S100A4, a member of the S100 protein family, has been established as pro-angiogenic factors [47]. In alkali burn rabbit models, S100A4 silencing downregulates VEGF and inhibits corneal neovascularization [22]. Furthermore, S100A4 knockdown promotes corneal wound healing after alkali injury by blocking the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [23]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling S100A4 expression in corneal alkali burns remain poorly understood. In the present study, we extended these findings by demonstrating that S100A4, together with S100B, was directly transcriptionally regulated by EZH2. Our bioinformatics analysis identified S100B, S100A4 and the PI3K-AKT pathway as key drivers in corneal alkali burn pathogenesis. Experimental validation confirmed that S100B or S100A4 knockdown suppressed PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a activation and subsequent corneal neovascularization. This study provided the first demonstration of S100B’s critical role in corneal angiogenesis and elucidated the molecular mechanism whereby both S100B and S100A4 expression were transcriptionally regulated by EZH2.

The most significant mechanistic advance of this study lied in the identification of ZRANB1 as a novel regulator of EZH2 protein stability in corneal pathophysiology. Beyond transcriptional regulation, the present study further uncovered a crucial post-translational mechanism governing EZH2 protein stability in alkali‑injured corneas. We identified that the deubiquitinase ZRANB1 was upregulated following alkali injury, directly interacted with EZH2, and functioned to deubiquitinate and stabilize EZH2 protein, which was abolished by its catalytically inactive mutant. Notably, while ubiquitin‑mediated degradation of EZH2 has been reported in cancer contexts [28, 29], its regulation by a specific deubiquitinase in corneal pathophysiology remained unexplored. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate deubiquitinase-mediated stabilization of EZH2 in corneal injury. Our findings provided the first evidence that ZRANB1‑dependent deubiquitination sustained EZH2 protein level in corneal injury, extending the known repertoire of post‑translational controls on EZH2 [48, 49]. Importantly, this stabilization enhanced EZH2’s transcriptional activation of S100B and S100A4, thereby amplifying downstream pro‑angiogenic signaling. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that ZRANB1 was distributed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, while EZH2 was predominantly nuclear, suggesting that ZRANB1-mediated deubiquitination and stabilization of EZH2 may occur in both compartments, with the stabilized EZH2 subsequently accumulating in the nucleus to exert its transcriptional functions. These results established a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism wherein deubiquitination‑dependent stability of EZH2 critically governed its pathogenic function in corneal neovascularization, offering a novel mechanistic insight that complemented and expanded existing models of EZH2‑driven angiogenesis [20, 50, 51]. Notably, while ZRANB1 was identified as the predominant deubiquitinase stabilizing EZH2 in this study, USP21 and USP7 also interacted with EZH2. Whether these enzymes functionally compensate when ZRANB1 expression is reduced merits further investigation.

This study delineates ZRANB1‑EZH2‑S100B/S100A4‑PI3K/AKT‑FoxO3a axis as a key driver of corneal neovascularization following alkali burns. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the precise upstream signals triggering ZRANB1 upregulation after alkali injury remain to be elucidated. Second, while our findings establish ZRANB1 as a key upstream regulator of EZH2 stability and a potential therapeutic target, future studies using LV-sh-ZRANB1 or specific ZRANB1 inhibitors in alkali burn models will be necessary to validate its in vivo therapeutic efficacy and translational potential. Third, whether additional effector molecules or parallel pathways operate downstream of S100B/S100A4 merits further exploration. Moreover, while our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis, their translational relevance would be strengthened by validation in large‑animal models or ex vivo human corneal tissues. Future studies might also explore combined targeting of ZRANB1/EZH2 with existing preventing neovascularization therapies, potentially offering synergistic efficacy [52–54]. In conclusion, this study not only expands the mechanistic understanding of alkali‑induced corneal angiogenesis but also nominates ZRANB1 and EZH2 as promising candidate targets for developing novel therapeutic strategies against pathological corneal neovascularization.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that EZH2 promoted corneal neovascularization after alkali burns. We found that EZH2 was significantly upregulated in injured corneas, where it transcriptionally activated S100B and S100A4 via direct promoter binding, thereby promoting the PI3K/AKT/FoxO3a axis. Furthermore, we identified that the deubiquitinase ZRANB1 stabilized EZH2 protein through direct interaction and deubiquitination, amplifying its transcriptional activity and pro‑angiogenic function. These findings established ZRANB1‑EZH2‑S100B/S100A4‑PI3K/AKT‑FoxO3a axis as a key pathogenic pathway in alkali‑induced corneal injury and provided potential therapeutic targets for clinical management of corneal alkali burns.
