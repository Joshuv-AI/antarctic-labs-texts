Promoting Microbiology Literacy and Holistic Health: IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC Perspectives on Microbes, Diet, Lifestyle and Society—A 5‐Year Journey
Authors: Abhilash Kumar, Princy Hira, Utkarsh Sood, Pushp Lata, Shailly Anand, Anjali Saxena, Amit Kumar Singh, Helianthous Verma, Vivek Negi, Nirjara Singhvi, Ankita Dua, Aeshna Nigam, Nidhi Garg, Banishree Jali, Reecha, Renu Solanki, Priya Singh, Kiran Bala, Shekhar Nagar, Vipin Gupta, Charu Tripathi, Jasvinder Kaur, Jaspreet Kaur, Rashmi Kumari, Nitish Kumar Mahato, Roshan Kumar, Mansi Verma, Parminder Kaur, Pratyoosh Shukla, Swati Majumdar Biswas, Meenakshi Dua, Sanjukta Subudhi, Rekha Kumari, Gauri Garg, Seema Makhija, Ravi Toteja, Charu Dogra Rawat, Rajeev Singh, Sukanya Lal, Juan L Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis manuscript summarises the 5‐year journey of the International Microbiology Literacy Initiative (IMiLI) and its South Asian Regional Centre (IMiLI‐SAC). The main focus of the centre has been to propagate microbiology literacy and at the same time translate microbiome science into society. IMiLI‐SAC played an important role in engaging audiences from diverse backgrounds across India and beyond by conducting lectures, organising workshops and outreach programs. In addition, it has also translated many educational resources in regional and cultural languages for wider presentation. IMiLI‐SAC played a pivotal role in connecting microbial science with real‐world issues like sustainable development, human health, dietary choices, lifestyle, preventive healthcare and planetary health. This initiative practically translates the role of microbes in different contexts using examples like microbiome studies in India, extremophiles and their biotechnological applications, and bioremediation of contaminated sites. It emphasises the important role of microbes as essential players to sustain life on earth, and changing the perception of society that views microbes as harmful or pathogens. It also supports the concept of ‘food as medicine’ and ‘antibiotic stewardship’. However, there are certain challenges like financial constraints, resources limitations and not as much awareness. Yet, IMiLI‐SAC proposes that coordinated and multilingual community engagement will address this gap between science and society. The long‐lasting vision of IMiLI‐SAC is to incorporate microbiology literacy into school curricula, policies and public health to have a sustainable and healthy world.

Introduction: Origin of IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC

The International Microbiology Literacy Initiative (IMiLI) was established in 2019 as a global effort to promote microbiology literacy in society. The main aim of IMiLI is to emphasise the critical role of microbes in human health, environmental sustainability and planetary well‐being. Initiated by Kenneth N. Timmis and colleagues, the concept builds on foundational work highlighting the urgent need for microbiology literacy (Timmis et al. 2019). Since its inception, IMiLI has evolved into an international platform that recognises microbiology literacy as essential for informed decision‐making at all levels of society, sustainable development and improved human and planetary health.

The South Asian Regional Centre of IMiLI (IMiLI‐SAC) was established in 2022 in Noida, National Capital Region, India, under the mentorship of Rup Lal and his team. The Centre is hosted by PhiXgen Pvt. Ltd. (https://phixgen.com/), a startup company dedicated to promoting microbial literacy among school children. As a collaborative partner, PhiXgen provides the space, infrastructure and logistical support necessary for the successful implementation of IMiLI‐SAC activities.

Initially conceived as a nodal centre to coordinate IMiLI activities in the region, IMiLI‐SAC was tasked with maintaining, creating and hosting on its website (http://imili.org) the IMiLI school curriculum in societally‐relevant microbiology— ‘topic frameworks’ and other educational resources, portrait galleries, recommendations for class excursions to experience microbes in action, (Timmis et al. 2024) facilitating outreach programs and strengthening regional engagement while coordinating with other IMiLI centres globally. Since its establishment, IMiLI‐SAC has actively advanced microbiology literacy, particularly across South Asia and India.

In addition, over the past 5 years, IMiLI and its Regional Centres, particularly IMiLI‐SAC, have promoted microbiology literacy among diverse audiences, including school students, college students and the general public. IMiLI‐SAC, along with IMiLI, has contributed to the creation of context‐specific educational materials, expert lectures and organised workshops, student cafés, school outreach programs and thematic sessions at national and international conferences (Ramos et al. 2026; Timmis et al. 2019). Although it began as a modest initiative aimed at integrating microbes into everyday consciousness, IMiLI‐SAC has expanded substantially, delivering microbial literacy programs across India and extending its activities to Nepal.

Central to these efforts is IMiLI‐SAC's emphasis on community engagement, school integration, development of innovative, interactive educational tools and translation of IMiLI resources into several Indian regional languages. These initiatives promote culturally adapted, experience‐based approaches that connect microbial science with everyday life and broader societal challenges such as sustainability and public health.

While IMiLI has developed open‐access resources and engaged society on the indispensable roles of microbes in climate action, preventive healthcare, nutrition, the circular economy and environmental stewardship (Aggarwal et al. 2023; Anand, Hallsworth, et al. 2023; Crowther et al. 2024; Ramos et al. 2025), IMiLI‐SAC has further strengthened this engagement by fostering collaborations with partners across Spain, China, Portugal, Germany and Costa Rica.

While earlier publications have established the critical importance of microbiology literacy (Timmis et al. 2019; Timmis, Baquero, Timmis, et al. 2025; Timmis, Baquero, Lal, et al. 2025; Timmis, Karahan et al. 2025; Anand, Parveen Shaikh, et al. 2023; Crowther et al. 2024; Ramos et al. 2025), this manuscript focuses on implementation. It presents a structured account of the evolution, organisation and activities of IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC over the past 5 years. This article highlights how coordinated regional efforts, multilingual educational tools and community‐based interventions have contributed to bridging microbiology and microbiome science with public health practices in real‐world contexts such as diet, lifestyle behaviours and preventive healthcare.

Bridging Science and Society Through IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC

Despite significant advances in microbiology and microbiome research, the translation of this knowledge into societal understanding and everyday decision‐making remains limited. Several leading scientific societies, including Applied Microbiology International (AMI), the International Society for Microbial Ecology (ISME), the Federation of European Microbiological Societies (FEMS), American Society for Microbiology (ASM), Association of Microbiologists of India (AMI) and Microbiologists Society of India (MBSI), have recognised the importance of microbiology literacy and have taken steps to promote awareness of the roles of microorganisms in health, environment and sustainability. However, the translation of microbiological knowledge into structured, accessible and widely adopted educational practices for society remains incomplete. As mentioned above, IMiLI adopts an implementation‐oriented approach through the development of open‐access, learner‐centred resources such as Topic Frameworks, MicroStar Portrait Galleries, class excursions and experiential learning modules, linking microbial science with everyday life (Figure 1).

Overview of activities under the International Microbiology Literacy Initiative (IMiLI). The framework highlights diverse approaches to enhancing microbiology literacy, including development of topic‐based conceptual frameworks, use of visual aids (diagrams, infographics and models) and portrait galleries integrating art and science. It also showcases hands‐on class experiments for experiential learning, workshops to bridge scientific knowledge with public understanding, outreach programs aimed at engaging school students, editorials that simplify research for broader audiences and special sessions conducted across schools, festivals and conferences to promote inclusive and accessible microbial awareness.

IMiLI established a decentralised network of regional centres to ensure that this vision is translated into practice. With the establishment of the first regional centre, IMiLI‐SAC, the practical implementation of IMiLI's global vision became more clearly defined. Since then, IMiLI has expanded into a network of regional centres, including IMiLI‐SAC (South Asian Centre), India; IMiLI‐EAH (East Asia Headquarters), China; IMiLI‐GIH (Granada International Hub), Spain; and IMiLI‐CAC (Central American Centre), with new centres in Portugal and Australia currently being developed.

These Regional Centres help adapt IMiLI resources to local languages, educational systems and cultural contexts, allowing microbiology literacy initiative to reach broader audiences more effectively. One of the major strengths of this network is the localisation of core resources into languages such as Hindi, Spanish, Chinese and Portuguese, along with region‐specific modifications that improve accessibility and relevance for local communities. This coordinated framework allows microbiology literacy to be communicated effectively at both regional and global scales. IMiLI‐SAC being the first regional centre demonstrated a functional model for other regional centres for scaling and localising microbiology literacy initiatives. IMiLI‐SAC coordinates with other regional centres and contributes to the maintenance of IMiLI's central resources and website (www.imili.org). The centre also actively engaged with educators, researchers and the wider public to promote microbiology literacy.

Since its inception, IMiLI‐SAC implemented a practical approach and adopted communication strategies that are context‐specific. The centre integrated the use of local languages and culturally and regionally relevant examples to ensure that microbiology literacy is reachable and significant to all. The placement of IMiLI‐SAC within Phixgen Pvt. Ltd. ensured a very sophisticated interaction between education, research, administration and outreach. This interaction facilitates a smooth translation of microbiology literacy to society.

Thus, the establishment of IMiLI‐SAC represents not only the regional expansion of IMiLI but also a critical step in translating microbiology literacy into practice. MiLI‐SAC serves as a key regional hub for implementing IMiLI's objectives in the South Asia. Driven by the long standing efforts of Kenneth Timmis, IMiLI provides a global framework for advancing microbiology literacy, while IMiLI‐SAC shows how this vision can be effectively translated into meaningful actions at the regional level. IMiLI‐SAC exemplifies how global scientific initiatives can be adapted to local needs through regional centres, bridging the gap between microbiology or microbiome science and societal understanding. IMiLI‐SAC focuses on contextualisation by incorporating public health challenges, cultural practices and societal needs into its activities.

IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC scope includes the development of localised educational resources, structured lecture series, workshops and outreach programs for the students, educators, policymakers and the general public. Through these initiatives, IMiLI‐SAC highlights the role of microbes in human health, environmental sustainability and everyday life. IMiLI promotes the responsible antibiotic use and microbiome‐friendly lifestyle choices.

Over the past 5 years, IMiLI‐SAC has promoted microbiology literacy into practice through activity‐based engagement. More than 40 expert lectures, ~10 workshops and multiple outreach initiatives have been conducted across schools, universities, conferences and public platforms in the last 5 years. In addition, Microsphere programs have been conducted every year on International Microorganism Day. These efforts of spreading microbiology awareness extend beyond India into neighbouring regions such as Nepal.

The educational content is designed according to local needs and realities, such as dietary practices, environmental concerns and public health challenges. Use of regional languages and interactive activities makes the content easy to understand and more understandable. IMiLI is evolving into a global network through collaborations with regional centres across Europe, East Asia and Central America. This shows the growth of IMiLI from a conceptual initiative to an operational global platform.

Supplementing the Vision of IMiLI: Microbes, Environment and Society

A central objective of the IMiLI is to spread awareness about the crucial role of microorganisms in sustaining the environment. Contributing to this goal, IMiLI‐SAC has actively highlighted the ecological and functional roles of microbes in different environments such as soils, oceans and extreme habitats. Its outreach activities highlight that microbes are the major drivers of life supporting processes on Earth, challenging the common perception in the society that plants and animals alone sustain life. Microorganisms contribute significantly to global nitrogen cycling, absorb large amount of oceanic carbon dioxide and produce nearly half of Earth's oxygen (Falkowski et al. 1998; Flombaum et al. 2013; Fowler et al. 2013). Their large numbers further underscore their importance, with total microbial cell numbers estimated at 1030–1031 and prokaryotes accounting for approximately 50–70 gigatons of carbon, which makes about 10%–20% of global biomass (Whitman et al. 1998; Locey and Lennon 2016; Bar‐on et al. 2018). Microorganisms play an essential role in our everyday lives. They are involved in production of many dairy products and fermented foods and also support agriculture, waste treatment and environmental sustainability. In addition, microbes are widely used in industrial and biomedical applications, including the production of antibiotics, vaccines, vitamins, enzymes and other bioactive compounds.

Despite their importance, microbes are still mostly viewed only as harmful disease causing organisms. IMiLI‐SAC works to change this perception by making microbiology easier to understand and relate to for students, educators, policymakers and the general public. These efforts highlights how microbes regulate ecosystem functions, maintain soil fertility, support biogeochemical cycles and act as natural agents of environmental clean‐up.

IMiLI‐SAC has actively worked to turn this vision into reality by organising several workshops, conferences, lectures and outreach programs to diverse audiences including students, policymakers and administrators. These activities increase awareness about the role of microbes in environmental protection and sustainability. Additionally, our team of more than 25 young microbiologists developed creative flipbooks to introduce children to microbiology through alphabets, using simple language and visuals to explain scientific concepts (Anand et al. 2021) (Figure 2). Also, interactive cue cards that connect different Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) with specific microbes help learners understand the microorganism role in achieving sustainability goals (Ramos et al. 2026). While these efforts represent an initial step, IMiLI‐SAC aims to expand them into a broader movement to strengthen microbiology literacy and environmental awareness at a societal scale.

Microbes as Earth's Natural Recyclers and Planetary Cleansing System: An IMiLI‐SAC Perspective

Building on its broader efforts to communicate that microbes are central to life on Earth, IMiLI‐SAC places particular emphasis through different activities on explaining the role of microorganisms as Earth's natural recyclers and environmental custodians. A key message consistently conveyed in IMiLI‐SAC lectures is that microbial communities sustain planetary functioning by driving essential biogeochemical cycles, including carbon, nitrogen, sulphur and phosphorus cycling. Audiences are introduced to the role of microbes in decomposing organic matter, recycling nutrients and maintaining ecosystem continuity through simple explanations. These processes are presented as ongoing natural systems that support agriculture, soil fertility and environmental balance in everyday contexts. IMiLI‐SAC further highlights the role of microbes as effective agents of environmental detoxification. A major component of IMiLI‐SAC outreach activity is to use real‐world scientific examples to illustrate microbial capabilities. For instance, we frequently present our studies on hexachlorocyclohexane (HCH)‐contaminated sites in India, where indigenous microbial communities acquired catabolic genes and developed degradation pathways for this synthetic pesticide within 10–15 years. These examples highlight the ability of microbes to adapt in response to pollutants and their role in environmental clean‐up (Lal et al. 2010, Phian et al. 2024). Through this, IMiLI‐SAC highlights an important message: while human activities generate waste and environmental stress, microbial systems are continuously working to restore balance. By communicating this perspective, IMiLI‐SAC helps improve public understanding of microbial functions and also supports IMiLI's broader aim of bringing microbiology into public awareness, societal thinking and decision‐making.

We present to students, young faculty members and society at large our research on the Manikaran hot springs located in the Himalayan ranges, which demonstrates that microorganisms can survive and remain metabolically active even at temperatures approaching 90°C (Dwivedi et al. 2015; Tripathi et al. 2017). The Manikaran hot springs in Himachal Pradesh, India, are considered sacred and many people visit the temples and bathe in the hot waters with the belief that they possess healing properties. IMiLI‐SAC uses such regional examples to connect scientific understanding with societal beliefs. We highlight our work on Thermus parvatiensis, a thermophilic bacterium isolated from these hot springs, which produces thermostable enzymes such as DNA polymerase and proteases with important industrial and biotechnological applications (Dwivedi et al. 2015; Tripathi et al. 2017). These examples demonstrate how extremophilic microbes not only survive under harsh conditions but also provide valuable biomolecules with wide‐ranging applications in biotechnology, diagnostics and industry.

Human Health, Lifestyle and Preventive Healthcare: IMiLI‐SAC Outreach, Lecture‐Based Engagement and Societal Translation

A major focus of IMiLI‐SAC has been to translate advances in microbiology and microbiome science into accessible, context relevant knowledge for society, with emphasis on human health, lifestyle and preventive healthcare. This section documents IMiLI‐SAC activities over the past 5 years, including expert lectures, workshops, multiple national and international conference sessions and annual Microsphere programs organised on International Microorganism Day (17 September) and outreach school programs. These initiatives have engaged diverse audiences ranging from school children and university students to educators, researchers, policymakers and community stakeholders across India and international platforms.

These activities form the core of the societal engagement strategy of IMiLI‐SAC, where complex microbiome concepts are translated into understandable, real‐life contexts. The human microbiome is presented as a complex, multisite system, including the gut, oral, skin, respiratory and urogenital microbiomes, thereby moving beyond a gut‐centric narrative. IMiLI‐SAC emphasises that microbial communities play essential roles in digestion, immune regulation, metabolism and protection against pathogens. Also, communicating that microbiome–health relationship is still an evolving scientific field and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Importantly, IMiLI‐SAC outreach integrates evidence from our own research to strengthen societal understanding. In several lectures, findings from microbiome studies on Indian cohorts, particularly tuberculosis (TB) patients (Maji et al. 2018), are presented to illustrate how disease conditions and prolonged antibiotic treatments can significantly alter gut microbial composition and diversity. These studies demonstrate that microbiome profiles are population‐specific and influenced by local diet, lifestyle and disease burden, reinforcing the need for context‐dependent interpretation of microbiome data.

This emphasis on population specificity is further supported by emerging evidence from Indian microbiome studies, which indicate that the Indian gut represents a distinct biological ecosystem rather than a simple variation of Western microbiomes. For this we use the data from recent reports that highlight the dominance of Prevotella copri in Indian populations, reflecting long‐standing high‐fibre, plant‐based dietary patterns, as well as greater genetic and functional diversity within the gut microbiome (Prasoodanan et al. 2021). Such findings reinforce IMiLI‐SAC's consistent message in lectures that microbiome‐based health strategies must be tailored to regional dietary habits, cultural practices and population‐specific microbial signatures. IMiLI‐SAC emphasises connecting microbiome science with everyday lifestyle practices and culturally relevant examples.

Lifestyle‐related discussions in IMiLI‐SAC activities extend to physical activity, sleep and stress management. These factors are framed within the broader concept of physiological balance and well‐being rather than attributing direct microbiome effects to specific practices without sufficient evidence. This ensures that public communication remains scientifically accurate while still being practical and relatable.

IMiLI‐SAC also incorporates translational and real‐world examples from recent microbiome research, including probiotics, synbiotics, microbiome‐based diagnostics and emerging therapies such as faecal microbiota transplantation and live biotherapeutic products (Anand, Hallsworth, et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2026). These examples are used to explain the ongoing shift from pathogen‐centric approaches to microbial ecosystem restoration and personalised healthcare.

To maximise engagement and impact, IMiLI‐SAC adopts diverse communication formats including café‐style dialogues, storytelling‐based lectures, visual teaching tools and school outreach programs (Ramos et al. 2026). These approaches are often adapted to local cultural and linguistic contexts, demonstrating how IMiLI initiatives are customised for different audiences.

Importantly, feedback from these engagements indicates that such roles of microbes in environmental sustainability are not widely recognised in society. IMiLI‐SAC therefore prioritises experiential and context‐specific communication approaches to bridge this knowledge gap. By linking microbial processes to visible environmental challenges, the initiative demonstrates how microbiology literacy can inform practical understanding and responsible environmental behaviour.

Building on the goals of Common Scientific Terminology and Translation (CSTT), which aims to make scientific language more accessible and standardised across diverse audiences, IMiLI‐SAC seeks to implement these objectives within the IMiLI framework in a more understandable and regionally relevant manner. Recognising that technical terminology remains a barrier to public understanding, IMiLI‐SAC is now initiating efforts to develop simplified and standardised vocabulary related to the human microbiome, particularly in regional languages. This initiative, informed by lecture interactions across diverse audiences, aims to improve the accessibility of emerging microbiome concepts and support informed health‐related decision‐making.

Overall, by integrating lecture‐driven dissemination, evidence from region‐specific microbiome research (including TB cohort studies), culturally contextualised examples and real‐world Indian microbiome insights, IMiLI‐SAC effectively bridges the gap between microbiome research and societal understanding. These efforts directly support IMiLI's mission to promote microbiology literacy as a foundation for preventive healthcare, sustainable living and informed public decision‐making, while explicitly addressing reviewer expectations for implementation evidence, contextual relevance and impact‐oriented communication.

Promoting Food as Medicine: A IMiLI‐SAC Outreach Concept on Antibiotic Stewardship

One of the serious global challenges, mainly in India, remains the excessive and improper use of antibiotics because of easy access, self‐medication and exposure to antibiotic traces in animal‐derived products or contaminated produce. This exposure to antibiotics leads to a rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR). To address this issue, IMiLI‐SAC works through lectures and outreach programs to raise awareness of antibiotic stewardship among students, society, policymakers and healthcare professionals. Additionally, we emphasise that inappropriate use of antibiotics disrupts gut microbial diversity and balance, pertaining to long‐term effects for patients and public health. The central idea is to emphasise the optimal use of antibiotics with the correct drug, dose and duration to reduce AMR. Further, there is a shift in approach from antibiotic‐dependent treatments to informed microbiome health practices. Therefore, IMiLI‐SAC promotes the idea of using “food as medicine, not medicine as food” and encourages diets that supplement gut health and reduce dependence on drugs.

However, this concept is propagated with a balanced perspective, understanding that antibiotics are important when required clinically, while advocating their judicious use. IMiLI‐SAC underscores the need to integrate microbiology literacy and nutrition guidelines with emphasis on improving dietary habits and health choices into school curricula to promote human health at the population level.

Microbes in Society: IMiLI‐SAC on Public Misconceptions

The outreach programs identified a common misconception among society that microbes are principally harmful. IMiLI‐SAC consistently addresses this gap by presenting evidence that the overwhelming majority of the enormous number—perhaps a trillion—of species of microorganisms are beneficial and essential for maintaining ecological balance and human health (Locey and Lennon 2016). On the contrary, only about 1400 species are known to cause infections or diseases (Rappuoli et al. 2023). IMiLI‐SAC attempts to explain this in simple, effective and creative ways for school and college students. Interactive games are designed like ‘match the microbes and their roles’ to make students understand advantageous microbes supporting important functions like nutrient cycling and absorption, synthesis of vitamins, immune response and inflammation control. Resource persons display small parodies/plays based on good and bad microbial characters to make learning exciting and easy to remember. Further, for play group children, interactive placards are used to show how microbes interact with each other and the environment in a humble way (Figure 2).

These hands‐on activities demonstrated by IMiLI‐SAC also introduce ideas like the gut‐brain axis, expanding the concept that gut microbiota affects not only the body but also the nervous system, influencing mood, depression and anxiety (O'Mahony et al. 2015; De Vos et al. 2022; Barakat et al. 2024). Therefore, the perception of microbes being pathogens only is changing, and their role as essential allies in maintaining overall health is recognised.

In addition to maintaining human health, IMiLI‐SAC also emphasises the role of microbes for the survival of the planet. As mentioned above, microbes are the most abundant and oldest life forms that are essential for life on Earth. Further, they are also important for waste treatment and environmental sustainability (Crowther et al. 2024). The diversity is so enormous that microbes even thrive in extreme environmental conditions, from the deep oceans to polar ice and hydrothermal hot‐springs displaying unparalleled resilience. IMiLI‐SAC team further explains the society of about adaptability of microbes through studies of extremophiles from Manikaran and Kheerganga hot‐springs in the Himalayas (Dwivedi et al. 2015; Tripathi et al. 2017). Microbes residing under such conditions not tolerate extreme temperatures and chemicals but also harbour unique genes and metabolic pathways. Their enzymes possess significant biotechnological and industrial potential due to thermostability and efficiency. Such findings, strengthen the idea that extremophiles are not just biological curiosities but key contributors to understanding life's limits, ecosystem functioning and innovative applications in science and technology. IMiLI‐SAC highlights these important roles of microbes through its delivered lectures.

Sustainable Development Goals and Advancing India's Bio‐E3 Policy

IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC promote microbiology literacy by helping society understand microbes as essential, irreplaceable partners and responsibly harnessing their power for a healthier, safer and more resilient future. Around the world, governments and organisations are launching initiatives to secure a peaceful and economically strong future. However, two global priorities that directly align with the mission of IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC are the United Nations‐Sustainable Development Goals (UN‐SDGs) and India's Bio‐E3 Policy (Bio‐Economy, Bio‐Employment and Bio‐Environment). A critical yet widely overlooked fact, even among policymakers, is that achieving the SDGs is impossible without microbial science and technology (Timmis et al. 2017; Crowther et al. 2024). Microbes are indispensable for advancing food security, health, clean water, sanitation, renewable energy, sustainable consumption and production, climate regulation and the protection of ecosystems on land and in the ocean. The presence of microbes in almost every environment and their roles in various biological and environmental systems make them powerful drivers for synergistic sustainability solutions that work for the benefit of humanity and the planet. Despite their enormous contributions, still microbes remain under‐represented in national and global sustainability agendas.

Similarly, Bio‐E3 can only be fully achieved when microbial innovation and microbial health management are placed in the core strategy and implementation. Microorganisms enhance the bio‐economy by generating enzymes, vaccines, antibiotics, fermented food products, probiotics, therapeutics based on microbiomes, bioplastic and biofuels (Tripathi et al. 2022). They expand bio‐employment by growth in biotech start‐ups, microbial farming, waste transformation, environmental microbiology services, circular manufacturing and bioinformatics. In essence, microbial technologies represent the delivery mechanism for the Bio‐E3 vision. Bio‐E3 remains aspirational rather than operational without microbes. Addressing emerging challenges including the accelerating rise of extreme drug resistant microbial threats, antibiotic misuse across sectors and the growing health burden in polluted environments requires strengthening microbiology literacy among policymakers, stakeholders and the public. Integrating such literacy into governance frameworks is essential for transforming Bio‐E3 into reality.

From Regional Outreach to Global Engagement: Expanding the Reach of IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC

Our activities under IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC have steadily expanded from regional outreach initiatives to wider national and international platforms. At the FEMS 2023 Congress in Hamburg, Germany, Professor Kenneth Timmis, founder of IMiLI, delivered a prestigious plenary lecture after receiving the FEMS‐Lwoff Award for Achievements in Microbiology 2023. He delivered a lecture on ‘Microbiology Literacy and Human Stewardship of Planet Earth: The Generational Contract’, which emphasised the origin of IMiLI and its global vision, highlighting the establishment of IMiLI‐SAC as the first regional centre in Noida, National Capital Region, to propagate microbiology literacy into societal practice. He further reinforced the relevance of IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC and its international visibility.

IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC activities are recognised at various international platforms. For instance, the Federation of European Microbiological Societies (FEMS) and the International Society for Microbial Ecology (ISME), for which Prof. Lal serves as an Ambassador to India, have invited our team to organise special thematic sessions and world café sessions on microbiology literacy and societal engagement during annual international conferences.

IMiLI‐SAC participated in the FEMS conference held in Hamburg, Germany, in 2023 and Milan, Italy, in 2025. In addition, we also participated in the ISME19 conference in Cape Town, South Africa, held in 2024. Also, there is a dedicated session in an upcoming ISME‐20 conference in Auckland, New Zealand (2026) (Session: Microbial marvels: advancing microbial literacy for a healthier world). In addition, our work and outreach activities were also highlighted through invited participation and lectures at the International Union of Microbiological Societies (IUMS) conference held in Florence, Italy (2024). Through these international forums, IMiLI‐SAC has contributed to discussions on microbial literacy, microbiomes, sustainability and the broader societal relevance of microbiology.

Challenges in Implementing Microbiology Literacy: IMiLI‐SAC Experience

Translating microbiology literacy into society and everyday life remains challenging despite efforts by IMiLI‐SAC. A major constraint is the perception of society that continues to visualise microbes as pathogens despite their beneficial roles. Change in perception can be executed when microbiology literacy is included in school curricula in a structured form. In addition, policy makers and administrators are yet to realise the importance of microbiology and its essential role in maintaining human health, environmental sustainability and overall planet health.

Further, complicated terminologies and language barriers also make it difficult for a non‐science society to understand technical terms such as microbiome and microbiota. Therefore, there is a need to adapt to new strategies and approaches in context to the regional diversity and cultures at the education levels.

Absence of substantial funding and the need for engaging and sustaining efforts also pose major limitations to scaling activities. At times, academicians do not actively participate in outreach activities beyond their respective teaching and administrative duties. Further, bringing together young learners, school children and the broader public on one platform requires continuing efforts to propagate the concept of microbiology literacy, and its importance for day‐to‐day life and planetary well‐being.

Further, oversimplifying microbiome science to be able to be understood by society also requires a critical balance. In addition, awareness campaigns and outreach programs cannot stand alone to solve issues like antibiotic misuse, as they need proper counselling and clinical knowledge.

Therefore, long‐term sustaining efforts and engaging society at large remain challenging and need to be addressed directly to realise the importance of microbiology literacy.

Path Forward: Advancing Microbiology Literacy as a Societal Movement

To advance microbiology literacy as a global societal movement, we must translate scientific knowledge from abstract lab science into the practical world for sustainable decision‐making.

IMiLI and IMiLI‐SAC are continuously working and associating with educators, policymakers, administrators and scientists to develop strategies that are societally meaningful and scientifically sound. Amongst many initiatives, a central strategy is to embed microbiology literacy into schools and higher education systems in multilingual and cultural resources to foster a public understanding of science. Therefore, expansion of outreach activities like workshops and lectures provides interactive platforms to connect science with society and understand the context of human and planet health, nutrition, environment and sustainability.

IMiLI‐SAC also aims to create simple and standardised vocabulary in regional languages in India to overcome communication blocks and enhance the understanding of notions like human microbiome among the public. Firming up coordination among regional centres and enhancing engagement with policymakers will further enable the translation of microbiology literacy into public health and sustainability frameworks.

IMiLI‐SAC aims to use the microbiome revolution as a practical tool to build healthier societies and a safer planet. It highlights new possibilities such as personalised healthcare through AI‐guided diets, engineered probiotics and targeted microbial interventions. Its vision is to link personal health with planetary health by making microbiology literacy an essential part of preventive and holistic healthcare.

We believe that this modest beginning over the past few years has laid a strong foundation and will gradually evolve into a large‐scale societal movement, making microbiology literacy an integral part of informed and sustainable living. We further hope that in the coming years, IMiLI will emerge as a force to reckon with in advancing microbiology literacy globally.
