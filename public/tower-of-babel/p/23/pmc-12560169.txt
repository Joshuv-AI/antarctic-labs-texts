Motor and Cognitive Fatigue in Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy
Authors: Oliver L Steiner, Claudia Angela, Rohat Geran, Fabian Klostermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP) is the most prevalent dysimmune disease of sensorimotor nerves. However, based on various non‐sensorimotor symptoms (NSMS), the concept of a purely peripheral condition has recently been challenged. Against this background, we aimed to differentiate motor and cognitive components of fatigue as the most prominent NSMS in CIDP.MethodsCognitive and motor fatigue constructs were assessed in 47 CIDP patients and 31 healthy controls (HC) using the Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognitive Functions (FSMC). The results were referred to score data for sensorimotor disability as well as for depressiveness, sleep quality, and alertness, describing functional levels potentially related to fatigue. Further, they were analyzed with respect to serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL), a biomarker of disease progression and potentially central involvement in CIDP.ResultsCIDP patients reported significantly higher scores of total (p < 0.001), motor (p < 0.001), and cognitive (p = 0.005) fatigue compared to HC. Within the CIDP group, motor fatigue was more pronounced than cognitive fatigue compared to HC (p < 0.001). Although alertness levels did not differ significantly between the groups, poorer alertness correlated with cognitive fatigue in the CIDP cohort (p = 0.02). Notably, only functional disability and depressiveness served as predictors for both fatigue components (p < 0.05). sNfL levels showed no significant association with fatigue.DiscussionThese findings show that the burden of CIDP is not sufficiently described by sensorimotor disability. A clinically relevant NSMS aspect of the disease is fatigue in motor as well as cognitive dimensions.Keywords: chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, cognitive fatigue, motor fatigue, serum neurofilament light chain

Introduction

Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP) is a chronic progressive or relapsing disorder, pathophysiologically characterized by dysimmune processes against the myelin sheath of peripheral nerve axons. Clinically, CIDP patients mostly present with symmetrical proximal and distal muscle weakness and corresponding sensory disturbances, but besides variants with only distal, patchy‐focal, pure motor or sensory deficits exist [1]. However, although CIDP is phenotypically dominated by sensorimotor deficits [1, 2], a growing body of evidence points to the additional presence of non‐sensorimotor symptoms (NSMS) with a strong negative quality of life impact [3].

Probably the most prevalent NSMS is fatigue [3, 4]. It affects 40%–80% of persons with CIDP [5], which equates to the prevalence of fatigue observed in, for example, multiple sclerosis (MS) [6]. This is of interest, since fatigue is a multidimensional construct, composed of components attributed to central versus peripheral factors [7]. In neurological disorders, the latter mainly relates to reduced neuromuscular endurance, resulting from impaired nerve conduction and transmission failure [7]. Accordingly, correlations between motor fatigue and peripheral nerve dysfunction were shown in persons with CIDP [8]. In contrast, cognitive fatigue refers to cognitive problems and reduced alertness, arousal, and motivation, thought to arise from disconnections within widespread CNS networks [7]. In this study, we used the Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognitive Functions (FSMC) to differentiate cognitive fatigue as an experience of mental or cognitive exhaustion, associated with increased efforts for accomplishing attention and concentration demands, from motor fatigue as a feeling of physical tiredness or exertion‐related decline in motor performance. Considering pathophysiology and lead symptoms, fatigue in CIDP has traditionally been interpreted as peripheral.

This perspective, however, may be incomplete for several reasons. In particular, recent research on CIDP‐related NSMS suggested that some NSMS indicate subtle central nervous system (CNS) involvement in CIDP. For example, persons with CIDP show slight, yet significant deficits in different cognitive tasks [9, 10]. In so doing, low cognitive performance was found to be associated with high levels of serum neurofilament light chain [11], a biomarker of axonal damage, also elevated in central neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease [12, 13]. Further, CIDP was shown to unfold effects at quite unexpected sites of the nervous system, for example, as structural and functional retinal changes [14, 15].

Against this background, we investigated whether fatigue in CIDP went beyond reduced motor endurance. Therefore, FSMC data were assessed in persons with versus without CIDP and compared between the groups. The results for the peripheral and cognitive fatigue components were also related to the sensorimotor disability scores, further NSMS and sNfL levels in the persons with CIDP. The findings were discussed with respect to the nature of fatigue in CIDP and a comprehensive assessment of the clinical condition as a basis for individualized treatment recommendations.

Methods

Study Design

We employed a cross‐sectional study design to compare persons with definite CIDP to age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls. The study received ethical approval from the institutional review board of Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA/165/16) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before study enrollment.

Participants

Forty‐seven persons with CIDP meeting the European Federation of Neurological Societies/Peripheral Nerve Society (EFNS/PNS) diagnostic criteria [1] for definitive CIDP were included. All of them received stable maintenance therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). Among the participants with CIDP, 30 presented with typical symmetric proximal, and distal sensorimotor deficits, whereas 16 exhibited CIDP variants. Exclusion criteria were other neurological conditions beyond CIDP. Further, 31 healthy age‐ and sex‐matched controls took part in the study.

Clinical and Psychometric Assessments

Fatigue

The FSMC, a validated 20‐item self‐report questionnaire, was the primary tool for measuring fatigue [16]. It quantifies fatigue severity across motor and cognitive domains (10 items each), with scores ranging from 10 to 50 per subscale and a total score from 20 to 100, higher scores indicating greater fatigue. Predefined cutoffs for the total score categorized overall fatigue as mild (≥ 43), moderate (≥ 53), and severe (≥ 63) [16].

Depression

The Rasch‐based Depression Screening (DESC_I) was used to quantify depressive symptoms over the preceding 2 weeks [17]. This instrument assesses the frequency of depressive thoughts and feelings, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 40. A score ≥ 12 is considered indicative of clinically relevant depressive symptoms.

Subjective Sleep Quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) evaluated subjective sleep quality over the past month [18]. The global PSQI score (range 0–21) was used, with scores > 5 indicating poor sleep quality.

Sensorimotor Disability

Functional disability in the CIDP group was assessed using the Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (R‐ODS), a 24‐item patient‐reported outcome measure [2]. It evaluates perceived difficulties in daily activities, depending on the integrity of sensorimotor functions. Each item is evaluated with 0 (worst) to 2 (best) points, so that total scores from 0 to 48 can be reached, higher values reflecting lower disability. Control subjects were confirmed to be fully independent in activities of daily living. R‐ODS scores in the CIDP group were used to explore correlations between the degree of disability and reported fatigue.

Alertness

Alertness can be divided into two components: phasic alertness, in which participants respond to a warning cue, and intrinsic alertness, in which participants must react to an unexpected stimulus without prior warning. In the present study, we used an intrinsic alertness subtest from the Wahrnehmungs‐ und Aufmerksamkeitsfunktionen‐Batterie (WAF) PsyExpert digital psychometry for neurocognitive assessment. Participants were instructed to press a response button as quickly as possible immediately upon the appearance of a circle on the screen. Reaction time (RT) in attention tasks serves as a surrogate marker for cognitive fatigue in neurological conditions [19]. For example, all studies performed in MS, showed significant correlations between self‐reported fatigue and performance‐based alertness measures [20], whereas no correlations were identified with cognitive performances, for example, in memory, language, or visuospatial domains.

sNfL Measurement

Serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) levels of 35 of the 47 CIDP patients were available. They had been determined by the Single Molecule Array (SIMOA) technology on the HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix Inc., Billerica, MA, USA) with the NfL advantage kit [21]. Using this digital immunoassay, individual molecules attached to paramagnetic beads can be detected with sensitivities reaching the femtomolar level [22]. Z‐scores for sNfL, adjusted for age and BMI, were computed using the online sNfL Reference App. (https://shiny.dkfbasel.ch/baselnflreference/) [23].

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 30.0.0). Data visualization was performed in Jupyter Notebook (version 7.0.8) with Python (version 3.12.4). Variable distributions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test alongside visual inspection of the data distribution. As none of the continuous variables met the assumption of normality, group comparisons were carried out using Mann–Whitney U tests.

To identify predictors of the cognitive and motor components of fatigue in relation to CIDP pathology, we designed two multiple regression models. Because several potential predictors were available, including all of them simultaneously would have increased the risk of model overfitting. Therefore, in the first step, we examined the bivariate associations between variables using Spearman's rank correlations (given that the assumptions of normality were not met). To account for multiple testing, p values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. In the second step, only those variables that showed significant associations with fatigue were entered into the multiple regression models.

Group Differences in Fatigue, Depression and Sleep

With respect to the FSMC data, CIDP patients exhibited significantly increased scores for total fatigue (mean ± SD: 55.8 ± 23.1 vs. 35.1 ± 13.6; p < 0.001, for the visual illustration see Figure 1), cognitive fatigue (25.1 ± 11.9 vs. 17.7 ± 7.5; p < 0.01) and motor fatigue (30.1 ± 11.7 vs. 17.3 ± 6.7; p < 0.001). When comparing the difference between cognitive fatigue and motor fatigue, we observed significantly higher FSMC motor components in CIDP patients compared to controls (−5.0 ± 5.7 vs. 0.4 ± 4.3; p < 0.001). DESC_I (7.47 ± 7.8 vs. 3.3 ± 3.2; p = 0.027) and PSQI (8.97 ± 4.1 vs. 4.8 ± 1.8; p < 0.001) were higher in the CIDP group. Alertness RT did not differ significantly between conditions (238.88 ± 97.85 ms vs. 221.04 ± 32.87 ms; p = 0.433).

Correlations Between Fatigue and Clinical Parameters in the CIDP Cohort

Significant positive associations were identified between DESC_I and FSMC cognitive fatigue (ρ = 0.682, 95% CI [0.484, 0.813], p < 0.001) and between DESC_I and FSMC motor fatigue (ρ = 0.659, 95% CI [0.452, 0.799], p < 0.001). Further, FSMC cognitive and motor fatigue strongly correlated with each other (ρ = 0.873, 95% CI [0.779, 0.929], p < 0.001). FSMC motor fatigue showed a strong negative correlation with RODS (ρ = −0.608, 95% CI [−0.767, −0.380], p < 0.001), and a similar negative correlation was found between FSMC cognitive fatigue and RODS (ρ = −0.531, 95% CI [−0.715, −0.278], p < 0.001). A correlation was found between alertness RT and FSMC cognitive (ρ = 0.432, 95% CI [0.142, 0.654], p = 0.02), whereas the correlation between alertness RT and FSMC motor remained insignificant (ρ = 0.362, 95% CI [0.603, 0.060], p = 0.085). All other variables were not significant.

Correlations Between Fatigue and Clinical Markers in the Control Group

FSMC cognitive and motor fatigue scores correlated strongly with each other (ρ = 0.797, 95% CI [0.62, 0.90], p < 0.001) and a correlation between depressiveness and RT was seen (ρ = 0.532, 95% CI [0.77, 0.16], p = 0.035). All other pairwise correlations were not significant.

Predicting Fatigue

In both analyses, the overall models were highly significant (p < 0.001). For FSMC cognitive fatigue, the model explained 66% of the variance. Both depressive symptoms (β = 0.44, p = 0.012) and functional disability (RODS; β = −0.51, p = 0.004) were independent predictors, with higher depressive symptoms associated with greater cognitive fatigue and higher RODS scores (better function) associated with less cognitive fatigue. For FSMC motor fatigue, the model explained 71% of the variance. Again, both depressive symptoms (β = 0.38, p = 0.019) and functional disability (RODS; β = −0.61, p < 0.001) were significant predictors. Higher depressive symptoms were related to greater motor fatigue, whereas higher RODS scores were associated with lower motor fatigue.

Discussion

CIDP is a dysimmune disease of the PNS, but recently subtle cognitive deficits have challenged an exclusively peripheral disease concept [3, 4]. This is of interest with respect to the most recognized NSMS, fatigue, which denotes either decreased motor or cognitive resilience, or a combination of both. Here, we determined these fatigue components in patients and controls and related the findings to further disease features assessed. We found significantly increased total, motor and cognitive fatigue scores in persons with CIDP. This ties in with previous findings of elevated fatigue in CIDP, but extends them by specifying behavioral domains affected by this problem.

In a condition primarily impairing peripheral sensorimotor nerve conduction, the prominent difference between persons with and without CIDP in motor fatigue can be considered an expected finding [4]. Less predictably and not investigated before [5], cognitive fatigue was also significantly higher in patients compared to controls. Different explanations for these findings are conceivable. Principally, motor and cognitive fatigue were found to be correlated with each other both in persons with and without CIDP, so it may be asked whether cognitive and motor fatigue components were distinguished sufficiently. In this regard, two results deserve mention. Firstly, the magnitude of CIDP‐related abnormality was significantly larger for motor than for cognitive fatigue, suggesting that the patients provided a nuanced picture of their situation, reasonably dominated by physical deficits and less by mental dysfunction. Secondly, cognitive, but not motor fatigue correlated with the RTs measured in the alertness task, showing that the components implied differential relations to particular behavioral capacities. Thus, motor and cognitive fatigue appear as separate, yet interconnected phenomena, which do not exclusively refer to one or the other aspect of behavior, in line with the conceptual framework of the FSMC [16]. In this view, the identified association of motor and cognitive fatigue with the R‐ODS could be understood as the feeling of increased efforts necessary to realize any action [24], be it mental or physical, growing with disease progression and, thus, sensorimotor disability, measured by the R‐ODS. In this regard, it also deserves mention that fatigue in both its motor and cognitive components was associated with depressiveness. Given the cross‐sectional design of our study, conclusions about causality within this relationship cannot be drawn. However, a reasonable concept could be that motor fatigue impacted negatively on mood and that consequently increased depressiveness drove cognitive fatigue.

In view of the nature of fatigue in CIDP, both positive and negative results are worthwhile to note. Firstly, although the RT results from the alertness test did not significantly differ between patients and controls, they showed a correlation with the cognitive fatigue levels. Since the RTs imply a sustained attention demand as the average latency of 26 responses, one could interpret that patients accomplished the imposed task as controls, but they did so at increased costs to compensate for reduced capacities to keep the focus on the task. Secondly, the absent correlation with the PSQI as a measure of the subjective sleep quality is remarkable, because different types of sleep disturbances were identified as major contributors to fatigue in other neurological conditions [25, 26]. This missing link can be considered an indication that fatigue in CIDP is not related to tiredness as a consequence of sleep deprivation. However, although the standardized evaluation of sleep by the PSQI is certainly meaningful, subtle contributions from potential sleep alterations could only be ruled out by the use of actigraphic or polysomnographic techniques providing objective data on circadian behaviors. Finally, the absent relation of fatigue to sNfL levels is interesting. sNfL as a biomarker of axonal damage was measured in the patients to gain insights into potential underpinnings of fatigue. In CIDP, it was shown to be elevated, which is not self‐evident in a demyelinating condition [13]. Furthermore, high levels were found associated with low cognitive performance in CIDP patients [11] and, in other neurological conditions, associations between cognitive fatigue and sNfL were found [27]. Against this background, a link between cognitive fatigue and sNfL values could have been expected, e.g., pointing to sequelae of neurodegenerative processes in CIDP, but such an association was not identified. Principally, sNfL elevations are not specific for either peripheral or central nerve damage [28]. Thus, if for the aggregate sNfL the peripheral fraction outweighed the central fraction (as can be reasonably assumed in CIDP) and if these contributions were independent from each other, then cognitive fatigue could still be a sign of axonal CNS damage in CIDP. However, in this scenario a correlation of motor fatigue with the sNfL levels should be present, which was not the case either. Thus, we presume that fatigue in CIDP is rather related to the neuroinflammatory process, mainly characterizing the peripheral disease, than to secondary neurodegenerative alterations.

Several limitations of the study should be considered. Firstly, the used cross‐sectional design only allows the description of symptoms correlated at one point in time. Only longitudinal studies could delineate their development and thus provide information about the directionality and potential causal relationships between associated symptoms. In particular, this holds true for the cluster of fatigue and increased depressiveness, raising the question of whether cognitive fatigue emerges in the context of a low affective state. Although this seems unlikely, given only slightly elevated DESC I value in the patient cohort, a definite answer to this issue could only be provided by further studies with a longitudinal design. Finally, the current investigation did not identify potential mechanisms of abnormal findings in the patient group. Thus, future studies might imply techniques such as functional neuroimaging, network analysis or immunological profiling to gain insights into the pathophysiological underpinnings of fatigue in CIDP.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that fatigue in CIDP goes beyond reduced motor resilience and encompasses a significant cognitive dimension. Increased awareness of this problem and its consequences could be helpful for a more comprehensive assessment of the disease burden and for an optimized biopsychological treatment of patients with CIDP.
