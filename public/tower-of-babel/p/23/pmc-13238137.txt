Optimization of nitrogen removal and microbial communities in biofloc systems via carbon-to-nitrogen ratio regulation and Candida utilis
Authors: Kangtao Cai, Chenxi Lu, Zhengfen Wu, Jinchao Tan, Huayou Chen, Ping Wu, Zhongjian Guo, Yong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiofloc-based systems provide a sustainable strategy for nitrogen management in intensive aquaculture, but their efficiency depends strongly on carbon availability and microbial community regulation. In this study, we evaluated the combined effects of carbon-to-nitrogen (C: N) ratio regulation and Candida utilis supplementation on nitrogen removal and microbial community dynamics in a biofloc-based Chinese soft-shelled turtle culture system. Three C: N ratios (10, 15, and 20) were established using glucose as the carbon source, with or without C. utilis addition. Water quality parameters, feed conversion ratio (FCR), bacterial community composition (16 S rRNA sequencing), and antagonistic activity against Aeromonas hydrophila were analyzed. Increasing the C: N ratio significantly enhanced ammonia nitrogen removal through stimulated heterotrophic assimilation. Supplementation with C. utilis further reduced inorganic nitrogen concentrations across all C: N conditions and improved feed utilization efficiency. Microbial community analyses showed increased bacterial richness and diversity, accompanied by significant shifts in community composition. Notably, opportunistic pathogens such as Aeromonas were markedly suppressed in yeast-supplemented treatments. Co-culture experiments further demonstrated that live C. utilis inhibited A. hydrophila, suggesting a metabolism-dependent antagonistic mechanism. These findings indicate that integrating C: N regulation with C. utilis supplementation can enhance nitrogen transformation efficiency and microbial stability in biofloc systems, providing a promising strategy for sustainable aquaculture management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01133-1.Keywords: Candida utilis, Carbon-nitrogen ratio, Microbial community structure, Chinese soft-shelled turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis), Feed conversion ratio

Introduction

Chinese soft-shelled turtle farming has grown rapidly in East China in recent years. However, it is worth noting that the high-density soft-shelled turtle factory farming has problems such as poor water quality, flooding of pathogenic bacteria, and low breeding efficiency due to excessive feeding and low feed utilization. At the same time, the sewage discharged through the water exchange will also cause environmental pollution. In recent years, the biological floc model has become a popular technology to solve the problem of excessive inorganic nitrogen and frequent water changes in aquaculture [21, 43]. But while improving the water quality by adding carbon sources blindly, unknown bacteria in the water body grow wildly, leading a certain risk to the aquaculture.

The addition of probiotics is common in aquaculture, which can help effectively transform toxic substances [35, 39], purify water quality [30], improve the structure of water environment flora and the intestinal flora structure of aquatic animal [18], improve the immunity of aquatic animal [11], and inhibit harmful bacteria [34]. The added strains include Bacillus, photosynthetic bacteria, yeast, lactic acid bacteria, etc. Hemmati et al., [13], Lara-Flores, [17], Wu, [41]. However, there are few reports on the effect of adding C. utilis in soft-shelled turtle greenhouse culture.

Microorganisms in bioflocs are the determinants of their specific functions, and changes in microbial community structure affect the functions of bioflocs [48]. The types of microorganisms in biological flocs are complex and diverse, and there are both beneficial and harmful microorganisms. If the type and quantity of beneficial microorganisms are adjusted to make them dominate the aquaculture water body, the proportion of harmful microorganisms can be reduced, and the biological flocs can be strengthened. Therefore, it is beneficial to the promotion and application of biological flocs in aquaculture production. The biological flocs formed by adding probiotics can reduce pathogenic bacteria in aquaculture water, promote the growth of beneficial microorganisms, and increase the proportion of beneficial bacteria in the flocs. The objective of this study was to explore the transformation mode and efficiency of inorganic nitrogen in the water body of turtle culture under different treatments, and to explore the microbial community and turtle culture efficiency in the water body.

Materials and methods

Strains and reagents

C. utilis is preserved in our laboratory. The Aeromonas hydrophila CGMCC 1.1801 used in the experiment was purchased from the China General Microorganism Culture Collection and Management Center. ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix and Bacterial Extraction Kits were purchased from Nanjing Vazyme Biotechnology Co., LTD. Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Breeding experiment design

The breeding experiment was carried out in the soft-shelled turtle greenhouse breeding pond of the Institute of Applied Microbiology, Jiangsu University. The breeding pond was 1.57 m long, 1.22 m wide, 0.72 m high, and the water depth was adjusted to 0.5 m. Three parallel groups were set up in each group, and 20 Chinese soft-shelled turtles of similar size and weight were reared in each parallel group. The culture water source was pond water after sedimentation and filtration, and the test period was 25 days. Feed twice a day (12:00, 21:00), The feed was the turtle compound feed of Nanqiang brand from Foshan city, Guangdong Province. The product standard number was Q/LLNX 003-2019, and the nitrogen content of the feed was 44% and the feeding amount is 2% of body weight. Weigh randomly every week, and adjust the feeding amount according to changes in body weight. During the breeding period, according to the principles and formulas researched by [3, 24], the quality of glucose added to control different C: N was calculated. Based on the feed nitrogen content (44% crude protein) and feeding rate (2% body weight), the daily glucose input was: 3.0 kg day⁻¹ (C: N = 10:1), 4.65 kg day⁻¹ (C: N = 15:1), and 6.3 kg day⁻¹ (C: N = 20:1). After feeding the turtle, glucose was added to the culture water of each group. C. utilis supplementation was performed once at the beginning of the experiment, coinciding with the first glucose addition (Day 0). A total of 1.5 L of a 24-h activated C. utilis culture was introduced into each experimental tank. No repeated supplementation was carried out during the 25-day culture period. The experiment followed a full factorial design with two fixed factors: carbon-to-nitrogen (C: N) ratio and Candida utilis supplementation. The C: N ratio was set at three levels (10, 15, and 20), and yeast supplementation included two levels (with and without C. utilis addition). This resulted in a 3 × 2 factorial arrangements comprising six treatment groups (CN10CG, CN10EG, CN15CG, CN15EG, CN20CG, and CN20EG), each with three independent replicates.

Determination of water quality parameters

Take three samples each pond and mix them according to each pond to determine the concentration of total ammonia nitrogen (TN) and nitrite nitrogen (NO2-N) with three times a day (8:00, 16:00, 9:00) in the breeding pond. Measure the concentration of nitrate nitrogen (NO3-N) and total nitrogen (TN) every day. Total nitrogen is determined by alkaline potassium persulfate digestion-ultraviolet spectrophotometer method, and ammonia nitrogen is determined by salicylic acid colorimetric method. The nitrite nitrogen was measured by N-(1-naphthyl)-ethylenediamine spectrophotometry [20]. The temperature and pH were measured by a multifunctional thermometer. The dissolved oxygen was measured by a dissolved oxygen analyzer. Alkalinity is regulated by sodium bicarbonate.

qPCR of water microflora

A total of 5 L was sampled by the three-point method for the water body in the breeding tank. After mixing the sampled water, take 100 mL and centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. Then, 100 mL of water sediment was extracted with Novozan bacterial extraction kit. qPCR was performed on the extracted genes. The specific description of the amplification system is adapted from [46, 47]: 10 µl qPCR master mix, 0.8 µl primers (5 µmol/L), and 2 µl DNA as template. The parameters of the PCR program were set as follows: after pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s were performed, and melting curve analysis was performed after the cycle.

Analysis of water microflora 16s rDNA sequencing

Water samples were taken when the breeding experiment was carried out for 30 days to study microbial community composition. A total of 5 L was sampled by the three-point method. After mixing the sampled water, take 100 mL and centrifuge at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The enriched precipitate was then placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and stored in a -80 °C refrigerator. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using the Water DNA Kit (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA), followed by DNA purity and concentration assessment using a Thermo NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA) and integrity checked by agarose gel electrophoresis [10]. The V3-V4 bacterial variable region of the 16 S rDNA gene was determined using primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT) [14]. Afterwards, the PCR products were purified and their sequence data were analyzed using the Illumina Miseq sequencing platform of Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology (Shanghai, China). Sequence reads were mass-purified prior to sequence analysis; sequences without identifiable reverse primers, less than 150 bp in length, and containing any non-qualitative reads were removed to obtain valid sequences for subsequent analysis. Next, sequences were assigned to operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with a similarity index of 97%. Next, sequences with a similarity index of 97% were assigned to the same operational taxonomic unit (OTU) based on the bacterial SILVA database [5, 19]. The Shannon and Simpson indices were used for diversity analysis, and the Ace and Chao indices were used for abundance analysis [51]. Community analysis and species Venn diagram, PCoA statistical analysis and mapping, ANOSIM analysis, and RDA analysis were performed using R language tools. Species composition was analyzed by drawing ternary phase diagrams. The sample hierarchical clustering uses QIIME to calculate the diversity distance matrix, and then uses the R language to make a picture tree. FAPROTAX function prediction was performed using the FAPROTAX manual database. All of the above analyses used the R statistical package (Majorbio I-Sanger Cloud Platform, www.i-sanger.com) to analyze the data.

Liquid antibacterial experiment of C. utilis

Add 1 ml of activated A. hydrophila to 100mL of medium in each group, and set three groups: A. hydrophila alone, A. hydrophila and 1 ml of C. utilis (activated), A. hydrophila and 1 ml of C. utilis (frozen inactivated after boiling), inserted and shaken at 30 °C and cultured at 150 rpm with three repetitions in each group. After 12 h, the culture medium was taken for PMA treatment, and then the gene was extracted for detection.

C. utilis plate inhibition zone experiment

Spread the activated A. hydrophila evenly on the solid medium plate, place a small round filter paper on top, and drop four groups of different liquids (10 µL) on the filter paper: culture medium, antibiotics, live C. utilis, dead C. utilis. The size of the inhibition zone was observed after culturing in a constant temperature incubator at 30 °C for 24 h.

Data processing and analysis

The data were processed by Oringin 2018 and SPSS 26, showing data ± SD. Comparisons of multiple groups of data were performed by LSD Duncan of ANOVA [33].

Results and discussion

Removal efficiency of C. utilis on ammonia nitrogen

High-density greenhouse aquaculture, even with optimized feeding protocols for Chinese soft-shelled turtles, frequently encounters severe water quality deterioration, marked by the rapid accumulation of ammonia nitrogen (NH4+-N) and nitrite nitrogen (NO2−-N) (Figure S1). This mirrors findings in intensive aquaculture systems where high stocking densities invariably lead to toxic nitrogenous compound buildup, negatively impacting cultured organisms and overall system health [36]. Conventionally, mitigating these accumulations requires routine water exchange [44]. However, this essential management strategy invariably results in the discharge of nitrogen-rich effluents, thereby increasing the environmental burden on surrounding water bodies.

To reduce the reliance on frequent water exchange while simultaneously maintaining water quality and minimizing environmental emissions, we evaluated two distinct management approaches. The first strategy involved manipulating the Carbon-to-Nitrogen (C: N) ratio through the direct addition of glucose. The second strategy utilized C. utilis, a microorganism previously demonstrated to assimilate inorganic nitrogen as its sole nitrogen source [26], to facilitate the assimilation and reduction of inorganic nitrogen within the aquaculture water. Results from the ten-day co-culture experiment demonstrated that supplementing the medium with a high concentration of glucose (C: N ratio of 20 or 15) significantly reduced ammonia nitrogen concentrations, which were maintained at low levels throughout the monitoring period (Fig. 1a-c). In contrast, at the intermediate C: N ratio of 10, the reduction in ammonia nitrogen was not significant. These results indicate that denitrification efficiency in aquaculture water varies with C: N ratio, and higher carbon availability corresponded to faster ammonia reduction. Previous studies have reported that elevated C: N ratios promote heterotrophic assimilation of inorganic nitrogen and enhance denitrification performance in aquaculture systems and wastewater treatment [7], consistent with our observations.

Changes in total ammonia nitrogen concentrations in six aquaculture water bodies under different treatments. Three carbon-to-nitrogen (C: N) ratios were applied: 20 (a), 15 (b), and 10 (c). For each C: N ratio, the control group (CG) received no C. utilis supplementation, whereas the experimental group (EG) was supplemented with 1‰ C. utilis culture. CN20CG and CN20EG denote the control and experimental groups at a C: N ratio of 20, respectively; the same nomenclature applies to the other treatments

With the addition of C. utilis, ammonia nitrogen was significantly reduced across all tested C/N ratios, including the lowest ratio (C: N = 10). In all cases, the experimental groups exhibited significantly lower ammonia nitrogen concentrations compared with the corresponding control groups without C. utilis supplementation (Fig. 1a-c). These results indicated that C. utilis can assimilate inorganic nitrogen and contribute to water quality regulation under a range of carbon-nitrogen conditions.

Determination of various nitrogen content in water body

As shown in the Fig. 2, it can be seen that the total nitrogen in the control group with different carbon-nitrogen ratios is less than that in the experimental group(P<0.05), while the difference in inorganic nitrogen is not very large compared to the total nitrogen, indicating that the content of organic nitrogen is higher in the experimental group. It was also proved that in the group supplemented with C. utilis, inorganic nitrogen was efficiently converted into microbe protein, and the content of biofloc increased. Relevant studies have also shown that the dominant microorganisms in the structure of the floc flora can affect the nutrient composition of the floc [16]. Moreover, artificially adding strains to the biofloc culture mode can accelerate the formation of bioflocs and water purification, and increase the crude protein content of bioflocs [12]. This is consistent with the results of this study.

Feed conversion ratio (FCR) of soft-shelled turtle

To investigate the effects of different C/N ratios and the addition of C. utilis on feed conversion ratio (FCR), growth performance of Chinese soft-shelled turtles was evaluated under biofloc-based culture conditions. As shown in Fig. 3, FCR decreased with increasing C/N ratio, and values in the experimental groups were consistently lower than those in the corresponding control groups (P < 0.05). This trend is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that biofloc-based aquaculture systems can improve growth performance and feed utilization in aquatic animals [1, 28, 43, 45]. In particular, Chen et al., [9] reported enhanced survival and growth of soft-shelled turtles following carbon source addition [9], which aligns with the reduced FCR observed at higher C/N ratios in the present study.

FCR of Chinese soft-shelled turtle. The data were processed by SPSS, showing data ± SD. Same line of data marked with different letters indicate significant difference (P < 0.05)

The improved feed efficiency may be attributed to the reduction of toxic inorganic nitrogen compounds, such as ammonia and nitrite, resulting in improved water quality and reduced environmental stress. In addition, carbon supplementation has been shown to reshape microbial community structure and enhance nonspecific immunity in soft-shelled turtles [9]. Notably, the addition of C. utilis further reduced FCR under medium and high C/N conditions (P < 0.05). This effect may be associated with the colonization of C. utilis in the culture water and its potential influence on the intestinal microbiota, including suppression of opportunistic and pathogenic bacteria.

Previous studies have demonstrated that a stable gut microbial community is essential for nutrient digestion, immune regulation, and overall growth performance in aquatic animals [31]. Yeast supplementation provides bioactive components such as polysaccharides, B vitamins, digestive enzymes, and growth-promoting factors, which can enhance digestive enzyme activity, intestinal morphology, and nutrient absorption efficiency [15, 50], [6, 32]. Therefore, the presence of C. utilis in the biofloc system likely contributed to improved feed utilization, resulting in a lower FCR in the experimental groups.

In addition to nitrogen removal efficiency, the economic feasibility of carbon supplementation is an important consideration for practical application. In the present study, industrial-grade glucose (approximately 1500 RMB per ton, ≈ 208 USD per ton) was used as a standardized carbon source to ensure experimental consistency. Under the tested conditions, the estimated carbon input cost ranged from approximately 15–33 USD per treatment over the 25-day culture period, depending on the C: N ratio. Although higher C: N ratios required increased carbon input, it is important to evaluate these costs in the context of overall system performance. Improved nitrogen assimilation reduced inorganic nitrogen accumulation and enhanced feed conversion ratio (FCR), which directly influences feed expenditure—the primary operational cost in intensive aquaculture systems. Therefore, part of the additional carbon cost may be offset by improved nutrient utilization efficiency and reduced water exchange requirements. Moreover, in practical production systems, lower-cost carbon sources such as molasses or agricultural by-products are commonly used in biofloc technology, which would further reduce operational expenses. Notably, our results showed that C. utilis supplementation enhanced nitrogen removal even under relatively low C: N conditions, suggesting that yeast inoculation may reduce the need for excessive external carbon input. Collectively, these findings indicate that optimized C: N regulation combined with microbial supplementation can achieve a balance between nitrogen control efficiency and economic sustainability in biofloc-based aquaculture.

Effects of C: N ratios and C. utilis addition on microbial alpha diversity

To elucidate the microbial mechanisms underlying nitrogen transformation in response to C: N regulation and C. utilis supplementation, microbial community profiling [2] was performed. six groups of water samples were subjected to 16 S rRNA high-throughput sequencing, generating a total of 339 Mb of high-quality data. Following quality filtering and dereplication, bacterial sequences were clustered into 828 OTUs at 97% sequence similarity (Figure S2). Rarefaction curves based on the Sobs index were constructed to evaluate species richness and sequencing depth, with plateauing curves indicating sufficient sequencing depth. As shown in the Figure S2, rarefaction curves of all six groups approached a stable plateau, demonstrating adequate sampling and sufficient sequencing depth to reliably characterize the bacterial community structure in each treatment.

To assess the effects of C: N ratios and C. utilis addition on microbial alpha diversity, Chao, Ace, Shannon, Simpson, and Goods coverage were calculated for all samples at a 97% similarity threshold (Fig. 4). Differences in species diversity were observed between the experimental and control groups across the different C: N ratios. Both the Chao and Ace indices increased with higher C: N ratios in both groups (Fig. 4b, d), indicating enhanced microbial richness with higher C: N ratios. Comparisons between the control and experimental groups at each C: N ratio level further revealed that the addition of C. utilis enhanced bacterial abundance, particularly at low and medium C: N ratios, while the effect was less pronounced at the highest C: N ratio. The Simpson index revealed that species diversity generally increased with rising C: N ratio in both groups; however, at C: N ratios of 15 and 20, the experimental groups exhibited higher diversity than the controls (Fig. 4a, c). These findings demonstrate that both increasing the C: N ratio and adding C. utilis can promote microbial richness and diversity in biofloc-based aquaculture systems, with the most pronounced effects observed at moderate C: N levels.

Alpha diversity index. (a) Sobs index; (b) Chao index; (c) Simpson index; (d) Ace index. Note: The data were processed by SPSS, showing data ± SD. Data marked with different letters indicate significant difference (P < 0.05)

Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity to evaluate species-level community composition [20, 49]. Species composition analysis showed that 213 OTUs were shared among all six treatments, forming a core bacterial community (Figure S4a). Overall, C. utilis supplemented groups exhibited a higher number of shared OTUs (314) compared with the control groups (255) (Figure S4b, c), indicating that yeast addition enhanced bacterial diversity primarily by enriching additional taxa rather than introducing entirely novel lineages. When analyzed according to C: N ratio, 255 OTUs were common across all control treatments, while the high C: N condition contained 112 unique OTUs (Figure S4d). Furthermore, the C: N 15 and C: N 20 treatments shared 120 OTUs beyond the core set (Figure S4e, f), suggesting that increasing C: N ratio promotes the proliferation of heterotrophic bacteria. Collectively, these results indicate that both C: N regulation and C. utilis supplementation contribute to community diversification, with the most pronounced increase in unique OTUs observed under higher C: N conditions.

Analysis of the distribution characteristics of bacterial communities

To investigate the effects of the C: N ratio and C. utilis addition on bacterial community structure, the distribution patterns of bacterial communities were analyzed. At the phylum level, the dominant taxa across all six groups were Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Patescibacteria, Myxococcota, Fusobacteriota, and Chloroflexi (Fig. 5a). In both control and experimental groups, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria increased with increasing C: N ratio. Conversely, Actinobacteriota decreased as the C: N ratio increased, and its abundance was consistently lower in the experimental groups than in the controls at all C: N levels. At low C: N ratios, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes were significantly enriched in the experimental group, whereas in the control group, increasing the C: N ratio promoted the enrichment of Firmicutes. Patescibacteria showed higher relative abundance in the experimental groups at C: N ratios of 15 and 20, suggesting a positive association with C. utilis addition. In contrast, Myxococcota increased with C: N ratio in the control groups but remained consistently lower in the experimental groups, indicating that C. utilis addition may suppress this taxon. Acidobacteriota decreased with increasing C: N ratio but remained more abundant in the experimental groups, implying that C. utilis promoted its persistence. Other phyla, including Fusobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Deinococcota, and Desulfobacterota, exhibited C: N-dependent shifts, with generally lower abundances in the experimental groups.

At the genus level, Klebsiella was detected mainly at high C: N ratios and was absent in the experimental group at medium C: N ratio, indicating inhibitory effects of C. utilis(Fig. 5b). The abundance of Micropruina declined with increasing C: N ratio and was lower in the experimental group at low C: N ratio. Genera such as Saccharimonadales and Ramlibacter were enriched at medium and high C: N ratios, while their abundance was reduced by C. utilis addition. The relative abundance of Aeromonas increased markedly with increasing C: N ratio; however, it was consistently and significantly lower in the experimental groups, demonstrating that C. utilis effectively suppressed Aeromonas proliferation.

Overall, Proteobacteria dominated all treatments (Fig. 5b), consistent with their functional roles as heterotrophic bacteria mediating organic matter degradation and denitrification [27]. The elevated abundance of Proteobacteria at higher C: N ratios was accompanied by enhanced ammonia nitrogen removal. Actinobacteriota and Firmicutes, which contribute to biofloc formation and flocculation [37, 42], [29], were less abundant in the experimental groups, likely due to competition and niche occupation by C. utilis. Together, these results indicate that adjusting the C: N ratio and inoculating C. utilis reshaped the bacterial community composition, promoted beneficial heterotrophic processes, and inhibited potential pathogens such as Aeromonas, thus improving water quality in the biofloc system.

Genus-level composition (Figure S5) showed clear C: N-dependent shifts. Actinobacteriota dominated at low C: N in both control and experimental groups, while increasing C: N promoted Proteobacteria dominance, consistent with previous findings that higher C: N favors heterotrophic floc-associated bacteria [4]. In control groups, Haliangium (Myxococcota) increased with C: N and peaked at C: N = 15, whereas specific Bacteroidetes taxa (VC2.1 Bac22 at C: N = 15 and Runella at C: N = 20) were enriched at higher C: N levels. Distinct Proteobacterial genera were associated with different C: N conditions, including Ancylobacter (low), Rhodobacteraceae (medium), and Ramlibacter and Klebsiella (high). In experimental groups, Actinobacteriota decreased with increasing C: N, and Patescibacteria appeared only at medium and high C: N. Compared with controls, C. utilis supplementation reduced the relative abundance of dominant Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria. Together with the higher organic nitrogen content observed in experimental treatments, these results suggest that yeast-derived biomass contributed substantially to biofloc composition, consistent with previous reports on protein accumulation in C. utilis [22, 25].

Analyses effects of C: N ratios and C. utilis addition on microbial beta diversity

To investigate the impact of C: N ratio and C. utilis addition on bacterial communities’ structure, comparative analysis of sample beta diversity was conducted. Hierarchical Clustering Analysis (HCA) at the genus level revealed distinct clustering patterns across the samples. At a low C: N ratio, the control and experimental groups clustered closely together, indicating high similarity in their bacterial community structures (Fig. 6a). The experimental group at the medium C: N ratio formed a separate cluster. In contrast, the high C: N ratio groups (both control and experimental) along with the control group at the medium C: N ratio aggregated into a third major cluster, with the highest similarity observed between the two high C: N ratio groups (Fig. 6a). This clustering suggests that within each C: N treatment level, samples exhibited similar community compositions, whereas significant structural differences existed between different C: N treatments. Overall, increasing the C: N ratio appeared to lead to a more complex bacterial community composition, concurrently driving the experimental and control groups toward greater structural similarity. While the addition of C. utilis altered the bacterial community structure, this homogenization effect (where experimental and control groups became more similar) was also evident at high C: N ratios (e.g., C: N = 20).

Beta diversity analysis. (a) Hierarchical clustering tree on genus level. (b) PcoA. (c) ANOSIM analysis of each group data

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) [40] indicated that the first two axes (PC1 and PC2) explained 48.51% and 20.58% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 6b). Samples generally clustered according to their respective treatment groups. At the low C: N ratio, the control and experimental groups formed distinct, non-overlapping clusters, confirming significant compositional differences. For the medium C: N ratio, the control and experimental samples were widely separated, indicating marked structural divergence. At the high C: N ratio, while the confidence ellipses overlapped, the sample points remained closely spaced (Fig. 6b), suggesting that the addition of C. utilis caused some structural differences between the groups, yet a degree of overall similarity persisted. ANOSIM further confirmed significant structural differentiation among the various grouping factors (Fig. 6c), results which were consistent with the Alpha diversity metrics. These analyses collectively indicate that both the varying C: N ratios and the introduction of C. utilis induced substantial shifts in the bacterial community structure.

Water physicochemical parameters are important environmental factors for microbial community structure in water [23, 42], The correlation analysis between the genus-level classification of bacterial communities in the six groups of water bodies and the nitrogen factors in the environment showed that TN was the most influential. At the level of carbon-nitrogen ratio of 10, the experimental group and control group showed a positive correlation with ammonia nitrogen, nitrite nitrogen, and nitrate nitrogen; when the carbon-nitrogen ratio was 15 and 20, the experimental group and control group were positively correlated with total nitrogen (Figure S6).

FAPROTAX function prediction

FAPROTAX is an artificially constructed database that assigns prokaryotic taxa (e.g., genus or species level) to associated metabolic or ecologically relevant functions (e.g., nitrification, denitrification). The predicted functions within FAPROTAX primarily focus on marine and lake biogeochemical cycles, specifically involving sulfur, nitrogen, hydrogen, and carbon. As is showed, functional prediction analysis revealed distinct patterns related to nitrogen metabolism based on the C: N ratios. At low C: N ratios, the group exhibited higher predicted capacity for nitrate utilization and nitrogen fixation (Fig. 7b, d), while the nitrate reduction function was notably suppressed in the experimental group (Fig. 7a) at a C: N ratio of 15 - being significantly lower than its control counterpart. Conversely, the nitrate ammonification function was more pronounced in groups subjected to high C: N ratios (Fig. 7c). Furthermore, the nitrite respiration function was more pronounced in the high C: N ratio experimental group. Regarding overall nitrate denitrification, the experimental group showed lower activity than the control group at the low C: N ratio level (Fig. 7f). However, at medium and high C: N ratios, the experimental group exhibited slightly greater, yet non-significantly different, nitrate denitrification activity compared to the control. Overall, the predictive trend suggests that nitrate denitrification potential generally increased with rising C: N ratios. Concerning functions related to animal parasites and symbionts, the results indicated that the control groups at both medium and high C: N ratios exhibited significantly higher activity than their respective experimental groups (Figure S3). This observation demonstrated that the addition of C. utilis reduced the abundance and proportion of key pathogenic/commensal taxa such as Aeromonas and Enterobacteriaceae.

Inhibition of A. hydrophila by C. utilis

To further validate the inhibitory effect of C. utilis on the growth of A. hydrophila, co-culture antibacterial experiments were conducted using both liquid medium and solid agar plates. After applying A. hydrophila on the plate, 1 milliliter of antibiotics, inactivated C. utilis, live bacteria C. utilis and sterile culture medium were respectively added to four filter papers a, b, c and d. And compared with each other to observe the resistance effect of live bacteria C on Aeromonas hydrophila. The results demonstrated that live C. utilis significantly inhibited the growth of A. hydrophila (Fig. 8a), whereas the inactivated yeast cells exhibited no inhibitory effect. To further validate whether C. utilis could inhibit the growth of A. hydrophila on solid media, a plate inhibition zone experiment was conducted. The results showed that live C. utilis was capable of growing on the agar plate overlaid with A. hydrophila, extending beyond the boundaries of the filter paper disc (Fig. 8b). Crucially, only a small, localized zone of inhibition was observed exactly adjacent to the live yeast colony (significantly smaller than zones produced by standard antibiotics).

(b) plate inhibition zone experiment, A: antibiotics, B: inactivated C. utilis, C: live C. utilis, D: sterile medium. Note: The data were processed by SPSS, showing data ± SD. Data marked with different letters indicate significant difference (P < 0.05)

Following co-cultivation of C. utilis and A. hydrophila in the liquid medium, the viable cell quantity of A. hydrophila was quantified using Propidium Monoazide quantitative PCR (PMA-qPCR) [8]. PMA is a type of fuel that can bind to the DNA of dead bacteria, preventing them from performing qPCR. By comparing with PMA-qPCR, it can be seen that if live C. utilis is added to the liquid culture medium of Aeromonas hydrophila, the number of live bacteria will decrease, while inactivated C. utilis cannot. However, inactivated C. utilis exhibited no inhibitory effect on A. hydrophila. This strongly suggests that the inhibition exerted by the live yeast is likely due to active metabolic processes, potentially through growth competition or the secretion of inhibitory compounds. This active mechanism might involve yeast glycoproteins. Consistent with this finding, previous studies demonstrated that yeast glycoproteins significantly inhibited a multidrug-resistant Aeromonas strain both in vitro and in vivo [38], mirroring the results observed in this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that regulating the C: N ratio and supplementing C. utilis can effectively improve water quality, microbial community structure, and feed utilization efficiency in a biofloc-based Chinese soft-shelled turtle culture system. Increasing the C: N ratio promoted ammonia nitrogen removal, while C. utilis addition further enhanced inorganic nitrogen assimilation, particularly under low C: N conditions. Microbial community analysis revealed that both higher C: N ratios and yeast supplementation increased bacterial richness and diversity and reshaped community composition, with a notable reduction in the abundance of opportunistic pathogens such as Aeromonas. In addition, the experimental groups consistently exhibited lower feed conversion ratios, indicating improved nutrient utilization and growth performance. Overall, the combined application of C: N ratio optimization and C. utilis supplementation offers a practical and environmentally friendly strategy for improving biofloc-based aquaculture. This approach has potential value for reducing nitrogen discharge, enhancing culture stability, and promoting sustainable production in intensive soft-shelled turtle farming systems.
