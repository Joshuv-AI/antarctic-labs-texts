NIH-FDA Nutrition Regulatory Science Workshop: advancing research and policy
Authors: Andrew A Bremer, Samantha Adas, Alison GM Brown, Kellie O Casavale, Steven Hermansky, Kirsten Herrick, Claudine J Kavanaugh, Holly L Nicastro, Sheri D Schully, Jill Reedy, Ashley J Vargas, Krista A Zanetti, Robin A McKinnon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNutrition plays a pivotal role in health, yet poor dietary habits are now the leading risk factor for illness and death. In the United States, diet-related conditions such as heart disease, cancer, diabetes, and obesity cause >1 million deaths per year and are leading contributors to the nation’s $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending.ObjectivesAddressing these issues requires coordinated efforts across multiple sectors and government agencies. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) are essential partners in this endeavor. A strong foundation of scientific research, coupled with effective, practical, and real-world regulatory activities, is critical to inform consumers and support a healthier food environment.MethodsIn December 2024, NIH and FDA convened a workshop to advance nutrition science for food-related policy decision making. A diverse group of researchers, policymakers, and other experts participated in the event to identify research gaps, explore strategies for enhanced NIH-FDA collaboration, and highlight emerging technologies in nutrition science, with a particular focus on the topic of ultra-processed foods.ResultsThe workshop was designed to do the following: 1) identify critical research gaps and priorities in nutrition regulatory science; 2) enhance scientific collaboration between NIH and FDA; 3) explore how scientific evidence informs food-related policies and regulations; and 4) highlight emerging technologies and data resource needs for nutrition research.ConclusionThis article summarizes the workshop proceedings and describes priority research gaps, opportunities, and suggested next steps that emerged from the discussions.Keywords: nutrition, health, dietary habits, food environment, food regulation

Introduction

Proper nutrition is foundational to optimal health. However, diet-related chronic diseases—such as heart disease, cancer, diabetes, and obesity—are the leading causes of disability and death in the United States, and leading drivers of the nation’s $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending [1]. Improving the American diet—and health—requires coordinated efforts across society and support from government agencies. Many federal agencies are critical for this effort, including the United States NIH, Food and Drug Administration (FDA), USDA, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Robust science coupled with regulatory and policy initiatives can provide consumers with vital information for themselves and their families and also inform and move the food industry to offer healthier options.

The NIH investment in nutrition science spans across nearly all Institutes and Centers at the NIH, funding scientists to better understand how nutrition relates to health and disease [2]. This investment in research generates evidence to inform FDA policy decisions. Examples include those related to: 1) the removal of artificial trans fats from the United States food supply; 2) the determination of how excess sodium intake is linked to increased risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease, which led to the implementation of voluntary targets for the food industry to lower the sodium content in their products; and 3) food labeling requirements, including the requirement to declare added sugars on the Nutrition Facts label. Furthermore, since 2010, an NIH-FDA joint leadership council has worked to help ensure that regulatory considerations are integrated into planning for biomedical research and that current science is incorporated into regulatory decision making. This partnership between FDA and NIH, and engagement with other federal partners, is critically important to ensure that regulators have access to relevant scientific evidence to inform regulatory decisions, and to monitor the impact of policies on population health in collaboration with others.

The NIH and the FDA convened a workshop on 17–18 December 2024 to highlight how nutrition science can generate evidence and data to inform food-related policy and regulatory decision making. The workshop also served to foster additional dialog and collaboration between NIH and FDA in supporting research that addresses priority nutrition research gaps [3]. The workshop gathered researchers, policymakers, and others to review the state of the science, critical research gaps, and potential strategies to further enhance collaboration between NIH and FDA that could inform nutrition policymaking and mitigate the chronic diet-related disease crisis in the United States.

The workshop consisted of 3 main sessions: 1) Session 1 set the stage for the workshop by discussing how science can inform policy decision making and the regulatory process; 2) Session 2 identified key research gaps and opportunities in nutrition science to inform regulatory policy and decision making; and 3) Session 3 focused on research opportunities, resources, technologies, and key priorities moving forward.

Session 1: Setting the Stage—FDA Responsibilities, NIH Research, and Science-Based Regulatory Policy

At the outset, presenters outlined the complementary roles and charges of relevant federal agencies: the FDA regulates the safety and labeling of the vast majority (∼80%) of food consumed by Americans; the USDA regulates primarily meat, poultry, and processed egg products (∼20%); and the NIH, the largest funder of biomedical research in the world, supports robust science that is foundational to inform regulatory decision making. Both FDA and NIH also collaborate within a larger federal ecosystem of United States nutrition-related science and policy agencies, including the CDC, USDA, and others. The term “FDA regulatory science” was defined as a field of scientific research focused on developing the tools, standards, and approaches needed to generate the data and information to support FDA regulatory decision making [4]. Speakers noted the challenges of regulatory decision making, such as the need for robust science, practicality in implementation, and the careful consideration of any potential unintended consequences.

The NIH-FDA Tobacco Regulatory Science Program

To help set the stage, speakers highlighted the NIH-FDA Tobacco Regulatory Science Program (TRSP) as a model program for regulatory science. The Tobacco Control Act of 2009 granted the FDA the authority to regulate tobacco products. Since its inception, the Center for Tobacco Products (CTP) has focused its research priorities on efforts to improve public health and has established partnerships with other federal agencies. Subsequently, in 2013, the NIH-FDA TRSP was established, which serves as a central hub for NIH-FDA research collaboration—guiding research priorities, issuing funding opportunities, and managing grants [5]. To help bridge the gap between research and policy, considerable efforts have been made to educate program staff, scientists, and grant reviewers about distinct agency goals and priorities. Furthermore, the review process for tobacco regulatory science ensures applications are evaluated rigorously using NIH peer-review criteria. From 2013 to 2024, the TRSP has funded >450 research projects that have generated thousands of scientific publications, many of which are referenced in FDA CTP regulatory documents. The TRSP’s success stems from dedicated resources, strong leadership support, regular evaluation of impact, and a team of knowledgeable staff that fosters collaboration across agencies.

Case study: supporting science and regulatory processes to remove partially hydrogenated oils from the United States food supply

Presenters noted that a substantial body of scientific research over many years demonstrated the adverse health effects of trans fatty acids (TFAs). These substances are linked to increased LDL-cholesterol levels, reduced HDL-cholesterol levels, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, higher risks of coronary artery disease, and all-cause mortality [6,7]. Decades of NIH-funded studies, including clinical trials and observational research, generated critical evidence that led to subsequent dietary recommendations to minimize TFA intake and the mandatory labeling of trans fats on the Nutrition Facts label from 2006, resulting in a ∼80% decrease in TFA intake [8]. As evidence continued to grow about the adverse health effects of artificial trans fats [partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs)], the FDA issued a final determination that PHOs were no longer “generally recognized as safe” in 2015, and PHOs have now essentially been removed from the United States food supply, likely averting thousands of cases of coronary artery disease annually [9]. This regulatory shift, driven by extensive scientific evidence and expert consensus, represents a major public health achievement in reducing cardiovascular disease risks. It also demonstrates how NIH-supported science can generate the evidence and data needed to inform FDA regulatory decision making.

Session 2: Key Research Gaps and Opportunities in Nutrition Science to Inform Regulatory Policy and Decision Making

Ultraprocessed foods

The gaps in knowledge regarding ultraprocessed foods (UPFs) present both scientific opportunities and regulatory challenges. Presenters noted the consistent association between the intake of foods considered ultraprocessed and negative health outcomes while also noting methodological challenges as well as opportunities.

UPFs are often identified by researchers using the Nova classification system, which categorizes foods based on the extent and purpose of the industrial processing they undergo. Foods classified as UPFs using Nova have formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use, such as food additives including colors, flavors, and sweeteners to enhance the taste, palatability, and/or convenience of a product [10]. Several workshop presenters noted that the function of substances added to foods, or a food’s formulation, is a better determinant of whether the food is ultraprocessed compared with the food processing technique(s) alone. Using this classification system, ≥60% of the United States food supply would be categorized as UPFs [11].

Presenters noted ongoing research gaps and limitations related to understanding the health effects of UPFs. Challenges included concerns about an overly inclusive definition of UPFs, highlighting that certain foods that may be categorized as UPF are independently associated with reduced risk of disease, such as whole grain bread and yogurt [12], and some foods considered ultraprocessed are also essential for certain populations, for example, infant formula. Other participants voiced concerns such as the potential for unintended consequences of an overly inclusive definition that could increase food insecurity if measures were taken to limit availability of foods meeting UPF criteria. Because of the growing concerns around UPFs, FDA, the United States Department of Health and Human Services, and USDA issued a Request for Information in July 2025 to gather information and data to help establish a federally recognized uniform definition for UPFs [13]. Presenters also noted that it can be challenging to discern formulation and levels of processing given the lack of specificity gathered through dietary assessment tools, and limited details of ingredients or additives in standard nutrient databases. Therefore, identifying a more specific UPF definition to be used in data analysis could advance the field. Given that food processing can serve essential functions such as those related to food preservation and safety, quality, and diversification, policymakers should balance public health concerns about the potential negative health impacts of processed food with the need to maintain a safe, accessible, and sustainable food supply.

In addition, presenters highlighted priorities and potential paths forward to strengthen research in this area. The majority of research on UPFs is based on observational studies and one highly cited randomized control trial [14]. Intriguing new preliminary results of an ongoing feeding trial were presented indicating differential outcomes associated with 3 different UPF diets—varying by hyperpalatability and nonbeverage energy density (i.e., the appeal and calorie content of the UPFs themselves)—compared with a minimally processed diet. Preliminary results presented indicated that UPF diets rated high in hyperpalatability and nonbeverage energy density resulted in increased caloric intake and weight gain among participants—similar results to earlier controlled-feeding studies. However, UPFs low in both hyperpalatability and nonbeverage energy density showed similar caloric intake and weight reduction to that of the minimally processed diet (albeit with increased loss of lean muscle mass), suggesting that a narrower definition of UPF may be warranted and possible.

Suggested future research opportunities identified by the presenters include: 1) improving the quality of dietary assessment in large epidemiological studies; 2) continuing an emphasis on domiciled feeding trials; and 3) improving the composition of dietary databases to enhance details on ingredients and additives. Currently, the high degree of overlap of foods considered ultraprocessed with foods high in added sugars, sodium, and saturated fat present methodological challenges for researchers. Therefore, the presenters suggested a dual strategy that entails maintaining focused efforts on foods high in those nutrients of concern while continuing to conduct and refine investigations on UPFs to provide more granular evidence-based policy recommendations. They further emphasized that consumer education is also crucial and cautioned that misclassification of foods and overly simplistic guidelines may lead to confusion and unintended consequences, such as reduced access to affordable and nutritious processed foods.

Methodologies for incorporating nutrition science into risk analysis in food policy

Food safety is an essential area of regulatory science. The benefits of nutrients for life, growth, and health must be balanced with the potential risks associated with exposure to food-borne pathogens and other environmental contaminants, including heavy metals/metalloids such as lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic. Air, water, and soil are common sources of these contaminants; thus, nutrient-dense foods (e.g., produce, grains, and seafood) sourced from these environments are focus areas for chemical and microbial food safety policies and regulations [15,16]. Importantly, analyzing risk is often a critical part of developing related policies and regulatory actions.

Presenters noted that risk analysis in food policy is a systematic 3-tier process of assessing, managing, and communicating about potential chemical or microbial risks from food exposures on human health [17]. Risk benefit analysis is a methodology that maintains the 3-tier framework of risk analysis and goes a step further to include parallel analyses of nutrition science [18]. Presenters provided background and examples of qualitative, semiquantitative, and quantitative risk benefit assessments methodologies and models [[19], [20], [21]]. Programs in the United States like the NIH Virtual Consortium for Transdisciplinary Environmental Research and the Complement Animal Research in Experimentation program include studies that can contribute to the evidence base informing models for more holistically evaluating the potential risks of consuming or not consuming certain foods and diets.

Scientific priorities for future research identified by presenters included research that combines methods, resources, and perspectives from nutrition and toxicology, including: 1) estimation of the ratios of nutrients to heavy metals and how those relate to internal biomarkers of exposure and health endpoints; 2) bioavailability of contaminants in foods and how nutritional deficiencies may impact bioavailability; 3) moderation effects of how nutrients in food impact the influence of heavy metals on child health; 4) developing dose response models for emerging hazards and nutrients, and characterizing uncertainty; 5) inclusion of contaminants to nutrient food composition databases for populations studies; 6) exposure assessment studies of dietary intake and biomarkers of contaminant and nutrient status; and 7) mechanisms of action of foods, nutrients, and dietary patterns in modulating potential health impacts of contaminants. Furthermore, presenters underscored the importance of prioritizing New Approach Methodologies to better model biological systems. Finally, presenters noted that enhancing collaboration among interdisciplinary teams, including experts in exposure assessments such as nutrition and epidemiology to complement the traditional inclusion of toxicologists will be key to advancing holistic approaches and to inform food policy decision making.

Impact analysis and implementation science related to nutrition regulatory actions

Impact analysis and implementation science are key areas of need in nutrition policy research. Economic regulatory impact analyses are required as a formal part of rulemaking and typically estimate costs and benefits to society [22]. In many areas, such as food labeling, social scientists may also conduct qualitative research to better understand consumers’ knowledge and interpretation of information provided to inform potential regulatory actions.

Various research study designs can be leveraged to inform nutrition policymaking. These can include evaluations of policies at the state or local level that could then inform potential federal actions, and post implementation studies to evaluate the effect of the policies, barriers to implementation, and any potential unintended consequences. Presenters emphasized that implementation challenges can prevent a program from achieving the intended public health benefits. One speaker discussed evaluating global policy trends related to UPFs, particularly actions that have been taken in Latin America, that could inform potential approaches in the United States [23]. Study designs and methods can vary greatly and can include evaluation of natural experiments, experimental studies, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-difference methods and instrumental variables approaches. In assessing nutrition labels and information, various methods exist to assess the value of the information provided in monetary terms; for example, value of information and willingness-to-pay methods. Studies can also focus on responses and impact on consumers and food industry practices.

Session 3: Looking Ahead—Research Opportunities, Resources, Technologies, and Key Priorities

Current databases and other research resources to inform nutrition regulatory science

A number of data and research resources can play a critical role informing nutrition regulatory science through providing food purchasing and nutrient and ingredient data. Several commercial data resources provide household-level and point-of-sale data, whereas others provide food labeling and packaging data, including the Nutrition Facts label information. This information can be supplemented by data collected from in-store retail systems that capture real-time detailed information about sales transactions, inventory levels, and consumer purchasing behavior. Integrating information from multiple databases facilitates dynamic monitoring of changes in the food supply and consumer purchasing behaviors. Analyzing and integrating these datasets is complex and time-consuming, involving collection, cleaning, matching, and evaluation. Another resource is final summary statistics, numerical data that enable researchers to evaluate policies and track changes in the food supply, household purchases, and regulatory compliance, among other trends.

The CDC-managed NHANES is a critical resource to assess the health and nutritional status of the United States population [24]. NHANES collects comprehensive data via interviews, examinations, and laboratory tests to assess the health of adults and children in the United States. Through a multistage sampling process, NHANES collects data on a wide variety of health-related topics including dietary intake, dietary supplements, prescription medications, blood pressure, chronic disease profiles, biospecimens, and more. In collaboration with USDA’s Agriculture Research Service through the What We Eat In America survey, NHANES provides the only nationally representative source of data on dietary intake [25]. NHANES data can inform the development of regulatory actions and policies, such as food labeling standards, food fortification standards, infant formula composition, dietary guidance, dietary reference intakes, efforts to reduce heavy metals in foods for infants and young children, and much more. Despite its significance, however, resourcing challenges limit the ability of NHANES to expand sample sizes, enhance dietary intake and other data collection, and conduct longitudinal studies. The adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) could also help streamline data processing and improve the frequency of data release.

Additional nutrition research resources that can inform nutrition regulatory initiatives include resources developed and maintained by the University of Minnesota Nutrition Coordinating Center (NCC), the Food and Nutrient Database and the Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR), as well as USDA’s FoodData Central [26], and NIH’s Automated Self-Administered 24-Hour (ASA24) Dietary Assessment Tool [27]. The NCC Food and Nutrient Database provides comprehensive food and nutrient composition data on over 19,000 foods with 168 nutrients, nutrient ratios, and other food components. As just one example, the NCC Database has been used to support research on plant-based alternative foods. The NDSR is a dietary analysis software that can be used to assess food and nutrient intake for regulatory research purposes [28]. This resource can be used for entry and nutrient analysis of 24-h dietary recalls and other food records, calculation of nutrients in menus for feeding studies, and calculation of nutrient content of recipes. The NDSR has also been used to quantify the proportion of sodium in the American diet from salt added to food during commercial processing compared with salt added at the table or in home food preparation. FoodData Central, developed by the USDA, integrates multiple datasets and 5 distinct data types to track nutrient composition as well as contaminants in the food supply [29]. These data could inform further study of coexposures of multiple nutrients and contaminants in foods and in dietary patterns on child growth and the development and prevention of chronic disease across the lifespan. ASA24, developed by NIH, is a free, web-based tool that enables multiple, automatically coded, self-administered 24-h diet recalls and/or single-day or multiday food records, also known as food diaries [30]. ASA24 also provides a Respondent Nutrition Report that includes a graphic interface with feedback for study participants on how their reported intake compares to dietary guidance for calorie, food group, and nutrient intake. Additionally, researchers using the ASA24 system can leverage a linkage with Nova to examine questions related to food processing, dietary patterns, health, and chronic disease [31]. These research resources could be further enhanced to expand the information needed for nutrition regulatory science.

On the horizon: technological innovation and other research opportunities to inform nutrition regulatory science

Presenters noted that several innovations show promise in advancing nutrition regulatory science. Specifically, new sensing capabilities of mobile and wearable devices are being explored to capture dietary intake efficiently and accurately. Furthermore, image-based technologies and novel algorithms are being explored to analyze food consumption patterns, nutritional content, and eating behaviors such as meal timing and frequency. For example, the Mobile Food Record is a smartphone-based method that allows users to capture and send food images to a database for analysis [32]. Presenters noted that this technology has been deployed in ∼30 dietary studies, involving more than ∼5000 research participants [33]. Additionally, the Automatic Ingestion Monitor (AIM-2) is a passive food intake sensor requiring no self-report of eating episodes; rather, it only needs compliance with wearing the device [34]. The AIM-2 is capable of automatic detection of food intake, characterization of meal microstructure, and capturing images of the food being eaten [35]. Using technology to analyze food image analyses is gaining popularity, and researchers are developing multidisciplinary teams to overcome challenges in food recognition created by food mixture complexities, image inconsistencies, and high intraclass diversity.

Presenters also noted that metabolomic studies are currently analyzing relationships between metabolites in biological samples (e.g., blood and urine) and diet, including UPFs. Metabolomics and ML tools further have the potential to identify objective markers of UPF exposure, provide novel insight into how UPFs may impact human health, and identify biomarkers that correlate with dietary patterns [36]. For example, studies have shown that hundreds of metabolites differ between diets high in UPFs and minimally processed foods, highlighting the potential of metabolomics in dietary assessment and analysis [37]. However, presenters highlighted challenges that remain, including the limitations of dietary assessment methods and dietary reference databases to accurately assess and classify food processing levels. ML tools are also being applied to metabolomics data to predict dietary intake, but additional research is needed to enhance the comprehensiveness of datasets and to improve dietary biomarker discovery. Furthermore, analyzing stool samples in cohort studies could also provide a more complete picture of microbial metabolites and their potential impact on diet and health.

AI, including ML and related technologies, are also emerging as powerful tools that can potentially revolutionize nutrition research by enhancing the analyses of complex data, informing study designs, predicting nutritional outcomes, and improving dietary recommendations. Presenters highlighted that a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between diet and health requires an integrated, systems-wide approach. This comprehensive approach will require multidisciplinary teams and likely new computational approaches such as Gaussian graphical models and food networks—which can help characterize dietary behaviors—to adequately address research questions. However, presenters again highlighted challenges and opportunities, including the need to standardize methodologies, develop new vocabularies and guidelines, and build capacity among researchers [38,39]. One promising example is the approach being used by Nutrition for Precision Health, powered by the All of Us Research Program. This multidisciplinary program is deploying a modular approach (both observational and controlled feeding) while leveraging the large All of Us cohort and data infrastructure to collect multiple data types (dietary assessment, metabolomics, genomics, etc.) to develop advanced AI/ML algorithms that predict how individuals respond to dietary changes [40,41]. Moreover, future research efforts will benefit from integrating environmental and behavioral factors into dietary research to enhance the accuracy and applicability of individual and public health research to inform programs, practices, and policy development.

Discussion

The NIH-FDA Nutrition Regulatory Science Workshop in December 2024 focused on advancing nutrition regulatory science by identifying key research gaps, priorities, and strategies for collaboration among federal agencies, researchers, and regulators. Discussions emphasized the need to strengthen the science and explore promising new methods and tools and highlighted the need for a structured approach to advancing nutrition regulatory science overall. To bridge the gap between scientific research and practical applications to inform regulatory decision making, behavioral, social, and environmental influences on how people make decisions about what food to buy and eat should be taken into consideration.

With respect to opportunities to improve research methodologies, discussions highlighted the importance of strengthening the quality of dietary assessment methods in research studies. This could include surveillance and large cohort studies, leveraging AI/ML, as well as new imaging technologies and metabolomics to analyze dietary patterns and relationships between food, dietary patterns, and chronic disease. Discussions also highlighted the importance of expanding domiciled feeding trials and the need to add ingredients and additives to food databases. Presenters also emphasized the need for well-conducted implementation studies, including assessments related to populations of interest.

Presenters further emphasized topics such as the need for additional research to understand the physical and mental health effects of foods considered to be ultraprocessed. The Nova-based classification of UPFs has shown overall associations with negative health outcomes but contains a subset of foods for which there are known positive health associations [42]; therefore, the mechanisms by which UPFs impact health urgently need further investigation. Pressing priorities also include exploring the nexus between nutrition and contaminants in food, the role of early-life nutrition and infant feeding practices, and enhancing public trust in federal nutrition policies through clear communication of complex nutrition data to consumers.

In terms of an approach for federal agencies to better address regulatory science needs, participants highlighted that a structured NIH-FDA nutrition regulatory science program (NRSP), modeled on the TRSP, would be a promising and pragmatic way forward. Under such a program, future research directions would be numerous and would foster the development of interdisciplinary teams as well as expand training programs to equip researchers with regulatory science expertise. To this end, in May 2025, NIH and FDA jointly announced the establishment of an NRSP [43]. At present, the operational aspects of the NRSP are still under development and its priority areas remain to be determined. To close, past FDA efforts to take regulatory action to improve public health, such as removal of PHOs from the United States food supply, can inform the research approach most needed to address current questions surrounding nutrition as well as certain food substances and additives. This workshop highlighted the importance of understanding and improving consumer well-being by addressing real-world issues that affect individuals and society. Research is needed to investigate how consumers interact with food choices within microenvironments and macroenvironments rather than just analyzing the consumption of nutrients in isolation. Moving forward, a coordinated research agenda across federal agencies is essential to drive impactful policy changes and improve public health outcomes. By fostering scientific innovation and evidence-based policies, NIH and FDA have an extraordinary opportunity to help create a healthier food environment and reduce the burden of diet-related chronic diseases across the nation.
