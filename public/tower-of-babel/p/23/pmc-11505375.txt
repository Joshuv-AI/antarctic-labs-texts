A Type I Photosensitizer-Polymersome Boosts Reactive Oxygen Species Generation by Forcing H-Aggregation for Amplifying STING Immunotherapy
Authors: Zhixiong Wang, Wen Ma, Zhen Yang, Dale O. Kiesewetter, Yicong Wu, Lixin Lang, Guofeng Zhang, Sofia Nakuchima, Jiji Chen, Yijun Su, Sue Han, Ling-Gang Wu, Albert J. Jin, Wei Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Activation of the innate immune Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) pathway potentiates antitumor immunity. However, delivering STING agonists systemically to tumors presents a formidable challenge, and resistance to STING monotherapy has emerged in clinical trials with diminishing natural killer (NK) cell proliferation. Here, we encapsulated the STING agonist diABZI within polymersomes containing a Type I photosensitizer (NBS), creating a nanoagonist (PNBS/diABZI) for highly responsive tumor immunotherapy. This structure promoted H-aggregation and intersystem crossing of NBS, resulting in a ∼ 3-fold amplification in superoxide anion and singlet oxygen generation. The photodynamic therapy directly damaged hypoxia tumor cells and stimulated the proliferation of NK cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby sensitizing STING immunotherapy. A single systemic intravenous administration of PNBS/diABZI eradicated orthotopic mammary tumors in murine models, achieving long-term antitumor immune memory to inhibit tumor recurrence and metastasis and significantly improving long-term tumor-free survival. This work provides a design rule for boosting reactive oxygen species production by promoting the intersystem crossing process, highlighting the potential of Type I photosensitizer-polymer vehicles for augmenting STING immunotherapy.

Introduction

An immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment poses a clinically formidable hurdle in treating solid cancers.1−3 Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) protein agonists can potentially overcome tumor immunosuppression by eliciting inflammatory innate immune responses; however, achieving effective systemic delivery of these agonists to tumors remains challenging.4−6 The strategic design of nanomedicine, utilizing carriers such as polymers and liposomes, presents a promising avenue to enhance both local and systemic therapeutic effects of cyclic dinucleotide (CDN)-based STING agonists.7−9 Despite the promising potential of these agonists as cancer therapies, clinical trials have revealed the emergence of tumor resistance to STING monotherapy with diminishing natural killer (NK) cell proliferation, attenuating the NK-driven antitumor response.10 Given these limitations, there is an urgent need to develop combinatorial strategies to enhance the efficacy of STING-based therapies in tumors.11,12

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a clinically promising therapeutic modality for various tumors with high spatiotemporal resolution and noninvasiveness.13−15 Beyond inducing direct oxidative stress damage to cancer cells, PDT has the potential to initiate immunogenic cell death (ICD) cascade, sensitizing immunotherapy.16,17 Nonetheless, the hypoxic microenvironment restricts singlet oxygen (1O2) generation, limiting the efficiency of Type II PDT in cancer treatment, as it relies highly on oxygen levels.18 In contrast, Type I PDT can generate superoxide anion (O2•–) and hydroxyl radicals (OH•) via electron transfer independently of oxygen, effectively combating hypoxia in solid tumors.19,20 Therefore, Type I photosensitizer-based polymers represent promising carriers for delivering STING agonists to amplify immunotherapy. Sulfur-substituted Nile Blue (NBS)-based Type I photosensitizers have garnered significant attention for their applications in cancer and bacterial infection treatment.21−23 However, the inevitable π–π stacking in these photosensitizers induces aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) in aqueous media, diminishing O2•– generation.24,25 In a previous study, we proposed a design rule to extend the triplet excited states for tuning the emission lifetime of phosphorescence organic molecules through strong coupling in H-aggregated molecules.26 Both phosphorescence and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation require electrons to transfer from a singlet excited state to a triplet excited state through intersystem crossing.27 Consequently, orienting Type I photosensitizers toward H-aggregation could significantly boost their ROS yields.

In this study, we explored the extraordinary mechanism for the heightened ROS yield attributed to promoting the intersystem crossing rate by H-aggregation and then design a polymersome-grafted NBS (PNBS) capable of orienting the Type I photosensitizer to the H-aggregation. The resulting PNBS exhibits O2•– and 1O2 yields more than 3-fold and 2.9-fold higher than parent NBS, respectively. Furthermore, we encapsulate the STING agonist diABZI within the PNBS to form a nanoagonist (PNBS/diABZI),28 enabling efficient delivery of both the agonist and photosensitizer to tumor sites (Scheme 1a). After a single intravenous systemic administration of PNBS/diABZI, orthotopic mammary tumors in murine were completely eradicated under laser irradiation, efficiently preventing tumor rechallenge via subcutaneous or intravenous injection of 4T1 tumor cells, significantly improving long-term tumor-free survival for at least 50 days. More in vivo studies demonstrated that these innovative nanovesicles induced direct tumor cell death and triggered an ICD cascade, activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and NK cells, and enhanced the sensitization of tumor immunotherapy (Scheme 1b).

Results and Discussion

Design of PNBS/DiABZI Nanovesicles

Benzophenothiazine-based photosensitizers represent classic Type I photosensitizers that efficiently generate O2•– to combat hypoxic tumors. NBS was synthesized following established procedures (Figure S1),29 and its chemical structure was confirmed through analyses including 1H NMR and mass spectrometry (Figure S2). Subsequently, the poly-Type I photosensitizer, P(DMA0.77-co-MAA0.19-g-NBS0.04)91, was synthesized using reversible addition–fragmentation transfer (RAFT) polymerization followed by carboxyl postmodification. The resulting product was characterized through 1H NMR, FT-IR, SEC analysis, and UV/vis absorbance spectra against a standard calibration curve and denoted as PNBS in the following section (Figures S3–S6). Loading diABZI into PNBS yielded PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles through nanoprecipitation. Transmission electron microscopy and dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed nanovesicles with mean diameters of approximately 60 and 80 nm, respectively (Figure 1a,b). The hydrodynamic diameters were kept almost constant upon 48 h in diverse culture mediums, displaying promising stability, favorable for storage and in vivo blood administration (Figure S7). Furthermore, atomic force microscopy characterized the size of the nanovesicles membrane as 4–5 nm (Figure 1c,d). Optical characterization of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles was conducted using UV/vis analysis. A blue-shifted maximum absorption band in the NBS aqueous phase compared to an organic solvent indicated the tendency of NBS to form H-aggregates in water rather than J-aggregates, and the extent of H-aggregation increased with NBS concentration (Figure S8). Notably, the absorption of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles exhibited a significant blue shift at low concentrations compared to NBS in water, suggesting the self-assembly of PNBS in aqueous environments facilitated the H-aggregation of NBS (Figure 1e). As anticipated, the fluorescence of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles was quenched by the ACQ effect (Figure 1f), indicating the likely transfer of absorbed photon energy to generate ROS or thermal energy via intersystem crossing or nonirradiation pathways, respectively.

To explore the photodynamic properties of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed to detect short-lived ROS, including O2•– and 1O2. Spin-trapping agents 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) were used to detect O2•– and 1O2, respectively. ESR signals indicated the formation of DMPO-O2•– and TEMP–1O2 adducts, with significantly stronger signals observed in PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles treated with 671 nm laser irradiation compared to NBS (Figure S9). Subsequently, the O2•– generation yield of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles was quantitatively determined using a fluorescence probe, dihydroethidium (DHE) (Figures 1g and S10). The O2•– generation yield of nanovesicles was three times that of NBS, as inferred from the slope of increasing fluorescence intensity against irradiation time. In addition, the 1O2 quantum yield of the nanovesicles was 2.9 times higher than that of NBS, calculated from the slope of absorption change of ABDA against irradiation time (Figures 1h and S11). No OH• was observed using the specific fluorescence probe of hydroxyphenyl fluoresce (Figure S12). The photothermal conversion efficiency of the nanovesicles mirrored that of NBS in aqueous conditions, and the nanovesicles exhibited relatively excellent photostability (Figures 1i and S13). Moreover, the acidic tumor microenvironment did not affect the structure of the nanovesicles or the production of ROS (Figure S14). Under hypoxic conditions, PNBS/diABZI demonstrated higher ROS and O2•– production compared to methylene blue (Figures S15 and S16). These findings demonstrate that the H-aggregation of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles facilitated the transmission of singlet excited electrons to triplet excited states through intersystem crossing, with negligible enhancement of the nonirradiation pathway. A commercial polymer PF127 encapsulated the NBS and diABZI as a comparison (PF127/NBS/diABZI) (Figure S17). The aqueous dispersion of PF127/NBS/diABZI exhibited an absorption spectrum similar to that of NBS without a significant blue shift, and the ROS generation of PF127/NBS/diABZI closely mirrored that of NBS in an aqueous solution. These results suggested that simple encapsulation could not promote the H-aggregation of NBS or enhance ROS generation.

Mechanism of Promoting Intersystem Crossing to Enhance ROS Generation

To investigate the possible mechanism of NBS to form a dimer or trimer in water, the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted using the Gromacs 2019.4 package (Figures 2a and S18).30 The results revealed spontaneous aggregation of NBS, leading to the formation of dimers and trimers from a monodisperse aqueous solution during 100 ns. The dimer exhibited a slip angle of ∼72° with a coplanar inclined arrangement, and the trimer had a slip angle of about 83°, surpassing the critical value of 54.7°, indicative of a typical H-Type packing molecular structure.31 In aggregation, the planes of NBS were nearly parallel, with distances of 3.4 and 3.5 Å in the trimer. This arrangement created a robust intermolecular π–π stacking interaction driving the H-aggregation of NBS. Interestingly, in the PNBS system, more face-to-face planar stacking was observed (Figure S19). In contrast, for PF127/NBS/diABZI system, NBS tended to aggregate on the surface of micelles with less ordered aggregation, and the resulting structures were not typical H-aggregates (Figure S20). Consequently, these findings offer a theoretical basis for the H-aggregation formation of NBS on the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicle membrane, resulting in a blueshift absorption peak around 628 nm. Further delving into the mechanism of promoting ROS generation from PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles, first-principles time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) investigations were conducted on a single molecule, dimer, and trimer in both singlet and triplet excited states for NBS (Figures 2b,c and S21).32 The energy levels of the NBS monomer at the lowest singlet (ES1 = 1.2) and triplet (ET1 = 1.08) excited states were closely matched (ΔE = 0.12), which enables the facilitation of the singlet–triplet intersystem crossing process. The NBS monomer theoretically exhibited only one major transition from S1 to Tn during intersystem crossing, attributed to S1 being similar only to the transition configuration (H1 → L) and isosurface of the T2 triplet excited state. Moreover, the NBS dimer increased the number of energy transition channels (S1 → T1, T2, T5, and T6), enhancing the spin–orbit coupling probability and promoting the intersystem crossing process. Trimer formation further increased the transmission of S1 → Tn (S1 → T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6, T7, T8, and T9). Additionally, nanovesicles exhibited a relatively extended triplet lifetime of ∼4.18 ns compared to NBS of ∼3.27 ns, furthering the intersystem crossing process (Figures 2d and S22). Therefore, incorporating NBS into the nanovesicle shell facilitates the formation of dimers and trimers, markedly augmenting the intersystem crossing process (Figure 2e,f).

In Vitro PNBS/DiABZI Nanovesicles-Mediated PDT

The cellular uptake and intracellular trafficking of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles were investigated in 4T1 cells using iSIM (Figure 3a,b), a rapid super-resolution imaging technique. Plasmid LAMP1-EGFP was employed to label lysosomes through transfection, allowing for the observation of membrane structures with ∼120 nm super-resolution.33 Subsequently, PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles were introduced into the cell dish to monitor their intracellular transport. The red fluorescence emitted by the nanovesicles was detected inside the lysosomes within 4 min and reached peak intensity at 16 min. This observation suggests that PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles were rapidly taken up through lysosome-dependent endocytosis. Subsequently, the ROS generation properties of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles were evaluated under 671 nm laser irradiation in both regular (21%) and hypoxic (1%) in vitro conditions (Figures 3c and S23). Remarkable O2•– and OH• generation was found in both conditions. This suggests that PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles show promise in effectively treating hypoxic tumors. In addition, 1O2 generation was detected under regular oxygen conditions, enhancing the damage potential to tumor periphery cells with higher oxygen concentration. This is attributed to the higher oxidative potential 1O2 than O2•–.

Next, to assess the lysosome damage that ignited the apoptotic pathway due to in situ ROS generation, a fluorescence probe, acridine orange (AO),34 was employed to evaluate the integrity of lysosomes, which emitted red fluorescence in acid lysosomes, and displayed green fluorescence in the cytoplasm (Figure 3d). Cells treated with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under 671 nm laser irradiation exhibited weak red fluorescence compared to other groups, indicating disruption of the acidic environment within lysosomes. PDT-induced cell apoptosis was quantitatively assessed using flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining (Figure 3e,f). As expected, neither PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under dark conditions nor laser treatment could induce cancer cell apoptosis. A remarkable 95% of apoptotic cells were detected in the group treated with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under 671 nm laser irradiation after 4 h. CCK-8 assays were employed to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity of varying PNBS/diABZI nanovesicle concentrations by 671 nm laser irradiation in both regular (21%) and hypoxic (1%) environments (Figures 3g and S24). IC50 values of PNBS/diABZI for 4T1 cells in regular and hypoxic conditions were determined to be ∼1.24 μg mL–1 and ∼1.62 μg mL–1, respectively. The PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles in varying concentrations were harmless to cells, which suggested good cellular biocompatibility for the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles in dark conditions. The viability of 4T1 cells was significantly decreased with the increasing concentration of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under 671 nm laser irradiation. All the results suggested that the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles displayed spatiotemporal precision treatment potential for hypoxia cancer cells.

Moreover, PDT induces apoptosis in tumor cells and promotes the maturation of DCs to stimulate antitumor immunity. ICD was assessed through immunostaining analysis with two critical markers,35 CRT and HMGB-1 (Figure 3h). Cells treated with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles showed a significant increase in CRT exposure on the cell surface under light irradiation. Compared with the PBS group, the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles +671 nm laser irradiation exhibited a negligible red fluorescence in the cell nucleus, indicating a noticeable down-regulation of HMGB-1 expression. In addition, extracellular ATP secretion was increased in cells treated with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under 671 nm laser irradiation, triggering immune activation (Figure S25). These results highlighted a robust ICD response in tumor cells by PDT and initiated subsequent immune responses (Figure 3i).

PNBS/DiABZI Nanovesicles Activate the STING Pathway In Vitro

The diABZI is a novel noncyclic dinucleotide that potently activates STING.36 We investigated the immune activation of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles on human monocytic leukemia cells, THP-1, and bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in vitro. We evaluated the activation of STING in THP-1 cells, diABZI and PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles induced the phosphorylation of STING (pSTING) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (p-IRF-3) (Figure 4a), and promoted transcription of IL6, the secretion of IFN-β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in BMDCs (Figure 4b–d). We further found that the expressions of CD80 and CD86 in the BMDCs treated with the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles were ∼3.46 times and ∼1.97 times higher than that in the PBS group, respectively (Figure 4e–h). These expressions significantly also increased compared to cells treated with free cGAMP. In addition, we found the sensitivity of the CD86 pathway to lower levels of STING activation, whereas more robust cytokine production, such as IFN-β, typically requires higher-dose cGAMP stimulation (Figure S26). These results indicated that the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles could significantly activate STING and promote the maturation of DC.

Systemic PNBS/DiABZI Nanovesicles Therapy Exerts Potent Antitumor Effects

We expected that the functional nanovesicles would efficiently deliver the diABZI to solid tumors. The accumulation of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles at tumor sites was monitored by real-time fluorescent imaging on a subcutaneous xenograft breast cancer model (Figure 5a). At 3 h postintravenous injection with the saline of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles, the fluorescence of the nanovesicles was observed at tumor sites, and the signals further strengthened with the extension of postinjection time and reached a peak value at 9 h, which provide precise guidance for laser irradiation time window of PDT (Figure 5b). Moreover, the nanovesicles maintained a high content at tumor sites, exhibiting fluorescence signals retention >75% of the maximum value at 24 h postinjection. After that, quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis revealed that nanovesicle accumulation in tumors was higher than in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Figures 5c and S27). These results indicated that the nanovesicles could effectively deliver the STING agonist to tumor sites and retain it for over 24 h.

We established an orthotopic 4T1 mouse breast tumor model to assess the impact of combined PDT and STING activation on cancer inhibition and antitumor immunity. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to six treatment groups: G1 (treated with PBS), G2 (treated with PNBS), G3 (treated with diABZI), G4 (treated with PNBS and exposed to a 671 nm laser), G5 (treated with PNBS/diABZI), and G6 (treated with PNBS/diABZI and exposed to a 671 nm laser) (Figure 5d). Tumor growth was continuously monitored through measurements and bioluminescence imaging throughout the study (Figure 5e–g). The group of PNBS nanovesicles was similar to that of the PBS-treated group, which suggested that blank PNBS nanovesicles could not inhibit tumor development. In contrast, the diABZI group exhibited moderate inhibition efficiency, and the PNBS/diABZI further enhanced the inhibition efficiency due to promoting diABZI accumulation and retention at tumor sites. Upon introducing PDT, tumor growth was effectively inhibited. For the PNBS-treated group, the tumor was eradicated entirely within 3 days following a single session of PDT. Regrettably, the tumor recurred on the seventh day, as detected by bioluminescence imaging. This indicates that while a single PDT treatment can effectively address the primary tumor, it may fall short of inducing long-term immune memory. The most promising results came from the group treated with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles under laser irradiation, as they exhibited complete tumor regression and sustained long-term survival without tumor recurrence. In the long term survival study, mice subjected to PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles with 671 nm laser irradiation demonstrated a markedly extended survival rate. All mice in this group (n = 8) achieved a complete cure, maintaining tumor-free status for at least 32 days (Figure 5h).

The body weight of mice in these groups remained relatively stable throughout the therapeutic process, suggesting ignorable systematic toxicity (Figure 5i). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) analysis further uncovered that PNBS/diABZI + Laser treatment induced the most significant levels of apoptosis and necrosis in the primary tumors (Figure 5j). In addition, immunohistochemical analysis indicated a downregulation in HMGB1 expression and an enhancement in the infiltration of CD8+T cells following treatment with PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles combined with laser irradiation (Figures S28 and S29). Histologic analysis of major organs from the mice in different groups showed negligible damage, further underscoring the safety profile of the nanovesicles and laser irradiation (Figure S30). The systematic blood hematology and biochemistry analyses in healthy mice showed minimal abnormalities in all parameters (Figure S31). These comprehensive results highlighted the superior tumor inhibition capability of PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles with minimal associated side effects.

PNBS/DiABZI Nanovesicles Stimulate Immune Activation and Prevent Cancer Recurrence and Metastasis

To validate the presence of immunological memory, the spleens of the treated mice were collected for 7 days post-treatment and subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Figures 6a,b and S32). Mice subjected to the combined treatment of STING and PDT (PNBS/diABZI + Laser) demonstrated approximately a 2.25-fold increase in memory T cells (CD8+CD44highCD62Llow) compared to the control group, 1.3-fold more than the nanoagonist (PNBS/diABZI), and 1.6-fold more than the small molecular agonist (diABZI). To explore the potential of PNBS/diABZI + Laser treatment in reshaping the immune cellular composition within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and enhancing systemic antitumor immune responses,37 our initial investigation focused on the activation of dendritic cells (DCs) in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) (Figure 6c,d and S33). PDT and immunotherapy induced by PNBS/diABZI + Laser resulted in the highest level of DC maturation among all treatments, with approximately a 2.69-fold increase in CD86 compared to the PBS group, 1.62-fold more than PNBS/diABZI, and 2.2-fold more than diABZI. Furthermore, the PDT combination with STING activation led to a 2-fold increase in DCs within tumor tissues compared to the PBS group (Figure S34).

It is well-recognized that TME is infiltrated with significant infiltration of immunosuppressive cells.38 The mice treated with PNBS/diABZI + Laser exhibited an approximate 3.06-fold reduction in M2-like macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+CD206+) within the TME than the PBS group (Figures 6e,f and S35). Likewise, the proportion of T regulatory cells (Tregs) (CD3+CD4+CD25+FOXP3+) within the tumor microenvironment (TME) significantly diminished following treatments with PNBS/diABZI + Laser, showcasing a remarkable 7.71-fold decrease compared to the PBS group (Figures 6g,h and S35). Significantly, the combination of STING with PDT treatment led to increased infiltration of natural killer (NK) cells, exhibiting a 2.2-fold augmentation compared to PBS, 1.52-fold more than diABZI, and 1.25-fold more than PNBS/diABZI (Figures 6i, S35, and S36).

The PNBS/diABZI-mediated PDT and immunotherapy triggered a robust intertumoral cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell response, resulting in an approximately 3.0-fold augmentation in the total number of cytotoxic CD8+ (CD45+CD3+CD8+) T cells within the TME when compared to the PBS control group. This increase was 2.14-fold higher than PNBS/diABZI, and 1.24-fold higher than the diABZI (Figure 6j,k). We measured the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the serum to assess the systemic immune response triggered by PDT and STING-mediated PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles. Following PNBS/diABZI + Laser treatment for 48 h, there was an observed increase in the concentrations of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), IL-6, and TNF-α in the serum (Figure 6l–n). These findings illustrated that the PNBS/diABZI nanovesicles and 671 nm laser irradiation induced a systemic immunostimulatory effect, leading to a potent anticancer immune response. The comprehensive analysis above indicates that combining PDT with STING activation triggers an immediate immune response and sustains a lasting immune memory.

To further confirm the durability of immune responses and their ability to inhibit tumor recurrence and metastasis, surviving mice from the initial treatment were rechallenged with 4T1 tumor cells. Upon subcutaneous rechallenge, those mice treated with PNBS/diABZI + Laser exhibited complete suppression of cancer and significantly prolonged survival compared to the untreated group-notably, all mice in the treated group (n = 8) achieved complete cure within 50 days (Figure 6o,p). Additionally, upon intravenous rechallenging, no tumor nodules were observed in the lungs of mice treated with PNBS/diABZI + Laser (Figure S37). These findings collectively demonstrate that the Type I photosensitizer polymersomes could effectively deliver the STING agonist to the tumor site to active STING pathway, combined with the PDT triggered the ICD, thereby enhancing immune activation and efficiently preventing cancer recurrence and metastasis.

Conclusion

Aggregation of photosensitizers in aqueous usually quenches ROS production and converts absorbed light into thermal energy, thereby limiting the efficacy of PDT. In this work, we have presented an unusual mechanism to enhance the ROS yields of photosensitizers by promoting H-aggregation. We grafted the Type I photosensitizer NBS onto a polymer and then self-assembled it into polymersomes (PNBS), which promoted the tendency of NBS to H-aggregation. The theoretical calculations demonstrated that H-aggregation could improve the intersystem crossing of electrons, thus boosting ROS generation. The O2•– and 1O2 yields of polymersomes are more than 3-fold and 2.9-fold higher than the parent photosensitizer NBS. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the PDT directly damages hypoxia tumors and promotes the proliferation of NK cells and CTLs. Therefore, the PDT can overcome the tumor resistance of diminishing NK cell proliferation in STING monotherapy.

We encapsulated the STING agonist diABZI to the polymersomes to form a nanoagonist (PNBS/diABZI), enabling efficient delivery of the agonist to tumor sites. After a single intravenous systemic administration of PNBS/diABZI, orthotopic mammary tumors in murine were completely eradicated under laser irradiation, while PDT and STING immunotherapy alone failed to suppress tumors. In addition, combining the PDT and diABZI efficiently prevented tumor rechallenge via subcutaneous or intravenous injection of 4T1 tumor cells, significantly improving long-term tumor-free survival for at least 50 days. The work provides a proof-of-concept example of Type I photosensitizer polymers as sensitizing vehicles for STING-based therapies to address resistance to STING monotherapy.
