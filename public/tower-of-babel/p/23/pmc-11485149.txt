AR expression-independent XRCC3 mediates DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation against prostate cancer
Authors: Hailong Xie, Mingjiang Dan, Yi Cen, Jing Ning, Chong Sun, Guangbin Zhu, Shourui Feng, Haiyan Wang, Jinxian Pu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAndrogen deprivation therapy (ADT) resistance is closely associated with altered AR status. Aberrant AR expression is critical for the induction of ADT resistance, necessitating the identification of an anti-PCa target independent of AR expression.MethodsTranscriptomic data and clinical information of PRAD were obtained from TCGA database. Genes with PCa-related and AR expression-independent were screened by bioinformatics, and characterized by PPI and GO functional enrichment analyses. Candidate genes were locked by co-expression correlation and disease-free survival (DFS) analyses. A prognostic gene set was established using LASSO Cox regression algorithm. Cox proportional risk regression was performed to identify a key prognostic gene. Expression of the target protein in PCa tissues was verified by The Human Protein Atlas database. In vitro validation of cellular function and molecular mechanism by knockdown and overexpression of the target gene.ResultsTwo AR expression-independent genes (SLC43A1 and XRCC3) were available for the optimal prognostic model. This gene set effectively predicted PRAD patients’ DFS at 1-, 3- and 5-year, where XRCC3 and tumor (T) stage were independent risk factors. XRCC3 was higher expressed in PRAD patients with T3-T4 stages and accompanied by poorer DFS. IHC staining also validated its higher expression in high-risk PCa tissues. In vitro experiments demonstrated that silencing XRCC3 significantly inhibited 22Rv1 and DU145 cell proliferation, migration and invasion, while promoted apoptosis. Further, silencing XRCC3 promoted DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation, which was absent with overexpression.ConclusionSilencing XRCC3 exerts anti-PCa effects by promoting DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation in an AR expression-independent manner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-05989-8.Keywords: Prostate cancer, AR, XRCC3, DNA damage, P53/Bax signaling pathway

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignant tumors in men, and contributes to increased mortality in men globally (Sekhoacha et al. 2022). PCa progression is closely related to the continuous stimulation of androgens, which derives androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), a primary treatment for PCa by decreasing the levels of androgens in patient through surgery or drugs (Evans 2018). However, most patients inevitably progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) in the later stage of ADT with a poor prognosis (Chandrasekar et al. 2015). CRPC patients are insensitive to ADT overall, mainly due to the adaptive resistance to AR signaling, which is particularly critical for AR-independent CRPC (Dai et al. 2017; Feng et al. 2023). Although the blockade of AR signaling transduction has a favorable anti-PCa effect, the tumor itself can acquire resistance by altering AR status, representing a key issue in ADT resistance.

AR status regulates CRPC development and progression through multiple pathways. Amplification and overexpression of AR genes can be found in 1% of primary PCa and approximately 60% of metastatic tumors (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2015). Metastatic PCa treated with AR antagonists are more likely to mutate than patients receiving ADT alone (Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2015). 30% of patients with metastatic CRPC have T877A mutation, which amplify the ligand specificity of AR and lead to AR activation by non-androgenic steroids and anti-androgenic drugs (Taplin et al. 1995; Visakorpi et al. 1995). Additionally, aberrant AR enhancers, such as increased copy number, structural rearrangement and binding site mutation of transcription factors, can also lead to AR overexpression (Sur and Taipale 2016). In CRPC, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin and JAK/STAT signaling pathways are aberrantly altered, and AR signaling is able to crosstalk with them to mediate resistance onset (Edlind and Hsieh 2014; Khurana and Sikka 2019). Interestingly, some tumors inherently lack or adaptively down-regulate AR due to PCa heterogeneity (Jamroze et al. 2021). These AR-independent CRPC are naturally insensitive to ADT and can rely on other paracrine pathway activation for survival (Li et al. 2023). Therefore, ADT efficacy is greatly limited by AR status, and finding anti-PCa targets independent of AR expression may be the key to overcome ADT resistance.

We screened anti-PCa targets independent of AR expression by bioinformatics combined with disease database. The risk features were further analyzed by establishing a prognostic model to identify a key target. The target protein expression was validated using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results from online protein database. Finally, the target gene was silenced by small interfering RNA (siRNA) technology to explore its molecular mechanism mediating functional changes of PCa cells in vitro.

Materials and methods

Differential expression gene (DEG) screening

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) expression profiles of 498 prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) and 52 paracancer samples were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.com). The raw data type of the RNA-seq expression profiles was fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM), which were used for subsequent analyses by converting to transcripts per million (TPM) expressed as log2 (TPM + 1). For comparison between tumor and paracancer samples, DEGs were screened with a threshold of |fold change (FC)|> 3 and P < 0.05. For comparison between patients with high AR (ARhigh, n = 249) and low AR (ARlow, n = 249) expression (average distribution based on the median value of AR expression), DEGs were screened with a threshold of |FC|> 1.5 and P < 0.05. Related volcano diagrams and heatmaps were plotted using the R software package (ggplot2). PCa-related genes were obtained from MalaCards: The human disease database (https://www.malacards.org/) (Input ‘Prostate Cancer’ in the search box of ‘Explore a Malady’ to enter the ‘Overview’ interface, and then click the ‘Associated genes’ column to obtain the PCa-related genes). The related gene sets were interacted using the Venny 2.1 tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/ tools/venny/) to screen for common targets.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis

Interaction relationship between target proteins were analyzed using the String database (https://cn.string-db.org/). The active interaction sources for PPI include textmining, experiments, databases, co-expression, neighbourhood, gene fusion and co-occurrence, and the interaction score was set to 0.7 (high confidence). PPI networks were only displayed for target proteins with connected nodes.

Gene ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis

GO functional enrichment was analyzed for biological process, cellular component and molecular function by calculating the strength (enrichment index) and false discovery rate (FDR) based on PPI network counts. GO terms with the top five highest enrichment and FDR < 0.05 were displayed.

Co-expression correlation analysis

Expression correlations between target genes and AR were analyzed by Spearman’s method based on RNA-seq expression profiles of 498 PRAD samples. Related heatmaps were plotted using the R software package (pheatmap).

Disease-free survival (DFS) analysis

Effect of gene expression on PRAD patients’ DFS (high expression, n = 169; low expression, n = 169) was analyzed by log-rank test using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). Hazard ratio (HR) > 1 was considered a risk factor.

Prognostic model establishment and analysis

Test data were derived from RNA-seq expression profiles and clinical information of 338 PRAD patients in TCGA. Gene expression was normalized by log2 (TPM + 1). PRAD patients were divided into low- (n = 169) and high- (n = 169) risk groups according to the median value by calculating the risk score for each sample. Least shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression algorithm was used for feature selection of the prognostic model and validated using the tenfold cross method. Cox regression analysis was used to construct the DFS prognostic model. Log-rank test was used to compare differences in DFS between low- and high-risk groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to compare the predictive accuracy of gene set and risk score. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional risk regression was used to further analyze the relationship between gene set and clinical characterization. All the analysis methods and R software packages (gglpot2, glmnet, survival and timeROC) were implemented by R (v4.0.3) software.

Clinical characterization analysis

Based on 498 PRAD samples in TCGA, 495 PRAD patients with tumor (T) stage information were screened and divided into T1 (n = 177), T2 (n = 205) and T3–T4 (n = 113) groups. Gene expression was normalized by log2 (TPM + 1) to compare its expression in different T stages by Kruskal–Wallis test. Further, 336 PRAD patients with DFS information were screened to compare the prognosis of patients with T1 (n = 127), T2 (n = 149) and T3–T4 (n = 60) stages by log-rank test. All analyses were realized by the R software packages (ggplot2, survival and survminer).

Gene-pathway correlation analysis

Based on 498 PRAD samples in TCGA, Spearman’s correlation between XRCC3 gene and marker gene set for DNA damage repair was analyzed by R software package (GSVA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical sample validation

IHC staining results for 10 PCa patients against XRCC3 proteins with corresponding clinical information were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). IHC staining results were analyzed according to intensity (negative, zero points; weak, one point; moderate, two points; strong, three points) and quantity (none, zero points; < 25%, one point; 25%–75%, two points; > 75%, three points) to calculate the immunoreactive score (IRS). IRS = intensity × quantity.

Cell culture

22Rv1 and DU145 cell lines were purchased from Guangzhou Boyao Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

siRNA construction and transfection

Specific siRNAs (siXRCC3-a, siXRCC3-b and siXRCC3-c) for silencing transcriptional expression of XRCC3, including negative control (NC), were synthesized by Guangzhou Boyao Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Cells were seeded into 6-well plate at 1 × 106 cells/ml and transiently transfected with 2 ml OPTI-MEM medium (Gibco, USA) and siRNA transfection reagent (Santa Cruz, USA). After 6 h of transfection, the plate was replaced with normal serum medium and cultured for 48 h. siRNA sequences were listed in Table S1.

Plasmid construction and transfection

pIRES2-ZsGreen1-XRCC3 plasmid was synthesized by Guangzhou General Biomedical Technology Co. Ltd (Guangzhou, China) for overexpressing XRCC3 with empty plasmid as NC. Cells were seeded into 6-well plate at 1 × 106 cells/ml with 2 ml serum medium. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used for transfection according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plate at 1 × 103 cells/well with 100 μl serum medium. At 6, 12, 24 and 48 h of culture, 10 μl CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added and incubated for 2 h protected from light, and the OD values of cells at 450 nm were detected by microplate reader. Cell proliferation curves were calculated and plotted according to the CCK-8 method.

Cell apoptosis assay

After 48 h of culture, cells were collected and prepared into cell suspension with 1 × binding buffer, and adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/tube. 5 μl Annexin V-APC and 10 μl 7-AAD reagent (Beyotime, China) were added and vortexed to mix well, and incubated for 15 min protected from light. 485 μl pre-cooled 1 × binding buffer was added to each tube and resuspended, and the data were analyzed by flow cytometry with NovoExpress software.

Cell migration assay

After 12 h of transfection, cells were seeded into 6-well plate at 5 × 105 cells/well with 2 ml serum medium. Dropped cells were washed away with PBS after drawing parallel lines on the bottom of culture plates with a pipette tip. Migrating cells at 0 and 48 h were imaged and the migration distances were calculated by Image J (v1.8.0) software.

Cell invasion assay

Invasion experiments were performed using a dual-chamber transmission system with a pore size of 8 μm. Upper chamber was pre-filled with 80 μl matrix solution and incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃. Cells were seeded into upper chamber of the insert at 5 × 104 cells/well with 200 μl serum-free medium, and the lower chamber with 500 μl serum medium. After 48 h of invasion, inserts were fixed with 100% methanol and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China). Invading cells on the inserts were imaged and counted by Image J (v1.8.0) software.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plate at 1 × 103 cells/well with 100 μl serum medium. Specific p53 inhibitor Pifithrin-α (PFT-α) hydrobromide (MCE, USA) was added at 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 μM and treated for 48 h. OD values of cells were detected according to CCK-8 method.

Caspase 3 activity assay.

Following manufacturer’s instructions, caspase 3 activity in cells was detected to assess their apoptosis level by the Caspase 3 Activity Kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, treated cells were lysed with lysis buffer (2 × 104 cells/μl) for 15 min on ice following washing with cold HBSS (Beyotime, China). After incubating the mixture composed of 10 μl cell lysate, 80 μl reaction buffer and 10 μl caspase 3 substrate (2 mM) in 96-well plate at 37 °C for 4 h, caspase 3 activity was quantified at 405 nm.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining.

Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA) for 30 min. Cells were incubated with dsDNA antibody (1:1000, Abcam, USA) overnight at 4 °C. After thrice washing with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibody-Alexa fluor 488 (1:200, Abcam, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After thrice washing with PBS, cells were nucleo-stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) and imaged under fluorescence microscope.

Western blot (WB) assay

Nucleoplasmic separation was performed according to the instructions of ReadiPrep Nuclear/Cytoplasmic Fractionation Kit (AAT Bioquest, USA). Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 0.1% protease-inhibitor (Beyotime, China). 40 µg of total protein was separated on 12% SDS gels and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against AR (1:1500, Bioss, China), XRCC3 (1:1000, CST, USA), γH2AX (1:1000, CST, USA), p53 (1:1500, Bioss, China), Bax (1:1500, Bioss, China), H3 (1:1500, Bioss, China) and ACTB (1:1500, Bioss, China) overnight at 4℃. Membranes were washed with PBS and then incubated in HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were detected using chemiluminescent luminol enhancer solution (YEASEN, China) and exposed using gel imaging system. Gray values of protein bands were calculated by Image J (v1.8.0) software.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were repeated thrice and the data were presented as mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism (v7.0) software was used to perform t-tests between two groups and one-way ANOVA analyses for more than two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Functional characterization of AR expression-independent genes

To screen for PCa-related genes independent of AR expression, we personalized 498 PRAD samples in TCGA by bioinformatics. By comparing with 52 paracancer samples, 1084 DEGs were found in tumor samples, including 336 up-regulated and 748 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1A, C). Further, comparison of 498 PRAD samples divided into ARhigh and ARlow groups based on the median value of AR expression revealed that 15,284 genes were not differently expressed in both groups (Fig. 1B, D). Then, 827 PCa-related genes were obtained from MalaCards database (Table S2). Interactions of 1084 DEGs, 15,284 non-differential genes and 827 PCa-related genes yielded 62 common targets, which were highly related to PCa and independent of AR expression (Fig. 1E). PPI networks revealed interactions between 62 targets, focusing on cell growth (like IGF1 and FGFR1), metabolism (like GSTM1 and CYP2E1), cycle (like CCNB1 and CDC20) and stress (like HSPA5 and KRT8) regulation (Fig. 1F). GO analysis further revealed their regulatory directions in biological process (like peptide antigen assembly with MHC class I protein complex, lipid hydroxylation and positive regulation of mitotic cell cycle spindle assembly checkpoint), cellular component (like platelet alpha granule lumen, endoplasmic reticulum lumen and collagen-containing extracellular matrix) and molecular function (like cadherin binding involved in cell–cell adhesion, serine-type endopeptidase activity and cell adhesion molecule binding) (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that AR expression-independent genes play an important regulatory role in PCa progression, which further demonstrates the reliability of the screen.

Prognostic characterization of AR expression-independent genes.

To further clarify the relationships between 62 common targets and AR, we observed their co-expression correlations with AR in 498 PRAD samples. Correlation heatmaps revealed that AR expression was positively correlated with 30 targets, negatively correlated with 8 targets, and uncorrelated with 24 targets (CRISP3, GDF15, KLK15, KLK4, MMP26, SLC43A1, TFF3, CD59, CTSK, CXCL14, CYP2E1, CYP3A5, ENO2, FGFR1, GSTM1, IGF1, LYVE1, MAD1L1, MCAM, NPY, SERPINA3, SNA12, TGM4 and XRCC3) (Fig. 2A). Then, we observed the prognostic characterization of 62 targets in PCa patients. Since none of them were associated with overall survival, prognostic characterization was performed by DFS. Clinically, DFS provides important guidance in assessing the efficacy of radical prostatectomy and ADT. Prognostic analysis indicated that 8 gene expression affected PCa patients’ DFS, with poor prognosis for high expression of BGLAP, DIABLO, GNRH1, MAD1L1, SLC39A1 and XRCC3, and the opposite effect for KLK4 and SLC43A1 (FIg. 2B–I). After excluding genes with co-expression correlation to AR, the prognostic gene set (KLK4, MAD1L1, SLC43A1 and XRCC3) was targeted, suggesting that this gene set has potential to be used as a characterization for assessing PCa patients’ DFS.

Identification of a key prognostic gene XRCC3.

To verify whether the gene set (KLK4, MAD1L1, SLC43A1 and XRCC3) has ability to predict PCa patients’ DFS, we performed prognostic model inclusion assessment using the LASSO Cox regression algorithm and identified the optimal gene set (SLC43A1 and XRCC3) for subsequent prognostic model establishment (Fig. 3A, B). Patients were equally divided into low- and high-risk groups based on the risk scores of 338 PRAD samples, and the distribution maps of risk scores, survival time and survival status were plotted. The results showed that our prognostic model could well distinguish the expression distribution of the gene set in both groups, and the DFS prognosis was worse in high-risk patients than in low-risk patients (Fig. 3C, D). ROC curves demonstrated that this gene set was effective in predicting the PRAD patients’ DFS at 1-, 3- and 5-year (Fig. 3E). Cox proportional risk regression analysis of the relationship between gene set and clinical characterization revealed that XRCC3 and T stage were the independent risk factors affecting PRAD patients’ DFS (Fig. 3F). It was also observed in PRAD patients with different T stages that XRCC3 was highly expressed in T3-T4 stages and that patients with this stage had significantly worse DFS than patients with T1 and T2 stages (Fig. 3G, H). Further, XRCC3 expression in PCa tissues was observed using the HPA database. IRS revealed higher XRCC3 expression in tumor tissues of high-risk patients (Fig. 4A–C). These results suggest that XRCC3 mediates tumor deterioration and may play a pro-carcinogenic role in PCa progression.

Silencing XRCC3 exerts anti-PCa effects in vitro

Subsequently, we verified the importance of XRCC3 for PCa progression by gene silencing. To demonstrate that XRCC3-mediated functional changes in PCa cells were independent of AR expression, we selected 22Rv1 (AR+ XRCC3+) and DU145 (AR− XRCC3+) cells for comparison, and WB assay verified the native expression of AR and XRCC3 proteins (Figure S1A-S1C). Expression of XRCC3 proteins in PCa cells was targeted for silencing by constructing 3 different siRNAs respectively, and WB assay determined the best silencing effect of siXRCC3-a for subsequent experiments (Figure S2A–C). Cellular functions revealed that silencing XRCC3 significantly inhibited PCa cell proliferation, migration and invasion, while promoting apoptosis (Fig. 5A–N). These results emphasize that XRCC3 mediates PCa progression and that the effects on PCa cell growth and malignant phenotype are not limited by AR expression, suggesting that targeted silencing of XRCC3 may be a novel anti-PCa strategy.

Silencing XRCC3 promotes DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation in vitro

Since XRCC3 is a key gene mediating DNA damage repair, we speculate that silencing XRCC3 may exacerbate cellular DNA damage. Gene-pathway correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between XRCC3 expression and DNA damage repair levels in PRAD samples (Fig. 4D), consistent with our speculation. Excessive DNA damage generates massive amounts of broken double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), while serine 139 on H2AX undergoes phosphorylation to form γH2AX, which are biomarkers for DNA damage (Giglia-Mari et al. 2011). IF and WB assays indicated that silencing XRCC3 promoted dsDNA accumulation and up-regulated γH2AX in PCa cells, while inhibiting its nuclear translocation (Fig. 6A–F). Under the stress of DNA damage, p53 genes are inducibly activated to form pro-apoptotic receptors, thus promoting Bax expression for programmed cell death (Vaddavalli and Schumacher 2022). To clarify the induction relationship between XRCC3 and p53/Bax signaling pathway, we performed reverse validation using the specific p53 inhibitor PFT-α. Pre-experiments demonstrated that 20 μM PFT-α was non-toxic to PCa cells with effective inhibition of p53 expression (Figure S3A–D). Thus, this dose was used for molecular mechanism validation. WB assay revealed that PFT-α attenuated silencing XRCC3-induced p53 and Bax up-regulation in PCa cells (Fig. 6G–J). Interestingly, overexpressing XRCC3 had no effect on DNA damage and p53/Bax signaling pathway in PCa cells (Figure S4A-S4D). Since p53/Bax signaling cascades to activate the caspase family to exercise apoptotic function, we reflected apoptosis level in PCa cells by detecting activity of the terminal apoptotic protein caspase 3. Consistent with the WB assay, PFT-α attenuated silencing XRCC3-induced caspase 3 activation in PCa cells (Figure S5A and S5B). These results suggest that silencing XRCC3 induces DNA damage and activates p53/Bax signaling pathway in PCa cells.

Discussion

Clinically, long-term application of AR signaling-targeting drugs (especially ADT) leads to PCa desensitization, including neuroendocrine differentiation as a subtype of AR-independent CRPC (Makino et al. 2021). Currently, there is a lack of effective treatments for AR-independent CRPC. Thus, targeting PCa-related molecules with AR expression-independent is critical to address this issue, while providing novel therapeutic strategies for ADT resistance.

We obtained 1084 DEGs by comparing 498 PRAD samples with 52 paracancer samples. Further, 15,284 AR-independent genes were screened based on the AR expression levels of PRAD samples. Then, 827 PCa-related genes were obtained from MalaCards database. Finally, they were interacted to generate 62 common targets. PPI network and GO analysis revealed their high correlations in cancer cell fate regulation and PCa progression. For example, KLF5 inhibits STAT3 activity and tumor metastasis in PCa by suppressing IGF1 transcription cooperatively with HDAC1 (Ma et al. 2020). CCNB1 and AURKA are critical genes for PCa progression and CRPC to vinblastine (Chen et al. 2022). Up-regulated FGFR1 expression is associated with the transition of hormone-naive to CRPC (Armstrong et al. 2011). These studies support the high correlations of 62 AR expression-independent genes with PCa progression as potential therapeutic molecules.

Further, we observed the co-expression correlations of 62 targets with AR in 498 PRAD samples. Correlation heatmaps revealed 24 targets independent of AR expression (CRISP3, GDF15, KLK15, KLK4, MMP26, SLC43A1, TFF3, CD59, CTSK, CXCL14, CYP2E1, CYP3A5, ENO2, FGFR1, GSTM1, IGF1, LYVE1, MAD1L1, MCAM, NPY, SERPINA3, SNA12, TGM4 and XRCC3). After excluding genes associated with AR expression, 4 genes (KLK4, MAD1L1, SLC43A1 and XRCC3) with DFS prognostic value were finally obtained. The prognostic value of these 4 genes has been reported in various cancers. In colorectal cancer, KLK4 expression constitutes an unfavorable prognostic biomarker for predicting poor DFS (Kontos et al. 2013). In ovarian cancer, the rs1801368 polymorphism of MAD1L1 worsens patients’ DFS (Bandala-Jacques et al. 2020). In PCa, high SLC43A1 expression as an independent prognostic factor for recurrence-free survival correlates with pathological T stage and Gleason grade (Rii et al. 2021). In breast cancer, the rs861539 polymorphism of XRCC3 is associated with lower DFS and poorer therapy response (Alkasaby et al. 2020). The close association of KLK4, MAD1L1, SLC43A1 and XRCC3 with prognosis in various cancers suggests their potential as a gene set for predicting PCa patients’ DFS.

We used the LASSO Cox regression algorithm for prognostic model inclusion assessment of these 4 genes and screened to the optimal gene set (SLC43A1 and XRCC3) to establish a DFS prognostic model for PRAD patients. High-risk patients had worse DFS compared with low-risk patients. In addition, this gene set was effective in predicting patients’ DFS at 1-, 3- and 5-year. Although the effects of SLC43A1 and XRCC3 on PCa patients’ DFS have not been reported, their pro-PCa risks have been clarified (Rii et al. 2021; Nowacka-Zawisza et al. 2015). Cox proportional risk regression analysis further revealed that XRCC3 was more closely associated with PCa progression, especially with higher expression in patients with high T stage and accompanied by worse DFS. IHC staining also demonstrated the higher XRCC3 expression in high-risk PCa tissues. These results emphasise the important mediating role of XRCC3 in PCa progression, suggesting that targeting XRCC3 may be an effective anti-PCa target.

XRCC3 is a homologous recombination-related protein mainly involved in homologous recombination repair of DNA damage and maintenance of chromosome stability (Pelttari et al. 2015). XRCC3 can be activated when DNA damage reaches a certain level in cancer cells, and cells that fail to repair properly will activate the apoptotic system, leading to programmed death (Pierce et al. 1999). Gene-pathway correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between XRCC3 expression and DNA damage repair level in 498 PRAD samples. In addition to mediating the regulation of DNA damage repair, XRCC3 promotes the progression of various cancers, such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, bladder cancer, breast cancer and melanoma (Zou et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2012; Qiu et al. 2010; Bertram et al. 2004). Consistent with this, we found a pro-carcinogenic role for XRCC3 in PCa. Silencing of XRCC3 expression in 22Rv1 (AR+ XRCC3+) and DU145 (AR− XRCC3+) cells using siRNAs significantly inhibited cell proliferation, migration and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. These results demonstrate that XRCC3 mediates PCa progression in an AR expression-independent manner, which may be related to XRCC3 gene polymorphism (Kanaeva et al. 2016). Given the close relationship between XRCC3 with DNA damage repair and PCa progression, we speculate that silencing XRCC3 may induce excessive DNA damage, which in turn inhibits PCa cell growth. Moreover, it has been reported that XRCC3 depletion induces spontaneous DNA breaks and p53-dependent cell death (Loignon et al. 2007). When DNA damage occurs, its repair requires p53 to activate p21, which blocks cell cycle in G2/M phase (Balakrishnan et al. 2010). However, if DNA damage is excessive and unrepairable, p53 up-regulates Bax to promote apoptosis by the mitochondrial pathway (Liu et al. 2023). Thus, p53/Bax signaling pathway may mediate silencing XRCC3-induced apoptosis in PCa cells. Further in vitro experiments also supported our speculation. Silencing XRCC3 inhibited its nuclear translocation and promoted dsDNA accumulation in 22Rv1 and DU145 cells, indicating excessive DNA damage. Additionally, silencing XRCC3 activated p53/Bax signaling pathway, which could be counteracted by the p53 inhibitor PFT-α. However, overexpressing XRCC3 did not cause DNA damage and affect p53/Bax signaling pathway in PCa cells. Similar findings have been reported in breast cancer, where overexpressing XRCC3 did not alter MCF-7 cell cycle progression in the absence of DNA damage (Xu et al. 2005). These results further emphasise the feasibility of targeting XRCC3 to mediate DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation against PCa.

In summary, we mined a target XRCC3 with AR expression-independent and DFS prognostic value in PCa. In vitro experiments demonstrated that silencing XRCC3 exerted anti-PCa effects by promoting DNA damage-induced p53/Bax signaling pathway activation. This study provides a novel strategy for PCa therapy, especially for ADT-resistant PCa.
