Computational framework for therapeutic target discovery via perturbation simulation: application to cystic fibrosis airway disease
Authors: George Sun, Yi-Hui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputational methods for therapeutic target discovery face challenges in integrating multi-scale biological data and predicting system-wide therapeutic effects. We present a computational framework that integrates single-cell transcriptomics, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and computational perturbation simulation to systematically discover novel therapeutic targets. The framework constructs a knowledge graph comprising 29 896 nodes (23 530 genes and 6366 pathways) with 322 136 edges, integrating gene–gene, gene–pathway, and module relationships. Using perturbation simulation algorithms, we systematically explored 265 candidate targets, scoring each based on perturbation magnitude, module response, therapeutic effect, and statistical significance. Applied to single-cell RNA sequencing data from 38 patients (51 415 cells), the framework identified 66 novel therapeutic targets, including nine very high novelty targets. Computational validation demonstrates efficient scalability (knowledge graph construction: <5 min; 265 perturbation simulations: <2 min) and robust performance across different module sizes. This approach represents a novel computational method for systems-level therapeutic target discovery, with generalizable applications to other complex diseases.

Introduction

Therapeutic target discovery in complex diseases requires integration of multi-scale molecular data and prediction of system-wide therapeutic effects [1–3]. Despite advances in computational biology, current methods face critical limitations. Traditional differential expression-based approaches [1, 3] focus on individual genes or pathways, missing systems-level complexity and functional relationships between molecular entities [3, 4]. Existing network-based methods [2, 5] are largely static, identifying disease-associated genes without predictive models for therapeutic intervention effects [4, 6]. Recent computational frameworks for target discovery and drug repositioning [7–9], including transformer-powered graph representation learning, regulation-aware graph learning, and hierarchical attention networks, rely on network embeddings or topology analysis but share three critical gaps: they do not integrate co-expression module information into knowledge graph structures, do not simulate perturbations to predict module-wide therapeutic responses, and lack systematic validation on disease-specific benchmark datasets with biological interpretation. While single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides unprecedented resolution of cellular heterogeneity [10–12], translating these molecular insights into validated therapeutic targets remains computationally challenging without addressing these limitations.

To address these limitations, we present a computational framework that integrates scRNA-seq data, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [13, 14], knowledge graph construction, and perturbation simulation to systematically discover therapeutic targets. Our framework introduces three key innovations absent from existing methods. First, we integrate WGCNA-derived co-expression modules directly into knowledge graph construction, enabling representation of module-level relationships alongside gene–gene and gene–pathway connections within a unified graph structure [15], a capability absent in the most closely related computational frameworks [7–9]. The resulting knowledge graph comprises 29 896 nodes (23 530 genes and 6366 pathways) with 322 136 edges, including 1002 module-based co-expression edges connecting hub genes. Second, we introduce a module-aware perturbation simulation algorithm that predicts system-wide responses to gene perturbations rather than focusing solely on direct gene effects, enabling computational exploration of 265 candidate targets in 1.8 min. Third, we develop a multicomponent therapeutic scoring system combining perturbation magnitude (30%), module response (40%), statistical significance (20%), and hub gene connectivity (10%), explicitly incorporating module-level effects into target prioritization [1, 3]. Together, these innovations enable virtual simulation of therapeutic interventions before experimental validation, a capability not offered by existing frameworks [7–9].

We demonstrate the framework on cystic fibrosis (CF) airway disease [16–19], analyzing 51 415 cells from 38 patients. CF is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene [17, 20], and while CFTR modulator therapies have shown promise [21–24], the complex pathophysiology of CF airway disease demands novel therapeutic targets beyond CFTR correction. Our scoring system identified 66 novel therapeutic targets, including nine very high novelty targets (therapeutic score > 2.73). The framework’s modular design requires only scRNA-seq or bulk RNA-seq data [10, 11], standard pathway databases (GO, KEGG) [25, 26], and a module identification method [13], making it readily applicable to other complex diseases.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from GSE150674 were processed using Seurat v4/v5 [10, 11] in R. The dataset includes 19 CF patients and 19 Control patients (38 patients total) from three institutions (UCLA, CFF, CSMC), with two experimental conditions: (i) fresh airway epithelial cells directly isolated from patients (40 709 cells from 42 samples: 22 CF, 20 Control), representing the in vivo disease state [12], and (ii) air–liquid interface (ALI) cultured cells from a subset of these same patients after 28 days of culture (10 706 cells), representing an in vitro model [27, 28].

Quality control metrics: genes per cell (>200), UMIs per cell (>500), mitochondrial expression (<10%). Gene expression was normalized using SCTransform [10, 29], and batch effects were corrected using Harmony [11, 30]. Quality control confirmed high-quality data with median genes per cell of 2500–3000 and low mitochondrial gene expression (<5%) [10].

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed separately for fresh tissue and ALI culture datasets using Wilcoxon rank sum test (Seurat FindMarkers) in R [10, 11, 31]. This separate analysis enables condition-specific identification of disease-associated genes while allowing cross-condition comparison.

Filtering criteria and gene counts: Genes were filtered through multiple sequential steps: (i) initial filtering: genes expressed in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\geq $\end{document}10% of cells in either CF or Control groups (fresh: 15 234 genes; ALI: 16 891 genes), (ii) statistical significance: adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document} <.05 (Benjamini and Hochberg [32]) (fresh: 1281 genes; ALI: 5330 genes), (iii) effect size: —log2FC— > 0.25 (fresh: 1281 genes; ALI: 5330 genes), and (iv) Final candidate selection for perturbation simulation: —log2FC— > 0.5 (fresh: 794 genes; ALI: 2608 genes). Differential expression analysis was performed using Seurat’s FindMarkers function with logfc.threshold = 0.25, which inherently filters results to genes with —log2FC— > 0.25. Therefore, all genes passing step 2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document} <.05) already meet the step 3 criterion (—log2FC— > 0.25), resulting in identical counts for these steps. The more stringent threshold (—log2FC— > 0.5) for perturbation simulation ensures that candidate targets show substantial expression changes, increasing confidence in therapeutic potential. From the 3113 unique genes meeting step 4 criteria across both datasets, 265 candidate targets were selected for perturbation simulation based on additional criteria including module membership and expression consistency.

Rationale for separate analysis: Fresh and ALI datasets were analyzed separately because they represent fundamentally different experimental conditions (in vivo versus in vitro) with distinct transcriptomic profiles. This approach enables (i) identification of condition-specific disease mechanisms, (ii) validation of findings across experimental contexts, and (iii) discovery of targets that are robust across both conditions. The substantial overlap in identified targets (44.7% module overlap) validates the biological relevance of our findings and demonstrates that core disease mechanisms are preserved across experimental conditions.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA R package [13, 14] on aggregated expression data (mean expression per sample). This aggregation step converts single-cell data to sample-level expression, enabling WGCNA analysis, which requires sample-level data. Expression matrices were prepared separately for each experimental condition (fresh tissue and ALI culture), resulting in 42 samples for fresh tissue and a subset of samples for ALI culture.

Module identification used blockwiseModules with minimum module size of 30 genes, enabling efficient processing of large gene sets. Module eigengenes were calculated as the first principal component of module gene expression, representing module activity.

Module–trait relationships were assessed by correlating module eigengenes with CF status (binary trait), though no significant correlations were found, consistent with the complex, multi-module nature of CF pathophysiology [2].

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler [33] in R with GO Biological Process [25] and KEGG [26]. For each WGCNA module, genes were tested for pathway enrichment using hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction [32, 33]. Significance thresholds: adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document} <.05, q-value <.2. Pathway results were filtered to remove redundant terms using simplify function [33].

Knowledge graph construction

Knowledge graph construction was implemented in Python using NetworkX [15, 34]. The algorithm proceeds in four steps:

Step 1: node creation. Nodes are created for (i) all genes identified in differential expression analysis (23 530 genes), (ii) all pathways identified in pathway enrichment analysis (6366 pathways from GO [25] and KEGG [26]), and (iii) WGCNA modules (four modules total: one from fresh tissue, three from ALI culture).

Step 2: edge addition from pathway enrichment. Gene-pathway edges are added based on pathway enrichment results [33]. For each enriched pathway, edges connect all genes in that pathway to the pathway node. This creates gene–pathway relationships representing functional associations [25, 26].

Step 3: co-expression edge addition. Co-expression edges are added based on WGCNA hub gene connectivity [13, 14]. Hub genes are identified as genes with high module membership (kME > 0.8) and high connectivity within their modules [15]. Co-expression edges connect hub genes within the same module, representing strong co-expression relationships. A total of 1002 co-expression edges were added.

Step 4: graph metrics calculation. Graph metrics are calculated including (i) number of nodes and edges, (ii) largest connected component size, (iii) average node degree, and (iv) graph density [15, 34]. The final graph comprises 29 896 nodes and 322 136 edges, with the largest connected component including 17 382 nodes (58% of the graph), indicating high interconnectivity.

Weight selection and optimization:

Initial weight selection was based on heuristic reasoning prioritizing module-level effects (40%) over individual gene properties (30% magnitude, 20% significance, 10% hub status), consistent with network pharmacology principles that emphasize system-wide therapeutic impacts [1, 3]. To validate these weights, we performed systematic evaluation of multiple weight combinations using cross-dataset consistency as the validation criterion. Specifically, we computed Pearson correlation of target rankings between fresh and ALI datasets for each weight combination, with higher correlation indicating more robust predictions across experimental conditions. We also evaluated enrichment of top-ranked targets in known CF-relevant pathways (inflammation, epithelial dysfunction, oxidative stress) to assess biological relevance.

Systematic evaluation revealed that our heuristic weights fall within a performance-equivalent range where multiple weight distributions achieve comparable cross-dataset consistency, demonstrating that results are robust and not sensitive to exact weight choices within this validated range. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that rankings remain highly stable across weight variations within this performance-equivalent range (Spearman rank correlation > 0.93), validating that our weight selection captures biologically meaningful signals rather than arbitrary choices. The biological rationale for the weight distribution reflects established principles: module response receives the highest weight because it captures system-wide therapeutic effects, magnitude receives substantial weight because larger expression changes are more likely to produce measurable therapeutic effects, statistical significance receives moderate weight to balance rigor with effect size [37], and hub status receives the lowest weight as a bonus factor for network centrality.

Graph structure and network topology considerations

The current perturbation simulation algorithm estimates module responses based on perturbation magnitude and module size, without explicitly modeling propagation through the knowledge graph topology. This design choice balances computational efficiency with interpretability, enabling rapid evaluation of all the targets. However, we acknowledge that incorporating network propagation methods (e.g. random walk with restart, diffusion kernels, graph convolutional networks) or advanced graph learning techniques such as large language model-enhanced knowledge graph approaches [38, 39] could potentially improve prediction accuracy by modeling how perturbations propagate through graph edges.

The current approach is justified because (i) module-level responses already capture indirect effects through co-expression relationships, (ii) the knowledge graph primarily serves to integrate multi-scale relationships (gene–pathway, module–gene) rather than modeling dynamic propagation, and (iii) computational efficiency enables systematic exploration of large target spaces. Future enhancements could incorporate graph-based propagation to model perturbation spread along edges, potentially using methods such as random walk with restart [5] or advanced graph learning techniques including large language model-enhanced knowledge graphs [38] and generative Bayesian learning [39] to predict how perturbations propagate through the knowledge graph structure. This limitation is discussed further in the Discussion section.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed in R (4.5.1) for preprocessing [10, 11], differential expression [31], WGCNA [13, 14], and pathway enrichment [33], and Python (3.9) for knowledge graph construction [15, 34] and perturbation simulation [6, 35]. Multiple testing correction used the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [32], and correlation analyses used Pearson correlation. All analyses used random seed 42; reproducibility was confirmed across multiple runs (Pearson correlation > 0.99).

Knowledge graph construction and computational performance

We constructed a knowledge graph using NetworkX [15, 34] in Python, integrating (i) 23 530 genes from differential expression and WGCNA modules, (ii) 6366 pathways from GO and KEGG enrichment [25, 26, 33], and (iii) 1002 co-expression edges connecting hub genes within modules [13, 14]. The graph comprises 29 896 nodes and 322 136 edges, with the largest connected component including 17 382 nodes (58% of the graph), indicating high interconnectivity [2, 5].

The current knowledge graph includes three relationship types: (i) gene–pathway relationships from enrichment analysis, (ii) gene–gene co-expression relationships from WGCNA hub genes, and (iii) module–gene relationships. Supplementary Table S5 provides the module assignment for each gene, enabling identification of co-expressed gene sets.

Perturbation simulation algorithm and target discovery

Using the perturbation simulation algorithm (see Method Section), we systematically explored 265 candidate targets, scoring each based on perturbation magnitude, module response, therapeutic effect, and statistical significance. Therapeutic scores were calculated as weighted combinations: perturbation magnitude (30%), module response (40%), statistical significance (20%), and hub gene status (10%) [1, 6, 35].

This analysis identified 66 novel therapeutic targets with strong therapeutic potential (Table 4), including nine very high novelty targets (therapeutic score > 2.73). Top-ranked targets include TMEM45A (therapeutic score=2.870), LINC00152 (score=2.927, a long noncoding RNA [36, 40]), and AKR1B10 (log2FC=−3.36) (Fig. 4). The complete list of all therapeutic targets is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Biological interpretation of predicted targets

To assess the biological plausibility of our predicted targets, we performed literature-based validation and functional annotation analysis for the top-ranked targets. The top-ranked novel target, LINC00152 (therapeutic score=2.927), is a long noncoding RNA upregulated in CF (log2FC=+2.36). While LINC00152 has not been previously associated with CF, long noncoding RNAs are increasingly recognized as regulators of inflammatory pathways [36, 40], suggesting potential roles in CF airway inflammation. The second-ranked target, TMEM45A (therapeutic score=2.870), is an uncharacterized transmembrane protein that is the most significantly upregulated gene in CF (log2FC=+2.20, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document}=6.73e-34). TMEM45A has been implicated in epithelial cell function and may play roles in membrane transport or cell adhesion processes relevant to CF airway disease. The third-ranked target, SLC5A8 (therapeutic score=2.784), is a solute carrier involved in metabolite transport. SLC5A8 has been associated with epithelial barrier function and may contribute to CF-related epithelial dysfunction.

Among downregulated targets, AKR1B10 (log2FC=−3.36) is the most downregulated gene in CF and encodes an aldo-keto reductase involved in epithelial protection and detoxification. AKR1B10 has been shown to protect against oxidative stress and epithelial damage, suggesting that its restoration may provide therapeutic benefit in CF airway disease. Similarly, GPX2 (log2FC=−2.06), a glutathione peroxidase, is a key antioxidant enzyme whose downregulation highlights impaired redox metabolism in CF. Restoration of GPX2 may help mitigate oxidative stress, a known contributor to CF pathophysiology.

These biological interpretations, while requiring experimental validation, demonstrate that our computational predictions identify targets with plausible biological roles in CF airway disease, supporting the framework’s utility for therapeutic target discovery.

(iii) Pathway enrichment-based ranking: targets ranked by pathway enrichment significance. Discussion

Computational contributions

The framework addresses key computational challenges through efficient algorithms: combining genes, pathways, and modules into a unified knowledge graph, enabling perturbation simulation across large target spaces, and supporting systematic evaluation of candidate targets (see Section 2.6.7 for performance details). The knowledge graph provides a comprehensive representation of molecular relationships [5, 15], enabling prediction of perturbation effects beyond directly targeted genes. This systems-level approach is essential for complex diseases [1, 2], where therapeutic benefit may require modulation of multiple pathways.

Methodological advantages

Compared with existing computational methods for target discovery [1, 3, 4], our framework offers several advantages: (i) integration: combines multiple data types (scRNA-seq [10, 11], pathways [25, 26, 33], networks [2, 5]) in a unified framework, (ii) prediction: provides predictive models for therapeutic effects rather than static associations [6, 35], (iii) systematic: enables systematic exploration of large target spaces, and (iv) generalizable: modular design facilitates application to other diseases and data types [47, 48].

The framework’s computational efficiency enables real-time target discovery and integration of new data [15, 34]. The knowledge graph can be incrementally updated as new pathways or relationships are discovered [25, 26, 33], supporting continuous improvement of target predictions [5].

Comparison with existing methods

As demonstrated in Section 3.6, our framework identifies distinct therapeutic targets compared with traditional baseline methods. Beyond quantitative differences, our approach offers three conceptual advantages over traditional DEG-based and network-based methods [1, 3]: (i) systems-level context through integration of WGCNA modules and knowledge graphs, capturing module-wide effects that DEG-based methods miss, (ii) predictive capability through perturbation simulation that predicts therapeutic effects rather than identifying associations, and (iii) multi-scale integration across genes, pathways, and modules, enabling identification of targets affecting multiple biological scales.

Compared with recent computational frameworks [7–9], our approach differs in three key aspects. First, module integration: we uniquely integrate WGCNA-derived co-expression modules directly into the knowledge graph structure, enabling module-aware target prioritization. Methods such as transformer-powered graph representation learning [7] and regulation-aware graph learning [8] use network embeddings but do not incorporate co-expression module information. Second, perturbation simulation: our framework simulates perturbations to predict module-wide responses, rather than relying solely on network topology, embeddings, or association prediction. Third, disease-specific application: we demonstrate application to CF airway disease with comprehensive biological interpretation, providing disease-specific validation that complements general drug repositioning [8] and cancer gene identification [7] approaches. Our integration of modules with perturbation simulation represents a novel computational paradigm for therapeutic target discovery.

Limitations and future directions

The current knowledge graph integrates three relationship types (gene–pathway, co-expression, module relationships); incorporation of protein–protein interactions, transcription factor–target relationships, and multi-omics data [2, 5] represents a natural extension. The module-based perturbation simulation prioritizes interpretability and efficiency, and future work could explore graph-based propagation methods or advanced graph learning techniques [38, 39] to model edge-level perturbation spread. Benchmark validation on standardized datasets remains limited by the scarcity of CF-specific resources, though cross-dataset validation supports the current approach [32]. Experimental testing of top-ranked targets in CF cell models and patient-derived organoids will be an important translational next step.

Generalizability and applications

The framework’s modular design facilitates extension to other complex diseases (asthma, COPD, inflammatory bowel disease) and data types [47, 48]. The computational methods are generalizable, requiring only (i) scRNA-seq or bulk RNA-seq data [10, 11], (ii) pathway databases (GO, KEGG) [25, 26], and (iii) module identification (WGCNA or alternative methods) [13, 14]. The framework’s scalability and efficiency support application to larger datasets and multi-disease analyses.
