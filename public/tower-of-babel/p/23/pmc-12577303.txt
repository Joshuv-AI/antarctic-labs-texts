Perceptions of patient safety culture in a teaching hospital in Brazil
Authors: Giulyane Targino Aires-Moreno, Laís Garcia Marques, Milena Motta Xavier Sodré, Márcia Maria Macedo Lima, Sheila Feitosa Ramos, Fernando de Castro Araújo-Neto, Rafaella de Oliveira Santos Silva, Thaciana dos Santos Alcântara, Alessandra Rezende Mesquita, Clarice Chemello, Divaldo Lyra-Jr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient safety is a global challenge that has a profound effect on healthcare. To reduce errors and improve outcomes, we must promote a safety culture. It should foster communication and teamwork. It has 42 questions across 12 dimensions. In Brazil, the HSOPSC is available in its online version as the Hospital Safety Culture E-Questionnaire. A punitive culture creates fear and reduces the reporting of adverse events. It also leads to a belief that leaders do not support safety. Background

Patient safety is a critical global challenge that undermines clinical outcomes and the functioning of health systems [1]. Despite advances, there is still a high incidence of errors that harm patients, especially in hospitals [1]. Such environments must promote a culture of patient safety that is favorable to safe and effective care for patients and professionals [2]. In this sense, patient safety culture is the product of individual and group attitudes, competencies, values, perceptions, and patterns of behavior that determine the commitment to, and the style and proficiency of, an health organization and safety management [3].

Berry et al. (2020) observed a relationship between improved safety culture and reduced in all-hospital harm (p < 0.01), serious safety events (p < 0.001), and severity-adjusted hospital mortality (p < 0.001) [4]. Another study found that hospitals with better work environments had a 48% decrease in patient mortality [5]. Hospitals that have a negative culture are characterized by organizational culture difficulties with consequences unprofessional behaviour, unsafe attitudes of professionals and its impact on patient care [6]. Also, teaching hospitals face challenges in promoting a safety culture. These include a diverse group of trainees, complex procedures, and a high patient volume [7]. In teaching hospitals, it is necessary to raise awareness among all human resources about the importance of patient safety to maintain the quality of patient care [8].

In this context, assessing patient safety culture can improve healthcare processes [9, 10]. This assessment can be based on the perceptions of healthcare professionals about their institutions, leadership, and the responsibility of coworkers regarding patient safety [6]. A tool that assesses patient safety culture addresses the perceptions of professionals about behaviors, values, and norms of an organization [2, 11].

A systematic review of studies conducted in hospitals in Latin America found that professionals reported a negative patient safety culture (48%) [12]. In addition, these professionals reported difficulties in reporting adverse events and coordinating teamwork in hospitals. These problems can be solved by implementing specific interventions, such as walk-rounds with executive managers and interprofessional team training [13, 14]. However, it is necessary to understand the patient safety culture of hospitals to improve the real needs of the environment. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the patient safety culture of a teaching hospital in Northeast Brazil.

Methods

A cross-sectional and descriptive study was conducted in a teaching hospital in Northeast Brazil, from July to October 2023. The hospital is a public teaching hospital with 168 beds and provides medium to high complexity care. It is part of the Public Health System of Brazil and performs 24,000 consultations per year with general, specialized, and outpatient care. Reports of this study were based on the tool Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in epidemiology (STROBE) [15].

Patient safety culture survey tool

This study used the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC) for data collection. This tool was previously developed by Sorra and Nieva (2004) [16]. The HSOPSC assesses the perception of hospital staff on patient safety culture. Several countries use this instrument. It has strong psychometric support and acceptable reliability [16–19]. In Brazil, the HSOPSC is available in its online version as the Hospital Safety Culture E-Questionnaire [20].

The HSOPSC has 42 questions included in the following dimensions: 1) Organizational learning-continuous improvement; 2) Teamwork within hospital units; 3) Hospital management support for patient safety; 4) Nonpunitive response to error; 5) Staffing; 6) Supervisor/manager expectations and actions promoting safety; 7) Teamwork across hospital units; 8) Hospital handoffs and transitions; 9) Feedback and communication about error; 10) Communication openness; 11) Frequency of event reporting; 12) Overall perceptions of safety. Each dimension has 5-point Likert scale response options. They range from 1– completely disagree– to 5– completely agree.

The dimensions were considered positive when the items presented 75% or more positive responses and they were considered negative when positive responses were equal to or less than 50% [21].

In addition to the HSOPSC dimensions, we requested: 1) Perception of patient safety in the work environment, rated as"excellent","very good","acceptable","poor”, or “failing”; 2) Years worked in the hospital; 3) Years worked in the area/unit; 4) Workload per week; 5) Number of adverse events reported in the last 12 months.

Participants

No sample size calculation was done (sampling technique). The public teaching hospital where this study was carried out had already prior registration on a platform with access to the online version of HSOPSC. All hospital professionals were eligible to participate in the research and recruitment took place via email and in person. By email, all hospital professionals have received the HSOPSC link by email. Reminders were sent 7, 10, and 15 days later. Personally, participants were constantly encouraged to participate in the research. Encouragement was given through posters on bulletin boards, notices on the hospital's desktop, and direct contact between researchers and professionals in their work environments. The expected response rate was over 50% [22].

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using SPSS 21.0® software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows; Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). To ensure data and quality analyses, we excluded answers that had less than 70% of items completed. Data collected were expressed using descriptive and differential statistics. We calculated the absolute and relative frequencies of categorical variables. To analyze individual items, we reversed the coding of results for questions that were worded negatively (e.g.,"Professionals fear to ask when something doesn't seem right").

Responses that averaged between 3.5 to 5.0 points showed high agreement among professionals. Averages between 1.0 and 3.4 indicated low agreement [23]. We used the non-parametric Friedman test to compare the mean perceptions of professionals from the 12 HSOPSC dimensions. Then, the Box’s M test was calculated to analyze the homogeneity of covariance among these data.

For correlation analyses, we used Pearson's correlation test. It related the independent variables (time worked in this hospital, time worked in the current area/unit, workload per week, and number of adverse events reported) with the dependent variable (general average of patient safety culture). After the correlation analysis, a simple linear regression test was applied to verify causality between the same variables. For this regression test, the normality analysis of the residuals (Mahalanobis and Cook’s distances) and the Durbin-Watson residual values ​​were performed. To check data normality, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used. We used Levene's test to assume normality. Data was considered significant when p < 0.05.

Research ethics

The Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Sergipe approved this study (number: 5.692.831). To participate in this research, all participants were invited to sign the Free and Informed Consent Form.

Results

Of the 2,088 professionals eligible to take part in the survey, 511 (24.5%) responded to the HSOPSC. Of the 511 questionnaires answered, 455 (89%) were valid. As they had over 70% of the items completed, we used them for analysis. Professionals who participated the most were nurses (n = 74; 16.3%), nursing technicians (n = 71; 15.6%), and physicians (n = 63; 13.8%). Professionals have been working at the hospital for six to ten years (n = 156; 34.3%), 29.9% (n = 136) have been working in their area/unit for two to five years, and 41.7% (n = 190) use to work 21–39 h per week. Table 1 in Appendix describes other characteristics related to the study participants.

The average percentage of positive responses about patient safety culture was 45.5%. The analysis found that no dimension of the instrument was strong (Table 2 in Appendix). Eight dimensions were considered: “Feedback and communication about errors” (45.9%), “Overall perceptions of safety” (43.6%), “Hospital management support for patient safety” (41.8%), “Supervisor/manager expectations and actions promoting safety” (38.3%), “Hospital handoffs and transitions” (36.6%), “Frequency of event reporting” (34.3%), “Nonpunitive response to error” (30.8%), and “Teamwork across hospital units” (25.8%). Table 3 in Appendix shows the average agreement of professionals, by category, with each HSOPSC dimension.

In the"organizational learning and continuous improvement"dimension, psychologists had a negative perception compared to nurses (p = 0.014), nursing technicians (p = 0.004), physicians (p = 0.031), and administrative/management professionals (p = 0.001). About"teamwork in the unit/service,"nutritionists had a negative perception compared to nursing technicians (p = 0.015) and physicians (p = 0.015). In contrast, in"hospital management support for patient safety,"administration/directorship professionals had a more positive perception of this dimension than nurses (p = 0.001), nursing assistants (p = 0.009), pharmacists (p = 0.030), psychologists (p = 0.001), and administrative assistants (p = 0.036).

A non-parametric Friedman’s test found significant differences in means between the 12 dimensions of the instrument (p < 0.001). Pearson’s test found no significant correlation among additional questions of HSOPSC. The “general average of patient safety culture"dimension had the following p-values:"time working in the hospital"(p = 0.854),"time in the area/unit"(p = 0.710),"hours worked per week"(p = 0.954), and"adverse events reported"(p = 0.643)."Time working in the hospital"had a positive correlation with"number of adverse events reported"(p = 0.04) (Table 4 in Appendix).

A weak positive correlation was found between “general average of patient safety culture” and “perception of patient safety in the work environment” (r = 0.196; p < 0.01). Also, simple regression confirmed the relationship between these variables (F (1,374) = 14.927, p < 0.001; adjusted R2 = 0.036). Yet, simple linear regression (B = 0.263, 95% [0.129–0.397]) showed that for every 0.263-point increase in “perception of patient safety in the work environment”,"general average of patient safety culture” increased by 1 point. The data were normally distributed.

Discussion

Patient safety culture is an essential principle for adequate patient care in healthcare settings [12, 24, 25]. The study found that professionals have a negative perception of patient safety culture. Professionals perceived that there is still a need to improve leadership support for frontline staff, shift handoff, patient care transitions, and adverse event reporting.

In this research, despite the efforts of researchers, the expected number of participants was below 50% of hospital population [22]. Several factors justify this: lack of time, research overload, and the questionnaire length [26–28]. Corroborating our findings, two systematic reviews found studies using the same tool that used 4.2% to 100% of participants in hospitals [29, 30]. In view of this, future studies should reduce the aforementioned causes for low answers and include more participants to obtain results closer to reality.

As in other hospitals in Latin America, this study did not find any strong dimension of patient safety culture [12, 22]. A scoping review of patient safety culture in hospitals in Brazil, which used the HSOPSC, found that most of the 12 dimensions were not positive [31]. Therefore, it is essential to identify flaws and assumptions in patient safety culture to support better decision-making [32].

This study concluded that “Organizational learning—continuous improvement” and “Teamwork in hospital units” were neutral dimensions. These findings were corroborated by studies conducted in public, private and/or teaching hospitals in Brazil, Bulgaria, China, Ethiopia, and Pakistan [31, 33–36]. Although these are neutral perceptions, these dimensions can be strengthened so that professionals realize that errors can lead to positive changes and learning. One approach to improving patient safety culture is enhancing teamwork and effective communication through TeamSTEPPS® program [37]. Aaberg and colleagues, (2019) implemented teamwork tools based on TeamSTEPPS® program and checked positive impact on patient safety culture and teamwork in surgical wards [14].

The literature corroborates the negative perceptions regarding “Frequency of reported events” and “Nonpunitive response to error” dimensions [31, 33, 34, 36]. These findings demonstrate that professionals fear punishment for their errors resulting from adverse events. An integrative review found that fear of retaliation and discomfort in confronting leaders are the main barriers to reporting adverse events in intensive care hospitals [38]. Therefore, it is necessary to build a culture that values ​​reporting adverse events which could be reached through educational strategies that address this issue [39, 40]. This will help us to understand the real situation of health services and allow for continuous and comprehensive assessment of the quality of care.

Distrust and fear may explain the low participation of professionals, even when anonymity is guaranteed. This is reflected in the negative perceptions about “non-punitive response to error” [41, 42]. Punitive culture does not encourage honest reporting of patient safety issues. Nwosu and colleagues (2022) stated that, to improve reports, professionals must know that errors and event reports will not be used against them [43]. Therefore, deconstructing punitive culture together with educational measures can foster bonds of trust, improve the management of care and reduce fear of retaliation.

Negative perceptions of “Hospital management support for patient safety” and “Supervisor/manager expectations and actions that promote safety” suggest that leaders ignore staff suggestions and do not correct errors related to patient safety. Thus, high daily demands, combined with a lack of support at work, can cause insecurity and anxiety, also reducing the engagement of professionals in work [26]. Therefore, we need to resize human resources based on service quality. This will make professionals more efficient, less fatigued, and more satisfied with their work. Certainly, the implementation of walk-rounds with executive managers will bring leaders closer to professionals who provide direct assistance to patients [13].

The dimensions “teamwork between hospital units” and “hospital transfers and handoffs” suggest the existence of obstacles in patient care transitions. Communication problems compromise the care process and generate conflicts between different hospital sectors. Some studies show that poor communication during care transitions may be due to incomplete medical record, non-compliance with checklists, verbal instructions from professionals, among others [44–46]. Therefore, it is necessary to improve verbal communication in care transitions, and this can be done through interprofessional education strategies.

This study found variations in the levels of agreement between administrative professionals and leaders compared to other professional groups. This finding could have two possible meanings. Perhaps leaders truly believe that their hospital patient safety culture is positive, or perhaps they are unaware of the reality faced by the interprofessional frontline team [12, 23]. Before that, leaders could participate in strategic clinical activities such as daily rounds to discuss cases with the healthcare team [47, 48]. Therefore, reducing the perceived gaps between professionals that play different roles may improve perceptions of patient safety culture.

It is important to note that hospitals with higher patient safety culture scores tend to have fewer adverse events [24]. However, the overall average patient safety culture score found in this study, below 50%, revealed that professionals have a negative perception. Retrospective studies in Brazilian public teaching hospitals, using medical record analysis, identified 102 to 386 adverse events in one year [49, 50]. To improve patient safety culture scores, we need to reduce punitive actions for errors, improve leadership support for patient safety, improve communication during staff shift changes and care transitions, among other measures.

In this study, there was a positive correlation between length of service at the hospital and the frequency of reported adverse events. A study in a public hospital in Spain found the opposite, as professionals with more than ten years of experience were less satisfied with the safety culture. They also reported adverse events less frequently [51]. In the case of professionals who have worked at the hospital for a shorter period of time, such as residents, they may not realize the importance of reporting adverse events due to high workload, lack of time, or lack of knowledge of how to report them [52, 53]. Given these results, the hospital must provide means to ensure that. Regardless of the length of professional experience, everyone should be aware of the need to report and be capable of doing so.

This study is scientifically relevant and contributes to the literature on patient safety culture. Future studies should promote practical evidence-based interventions to investigate whether improving patient safety culture positively impacts clinical, economic and humanistic outcomes. The strengths of this study were the inclusion of professionals from different categories who work at the hospital to understand the perceptions of those involved in care and those involved in management activities. Another advantage was the use of the HSOPSC, which, in Brazil, is integrated into the Brazilian Health Regulatory Agency (Anvisa) system, and this may enable future comparative research with other hospitals. Moreover, we directly approached hospital professionals to participate in this research. This was a differential since most studies extend this invitation exclusively via electronic communication. However, this study had limitations. The low response rate to the questionnaire may limit inferences about the perception of patient safety in this scenario. Therefore, selection bias may also be present since proactive professionals are more likely to engage actively in participatory processes.

Conclusion

The data show weaknesses in the patient safety culture. Healthcare professionals perceive the hospital environment as unsafe for patient care. The culture of punishment for errors is still present in the routine of professionals, generating fear and a low frequency of reporting adverse events. A further challenge is that professionals feel their leaders are not sufficiently engaged in patient safety. Therefore, both workers and managers must implement effective actions to promote cooperative interprofessional work. Besides, they should foster a non-punitive, transparent culture, with systematized and documented processes.
