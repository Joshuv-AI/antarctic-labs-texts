Macrophage dynamics and the role of TREM2 at the oral mucosal barrier
Authors: Taiana C Leite, Jiamiao Chen, Alexander A Diacou, Sangmin Park, Andrew H Jheon, Jie Yin, Quinn T Easter, Nicholas F Fitz, Kevin M Byrd, Daniel R Clark
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrophages have a critical role in mucosal barrier immunity, demonstrating dynamic phenotypes that promote and resolve inflammation. However, the regulation of this polarization is not fully understood. Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is an immunoregulatory receptor expressed on macrophages. The role of TREM2 in mucosal immunity has not been previously studied. Our objective was to investigate TREM2 on macrophages during periodontal disease (PD), a chronic inflammatory condition affecting the oral mucosa.MethodsPD was induced using a ligature method in wild-type (WT) and TREM2−/− mice. Macrophages were isolated from oral mucosa and analyzed via flow cytometry. PD severity was assessed via quantification of bone loss and local inflammatory cytokine expression. Lastly, oral mucosal macrophages from WT mice and humans were analyzed via single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).ResultsWe demonstrated TREM2 expression on oral mucosal macrophages, and the proportion of TREM2+ macrophages significantly increased during resolution of PD and decreased during active PD. Analysis of TREM2−/− mice revealed a more severe PD phenotype with significantly increased tissue destruction and increased Il1b, Tnfa, and Il6 expression in the oral mucosa compared to WT. Transcriptional characterization of oral mucosal macrophages from mice and humans using scRNA-seq demonstrated subpopulations of TREM2+ macrophages characterized by similar gene signatures and pathways consistent with immunoregulatory functions.ConclusionThis study improves our understanding of macrophage polarization and introduces TREM2 as an important immunoregulator in the oral mucosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07135-5.Keywords: Macrophages, Periodontal disease, Inflammation, Oral mucosa, Mice

Background

Chronic inflammatory conditions frequently occur at mucosal barrier surfaces lining the oral and gastrointestinal tract [1–3]. Such conditions include periodontal disease (PD), oral lichen planus, Crohn’s disease, and ulcerative colitis [2, 4]. Host defense and tissue homeostasis at these mucosal barriers rely, in part, on the plasticity of the innate immune response [5]. Macrophages play a critical role in mucosal barriers with their demonstrated ability to both promote and resolve the inflammatory response and contribute to tissue repair [6]. Studying macrophage plasticity has revealed dynamic phenotypes with heterogeneous functions in a disease- and tissue-specific context [7].

Understanding mechanisms through which macrophages respond to their local environment and change their activity and phenotype is critical to improved management of conditions affecting the mucosal barriers. The triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is a transmembrane receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily that is expressed on myeloid cells and demonstrates immunoregulatory functions [8–11]. Signaling through TREM2 downregulates inflammatory cytokine expression by macrophages, in part, via intracellular competition with Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling [8, 12]. Macrophages derived from TREM2-deficient mice demonstrate increased cytokine production [8]. TREM2 signaling has also been shown to increase cell migration and phagocytosis [8, 12, 13]. This receptor has been most extensively studied in microglia, resident macrophages of the brain, due to its known contribution to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) [13–15]. Dysregulation of TREM2 has been linked to other chronic conditions, such as liver disease, metabolic syndrome, atherosclerosis, osteoarthritis, and cancer [16–20]. However, proper regulation of TREM2 appears to be disease- and tissue-specific, as increased and decreased TREM2 activity have been shown to be pathologic across multiple conditions [21]. These findings on TREM2 mirror both the protective and pathological effects of macrophage activity.

PD is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting the oral mucosal tissues supporting the dentition [22, 23]. In PD, oral microbial stimuli initiate an inflammatory response within the oral mucosa [23, 24]. The early cellular immune response is characterized by infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages into the mucosal tissue [22, 25]. Removal of the microbial stimuli results in an active process of inflammatory resolution [26], while a continued pathogenic stimulus further promotes a macrophage and lymphocytic response in the oral mucosa [25, 26]. A prolonged immune response ultimately results in downstream activation of matrix metalloproteinases and osteoclast activity, leading to the local destruction of the epithelium, connective tissue, and bone that support teeth [22–25, 27].

Macrophages have been studied in the context of PD and demonstrate heterogeneous phenotypes within the oral mucosa across disease and health conditions [28]. Early responding macrophages in PD exhibit a phenotype characterized by the expression of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines that further propagate the immune response [29–31]. During resolution of PD, an alternative macrophage is present with a phenotype characterized by expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines and promotion of phagocytosis [28, 32]. There is a large body of research that characterizes these pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes as either M1 or M2 phenotypes, respectively [28, 30, 33–35]. As macrophage polarization becomes dysregulated, with an abnormally increased M1/M2 ratio, inflammation intensifies, and PD progresses [27, 36, 37]. However, given the complexity of macrophage heterogeneity and the disease- and tissue-specific phenotypes observed in other conditions, a more in-depth characterization of oral mucosal macrophage phenotypes, beyond M1 or M2, is warranted [31].

Given the crucial role of macrophages in periodontal health, disease, and resolution, this study aimed to better understand the mechanisms regulating macrophage activity in the oral mucosa. We focused on investigating TREM2 as an immunoregulatory molecule on macrophages, as its role in the oral mucosa is largely understudied. We hypothesized that TREM2 is critical in regulating macrophage polarization towards an immunoregulatory phenotype within the oral mucosa. To test our hypothesis, we used a mouse model of PD to characterize TREM2-expressing macrophages in different conditions of periodontal health, disease, and resolution. Our results showed that TREM2 is differentially expressed in oral macrophages across the conditions and is enriched on macrophage surface during the resolution of PD. Further, Trem2-deficient mice demonstrated inflammatory dysregulation locally in the oral mucosa with increased PD severity. High throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of oral mucosal macrophages demonstrated multiple transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations. Trem2 was enriched in a subpopulation that expanded during resolution and demonstrated an immunoregulatory gene signature. Finally, we validated our findings in human oral mucosa by incorporating a scRNA-seq analysis from human subjects with and without PD. We showed TREM2 expression was similarly limited to the macrophage cell group and observed a similar enrichment within a macrophage subpopulation that exhibited an immunoregulatory transcriptional phenotype. Our data suggest that TREM2 is an important regulator of inflammation within macrophages of the oral mucosa, and future work may be directed at altering its activity towards immunomodulatory therapies for PD and other conditions similarly affecting mucosal barriers.

Methods

Periodontal disease mouse model

For all experiments, four-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice were used. Wild-type (WT) and TREM2−/− mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (#000664 and #027197, respectively). The animals were housed in a temperature-controlled environment with access to food and water ad libitum. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups for comparisons across multiple experiments and assays: periodontal healthy controls (control), PD induction (disease), and PD resolution (resolution). In the disease and resolution groups, PD was induced using the ligature method, as described previously by our group [37] and others [38] (Fig. 1). Briefly, mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane, and 6.0 silk sutures were tied around the second maxillary molars of the mice in a subgingival position. Ligatures remained in place for seven days to induce PD. In the disease group, mice were euthanized after the seven days of PD induction and tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses. In the resolution group, the ligatures were removed after seven days of PD induction, and mice were allowed to recover for an additional five days before they were euthanized and tissues were harvested.

Flow cytometry

Cells were isolated from the maxilla containing the affected teeth and supporting hard and soft tissues. The maxilla of each mouse was dissected in the control, disease, and resolution groups (n = 6 per group). For cell isolation, the maxilla was manually disassociated and then filtered through a 100 mm cell strainer. Subsequently, tissues were digested with collagenase type 1 (0.2 mg/mL, Worthington) for 1 h at 37 °C. Isolated cells were washed, centrifuged, and resuspended in incubation buffer (0.5% BSA in PBS). Cells were blocked for 10 min in 10% rat serum. Fixable Viability Dye (BD Biosciences) was used to identify dead cells. Next, cells were stained with directly conjugated fluorescent antibodies (rat anti-mouse): CD45 (clone 30-F11, BD Biosciences), MHC Class II (clone M5/114.15.2, BD Biosciences), Ly6C (clone AL21, BD Biosciences), Ly6G (clone 1A8, BD Biosciences), CD11b (clone M1/70, BD Biosciences) and TREM2 (clone 78.18, Bio-Rad Laboratories. Inc). Fluorescence Minus One controls were used to gate for background staining. Flow cytometry was performed using a FACSAria (BD Biosciences) instrument, and FlowJo Software 9.6 (Treestar) was used for analysis.

Bone loss assessment

The severity of PD was quantified by measuring the extent of bone loss in the maxilla of WT and TREM2−/− mice in control, disease, and resolution groups (n = 5–7 per group). The hemimaxilla containing the three molar teeth was isolated, defleshed, placed in 30% hydrogen peroxide overnight, and fixed in 70% ethanol. Using a dissecting microscope (Leica M165 FC, Leica Microsystems Ltd), magnified images of each hemimaxilla were obtained, to include one image of the buccal aspect and one image of the palatal aspect of the hemimaxilla of each animal. A ruler was placed adjacent to each sample to allow measurement calibrations in each image. ImageJ (U.S. National Institutes of Health) was used to make linear measurements on the obtained images from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC). Measurements were made at six sites on the second molar and at 4 sites for the 1st and 3rd molars, as the most peripheral sites were largely unaffected by the ligature (Fig. 1E). Bone loss was reported as the mean of all measurements per animal.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was used to evaluate inflammatory gene expression locally in the oral mucosae of WT and TREM2−/− mice across the three groups. Tissue from the oral mucosa was isolated from hemimaxillae of mice from control, disease, and resolution groups (n = 5–7 per group), and prepared for qRT-PCR. Briefly, the isolated tissues were homogenized in Trizol (Invitrogen), and RNA was extracted. cDNA was reverse transcribed using Superscript III (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green (SYBR™ Select Master Mix, Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene Gapdh. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Micro-computed tomography (µCT)

We performed µCT scans to assess bone mineral density (BMD) of mouse mandibles in WT versus TREM2−/− mice. Mandibles were dissected, defleshed, and fixed/stored in 70% ethanol. Samples were scanned using Scano Medical µCT 50 system (SCANCO Medical, Switzerland) set at 70 kVp, 114 µA, 0.36 degrees rotation step (180 degrees angular range), and a 1500 ms exposure per view with a slice thickness of 6 µm. Scanned images were analyzed using Scanco Medical µCT software (HP, DECwindows Motif1.6) for 3D reconstruction and viewing of images. After 3D reconstruction, volumes were segmented using a global threshold of 0.5 g HA/cc. BMD was measured using the Scanco Medical µCT Evaluation Program v6.5 at two distinct regions of interest (ROI) within each mandible. ROI 1 was in the condylar neck region (determined to be at the midpoint of a straight line drawn from the proximal edges of the condylar and gonial processes) and ROI 2 was in the body of the mandible below the alveolar process surrounding the first molar.

Mouse single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) and analysis

The mouse dataset analyzed in this study was previously generated by our group [39] and is publicly available (NCBI GEO: GSE244633). Briefly, oral mucosal tissues were harvested from mice across periodontal health, disease, and resolution conditions (n = 3 per group). Following tissue dissociation, viable CD45 + immune cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and processed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10 × Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq4000, with each sample sequenced on an independent lane. The mm10 reference genome (10 × Genomics refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A) was used for alignment and quantification via Cell Ranger (v6.1), generating unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices.

Post-processing and visualization were conducted using Trailmaker (Parse Biosciences). Quality control was performed by filtering out: (1) barcodes with < 500 detected transcripts, (2) cells with > 15% mitochondrial transcript content, and (3) cells with doublet scores > 0.5. Cell cycle-related genes were excluded from downstream analysis.

Data normalization was performed via log transformation. Highly variable genes (n = 2000) were selected for principal component analysis (PCA), with the top 40 PCs used for batch correction and dimensionality reduction. Harmony was used for integration, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was generated using Euclidean distance and a minimum distance of 1.0. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.8. Cell types were determined based on marker genes (Additional file 1). For focused macrophage analysis, cells were manually annotated using CellTypist (https://www.celltypist.org/), and macrophage clusters were extracted and reanalyzed without additional filtering, following the same integration and clustering parameters.

Human scRNA-seq and analysis

The human scRNA-seq dataset analyzed in this study was compiled from our previously published integrated atlas of the human periodontium, available on CELLxGENE (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/71f4bccf-53d4-4c12-9e80-e73bfb89e398) and NCBI GEO (GSE152042, GSE161267, GSE164241, and GSE266897) [40]. The integrated dataset includes oral mucosal cells from 30 samples across four independent studies, representing both periodontal health and disease conditions (n = 97,744 total cells) [41–44].

Data were reprocessed and analyzed using Trailmaker (Parse Biosciences) with human-specific quality control and integration parameters. Cells were filtered by: (1) a minimum of 500 detected transcripts per cell, (2) less than 15% mitochondrial gene content, and (3) doublet score ≤ 0.5. Cell cycle genes were excluded from all downstream analyses.

Following log normalization, the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, with 13 PCs retained to capture ~ 95% of variance. UMAP embeddings were calculated using cosine distance and a minimum distance parameter of 1.0. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.5.

To specifically analyze macrophage heterogeneity, major immune cell types were annotated using CellTypist (https://www.celltypist.org/). Macrophage barcodes were manually selected from annotated clusters and extracted for subclustering. No additional filtering was applied after subsetting. Reclustering was performed using the same dimensionality reduction and clustering parameters described above to identify transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations.

Statistics

Bone loss and BMD were calculated per sample and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between more than two groups were carried out first using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by within-group comparisons made via Tukey’s post-hoc test. Differences between two groups were analyzed using student’s t tests. For qRT-PCR, technical triplicates were used, and the mean threshold cycle (CT) value was calculated. Relative mRNA expression (2−ΔCT) was calculated with Gapdh as endogenous control and was presented as mean ± SD. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.10 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Results

PD was induced in mice using a ligature model, previously used by our group and others [37, 38, 45] (Fig. 1). Briefly, a ligature is tied around the second maxillary molar, facilitating microbial plaque accumulation and a local inflammatory response (Fig. 1A and B). The PD model results in characteristic soft (oral mucosa) and hard (bone) tissue destruction, when compared to healthy control (Fig. 1C). Three experimental groups were included for analysis throughout different experiments carried out in this work: periodontal health (control), disease, and resolution. In the resolution group, the suture was removed after PD was induced and the animal was allowed to recover for a short duration (Fig. 1D). We quantified significant bone loss following PD induction and significant bone recovery after removal of ligatures (Fig. 1E and F), as increasing bone loss serves as a proxy for increasing PD severity [38].

TREM2 is differentially expressed in oral mucosal macrophages of mice in conditions of periodontal health, disease, and resolution

We isolated macrophages from the oral mucosa of mice within the three groups of periodontal health, disease, and resolution. The isolated macrophages were analyzed via flow cytometry to determine the level of TREM2 surface expression. Macrophages were identified as viable CD45+, Ly6G−, MHCII−, CD11b+. The gating strategy is depicted in Fig. 2A. The TREM2 expression on the identified macrophages was analyzed and the proportion of TREM2+ macrophages over total macrophages is reported. The proportion of TREM2+ macrophages significantly expanded during resolution from PD (0.166 ± 0.017) and significantly decreased during PD (0.084 ± 0.018), compared to periodontal health controls (0.11 ± 0.007) (Fig. 2B). These data demonstrate, for the first time, the presence of TREM2 surface expression on macrophages within the oral mucosa. Additionally, the differential expansion and contraction of the TREM2+ macrophage populations during resolution and PD, respectively, suggest an immunoregulatory role for TREM2 in the oral mucosa. We further examined TREM2 expression on other Cd11b+ cell types, neutrophils (Ly6G+) and dendritic-like cells (MHCII+) (Fig. 2C). TREM2+ neutrophils represented less than 2% of the Cd11b+ cell population, with a significant expansion during resolution compared to control conditions. TREM2+ dendritic-like cells, represented less than 1% of the Cd11b+ cell population with no significant differences across groups.

TREM2-deficient mice demonstrate increased PD severity and inflammatory dysregulation

Given the potentially important immunoregulatory role of TREM2 on oral mucosa macrophages, we next sought to evaluate the effects of global depletion of TREM2 on the oral mucosal phenotype in mice. Towards this goal, we compared TREM2−/− mice to age-matched WT mice on the same C57BL/6 J background. PD was induced in WT and TREM2−/− mice in the same manner as described in Fig. 1. To compare phenotypes, we evaluated the extent of bone loss in the area supporting the dentition and the local inflammatory cytokine expression within the oral mucosa across conditions of periodontal health, disease, and resolution. TREM2−/− demonstrated significantly increased bone loss in control conditions (0.215 mm ± 0.006 mm) compared to WT mice (0.175 mm ± 0.032 mm) (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, TREM2−/− mice demonstrated significantly more bone loss in resolution conditions compared to WT (0.332 mm ± 0.054 mm and 0.245 mm ± 0.027 mm, respectively), suggesting an attenuated recovery process compared to WT mice. The phenotype within the oral mucosa was further assessed through quantification of proinflammatory cytokines that have previously been shown to contribute to PD pathology (i.e., interleukin-1 β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α)) [46, 47]. We isolated oral mucosal tissue and performed RT-qPCR. Results revealed that Il1b, Il6, and Tnfa were significantly upregulated in TREM2−/− relative to WT mice across most conditions of periodontal health (0.037 ± 0.022 and 0.006 ± 0.004, 0.112 ± 0.083 and 0.03 ± 0.031, and 0.04 ± 0.03 and 0.007 ± 0.008, respectively), disease (0.137 ± 0.105 and 0.027 ± 0.022, 0.27 ± 0.272 and 0.065 ± 0.037, and 0.012 ± 0.004 and 0.075 ± 0.113, respectively), and resolution (0.028 ± 0.012 and 0.013 ± 0.009, 0.025 ± 0.027 and 0.032 ± 0.014, and 0.016 ± 0.012 and 0.004 ± 0.004, respectively) (Fig. 3C). Together, these findings show that the oral mucosal phenotype in TREM2−/− mice is characterized by increased PD severity (increased bone loss) and local inflammatory dysregulation, further supporting the important immunoregulatory role of TREM2 in the oral mucosa.

We were next interested in evaluating any additional phenotypes in TREM2−/− outside of the oral mucosa that might have been extraneously contributing to the PD phenotype we characterized. Specifically, we evaluated a potential skeletal phenotype within the jaw due to previous reports of TREM2 affecting osteoclast activity [48, 49]. To determine the extent to which TREM2 depletion may have resulted in a skeletal phenotype within the jaw contributing to the PD phenotype observed, we assessed the bone mineral density (BMD) of the mandibles in WT and TREM2−/− mice via micro-computerized tomography (µCT). The BMD measured at two separate regions of interest (ROIs) of the mandible showed no significant difference between TREM2−/− and WT mice (1234.745 ± 21.920 and 1224.056 ± 3.95 mg HA/cm3, respectively, for ROI 1, and 978.882 ± 15.227 and 1002.128 ± 7.072 mg HA/cm3, respectively, for ROI 2) (Fig. 3D). Thus, the increased bone loss and PD severity in the TREM2−/− mice were likely a result of the local inflammatory dysregulation in the oral mucosa.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) demonstrates temporal dynamics of macrophage heterogeneity and differential Trem2 expression across macrophage subpopulations

Next, we employed scRNA-seq to explore the macrophage heterogeneity within the oral mucosa and the transcriptional signature associated with TREM2 expression. We reanalyzed our previously generated scRNA-seq dataset of CD45 + immune cells isolated from mouse oral mucosa across periodontal health, disease, and resolution conditions (n = 3 per group; 18,884 total cells) [39]. We first annotated the immune cell types within the isolated CD45 + cells using characteristic cell type gene markers [32, 50]. This clustering analysis revealed the expected immune cell diversity within the oral mucosa, including T cells, NK cells, neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells, B cells, and macrophages (Fig. 4A; Additional File 1). Notably, Trem2 expression was largely restricted to the macrophage cell population (Fig. 4B). To better understand the heterogeneity within the macrophage compartment, we isolated and reclustered macrophages (n = 832 cells in the health group, n = 761 cells in disease, and n = 847 cells in resolution), resulting in six transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations (Fig. 4C). Multiple subpopulations demonstrated enrichment of characteristic macrophage markers, Cd14 and Thbs1 (clusters 1, 2, and 5) [51, 52], while others demonstrated differential enrichment for antigen-presenting genes (cluster 3) and genes regulating response to interferon signaling, i.e., Bst2 and Siglech [53–55] (cluster 4) (Fig. 4D; Additional File 2).

We next assessed how these subpopulations changed across conditions. Figure 4E shows the proportion of each subpopulation within total macrophages to understand differential expansion and contraction of the subpopulations as a function of periodontal health, disease, and resolution. Subpopulations 1 and 2 were observed to contract in disease and expand in health and resolution, while subpopulation 3 contracted in health and resolution, and expanded in disease. The differential expansions and contractions of subpopulations may suggest their contribution to the conditions within the oral mucosa. We then evaluated Trem2 expression within each macrophage subpopulation and found differential enrichment of Trem2 across subpopulations (Fig. 4F). The highest expression of Trem2 was observed in cluster 2, which also showed expansion in the resolution group. In further interrogating the transcriptional profile of each subpopulation, we showed that cluster 2 demonstrated downregulation of proinflammatory genes, including Pla2g7, Cxcl2, Il1b, and Nfkb1 [28, 35, 56, 57], compared to other clusters (Fig. 4G). Additionally, phagocytosis-associated genes (Fcer1g, Rack1, Cyba, Tyrobp) [58–61] were also differentially expressed across clusters, with highest expression in clusters 1 and 2. Taken together, these findings provide additional validation of the expression of Trem2 within oral mucosal macrophages, and they characterize an immunoregulatory macrophage subpopulation that expands during resolution and demonstrates high expression of Trem2.

Analysis of human oral mucosa cells validates TREM2 enrichment in macrophage subpopulations with an immunoregulatory phenotype via scRNA-seq

We next sought to understand the extent to which our findings from mice would translate to human oral mucosa. Therefore, to validate our findings, we reanalyzed the v1 Human Periodontitis Atlas, an integrated scRNA-seq dataset originally compiled by our group of cells isolated from human oral mucosa tissue in conditions of periodontal health and disease [40]. In the scRNA-seq analysis, cell type characterization of the isolated oral mucosa cells revealed a similar immune cell repertoire as identified in our mouse dataset, along with a broader inclusion of fibroblasts and keratinocytes (Fig. 5A). Also mirroring what we found in mouse cells, TREM2 expression was largely limited to the macrophage population in human cells (Fig. 5B). We analyzed the macrophage population separately (n = 999 cells from health and n = 1050 from disease) and performed reclustering, resulting in 10 transcriptionally distinct subpopulations (Fig. 5C; Additional File 3). We were interested in investigating the differential TREM2 expression across macrophage subpopulations and, again, identified a cluster enriched in TREM2 expression (cluster 2) (Fig. 5D). We next performed differential gene expression analysis across the macrophage subpopulations and developed a curated heatmap with genes associated with pro- and anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes (Fig. 5E). The TREM2-enriched subpopulation (cluster 2) demonstrated downregulation of genes associated with M1-like phenotypes and proinflammatory processes, such as AREG, IL1B, EREG, S100A8, and FCN1 [62–66]. This cluster was also characterized by upregulation of genes associated with M2-like phenotypes and immunoregulatory functions, such as C1QB, MAFB, STAB1, DAB2, APOC1, and LGMN [67–73]. Lastly, we compared the upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (p < 0.05; logFC > 0.25) in the TREM2-enriched clusters of both mouse and human datasets (cluster 2 in both). We identified 43 genes to be similarly upregulated across both species (Fig. 5F, Additional file 4). Many of the shared genes include those associated with M2-like macrophage phenotypes (CEBPB, KLF4, LGALS3, CCR1, ANXA5) [74–77]. Multiple heat shock protein genes were upregulated in both species (HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSPA8) [78–80], as well as genes involved in macrophage motility (ACTR2, WASF2, CCDC88A) [81–83]. F13A1 was also upregulated across both species, suggesting the subpopulation may contribute to wound-healing and regeneration similarly in mice and humans [84, 85]. This shared list further validates the immunoregulatory role of TREM2 and of how our findings in mouse models can translate to humans.

Overall, these data demonstrate TREM2-enriched macrophage subpopulations to have an important immunoregulatory role in the oral mucosa with a related M2-like transcriptional phenotype. Furthermore, the findings in this human dataset validate our findings in mice and strongly suggest an important role of TREM2 in macrophages at the oral mucosal barrier.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a mouse model of PD to further examine mucosal immunity and analyze macrophage dynamics and regulatory mechanisms within the oral mucosa. Mucosal barriers host the largest population of immune cells in the body, with around 70% of the total number [86]. The local immune system is continuously challenged to reach a delicate balance between pathologic and physiologic responses to different stimuli, as these barriers are permeated by local commensals and innocuous antigens, but may also be invaded by noxious pathogens [3]. However, the oral mucosa remains understudied as a mucosal barrier [3]. Therefore, by investigating the microbial-host response occurring in the oral mucosa, and in PD specifically, it is possible to obtain a better understanding and gain new insights regarding mucosal immunity in general. Moreover, acquiring specimens from the oral mucosa of human subjects is more feasible than acquiring specimens from mucosal tissues from other sites, thus facilitating future research of mucosal barriers.

We chose to focus this study on the role of TREM2 in the oral mucosa, as this receptor has not yet been investigated in oral mucosal macrophages. We were able to show, for the first time, that TREM2 marks a functionally distinct macrophage subset that presents an immunomodulatory phenotype. Previous work has extensively demonstrated that microglial cells expressing TREM2 play a protective role in the brain against AD and other neurodegenerative conditions by reducing inflammation and accumulation of amyloid plaques [87]. In the brain, TREM2 becomes activated in microglia upon binding with ligands, such as lipoproteins, phospholipids, and lipopolysaccharides and promotes phagocytic activity [11, 88]. TREM2 variants that lead to partial loss of function of the TREM2 protein have been identified and linked to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative conditions by hindering microglial activity and resulting in neuroinflammation [15].

TREM2 has also been shown to play an immunoregulatory role in chronic conditions outside of the central nervous system. For example, Trem2-deficient mouse models of liver injury had worse disease and survival when compared to WT [16]. Similarly, Fang et al. observed more severe inflammation in Trem2-deficient mouse models of osteoarthritis compared to WT [17]. In another study, Jaitin et al. identified a novel Trem2-expressing lipid-associated macrophage subpopulation in mouse models of obesity. In Trem2-deficient mice, metabolic syndrome became more severe, with macrophages failing to be recruited to enlarged adipocytes, leading to adipocyte hypertrophy, systemic hypercholesterolemia, inflammation, and glucose intolerance [20]. Lastly, TREM2 was also studied in the context of cancer. In ovarian cancer models, targeting TREM2 in tumor-associated macrophages using an antibody improved outcomes by inhibiting the anti-inflammatory effects of TREM2 [18].

Given its role in other peripheral inflammatory conditions, we hypothesized that TREM2 could also play a role in the immunoregulation of macrophages of the oral mucosa during health and PD. Using ligature-induced murine models of PD, we demonstrated, for the first time, that macrophages of the oral mucosa express TREM2, as validated via flow cytometry and scRNA-seq. In disease conditions, there were significantly fewer TREM2-expressing macrophages than in periodontal health and resolution conditions, suggesting an inverse association between TREM2 and a proinflammatory state. Indeed, the literature similarly shows that TREM2 activation hinders myeloid responsiveness to proinflammatory stimuli [8, 11, 89] and that proinflammatory stimuli downregulate TREM2 expression [90].

In using TREM2−/− mice in our models of PD, we observed PD phenotype in control conditions, as measured by inflammatory dysregulation in the oral mucosa and increased alveolar bone loss. The TREM2−/− mice also demonstrated dysregulation in the resolution condition, with continued inflammatory dysregulation and failed to significantly recover bone height compared to WT. No significant differences in bone loss were observed during the PD condition in WT vs TREM2−/− mice. Given the inflammatory dysregulation demonstrated in TREM2−/− mice, it may have been expected to observe increased bone loss during PD. However, the ligature model used here induces a severe and acute inflammatory response in both WT and TREM2−/− mice that may have obscured any additional pathologic effect of the inflammatory phenotype in TREM2−/− mice.

Our analysis consistently showed dysregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression in the oral mucosa. Similarly, in a study by Fang et al. using Trem2-deficient mice in a model of osteoarthritis, expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the synovial tissues was higher in the TREM2−/− osteoarthritis mice compared to WT osteoarthritis mice [17]. Our results, therefore, indicated that the activation of TREM2 may be required for adequate resolution of inflammation and tissue healing. Accordingly, previous studies have demonstrated the presence of TREM2-expressing macrophages in the context of inflammatory resolution, for example, in the lungs and liver [91, 92]. Of note, we did not investigate the behavior of other immune cells, such as T cells or B cells, in TREM2−/− mice. In future studies, it may be worthwhile examining how other immune cells infiltrate the oral mucosa when TREM2 is inhibited, given the impact of the role played by macrophages in propagating the immune response and recruiting immune cells [30, 33].

Other studies have characterized an osteopenic phenotype in the long bones of TREM2−/− mice due to a possible perturbation of osteoclastogenesis [93]. However, specific observation in the jaw bone has been limited. In our study, BMD measurements in two separate sites in the jaws of TREM2−/− mice showed that there were no alterations in BMD (Fig. 2D), suggesting that the increased loss of bone was a function of upstream macrophage perturbations rather than a more general skeletal phenotype. Indeed, osteoporotic mouse models show a much milder phenotype in the jaws when compared to long bones [94, 95].

We further profiled TREM2 using scRNA-seq to analyze the immune cell repertoire in our mouse models across the three periodontal conditions of health, disease, and resolution. We confirmed that Trem2 expression is limited to macrophages, as other studies have indicated [10, 11]. We identified six transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations in oral mucosa, which differentially contracted and expanded as a function of periodontal condition, indicating that different subpopulations of macrophages likely play different roles in each condition. TREM2 was expressed across multiple subpopulations. The highest TREM2 expression was observed in Cluster 2, which expanded during health and resolution conditions. Upon additional analysis, this cluster also presented downregulated proinflammatory genes, such as Cxcl2 and Il1b [28, 35], and upregulated anti-inflammatory genes, such as Rack1 and Fcer1g [58, 59]. This strengthens our data showing that TREM2-expressing macrophages function in an immunomodulatory manner, which was further confirmed by the contraction of this group of macrophages during PD.

To validate the data above, we reanalyzed a scRNA-seq dataset of human oral mucosa cells in conditions of health and PD. We found, again, that TREM2 is almost exclusively expressed in macrophages. Additionally, we also identified a TREM2-enriched subpopulation of macrophages in human oral mucosa (Cluster 2). Interestingly, this subpopulation was also enriched for marker genes for macrophage M2 polarization. This subpopulation may play important roles in macrophage polarization during health and resolution. In both mouse and human data, there were macrophage clusters that were enriched for either M1-like phenotype marker genes, or M2-like marker genes. It is important to note, however, that there were other transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations that did not fit into this M1-M2 classification, highlighting the complexity of this cell’s phenotypic range. When analyzing the genes in the TREM2-enriched macrophage subpopulation of mouse and human datasets (cluster 2 in both), we identified 43 genes that were similarly upregulated across both species. The shared genes encompassed many immunoregulatory functions, further validating the immunoregulatory role of TREM2 in macrophages across species.

In conclusion, this study introduced, for the first time, TREM2 as an important immunomodulator on macrophages in the oral mucosa. Our findings position TREM2 as an attractive therapeutic target in the management of PD given its potential role in promoting inflammatory resolution [11]. As signaling through TREM2 promotes an anti-inflammatory effect and that TREM2 expression on macrophages was reduced during PD, an effective immunomodulatory treatment may be aimed at sustaining TREM2 expression and activity. In fact, this strategy is actively being pursued in AD research. Monoclonal antibodies that target the TREM2 stalk region and stabilize its expression on the surface were shown to decrease neuroinflammation and AD pathology [96]. Similarly, activating monoclonal antibodies and agonist of TREM2 have been developed and are currently in Phase I and II clinical trials for the treatment of AD [97]. There are currently no studies focused on targeting TREM2 in such a way in mucosal barrier conditions, Future avenues of research could, therefore, employ similar strategies in PD as a preventative or as an immunomodulatory adjuvant to enhance treatment outcomes.
