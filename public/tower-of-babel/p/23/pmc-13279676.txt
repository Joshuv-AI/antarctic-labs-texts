Protein Nanocages as Versatile Vectors for Nucleic Acid Delivery: Main Systems and Their Loading Mechanisms
Authors: Carlos Francisco Coffeen, Aquetzali Estefanía Hernández-Gutiérrez, Ismael Bustos-Jaimes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid advancement of nucleic acid-based technologies, such as mRNA vaccines and CRISPR-based gene editing, has transformed modern medicine. Yet, their full therapeutic potential remains constrained by the need for precise cargo loading and efficient delivery. This limitation has inspired the development of novel vector systems capable of both protecting nucleic acids and directing them to specific cellular targets. Protein nanocages have emerged as a highly versatile and programmable class of delivery vectors. Considerable research has focused on repurposing and engineering various platforms, including virus-like particles, encapsulins, and ferritins, to serve as robust nanocarriers. Using innovative strategies, researchers have developed sophisticated cargo-loading mechanisms that have evolved from simple electrostatic attraction to high-specificity, affinity-based systems that mimic viral packaging. At the same time, a deeper understanding of how nucleic acids interact with the nanocage interior is revealing some key structural factors that drive efficient packaging. In this review, we highlight the key advances in the design and application of protein nanocages for nucleic acid delivery. We analyze the primary systems and their distinct loading mechanisms, discuss current challenges, and explore future directions to help establish engineered nanomaterials as a new and powerful category of medical therapies.Keywords: Protein nanocages, Nucleic acid delivery, Virus-like particle, MRNA therapeutics, Gene therapy

Introduction

The dawn of the twenty-first century has witnessed a paradigm shift in medicine, moving from traditional small-molecule drugs and biologics toward a new class of therapeutics based on nucleic acids. These powerful technologies range from gene-silencing agents, such as small interfering RNA (siRNA) and short hairpin RNA (shRNA), to gene-editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9, as well as messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines and therapeutics. Together, they hold the promise of treating and potentially curing even the most challenging diseases at their genetic source [1–3]. The great success of mRNA-based vaccines against COVID-19 has cast a global spotlight on the potential of this technology, highlighting its capacity for rapid development and deployment [4, 5]. At the same time, the arrival of sophisticated gene-editing tools offers the potential for precise, permanent correction of the genetic defects underlying a host of inherited disorders [6, 7]. These technologies have progressed from theoretical concepts to clinical realities, with gene therapies now advancing through trials for conditions ranging from cancer to genetic blindness and neurodegenerative diseases [1, 6, 7].

However, the full therapeutic potential of nucleic acids is fundamentally constrained by a single, formidable obstacle: delivery. By their very nature, nucleic acids are fragile, anionic macromolecules that are rapidly degraded by ubiquitous nucleases in the bloodstream and are incapable of passive diffusion across the lipid bilayer of cell membranes [1, 8]. Furthermore, using nucleic acids as therapeutics often requires precise delivery. For the treatment of glioblastoma multiforme, for example, a vector must traverse the highly restrictive blood–brain barrier (BBB) [9, 10]. For cancer therapy, a carrier must selectively target tumor cells while sparing healthy tissue to minimize toxicity [1, 11]. Moreover, the delivery of gene-editing machinery presents a unique safety challenge; while expression is required to edit, prolonged expression of nucleases like Cas9 can lead to cytotoxic and genotoxic side effects, including off-target mutations and unwanted immune responses [3, 12–14]. Therefore, the development of safe, transient, efficient, and specific delivery vectors is not merely a complement to nucleic acid therapy, it is the central issue upon which the entire field hinges.

While synthetic vectors, particularly lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), have proven highly effective in clinical use and represent a cornerstone of the field (most notably in approved mRNA vaccine systems) [15–17], the scientific community continues to explore alternative platforms that offer a distinct set of attributes. This focus on alternatives is driven not by any deficiency in lipid-based systems, but by the pursuit of platforms with different, and in some cases, more precisely controllable characteristics. A comprehensive search of the existing literature reveals a rapidly evolving landscape of potential options, with self-assembling nanocarriers that offer precise and highly controlled structures emerging as a major trend.

Protein nanocages have emerged as a promising alternative for nucleic acid delivery, owing to their exceptional versatility. These systems self-assemble into monodisperse protein shells with precisely defined architectures, typically exhibiting highly symmetric icosahedral or other polyhedral geometries [18]. Their protein-based nature confers inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability, and they lack the mechanism to self-replicate, which minimizes concerns about toxicity and long-term accumulation within the body [18, 19]. Moreover, both their exterior surfaces and internal cavities are highly adaptable to genetic and chemical engineering. This programmability is a critical advantage, enabling the tailored design of nanocages with finely optimized properties. For example, researchers can precisely engineer the outer surface to incorporate targeting ligands, antibodies, or peptides, directing the nanocage to specific cells or tissues while extending its circulation time in the body [20–23]. Simultaneously, the internal cavity can be customized to encapsulate a diverse range of cargo, including therapeutic drugs, imaging agents, enzymes, or nucleic acids. This dual-level control allows for the creation of truly multifunctional nanoplatforms that can perform complex tasks, such as targeted drug delivery combined with real-time imaging, or enzyme delivery for in situ biosynthesis [24–26]. In this review, we evaluate a diverse array of platforms, including virus-like particles (VLPs), encapsulins, ferritins, lumazine synthase (LS), and heat shock proteins (HSPs). We then analyze and summarize the most recent advances and key features of optimal delivery systems, including their architecture with a focus on loading mechanisms, and their applications in therapy, genome editing, and vaccinology.

Main Delivery Systems: A Critical Analysis of Ferritins, Encapsulins, Heat Shock Proteins, and Virus-Like Particles

Virus-Like Particles and Viral Vectors

The success of nucleic acid-based therapeutics, such as gene therapies and RNA-based vaccines, depends on the development of safe and efficient delivery systems. An ideal vector must protect its fragile cargo from degradation, evade immune surveillance, and deliver it specifically to target cells. Protein nanocages have emerged as a highly promising class of delivery platforms due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and remarkable versatility [11, 27–29]. In this context, virus-like particles (VLPs) represent the most broadly studied and extensively developed class of protein nanocages for delivery applications. These non-infectious nanoparticles self-assemble from viral structural proteins but lack any viral genetic material [22, 30]. Their well-defined, repetitive architecture makes them highly effective for a wide range of biomedical uses, including vaccination and advanced gene therapy.

Icosahedral symmetry is one of the most common structural modes of organization in viruses, characterized by a quasi-spherical capsid composed of repeating protein subunits. This geometric configuration offers exceptional structural stability and a multivalent, programmable surface that is well suited for functionalization. Icosahedral capsids appear in a wide range of viral families, including Adenoviridae, Bromoviridae, Hepadnaviridae, Parvoviridae, and Polyomaviridae, among others [1, 12, 31–35]. Their inherent specificity for packaging their own genetic material makes them ideal gene delivery platforms, as they can be rationally engineered to encapsulate functional nucleic acids, such as mRNA or DNA, for a specific biological function. This potential has been successfully exploited in prime-boost vaccination strategies combining mRNA and VLPs directed against the same antigen, which have been shown to elicit significantly higher and more durable neutralizing antibody responses compared to either component alone [36, 37].

VLPs derived from bacteriophages (e.g., MS2, Qβ, and P2) and plant viruses (Bromoviridae family) are among the most studied platforms due to their high-yield production in E. coli or plants, excellent stability, and lack of pre-existing immunity in humans [12, 20, 33, 34, 38–41]. A key structural feature enabling nucleic acid packaging in many of these systems is the presence of specific RNA stem-loop structures that act as packaging signals, recognized by the capsid protein during assembly [1, 39, 42]. This enables highly efficient and targeted in vivo packaging. For example, Qβ and MS2 VLPs, derived from single-stranded RNA bacteriophages, have icosahedral capsids with diameters of ∼ 30 nm, composed of 180 copies of a single coat protein (CP). In vivo packaging of target RNA into Qβ VLPs is facilitated by a hairpin structure derived from the Qβ RNA, which has a high affinity for its CP [34, 39]. Another example is the Cowpea Chlorotic Mottle Virus (CCMV) VLPs, which have icosahedral capsids with diameters of ∼28 nm, formed by 180 copies of the CP. Their assembly is pH-sensitive: disassembly occurs at neutral or high pH, while reassembly around RNA cargo occurs under acidic conditions, making them ideal for use as an RNA delivery vector [41, 43–47]. Similar to the CCMV, the Cucumber Mosaic Virus is another plant virus that has been used as a vaccine platform. Its ∼30 nm capsid has been engineered to incorporate a universal T-cell epitope derived from tetanus toxin, producing the vaccine candidate mCuMVTT-RBM. Used in combination with the approved mRNA-1273 vaccine in a heterologous prime-boost regimen, it demonstrated strong immunogenicity and flexibility, making it a valuable platform for optimizing the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of next-generation vaccine strategies [48–50]. Some of these “simpler” VLPs have proven to be exceptionally malleable, serving as versatile platforms for both internal cargo encapsulation and external surface display of antigens or targeting ligands. However, despite their strengths, they face several challenges. Due to their evolutionary origins, they often lack the intrinsic machinery needed to target mammalian cells and navigate through their cellular compartments; thus, they require surface modifications. The most common of these modifications aims to facilitate endosomal escape for efficient cargo release, or to alter tropism for improved targeting or redirection [51, 52]. Therefore, modifying their surface is essential for using these VLPs as gene delivery vectors in human cells. This is typically achieved by genetically fusing or chemically conjugating specific peptides or molecules to the capsid. Examples include folic acid on truncated hepatitis B virus core antigen (tHBcAg) VLPs, tumor-targeting peptides on JC polyomavirus (JCPyV) VLPs, and the GE11 peptide on the Macrobrachium rosenbergii nodavirus (MrNV) VLPs for EGFR-positive colorectal cancer cells [1, 31, 32, 52, 53].

Moving beyond simple, single-protein capsids producible in bacterial systems, more complex eukaryotic VLPs have been engineered to carry out more sophisticated functions. One such system is based on Murine Leukemia Virus (MLV) VLPs. The wild-type MLV forms icosahedral capsids approximately 85–105 nm in diameter, with complete viral capsid assembly occurring exclusively at the plasma membrane. However, expression of the Gag polyprotein alone in eukaryotic cells is sufficient to drive the assembly of VLPs [36, 54]. MLV-derived VLPs have been adapted to create a novel ribonucleoprotein (RNP) delivery platform called “Nanoblades,” which are produced by transfection in human 293 T cells. In this system, the Cas9 gene-editing protein is fused directly to the C-terminus of the structural Gag protein. This design yields a sophisticated, all-in-one particle capable of packaging and delivering the complete Cas9, along with its own single guide RNA (sgRNA). It enables rapid, transient, and transgene-free genome editing across a wide range of target cells [55, 56]. Further advances have led to the development of engineered VLPs (eVLPs), derived from the nanoblade system through systematic structural evolution. Specific mutations in the capsid protein were introduced to improve packaging efficiency for large RNPs over native viral genomes, demonstrating that capsid structure can be fine-tuned for highly specialized delivery tasks [6–8, 57].

Another system is the VLPs derived from Rabies virus (RABV), a negative-sense RNA virus with a genome of ~ 12 kb, encoding five structural proteins: nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), large polymerase protein (L), matrix protein (M), and glycoprotein (G). The N, P, and L proteins form the nucleocapsid, while the M and G proteins make up the envelope. RABV VLPs for mRNA vaccines are produced by co-expressing G and M proteins. This multi-component design enables stabilization of the G protein in its prefusion conformation, significantly enhancing induction of strong and durable neutralizing antibody responses [37]. A different system utilizes chimeric VLPs created by fusing the Vesicular Stomatitis Virus G protein (VSV-G) with the archaeal ribosomal protein L7Ae. L7Ae recognizes specific secondary structures in RNA known as kink-turn motifs. The VSVG-L7Ae protein is co-expressed in producer cells along with the desired mRNA, which is then packaged into the VLPs. This platform serves as a transient transgene delivery tool, demonstrating high transduction efficacy in hard-to-transfect cell lines, such as induced pluripotent stem cells and monocytes [58, 59].

Further diverging from traditional VLPs, recombinant viruses or viral vectors can be classified into a “special” category. These are worth mentioning because, although they are more complex and deviate slightly from the classical definition of a protein nanocage, they represent the ultimate goal in delivery system design: an all-in-one, programmable platform capable of integrating multiple advanced features. These include in vivo nucleic acid self-recognition and loading, high tissue-targeting specificity, the ability to express and regulate nucleic acids within the cell, and most importantly, no potential to become infectious. The last point remains a significant concern, even for the engineered viruses discussed below.

This category includes more complex systems like those derived from adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), adenoviruses (AdV), and retroviruses (lentivirus and others), among others. AAVs are a leading platform for in vivo gene therapy, with several AAV-based therapeutics having already gained regulatory approval for treating human diseases [10, 21, 36]. Adeno-associated viral vectors are structurally simple, featuring a non-enveloped icosahedral capsid (~ 25 nm) composed of three types of structural proteins: VP1, VP2, and VP3 [36]. AAVs are replication-deficient parvoviruses, which have traditionally required co-infection with a helper adenovirus or herpes virus to achieve efficient infection [60]. Their capsid is a highly engineerable component; AAVs have evolved through high-throughput in vivo selection to create novel capsids with enhanced tissue specificity, which reduces off-target organ transduction and thus improves their safety and targeting precision [21]. However, their clinical utility is often challenged by their limited cargo capacity and, in some cases, by pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against common AAV serotypes in the human population [60]. AdV are non-enveloped, icosahedral viruses with a linear, double-stranded DNA genome spanning 26 to 45 kb. Similar to AAV, AdVs do not integrate into the host genome and are highly efficient for gene delivery across various cell types. According to their specific serological type, these viruses can bind to specific cell surface proteins, facilitating their entry into target cells [61, 62]. Because of this, along with their well-characterized biology, high genetic stability, large transgene capacity (up to 36 kb), and ease of large-scale production, AdVs were among the first DNA viruses developed for therapeutic use [60, 63, 64]. As a gene delivery vector, the high efficiency of AdV has led to the approval of several protocols for clinical trials and even for commercial use [60, 65]. Although generally safe, a significant limitation of using adenoviral vectors in clinical trials is the rapid induction of an inflammatory response against these infectious particles. In some cases, such as vaccination, inflammation can be beneficial, but in others, like gene therapy, it may be detrimental [66, 67].

Finally, retroviruses are encapsulated reverse-transcribing molecular parasites with single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) genomes. These RNA viruses generate a double-stranded DNA copy of their genome during replication in host cells. Retroviruses have significantly influenced human evolution and the divergence of hominids. In vertebrate genomes, sequences from human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs) have integrated over evolutionary time. The classical structure of HERVs comprises an internal region with four genes: gag, pro, pol, and env. These are flanked by two long terminal repeats, which are non-coding but serve key regulatory functions. The gag gene encodes a structural polyprotein for the capsid, nucleocapsid, and matrix; the pro gene encodes the protease; the pol gene encodes reverse transcriptase and integrase; and the env gene encodes the viral envelope protein. Among retroviruses, the lentivirus system is the most extensively studied and well characterized to date [68–70]. Lentiviruses belong to the Orthoretrovirinae subfamily within the Retroviridae family. They originate from the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) and serve as powerful genetic tools, offering several advantages over other viral vector systems. The main advantage of lentivirus is its ability to integrate genetic material into the genomes of both dividing and nondividing host cells, both in vitro and in vivo. They are structurally more complex, possessing a lipid envelope decorated with fusogenic glycoproteins that mediate cell entry [51, 71, 72]. This envelope is a critical structural component that can be engineered with different glycoproteins to alter cellular tropism, enabling targeted delivery [7, 44]. While highly efficient, their production in mammalian cells remains a significant bottleneck due to scalability challenges and high costs. Both AAVs and LVs are being re-engineered for transient, “hit-and-run” delivery, blurring the lines between conventional vectors and VLP-based systems.

Engineered lentiviruses have been developed to deliver Cas9 as an mRNA instead of a DNA transgene. This approach was shown to be highly efficacious in a mouse model of wet age-related macular degeneration, achieving significant gene knockout with no detectable anti-Cas9 immunity, a major improvement over AAV-based strategies [10]. These engineered LVs can also be programmed with specific tropisms; for instance, by altering the envelope glycoproteins, particles can be targeted to specific immune cells, such as CD4 + T cells, while avoiding others [51, 73]. Another retrovirus-based system, a novel mRNA delivery vector known as SEND (Selective Endogenous Encapsidation for cellular Delivery), utilizes the domesticated retrotransposon-derived protein PEG10 to package, secrete, and deliver specific mRNAs [9, 74]. The PEG10 gene, essential for placental development, encodes PEG10-RF1, a Gag homolog protein, and PEG10-RF1/2, representing a fusion of the Gag and Pol domains through a programmed ribosomal frameshift [75, 76]. The SEND system consists of three elements: the structural protein PEG10, a cargo mRNA, and a fusogen protein that enables attachment and internalization into target cells. PEG10 assembles the VLP by encapsulating its own mRNA. The fusogen protein, such as the VSV-G envelope protein or the endogenous fusogen Syncytin-A, decorates the VLP surface. Once formed, VLPs exit the producer cell and attach to another cell (called the recipient cell) via the fusogen and deliver the mRNA to the recipient cell. As a modular and fully endogenous system, SEND offers a minimally immunogenic platform for repeated dosing, broadening the potential of nucleic acid-based therapies.

Ferritin and Encapsulin: Repurposing Natural Storage Compartments

Ferritins and encapsulins are naturally occurring protein nanocages that typically encapsulate molecular cargo such as small molecules or enzymes. Their intrinsic stability and hollow architecture make them attractive platforms for delivering drugs or nucleic acids. Ferritins are highly stable iron-storage nanocompartments found in humans and various other organisms, where they play a fundamental role in regulating iron homeostasis. Structurally, ferritin resembles viral particles and consists of 24 protein subunits that self-assemble into a spherical hollow nanocage. These nanocages form spontaneously, with an outer diameter of approximately 12 nm and an internal cavity measuring about 8 nm in diameter. Owing to these structural features, ferritin has demonstrated significant potential as a versatile vaccine platform in both preclinical and clinical settings [77, 78].

This versatility also extends to therapeutic delivery, including both small-molecule drugs and nucleic acids. Human H-chain ferritin (HFn), for instance, has the intrinsic ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) by interacting with the transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), which is highly expressed in both brain endothelial cells and tumor cells [79–81]. This property has been exploited to deliver therapeutics to notoriously inaccessible glioblastomas. To enhance this natural tropism and improve drug loading, HFn has been engineered in several ways. One study developed an HFn variant displaying an integrin α2β1-targeting peptide, which not only improved tumor targeting but also dramatically increased the loading capacity for the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin [79]. Moreover, these nanocompartments have also been optimized for the delivery of nucleic acids. The naturally negatively charged inner surface of HFn has been modified by mutating some residues to cationic arginines. This HFn + variant showed significantly higher encapsulation efficiency for siRNA and effectively suppressed gene expression in glioma cells in vivo [80, 81]. Beyond cancer, the low immunogenicity of apoferritin has been leveraged to deliver siRNA and microRNAs to various cell types, including primary immune cells, without triggering an immune response, highlighting its potential for treating inflammatory diseases [4, 82, 83].

Distinct from viral particles or ferritins, encapsulins are a class of protein nanocompartments native to bacteria and archaea that have emerged as a highly versatile platform for nanomedicine and biotechnology [13, 25]. These self-assembling icosahedral nanocompartments are structurally similar to some bacteriophage capsids. The fold of the encapsulin monomer closely resembles that of the HK97 bacteriophage, which contributes to its stability and ability to self-assemble. Encapsulins function as specialized metabolic organelles by sequestering ferritin-like proteins, DyP-type peroxidases, and other cargo proteins, all of which play a crucial role in regulating cellular homeostasis and mitigating oxidative stress [84–87]. Their inherent robustness, biocompatibility, and unique mode of cargo loading have made them a focal point of protein engineering efforts. This cargo loading is directed by short C-terminal sequences known as targeting peptides (TPs) or cargo-loading peptides (CLPs), which are genetically fused to the cargo protein [23, 84]. These peptides mediate the selective packaging of cargo into encapsulins, relying on a highly conserved sequence whose function is mutation-sensitive due to precise ionic, hydrophobic, and shape-complementary interactions at the binding site. These features position encapsulins as versatile candidates for the development of nanoscale delivery systems, particularly for protein and nucleic acid-based therapeutics [87, 88].

Encapsulins exhibit remarkable structural diversity across different microbial species, forming shells of varying size and complexity, ranging from 20 to 42 nm in diameter. The encapsulin from Thermotoga maritima is one of the most extensively studied proteins, assembling into relatively small, 24 nm-diameter cages composed of 60 subunits. Its primary function is to encapsulate a dye-decolorizing peroxidase, protecting the cell from reactive oxygen species. In contrast, the encapsulin from Myxococcus xanthus forms larger and more complex cages composed of 180 subunits of the EncA protein (32 nm in diameter) [5, 23, 84, 86]. This system naturally encapsulates ferritin-like proteins (EncB and EncC) to create a massive iron-storage compartment, crucial for managing iron toxicity and oxidative stress [84, 89]. Another example is the encapsulin from Quasibacillus thermotolerans, which forms a significantly larger shell measuring 42 nm in diameter, composed of 240 subunits. It has only one type of cargo protein, representing a distinct class of ferritin-like proteins with an unusual ferroxidase center. Q. thermotolerans encapsulin forms large, electron-dense encapsulins enriched in iron (Fe) and phosphorus (P), which function as storage reservoirs [85]. This structural and functional diversity highlights the evolutionary adaptability of the encapsulin system, providing a rich pool of distinct scaffolds that can be utilized for various bioengineering applications, ranging from targeted drug delivery to the creation of advanced nanoreactors.

A key advantage of the encapsulin platform is the capacity for simultaneous, yet independent, modification of its interior and exterior surfaces, enabling the creation of highly sophisticated, dual-function nanoparticles. Additionally, the native cargo-loading mechanism, which relies on the specific recognition of the CLP by the encapsulin’s internal surface, can easily be exploited to package heterologous molecules of interest [13, 84, 86]. These modular features have been strategically leveraged to engineer multifunctional particles for diverse biomedical applications. One example is the development of a multi-component vaccine candidate, where an encapsulin from T. maritima was engineered to display an influenza virus epitope (M2e) on its surface while simultaneously packaging a fluorescent reporter protein (GFP) within its core. Immunization with these particles elicited antibody responses against both the external antigen and the internal cargo, demonstrating their potential for generating complex, multi-component immune responses [5]. Similarly, this modularity has been applied to targeted cancer therapy by displaying a DARPin (an antibody mimic that targets HER2) on the surface, while loading the cytotoxic enzyme MiniSOG as a cargo, creating a targeted drug delivery system that is assembled in a single step in vivo [23]. This same modularity has enabled the use of encapsulins for packaging nucleic acids. Building on the principles of independent surface modification and programmable cargo loading, researchers have extended the encapsulin platform beyond proteins to include other biologically relevant molecules, such as RNA. In a comparable strategy, encapsulins were engineered by fusing the lysine-rich N-terminal peptide of the DNA-binding protein from starved cells (Dps) to the shell protomers. This modification increases the positive charge within the lumen, promoting electrostatic attraction of polyanionic nucleic acids during in vivo assembly. The resulting Dps-functionalized encapsulins were able to package functional RNAs up to approximately 3 kb in length while protecting their integrity from nuclease degradation. These findings highlight the potential of encapsulins as versatile, programmable, and structurally stable systems for therapeutic nucleic acid delivery in advanced biomedical applications [90].

Other Nanocompartment Systems: Heat Shock Proteins and Lumazine Synthase

Beyond the more extensively studied viral and storage proteins, the field of bionanotechnology has repurposed other self-assembling protein architectures for the therapeutic delivery of nucleic acids and other biomolecular cargo. Among these, small heat shock proteins (HSPs) and the bacterial enzyme lumazine synthase (LS) have helped to expand the repertoire of protein nanocompartments. Each system exhibits unique structural and functional characteristics shaped by its distinct biological origin, making them suitable for specific biomedical applications.

HSPs are molecular chaperones that naturally form dynamic, cage-like oligomers to protect other cellular proteins under conditions of environmental stress. This intrinsic protective role has been adapted to develop nanocarriers for fragile therapeutic molecules. As a non-viral system, HSPs offer a significant advantage in their potentially reduced immunogenicity, which is a key consideration for therapies requiring repeated administration [91–93]. One notable example is the small heat shock protein Hsp16.5 from Methanococcus jannaschii, which self-assembles into a hollow, spherical nanocage composed of 24 identical subunits, with an outer diameter of 12 nm and an internal cavity ranging from 6.5 to 9 nm in diameter. These nanocages feature 3 nm pores that enable the passive diffusion of small molecules while maintaining their structural integrity under harsh conditions, including a wide pH range (5–11) and elevated temperatures (up to 70 °C). Due to these characteristics, HSP nanocages have been developed as delivery vectors for various therapeutic and diagnostic agents, including anticancer drugs, photosensitizers, imaging agents, and siRNAs [91, 94].

Studies have demonstrated that HSP nanocages can effectively condense and protect siRNAs, facilitating their delivery into tumor cells to silence target genes, such as those coding for the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), and trigger apoptosis [94]. To overcome non-specific uptake, HSPs have been extensively engineered with surface modifications. The addition of peptides, such as the trans-activator of transcription, enhances cellular entry and lysosomal escape, while targeting ligands like Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) or the cyclic peptide iRGD direct the nanocages to specific integrins overexpressed on colorectal or pancreatic cancer cells [94–96]. Beyond nucleic acids, these engineered HSPs have been successfully loaded with proapoptotic peptides (KLAK) or small-molecule hydrophobic drugs, significantly enhancing the cytotoxic effect on target cancer cells compared to the free drug [28, 95]. Furthermore, stimuli-responsive HSP-based systems have been developed that respond to the acidic and enzyme-rich tumor microenvironment, enabling site-specific cargo release and improved therapeutic outcomes [96].

Lumazine synthase (LS) is a family of enzymes involved in the penultimate step in the biosynthesis of riboflavin. In certain prokaryotes, including Bacillus subtilis and Aquifex aeolicus, LS can self-assemble into hollow icosahedral structures composed of 60 identical subunits, with an external diameter of approximately 15 nm and an internal diameter of 9 nm. Among these, LS from A. aeolicus (AaLS), a hyperthermophilic bacterium, has attracted particular interest due to its exceptional thermal and structural stability. The morphology and functional versatility of these spherical assemblies, especially those formed by AaLS, have been extensively reviewed [97–99].

Due to its robust architecture and high tolerance for genetic modification, AaLS has been re-engineered as a protein-based nanocarrier for nucleic acid encapsulation [24, 97, 99]. The primary strategy employed for RNA packaging involves electrostatic engineering, in which positively charged mutations are introduced into the interior of the cage. This “supercharged” cationic lumen can attract and encapsulate RNA in vivo [99, 100]. However, later studies revealed that RNA loading is not governed solely by charge. Instead, RNA flexibility and secondary structure also play significant roles, suggesting that encapsulation is facilitated through a more nuanced mechanism involving shape complementarity and molecular fit [101].

Building on this concept, AaLS has been transformed from a simple nanocontainer into a sophisticated artificial nucleocapsid. Hilvert and colleagues recently reported a circularly permuted AaLS variant, focusing on incorporating high-specificity RNA–protein interactions, and structurally remodeling the native cage to enable selective RNA encapsulation. By combining rational design with iterative evolution, AaLS was repurposed into a platform that mimics key features of natural viral capsids, including signal-mediated assembly and enhanced cargo protection [24, 99, 102]. This work not only establishes a programmable platform for RNA delivery but also offers a compelling molecular model for the evolutionary transition from cellular proteins to primitive viral capsids. Together, LS and HSPs demonstrate the vast potential of re-engineered natural protein architectures as customizable, non-viral nanocompartments for advanced therapeutic applications.

All these platforms, sourced from all domains of life, have been repurposed for therapeutic delivery due to their shared ability to self-assemble into stable, hollow architectures. Despite this common feature, they exhibit a remarkable diversity in their size, subunit composition, and biological origin. Comparative Analysis of Loading Mechanisms in Protein Nanocages

The viability of protein nanocages as vectors for gene therapy, vaccination, and other biotechnological applications is critically dependent on their ability to efficiently and selectively encapsulate nucleic acid cargo. The broad diversity of natural and engineered nanocage systems has given rise to a parallel diversity in loading strategies, which can be broadly classified by the primary physicochemical forces driving encapsulation and the context in which loading occurs, either through in vitro assembly or in vivo co-expression. Electrostatic Interactions: A Foundational, Charge-Driven Approach

The most basic mechanism for nucleic acid encapsulation leverages the inherent physicochemical properties of its components: the negatively charged phosphate backbone of RNA and DNA, and the tunable charge of a protein nanocage’s luminal surface. A strong electrostatic attraction can be established by genetically engineering the interior of a nanocage to present a high density of positive charges, thereby driving the encapsulation of polyanionic nucleic acids. This “charge complementarity” approach has been successfully implemented across various protein nanocage platforms, including encapsulins, lumazine synthase, and ferritins.

Comparable mechanisms are observed in nature; for example, single-stranded RNA viruses use ARMs on their capsid proteins to establish electrostatic interactions with their negatively charged RNA genomes. For example, CCMV VLPs have been disassembled to remove cellular RNA and subsequently reassembled in vitro to encapsulate heterologous self-amplifying replicon RNAs via their ARMs. These reconstituted particles can then be delivered to dendritic cells, where they enhance mRNA uptake and replication [83, 107]. The same idea has also been applied in non-viral systems. For example, encapsulins have been engineered for RNA packaging in vivo by fusing the E. coli Dps-N peptide (MSTAKLVKSKATN), a lysine-rich sequence, to the N-terminus of the encapsulin protomer. The resulting positive charges on the nanocage interior drive non-specific RNA encapsulation via electrostatic interactions [86]. By modifying the luminal surface, an engineered variant of the AaLS capsid, termed AaLS-pos, was designed with a positively supercharged interior, enabling it to encapsulate a mixture of cellular RNAs when expressed in bacteria [100].

Ferritin nanocages, particularly HFn, are a widely explored platform for delivering nucleic acids. Traditionally, HFn has been used for siRNA encapsulation via acid-induced disassembly and reassembly. Under acidic conditions, the cage disassembles and later reforms at neutral pH, entrapping nucleic acid cargo during the process [3, 79, 80, 82, 92, 108–110]. Building on this strategy, the group led by Xue-Qing Zhang enhanced the RNA loading capacity of HFn by genetically engineering its internal cavity to carry a net positive charge. This modification significantly enhanced electrostatic interactions between the inner surface of the nanocage and RNA molecules, thereby improving the loading efficiency of therapeutic siRNAs and other nucleic acids [81]. While similar in concept to charge-based strategies used in other protein nanocages, such as CCMV and encapsulins, this approach relies on introducing specific positive point mutations rather than incorporating an ARM, offering an alternative for enhancing RNA affinity. Another group employed a completely different approach by pre-incubating siRNA with iron before loading it into HFn, which also increased the loading efficiency. Notably, upon delivery to endosomal compartments, the iron facilitated lysosomal escape of the encapsulated siRNA [109].

HSP nanocages also utilize the charge complementarity principle, but instead of luminal encapsulation, their external surface has been functionalized with cationic peptides, such as poly-arginine (R9) or Tat peptides. These positively charged motifs then facilitate the electrostatic adsorption of siRNA to the nanocage surface, creating a stable complex for delivery [92–94]. Although the HSP structure is similar to that of HFn, little is known about its disassembly and reassembly. This lack of understanding may explain why these proteins have, to date, only been surface-modified. Nevertheless, there remains potential for further exploration. Notably, the small HSP from the cyanophage appears promising, as it forms one of the largest HSP nanocages with proper icosahedral symmetry (60 subunits) [91]. Collectively, these electrostatic loading strategies provide a solid foundation for designing protein nanocages as nucleic acid delivery systems. However, although straightforward and effective for achieving bulk loading, a primary limitation of electrostatic-driven encapsulation is its inherent lack of specificity. Systems such as the Dps-N encapsulins or AaLS-pos tend to sequester a heterogeneous population of host-cell RNAs alongside the intended cargo, which can complicate purification and limit their use as a nucleic acid delivery vector [90, 100]. Furthermore, this mechanism offers little control over the number of encapsulated molecules, often resulting in a polydisperse product with variable loading stoichiometries.

Highly Specific Systems: Engineering Sequence-Specific Recognition

To overcome the limitations of electrostatic attraction, researchers have developed sophisticated loading mechanisms based on high-affinity, specific molecular recognition systems. [81, 86, 92, 99]. These systems typically pair a short RNA sequence or structural motif with its complementary RNA-binding peptide that is genetically fused to the interior surface of the nanocage protomer. Similar to viral ARMs, these complementary recognition systems also exist in nature, where they are found predominantly in the non-coding RNA sequences of viruses and riboswitches [111, 112]. Independent of its natural counterparts, this same concept was developed de novo to create synthetic RNA molecules in vitro, known as aptamers. Aptamers are short, single-stranded nucleic acid sequences that fold into unique three-dimensional structures, enabling them to bind specific molecular targets with high affinity and specificity, similar to antibodies [113]. Unlike naturally evolved binding molecules, aptamers are generated in vitro through an iterative selection process known as SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment) [114, 115]. This complementary system allows the nanocage to selectively package only those nucleic acids that bear the correct targeting “zip code.”

Taking advantage of these naturally occurring complementary systems, Hung-Wei Yang and coworkers developed a bioengineered Qβ bacteriophage to treat radioresistant glioblastomas. They designed a novel three-way junction (b-3WJ) RNA scaffold capable of carrying two distinct RNAi sequences for the simultaneous silencing of multiple genes. An innovative feature of this scaffold is a “broccoli” light-up aptamer, which enables real-time monitoring of RNA cleavage within cells using fluorescence microscopy. In this system, specific encapsulation is driven by the interaction between the Qβ capsid protein (QβCP) and a native RNA motif, known as the QβCP binding hairpin, which is inserted at the 5’ end of the RNA. This hairpin serves as a recognition signal, guiding capsid assembly around the RNA for efficient packaging [116]. A similar strategy involves the cationic λN + peptide, engineered from the λ-phage antitermination complex, which binds an RNA stem-loop called BoxBr, a variant of the original BoxB. This peptide was fused to the N-terminus of a modified AaLS via circular permutation (λcpAaLS). The 5′- and 3′-UTRs of the same λcpAaLS gene were equipped with the BoxBr packaging signal [24, 102]. The introduction of the λN + peptides modified the arrangement of the 16-nm wild-type nanocompartment, resulting in spherical structures with diameters ranging from 27 to 29 nm. To improve the RNA packaging capabilities of λcpAaLS capsids, a two-step directed evolution approach was used. This process led to the accumulation of mutations that likely induced structural changes, thereby restricting nuclease access to the capsid lumen [24]. However, even after direct evolution, only ~ 10% of the resulting particles contained the full-length target RNA, underscoring the difficulties associated with packaging and protecting nucleic acids in a cell. To overcome this, Hilvert’s group (2021) tried a different approach in which a circularly permuted AaLS variant [117] was first evolved through intermediates by selecting variants that capture capsid-encoding mRNA transcripts flanked by BoxB tags. To improve the RNA packaging properties of the resulting nucleocapsid variant, NC-3, its gene was mutagenized using error-prone PCR. The resulting library was subjected to three cycles of expression, purification, and nuclease challenge, followed by re-amplification of the surviving mRNA. The best variant, NC-4, contained nine new mutations that rearranged the subunits into a 240-subunit icosahedral capsid impermeable to nucleases. This variant also acquired mutations in its mRNA, including a robust display of a high-affinity packaging signal within a cassette of lower-affinity motifs. This finding suggests that NC-4 similarly evolved a key hallmark of viral RNA packaging: signal-mediated assembly [102].

In a separate study, Finn and coworkers modified the wild-type M. xanthus encapsulin structural protein (Enc) to enable RNA packaging in encapsulins via sequence-specific interactions. They inserted a hexahistidine tag onto the N-terminus of the Enc gene, creating the HEnc variant. This modification enabled non-specific packaging of host-cell RNA during recombinant protein expression, including the 23S and 16S rRNAs, previously observed by Kwon and Giessen [90]. To achieve sequence-specific RNA capture, a 37-nucleotide RNA aptamer (Shot47sss), which binds polyhistidine tags with picomolar affinity, was genetically encoded into the 5′ or 3′ UTR of the HEnc mRNA. Variants were created containing one or two copies of this aptamer. Real-time quantitative PCR revealed that while all polyhistidine-tagged variants packaged their own coding mRNA, the HEnc variant with two aptamers captured significantly more. Interestingly, this targeted approach also enhanced the co-packaging of non-specific host-cell mRNAs, though at much lower levels. All engineered encapsulins formed particles with T = 1 and T = 3 symmetry, morphologically similar to the wild type. While this method successfully packaged the target RNA without altering particle morphology, the study noted that the overall amount of packaged RNA remained significantly lower than that achieved by other VLP systems, such as PP7 [22]. In the context of recombinant viruses, the aptamer-based concept has also been adapted to several systems. A modular and precise loading mechanism has been developed using a lentiviral system, termed mLP-CRISPR, which simultaneously delivers mRNA encoding one of the longest Cas9 proteins (SpCas9) and its sgRNA. The lentiviral nucleocapsid protein is a domain of the immature Gag polyprotein, which contains two zinc finger motifs that mediate the packaging of the viral genomic RNA into virions. The bound genomic RNA serves as a scaffold for Gag multimerization and is crucial for particle assembly [118].

Therefore, to develop this modular lentivirus that encapsidates heterologous mRNAs specifically over cytoplasmic RNAs, Cai’s group fused the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein (MS2C) to the lentiviral Gag polyprotein and inserted the corresponding MS2 stem-loop into the target mRNA. The high-affinity interaction between the Gag-MS2C fusion and the MS2 stem-loop drives the selective packaging of the therapeutic cargo. A single MS2C monomer fused to the N-terminus of Gag was found to be the most efficient. Packaging efficiency also depended on the number of stem-loop copies in the mRNA. Side-by-side comparisons showed that increasing repeats progressively enhanced mRNA transfer; however, balancing efficiency against plasmid instability and reduced cargo space led to the selection of six stem-loop repeats. This approach enables packaging of up to 100 copies of therapeutic mRNAs into each assembling particle [10]. Using an alternative strategy, the entire Cas9 RNP complex was loaded into the lentivirus by replacing the tetraloop of the sgRNA scaffold with a ‘com’ aptamer. In this way, the sgRNA itself becomes the handle for packaging, and, as it is already bound to the Cas9 protein, the specific Gag–aptamer interaction pulls the complete, functional RNP into the lentiviral VLP. This approach has the advantage of enabling rapid, transient gene editing with reduced off-target activity, while minimizing non-specific encapsulation of host RNAs and producing more homogeneous, functionally defined delivery vehicles [57].

Beyond aptamer-mediated interactions, some strategies integrate a nucleic acid-binding protein into the inner surface of the protein nanocage. One prime example, MLV Nanoblades, has a Cas9 fused to the C-terminus of the retroviral Gag protein such that the therapeutic protein is an integral part of the viral particle itself. As Gag proteins self-assemble to form the VLP, they inherently carry the Cas9 protein along with its associated sgRNA. This approach results in the efficient, transgene-free packaging of the entire pre-formed Cas9/sgRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, ready for immediate action upon delivery to a target cell. This mechanism bypasses the need for a specific packaging signal on the nucleic acid, instead treating the therapeutic protein as a structural component [36].

In contrast, other systems exploit the virus’s natural genomic packaging machinery. In the parainfluenza virus (PIV5-L) vector, the viral genome is engineered to replace structural genes with therapeutic genes. Loading begins when the viral nucleocapsid protein recognizes and encapsidates this modified genomic RNA, forming a protective helical ribonucleoprotein structure that serves as the delivery core. Each N-protomer binds six nucleotides and extends into the pockets of its N + 1 and N − 1 neighbors. The RNA-binding pocket’s structure and amino acid sequence identity are highly conserved among paramyxoviruses and can be divided into two subpockets [119]. Encapsidation occurs co-transcriptionally, shielding the genome from nucleases and providing a template for transcription and replication, thereby leveraging the virus’s efficient genome-packaging process to load therapeutic RNA [120].

Similarly, alphaviruses and flaviviruses, often deployed as RNA replicon vectors, employ complex regulatory mechanisms for the selective packaging of RNA. RNA replicons from single-stranded RNA viruses are attractive for vaccine development due to the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) activity, which generates ~ 10⁶ copies per cell after subgenomic RNA enters the cytoplasm [44, 73, 107]. In alphaviruses, the capsid protein binds a proposed packaging signal, although infectious particle formation can occur without it; instead, multiple enriched RNA sites are recognized [121, 122]. Flavivirus assembly is even more intricate, coupling packaging with replication at the endoplasmic reticulum and requiring non-structural proteins such as NS2A and the NS2B–NS3 protease complex. This multi-component regulation ensures high-fidelity assembly but restricts its production to a few eukaryotic cell lines, limiting scalability compared with simpler, re-engineered protein nanocages [73, 123].

Structural Factors that Determine the Loading of Nucleic Acids

Although electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions are the primary driving forces behind encapsulation, the process’s efficiency depends on a complex network of structural factors that involve both the genetic material and the capsid. For example, studies with Qβ VLPs have shown that RNA topology is a critical factor, with intrinsically compact RNAs being packaged more efficiently than unstructured and elongated molecules [39]. The presence of specific sequence elements also influences efficiency; while particular aptamers are essential in affinity-based systems, other motifs, such as the ribosome binding site (RBS) or the Broccoli fluorogenic aptamer, have been shown to hinder encapsulation, likely due to their structural properties or interactions with host factors [39, 101]. The relationship between cargo and capsid is, in fact, bidirectional: excessive cargo can impose steric constraints that result in the formation of aberrant or distorted capsids, as observed in Myxococcus xanthus encapsulin systems [86].

Studies with AaLS-pos, the positively supercharged variant of AaLS, have identified additional factors that determine RNA encapsulation. The limited internal volume of protein nanocages imposes a physical limitation on the size of the RNA that can be encapsulated. Molecules larger than 400 nucleotides are excluded from AaLS-pos; this length also defines the ratio between the RNA’s negative charge and the capsid’s internal positive charge, which influences binding affinity. The nucleotide sequence is equally critical: even with the same length and charge, variations of up to 200-fold in encapsulation yield have been observed, indicating that base composition alters internal interactions and capsid selectivity [101].

mRNA secondary structure also plays a significant role: less stable and more flexible RNAs can better adapt to the internal volume and maximize electrostatic interactions, whereas rigid structures, such as the Broccoli-F30 aptamer, reduce efficiency. Similarly, shape complementarity between the RNA and the capsid lumen promotes packaging when a suitable three-dimensional fit exists, but hinders it when geometry is incompatible. Competition with endogenous host RNA also limits the proportion of capsids containing the RNA of interest, even at high expression levels, as the relationship between expression and encapsulation is not always direct [24, 101].

The architecture of the capsid itself is another determining factor. Features such as the precise distribution of positive charges on its inner surface, the size and plasticity of its pores (reduced in some designs to ~ 2.5 nm to protect genetic material from nucleases), and rapid, coordinated assembly kinetics are crucial for efficient encapsulation and for protecting the genetic material [102, 124]. Structural stability, reinforced through strategies such as domain swapping between subunits, is also fundamental for nanocage integrity [102].

Taken together, these studies show us that nucleic acid encapsulation depends not only on electrostatic interactions, but on a complex network of factors including RNA size, sequence, stability and flexibility, shape complementarity, competition with other nucleic acids, in addition to capsid architecture and stability, pore size and control, charge distribution, and assembly kinetics. An integrated optimization of all these elements will be essential for developing more powerful and safer nucleic acid delivery platforms.

Loading Context: In Vitro Reassembly vs. In Vivo Encapsulation

While the choice of nanocage platform and its nucleic acid loading mechanism defines its intrinsic packaging capabilities, an equally critical factor is how and where the nucleic acid cargo is loaded. The loading process can occur under two distinct conditions: in vitro reassembly or in vivo encapsulation. In vitro loading typically involves the disassembly of the protein nanocontainer during the downstream processing. For example, in vivo-assembled VLPs are typically disassembled to remove encapsulated impurities and improve particle morphology, then they are reassembled by incubation with the nucleic acid cargo. In this case, disassembly is often achieved using a high-pH solution combined with a denaturant or reducing agent [125, 126]. This method has been widely used for human apoferritin, which can be disassembled at pH 2.0, mixed with siRNA or miRNA, and efficiently reassembled at neutral pH [4, 82, 83]. Cowpea chlorotic mottle virus (CCMV) VLPs can also utilize this approach and have been successfully loaded with different types of RNAs for therapeutic applications [40, 41, 43–46, 107]. The primary advantage of in vitro assembly is the ability to use purified components and load chemically modified or synthetic nucleic acids that cannot be produced in a cellular context. However, there is also the disadvantage that only a few protein nanocompartments can withstand this process without complications or even aggregation of their subunits. In addition, this adds an extra step to the production of the final product. To circumvent these issues, a low-volume, high-resolution screening method was recently developed to efficiently monitor and determine optimal conditions for cross-flow diafiltration VLP disassembly, thereby accelerating downstream process development [125, 127].

In contrast, in vivo packaging occurs concurrently with the synthesis of the nanocage proteins within a host expression system. In this scenario, the cage self-assembles around the nucleic acid cargo present in the cellular environment. This is the native mechanism for many viruses and has been used for numerous engineered systems, including recombinant viruses, Qβ VLPs, and encapsulins, among others [5, 22, 24, 30, 39, 89, 102]. The Cowpea mosaic virus (CPMV) provides a highly sophisticated example of this, employing a replication-dependent mechanism for specific RNA packaging in plants. To accomplish RNA packing, the CPMV replication machinery, along with the coat protein and the RNA of interest, was co-expressed, ensuring that only the replicated target RNA was efficiently packaged into VLPs. To incorporate a specific RNA molecule, the DNA sequence corresponding to the RNA of interest is cloned into an RNA-2 expression cassette. This cassette is then co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana along with two other components: RNA-1, which encodes the viral replication machinery, and the VP60 coat protein, resulting in custom RNA-loaded VLPs [128].

Another relevant example is the SEND system, a fascinating example of an endogenous, in vivo RNA packaging and delivery mechanism in mammalian cells. The PEG10 structural protein has an intrinsic ability to bind and encapsulate its own mRNA in a selective manner. It contains two putative nucleic acid-binding domains, NC and RT, released upon PEG10 self-processing [129]. Segel and coworkers generated deletions of these domains and found that mRNA export depends on the PEG10 NC, as loss of the zinc finger CCHC motif (residues 416–429) substantially reduced export of its mRNA. However, both NC and RT domains are required for PEG10 to bind these mRNAs. ​​They also found that PEG10 strongly binds to the 5′ UTR, with additional binding near the boundary between the nucleocapsid and protease coding sequences, as well as at the beginning of the 3′ UTR [74]. Therefore, Peg10 can be reprogrammed to package different protein-coding RNAs, as long as they contain the flanking 5’ and 3’ UTR sequences of the Peg10 mRNA [130]. This modular platform has been demonstrated to efficiently deliver functional mRNAs in cell culture, including those encoding the Cre recombinase and Cas9 protein. Another group tested SEND-mediated intercellular mRNA transfer in vivo using engineered mouse models. They delivered the SEND system through plasmids via hydrodynamic injection. While initial bioluminescence imaging yielded ambiguous results, subsequent experiments using a Cre-lox reporter mouse line confirmed successful VLP-mediated transport and functional mRNA delivery between organs. When SEND components carrying Cas9 mRNA were delivered, gene editing occurred not only in the liver but also in the spleen, indicating successful VLP-mediated transport and delivery of mRNA across organs [131]. The main advantage of this in vivo approach is its potential for enhanced scalability. By leveraging the host cell’s machinery, it consolidates the complex steps of protein synthesis, RNA replication, and cargo encapsulation into a single, streamlined bioprocess, which can lead to a more uniform and correctly assembled final product.

Challenges for Nucleic Acid Delivery with Protein Nanocages

An important consideration when using VLPs is that they are a highly immunogenic platform due to their unique structural properties. Their particulate nature (20–200 nm) enables efficient drainage into lymph nodes and rapid uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). A key feature of VLPs is the high-density, repetitive display of surface epitopes, which promotes B-cell receptor cross-linking and induces strong antibody responses, even in the absence of adjuvants [132]. Furthermore, VLPs are potent inducers of cellular immunity. They are efficiently processed by dendritic cells (DCs) and can undergo cross-presentation, in which antigens are loaded onto MHC class I molecules to activate CD8 + cytotoxic T cells (CTLs). In addition, VLPs often encapsulate host nucleic acids during assembly, which act as endogenous adjuvants by stimulating Toll-like receptors (TLRs), thereby amplifying innate and adaptive immune responses [133, 134]. In this context, a major advantage of engineered VLPs over traditional viral vectors is their ability to avoid pre-existing humoral immunity. For example, a simplified Semliki Forest virus VLP system showed negligible levels of pre-existing antibodies in human populations, overcoming the neutralization barriers that significantly limit AAV efficacy [135]. Although structurally similar to VLPs, encapsulins tend to exhibit significantly lower immunogenicity. VLP particles have been shown to generate higher titers of self-recognizing total IgG in both BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice. However, a deeper comparison across different encapsulin systems and VLP platforms is required to draw definitive conclusions [22]. Ferritins have also been evaluated for their immunogenicity and, unlike encapsulins and VLPs, have been shown to elicit low to non-significant titers of self-recognizing antibodies.

A major limitation of wild-type AAV vectors is their broad tissue tropism, which often results in viral particle accumulation in off-target organs. To reduce these off-target toxicities, recent advances focus on capsid engineering, in which antigen-specific ligands, such as single-chain variable fragments or nanobodies, are inserted into the capsid structure [136]. This strategy, together with the removal of native binding domains, such as heparan sulfate proteoglycan, redirects viral tropism to tumor-specific markers (e.g., EGFR). Data indicate that these changes can limit cytotoxicity to antigen-positive tumor cells while sparing non-target tissues, even when delivering strong cytotoxic payloads. In addition, the higher potency of these targeted vectors allows therapeutic effects at lower genome copy numbers, further reducing the risk of dose-dependent toxicity in surrounding tissues [137].

Recent literature emphasizes that cellular DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) resulting from viral vector integration pose significant genomic risks, including p53-mediated DNA damage responses and complex chromosomal rearrangements [138]. Furthermore, the linear nature of standard AAV genomes promotes heterologous concatemerization and random insertion driven by inverted terminal repeat sequences, while non-native regulation can result in toxic overexpression phenotypes [139–141]. Therefore, the development of DSB-free systems for genomic integration is key to advancing DNA delivery methods. The use of integrases coupled with circular AAV genomes for site-specific gene insertion is an example of successful heterologous gene integration without the inherent risk of DSBs [139].

Conclusion

Studying protein nanocages as vectors for nucleic acid delivery has expanded the therapeutic options, ranging from repurposing natural systems to the precise engineering of programmable nanomachines. Loading strategies have evolved from non-specific electrostatic attraction to highly specific, affinity-driven encapsulation inspired by viral systems. Protein nanocages have been re-engineered to package cargos ranging from siRNAs to large mRNAs and ribonucleoprotein complexes. The ability to dually modify both the interior for cargo loading and the exterior for cell targeting underscores the immense potential of these platforms for creating multifunctional therapeutics.

Despite this progress, significant challenges remain: 1) Consistently high‑efficiency loading without host contaminants; 2) surface functionalization for specific cell binding and endocytic uptake, which varies with cage origin and modifications; 3) effective endosomal escape to release cargo into the cytoplasm; 4) in‑vivo stability, barrier navigation, low immunogenicity, and off-target toxicity; 5) scalability, cost‑effective manufacturing for clinical use.

Future directions focus on “intelligent by design” systems that combine computational modeling with high‑throughput directed evolution to co‑optimize cages and their nucleic‑acid payloads. This will allow the creation of artificial nucleocapsids mimicking viral efficiency and hybrid platforms merging non‑immunogenic bacterial scaffolds with viral functionalities. Translating these nanovectors into therapies will require coordinated advances across protein engineering, nucleic‑acid chemistry, and medicine.

Overall, protein nanocages are poised to become a new class of non-viral delivery platforms that combine the safety of protein-based materials with the precision and efficiency traditionally seen only in viral vectors, opening pathways for next-generation gene-editing, mRNA, miRNA, and siRNA therapies.
