Genome-wide analysis of terpene synthase family in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) explores the potential cross-talk in terpenoid biosynthesis
Authors: Li Chunmei, Ye Fuyang, Jin Han, He Haiyang, Du Zijun, Zhu Jing, Chen Zhenbang, Liu Kai, Wan Xiaorong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTerpenoids constitute a diverse group of primary and secondary metabolites that are extensively distributed in living organisms and play key roles in growth, development, and environmental adaptation. Terpenoids are derived from two isomeric precursors that are interconverted by isopentenyl-diphosphate delta-isomerase (IDI), in both the plastids and cytoplasm of plants. The plastidial pathway supplies precursors for diterpenoids and carotenoids, whereas the cytoplasmic pathway provides precursors for sesquiterpenoids and triterpenoids. A family of terpene synthases (TPSs) produce most terpenoids such as sesquiterpenes, hemiterpenes, monoterpenes, diterpenes and sesterterpenes, which in allotetraploid peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) have been relatively underexplored.ResultsIn this study, 77 AhTPS genes were identified in the peanut genome and phylogenetically classified into five subfamilies. These AhTPSs are organized in clusters across chromosomes and exhibit conserved gene structures and motifs within each subfamily. AhTPSs in the TPS-c and -e/f subfamilies, specifically copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS) and kaurene synthase (KS), were localized to plastids through transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The expression of AhCPS3 was detected a significant increase in response to abscisic acid (ABA) and methyl jasmonate (MeJA), which was notably distinct from the expression patterns of other AhCPSs and AhKS. Furthermore, 12 terpenoids were identified during seed development. Module-trait correlation analysis disclosed that the expression levels of genes encoding AhCPSs (involved in diterpene biosynthesis) and phytoene synthases (AhPSYs, involved in carotenoid biosynthesis) were significantly correlated with the abundances of soyasaponins (triterpenoids) during seed development in peanut. Additionally, the gene encoding β-amyrin synthase (AhβAS), which produces the backbone of triterpenoids, was identified in a significant module and was also induced by ABA and MeJA. Protein-protein interaction networks indicated AhCPSs, AhPSYs, and AhβAS shared a common interacting protein, AhIDI.ConclusionsThese findings provide valuable insights into the potential cross-talk in terpenoid biosynthesis across different cellular compartments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12013-x.

Background

Terpenoids, alternatively referred to as isoprenoids, constitute a diverse group of compounds that are extensively distributed in living organisms. Plants harbor the most abundant terpenoids, encompassing a multitude of primary and secondary metabolites that are crucial for their growth, development, and environmental adaptation [1, 2]. The biosynthesis of terpenoids involves the condensation of two common C5-isoprene units, namely isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and its isomer, dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP), which are formed via both mevalonic acid (MVA) and methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathways [3, 4]. Although MVA and MEP pathways occurs in separate compartments, cross-flows have been identified [4]. A range of prenyl diphosphate precursors, including IPP (C5), DMAPP (C5), geranyl diphosphate (GPP, C10), farnesyl diphosphate (FPP, C15), geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP, C20), and geranylfarnesyl diphosphate (GFPP, C25), are utilized by a large family of terpene synthases/cyclases (TPSs) to form basic terpene skeletons [5–9]. Consequently, the divergent functions and subcellular localizations of TPS enzyme families contribute to determining the diversity of terpenoids in plants [2]. In general, through the specific activities of TPSs, the MVA pathway generates sesquiterpenes (C15) in cytoplasm, whereas the MEP pathway generates hemiterpenes (C5), monoterpenes (C10), diterpenes (C20) and sesterterpenes (C25) in plastids [3, 4, 10]. IPP, FPP and GGPP also serve as substrates for squalene synthase (SQS) and phytoene synthase (PSY), which although are not members of the TPS family, to form triterpenes (C30) and carotenoids (C40), respectively [2].

TPSs are categorized into class I and II based on their structure and catalytic mechanisms. Class I TPSs contain conserved DDxx(D, E) and NSE/DTE motifs near the C-terminus, whereas class II TPSs possess a DxDD motif near the N-terminus [11, 12]. Phylogenetically, the entire TPS family can be classified into seven subfamilies, among which TPS-c represents class II TPSs, whereas TPS-a, TPS-b, TPS-d (gymnosperm-specific), TPS-e/f, TPS-g, and TPS-h (specific to Selaginella moellendorffii) belong to class I TPSs [13]. It has recently been proposed that the ancestral TPS encoded a bifunctional TPS with activities of both copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS) and kaurene synthase (KS) for phytohormone biosynthesis. During evolution, this ancestral TPS gave rise to three TPS lineages, namely, the TPS-c (CPS), TPS-e/f (KS), and the remaining TPS (h/d/a/b/g) subfamilies [14, 15]. To date, the TPS family has been extensively identified across numerous plant species, thereby highlighting the significant diversity in the size of the TPS family among different species [6, 9, 13, 16–20]. For example, whereas Physcomitrella patens features a single TPS gene [6], plants such as Eucalyptus grandis and Eucalyptus globulus boast a repertoire of over 100 TPSs [16]. Nevertheless, a large proportion of the plant terpenoids studied so far are evolutionary adaptations unique to specific plant lineages, helping them occupy ecological niches. As a result, these are classified as specialized terpenoid metabolites [1, 9, 21]. As of now, the biochemical and biological functions of TPSs in have been characterized a few plant species, such as in rice (Oryza sativa) [22–33], tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [9], foxtail millet (Setaria italica) [34], tea plant (Camellia sinensis) [35], and maize (Zea mays) [36, 37].

The allotetraploid peanut, rich in fats and proteins, make a valuable contribution to the global oilseed economy. Moreover, contemporary research has identified a diverse range of nutritional components in peanuts, such as carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, and phytonutrients (e.g., resveratrol, isoflavonoids, phenolic acids, and phytosterols), that potentially contribute to overall health and wellness [38]. However, other nutrient components, such as terpenoids, have yet to be sufficiently studied. Understanding TPS enables molecular breeding for improved varieties and provides foundational knowledge for species-specific natural terpenoid discovery in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.). As so far, little research has focused on the TPS family or terpenoids within peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.). In this study, based on the analysis of the peanut genome (peanutgr.fafu.edu.cn), we established that peanut genome contains 77 TPS genes, termed AhTPSs, and also determined the subcellular localizations and expression patterns of AhTPSs in the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies. Additionally, a re-analysis of metabolomic data from our previous study [39] revealed an abundance of triterpenoid saponins (soyasaponins) in developing seeds, on the basis of which, using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), we identified significant correlations between the expression patterns of AhCPSs and AhPSYs, which are involved in diterpene and carotenoid biosynthesis in plastids, and the abundance of soyasaponins produced in the cytoplasm of developing seeds. These findings provide further insights into the cross-talk of terpenoid biosynthesis in different cellular compartments.

Methods

Identification of TPSs in peanut

As a reference, we used genomic sequences from the ‘Shitouqi’ cultivar, obtained from the “Peanut Genome Research” website (http://peanutgr.fafu.edu.cn/Download.php) [40]. The Arabidopsis TPS (AtTPS) sequences were procured from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (https://www.arabidopsis.org/download/list?dir=Public_Data_Releases), and rice TPS sequences were acquired from the Rice Genome Project (https://rice.uga.edu/download_osa1r7.shtml). The sequences of the AtTPSs and OsTPSs (listed in Table S1) were employed as queries for BLASTP searches against peanut protein sequences, using an e-value threshold of 1 × 10−6, to predict the AhTPS family. In parallel, PF03936 and PF01397, specific domains of TPSs, were retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/), and hidden Markov model online searches were conducted to identify AhTPSs [41]. The sequences identified using both methods were cross-referenced and each predicted sequence was further validation based on NCBI-CDD searches (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) to ascertain the integrity of the TPS domain.

Phylogenetic and intraspecific collinearity analyses of TPSs

Full-length sequence alignments of TPSs from Arabidopsis, rice, and peanuts were carried out using ClustalW in the MEGA6.0 software platform [42]. A phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed employing the Neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates, and default settings for other parameters. Pairwise comparisons of genome-wide protein sequences of peanut were performed using BLAST with the e-value setting as 1e−5. The results were submitted to MCScanX from TBtools [43] for intra-species collinearity analysis. Genomic positions of the AhTPS genes were extracted and were visualized by Circos software from TBtools [43].

Gene structure, conserved motifs, cis-elements, and subcellular localization prediction of the AhTPS family

Information pertaining to exon-intron structurals was derived from a GFF file of the peanut genome. The conserved motifs of TPS proteins were analyzed using the MEME program available at http://www.meme-suite.org, with the maximum number set to 10. For cis-element analysis, a 2000-bp sequence upstream from the ATG start codon for each AhTPS gene was extracted and subsequently subjected to the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). Subcellular localization was predicted using the online tool DeepLoc2.1, found at https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/, with these features being visualized using TBtools tools.

Plant materials and treatments

The Zhongkaihua 1 cultivar, developed by Zhongkai University of Agriculture and Biology (Guangdong province, China), was used in this study. Plants were cultivated in a plant growth chamber at 28 °C with 14-h of light:10-h dark photoperiod for 7 days. For phytohormone treatment, 7-day-old seedlings were sprayed with solutions of 100 µM gibberellin (GA3), 2 mM salicylic acid (SA), 100 µM methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and 100 µM abscisic acid (ABA), respectively. A mock treatment was conducted using a 0.1% Tween 20 solution. In addition to simulate drought stress, 7-day-old seedlings were submerged in a 20% PEG6000 solution. Following each treatment, leaf samples were collected at time intervals of 0, 0.5, 1, 4 and 6 h for total RNA extraction, with each time point being represented by three biological replicates.

Subcellular localization assay

The full-length coding sequences of AhCPS1-1 A, AhCPS2-6B, AhCPS3-6B and AhKS1-8B were amplified from the cDNA of Zhongkaihua 1 and inserted into the C terminus of the green fluorescent protein (GFP) within a pCambia2300-35 S-eGFP vector. The resulting constructs were subsequently used to transform N. benthamiana plants, mediated by Agrobacterium GV3101, with empty pCambia2300-35 S-eGFP plasmids serving as controls. Fluorescence signals in the leaves were detected at 48 to72 h post-infiltration using a Nikon Ni-U fluorescence microscope. Details of the primer sets used in the construction of the aforementioned vectors are listed in Table S2.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), following the manufacturer’s instruction. Subsequently, the extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a dedicated kit (Vazyme). qRT-PCR was conducted using 10-µL reaction mixtures containing 0.1 µM of each primer and a 1× SYBR green PCR master mix (also from Vazyme). Reactions were performed using a CFX96 real-time PCR system(Bio-Rad), under the following thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s and 55°C for 30 s. For each of the designated time points, we analyzed triplicate technical replicates for each of three biological replicates. The constitutively expressed Actin 1 housekeeping gene in peanut, was used as an internal control [39]. Levels of expression levels are presented relative to that of Actin 1, as determined using the 2−ΔCt method [44]. All primer sets used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S2.

WGCNA analysis

The RNA-seq and metabolomic datasets were obtained from our previously published study [39]. Gene co-expression network analysis was conducted employing the WGCNA package [45], and module-trait relationship analysis was utilized to establish the correlation between module eigengenes and the levels of triterpenoid soyasaponin. Genes with an average FPKM of less than 1 were filtered out. A correlation matrix threshold of 6 was selected as the soft power. The minimum module size was set to 30, and the minimum height for merging modules was determined to be 0.2. Gene clustering was executed hierarchically using the Dynamic Hybrid Tree Cut Algorithm. Subsequently, modules with high similarity were identified based on clustering and were merged into new modules based on their eigengenes. The internal correlations within each module were analyzed and visualized in the form of a heatmap.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

The protein sequences of AhCPSs, AhPSYs, and AhβAS were uploaded to the STRING database (https://cn.stringdb.org/), and a protein interaction diagram was generated by selecting peanut as the reference species using default parameters. The resulting interaction network was then visualized using Cytoscape software.

Results

Genome-wide identification and characteristics of the AhTPS family in peanut

To identify candidate TPS genes, we executed a BLASTP search against the peanut genome, using characterized TPSs from rice and Arabidopsis as queries (Table S1). In addition, by exploiting the characteristic Pfam domains (PF01397 and PF03936) located at the N- and C-termini of the TPS proteins, we performed an HMMER search against the peanut genome. Finally, by integrating the results from both methods, 91 sequences were retrieved as potential TPS proteins (Table S3). Subsequently, the candidate sequences with a single incomplete N- or C-terminal domain were filtered out. Ultimately, we identified 77 members of the TPS gene family within peanuts, termed AhTPSs (Table S4). The lengths of the encoded proteins ranged from 264 (AH18G31370) to 912 (AH18G11990) amino acids. The predicted isoelectric points spanned values ranging from 4.82 (AH18G11900) to 8.63 (AH20G19740) (Table S4), And molecular weights ranged from 30.8 (AH18G31370) to 104.4 (AH18G11990) kDa (Table S4). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using TPS sequences from Arabidopsis, rice, and peanut. Within the tree, these AhTPSs were grouped into five clades corresponding to subfamilies TPS-a (36), -b (20), -c (6), -e/f (2), and -g (13) (Fig. 1; Table S4). TPS-a and TPS-b accounted for a relatively large proportion, comprising 72.7% of the AhTPS family (Fig. 2; Table S4). Chromosome distribution analysis revealed that the 77 AhTPSs were distributed across 17 chromosomes, with Chr18 harboring the largest number (29), followed by Chr08 (13). Moreover, we established that a larger number AhTPSs are located on chromosomes in sub-genome B (Chr11-20) (48) than in sub-genome A (Chr01-10) (29). Specifically, compared to the sub-genome A (11), sub-genome B contained a significantly higher number of TPS-a subfamily members (25) (Fig. 2; Table S4). Furthermore, intraspecific collinearity analysis of the AhTPS genes suggested that duplications occurred not only between the sub-genomes A and B but also within each of the two sub-genomes (Fig. 2). Given that a clustered tandem distribution of TPSs on chromosomes has been reported in multiple species, including tomatoes and celery (Apium graveolens) [17, 46], these AhTPS genes are widely considered to be associated with tandem duplication events.

Conserved domains, gene structure, and cis-regulatory elements of AhTPS family

To further elucidate the function of AhTPSs, we used the MEME tool to identify 10 conserved motifs in the AhTPS family, which comprised between 15 and 41 amino acids (Fig. S1). A majority of the AhTPS proteins classified in the same subfamily tend to be characterized by similar motif structures (Fig. 3A-C). Notably, the DDxxD motif was found to be present in motif 8 (Fig. S1). In particular, 69 members of Class I (with the exception of AH18G31370 in the TPS-a subfamily and AH18G20390 in the TPS-b subfamily) were found to contain the DDxxD motif, three of which displayed a mutated DDxxE motif (Table S5; Fig. S2). The NSE/DTE motif (motif 4), which is present in most members of the TPS-a (apart from AH20G19740, AH18G31370, and AH08G26570), TPS-b (apart from AH09G32270 and AH18G24300), and TPS-e/f subfamilies, was found in only two members of the TPS-g subfamily (Table S5; Fig.S1, S2). Interestingly, the RRx8W motif, which is known to play a pivotal role in monoterpene biosynthesis [3, 6], was not conserved in the AhTPS family (Table S5; Fig.S1, S2). In addition, the extended DDxxD motif, TTxxDDxxD, and the PIV motif are specific to KSs involved in phytohormone biosynthesis [14, 15]. We found that members of the TPS-e/f subfamily contained the TTxxDDxxD and PIV motifs and are thus designated AhKS1-7 A and AhKS1-8B (Table S5; Fig. S2). Members of the TPS-c subfamily were established to have conserved DxDD motif characteristics of class II TPSs (Table S5, Fig. S2) and are named AhCPS1-1 A/B, AhCPS2-6 A/B and AhCPS3-6 A/B, respectively. The LHS and PNV motifs specific to CPSs involved in phytohormone biosynthesis were found in TPS-c subfamily members, with the exception of AhCPS1-1B, which harbors a mutated LYS and PNS (Table S5, Fig. S2). To further investigate the structural features of AhTPS genes, the exon-intron analysis was performed, which revealed that genes within the TPS -c and -e/f subfamilies exhibited are characterized by similar structures, with a higher number of exons, ranging from 13 to 15. In contrast, members of the other subfamily had fewer exons, varying from 3 to 9 (Fig. 3D), a pattern similar to that observed in celery [17].

The cis-elements in promoter regions play essential roles in regulating gene expression. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory network of AhTPS genes, we analyzed the respective promoter regions(Table S6), the results of which revealed that the top 20 enriched cis-elements are associated with light (Box 4, GT1-motif, G-box, TCT-motif, and GATA-motif), phytohormones (ERE, ABRE, CGTCA-motif, and TGACG-motif, which are related to ethylene, ABA, and MeJA, respectively), stress responses (ARE, STRE, and WUN-motif, which are associated with anaerobic induction, general stress, and wounding, respectively), development (as-1 and AAGAA-motif), and transcription factors binding (MYC, MYB, Myb, MYB-like sequence, and Myb-binding site) (Fig. 4A). Notably, among the promoters of AhTPS gene family members, the Box 4 was identified as the most prevalent elements, followed by MYC, with the promoters of more than 70 AhTPS genes containing both cis-elements (Fig. 4B). The presence of this diverse range of elements within the promoter regions of AhTPS genes would thus tend to provide evidence for the potential involvement of these genes in a wide range of biological processes.

Subcellular localization of the AhTPSs in TPS-c and e/f subfamilies

TPSs catalyze C5-C25 prenyl diphosphate precursors in multiple cellular compartments, thereby contribution to generation of the fundamental skeletons for a diverse array of terpenoids. Consequently, the subcellular localization of TPSs is considered a key determinant of terpenoid diversity [2, 5–9]. To further elucidate the subcellular localization of individual AhTPS members, we made predictions based on the application of DeepLoc2.1, the results of which indicated that the majority of the AhTPS family members are probably localized within the cytoplasm and plastids (Table S4, Fig. 5A). Specifically, members of the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies, as well as a majority of those in the TPS-b subfamily, were predicted to have robust plastid localization scores (Table S4, Fig. 5A). However, the majority of TPS-a subfamily members were anticipated to preferentially localize within the cytoplasm (Table S4, Fig. 5A).

Given the conserved roles of the TPS-c and e/f subfamilies, which respectively encompass the CPS and KS enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of gibberellins, phytohormone essential for plant growth, we focused on these two subfamilies. To validate the predicted subcellular localizations, we conducted transient expression analysis in tobacco (N. benthamiana) plants transformed with constructs containing AhCPS or AhKS fused to eGFP. However, using PCR and sequencing, we succeed obtaining only the coding sequences of AhCPS1-1 A (AH01G03110), AhCPS2-6B (AH16G02160), AhCPS3-6B (AH16G02130) and AhKS2-8B (AH18G23540) from the peanut plants. This outcome may be attributed to the general expression advantages of genes originating from the sub-genome B, and the coding sequences of these genes were subsequently fused to eGFP at their C-termini. The experimental results confirmed that all four of the detected proteins were plastid localized (Fig. 5B), which is consistent with the predictions made using DeepLoc2.1 (Table S4, Fig. 5A), and also with the localization patterns of CPSs and KSs observed in other plant species, such as rice [47].

Expression profiling of AhTPS genes in the TPS-c and -e/f subfamilies

To investigate the expression patterns of AhTPSs in the TPS-c and -e/f subfamilies, we analyzed the expression of AhCPS1-1 A, AhCPS2-6B, AhCPS3-6B and AhKS1-8B in leaves in response to GA3, SA, MeJA, ABA and PEG treatments. The findings revealed that the expression of AhCPS1-1 A, AhCPS2-6B, AhCPS3-6B, and the downstream AhKS1-8B was downregulated in response to GA3 treatment (Fig. 6). We speculated that this suppression could be ascribed to a feedback regulation of endogenous gibberellin biosynthesis triggered by the application of exogenous GA3. Notably, the expressions of AhCPS1-1 A was repressed by all five of the selected treatments. Contrastingly, treatments with SA and PEG were observed to promote an upregulated expression of AhCPS2-6B after 1 and 0.5 h, respectively, whereas expression was found to be downregulated in response to treatments with MeJA and ABA, with relatively low expression levels. Conversely, we detected a significant upregulation in the expression of AhCPS3-6B following treatments with MeJA and ABA for 1 and 0.5 h, respectively, whereas there were reductions in the expression of this gene in response to treatments with SA and PEG (Fig. 6). In this regard, it should be emphasized that compared with other AhCPSs and AhKSs, the promoter region of AhCPS3-6B contains a higher number of ABRE and CGTCA-motif elements, which are associated with ABA and MeJA signaling, respectively(Fig. 4A). These observations implied a potential functional divergence among AhCPSs, and indicated that AhCPS3-6B may predominantly produce metabolites in environments promoting JA and ABA signaling, whereas AhCPS2-6B potentially contributes to the pathways mediated by SA and drought stress.

Identification of key AhTPS genes regulating terpenoid biosynthesis during seed development

To date, the terpenoids in peanuts has yet to be comprehensively identified. A re-analysis of our previously published metabolomic data, obtained from peanut seeds at three distinct developmental stages based on UPLC-MS/MS analysis [39], revealed the presence of monoterpenoids, triterpenes, and their derived triterpenoid saponins in developing peanut seeds (Table S7). Among these, three types of triterpenoids, namely, soyasapogenol E-3-O-rhamnosyl(1,2)glucosyl(1,2)glucuronide, soyasaponin γg, and soyasaponin βg, were found to show differential abundances at different stages or among different cultivars (Table S7, Fig. 7A). Studies have reported that oxidosqualene synthases (OSC), which are responsible for generating the basic skeletons for triterpenoids, are not TPS family members, although they share structural homologies with CPSs in the TPS-c subfamily and utilize substrates derived from upstream MVA pathway for cyclization [15, 48, 49]. To investigate whether classic AhTPSs are correlated with triterpenoid saponin biosynthesis, we performed WGCNA analysis, which yielded 10 modules. Among these modules, MEtan and MEturquoise were found to show significantly positive correlations with both soyasaponin γg and βg, while MEblue and MEblack showed negative correlations (Fig. 7B). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using genes from these modules based on their positive or negative correlations. The significant pathways were shown in Fig. 7C and D. Terpenoid biosynthesis-related pathways, such as those associated with the metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides, carotenoid biosynthesis, and diterpenoid biosynthesis pathways, were found to be significantly enriched in both positively and negatively correlated genes, and were accordingly subjected to further detailed analysis (Fig. 7C, D). The gene encoding β-amyrin synthase (AhβAS-4B, AH14G00680), an OSC, was found in the positively correlated modules (Tables S8, S9). Interestingly, we also identified several classic AhTPSs, constituting half of the AhCPSs. Specifically, AhCPS3-6 A was identified in the positive module, whereas AhCPS2-6 A, AhCPS2-6B, AH08G19990 (sesquiterpene synthase), AH20G19740 (sesquiterpene synthase), and AH13G15850 (tricyclene synthase) were identified in the negative modules (Tables S8, S10). In addition, we also identified the genes encoding phytoene synthase, AhPSY3-7A (AH07G11280) and AhPSY2-7B (AH17G03400), which convert GGPP to phytoene and provide precursors for carotenoid biosynthesis in plastids [50] (Tables S8, S9, S10). The expression patterns of AhCPSs, AhPSYs and AhβAS during seed development were subsequently visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 7E). The results indicated that the same genes located in sub-genomes A and B showed similar expression patterns, such as AhCPS2-6 A and AhCPS2-6B, AhPSY2-7A and AhPSY2-7B, even though they were included in different modules or were not detected in any module (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, during seed development, AhCPS3-6 A/B showed an expression pattern opposite to that of AhCPS2-6 A/B (Fig. 7E), thereby supporting the hypothesis of functional divergence among AhCPSs. These findings thus tend to suggest that these AhTPSs or AhPSYs may play vital regulatory roles in soyasaponin biosynthesis during seed development, despite not functioning as enzymes that produce intermediates or precursors for soyasaponin biosynthesis.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis of ahcpss, AhPSYs and AhβAS identified in significant modules

We further analyzed the interaction network of AhCPSs, AhPSYs and AhβAS-4B using STRING (Fig. 8; Table S11). The results showed that AhCPS2-6B and AhCPS3-6B share a common interaction network, including proteins involved in terpenoid backbone and terpenoid-derived chlorophyll biosynthesis (Fig. 8A, B). The interaction network of AhPSY2-7B also comprises proteins involved in terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, as well as those involved in the biosynthesis of carotenoids and sterols (Fig. 8C), while only proteins in the terpenoid backbone biosynthesis were present in the interaction network of AhPSY3-7 A (Fig. 8D). Moreover, most of proteins in the AhβAS-4B interaction network are associated with triterpenoid biosynthesis (Fig. 8E). Surprisingly, the gene (Ahy_B04g073671/AH14G32230) encoding isopentenyl-diphosphate delta-isomerase (AhIDI-4B), which is the only enzyme shared in both MVA and MEP pathways and catalyzes the interconversion of IPP and DMAPP to regulate the levels in multiple subcellular compartments [10, 51–53], showed high scores with all AhCPSs (Fig. 8A, B), AhPSYs (Fig. 8C, D) and AhβAS-4B (Fig. 8E) with more than one interactive relationship including co-expression. Subcellular localization assays have demonstrated that AhCPS1-1 A, AhCPS2-6B and AhCPS3-6B was localized within the plastids (Fig. 4B). In addition, PSY has been extensively studied in a range of different species and is plastid localized [54–58]. Similarly, AhPSYs, as predicted by DeepLoc2.1, were also established to be localized within the plastids (Table S12). In contrast, triterpenoid biosynthesis occurs within the cytoplasm, utilizing isoprenoids derived from the cytoplasmic MVA pathway [2, 59]. AhβAS, as predicted by DeepLoc2.1, showed strong localization in the cytoplasm and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Table S12), which is consistent with the localization of βAS in Bupleurum chinense [60]. IDIs in rice and Arabidopsis have been reported to localize within organelles, such as mitochondria, peroxisomes, and ER in the cytoplasm, with one isoform capable of being imported into plastids [10, 51–53]. Further BLAST analysis of the peanut genome disclosed two AhIDIs in sub-genomes A and B with over 98% sequence identity. Online predictions showed that both AhIDIs possessed transit signals of plastids, mitochondria, and the cytoplasm (Table S12), thus tending to indicate that AhIDI may show localization patterns in multiple compartments similar to those of the IDIs in rice and Arabidopsis. Collectively, these results suggested the possibility that the presumed indirect or direct interactions of AhIDI-4B with AhCPSs, AhPSYs and AhβAS-4B may influence the DMAPP/IPP flux to diterpene or carotenoid biosynthesis in plastid or triterpenoid biosynthesis in the cytoplasm.

Discussion

Identification and characterization of the AhTPS family

Terpenoids constitute the largest class of secondary metabolites in plants, the diversity of which is significantly influenced by the number of TPS gene family members [2]. It has been established that in plants, the number of TPS genes in plants varies from 20 to 150, and these genes can be categorized into eight subfamilies (TPS-a ~ TPS-h), among which, the TPS-a, -b, and -g subfamilies are specific to angiosperms, TPS-d subfamily is exclusive to gymnosperms, and TPS-h genes have been found only in species of Lycopodium [6]. TPS-c and TPS-e/f genes are present in both angiosperms and gymnosperms, in which they are primarily involved in the biosynthesis of diterpenoids [14]. Although known for its rich contents of fats and proteins, there is currently limited information available regarding the terpenoids and TPS genes in peanut. Within the peanut genome, we identified 77 members of TPS gene family, which can be divided into TPS-a, -b, -c, -e/f and -g subfamilies (Fig. 1; Table S4). Previous research has indicated that the sub-genome B possesses a greater number of genes than the sub-genome A [40], and consistent with this finding, we detected a larger number of AhTPSs in sub-genome B than in sub-genome A, which was particularly evident with respect to the TPS-a subfamily (Fig. 2; Table S4). Typically, the TPS-a subfamily possesses a relatively larger number of members than the other TPS subfamilies in plants. For instance, 22 of the 32 TPSs in Arabidopsis are the TPS-a subfamily genes, and similarly, in tomato, apple, and rice, 12 of 29, 4 of 10. and 30 of 46 TPSs are TPS-a subfamily members, respectively [9, 62]. In the present study, 38 AhTPSs were identified as members of TPS-a subfamily (Figs. 1 and 2; Table S4), which is coincident with findings in other plant species. Furthermore, a study on celery has reported that members of the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies are characterized by a larger number of exons than genes of other subfamilies [17], which is consistent with our findings that exons are more numerous in the TPS -c and -e/f subfamilies than those in other subfamilies (Fig. 3). This observation may thus suggest a potential association between exon number and functional diversity within these subfamilies.

Conservation and potential functional divergence of AhCPSs and AhKS

Gibberellins are phytohormones that play essential roles in multiple biological processes within plants. It has been established that the early steps in gibberellin biosynthesis occur within plastids, in which GGPP is initially converted to copalyl diphosphate (CPP) and subsequently to ent-kaurene, in reactions catalyzed by CPS and KS, respectively [14, 15]. The findings of recent studies have provided evidence to indicate that the ancestral TPS gene encoded a bifunctional CPSKS, which produced the ent-kaurene required for gibberellin production in all extant lineages of land plants [14, 15]. During the evolution, the ancestral CPSKS gave rise to three TPS lineages, leading to the extant TPS-c (including CPSs), TPS-e/f (including KSs), and the remaining TPS (-h/d/a/b/g) subfamilies dedicated to secondary metabolism. Whereas TPS-c and TPS-e/f have retained CPS and KS involved in primary metabolic gibberellin biosynthesis, they have also evolved genes for other diterpenoid biosynthesis in secondary metabolism [14]. In Arabidopsis, a single CPS and KS are dedicated to gibberellin biosynthesis [9], whereas in rice, the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies each encompass three CPSs and one KS, along with eight KS-like genes, which are responsible for the biosynthesis of gibberellins and other diterpenoids in secondary metabolism [63]. Moreover, the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies in tomato contain two CPSs and six KS or KS-like genes [9]. In the current study, we established that the TPS-c subfamily comprises six members (AhCPSs), while the TPS-e/f subfamily contains only two AhKSs. Unlike other subfamilies, the sub-genomes A and B contain an equal number of members in the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies (Figs. 1 and 2; Table S4), thus indicating that members of these two subfamilies are more conserved between sub-genomes A and B than members of other subfamilies.

The DxDD motif is conserved among CPSs from various species, and LHS-PNV motif are key amino acids in catalytic reactions responsible for gibberellin biosynthesis [15]. In the present study, the LHS-PNV of AhCPS1-1B is replaced by LYS-PNS, and we failed to obtain the coding sequences of AhCPS1-1B by PCR in peanut (Fig. S2, Table S5), thereby raising the possibility that AhCPS1-1B may not be responsible for gibberellin biosynthesis or may have lost its function. In rice, all three CPSs are plastid localized, among which OsCPS1, the only one containing the LHS-PNV motif, produces CPP for gibberellin biosynthesis, while OsCPS2 and OsCPS4, whose transcripts accumulate in response to MeJA induction, produce CPP for diterpenoid phytoalexins in defense against microbial infections [64, 65]. In the current study, we found that the three AhCPSs are localized in the plastids (Fig. 5) and all possess the LHS-PNV motif, which is essential for gibberellin biosynthesis (Fig. S2, Table S5), although they showed diverse patterns of expression in response to different treatments (Fig. 6). The relatively opposite expression patterns between AhCPS2-6 A/B and AhCPS3-6 A/B were observed as well during the seed development (Fig. 7E). It is noteworthy that AhCPS3-6B was markedly induced by MeJA and ABA (Fig. 6), thus providing evidence to indicate a role in the biological processes mediated by the JA and ABA signaling pathways. Collectively, these findings indicated that functional divergence occurred among AhCPSs. However, it remains to be established whether multiple AhCPSs could produce other potential secondary diterpenoids beyond gibberellins, similar to the CPSs in rice. Moreover, the conserved DDxxD motif of KSs specifically involved in gibberellin biosynthesis extends to TTxxDDxxD [14, 66]. Both AhKS1-7 A and AhKS1-8B were found to have the KS-specific TTxxDDxD and PIV motifs (Fig. S2, Table S5), which tends to indicate their involvement in gibberellin biosynthesis.

Potential cross-talk in terpenoid biosynthesis across subcellular compartments in peanuts

Although peanuts are noted for their high levels of oleic acid, protein, and amino acids, other metabolites, such as terpenoids, have received comparatively less attention. The terpenoid biosynthesis occurs via the condensation of IPP and DMAPP, which are derived from either the cytoplasmic MVA pathway or the plastidial MEP pathway. Generally, the MVA pathway supplies precursors for sesquiterpene and triterpene, whereas the MEP pathway generates hemiterpene, monoterpene, diterpene, sesterterpene and carotenoids precursors [2–4, 10]. Although cross-talk between the MVA and MEP pathways has been well-documented in earlier studies, employing techniques such as co-expression analysis and mutants [4, 67–71], the interaction between downstream terpenoid biosynthesis pathways, such as those for diterpenoids and triterpenoids, has not been extensively explored.

In the present study, based on a re-analysis of our previously reported metabolomic data obtained by UOLC-MS/MS, we identified 12 terpenoids in developing peanut seeds [39], including monoterpenoids, triterpenoids, and derived triterpenoid saponins (Fig. 7A). In addition, WGCNA analysis disclosed four gene modules showing a significant association with soyasaponin γg and βg (Fig. 7B). AhβAS-4B, which shares structural homology with CPS and is involved in the formation of the basic scaffold for soyasaponins [15, 72], was identified in the positive module MEtan (Fig. 7B-E; Table S9). Unexpectedly, three AhCPSs involved in diterpenoid biosynthesis and two AhPSYs involved in carotenoid biosynthesis were detected in these modules (Fig. 7B-E; Table S10). Notably, the expression of AhCPS3-6B was found to be positively associated with AhβAS-4B during seed development and in response to MeJA and ABA treatments (Figs. 6 and 9). Moreover, interaction network analysis of AhCPSs, AhPSYs and AhβAS-4B revealed a common putative interacting protein, AhIDI-4B (Fig. 8). Taken together, these findings suggested that the potential cross-talk between AhCPSs, AhPSYs and soyasaponin biosynthesis at both the transcriptional and protein levels may channel the DMAPP/IPP flux into the corresponding diterpene and triterpene biosynthesis in both the plastids and the cytoplasm of peanut seeds. On the basis of these observations, we propose a model for the cross-talk between AhCPS3-mediated diterpene biosynthesis in plastids and AhβAS-mediated triterpene biosynthesis in cytoplasm during biological processes regulated by MeJA or ABA (Fig. 10). However, further studies are needed to validate this model.

Conclusions

Our findings in this study provide valuable insights regarding the genome-wide identification, classification, chromosomal distribution and structural characteristics of the TPS gene family in peanut. The expression profiles and subcellular localizations of AhCPSs and AhKSs in the TPS-c and TPS-e/f subfamilies were analyzed, the findings of which, including the plastid localizations of AhCPSs and AhKSs, and the distinct expression patterns of AhCPS3-6B in response to ABA and MeJA treatments, provide evidence of potential functional divergence. The identification of 12 terpenoids during seed development and correlation analysis through WGCNA and protein-protein interaction network analyses revealed interconnections between different terpenoid pathways, particularly the significant association between genes involved in diterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis. Collectively, these findings not only enhance our understanding of terpenoid metabolism in peanuts but also pave the way for potential genetic engineering strategies that by modulating terpenoid biosynthesis, could contribute to improving the nutritional and functional properties of peanuts.
