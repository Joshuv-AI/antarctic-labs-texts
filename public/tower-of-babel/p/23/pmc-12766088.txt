Effects of single-frequency ultrasonic modification on starch structure and digestive properties: A comprehensive meta-analysis and acoustic simulation
Authors: Ping Shi, Siyu Liu, Jinjuan Lu, Xuemei Yao, Jiesi Li, Meifang Li, Shengyun Qin, Yanbei Liu, Rongping Bu, Hafiz Umer Javed, Yuansen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study employed meta-analysis (25 studies, 121 data points) combined with acoustic simulation to investigate the effects of ultrasound on resistant starch (RS) formation under varying conditions, including starch sources, crystal types, frequency, power, duration, temperature, and concentration. Meta-subgroup analyses revealed significant RS increases in fruit (MD = 5.731) and cereal (MD = 1.579) starches. RS content increased in type A and B starches (p > 0.05), in contrast to the significant reductions observed in legume (MD = −6.528) and type C starches (MD = −5.909) (p < 0.05). Meta-regression indicated that higher frequency and power, along with lower duration, temperature, and concentration, positively affected RS content. Acoustical simulation shows that starch suspensions at 40 kHz, 25 °C, and 10 % w/v have the lowest sound pressure (9.33 Pa). These findings provide valuable insights into the potential application of ultrasonically modified starch in low-glycemic food industry.

Introduction

Starch, a widely distributed polysaccharide, serves as the primary energy reserve in plants, fulfilling a crucial role in human nutrition by supplying energy. According to the Englyst digestive classification system (Englyst et al., 1992), starches are classified into three categories based on their digestive rates: rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS). RDS induces a sharp rise in blood glucose, and chronic intake is associated with an increased risk of obesity, hyperglycemia, fatty liver, and type 2 diabetes. In contrast, SDS and RS offer physiological benefits such as modulation of gut microbiota and a reduced risk of colorectal cancer (Zhang et al., 2020). The high digestibility of native starch has been mitigated through physical, chemical, and enzymatic modifications (Kaur et al., 2012).

Non-thermal processing technologies have attracted considerable attention for their ability to modify starch structure without causing thermal degradation of nutrients or sensory qualities (Kumari et al., 2024). Ultrasound treatment has been recognized as a “clean label” technology, providing a sustainable and efficient alternative for starch modification (Rahaman et al., 2021). The passage of ultrasound through a liquid generates alternating pressure waves, leading to the formation, growth, and eventual violent implosion of microscopic gas bubbles. This implosion generates extreme localized conditions (1700–4700 °C; 180–300 MPa), whose resulting shock waves damage starch granules by producing surface pores, indentations, and cracks (Estivi et al., 2022), altering the molecular structure of starch and affecting its digestive properties. The effect of ultrasound on starch digestibility is highly variable and depends critically on frequency, power, duration, and temperature. Kaur and Gill (2019) reported RS content of wheat, rice, corn, and barley starch were increased by high-intensity ultrasound treatment (24 kHz, 100 W). Zhang et al. (2021) investigated RS content of corn, potato and pea starches were decrease at high power (600 W). The influence of ultrasonication on resistant starch is inconsistent across studies.

Meta-analysis statistically combines results from multiple studies to derive more reliable conclusions (Damaiyanti et al., 2023). Previous meta-analyses on starch modification have examined the effects of starch structure (Faridah et al., 2022), annealing (Anugerah et al., 2022), and moist heat treatment (Damaiyanti et al., 2023). However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has conducted a meta-analysis on the effects of ultrasonically modified starch, particularly one that integrates acoustic simulation to quantify the characteristics of ultrasound. We hypothesize that ultrasonic parameters (e.g., higher power or frequency) will lead to an increase in RS content compared to native starch.

This study aims to comprehensively examine the effects of ultrasound treatment on starch structure and its digestive properties through a combination of numerical simulations and meta-analysis. Specifically, subgroup and meta-regression analyses were performed for categorical (starch source and crystal type) and continuous (frequency, power, concentration, time, temperature) variables, respectively. These results were integrated with acoustic simulations to model pressure distributions in starch solutions under various conditions. This comprehensive analysis provides a theoretical basis for using ultrasound to modulate starch digestibility and broadens applications in low glycemic index food industry.

Materials and methods

The meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the methodologies described by Afandi et al. (2021) and Javed et al. (2024). The meta-analysis process comprised four main stages: literature selection, data extraction, data analysis, and interpretation.

Data collection

Literature searches were performed using the Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and PubMed databases to identify relevant research articles pertaining to the ultrasonic modification of starch. The search keywords included “starch” and “ultrasound” in both topic and title fields. A PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the article selection process is provided in Fig. 1. Specifically, a total of 2546 publications were initially retrieved from three databases. After removing 743 duplicates, the remaining records were screened based on title and abstract, resulting in 221 articles that involved ultrasound treatment of starch. These 221 papers were then subjected to full-text assessment. Studies focusing on pre-ultrasound treatment, ultrasound-assisted treatment, or those with incomplete data were excluded. Data was extracted from the selected articles into an Excel spreadsheet. The extracted information included: paper title, authors, publication year, starch source, crystal type, ultrasound frequency, ultrasound power, ultrasound duration, temperature, slurry concentration, as well as mean and standard deviation of RS content. A total of 121 data points were obtained for the meta-analysis. These data points correspond to varying samples, ultrasound processing parameters, and RS measurements. Accordingly, multiple data points could be extracted from a single study. The compiled dataset was subsequently used for meta-analysis.

Meta-analysis

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was evaluated using the Gaussian distribution and the Shapiro-Wilk test. The significance level of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant relationship between the factor and RS content. Comprehensive Meta Analysis V3 (Biostat International, Inc., Tampa, Florida, USA) was used to assess the effect of RS content between ultrasound conditions, which exports the forest plot, funnel plot and meta-regression result. The heterogeneity of the studies was tested using the heterogeneity value. The forest plot compared the RS content between native starch and modified starch. Egger's test was used to evaluated the publication bias in funnel plot, p < 0.05 means existing publication bias. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically omitting one study at a time to measure its influence (Egger et al., 1997). GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2) (Dotmatics Inc., Boston, Massachusetts, USA) was used to generate the plot. The governing equations for acoustic pressure Navier-Stokes are solved simultaneously using the COMSOL Multiphysics™ 6.2 (COMSOL AB, Solna, Sweden).

Results and discussion

Characteristic and profile of articles

Ultrasound technology has recently been widely used as a modification technique to alter starch structures in order to increase the formation of resistant starch. The single-frequency ultrasonic water bath system is the most commonly used setup for starch modification. The structural changes in starch are primarily attributed to the generation of free radicals and cavitation-induced localized high temperatures and pressures during ultrasonication (Estivi et al., 2022). Seven key factors influence these effects: starch sources, crystal type, ultrasound power, ultrasound frequency, ambient temperature, starch concentration and treatment duration (Hj et al., 2021; Iuga & Mironeasa, 2020). Overall effect size

The frequency distribution and the normal Q-Q plot of ultrasound treatment on RS content were shown in Fig. 3. The results indicated that the data followed a normal (Gaussian) distribution. The overall effect size for each study was displayed in Fig. 4. A change in RS content was considered statistically insignificant (p > 0.05) when the 95 % confidence interval (CI) of the effect size included zero (Afandi et al., 2021). The overall effect size was −0.337 ± 0.745, and its 95 % confidence interval crossed the centerline (x = 0), suggesting that the alteration in RS content was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity value above 75 % indicated high heterogeneity (Higgins et al., 2003). The analysis revealed an exceptionally high degree of heterogeneity (I2 = 99.996 %), indicating substantial variation in the findings across studies. The considerable heterogeneity could be primarily attributed to variations in starch sources, crystal types, and differing ultrasonic processing parameters (e.g., frequency, power, duration, temperature, and concentration). This considerable dispersion was manifested as both high and low RS content reported in different investigations, making it challenging to establish generalized guidelines for ultrasound modification on starch based solely on the overall effect size. Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

To assess the validity of the meta-analysis, a publication bias analysis was conducted following established methodologies (Suurmond et al., 2017). In the absence of bias, the funnel plot should ideally exhibit an inverted and symmetrical shape, reflecting unbiased distribution of effect estimates (Higgins et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 5A, the funnel plot demonstrated a symmetrical distribution, with the majority of effect sizes clustered near the center. Furthermore, Egger's test yielded a p-value of 0.154, indicating no significant publication bias. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig. 5B) demonstrated the stability of the pooled effect size (MD = −0.337 ± 0.745), with the iterative exclusion of individual studies producing no substantial change in the estimate, confirming the robustness of the conclusion. This supports the robustness of the findings and justifies further subgroup and meta-regression analyses.Fig. 5Funnel plot of ultrasound effect on resistant starch. Subgroup analysis of starch sources and crystal type

Subgroup analyses are particularly suitable for examining categorical variables, such as crop and crystal type. Based on crop type, the data were classified into four categories: cereals, beans, tubers, and fruits. A random-effects model was applied for subgroup analysis (Faridah et al., 2022). The effect of ultrasound treatment on RS content across different crop types was presented in Fig. 6A. The results indicated that ultrasound had a significant negative effect on RS content in legume crops (e.g., pea), with a mean difference (MD) of −6.528 ± 1.102 (95 % CI: −8.687 to −4.369) across 20 data points. In contrast, a significant increase in RS content was observed in fruit crops (including amaranth, kiwi, and banana), with an MD of 5.741 ± 0.382 (95 % CI: 4.988 to 6.474) from 10 data points. For tuber and cereal crops, the MD values were − 1.091 (95 % CI: −2.898 to 0.715) and 1.652 (95 % CI: −0.840 to 3.998), respectively. In both cases, the 95 % confidence intervals crossed the null line (x = 0), indicating that the changes in RS content were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the botanical origin of starch significantly influences the effect of ultrasound on RS formation. The influence of ultrasound on RS formation was further evaluated across different starch crystal types (A-type, B-type, and C-type), as illustrated in Fig. 6B. Ultrasound treatment significantly reduced RS content in C-type starch (all from legumes), which suggested a potential link between the C-type crystalline structure and the RS-lowering effect of ultrasound. This could be explained by the hypothesis that the intermediate and more complex structure of C-type starch is more vulnerable to ultrasonic disruption compared to A- and B-types. In aqueous solution, amylose fragments with degree of polymerization (DP) 10–13 generated A-type crystals (monoclinic crystal), while those with DP > 13 directed to B-type (hexagonal crystal) or C-type (monoclinic and hexagonal crystal) structures (Chen et al., 2024; Gaenssle et al., 2021). The structure of type A crystal and type B crystal contains 8 and 12 double helix chains respectively, and the number of water molecules were 36 in B-type structure (Jane et al., 1997) (Fig. 6C). The larger voids and looser molecular arrangement in B-type starch make it more vulnerable to ultrasonic disruption than the tightly packed A-type. This structural susceptibility enhances enzymatic accessibility, thereby reducing RS content. Additionally, ultrasound treatment generally decreases starch crystallinity by disrupting the crystalline regions of branched starch molecules (Wang, Xu, et al., 2020). Therefore, A-type starch was more conducive to RS formation under ultrasound treatment compared to other crystal types.

Meta-regression analysis of ultrasonic effect on resistant starch

To quantify the effects of continuous ultrasound parameters (including frequency, power, duration, temperature, and concentration) on RS production, a meta-regression analysis was performed to evaluate both their individual and interactive contributions. The results of the meta-regression model were summarized in Table 3. A meta-regression was performed to investigate the potential influence of 10 covariates on the observed heterogeneity. The overall model was statistically significant (Q = 34.73, df = 10, p < 0.001), indicating that the covariates collectively influenced the effect sizes. However, the explanatory power of the model was limited. The R2 analog value was only 0.08, meaning that the vast majority (92 %) of the heterogeneity remained unexplained. This pronounced limitation is further evidenced by the large, significant residual variance (Tau2 = 60.01, I2 = 100 %, p < 0.001). These results suggest that despite a statistically significant collective association, the examined covariates are not meaningful explanatory factors. The relationship between ultrasound parameters and RS content may be non-linear or involve complex interactions between multiple factors (e.g., the effect of amplitude is contingent upon treatment time and starch botanical origin). Our simple linear meta-regression model was not equipped to capture such higher-order complexities. As illustrated in Fig. 7A, meta-regression revealed that higher ultrasonic frequency (20–40 kHz; β = 0.187, p = 0.367) and power (50–750 W; β = 0.008, p = 0.099) were associated with increased RS content. In contrast, longer treatment duration (β = −0.008), higher temperature (β = −0.082), and increased starch concentration (β = −0.002) correlated with reduced RS yields. Among these, treatment time had a statistically significant negative effect on RS content (p = 0.033). The effects of ultrasound treatment frequency and power on RS content formation are presented in Fig. 7B-C. meta-regression analysis demonstrated that RS content increased significantly with higher ultrasonic frequency and power. The mechanisms by which low-frequency ultrasound modifies starch are complex, involving thermal, shear, and cavitation effects (Dolas et al., 2019). As the ultrasound power increased, the production of resistant starch was promoted. One explanation for this phenomenon was that high power ultrasound creates localized high temperatures that lead to the reorganization of disordered starch chains and the formation of highly ordered helical structures that are resistant to enzymatic digestion (Wang, et al., 2022). Brotchie et al. (2009) observed that mean bubble size increased with acoustic power in a multi-bubble system. Ding et al. (2019) observed a decrease in RS content in RS3 starch when subjected to ultrasound power in the range of 100–300 W. However, when power exceeded 300 W, RS content showed an increasing trend. These findings suggest that selecting appropriate ultrasound power can enhance RS content.

The effects of ultrasound treatment time, temperature, and concentration on RS content formation are presented in Fig. 7D-F. Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that RS content decreased with higher ultrasonic duration, temperature and concentration. Prolonged sonication resulted in greater structural disruption of starch granules. For instance, Jafari and Koocheki (2024) demonstrated that extended ultrasound exposure caused increased surface damage and cracking in starch granules. Higher temperatures were negatively associated with RS formation (β = −0.080), with more pronounced effects at lower temperatures. Although elevated temperatures increase the equilibrium vapor pressure and facilitate cavitation inception, the accompanying rise in vapor pressure may attenuate the energy released upon bubble collapse (Ibisi & Brown, 1967).

Ultrasonic field simulation

The acoustic pressure and acoustic pressure levels for two ultrasound frequencies (20 kHz and 40 kHz) were analyzed based on COMSOL simulation results (Fig. 8). In the ultrasonic cleaner, the maximum pressure amplitudes of 20 kHz and 40 kHz were 5.42 × 103 Pa and 15.2 Pa, respectively, and the minimum amplitudes were − 683 Pa and − 133 Pa, respectively. The maximum pressure amplitudes of the ultrasonic cell crusher were 103 Pa and 104 Pa, respectively, and the minimum amplitudes were − 232 Pa and − 147 Pa, respectively. Low-frequency ultrasound (e.g., 20 kHz) was more conducive to cavitation bubble formation than high-frequency ultrasound due to its longer rarefaction-compression cycle, which allows bubbles to grow larger before collapse (Wang, Yang, et al., 2020). Hu et al. (2015) compared ultrasonic frequencies of 20, 25, and dual 20 + 25 kHz, and aluminum foil tests indicated that 20 kHz yielded higher cavitation efficiency than 25 kHz. The COMSOL simulations also evaluated the effect of temperature (Fig. 8). Acoustic pressure was significantly influenced by temperature. Under the conditions of 20 kHz,10 % concentration and 0 °C, its acoustic pressure was 4.43× 103 Pa, which was significantly lower than the acoustic pressure of 25 °C under the same conditions (5.42 × 103 Pa). The cavitation effect ultrasonic process was accompanied by local high temperature, which may cause local gelatinization of starch (Estivi et al., 2022). To mitigate heating, samples were often placed in cold water baths (Bonto et al., 2021). However, lower temperatures reduce acoustic pressure and weaken cavitation, potentially inhibiting RS formation. Thus, temperature control was a critical factor in ultrasound-mediated starch modification, and RS yield may vary considerably with thermal conditions.

Furthermore, acoustic properties were compared between 10 % and 25 % w/v starch suspensions under both ultrasound modes (Fig. 8). Significant differences in acoustic pressure were observed between the ultrasonic cleaner and the ultrasonic cell crusher. Within the ultrasonic cleaner, the 25 % v/w starch suspension exhibited higher sound pressure than the 10 % v/w suspension. In contrast, with the ultrasonic cell crusher, the 10 % w/v suspension produced higher sound pressure. This divergence originates from fundamental structural differences: Ultrasonic probes operate optimally in single-phase media (e.g., homogeneous starch suspensions), whereas ultrasonic cleaners generated uniform acoustic fields better suited for biphasic systems like slurry starch-water (Bonto et al., 2021). This result was consistent with the meta-regression analysis, indicating that lower concentrations were more conducive to resistant starch formation.

Conclusion

Ultrasound treatment induces both structural disruption and reorganization within starch, significantly influencing RS formation. Meta-subgroup analyses indicated that fruit and cereal starches exhibited a positive effect on RS content, with MD of 5.731 and 1.579, respectively. Furthermore, increases in RS were observed in both A-type and B-type starches. Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that higher ultrasound frequency and power, combined with reduced treatment duration, lower temperature, and decreased starch concentration, positively affected RS yields. Simulation results further indicate that mild ultrasonic conditions (40 kHz, 25 °C, 10 % w/v starch suspension) generate attenuated cavitation energy fields. The impact of ultrasonic treatment on the fine structure of starch, specifically its influence on molecular chain length, constitutes a key focus for future research. These findings offer a deeper understanding of starch molecular modifications during ultrasound-assisted processing and provide valuable insights for the development of low-glycemic foods.
