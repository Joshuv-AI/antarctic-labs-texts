Community‐based survey of perceived stigma in dementia: Insights from a collectivist culture
Authors: Deniz Büyükgök, Sevde Nur Karabulut, Ata Onur Kepenek, Neşe Direk, Işın Baral‐Kulaksızoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIn collectivist cultures, internalization of negative beliefs fosters stigma. This study aimed to assess the relationship between Alzheimer's disease (AD) knowledge and perceived stigma, focusing on sociodemographic variables; gender, marital status, occupation, and caregiving.METHODSIn this cross‐sectional study, 529 participants without psychiatric or neurological disorders completed the AD knowledge scale, Affiliated Stigma Scale (ASS), and stigma survey.RESULTSAge showed no significant association with dementia knowledge or stigma. Higher education, living with family/spouse, and being dementia caregiver predicted perceived stigma. Caregivers demonstrated higher knowledge; but similar stigma levels compared to non‐caregivers which was significantly related to the ASS‐affective component. Healthcare workers also had greater knowledge but did not differ in terms of stigma.DISCUSSIONKnowledge improved by education and caregiving, but stigma persisted, shaped by emotional and cultural dynamics. These findings suggest that future interventions may benefit from integrating empathy‐focused and attitude‐based modules, as knowledge alone is insufficient to reduce stigma.

BACKGROUND

In collectivist cultures like Turkiye, one of the prominent social values is the respect for elderly family members: senescence is considered both as a period of wisdom, passing down the experience to future generations, and displays a source of empathy demonstrating what the future holds for each individual's mental and physical well‐being. While this cultural norm can create a supportive environment, it can also lead to stigma, especially for the informal caregivers (i.e., family members) of individuals with dementia. Link and Phelan (2001) define stigma as the co‐occurrence of labeling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination within a power situation. 1 In the context of Alzheimer's disease (AD), this process manifests as the systematic devaluation of both the patient and their caregivers, leading to social isolation and reduced access to care. Many elderly individuals with memory loss are neglected, partly due to the belief that forgetfulness is simply a natural part of aging. 2 This traditional attitude may lead to stigmatization and stigmatization may result with failing to receive necessary health care from the early stages, 3 worsening the prognosis 4 increasing the caregiver burden, 5 and even leading to increased mortality 6 in patients with dementia.

A recent study reported limited dementia‐related knowledge in Turkish population. 7 Besides, discordance in dementia‐knowledge across genders; where females tend to have lower level of education but more aware of dementia symptoms which shows the effect of cultural values. 8 As elderly individuals are generally cared for within the family as a sign of respect and cultural responsibility, 9 few are placed in nursing homes, which can result in societal disgrace. Consequently, the responsibility of caring for elderly individuals with cognitive decline typically falls to family members, particularly female relatives. Therefore, it is crucial to identify and educate these key groups in society, as well as to understand the factors that may contribute to stigma.

According to the World Alzheimer Report 2024, AD and other dementias are the seventh leading cause of death worldwide (WHO, 2024), yet the majority of individuals living with dementia remain undiagnosed, largely due to stigmatization. 3 There have been various types of stigmas identified, specifically in relation to dementia and their caregivers. This includes, but is not limited to, general public stigma, self‐stigma, stigma through association with the stigmatized person, provider‐based stigma and structural stigma. 10 , 11 Public stigma focuses on the general population's common social attitudes towards specific social groups 12 including patients with psychiatric or neurological disorders. 13 As individuals internalize public stigma, those may perceive prejudices, develop negative judgments and align themselves with these negative beliefs about mental illness, resulting in self‐identification with such judgements. 14 , 15 Assigning specific attributes, negative beliefs, traits (i.e., stereotypes), and emotional responses (i.e., prejudice) may conclude in social distancing and rejection of the people with dementia, internalizing shame, lowering self‐esteem, increasing anxiety and depression levels. 11 Fear of being stigmatized leads to silencing the situation and causing ignorance of the disease.

The aim of this study is to conduct a culture‐specific assessment of the relationship between the AD knowledge and perceived stigma. The main goals were descriptively analyzing the impact of sociodemographic variables, such as age, gender, marital status, occupation, and being a family caregiver of individuals with dementia. Specifically, higher education and caregiving experience are expected to be related to increased dementia knowledge and this in turn, reduce the stigma.

METHODS

Participants and procedure

Participants in this study were recruited using all three methods that are frequently employed in research. Participants invited and participated in the study by completing print‐out forms of the surveys, surveys via links shared on social media, and surveys accessed using quick response (QR) codes on the posters posted on hospital bulletin boards targeting both healthcare workers, dementia caregivers and other visitors. To ensure representativeness of wide range of social characteristics, participants aged 18 and over who were literate were included. Individuals who are under medical treatment with severe psychiatric or neurological disorders were excluded from the study.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Faculty of Medicine (date: June 13, 2025; no:3306067), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic Review: Literature search was done with the keywords Alzheimer's disease, dementia knowledge, stigma, healthcare professionals, caregivers, education. Previous studies consistently showed that higher education and health specialization improve dementia knowledge, while findings on stigma are inconsistent. Studies from collectivist societies are limited. Interpretation: In this community‐based study from Turkiye, dementia knowledge increased with education, female gender, healthcare profession, and caregiving. However, stigma (STIG‐MA, ASS) was resistant to these factors. Emotional aspects of affiliate stigma emerged as a key contributor suggesting that knowledge alone is insufficient to reduce stigma. Future Direction: Anti‐stigma strategies should address emotional burden and cultural norms in addition to providing knowledge. Future research should evaluate stigma‐focused interventions for healthcare professionals and caregivers, not only by measuring changes in knowledge but also by assessing improvements in clinical practice, caregiver support strategies, and health service delivery.

Measures

Sociodemographic data form

A sociodemographic data form was developed by the researchers, which included questions regarding participants’ age, gender, educational level, marital and employment status, chronic medical conditions, and history of psychiatric treatment. Additional questions included whether participants are healthcare professionals, had a family history of dementia, and ever provided care for a person with dementia, as these factors may affect the level of knowledge and stigma. This information was used to describe the sample and to explore potential associations between sociodemographic variables and other measures included in the study.

Alzheimer's Disease Knowledge Scale

The Alzheimer's Disease Knowledge Scale (ADKS) was employed to evaluate participants' understanding of AD, a crucial aspect in assessing awareness and stigma related to the condition. Originally developed by Carpenter et al. (2009), the scale consists of 30 true/false items. 16 Each correct answer scores 1 point, yielding a total score between 0 and 30, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. Turkish validated form was used 17 which is reported with high reliability ratios for the ADKS‐Tr.

The Affiliated Stigma Scale

The Affiliated Stigma Scale assesses stigma experienced by family members of individuals with psychiatric or cognitive disorders. 18 It consists of 22 items grouped under three subdimensions: affective, behavioral, and cognitive. Higher total scores indicate greater levels of affiliate stigma perceived by the respondent. Items are rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of affiliated stigma. The psychometric properties of the ASS have been studied in dementia patients. 19 In the Turkish validation study, the original three‐factor structure expanded into four factors: others‐related/cognitive, affective, self‐related, and behavioral. 20

Stigma survey

Perceived stigma related to AD was assessed using the 10‐item stigma scale for AD (STIG‐MA), originally developed by Piver et al. (2012). 21 Participants respond to the scale by imagining themselves as individuals diagnosed with AD. The questions were answered as “yes, maybe, I don't know, no” and scored between 0‐3. The overall score reflected stigma severity, with a maximum of 30 points. Scores of 0‐7 indicated mild stigma, 8‐11 moderate stigma, and 12 or higher high stigma. In this current study, the Turkish version of the STIG‐MA 22 was used to evaluate the level of stigma participants perceived when imagining themselves as individuals diagnosed with AD.

Data analysis

The aim of the study was to recruit a representative sample of the population and assess the level of Alzheimer's knowledge and stigmatization in the population. Therefore, the effects of sociodemographic variables on the scale scores across the entire sample were first to examine. Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As might be expected in a heterogeneous population, dementia caregivers and healthcare workers (i.e., physicians, psychologists, nurses) were grouped and compared with other participants. Parametric tests (t‐test) were used for normally distributed variables, while nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis) were applied for non‐normally distributed variables. Chi‐squared (χ 2) tests were used for categorical variables.

One‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to examine associations between stigma scale scores and sociodemographic variables. Pearson (r) or Spearman (rs ) correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationship between disease knowledge and stigma. Linear regression with enter selection included sociodemographic variables (age, gender, education, marital status) as covariates to identify predictors of dementia knowledge and stigmatization, regardless of significance. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v30.0.

RESULTS

In this study, 532 participants completed the survey; due to missing data exceeding 5% of the total survey, 529 participants' data were included in this study. The sample consisted of 184 male (34.8%) and 325 female (65.2%) participants. The education levels were equal between male and female participants in the overall sample (χ = 1.125; p = 0.771). The age range of the sample group was between 18 and 83, with the mean of 42.58 (SD = 13.00). Healthcare professionals constitute 17.5% (n = 83) of the total sample group; including physicians, nurses, and psychologists. Among the total sample, there were 80 participants who had a family member with dementia to care for and 65% of them (n = 52) were female. A large majority of the sample were married, lived with a partner or family (79.8%) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to cross‐sectionally explore the relationship between Alzheimer's dementia knowledge and stigma within the context of Turkish culture. Knowledge was positively correlated with education, female gender, caregiving and healthcare profession, but stigma remains unaffected by these sociodemographics.

One of the prominent findings of this study was that dementia knowledge scores of female were higher than those of males, consistent with repeatedly reported higher dementia awareness among women. 23 , 24 These gender‐based differences in dementia knowledge may include the cross‐cultural ascription of caregiving roles to women and their greater interest in health issues. This study results also revealed a culturally significant finding: Among participants in our study, there was no gender‐specific difference in education, as compared to studies conducted in Turkiye at the early 2000s, 8 it is evident that the education levels of women in our society have increased in last 20 years, but their informal caregiving roles for the family persist. Regarding the role of age in dementia knowledge (ADKS), our findings showed a nonsignificant trend but with high effect size indicating that age may still be a practically meaningful factor in determining knowledge levels. The lack of statistical significance despite the high effect magnitude suggests that the relationship between age and dementia knowledge is substantial, but our study might have been limited by uneven age distribution in the sample. While we successfully recruited a large and diverse group across different age, education categories and professions, and the goal of representativeness was partially achieved, the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their generalizability.

Among the key socio‐demographic factors, education level was associated with knowledge of dementia as previously reported in different cultures. 25 , 26 It is concluded that higher education increased health literacy and access to accurate information about dementia. According to the Turkish Statistical Institute (TUIK), among the population aged 25 years and older in Turkiye, 46.5% have completed high school or higher education (reported as ‘12‐15 years of education’ in this study). In the Turkish context, the disparity in education levels may be particularly pronounced in middle‐aged and older age groups, and therefore, community‐based awareness campaigns should be tailored to appeal to middle‐aged and older people. Although the effects of gender and education align with previous studies, our results indicating no significant relationship between age and both knowledge and stigma contradicted earlier research. 27 Besides, a recent detailed study suggested that dementia knowledge is associated with reduced stigma, but only among younger adults. 28 Thus, this discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the sample in the current study predominantly consisted of individuals in younger to middle adulthood. In contrast, it is quite striking that stigmatization (both with STIG‐MA and ASS) was not correlated with either education level or gender. This aligns with some contradictory discussions in the literature; stigma is more explained by cultural norms, personal beliefs, and emotional factors. 29

Although marital status is a sociodemographic information asked in almost all stigma studies, its impact is rarely discussed. 30 It is often considered as a covariate and its effect being adjusted for, yet the difference observed in this study suggests that its impact on stigma should be specifically evaluated rather than ignored. In this study, participants who were married, living with a family member or a partner had significantly lower stigma scores than those living alone. Stigmatization may likely to be more pronounced for those experiencing caregiving for the first time through patient care. The experience of caring for each other as a family member thought to mitigate stigmatization during the conditions like an illness.

Professional pursuits can be considered to have an impact on knowledge and perception of health issues. The significantly higher ADKS scores among healthcare professionals compared to other professional groups can be explained, as expected, by medical education and clinical experience. However, notably, the fact that healthcare professionals' STIG‐MA and ASS scores did not differ from other professional groups suggested that medical knowledge alone does not reduce stigma, which is also consistent with similar examples in literature. 31 In particular, healthcare professionals in this study scored above the threshold reported in the cultural validation study of STIG‐MA 22 and that was even higher than the other participants, even though no significant difference was found. Similar to the healthcare professionals, family caregivers of dementia patients had higher ADKS scores, but in terms of stigma (i.e., STIG‐MA) they did not significantly differ from participants who were not caregivers. STIG‐MA scale assesses not only the empathy towards dementia patients but also the anticipated stigma they may encounter. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that higher levels of knowledge may paradoxically influence AD related stigma. The study by Stites et al. (2025) observed that caregivers demonstrated higher levels of stigma as the factual knowledge of AD increased. 32 Though cultural differences may serve in different ways, the lack of correlation between dementia knowledge and stigma in our sample may be driven more by deeply rooted social perceptions, emotional reactions, or cultural beliefs rather than mere factual knowledge in collectivist cultures like ours.

Accordingly, affiliate stigma (assessed with ASS) refers to the extent of self‐stigmatization among associates of the targeted minorities. 33 For further, ASS's, four factors structure (others‐related/cognitive, affective, self‐related, and behavioral) suggests that in collectivist cultures, stigma‐related cognitions may be experienced differently depending on whether they concern others’ evaluations or one's own self‐concept.

The observation that the ASS‐Affective subscale accounted for nearly one‐fifth of the variance in caregivers’ stigma highlights the importance of emotional processes in shaping stigmatizing experiences. Thus, stigma may be more related to emotional, cultural, and social attitudes than cognitive knowledge, and highlights the need to integrate specific experiential modules targeting attitudes. Moreover, the stigma scales scores of the dementia patient caregivers show that they become less affected by stigmatization as they gain information about the disease; however, this does not protect them from feeling emotionally stigmatized. It is also possible that caregiver burden, as reported in previous studies, 34 may be related to the unspoken emotional dimension of stigmatization. Self‐stigma, importantly, may lead to avoidance of obtaining information; however, coping with the emotional weight of self‐stigma reduces avoidance behaviors and brings about actions that serve regulation. 35 Thus, the correlation between stigma scale and the ASS, which measures the internalized (i.e., perceived) stigma with tailored subcomponents may explain persistent stigma despite increased knowledge in dementia caregivers.

The present findings indicate that dementia knowledge is clearly affected by education, gender, and caregiving roles. In contrast, stigma appears less responsive to these sociodemographic variables and is instead shaped primarily by cultural norms and emotional dynamics that are particularly salient in collectivist societies. Targeted interventions that move beyond knowledge acquisition toward addressing attitudes and emotional burden became prominent needs in the battle against dementia.

Notably, the persistence of stigma among healthcare workers despite their higher knowledge indicates that informational approaches are insufficient within professional training, necessitating interventions that specifically target attitudes and implicit biases in clinical contexts.

These findings underscore that dementia‐related stigma persists despite higher levels of knowledge, particularly among healthcare professionals and caregivers. Translationally, this suggests that health service planning should incorporate interventions that go beyond information delivery. Structured anti‐stigma workshops, empathy‐focused training modules, and role‐playing exercises that directly address implicit biases and emotional responses could be integrated into dementia care programs. Such approaches may be more effective in reducing stigma and improving the quality of care than traditional knowledge‐based education alone.

Strengths and limitations

This study's large sample size, being one of the few studies in Turkey measuring dementia knowledge, is a strength. Furthermore, the lack of personal information and the large number of online surveys ensured that participants responded candidly and honestly. However, due to its cross‐sectional design, causality cannot be established between the results. On the other hand, although the age range of the participant group was wide, the concentration of frequency in the younger group may have underpowered the age effect on dementia knowledge and affiliated stigma. Further studies may include more balanced age subgroup distributions to confirm these trends.
