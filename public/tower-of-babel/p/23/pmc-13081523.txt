Protocatechualdehyde-functionalized BMSC-derived exosomes orchestrate calcium signaling in the immunoinflammatory microenvironment to promote periodontal regeneration
Authors: Le Fan, Tianyou Wang, Meirui Ma, Yiqiao Wang, Xiaodong Wang, Xuan Tang, Yuanyuan Ma, Zhipeng Gu, Zhichao Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe significant global burden of periodontitis has increased over the past few decades. However, the regeneration and reconstruction of periodontal tissue remain major challenges in clinical practice. Successful periodontal treatment relies on the regulation of calcium signaling. Bone mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC Exo) hold great potential for promoting calcium influx to enhance osteogenic differentiation, modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, and promote angiogenesis, but their therapeutic efficiency is limited by inadequate stability. To overcome this problem, a mild and facile approach using protocatechualdehyde was employed to modify BMSC Exo (PA@BMSC Exo) through covalent and noncovalent polyphenolic interactions. The resulting PA@BMSC Exo system enhanced the stability against external stress and augmented its ability to induce intracellular calcium entry. Osteogenesis in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) and integrated periodontal regeneration in periodontitis were observed following PA@BMSC Exo treatment. Furthermore, these multifunctional PA@BMSC Exo also promoted immunoinflammatory regulation and neovascularization. Bioinformatic analysis based on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) predicted that the osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs was enhanced by calcium influx mediated through the voltage-gated calcium channel Cav3.3. Intracellular Ca2+ levels were further validated in hPDLSCs, as well as in RAW264.7 cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Overall, a facile engineered PA@BMSC Exo system was successfully developed to reverse periodontal destruction by orchestrating calcium signaling and the entire periodontal microenvironment, potentially opening a new avenue for the clinical treatment of chronic periodontitis.Graphical Abstract Schematic overview of nanopore features that can be investigated using bioinformatic tools. Introduction

Periodontitis is the sixth most prevalent human disease worldwide that affects 11.2 % of the global adult population and imposes a significant healthcare, social, and economic burden [1, 2]. As a pathogen-induced inflammatory disease, chronic periodontitis leads to gingival inflammation, progressive destruction of attachment tissues and alveolar bone, and eventually tooth looseness and loss [3]. Current treatment strategies, such as scaling, root planing and local antimicrobial therapy, are employed to inhibit the progression of chronic inflammation [4]. However, the regeneration and reconstruction of periodontal alveolar bone loss are still great challenges to overcome in clinical practice [5].

Recent evidence highlights that calcium serves as a crucial second messenger that regulates multiple cellular biological processes involving the regeneration of periodontal alveolar bone [6–9]. The Ca2+ signaling pathway regulates mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) self-renewal and delays senescence, with dose-dependent calcium influx rejuvenating aged MSCs. Hydrogen sulphide activates the transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 4 (TRPV4) channel-mediated calcium flux, thereby rejuvenating aged human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) [10]. Elevated levels of intracellular Ca2+ via calcium influx also regulate bone formation by stimulating cell recruitment, proliferation, differentiation, and mineralization. Wang et al. constructed an electroactive membrane that spatiotemporally promotes the recruitment and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs via Cav3.2 channel-mediated intracellular calcium influx [11]. In the inflammatory microenvironment, the activation of the TRPM2 ion channel regulated osteogenesis of BMSCs via evoking Ca2+ influx and enhanced alveolar bone healing process of tooth extraction sockets of patients with periodontitis [6]. Moreover, Ca2+ can modulate the inflammatory microenvironment to promote osteogenesis via the transition of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages and upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as transforming growth factor (TGF-β) and interleukin 10 (IL-10) [12]. Additionally, endogenous Ca2+ release maintains intracellular Ca2+ levels through inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors, along with store-operated Ca2+ entry via Orai1 channels. This process stimulates the proliferation of endothelial progenitor cells and facilitates tube formation, thereby promoting angiogenesis [13]. Taken together, calcium influx may represent a novel target for regulating the periodontal microenvironment to enhance regeneration.

MSCs residing in periodontal tissues play a crucial role in periodontal regeneration. Owing to their remarkable abilities for self-renewal, multipotentiality, immunomodulation, and tissue regeneration, MSCs have been considered as promising cell source for periodontal regeneration [14]. The role of MSCs in regenerative medicine is primarily attributed to their paracrine functions, which involve the release of bioactive substances rather than direct proliferation and differentiation at the injury site [15]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), the major paracrine effectors, have attracted considerable attention as crucial messengers in intercellular and interorgan communications. They modulate multiple cellular responses through exchanging their heterogeneous contents, such as proteins, lipids, nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, and miRNA), metabolites, and even organelles [16, 17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MSC-derived EVs enhanced the repair and regeneration of damaged tissues by enhancing intracellular calcium influx. For instance, in a diabetic wound healing model, Wang et al. found that human umbilical cord MSC-derived small extracellular vesicles (hucMSC sEVs) could deliver the transcription factor SP2, which subsequently activated TRPC6 expression by binding to its specific promoter regions (−1519 to −1725 bp) and restored Ca2+ influx [18]. Similarly, systemic infusion of apoptotic EVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells upregulated Piezo1 expression and evoked Ca2+ influx, thereby rescuing mechanical unloading-induced apoptotic resistance and senescence in bone marrow cells [19]. Despite that MSC EVs have great therapeutic potential to regulate calcium influx in periodontal regeneration, their drawbacks, including limited stability and aggregation, have hampered their efficacy and clinical application [20, 21].

At present, surface engineering strategies primarily encompass noncovalent and covalent modifications of EV membranes (direct modification), as well as genetic manipulation of parent cells (indirect manipulation). These strategies have been developed to address these challenges and to impart additional functions to EVs [22]. However, the available approaches tend to be complex and often require harsh conditions [23, 24]. Polyphenols shed new light on the surface modification of EVs to preserve the integrity of their membrane and functionality under mild and easy-to-handle conditions [20]. Intriguingly, the catechol or galloyl groups of polyphenols provide chelation sites for metal ions, including Ca2+, which may further facilitate the intracellular calcium influx [25]. A predictive quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) model has confirmed that polyphenolic flavonoids can activate voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCC) in osteogenesis [26]. Notably, polyphenols also confer antioxidative and antimicrobial functionalities to EVs, enhancing their potential for periodontal regeneration [27]. In this study, a PA@BMSC Exo system was constructed by coating BMSC EVs with polyphenolic protocatechuic aldehyde (PA) for enhenced long-term stability and efficacy. To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms by which PA@BMSC Exo promoted osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs, we further investigated the profile of functional genes of hPDLSCs in the control and PA@BMSC Exo groups via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Based on KEGG enrichment pathway analysis and previous studies, we focused on the remarkably enriched calcium signaling pathway and its upregulated key effector gene CACNA1I (Cav3.3) relative to osteogenesis in hPDLSCs. The calcium influx in hPDLSCs, as well as in RAW264.7 cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was further assessed to reveal the mechanism of PA@BMSC Exo on osteogenesis, immunomodulation, and angiogenesis. Collectively, these findings provided a novel and multifunctional cell-free approach utilizing PA@BMSC Exo for the periodontal regeneration (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Characterization of BMSC Exo and PA@BMSC Exo

We firstly successfully isolated the BMSC-derived exosomes and identified them via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Western blotting (Fig. 1A and Figure S1). Then, BMSC Exo were successfully functionalized with the polyphenolic compound PA to construct PA@BMSC Exo nanoplatform. Under alkaline conditions, PA underwent oxidative polymerization and covalent Schiff base reactions, while simultaneously engaging in non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic forces. It led to the formation of a stable polyphenolic coating on the surface of the BMSC Exo. TEM analysis revealed a well-defined, shell-like layer approximately 10 nm thick encasing the exosomes (Fig. 1B), indicating a successful and uniform surface modification. As a control, polymerized PA nanoparticles (PPA NPs) were also synthesized in the absence of exosomes (Fig. 1C). The slight increase in hydrodynamic diameter and the decrease in zeta potential (Fig. 1D and E) further confirmed the successful deposition of the polyphenolic layer, with negligible impact on colloidal stability. Notably, both PA@BMSC Exo and PPA NPs exhibited similar surface charge profiles, suggesting the formation of a dense and homogeneous coating on the exosome surface.

Persistent infection leads to accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the periodontal tissues. Previous studies have shown that the elimination of ROS can efficiently mitigate the inflammatory response and restore the periodontal destruction [28]. Functionally, both aqueous ABTS and organic DPPH radical scavenging assays demonstrated that both PA@BMSC Exo and PPA NPs exhibited significantly enhanced radical scavenging activity compared to unmodified BMSC Exo (Fig. 1F and G), highlighting the role of the polyphenolic coating in regulating oxidative stress at pathological sites. Then, the intracellular antioxidative behaviors of PA@BMSC Exo in RAW264.7 were assessed using 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA)-based immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that PPA NPs significantly suppressed ROS production compared to controls, while BMSC Exo alone had no significant effect. Notably, PA@BMSC Exo effectively scavenged excess ROS to comparable level of PPA NPs, demonstrating that PPA surface modification conferred antioxidative activity to the BMSC Exo (Figure S2). Importantly, preserving EV stability plays a crucial role in the clinical translation of EV-based therapies [29]. We found that PA@BMSC Exo maintained stability under various environmental challenges, including physiological temperature, UV irradiation, and acidic pH conditions (Fig. 1H-K), demonstrating robust structural integrity. This enhanced stability may be attributed to the protective polyphenolic shell, which could facilitate the therapeutic application of exosomes in complex physiological and pathological environments [20]. Notably, the polyphenolic modification did not compromise the internalization of BMSC Exo by RAW264.7 cells, HUVECs, and hPDLSCs (Fig. 1L), that demonstrated the great potential of PA@BMSC Exo for in vivo biotherapeutic applications. To assess biocompatibility, hPDLSCs were cultured in the media containing PPA, BMSC Exo, and PA@BMSC Exo at a concentration of 25 µg/mL. Quantitative CCK-8 assays demonstrated that cell viability in the three experimental groups did not differ significantly from that of the control group. The Live/Dead viability assay showed that hPDLSCs grew and proliferated well on day 3 (Figure S3).

Antibacterial activity of PA@BMSC Exo in vitro

Bacteria are the well-known initiating factors in the development of periodontitis [30]. To evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of PA@BMSC Exo NPs, we selected two major periodontal pathogens: the Gram-negative bacteria Porphyromonas gingivalis (P.g) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (F.n). Both PPA and PA@BMSC Exo demonstrated strong antibacterial effects against these pathogens, significantly surpassing the control and BMSC Exo groups (Fig. 2A). Compared to the control and BMSC Exo groups, the live/dead fluorescence imaging revealed that PPA and PA@BMSC Exo groups showed a dramatic decrease in viable bacteria (green fluorescence) and a corresponding increase in dead bacteria (red fluorescence) (Fig. 2B). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to further show membrane morphological changes of bacteria with different treatments. Untreated P.g and F.n displayed smooth, intact membranes, while BMSC Exo treatment alone caused minimal structural alternation. In contrast, PPA and PA@BMSC Exo treatments induced severe membrane destruction and fragmentation (Fig. 2C). When the bacterial plasma membrane is damaged, the metabolic activities of various transmembrane enzymes are compromised, leading to the death of bacteria. Polyphenols could increase membrane permeability through physical destabilization of the membrane [31]. A previous study demonstrated that the destruction of cytoplasmic membrane integrity by PA further led to intracellular instability, membrane depolarization, and reduction in intracellular pH [32]. These findings robustly confirmed that polyphenolic PA was the main factor contributing to the antibacterial activity of PA@BMSC Exo nanoparticles against P.g and F.n.

Osteogenic ability of PA@BMSC Exo in vitro

The osteogenic differentiation capacity of hPDLSCs, a type of MSC with self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation abilities, may compensate for periodontal supporting tissue defects [33]. Thus, we evaluated the osteogenesis of hPDLSCs in vitro. After 10 days of culture, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining demonstrated significantly enhanced osteogenic differentiation in hPDLSCs treated with PPA and BMSC Exo compared to negative controls. Notably, the PA@BMSC Exo group exhibited ALP staining intensity comparable to that of the positive control (cells cultured in osteogenic induction medium without P.g LPS) (Fig. 3A). Fluorescence imaging further revealed that treatments with BMSC Exo or PA@BMSC Exo markedly increased the expression of early osteogenic markers, including ALP and RUNX2 (Fig. 3B). These observations were consistent with the qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 3C) which demonstrated that PPA and BMSC Exo treatments showed greater upregulation of ALP, RUNX2, osteopontin (OPN) and osteocalcin (OCN), with a tendency for further increase following PA@BMSC Exo treatment. Likewise, after 21 days of culture, alizarin red staining revealed substantially greater calcium deposition in the PA@BMSC Exo group compared to all other treatments (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis using cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) extraction validated that PA@BMSC Exo system synergistically promotes matrix mineralization (Figure S4). In fact, polyphenolic compounds could promote the cell migration and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs which participate in the healing process of alveolar bone [34]. A similar effect of BMSC-derived small extracellular vesicles has been confirmed in a previous study as well [35]. Liu et al. further found that exosomal lncRNA HCP5 derived from human bone marrow stromal cells (hBMSCs) enhanced osteogenesis of hPDLSCs through the miR-24-3p/HO1/P38/ELK1 signaling pathway [36].

The PA@BMSC Exo nanoparticles facilitate integrated periodontal regeneration and osteogenesis in periodontitis

Based on the favorable oxidative stress regulation, bactericidal activity, and osteogenic-promoting capability of constructed PA@BMSC Exo, the progression of periodontitis and subsequent tissue regeneration were systematically evaluated through micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Micro-CT analysis (Fig. 4A and B) demonstrated that the cementoenamel junction-alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) distances in the control group (day 14: 0.6302 ± 0.0476; day 28: 0.8055 ± 0.0321) significantly exceeded those in the sham group (day 14: 0.3204 ± 0.0158; day 28: 0.3430 ± 0.0129). Both PPA (day 14: 0.5116 ± 0.0188; day 28: 0.6007 ± 0.0166) and BMSC Exo treatment (day 14: 0.4034 ± 0.0197; day 28: 0.4576 ± 0.0281) significantly reduced the CEJ-ABC distances. The PA@BMSC Exo group exhibited the most pronounced reduction in CEJ-ABC distances (day 14: 0.3593 ± 0.0081; day 28: 0.3850 ± 0.0223), indicating enhanced periodontal regeneration at 14- and 28-days post-surgery. Additionally, the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) ratio was significantly elevated in BMSC Exo (day 14: 51.0610 ± 2.0069; day 28: 46.3851 ± 3.7881) and PA@BMSC Exo groups (day 14: 57.1555 ± 2.7475; day 28: 53.9727 ± 2.9990) compared to that in the control group (day 14: 39.9045 ± 3.6071; day 28: 32.3450 ± 2.4781), with PA@BMSC Exo achieving the maximal BV/TV values at both time points. H&E staining (Fig. 4C) further revealed attenuated alveolar bone resorption and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration in periodontal ligaments following treatments with PPA and BMSC Exo. The most significant restoration and reconstruction of periodontal tissue were evident in the PA@BMSC Exo group. In addition to the osteogenic ability of PA@BMSC Exo on hPDLSCs in vitro, we further assessed the expression and localization of osteogenic markers, such as OPN and OCN, around the alveolar bone crest in vivo. Consistent with in vitro findings, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the expression of both osteogenic markers in the control group significantly decreased compared to that of sham group, indicating compromised bone formation in periodontitis. A novel barrier collagen membrane was constructed through layer-by-layer assembly technique which innovatively used the commercial bifunctional linker 4-(bromomethyl) phenylboronic acid to integrate polyphenols with minocycline. This composite membrane enhanced periodontal bone regeneration under infection conditions [34]. Huang et al. found that PA effectively alleviated bone destruction of LPS-induced calvarial osteolysis in vivo as well [37]. Local injection of hBMSCs Exo reversed alveolar bone loss in experimental periodontitis in vivo [36, 38]. Intriguingly, treatment with PA@BMSC Exo significantly upregulated the expression of both OPN and OCN, exceeding the effects observed with either PPA or BMSC Exo treatment (Fig. 4D). In summary, the PA@BMSC Exo system mitigated inflammation-induced bone destruction and augmented osteogenesis in periodontitis owing to the synergistic effects of both PA and BMSC Exo.

Immunomodulatory activity of PA@BMSC Exo in vitro

In the inflammatory periodontal microenvironment, immune cells are recruited and activated by bacterial pathogens during the onset of periodontitis. The overactivation of the host immune response directly promotes osteoclastic activity and alveolar bone loss [39]. The phenotype transformation from M1 phase to M2 appears to be an adjustable and promising event that avoids excessive inflammatory responses and initiates early healing stage. To assess the immunomodulatory properties of PA@BMSC Exo, an in vitro inflammatory model was established using LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. Compared to the positive control (non-LPS-treated cells), LPS treatment (negative control) significantly upregulated the expression of M1 polarization markers and pro-inflammatory factors, thereby validating the successful construction of the inflammatory model in vitro (Fig. 5A–D). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that both PPA and BMSC Exo treatments effectively modulated macrophage polarization, as evidenced by significant suppression of iNOS (M1 marker) and enhanced CD206 (M2 marker) expression in RAW264.7 cells. Remarkably, PA@BMSC Exo treatment exhibited superior immunomodulatory effects, showing more pronounced iNOS reduction and greater CD206 induction compared to either PPA or BMSC Exo alone (Fig. 5A). This phenotypic switching was further validated at the transcriptional level by qRT-PCR, where PA@BMSC Exo treatment significantly downregulated key M1-associated markers (CD86, IL-1β, iNOS, and TNF-α) while upregulating M2-related genes (ARG1, CD206, and TGF-β3) compared to controls (Fig. 5B and C). Cytokine profiling by ELISA demonstrated that both PPA NPs and BMSC Exo reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β) while promoting the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Notably, PA@BMSC Exo treatment elicited the most potent anti-inflammatory response in P.g LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells among all groups, indicating a synergistic suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators coupled with enhanced anti-inflammatory cytokine production (Fig. 5D). A previous study also confirmed that PA-modified cellulose acetate membrane enhanced the polarization of pro-healing M2 macrophages, regulating the osteoimmune microenvironment for bone formation [40]. BMSC Exo incorporated on tannic acid (TA) modified sulfonated polyetheretherketone (SPEEK) have been demonstrated to promote M2 polarization and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines release through negatively modulating the NF‐κB pathway [38]. Consistently, miRNAs in hBMSC-derived exosomes suppressed P.g-triggered inflammatory response in macrophages and enhanced M2 macrophage polarization in vitro [41]. PA@BMSC Exo NPs exert a coordinated immunomodulatory effect by functionally integrating immunomodulation ability of PA with BMSC Exo.

Pro-angiogenic effect of the PA@BMSC exo in vitro

To evaluate the pro-angiogenic capacity of PA@BMSC Exo NPs under inflammatory conditions, we conducted comprehensive in vitro functional assays using HUVECs. Cell migration assays revealed that both PPA and BMSC Exo treatments significantly enhanced HUVEC migration, with PA@BMSC Exo demonstrating the most pronounced effect (Fig. 6A). The results suggested that PA@BMSC Exo NPs possessed an enhanced ability to recruit vascular endothelial cells under pathological conditions. PA@BMSC Exo treatment also substantially increased HUVEC tube formation, as evidenced by quantitative analyses of the number of nodes, segments, and total vascular network length, followed by BMSC Exo and PPA treatments (Fig. 6B and C). Correspondingly, qRT-PCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of pro-angiogenic factors in the PA@BMSC Exo group, including interleukin-8 (IL-8), vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A), vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) (Fig. 6D). The metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) integrating Cu2+ and PA enhanced tube formation and VEGF secretion of HUVECs [42]. A previous study also found that BMSCs increased the expression levels of VEGF, VEGFR2, Ang-1, and Tie-2 in HUVECs [43]. These results demonstrated that PA@BMSC Exo synergistically combined the pro-angiogenic properties of both PA and BMSC Exo, effectively promoting endothelial cell migration and vascular network formation in vitro. This composite system represented a promising strategy for therapeutic angiogenesis under inflammatory conditions.

Immunomodulatory and pro-angiogenic abilities of PA@BMSC Exo in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo immunomodulatory properties of PA@BMSC Exo, immunohistochemical staining was performed. The results revealed distinct macrophage polarization among the experimental groups. The control group, characterized by features of inflammation progression, exhibited pronounced M1 macrophage infiltration (iNOS-positive cells) with minimal presence of M2 macrophages (CD206-positive cells) (Fig. 7A), accompanied by significantly elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α compared to the sham group. Notably, both the PPA and BMSC Exo groups promoted a homeostatic M1/M2 macrophage phenotype balance while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression compared to controls. However, the most distinct immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects were observed in PA@BMSC Exo group (Fig. 7B). These in vivo findings not only corroborated our previous in vitro results but also demonstrated the superior synergistic effects achieved by combining PA with BMSC Exo. Likewise, PA induced M2 macrophage-dominated immune microenvironment at the bone defect site in vivo [40]. Additionally, TA-SPEEK scaffolds loaded with BMSC Exo enhanced osseointegration in vivo [38]. This dual action not only effectively reprograms macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype but also creates a favorable anti-inflammatory microenvironment that is conducive to periodontal regeneration.

A tightly orchestrated crosstalk between macrophage-driven immunomodulation and neovascularization networks is essential during periodontal repair. Impairment of vascular functions occurs in the inflammatory periodontal microenvironment, which compromises the process of periodontal regeneration as well [44]. Adequate vessel supply is crucial for successful periodontal treatment through cellular and molecular regulation [45]. Therefore, we evaluated the pro-angiogenic capacity of PA@BMSC Exo in periodontitis. Compared to the sham group, the control group exhibited significantly suppressed CD31 and VEGFA expression, indicating progressive microangiopathy (Fig. 7C). Notably, while PPA and BMSC Exo moderately elevated CD31 and VEGFA levels, PA@BMSC Exo induced maximal expression of these angiogenic factors specifically adjacent to the alveolar bone crest, demonstrating precision-targeted vascular regeneration. The poly (protocatechualdehyde)-coated gallium-doped bioactive glass nanoparticles promoted the endothelial cell migration and proliferation, as well as VEGF secretion within the wound tissue [46]. Exosomal miR-21-5p [43] and miR-486 [47] derived from BMSCs promote angiogenesis following ischemic stroke injury. Overall, the PA@BMSC Exo system enhances neovascularization in the periodontal tissue.

PA@BMSC Exo system regulates osteogenesis, immunomodulation, and angiogenesis through Cav3.3-mediated calcium influx

Given the osteogenic effect of PA@BMSC Exo in periodontitis, we further explored the underlying mechanism using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). The bioinformatic analysis based on RNA-seq results is presented in Fig. 8. The volcano plots (Fig. 8A and B) displayed a total of 261 differentially expressed genes (|Log2 FC|>1.0, Q value < 0.05). Furthermore, KEGG functional enrichment analysis revealed the top 20 signaling pathways involved in this biological process (Fig. 8C and D), indicating that the calcium signalling pathway might play an important role in the biological process. Subsequently, the heatmap (Fig. 8E) involving calcium signaling pathway demonstrated that the expression level of the key effector gene CACNA1I (also known as Cav3.3) was profoundly upregulated in the PA@BMSC Exo group compared to the control group.

The immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses were performed to confirm the results obtained from RNA-seq. Western blot analysis confirmed the upregulation of Cav3.3 expression in hPDLSCs under co-culture with PPA NPs and BMSC Exo compared to control. Notably, the expression level in the PA@BMSC Exo group was significantly higher than in the groups treated with PPA NPs or BMSC Exo alone, suggesting a synergistic interaction between PPA NPs and BMSC Exo (Fig. 8F). Consistent with the Western blot results, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated elevated Cav3.3 expression in the PPA and BMSC Exo groups, while the PA@BMSC Exo group exhibited the highest expression level (Fig. 9A). To determine whether PA@BMSC Exo enhances intracellular calcium accumulation in hPDLSCs through activation of Cav3.3 channels, we conducted live-cell imaging using Fluo-4 AM, a calcium-sensitive fluorescent probe. The results revealed higher intensity in the PPA and BMSC Exo groups compared to the control group, while a significantly enhanced calcium ion influx was observed in the PA@BMSC Exo group (Fig. 9B). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significantly lower Cav3.3 expression in the control group versus the sham group (Fig. 9C), indicating compromised calcium influx. Both the PPA and BMSC Exo groups exhibited elevated Cav3.3 expression, whereas the PA@BMSC Exo group displayed predominant enrichment of Cav3.3-positive cells adjacent to the alveolar bone crest. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PA@BMSC Exo synergistically enhances calcium influx via Cav3.3 upregulation. This coordination arises from PPA NPs potentiating the bioactivity of BMSC Exo, surpassing the individual effect of each component. Voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) play a crucial role in the maintenance of calcium homeostasis. An increase in intracellular calcium concentration via L-type VGCCs has been reported to enhance osteogenic differentiation of undifferentiated periodontal ligament cells [48]. Meanwhile, Cav3.3, a member of the low-voltage-activated T-type VGCC family, regulates the calcium signaling cascade and maintains mitochondrial calcium homeostasis through direct interaction with MICU1 [49]. Additionally, Cav3.2 channel, another subunit of T-type VGCCs, mediates intermittent Ca2+ transients and intracellular Ca2+ elevation, which modulate cell migration and differentiation of hPDLSCs as well [11].

B) The investigation of intracellular Ca2+ concentration stained by Fluo-4 AM probe. C) Representative immunofluorescence staining of the periodontal tissue with Cav3.3 (green fluorescence) at day 14 (n = 3)

A high level of cytosolic Ca2+, achieved by increasing the calcium influx through VGCCs, plays a crucial role in the immunoregulation [50] and angiogenesis [43, 51]. Then we measured the intracellular calcium levels in RAW264.7 cells and HUVECs as well. The immunofluorescence intensity of Cav3.3 was higher in the PPA and BMSC Exo groups compared to the control group, with the greatest increase observed in the PA@BMSC Exo group. Consistently, compared to control group, intracellular Ca2+ levels enhanced with the treatment of PA@BMSC Exo, as shown in Figure S5 and S6. When exposed to non-invasive near-infrared irradiation, this hydroxyapatite-clad titanium surface could proactively and remotely modulate the spatiotemporal osteoimmune microenvironment by activating VGCC-mediated calcium influx in macrophages [50]. With regard to pro-angiogenic effect, terahertz irradiation at 2.52 THz (100 mW/cm2) enhanced the angiogenic capacity of HUVECs by increasing the permeability of the VGCC [51]. Moreover, the T-type VGCCs, including Cav3.1 and Cav3.3, increased VEGF secretion to promote angiogenesis [43].

In summary, the excellent performance of the PA@BMSC Exo system in periodontal regeneration can be attributed to the following synergistic processes and mechanism: (1) antioxidative and antibacterial abilities of PA; (2) M2 macrophage polarization and alleviation of local inflammation; (3) neovascularization initiated by immunoinflammatory modulation; (4) alveolar bone formation induced by the synergetic effects of PA and BMSC Exo; and (5) the underlying mechanism of PA@BMSC Exo involving Cav3.3-mediated calcium influx.

Conclusion

In this study, we constructed an integrated “all-in-one” nanoplatform to modulate periodontal microenvironment via evoking Cav3.3-mediated calcium influx. The polyphenolic PA offers a mild, easy-to-handle, and biocompatible approach for surface engineering of BMSC Exo through oxidative polymerization and noncovalent and covalent interactions, thereby improving their stability and functionality. Notably, PA also facilitated calcium chelation, amplifying biological responses. The PA@BMSC Exo system demonstrated excellent antioxidative, antimicrobial, immunoinflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and osteogenic properties. Intriguingly, this platform reveals that intracellular calcium influx triggers a coordinated regenerative process, including immunoinflammatory modulation, neovascularization, and alveolar bone formation within the inflammatory microenvironment. Overall, the PA@BMSC Exo system provides a facile and effective cell-free strategy, representing a promising advancement in periodontal treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Rat BMSCs were harvested as previously described [35]. Briefly, cells were aseptically isolated from 3–4-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. Following euthanasia, the marrow cavities of both tibiae and femora were flushed with cold PBS. The harvested cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in complete culture medium (DMEM/F12) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin, then plated in 25 cm² culture flasks maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Medium was replaced every 48 h to remove non-adherent cells, with adherent BMSCs typically reaching 80–90% confluence within 7–10 days. Cells between passages 3–5 were used for all experiments. Using single-cell cloning, hPDLSCs were isolated from periodontal ligament tissue of healthy teeth. All cells were maintained in a specific medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution under standard culture conditions. To establish an in vitro inflammatory microenvironment mimicking periodontitis, all experimental procedures were conducted in the presence of 1 µg/mL Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (P.g LPS) to simulate pathological conditions.

Purification and characterization of BMSC exosomes

Upon reaching 90% confluence, BMSCs were cultured in exosome-free medium for 48 h. The conditioned medium was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300 ×g for 10 min, 2 000 ×g for 20 min, and 10 000 ×g for 30 min. Exosomes were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 110 000 ×g for 70 min at 4 ℃, resuspended in PBS, and either used immediately or stored at −80 ℃. Exosome characterization included morphological analysis using TEM (FEI TECNAI), particle size and concentration measurement via NanoFCM N30E nanoparticle tracking, and protein quantification with a BCA assay kit (Cowin, China). Additionally, western blotting confirmed expression of exosomal markers CD9 and TSG101.

Construction and characterization of PA@BMSC Exo

To prepare the polyphenol precursor solution, 100 mg of 3,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (Protocatechuic aldehyde, PA, 98%, Energy Chemical) was fully dissolved in 100 mL of pH 8.5 buffer under ultrasonic agitation. BMSC Exo were then dispersed into the PA solution at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and incubated on a shaker at 100 rpm for 24 h. During this process, PA underwent oxidative polymerization and established multiple noncovalent and covalent interactions, forming a stable polyphenolic coating on the surface of the BMSC Exo. The resulting PA@BMSC Exo were harvested by centrifugation (16 500 rpm, 20 min) and washed three times with deionized water.

For comparison, polyphenolic nanoparticles without exosomes (PPA NPs) were also synthesized. Specifically, 200 mg of PA was dissolved in a mixture of 125 mL deionized water and 5 mL acetonitrile under constant stirring. After 30 min, an equimolar amount of potassium iodate (KIO3, 310 mg, 99%, Energy Chemical) dissolved in 20 mL deionized water was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 6 h. The resulting PPA NPs were collected by centrifugation at 14 000 rpm for 10 min, followed by washing three times with deionized water.

The prepared materials were then comprehensively characterized. The morphology of PPA NPs was examined using SEM (Phenom Pro) after spin-coating 0.5 mg/mL solutions onto silicon wafers, drying, and gold sputter-coating. TEM was employed to observe PA@BMSC Exo by depositing 0.5 mg/mL sample solutions onto copper grids and drying them prior to imaging. Particle size and zeta potential measurements were performed using a Nano ZS ZEN3690 instrument with a sample concentration of 50 µg/mL.

To evaluate the stability enhancement of BMSC Exo conferred by the polyphenolic coating, PA@BMSC Exo and native BMSC Exo were subjected to various environmental conditions: UV irradiation (150 W, 360 nm, up to 30 min), acidic pH (pH 6.5, up to 72 h), and physiological temperature (37 ℃, up to 72 h). At designated time points, the remaining protein content was quantified using the BCA assay to assess structural integrity and overall stability.

ABTS and DPPH assay tests

The radical scavenging activity in the ethanol phase was evaluated using the DPPH assay. A 0.1 mM DPPH ethanol solution was prepared, along with 1 mg/mL sample solutions of PPA NPs, BMSC Exo, and PA@BMSC Exo. For the assay, 300 µL of the DPPH solution was mixed with 2.6 mL ethanol, followed by the addition of 100 µL sample solution. Absorbance at 517 nm was monitored at multiple time points over a 30-min period to assess free radical scavenging capacity.

The aqueous-phase antioxidant capacity was assessed using the ABTS assay. A 7 mM ABTS stock solution and a 2.45 mM potassium persulfate solution were mixed in a 1:2 molar ratio and allowed to react overnight in the dark at room temperature to generate ABTS•+ radicals. For testing, 100 µL of the ABTS reagent was diluted in 2.8 mL deionized water, and 100 µL of each sample (1 mg/mL) was added. Absorbance at 734 nm was measured over a 30-min period to determine the water-phase radical scavenging efficiency.

Evaluation of intracellular ROS production

RAW264.7 cells were cultured for 48 h with or without PPA, BMSC Exo, or PA@BMSC Exo (25 µg/mL, weight concentration). During the final 6 h, 100 µM H₂O₂ was added to all groups. Intracellular ROS was measured using DCFH-DA probes (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in DMEM (37 ℃, 20 min), washed thrice, then analyzed by CLSM. For fluorescence intensity measurements (Ex 488 nm/Em 525 nm), cells were resuspended in 96- well plate with fresh DMEM and measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch 2, USA).

Antibacterial activity

P.g and F.n were chosen to assess the antibacterial effectiveness of the nanomaterial formulations. Bacterial cultures were grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium enriched with yeast extract (5 mg/mL) under anaerobic conditions (5% CO₂, 10% H₂, and the remainder N₂) at 37 ℃. The bacterial suspensions were incubated with PPA, BMSC Exo, or PA@BMSC Exo (25 µg/mL) in an anaerobic chamber at 37 ℃ for 6 h. After treatment, 100 µL samples were diluted 103 times with saline and plated on blood agar plates for colony counting after 72 h. Concurrently, samples were stained using the SYTO 9/PI Live/Dead Bacterial Viability Kit (MKbio, China) to differentiate between live and dead cells through fluorescence microscopy. The ultrastructural changes in bacteria caused by the different treatments were examined using SEM. The treated bacterial cultures were gently rinsed with PBS, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) for 2 h, and then dehydrated through a series of ethanol gradients (25%, 50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for 15 min at each concentration. Critical point drying was performed, followed by gold sputter-coating, and imaging was carried out at 20 kV with a magnification of 20 000 ×.

Cellular uptake assay of PA@BMSC Exo

To track exosome internalization, purified exosomes were labeled with the lipophilic fluorescent dye DiD (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) by incubating at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Unbound dye was removed via ultracentrifugation (110 000 × g, 70 min, 4 ℃) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PA@BMSC Exo were prepared following established methods. DiD-labeled PA@BMSC Exo were co-cultured with RAW264.7, HUVECs, and hPDLSCs for 6 h. Cells were then fixed, stained with Actin-Tracker Green (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and DAPI for cytoskeletal and nuclear visualization, and imaged via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Olympus FV3000) to assess exosome internalization.

The biocompatibility of PA@BMSC Exo

The biocompatibility of PA@BMSC Exo was evaluated by analyzing hPDLSCs proliferation and viability. Cells were cultured in the media containing PPA, BMSC Exo and PA@BMSC Exo, respectively. Proliferation was measured using a CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with optical density (OD) recorded at 450 nm on day 1, 3, 5, and 7. For viability assessment, hPDLSCs cultured for 3 days were incubated with a live/dead staining (Calcein AM/PI, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 20 min at 37 °C and then imaged using CLSM.

Macrophage polarization and ELISA measurements

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in confocal dishes (1 × 10⁴ cells/dish) and 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well). Inflammatory conditions were induced by 48 h exposure to 1 µg/mL P.g LPS. Expression of CD206 (mannose receptor) and iNOS was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining with CLSM visualization. qRT-PCR quantified M1 (CD86, IL-1β, iNOS, and TNF-α)/M2 (CD206, ARG1, and TGF-β3) macrophage markers, and cytokine levels (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10) in supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Abclonal, China) according to manufacturer protocols, with concentrations determined via standard curves.

Cell migration

A transwell assay with an 8 μm pore size was utilized to evaluate cell migration. Initially, HUVECs were deprived of serum for 24 h. Then, 200 µL of a cell suspension (3 × 104 cells/mL) was added to the upper chamber, while 500 µL of various culture media was introduced to the lower chamber. After a 24 h incubation in the different culture media, the cells that migrated through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet.

Tube formation assay in vitro

The angiogenic capacity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was evaluated utilizing a tubule formation assay. Prior to the introduction of cells, 96-well plates were coated with 50 µL of Matrigel per well. HUVECs were subsequently plated at a density of 5× 103 cells per well in various culture media. Following a 6 h incubation period, the maximum tube formation was documented using an inverted microscope, and the resulting images were analyzed and quantified with ImageJ software employing the Angiogenesis Analyzer tool. Furthermore, HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well to assess the expression of genes associated with angiogenesis (IL-8, VEGF-A, VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and eNOS) through quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR).

Osteogenesis evaluation of hPDLSCs in vitro

As a positive control, human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in an osteogenic induction medium enriched with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid, and 100 nM dexamethasone (Cyagen, China). To establish pathological conditions, the experimental groups were cultured in the osteogenic induction medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL P.g LPS, and treated with or without the addition of PPA, BMSC-derived exosomes (BMSC Exo), and PA@BMSC Exo at a concentration of 25 µg/mL. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was evaluated on day 10 using the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for ALP (1:200, Abcam), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2, 1:200, Abcam) was conducted. The expression levels of osteogenic markers, including ALP, RUNX2, osteopontin (OPN), and osteocalcin (OCN), were quantified through quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). On day 21, mineralization was assessed via alizarin red staining (Cyagen, China). The images were obtained through CLSM. All experiments were performed in triplicate biological replicates to ensure reliability of the results.

Immunofluorescence staining of Cav3.3 and calcium ions in vitro

The expression of Cav3.3 in RAW264.7 cells, HUVECs, and hPDLSCs was assessed by immunofluorescence staining. Cells were cultured for 48 h in an inflammatory microenvironment induced by P.g LPS (1 µg/mL), with or without supplementation of PPA, BMSC Exo, or PA@BMSC Exo (each at 25 µg/mL). Subsequently, Cav3.3 expression was evaluated using CLSM with a specific antibody (1:200, Alomone Labs). Likewise, intracellular calcium ions (Ca²⁺) were visualized using Fluo-4 AM (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, after the 24-h culture period, cells were incubated with a working solution of Fluo-4 AM (10 µM) containing 0.01% pluronic acid in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) at 37 °C for 45 min, followed by CLSM imaging.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from BMSCs exosomes or osteogenic hPDLSCs using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of total protein (30 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following proteins: TSG101 (1:1000, Abcam), CD9 (1:1000, Abcam), Cav3.3 (1:1000, Alomone Labs). Subsequently, the membranes were probed with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000, Cell Signaling Technology), and protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (General Electric Company, USA).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from hPDLSCs using TRIzol reagent. Sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform at BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China). Raw reads were filtered with SOAPnuke, and a heatmap was generated with Pheatmap (v1.0.8) to visualize gene expression patterns across samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.4.5) with a significance threshold of Q ≤ 0.05. The annotated differentially expressed genes were subsequently subjected to KEGG and GO enrichment analyses using Phyper, based on the hypergeometric test. The significance of terms and pathways was corrected by the Q-value (Q ≤ 0.05) using Bonferroni adjustment.

Animals

All experimental procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2023-001675). Male Sprague-Dawley rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing approximately 200 ± 50 g, were randomly assigned to one of five experimental groups: sham, control, PPA, BMSC Exo, and PA@BMSC Exo (n = 5 per group). Under anesthesia induced by sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg), a 4 − 0 silk ligature was surgically placed around the maxillary second molars, ensuring that the periodontal tissue remained intact. Following a one-week post-ligation period, injections of PPA, BMSC Exo, and PA@BMSC Exo (1 µg/µL, 100 µL) were administered into the periodontal pockets twice weekly, while the sham and control groups received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The rats were euthanized at specified time points (2 and 4 weeks) to collect specimens, which were then immediately fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for subsequent histopathological examination.

Micro-CT analysis

The maxillae were subjected to scanning utilizing a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (SCANCO Micro CT, Switzerland). The scanning parameters were configured to a voltage of 80 kV, a current of 114 µA, and a resolution of 10 μm. Three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative analysis were performed using the integrated software of the system. A semi-automated methodology was implemented to delineate a region of interest (ROI) within the area of the bone defect. The distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) and the bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV) within the ROI were computed and analyzed utilizing the software’s built-in analytical tools.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

After micro-CT scanning, specimens were decalcified, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for histological assessment. Immunohistochemical staining employed antibodies against: CD206 (1:400, Abcam), iNOS (1:400, Abcam), IL-1β (1:200, Proteintech), TNF-α (1:200, Proteintech), CD31 (1:200, Proteintech), VEGF-A (1:200, Proteintech), OPN (1:200, Invitrogen), and OCN (1:200, Abcam). All sections were imaged with a Leica Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Germany). Immunofluorescence detection of Cav3.3 (1:200, Alomone Labs) was imaged via CLSM.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RN001 RNA Quick Purification Kit (ESscience, China). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with Hifair® III Reverse Transcriptase (Yeasen Biotech, China). qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Flex System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotech, China). Primer sequences for macrophage polarization (RAW264.7), pro-angiogenic (HUVECs), and osteogenic genes (hPDLSCs) are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons (SPSS 21.0, USA). A significance threshold was set at P ≤ 0.05.
