Platelet concentrates integrated with tissue engineering scaffolds: a promising approach for wound healing application
Authors: Yinglin Xu, Chunlei Dai, Jianlin Huang, Yiqun Wu, Liying Wang, Jun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound healing is a complex process involving various factors and reconstruction of tissue structures. Platelets and their bioactive substances play an indispensable role in various stages of wound healing. Tissue engineering scaffolds are instrumental in maintaining, repairing, and enhancing tissue structure and function. Combining platelet concentrates with tissue engineering scaffolds heralds a novel direction for wound healing research. In this combination, platelet concentrates serve as tissue repair facilitators, while scaffolds function as tissue support and drug release platforms. This synergistic approach produces superior tissue repair outcomes in comparison to using either method independently. As a result, it is crucial to consolidate the application of platelet concentrates with tissue engineering scaffolds in the context of wound healing. This review begins by exploring the role of platelet concentrates and tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing, while also outlining the essential criteria that scaffolds must meet. It then summarizes the application advances of platelet concentrates combined with tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing. Finally, we highlighted current limitations in translation, including interpatient variability, formulation reproducibility, and complex regulatory hurdles. Developing innovative strategies and delving deeper into potential molecular mechanisms will drive further advancements in wound healing therapies through biomaterials engineering.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The skin serves as a protective barrier between the human body and the external environment, shielding the body from the infiltration of harmful substances [1]. Skin defects or ruptures, which may result from physical or thermal injury, are defined as wounds [2]. Owing to the disruption of the skin’s tissue structure and function [3], wound healing becomes a complex biological process that involves tissue regeneration. The wound healing process comprises four overlapping stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodeling constitute the wound healing process [4, 5]. Hemostasis refers to the process of closing the wound by blood clotting. Once the skin is damaged, the vascular endothelium exposed by the injury stimulates platelets to release growth factors (GFs) and cytokines. These substances constrict the blood vessels and initiate the coagulation cascade. Activated platelets aggregate to form platelet plugs, which obstruct the rupture in the blood vessel wall. These plugs are further strengthened by fibrin, forming insoluble blood clots. Numerous cytokines, secreted by activated platelets during hemostasis, stimulate the chemotaxis of neutrophils and macrophages, thereby precipitating the onset of the inflammatory phase [6, 7]. Inflammatory cells, such as neutrophils, macrophages and leukocytes, infiltrate the injured area due to the increased capillary permeability [8]. Neutrophils release chemokines that attract monocytes from blood vessels into damaged tissue. Macrophages supply chemotactic factors, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukins [9], which promote the migration of vascular endothelial cells (EC) and fibroblasts, facilitating the proliferative phase of wound healing. During this proliferative phase, fibroblasts, deposition of new extracellular matrix, angiogenesis and formation of granulation tissue occur [10]. In the final remodeling phase, the nascent, immature granulation tissue transforms into dense, tough fibrous scar tissue. This transition results from the transformation of type III collagen fibers into type I collagen fibers and the subsequent reorientation of the fibers [11]. Each of these four processes is integral to the wound healing process.

Platelets are cell fragments shed by megakaryocytes in the final stages of development [12]. The cytoplasm of platelets contains α、δ、and λ granules [13–16]. Alpha granules encompass a range of biologically active proteins that participate in coagulation/anticoagulation, cell adhesion, immunity and inflammation [17]. GFs, cytokines and chemokines present in α granules have demonstrated the ability to promote inflammatory responses and cell recruitment, proliferation and differentiation [18]. In recent years, platelet concentrates have shown promising regenerative effect in the biomedical field [19].

Platelet concentrate is a blood derivative with elevated concentrations of platelets, fibrin, and leukocytes. The common forms of platelet concentrate primarily include platelet-rich plasma (PRP), concentrated GFs (CGFs), platelet lysate (PL) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF). To address the diverse requirements of tissue engineering, platelet concentrates have evolved through multiple generations—from the first-generation PRP to the second-generation PRF and third-generation CGF [20, 21]. These derivatives differ fundamentally in their preparation protocols (e.g., the use of anticoagulants and centrifugation speeds), structural properties, and growth factor release kinetics [22]. For instance, while PL provides an instantaneous burst of cell-free growth factors via freeze-thaw lysis [23], PRF and CGF form robust, autologous fibrin matrices that enable sustained growth factor release over several weeks [24]. In the context of regenerative medicine, a tissue engineering scaffold is defined as a three-dimensional, biocompatible matrix designed to mimic the native extracellular matrix (ECM). They can sustain, repair and enhance tissue structure and function [25]. In routine wound care, traditional dressings like gauze or basic hydrocolloids serve mostly as passive barriers. They absorb excess fluid and keep infections at bay, which is usually enough for minor or superficial cuts. However, when dealing with severe or chronic wounds, these standard therapies often fall short. Therefore, tissue engineering scaffolds are more suitable and necessary in these complex situations. They provide an appropriate platform for cell adhesion, migration and proliferation, and permit the release of GFs that promote cell survival, proliferation and differentiation, thereby facilitating tissue repair and regeneration [26, 27].

The combination of platelet concentrates with tissue engineering scaffolds represents a novel direction for wound healing research. In this synergy, platelet concentrates provide facilitators for tissue repair, while scaffolds serve as tissue support and drug release platforms. This combination therapy produces superior tissue repair outcomes compared to either element used individually. Consequently, it is essential to summarize the application of platelet concentrates in conjunction with tissue engineering scaffolds for wound healing. This review first introduces the role of platelet concentrates and tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing and outlines the requirements that the scaffolds should meet. Then summarizes the application of platelet concentrates with tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing, with different forms of platelet concentrates presented separately. Finally, the challenges and prospects of combining platelet concentrations and tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing are discussed. Our ultimate goal is to inspire further development of wound healing therapies through biomaterial engineering to better address the challenge of wound repair (Fig. 1).

Although recent literature has thoroughly documented the biological benefits of platelet-derived therapies, most existing reviews tend to merely list successful combinations of biomaterials [28–30]. Such approaches often overlook the necessity of properly matching the specific physical properties of biomaterials with the specific structures of scaffolds. To address this gap, this paper offers a unique, structured, and critical perspective. Unlike previous publications, we systematically analyze four major types of platelet concentrates—PRP, PL, CGF, and PRF—clearly contrasting their distinct physical states and growth factor release kinetics. Based on this classification, we move beyond simply listing material formulations to detail how the specific physical weaknesses of bare platelet derivatives—such as mechanical fragility and rapid enzymatic degradation—dictate the selection of complementary tissue engineering platforms, including hydrogels, sponges, and electrospun nanofibers. Furthermore, this review does not merely focus on success stories from in vitro and preclinical studies but uniquely explores the persistent translational bottlenecks facing these composite systems—encompassing aspects ranging from autologous biological variability to stringent regulatory hurdles. By elucidating these critical physico-biological interactions and emphasizing the need for a shift toward intelligent, multi-stage delivery systems, this review aims to provide a rigorous translational roadmap for the rational design of next-generation wound healing therapies.

Role of platelet concentrates in wound healing

The role of platelet concentrates in wound healing can be summarized through the function of platelets and their associated components across the four distinct phases of tissue repair: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. During the hemostasis phase, platelets act as essential components in the coagulation process. Upon activation, they aggregate to form platelet plugs that seal ruptures in the vessel wall and further solidify to form insoluble blood clots. These blood clots, composed of platelets, leukocytes, fibrinogen and erythrocytes, not only secure hemostasis but also initiate the subsequent inflammation phase [31, 32]. In this phase, activated platelets release chemokines, histamine, adenosine, and other biologically active substances to recruit additional platelets and chemotactic leukocytes for microbial sterilization and debris clearance [18, 33].

As wound healing progresses, activated platelets secrete a multitude of GFs, such as platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), TGF-β, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), insulin growth factor (IGF), to drive the subsequent repair phases [34]. These GFs play a crucial role by stimulating cell proliferation and angiogenesis during the proliferation phase, and orchestrating ECM synthesis and collagen deposition during the remodeling phase. During the proliferation phase of angiogenesis, VEGF and PDGF serve as the primary molecular triggers. When VEGF binds to its receptor (VEGFR2) on local endothelial cells, it activates the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways [35, 36]. The ERK signaling cascade directly stimulates the proliferation and migration of these endothelial cells to form primitive capillary buds, while the PI3K/Akt axis inhibits pro-apoptotic signals to ensure their survival in the harsh wound microenvironment [35, 36]. As healing progresses into the tissue remodeling phase, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) becomes the primary regulator of the extracellular matrix (ECM). TGF-β exerts its fibrogenic effects through the classical Smad pathway, driving the synthesis of type I and type III collagen as well as fibronectin [37, 38]. Furthermore, these initial GFs trigger surrounding cells to release additional GFs, cytokines and other active substances through autocrine or paracrine pathways [18, 39]. For instance, IGF-1 secreted by fibroblasts has an autocrine effect on the cells themselves, and TGF-β3 exerts a paracrine effect on keratinocytes [40, 41].

Crucially, platelet concentrates also contain anti-inflammatory cytokines that modulate inflammatory responses, actively preventing excessive tissue damage and facilitating the vital transition from the inflammation phase to the proliferation phase [42–44]. For example, interleukin-10 (IL-10) suppresses the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1β) by inhibiting the activation of macrophages [45]. Other cytokines, such as interleukin-4 (IL-4), redirect the local microenvironment toward tissue repair by inducing M2 polarization of macrophages [46]. Finally, successful progression through all four phases requires a three-dimensional provisional framework for cell migration and the encapsulation of platelets and leukocytes. Adhesion factors that constitute this framework, such as fibrin, vitronectin, and fibronectin, are abundant in platelet concentrates, structurally supporting the entire healing process from initial clot formation through to final tissue remodeling [47–48].

Characteristics of tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing

In the wound repair process, tissue engineering scaffolds serve several significant functions: (i) They act as a physical barrier covering the skin surface, preventing infection by pathogenic agents in the environment [49]; (ii) They support cell adhesion, migration and proliferation, thereby promoting tissue regeneration; (iii) They encapsulate and release a variety of molecules necessary for various cellular activities. These specific functions establish the requirements for the scaffolds.

To effectively support tissue repair, the scaffold must meet specific mechanical and structural benchmarks. Mechanically, the scaffold needs to mimic the natural elasticity of human skin to accommodate movement and withstand local stress. This typically requires a Young’s modulus between 0.1 and 200 MPa, along with a tensile strength ranging from 5 to 30 MPa [50–53]. Beyond mechanical support, the physical architecture of the matrix heavily influences cell behavior. Because different cell types exhibit distinct adhesion and migration behaviors on the same scaffold, structural parameters must be explicitly tailored to support the cell populations most relevant to wound healing, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, keratinocytes, and infiltrating immune cells [54]. A highly interconnected network with an overall porosity of 60% to 90% is generally required to allow sufficient nutrient diffusion and waste removal. Within this network, pore sizes spanning 50 to 150 μm are widely considered optimal [55]. This specific scale provides enough physical space for key repair cells to infiltrate the site, multiply, and eventually lay down new, organized collagen [56]. Furthermore, to provide sustained support without hindering final tissue remodeling, these scaffolds must be biodegradable. Crucially, the in vivo biodegradation rate of the scaffold must be precisely tuned to synchronize with the rate of new tissue regeneration and the desired release kinetics of encapsulated therapeutic agents [57].

To translate these ideal properties into physical scaffolds, researchers typically rely on a combination of natural and synthetic polymers. Natural materials like collagen, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, gelatin, and silk fibroin are heavily favored because they inherently possess cellular recognition sites and offer excellent biocompatibility. Conversely, synthetic polymers—such as polycaprolactone (PCL), poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), and polyethylene glycol (PEG)—are often blended into the formulations to provide precise, batch-to-batch control over the scaffold’s mechanical strength and degradation kinetics. In terms of fabrication, several techniques have become standard practice depending on the intended clinical application. Electrospinning is widely used to create nanofibrous mats that structurally replicate the native ECM. For highly absorbent, porous sponges, freeze-drying (lyophilization) remains the go-to method. Meanwhile, chemical or physical crosslinking is the conventional route for formulating moisture-donating hydrogels. More recently, 3D bioprinting has gained significant traction, as it allows for the layer-by-layer construction of customized, patient-specific scaffolds with complex geometries, offering spatial control over material and cell deposition.

Applications in wound healing

Considering the enhanced wound repair capabilities achieved through the combination of platelets and tissue engineering scaffolds, we systematically review the research advancements in this combination therapy. In this section, the applications of PRP, PL, CGFs and PRF are listed separately. These concentrated platelets can not only collaborate with tissue engineering scaffolds but can also function as scaffolds themselves.

Platelet-rich plasma

The term PRP was initially introduced in 1954 as a platelet concentrate designed for transfusion in patients suffering from severe thrombocytopenia [58]. However, its first application in the field of tissue engineering was in 1998, where it was utilized as a source of GFs for bone tissue healing [59]. PRP is a derivative obtained from the patient’s autologous blood via centrifugation [60]. While baseline whole blood typically contains 150,000 to 350,000 platelets/µL, a widely accepted therapeutic threshold for defining PRP is a platelet concentration of at least 3 to 5 times the baseline (approximately 1,000,000 platelets/µL) [61]. An experiment was conducted to compare the effectiveness of PRP and bone marrow in healing wounds using collagen as a matrix. This experiment revealed that PRP is as rich in GFs as bone marrow [62]. Formigli et al. found that when multifunctional scaffolds were constructed with PRP and Integra (R), cells exhibited improved adhesion and migration. Furthermore, they emerged with a fibroblast phenotype, implying that these bioactive scaffolds contribute to cell growth and differentiation [63]. This section presents the applications of PRP in combination with tissue engineering scaffolds to construct bioactive scaffolds. These scaffolds are categorized according to their properties into hydrogel scaffolds, sponge-like scaffolds, electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds, and other scaffolds. Notable examples are provided for each category.

PRP-integrated hydrogel scaffolds

Hydrogel is a three-dimensional mesh structure with strong solubility and water retention capacity. Its excellent biocompatibility, non-toxicity, non-irritation, adhesion and deformability make it widely applicable in the field of wound healing [64]. Jain et al. demonstrated that the biological activity of PRP is maintained during the encapsulation and release process when combined with hydrogels, suggesting that hydrogels may serve as a delivery platform for PRP [65]. To visually illustrate the structural evolution and functional optimization of PRP-hydrogel formulations, Fig. 2 systematically outlines their three developmental stages. It highlights the step-by-step technological progression: from the initial pure PRP hydrogel, which was mechanically fragile and prone to rapid degradation, to a physically reinforced encapsulated scaffold, and finally to a composite hydrogel that combines molecular cross-linking with sustained-release capabilities and shape-adaptive injectability.

PRP can independently form a hydrogel, typically comprising a blend of PRP, fibrin glue, thrombin, and/or calcium [66]. The addition of exogenous calcium or thrombin cleaves the fibrin source in plasma into insoluble fibrin. Rahman et al. studied the effects of PRP hydrogels on mouse wound healing, finding increased IL-6 expression, detected by qPCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), in the PRP hydrogels-treated group [67]. This suggests that PRP hydrogels promote vascular formation and collagen deposition by upregulating IL-6 levels through GFs. Although TGF-β1 in PRP hydrogels can accelerate wound healing by promoting extracellular matrix deposition, it may also inhibit keratinocyte migration, potentially causing wound healing inhibition and scarring. Since human skin heals through epithelial cell regeneration, while mouse wounds heal through contraction, the impact of PRP hydrogels on scarring is difficult to assess in mouse models. Samberg et al. combined human adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) with PEGylated PRP hydrogels. In vitro culture of this composite revealed enhanced ASC viability and upregulated angiogenic gene expression after 14 days, which was attributed to the high concentration of platelets. To evaluate its in vivo efficacy, a rat wound model was established, utilizing wounds treated with normal saline as the control group. The results indicated that the PEG-PRP/ASCs group exhibited faster and more abundant angiogenesis compared to the saline-treated control, thereby significantly facilitating the wound healing process [68].

PRP hydrogels can also be involved in tissue-engineered scaffolds as a GFs reservoir. Ranjbarvan et al. used PRP hydrogels-encapsulated electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL) scaffolds to explore the possibility of its application in skin tissue repair [69]. Their findings revealed that PRP hydrogel-coated scaffolds increased the expression of keratinocyte markers, such as keratin, and enhanced keratinocyte adhesion and proliferation. Additionally, introducing PRP hydrogels into PCL fiber scaffolds improved scaffold hydrophilicity and promoted cell adhesion and growth [70]. Therefore, incorporating PRP hydrogels into tissue engineering scaffolds shows promise for enhancing wound healing.

However, the resulting PRP hydrogels are mechanically weak, which limits their ability to maintain shape, insulate water, and store GFs. Conventional PRP hydrogels are susceptible to enzymatic degradation in physiological environments and are completely degraded within a few days of application [71]. To maintain angiogenesis and support collagen deposition throughout the entire wound healing process, the GFs in PRP should ideally retain for 1–3 weeks and be released slowly and continuously [72]. These poor mechanical properties pose challenges in filling and covering irregularly shaped wounds. Due to their rapid degradability and structural instability, the GFs within these hydrogels can be easily inactivated, reducing their effectiveness in wound treatment [73, 74]. Therefore, there is a need to develop stable, biocompatible PRP-loaded cross-linked hydrogels that can effectively serve as reservoirs for GFs in wound healing applications (Fig. 3A and B). Inspired by the exoskeletons of insects, Zhang et al. developed a dual-network hydrogel using laccase and Ca²⁺/thrombin. This cross-linking enhanced mechanical strength and structural stability, and the hydrogel demonstrated controlled release as well as strong support for cell proliferation and migration [75].

Building upon the knowledge that sodium alginate (SA) can be activated by divalent cations such as Ca2+ to form hydrogels [76], Zhang et al. mixed PRP and SA to create a double network hydrogel (DN hydrogel) catalyzed by Ca2+/thrombin (Fig. 3C) [77]. This amorphous DN hydrogel can be injected via a syringe into irregularly shaped wound surfaces, filling the wound and offering certain advantages for clinical applications. A comparison of the mechanical properties of DN hydrogels and PRP hydrogels using rheological methods confirmed that the double cross-linked structure results in much higher mechanical properties. To investigate whether DN hydrogels could achieve prolonged release of GFs, the release kinetics of EGF and VEGF from PRP hydrogels and DN hydrogels were examined using ELISA. The results showed that DN hydrogels exhibited a relatively slow and stable release of both GFs.

Similarly, Zhou et al. utilized carboxymethyl chitosan-polyamide amine (CMC-PAMAM) to reduce silver ions, thereby obtaining Ag@CMC-PAMAM nanoparticles. This, along with SA and PRP, formed composite hydrogels [78]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that the density of the hydrogel network was positively proportional to the proportion of doped composite nanoparticles. In addition to the considerable biocompatibility and promoting wound healing, the introduction of silver ions also imparted antibacterial properties to the hydrogel. This demonstrates the ability of this composite hydrogel to inhibit or kill bacteria at the wound site. In addition, the SA-PRP hydrogel prepared by 3D printing technology has been shown to restore the integrity of the skin barrier [79].

Chitosan hydrogels, known for their high biocompatibility, low toxicity, biodegradability and high moisture retention, are widely used as wound healing dressings [64]. They positively influence blood clotting, oxygen transport, epithelial cell formation and tissue regeneration. Qian et al. used 4-carboxybenzaldehyde functionalized PEG and then cross-linked ethylene glycol chitosan to form a hydrogel called CBPGCTS (Fig. 3D) [80]. A key innovation of this study was the incorporation of lyophilized silk fibroin (SF) alongside PRP into the hydrogel network. Because SF possesses strong anti-enzymatic degradation properties, it effectively shielded the encapsulated PRP from the protease-rich hostile environment typical of chronic wounds. In vitro protease degradation assays revealed that while standard PRP hydrogels rapidly lost their growth factors, the CBPGCTS-SF@PRP composite significantly prolonged the retention and sustained release of key bioactive components, including PDGF-BB, nerve growth factor (NGF), and extracellular vesicles (EVs). Furthermore, when applied to a full-thickness diabetic wound model, this composite hydrogel accelerated wound closure by promoting robust angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization, thereby highlighting the importance of protecting PRP from enzymatic degradation to maximize its therapeutic efficacy.

Collagen hydrogels have excellent biocompatibility and are widely used in tissue engineering [81]. Wang et al. combined type I collagen and PRP to prepare hybrid 3D scaffolds as a biologically derived engineered skin (Fig. 3E) [82]. The scaffold facilitated cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation at the wound site, and exhibited excellent anti-inflammatory properties and pro-angiogenic properties after incorporation of adipose mesenchymal stem cell exosomes. The potential of this engineered skin for clinical applications was demonstrated. Matthew et al. made a PRP cross-linked collagen hydrogel and placed it on fresh human skin tissue for incubation, finding that the scaffold incorporating PRP had a greater ability to attract dermal-derived stem cells than the collagen-only scaffold [83]. Animal wound models were constructed and treated with different dressings. A significantly smaller wound area was found in the PRP-collagen scaffold treatment group, suggesting a greater epithelial regenerative capacity, as demonstrated by the regeneration of hair follicles and sweat glands, and an increased angiogenic capacity.

As a collagen degradation product, gelatin-based hydrogels are also used in conjunction with PRP for wound treatment [84–87]. Tavakoli et al. designed bioactive multilayered shell and core fiber hydrogels loaded with PRP using gelatin, chitosan, and SA by 3D bioprinting technology [88]. The nanofibers contributed to cell proliferation and adhesion, while chick chorioallantois membrane (CAM) experiments demonstrated their vasculogenic capacity. Zhao et al. introduced alginate and gelatin as bioinks loaded with PRP for in situ 3-dimensional bioprinting [89], which can be rapidly filled according to the shape of the wound [90]. Alinezhad et al. added polydopamine to alginate-gelatin-PRP hydrogels [91]. The introduction of polydopamine enabled photothermal therapy, which improved healing through near-infrared light. The composite hydrogel thus possesses antioxidant, antimicrobial and photothermal properties simultaneously. Similarly, Koyuncu et al. implanted fibroblasts in PRP-loaded gelatin-chitosan scaffolds and subsequently introduced near-infrared light irradiation to achieve photo-activation of fibroblasts and platelets, which resulted in the inhibition of ROS production in fibroblasts [92]. Gelatin-based hydrogels used as a material for 3D printing may suffers from poor thermal stability, which limits their application [10, 93]. This deficiency can be addressed by functionalizing the gelatine, for example, using a methacrylic acid group, to obtain gelatine methacrylate (GelMA) [94]. Rita et al. doped PRP in GelMA hydrogel and printed it with a given procedure and parameters into a porous scaffold for tissue growth [95]. The addition of PRP does not adversely affect the rheology and elasticity of GelMA hydrogels, as was confirmed in their experiments. Evaluation of GFs release from the scaffold revealed that within 14 days VEGF achieved a burst release at the outset, while PDGF-BB showed a continued slow release. Considering the mechanism that VEGF induces vascular sprouting and that vascular maturation requires the action of PDGF-BB [96–98], it is believed that this composite scaffold can promote angiogenesis. The pro-angiogenic ability in vivo was explored by incubating CAM with GelMA scaffold alone and PRP-GelMA scaffold. The degree of vascularization and area of vascularization were significantly higher in the PRP-GelMA group than in the GelMA group, which further demonstrates the pro-angiogenic nature of the composite scaffold.

Due to the low mechanical strength of pure GelMA, which is prone to deformation and fracture, Lu et al. added silk methacrylate (SFMA) protein to improve its mechanical properties (Fig. 3F) [99]. The resulting hydrogels of suitable compression modulus are highly cross-linked and allow for the slow release of GFs. Notably, ADCSs achieved high viability and cell viability on GelMA/SFMA/PRP hydrogel surfaces, as well as increased cell proliferation and migration. In vivo healing experiments using a mouse pressure ulcer model demonstrated that GelMA/SFMA/ PRP/ADSCs hydrogels effectively promoted angiogenesis and nerve regeneration, improved collagen deposition and greatly accelerated wound healing.

To address the vulnerability of diabetic wounds to infection and long periods of inflammation, Wei et al. used an antimicrobial peptide (AMP) to modify hyaluronic acid (HA) and exploited the pro-angiogenic and collagen deposition abilities of PRP, combined with oxidized dextran to synthesize a composite hydrogel (Fig. 3G) [100]. The synthesized hydrogels are effective against inflammation and infection and promote diabetic wound healing. However, the complexity of this system may make mass production and clinical translation difficult. Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was used as a bioink for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers by Diaz-Gomez et al. [83]. CMC scaffolds support cell adhesion and angiogenesis, and their transparent nature facilitates direct observation of wound healing status. After the addition of PRP, GF in the scaffold was slowly released and the presence of GFs resulted in improved wound repair properties. According to reports, this scaffold completely degrades within seven days. For very severe or deep chronic wounds, it may not provide sufficient long-term structural support or sustained release of GFs. Wang et al. combined PRP with polypyrrole and bacterial cellulose [101]. The conductivity of polypyrrole enables the resulting hydrogel to exert potent antibacterial effects through electric field-mediated charge storage, while also facilitating electrical signal conduction, thereby regulating the kinetics of growth factor release. This composite hydrogel promotes the healing of diabetic wounds in multiple ways. Other examples of PRP combined with hydrogel for wound treatment are shown in Table 2.

PRP-integrated sponge-like scaffolds

Sponge-like scaffolds are distinguished by their highly interconnected macroscopic pores, typically exhibiting an overall porosity exceeding 80% and pore diameters spanning 100 to 300 μm [108]. This architecture sharply contrasts with other tissue engineering platforms. While electrospun meshes provide high total porosity, their individual pores are usually restricted to the submicron or single-digit micrometer scale, which can physically impede deep cellular infiltration [109]. Traditional hydrogels, conversely, consist of dense nanoporous networks that excel at moisture retention but inherently lack the large physical voids necessary for rapid tissue ingrowth. The distinct macroporosity of sponges provides the precise physical space required for immediate cell migration, exudate absorption, and extensive neovascularization [110]. (Fig. 4A)

Because these sponges are primarily fabricated from the same natural biopolymers used in hydrogels—such as collagen, gelatin, and chitosan—they inevitably share similar mechanical vulnerabilities. When fully hydrated in a wound bed, pristine biopolymer sponges often exhibit weak load-bearing capacity, rapid enzymatic degradation, and a tendency to structurally collapse under local tissue pressure. To preserve their macroporous architecture during the critical phases of healing, these scaffolds typically require secondary stabilization through chemical crosslinking or blending with more rigid synthetic polymers. Despite these mechanical fragility issues, materials like collagen remain the primary structural basis for these sponges. As the major protein of the ECM, collagen inherently offers unmatched biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and intrinsic cell-binding sites, making it highly amenable to functional surface modifications.

The collagen scaffold’s surface can be functionalized, and its porous structure allows it to be loaded with drugs, allowing it to achieve slow drug release [111, 112]. Horimizu et al. used a collagen sponge as a scaffold for loading lyophilized PRP [113]. Examining the distribution of CT41+ platelets in the PRP-loaded sponges demonstrated that PRP could enter the pores deep inside the collagen sponges. This composite scaffold has a good infiltration effect on connective tissue and promotes collagen deposition of cells. In addition, wounds implanted with PRP collagen sponge scaffolds showed good neo-capillary production, collagen deposition, granulation and adipose tissue formation. It is worth mentioning that the introduction of periosteal fibroblasts into the PRP/collagen composite scaffold resulted in enhanced wound repair. Similarly, Chang et al. used an acellular animal skin collagen sponge, which has the advantages of natural skin structure and a large number of GFs, to encapsulate PRP as an artificial dermal regeneration template for diabetic wound healing [114]. They added collagenase to simulate the inhibition of diabetic wound healing. Cell experiments demonstrated that this scaffold material could promote the proliferation of skin cells and collagen production in rats. The introduction of animal wound models demonstrated that PRP/ collagen sponge had a better effect on diabetic skin wound healing than PRP or collagen alone.

Given that collagen has a modified surface and polydopamine (PDA) surface coating can prevent drug release from the drug delivery system, Zheng et al. prepared PDA-functionalized collagen scaffolds (PDA-CSS) to evaluate their promotion of dermal regeneration and in vivo drug delivery efficiency (Fig. 4B) [115]. ELISA was used to detect the release of PDGF-BB and VEGF in different systems, and it was found that the PDA-CSS@PRP group showed a longer release time and a higher release amount. The migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human immortalized keratinocytes cells was evaluated, with PDA-CSS@PRP significantly promoting the migration of both types of cells. The animal wound model was treated with PDA-CSS@PRP, CSS@PRP, CSS and blank control respectively. After 21 days, the wound healed well in the PDA-CSS@PRP and CSS@PRP treatment groups, and the effect of the former was better than that of the latter. Tissue samples were taken for H&E staining histological analysis, and there were thicker granulation tissue and more new blood vessels in the CSS@PRP group. There was less infiltration of inflammatory cells and less necrotic tissue, especially at PDA-CSS@PRP. Collagen deposition enhanced by PDA-CSS@PRP is visible under Masson trichrome staining. It can be seen that the regeneration effect of CSS@PRP on a wound can be enhanced by PDA modification.

Lu et al. prepared chitosan-gelatin sponges (CSGT) by lyophilization using tannic acid to cross-link chitosan and gelatin and combine them with ascorbic acid [116]. CSGT scaffold has good mechanical properties and stable structure. Its swelling rate and water retention are better than that of chitosan and gelatin, which is beneficial to absorb wound exudate. These properties make it a potential wound dressing. When PRP was loaded with a CSGT sponge, the composite scaffold showed good antibacterial activity, which may be attributed to the rough surface or the introduction of tannic acid. CSGT/PRP sponge has good biocompatibility, can promote the growth and proliferation of epidermal cells, and does not adhere to the tissue, which will not lead to secondary injury of the wound while accelerating wound healing. Wang et al. added nano silver to chitosan filament emulsion and lyophilized it to form a tissue engineering scaffold, which was lyophilized again to load with PRP (Fig. 4C) [117]. The prepared sponge scaffold has a high porosity with good moisture retention capacity and mechanical properties. The introduction of nano-silver conferred good antibacterial properties to the composite scaffold, which could effectively promote the healing of infected wounds and result in a significant reduction of bacteria in the wound exudate.

PRP-integrated electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds

Electrostatic spinning is commonly used in tissue engineering, where it can produce fibrous structures similar to the structure of the ECM [118]. Bertoncelj et al. used electrostatic spinning to make hydrophilic nanofibers mimicking ECM from chitosan and polyethylene oxide to serve as a platform for loading PRP (Fig. 5A) [119]. They demonstrated the fibers’ resistance to moisture by immersing them in phosphate buffer saline (PBS), but PBS does not fully mimic natural wound exudate. Furthermore, although the encapsulated PRP activity was not affected, the GFs within the scaffold tended to exhibit rapid release and the fibrous structure greatly inhibited cell migration.

Meamar et al. cultured human placental mesenchymal stem cells (hPDMSCs) in electrostatically spun gelatin fibers for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcer wounds [120]. hPDMSCs achieved improved migration and proliferation in the scaffold. Patients with diabetic foot ulcers were divided into three groups: the hPDMSCs/gelatin composite scaffold group, the PRP combined composite scaffold group and the no-treatment control group. Comparing the wound closure rate, wound size and patient pain in the three groups, it was found that the PRP combined with the hPDMSCs/gelatin stent group had the best wound healing effect, followed by the hPDMSCs/gelatin stent group. This demonstrated the potential of PRP combined with electrostatically spun wire stents for the treatment of chronic wounds.

The polyglycerol sebacate (PGS)/PCL/PRP nanofibers prepared by Shafizadeh et al. support cell adhesion and proliferation due to their elastic modulus being close to that of skin (Fig. 5B) [121]. Heydari et al. prepared an electrospun PGS/poly(lactic acid) (PLA)/PRP scaffold and found that the addition of PRP suppressed the expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors while upregulating anti-inflammatory factors. Furthermore, this scaffold enhanced collagen deposition and angiogenesis, showing promise for applications in skin tissue engineering (Fig. 5C) [122]. Similarly, Alghamdi et al. found that PGS/PLA-PRP electrospun scaffolds can accelerate wound healing in diabetic patients and improve healing outcomes [123].

Other PRP-integrated scaffolds

In addition to hydrogel, sponge, and electrospun scaffolds, there are many other examples of scaffolds using PRP for wound treatment [124–131]. The following are three typical examples: microspheres, microsphere-composite gels, and bio-textile-based scaffolds.

Taking advantage of the fact that microspheres can increase the half-life of the GFs encapsulated within them [132], Zhou et al. loaded PRP into gelatin microspheres (Fig. 6A) [133]. Animal wound models were established and controlled experiments were conducted to compare wound closure rates, inflammation levels and vascular density in each group, and PRP/gelatin microspheres were found to accelerate the wound healing process. Yu et al. prepared a granular gel that could be applied and adhered to the wound site by combining PRP with a fibrin network formed by thrombin and two types of microspheres(Fig. 6B) [134]. The microsphere gel was shown to improve the healing process by altering the expression of wound healing-related genes. Liu et al. processed domestic silkworm cocoons to obtain cocoon scaffolds, which were immersed in PRP and then lyophilized to obtain composite scaffolds (Fig. 6C) [135]. The composite scaffolds were assessed for their ability to absorb water, retain moisture and promote wound healing, demonstrating the potential of bio-derived natural scaffolds for tissue engineering applications.

Advantages and Limitations of PRP-loaded Scaffolds

Incorporating PRP into tissue engineering scaffolds can address several inherent limitations of PRP therapy when used alone. First, untreated PRP is typically released rapidly and is quickly degraded by the protease-rich environment of chronic wounds. Embedding PRP within a scaffold matrix provides a physical barrier that prevents enzymatic hydrolysis, thereby enabling the sustained, localized release of growth factors over several days or weeks. This prolonged release profile is better aligned with the extended timeline of new tissue formation. Second, PRP alone lacks sufficient mechanical strength to fill large or deep tissue cavities. Scaffolds address this limitation by providing a robust three-dimensional framework that supports cell adhesion and tissue growth, working in synergy with the biological signals provided by PRP.

Although these synergistic effects offer numerous benefits, the preparation of PRP-rich scaffolds presents several unique challenges, making it difficult to translate them into broader clinical applications. The greatest challenge lies in standardization. Because PRP is an autologous blood product, its growth factor composition and platelet concentration can vary significantly depending on the donor’ s age, underlying health conditions, and the specific centrifugation protocol used. This inherent heterogeneity makes it nearly impossible to precisely determine therapeutic doses within the scaffold matrix, leading to inconsistent healing outcomes among patients. Furthermore, from a manufacturing perspective, incorporating liquid PRP into hydrogels or polymer precursors may dilute the formulation or interfere with cross-linking kinetics, and in some cases, reduce the scaffold’ s intended mechanical strength and degradation rate.

Concentrated growth factors

GFs induce cell migration and proliferation, stimulate the production of cellular up-regulated proteins and promote ECM remodeling and angiogenesis, thereby accelerating the wound healing process [136, 137]. PDGF is essential in connective tissue remodeling. PDGF can not only attract fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells to the wound site and stimulates mitosis, but also promote chemotaxis of macrophages and neutrophils [138]. In addition, the synthesis of collagen, fibronectin, proteoglycan, glycosaminoglycan and HA in the extracellular matrix is also associated with PDGF [139–142]. PDGF-BB is considered to be the most effective PDGF isoform for promoting wound healing [143]. Animal studies and clinical trials have demonstrated the potential of PDGF-BB to promote tissue repairs such as wound healing, bone regeneration, and gingival attachment [144].

CGFs-integrated hydrogel scaffolds

The promoting effect of GFs on wound healing is limited by their short half-life. Hydrogel scaffolds coupled with PDGF-BB often act as a platform for loading, delivering and achieving the slow release of GFs [145, 146]. Kim et al. designed a thermoresponsive poly(organophosphazene) hydrogel for the sustained release of tannic acid (TA) and PDGF. These two active substances are stably released from the hydrogel to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and promote angiogenesis, thereby exerting a synergistic effect through anti-inflammatory action and promoting healing (Fig. 7A) [147]. Zhang et al. prepared PDGF-BB/SA/Dex, a hydrogel loaded with PDGF-BB, using SA and dextran to achieve slow and sustained release of PDGF-BB [148]. The number of CD31 + and vWF+ cells produced and VEGF-A and vWF expression detected by qRT-PCR was significantly higher in PDGF-BB-activated BMSC cells cultured in an endothelial induction medium compared to BMSC incubated in the same culture setting without PDGF-BB, indicating that the composite hydrogel had positive regulation effect on angiogenesis.

The ongoing inflammatory response during diabetic wound healing has been a huge challenge. Wan et al. produced a bilayer hydrogel scaffold consisting of a gelatin cryogel loaded with silver and a 3D-printed gelatin scaffold loaded with PDGF-BB [149]. The double-layered hydrogel scaffold has been shown to be non-inhibitory to the growth and proliferation of fibroblasts and keratinocyte and is highly resistant to bacteria. Animal studies and histological analysis demonstrate its pro-angiogenic, collagen deposition and re-epithelialization abilities. In conclusion, the composite scaffold combines the benefits of silver and PDGF-BB, exhibiting high anti-infeous capability and performing positive effect on tissue repair.

Another reason that impede the wound healing process is vascularization. PDGF-BB may assist the pro-angiogenic effects of VEGF, but the expression of these two GFs directly determines the therapeutic potential and should neither be overexpressed nor deficient [150–154]. Certelli et al. used fibrin hydrogels as a platform to control the release and in vivo distribution of recombinant VEGF and recombinant PDGF-BB [155]. They explored the optimal dose of these two GFs co-induced to achieve angiogenesis and even arteriogenesis in diabetic wounds in a short period. However, the degradation of the fibrin scaffold prevents the GFs from remaining in the body for long periods, resulting in the degradation of new capillaries often after four weeks. A certain amount of peptide-inhibiting protein can be incorporated into the hydrogel to slow the degradation of the fibrin hydrogel. In another work, Yang et al. used light-cured glycol chitosan hydrogels to deliver VEGF and PDGF-BB [156]. In vitro release experiments demonstrate the excellent controlled release ability of the hydrogel, showing a burst release on the first day followed by a slow sustained release over 30 days. This certainly makes it a more suitable pro-angiogenic, prompting it to become a competitive candidate for wound repair.

CGFs-integrated electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds

Wang et al. designed and constructed a PDGF-BB-based electrospun nanofiber composite membrane for phased, multifunctional tissue repair (Fig. 7B) [157]. This fiber is embedded with zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF)-8 nanoparticles loaded with PDGF-BB and Zn²⁺, and its surface is coated with ZIF-67 nanoparticles containing Co²⁺. In the early stages of wound healing, Co²⁺ is rapidly released, followed by the sustained release of PDGF-BB and Zn²⁺, continuously exerting antibacterial and pro-angiogenic effects.

Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is often used as a material for electrostatic spinning. To synergistic the stimulatory effect of micro-RNA-145 (miR-145) and PDGF-BB on vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) differentiation of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), Hu et al. constructed electrospun PLGA scaffolds and introduced liposomes for delivery. Liposomes loaded with miR-145 and PDGF-BB are evenly distributed on a PLGA scaffold from which miR-145 and PDGF-BB are slowly released. It was found that the composite stent achieved excellent in vitro angiogenesis, in addition to accelerating wound healing and reducing inflammation. Elevated expression of the VSMC marker α-SMA and the EC marker CD31 demonstrated the in vivo pro-angiogenic effect of the composite scaffold. Lee et al. obtained a PLGA/collagen scaffold loaded with recombinant human PDGF-BB (rhPDGF-BB) by electrostatic spinning and evaluated its mechanical properties and water absorption [158]. When the scaffolds were implanted into animal wound models, the rhPDGF-BB/PLGA/collagen group demonstrated better wound healing activity than the PLGA/collagen and PLGA groups, as evidenced by higher type I collagen deposition. The increased expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 in hair follicles also demonstrates the good regenerative capacity of the composite scaffold for hair follicles. In a similar study, vancomycin and gentamicin were added in addition to rhPDGF-BB encapsulated in an electrically accessible PLGA nanofiber scaffold (Fig. 7C) [159]. The introduction of antibiotics has further improved the antimicrobial properties of the stent, enabling it to combat the susceptibility of diabetic wounds to infection, thereby reducing the risk of amputation.

PLGA can also encapsulate GFs in the form of nanoparticles. Xie et al. loaded VEGF into an electro-accessible chitosan/poly (ethylene oxide) scaffold and encapsulated PDGF-BB in PLGA nanoparticles embedded in electro-accessible fibers (Fig. 7D) [160]. Owing to the inability of electrically accessible nanofibers to sustain GF release, in contrast to nanoparticles, the composite scaffold demonstrates an initial burst release of VEGF followed by prolonged PDGF-BB release. This is consistent with the previously mentioned mechanism of VEGF inducing vascular sprouting and PDGF-BB further inducing vascular maturation. This two-stage fast/slow-release pattern significantly accelerates wound healing by promoting angiogenesis. In another study using the release kinetics of nanofibers and nanoparticles to achieve multi-stage release, Lai et al. embedded EGF and basic FGF directly into electrospun collagen/HA nanofiber scaffolds and loaded VEGF and PDGF-BB into gelatin nanoparticles distributed on the fibers [95]. The different rates of release of multiple GFs allow the composite scaffold to promote re-epithelialization and vascularization of the wound well, suggesting a new idea for chronic wound healing.

Other substances, such as gelatin and poly (L-lactic acid) (PLLA) canalso be used in electrostatic spinning. Diaz-Gomez et al. prepared scaffolds mimicking ECM by electrostatic spinning gelatin with a polysaccharide EUP3 [100]. The natural components endowed the scaffolds with high affinity for GFs, enabling binding of endogenous PDGF-BB and responsive release of the EUP3/PDGF-BB complex to promote neotissue formation. Piran et al. prepared an electrostatically spun trilayer scaffold with a middle layer of gelatin fibers doped with GFs and an outer layer of PLLA [161]. Composite scaffolds provide attachment sites for cell growth and can significantly promote fibroblast migration and proliferation, suggesting that this scaffold may have the ability to promote wound repair. Chyuan et al. modified the surface of SA/PCL electrospun scaffolds with PDGF-B plasmids, achieving in situ gene transfection and harnessing the tissue-regenerative effects of PDGF-B [162].

CGFs-integrated other scaffolds

More specifically, Li et al. prepared a sponge structured scaffold mimicking ECM by electrostatic spinning using a gelatin hydrogel incorporating a polysaccharide (EUP3) as the raw material (Fig. 7E) [163]. One of the highlights of this study is that they did not use any exogenous GFs, cells or nucleic acids. Once the scaffold is implanted, EUP3 will specifically bind to endogenous PDGF-BB produced at the wound site to collect it into the scaffold, and then gradually release PDGF-BB in the scaffold as collagenase secreted by cells degrades the gelatin scaffold. Alshirah et al. combined protein-modified CGF with silver nanoparticles, demonstrating enhanced structural stability and antimicrobial properties [164].

There have also been other studies of tissue-engineered scaffolds that bind PDGF-BB. Jin et al. encapsulated rhPDGF-BB in PLGA microspheres and embedded PLLA nanofiber scaffolds to slowly release rhPDGF-BB from the microspheres [165]. Composite scaffolds not only promote cell migration and angiogenesis but also have been shown to enhance chemokine expression in vivo. Murali et al. proposed a bionic scaffold of PDGF-BB combined with collagen-polydialdehyde guar gum for tissue repair. The granulation tissue of the wound implanted with composite scaffolds showed higher collagen and hexosamine contents, accompanied by increased angiogenesis. The combination of CGFs and tissue-engineered scaffolds is considered a promising strategy for wound treatment.

Platelet lysate

PL is a liquid derivative obtained by lysing platelets. It contains higher levels of GFs than PRP, with concentrations of key GFs generally ranging from 0.1 to 300 ng/ml [142]. Unlike CGFs, which are released gradually over several weeks, PL delivers an instantaneous, massive burst of free GFs. Although this rapid delivery can immediately stimulate local cell proliferation, the therapeutic effect is significantly reduced because the GFs are rapidly cleared and degraded by enzymes in the body [166]. PL has been shown to function as a biomaterial that enhances cell proliferation and angiogenesis, suggesting its potential to participate in wound repair [167]. Combining PL with tissue engineering scaffolds will undoubtedly be a competitive candidate for wound dressings.

PL-integrated hydrogel scaffolds

Mehmood et al. designed a self-pumping Janus fibrous hydrogel scaffold with a tri-layer structure (Fig. 8A) [168]. The inner layer consists of polyurethane nanofibers that prevent the accumulation of wound exudate and allow it to be drained through microneedle channels in the intermediate layer. Additionally, the intermediate layer is composed of a supramolecular network made from dopamine-modified PL-methacrylate and shell silver-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles, which enables the self-pumping function. This scaffold effectively operates at various stages of wound healing, demonstrating excellent chronic wound treatment outcomes. Daikuara et al. constructed bioinks combining PL and GelMA for 3D-printing tissue engineering scaffolds for wound healing (Fig. 8B) [169]. Its application requires that scaffolds have the properties of sustained release of GFs, promotion of cell growth and collagen deposition. The ability of the scaffold to retain and release GFs was demonstrated by the continuous release of multiple GFs in the scaffold for more than two weeks. Dermal fibroblasts achieved good cell viability and increased cell proliferation on the scaffold. Noteworthy, the fluorescence staining images showed that the expression of ECM components, such as type I collagen, type III collagen, fibronectin and elastin, were all enhanced on this scaffold, especially type I and Type III collagen. Similarly, Tanadchangsaeng et al. incorporated ASC into the scaffold, which enabled the resulting construct to function as a skin substitute that delays wound contraction [170].

Lima et al. encapsulated PL in a collagen hydrogel particle, allowing the GFs in PL to be slowly released from the gel bead [166]. The ASC encapsulated inside the PL/ collagen hydrogel beads showed promoted cell proliferation and the cells were found to establish connections and form a thin film between adjacent particles. In addition, when cultured in contact with cell-free gel particles and monolayer grown ASC cells, those containing PL experienced cell adhesion and cell bonding between different particles. The ability to promote angiogenesis in the CAM model also reflects the tissue repair ability of the hydrogel particles. Costa-Almeida et al. produced a hydrogel silk thread containing PL and ASC and demonstrated that cell growth and collagen deposition were allowed on the fibers [171].

Evidence from these in vitro studies suggests the possibility of using PL combined with hydrogel scaffolds for tissue repair, but more in vivo studies are needed to confirm this. Bhatnagar et al. used chitosan to protect the GFs in synergy with red hemp nanocrystalline cellulose, which has a slow release effect, to prepare a hydrogel, and this formulation was shown to promote wound closure (Fig. 8C) [172]. Bernal Chavez et al. synthesized a Pluronic F-127 thermo-responsive hydrogel incorporating PLGA nanoparticles loaded with PL(Fig. 8D) [173]. Encapsulation within PLGA nanoparticles protects PL from rapid in vivo degradation. The thermal sensitivity of the hydrogel allows it to spray onto the wound surface and form a protective film. The ability of the novel hydrogel to accelerate tissue repair has been demonstrated in both in vivo and in vitro studies. Another thermosensitive hydrogel was prepared by Lim et al., using hydroxybutyl chitosan (Fig. 8E) [174]. GFs in PL are continuously released from hydrogels and promote cell migration and proliferation, angiogenesis and collagen deposition, resulting in better wound healing effect. Notodihardjo et al. employed a gelatin hydrogel to deliver PL, and studies in full-thickness murine wound models showed that PL-impregnated scaffolds promote granulation tissue formation and epithelialization [175].

PL-integrated electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds

SF can be electrospun into nanofibers, and the change of its crystallinity can control the release kinetics [176–180]. From a physiological perspective, optimal wound healing requires the sustained release of growth factors over an extended period (typically 1 to 3 weeks) to fully support the prolonged proliferation and remodeling phases. By virtue of these properties of SF, Pignatelli et al. synthesized a kind of SF nanofiber containing PL, and controlled the release of GFs by controlling the crystallinity of SF [181]. The electrospinning scaffold plays a role in storing PL, preventing it from degrading too quickly, and maintaining its activity at high temperatures (60℃). Although tissue repair typically occurs naturally at body temperature (approximately 37 °C), this exceptional heat resistance offers key translational advantages, significantly extending its shelf life at room temperature and allowing for easy transport without the need for refrigeration. The high cell viability of human dermal fibroblasts on PL/SF scaffolds offers the possibility of using this scaffold for skin tissue repair. In another study, Cordenonsi et al. synthesized a bioactive electrospinning scaffold combining SA, Pullulan and PL, and investigated the optimal ratio of each component [182]. The composite scaffold is also able to support the adhesion and proliferation of fibroblasts and allow them to differentiate, which will contribute to the wound repair process. Khanam et al. incorporated PL into SA/PEG-spun fibers and modified them using dopamine[183]. The modified scaffold improved hemostasis and wound closure and reduced the scar index to 7.3.

The multilayer scaffold produced by electrospinning has become a promising active material for wound management due to the synergistic effects of the different functions of its various layer structures. Blanquer et al. prepared a five-layer nanofiber scaffold with alternating layers of PCL and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) [184]. The PVA layers were loaded with PL, allowing for the sustained release of active substances, while the PCL layers supported the adhesion of various wound healing-related cells. Losi et al. prepared a double-layer scaffold consisting of an electrospun PL and fibrin active layer and a polymer supporting layer [185]. Fibrin encapsulation and electrospinning did not affect the bioactivity of PL. Compared to the control group, the composite scaffold showed faster and more complete wound healing in diabetic wounds due to the gradual release of GFs in PL. Taborska et al. prepared electrostatically spun scaffolds using poly(L-lactide-co-epsilon-caprolactone) and poly(epsilon-caprolactone) (PLCL/PCL) as materials [186]. The surface of the scaffold was covered with a fibrin film for delivery of PL, FGF and VEGF. The film exhibited sustained and rapid release of multiple GFs, effectively stimulating chronic wound healing.

PL-integrated other scaffolds

Sandri et al. embedded PL in a porous foam scaffold consisting of chondroitin sulfate and SA [187]. The porous structure of the scaffold gives it a high specific surface area, which facilitates cell adhesion. The incorporation of PL increased the proliferation level of fibroblasts and HUVEC on the scaffold. The results of these in vitro experiments raise the possibility of the scaffold acting on skin wounds. Ito et al. wrapped PL in collagen/gelatin scaffolds and gradually released GFs as collagenase degraded the scaffolds [188]. They found that PL in appropriate concentrations significantly promoted cell proliferation, granulation tissue formation, and angiogenesis. Excessive concentrations of PL have been shown to inhibit the growth and proliferation of certain cell types, thus increasing PL concentration does not necessarily enhance wound healing. Losi et al. loaded PL onto a poly(ether)urethane-polydimethylsiloxane/ fibrin scaffold to demonstrate the function of this composite scaffold for accelerating diabetic ulcer healing [189]. Animal wound models were constructed and divided into groups for different treatments, and the composite stent group experienced faster wound closure. HE staining and Massone tricolor staining were performed on tissue samples, and the PL-containing stent group showed improved granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition and re-epithelialization. These evidences demonstrate the infinite possibilities of tissue engineering scaffolds containing PL for skin wound healing and other tissue repair applications.

Platelet-rich fibrin

PRF can be incorporated into hydrogels [190, 191, 192], sponges (Fig. 9A) [193], electrostatically spun scaffolds (Fig. 9B) [87, 194], and other scaffolds. To address the lack of GFs in hydrogel dressings as well as bacterial infections, Mirjalili et al. made nanoparticles of PRF together with chitosan and loaded them into gelatin-chitosan hydrogels [195]. The composite structure enabled the slow release of proteins from the scaffold, preventing bacterial infection of the wound. Egle et al. immersed PRF in fucoidan/chitosan (FU_CS) hydrogels, allowing for the uniform distribution of the PRF network within the hydrogels and enabling the sustained release of GFs [196]. Multilayer scaffolds have also been used to load PRFs due to the large differences in the structure of single-layer scaffolds and real skin [197]. Shahriari-Khalaji et al. prepared a bilayer scaffold consisting of hydrogel and electrostatically spun scaffold (Fig. 9C) [198]. The hydrogel consisted of CMC, catechol-grafted oxidized alginate and PRF adhered to an electrospun layer containing gallium. The composite scaffold exhibited excellent ability to promote cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation, as well as antimicrobial properties, and in vitro and ex vivo vasculogenic capacity, which enabled effective treatment of infected burn wounds. Similarly, Tavakoli et al. constructed a composite scaffold based on pectin/polyacrylic acid (Pec/PAA) that combines 3D printing with electrospinning. The upper layer was loaded with PRF to supply GFs, while the lower layer incorporated simvastatin to support cell adhesion, presenting a novel approach for wound healing materials [199].

Conclusion and prospects

The traditional therapeutic approaches for wound healing are no longer sufficient to meet the current therapeutic needs due to the complexity of the wound healing process. The combination of concentrated platelets and tissue engineering scaffolds presents a new and promising method for wound repair. In this approach, concentrated platelets serve as a source of GFs, providing the necessary GFs for wound induction, stimulation, and promotion. Tissue engineering scaffolds, on the other hand, serve as platforms for the sustained release of GFs and support cell adhesion, migration and proliferation. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the combination of platelet concentrates and tissue engineering scaffolds is a faster and more efficient approach compared to traditional wound dressings such as gauze and bandages. This review provides a systematic summary of the advancements in platelet concentrates combined with tissue engineering scaffolds in wound healing in recent years, with the aim of inspiring more diverse ideas for this innovative strategy.

Although PRP, PRF, CGFs, and PL are all derived from autologous blood, their unique preparation methods determine their biological properties during the wound healing process. They can be encapsulated within scaffolds and released as the scaffolds degrade. Alternatively, they can be coated onto the surface of the scaffolds, enabling functionalization. It is worth mentioning that certain concentrated forms of platelets can serve as scaffolds themselves in this process. PRP requires external activation to form a fibrin clot from a liquid state. In contrast, PRF and CGFs utilize endogenous fibrin polymerization processes to form three-dimensional fibrin networks without the need for anticoagulants. These networks not only serve as temporary matrices for cell attachment but also release GFs over an extended period, which aligns with the prolonged proliferation phase required for chronic wounds. PL lacks cells and the spatial structure necessary to guide tissue regeneration, functioning solely as a pure mixture of soluble growth factors.

Here, tissue engineering scaffolds are categorized as hydrogel scaffolds, electrospun nanofiber scaffolds, sponge scaffolds and other scaffolds. Each type of scaffolds plays distinct roles in their respective tissue engineering applications due to its unique characteristics such as water content, porosity, swelling properties, mechanical strength and degradability. Hydrogels excel at maintaining a moist microenvironment, which is critical for preventing tissue desiccation and promoting the diffusion of nutrients and water-soluble growth factors. However, their dense network of nanopores often lacks the required for rapid cell infiltration and typically exhibits low mechanical strength. In contrast, sponges possess highly interconnected macroporous channels that can readily absorb excess wound exudate and provide ample three-dimensional space for extensive neovascularization and deep cellular growth; however, once fully saturated with water, they may collapse structurally due to physical stress. Meanwhile, electrospun nanofiber scaffolds uniquely mimic the topographical structure of the natural extracellular matrix. With their extremely high surface-to-volume ratio and ordered fiber architecture, they provide the necessary physical cues to guide directed cell migration and promote robust cell adhesion, making them well-suited for wound re-epithelialization. Therefore, exploring how to leverage the distinct performances of platelet concentrates and tissue engineering scaffolds to achieve organic combination and ultimately maximize their effectiveness in practical application is a significant topic for discussion.

(1) Adapting the scaffold to the irregular shape of the wound poses a challenge. Unlike artificially constructed animal wound models that have regular shapes, real wounds often exhibit irregularities. This makes it difficult for pre-fabricated scaffolds to provide complete coverage One potential solution is to develop injectable or sprayable scaffolds that can conform to the shape of the wound upon application. Alternatively, temperature-sensitive or pressure-sensitive can be employed to facilitate a transition from liquid state to a solid scaffold in situ at the wound site. These approaches aim to create a dressing that can effectively mold itself to the unique contours of the wound.

(2) Using the release dynamics of different types of scaffolds to construct a multi-stage release system to better address the requirements of wound healing. One approach involves the combination of two scaffolds with different degradation rates. The first scaffold, characterized by rapid degradation, can encapsulate factors needed in the early stages of wound healing to facilitate their quick and substantial release. The second scaffold, designed to degrade at a slower rate, can encapsulate factors required throughout the healing process, ensuring a sustained and prolonged release. In addition, the introduction of heat-sensitive, light-sensitive, magnetically sensitive or pressure-sensitive materials can enable the scaffolds to exhibit controlled release properties, enhancing the precision and timing of factor delivery. This strategy allows for a tailored and optimized release of therapeutic factors to support the dynamic healing needs of the wound.

(3) While numerous studies have shown the beneficial effects of this innovative strategy on angiogenesis, collagen deposition and re-epithelialization, the specific mechanisms underlying these processes remain largely unexplored. Understanding the signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms involved in implementing these processes would greatly benefit future research and the exploration of novel methods. By elucidating the precise mechanisms through which this strategy operates, researchers can pave the way for the development of new approaches and interventions in the future.

(4) Exploring the combination of this innovative strategy with other therapies holds significant promise. By incorporating components with photothermal or photodynamic properties into the system, wound healing can be further enhanced. These properties not only directly promote tissue repair but also indirectly facilitate wound healing by eliminating inhibitory factors through photothermal/photodynamic antibacterial action. This approach can potentially maximize the effectiveness of tissue repair by addressing both direct and indirect factors that influence wound healing.

(5) Indeed, ensuring the safety of composite scaffolds is paramount. While pursuing high efficacy, it is crucial to prioritize the high biocompatibility, low toxicity and low immunogenicity of tissue engineering scaffolds. Failing to do so would contradict our original intention of improving patient outcomes.

(6) The ultimate objective of all explorations is to translate findings into improved treatment strategies that can benefit patients in clinical settings. The feasibility of combining platelet concentrates and tissue-engineered scaffolds for wound healing has been demonstrated in animal wound models, primarily using mouse examples. However, since there are differences in wound healing patterns between mice and humans, with mice exhibiting wound contraction and closure while humans undergo epithelial regeneration, it is essential to expand beyond mouse models. Conducting animal experiments on larger species, such as orangutans, which have wound-healing patterns more similar to humans, could provide valuable insights and help validate the efficacy of the therapy. Furthermore, to ensure successful clinical translation, a substantial amount of clinical research data must be generated to enhance the credibility and reliability of the treatment approach. In addition to optimizing the biological and structural design of these composite dressings, their transition from the laboratory bench to routine clinical use is hindered by numerous translational challenges. The most fundamental issue lies in interpatient variability. Because platelet concentrates are inherently autologous, baseline concentrations of active growth factors and cytokines fluctuate significantly depending on a patient’s age, overall health, and metabolic status. This biological heterogeneity complicates the determination of standardized therapeutic doses and makes it difficult to predict consistent healing outcomes across different patient populations. Coupled with obstacles related to scaling and reproducibility, these biological unpredictabilities become even more complex. While scaffold components can typically be mass-produced with precise batch-to-batch consistency, the integration of fresh, patient-derived blood products requires preparation at the point of care. Developing reliable and reproducible experimental workflows remains a major engineering and operational bottleneck. Future advancements in this field depend not only on material innovation but also on active collaboration with regulatory agencies to establish clear quality control standards and streamline the approval process for personalized regenerative therapies.

We firmly believe that through continued in-depth and diverse studies, innovative strategies for wound repair will be refined and undergo long-term development. With a strong commitment to safety and ongoing research, we can advance the field and bring about significant improvements in wound healing approaches.
