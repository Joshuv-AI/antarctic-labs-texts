Right-sided complete blindness post-kyphoscoliosis correction surgery – A case report
Authors: Dipen Ariwala, Sandeep Sonone, Chitranshu Shrivastava, Harini Seshadri, Sunil Bhosale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Visual loss post spine surgery is a rare but serious complication that can occur after elective spine surgeries. Three pathological mechanisms usually cause it: raised intraocular pressure-mediated damage, central retinal artery occlusion, and cortical blindness.Case Description: A 13-year-old male developed permanent vision loss after undergoing prone spinal deformity correction surgery (i.e., D3 vertebra excision and overcorrection of the spinal curve) for spastic paraplegia. He developed a complete loss of vision in the right eye on postoperative day 2, which was later diagnosed as central retinal artery thrombosis. Although the authors padded both eyes when using a horseshoe headrest, they now acknowledge that such cases should routinely employ a 3-pin head holder to make sure that the eyes are entirely free.Conclusion: In this case, the long duration of surgery and intraoperative hypotension likely caused this catastrophic postoperative complication. In the future, an arterial line to avoid hypotension, prophylactically raising the head 20°, and use of the 3-pin head holder should be routinely employed to prevent/limit the risk of intraoperative retinal artery occlusion.

INTRODUCTION

Visual loss post spine surgery (VLPS) is a rare complication of spine surgery performed in the prone position.[6] Usually, VLPS is attributed to three pathological mechanisms: raised intraocular pressure-mediated damage, central retinal artery occlusion, and cortical blindness.[1] However, other intraoperative iatrogenic variables include: sudden blood pressure drop, blood loss, and prolonged hypotension. Here, a 13-year-old male undergoing cervicodorsal kyphoscoliosis deformity correction surgery in the prone position developed the delayed 2-day onset of complete right central retinal artery occlusion, leading to irreversible blindness.

CASE REPORT

Clinical presentation with spastic paraplegia

Over the course of a few days, a 13-year-old male [Figures 1-3] developed severe bilateral lower extremity weakness with loss of bladder control (i.e., spastic paraplegia with bladder involvement (American Spine Injury Association [ASIA]/International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury [ISCoS] type B).[3] The patient’s height, weight, and Tanner stage were 153 cm, 48 kg (body mass index 20), Tanner-4, respectively. The patient had normal bilateral vision. The cervicothoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed complete anterior cord compression just above/below the T3 hemivertebra [Figure 4]. Using a posterior approach with D3 vertebra excision and overcorrection of the spinal curve (since he had D10 hemivertebra) to achieve coronal and sagittal alignment was planned.

Surgery: Failure to use intraoperative neural monitoring and total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA)

Anesthesia included the placement of an arterial line and administration of Propofol (2 mg/kg) and Inj. Fentanyl (2 mm/kg). Inj. Atracurium (1 mg/kg) was maintained on Sevoflurane 2. Note, there was no intraoperative monitoring (IONM), and no TIVA was used, compatible with IONM.

Padding of eyes and use of horseshoe head holder; both incorrect prophylaxis against intraoperative visual loss

In the prone position, eyes were padded, and his head was supported in a horseshoe-shaped headrest. Surgery required over 9 h during which time he sustained hypotension and a massive intraoperative blood loss of 2.4 L; he received three pints of fresh frozen plasma to counter the onset of hypocoagulation, and his anemia was treated with three units of packed red blood cells (i.e., VOLUME USED: 500 CC). In addition, intraoperatively, the head was manipulated to achieve sagittal correction; the elevation continued for the rest of the procedure. Postoperative radiograph of the cervicodorsal spine showed acceptable spinal deformity correction [Figure 5]. By 2 days postoperatively, the patient had complete lower limb recovery of sensory and motor function (i.e., ASIA/ISCoS E). One month later, he was mobilized with a total body contact brace.

Fundoscopy showing cherry red spot.

Postoperative right eye complete blindness due to retinal artery occlusion

By postoperative day 2, the patient complained of painless, complete loss of vision in the right eye. An urgent ophthalmology consultation was followed by both MRI and computed tomography brain studies that proved normal, along with a negative carotid Doppler. The MRI of the right eye revealed mild right-sided T2/short-tau inversion recovery hypersensitivity secondary to intra- and periorbital edema, but the right optic nerve showed no abnormal signal. The fundoscopic examination, however, revealed a cherry red spot, suggesting central retinal artery occlusion [Figure 6]. Immediately, anterior chamber paracentesis was performed. Nevertheless, ultrasonography of the right orbit revealed right central retinal artery thrombosis [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

VLPS is mostly attributed to prone positioning during spine surgeries. Surgeries that are traditionally long, such as deformity correction or multiple-level posterior fixation, will create greater disturbance in the internal eye environment and disturb eye physiology. Three main pathological mechanisms have been identified that explain VPLS: (1) ischemic optic neuropathy, (2) cortical blindness, and (3) central retinal artery thrombosis. (2) Fundus examination and intraocular pressure measurement carried out postoperatively help to differentiate the three.

Several systemic and ophthalmic causes may predispose to visual loss post-surgeries performed in the prone position. Whether comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of cerebrovascular event, epilepsy, sickle cell spectrum, cerebral palsy, and hypercoagulation states are at a greater risk is still a subject that is yet to be explored. Stevens et al.[6] published a retrospective review of 3450 patients who underwent spinal surgery, out of which seven developed loss of visual acuity postoperatively. Of these seven patients, three had complete resolution of visual acuity [Table 2].[6]

Preexisting eye phenotypes such as eyes with a shorter axial length, suspicion of angle closure glaucoma, and history of retinal artery thrombosis should be identified preoperatively, and necessary intraoperative arrangements to monitor ocular measurements should be made. Drugs received intraoperatively or preoperatively, such as procoagulants, colloids, blood transfusions, and norepinephrine, also contribute to the pathology of retinal artery thrombosis. In our case, none of the above conditions existed for our patient, as a thorough preoperative assessment by surgeons, ophthalmologists, and anesthetists was done. An important predisposing factor could be the duration for which the patient was in the prone position during surgery (over 10 h for our patient). A right sloping lateral tilt was given intraoperatively, which may have spared the left eye (higher position) but selectively disturbed the pressures of the right eye (lower). Massive blood loss involved in long spine surgeries may also contribute to the result. However, Myers et al.[4] concluded that blindness after spinal surgery was more common than previously reported and more common after complex instrumental fixation. They also found no difference between the patients’ hematocrit and blood pressure levels and those of age-matched controls.[4]

Future preventive measures should include: avoidance of intraoperative hypotension attributed to severe acute blood loss/anemia, prophylaxis to position the patient’s HOB up 20+° (reverse Trendelenberg), and immobilize the head with a 3-pin head hold[2] (i.e., thus avoiding any pressure on the eyes with a horseshoe-head rest).

A proper 3-stage protocol must be in place to prevent, detect, and immediately address visual loss during spinal surgeries. Preoperative and immediate postoperative ophthalmic assessment must be a routine for spinal deformity correction surgeries. A surgery, if suspected to last long, can be carried out in stages to prevent this outcome in high-risk individuals. Substitute positioning, such as lateral position surgery, can be used and improved on to match the results and desirability of prone positioning.

CONCLUSION

Following a cervicothoracic scoliosis deformity procedure performed in the prone position for over 9 h, a 13-year-old male sustained complete right-eye blindness due to complete retinal artery occlusion. Future preventive measures should focus on minimizing risk factors for perioperative visual loss. These include strict avoidance of intraoperative hypotension secondary to acute blood loss or anemia, maintaining the patient’s head in a reverse Trendelenburg position (head of bed elevated ≥20°), and ensuring proper head immobilization using a three-pin head holder to prevent direct ocular pressure associated with horseshoe headrests.
