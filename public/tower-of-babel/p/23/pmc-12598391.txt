Risk of Euglycemic Diabetic Ketoacidosis Can Persist After Discontinuation of Sodium-glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitors
Authors: Sai Sumedha Bobba, Elizabeth Campbell, Fernando Bril
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEuglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (EDKA) is a complication of diabetes often associated with prolonged fasting, pregnancy, and, more recently, the use of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. We present a 69-year-old woman with EDKA in the setting of cardiac surgery for multivessel coronary artery disease even after the SGLT-2 inhibitor was discontinued 5 days prior to the surgery. Postoperative lab values revealed concern for EDKA due to low bicarbonate, elevated anion gap, elevated β-hydroxybutyrate, and normal blood glucose levels. IV insulin and dextrose were administered to resolve the acidosis, but the anion gap again became elevated once the insulin infusion was stopped. We observed persistence of ketonemia and urinary glucose excretion postoperatively. This case implies that patients on SGLT2 inhibitors may be at risk for EDKA for a prolonged period even after discontinuing the medication for the recommended time before a major surgery.

Introduction

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are a class of medications that act on the proximal convoluted tubule of the renal cortex to increase the amount of glucose excreted in the urine [1]. Due to their effects on lowering blood glucose levels and cardiorenal protection, they are often prescribed to patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D), chronic kidney disease, and heart failure. However, due to SGLT2 inhibitors’ effects on promoting ketogenesis, they can lead to euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (EDKA), a potentially life-threatening complication, especially in patients with prolonged fasting or those reducing insulin doses significantly. In a systematic review of cases of EDKA secondary to SGLT2 inhibitors, surgery was observed to be the most common precipitating factor (33%) [2]. In a recent meta-analysis, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors was associated with a 30% increased risk of EDKA during cardiac procedures [3].

The American Diabetes Association currently recommends that SGLT2 inhibitors be stopped at least 3 days (4 days for ertuglifozin) prior to scheduled surgery due to the risk of inducing EDKA [4]. EDKA is a rare but known complication of diabetes mellitus presenting with 3 components: normal blood glucose levels (less than 250 mg/dL [SI: 13.3 mmol/L]), increased anion gap metabolic acidosis (arterial pH less than 7.30, serum bicarbonate [HCO3−] less than 18 mEq/L [SI: 18 mmol/L] [reference range, 22-32 mEq/L] [22-32 mmol/L], and anion gap greater than 15 mEq/L [SI: 15 mmol/L] [reference range, 4-16 mEq/L] [4-16 mmol/L]), and ketonemia [5]. Because patients are often required to fast prior to scheduled surgeries and they oftentimes do not eat immediately after surgery, they are at an increased risk of EDKA if SGLT2 inhibitors are not stopped far enough in advance. While this is a known phenomenon, literature regarding the optimum time to stop them in anticipation of surgery is scarce, and current recommendations are mostly based on their pharmacokinetics. However, their effects are known to persist for longer periods of time [6]. We present a case of postoperative EDKA, which developed despite empagliflozin cessation 5 days prior to surgery, highlighting that in some cases these medications should be stopped for a longer period of time.

Case Presentation

A 69-year-old female with a past medical history of coronary artery disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, T2D, hypothyroidism, and gastroesophageal reflux disease presented to an outside hospital for severe chest pain with associated dyspnea. Initial workup diagnosed non-ST segment elevation myocardial infarction, and left heart catheterization demonstrated multivessel coronary disease. Percutaneous coronary intervention was attempted, but it was unsuccessful due to severe calcifications. She was subsequently transferred to our hospital for evaluation for coronary artery bypass grafting. She has a long-term history of T2D, and prior to admission, she was on metformin 1000 mg daily and empagliflozin 25 mg daily, which was started 3 months prior to presentation. Her glycated hemoglobin A1c at the time was 8.2% (SI: 66 mmol/mol) (reference range, <6.5% [<48 mmol/mol]).

Metformin and empagliflozin were discontinued on presentation at the outside hospital 5 days before the day of surgery. She was started on a basal bolus regimen with glargine 9 units daily and aspart 3 units with meals plus a sliding scale, totaling about ∼0.3 U/kg. The last dose of glargine was given at 2300 the night before surgery. Her labs prior to surgery were as follows: glucose: 138 mg/dL (SI: 7.7 mmol/L) (reference range nonfasting 70-200 mg/dL [3.9-11.1 mmol/L]), HCO3−: 26 mEq/L (SI: 26 mmol/L), anion gap: 10 mEq/L (10 mmol/L), creatinine: 0.8 mg/dL (SI: 70.7 μmol/L) (reference range, 0.4-1.2 mg/dL [35.4-106.1 μmol/L]). She started to fast at midnight on the day of her surgery. The surgery lasted approximately 4.5 hours and ended around 1800. She received 4 units of regular insulin at the conclusion of the surgery. After coronary artery bypass grafting, she was placed on a short course of vasopressors then extubated on the same day. She received 3 doses of atropine for symptomatic bradycardia with 2:1 heart block between 6 to 14 hours after surgery. She underwent a cosyntropin stimulation test due to ongoing postoperative hypotension. Despite adequate response, she was placed on stress dose steroids postoperatively by the surgical team and weaned off prior to discharge.

Diagnostic Assessment

The morning following surgery, patient was alert and oriented and had no complaints, except mild nausea and fatigue. Bladder temperature was 37.6 °C (99.7°F), heart rate 111 beats per minute, blood pressure 101/50 mmHg, and oxygen saturation appropriate on a nasal cannula at 6 L/min of oxygen. The patient's labs were as follows: glucose: 146 mg/dL (SI: 8.1 mmol/L), HCO3−: 17 mEq/L (SI: 17 mmol/L), anion gap: 22 mEq/L (SI: 22 mmol/L), pH: 7.30, creatinine: 0.7 mg/dL (SI: 61.9 micromol/L). The patient had eaten a small breakfast consisting of toast and fruit. While there were no abnormal symptoms reported during this time, her lab values raised concerns that she had developed EDKA, and endocrinology was consulted. A β-hydroxybutyrate level was obtained and notably elevated at 82.2 mg/dL (SI: 7.9 mmol/L) (reference range, 0.0-3.1 mg/dL [0.0-0.3 mmol/L]), and other causes of acidosis were excluded, confirming the diagnosis of EDKA. Other labs obtained through the admission included: C-peptide of 1.46 ng/mL (SI: 0.48 nmol/L) (reference range, 0.9-7.1 ng/mL [0.3-2.4 nmol/L]) with blood glucose of 225 mg/dL (SI: 12.5 mmol/L), and antibodies were all negative (including glutamic acid decarboxylase-65, zinc transporter 8, islet cell autoantigen-512, and antipancreatic islet cell), indicating normal pancreatic function and ruling out latent autoimmune diabetes.

Treatment

The patient was started on dextrose 10% and IV insulin infusions with appropriate response (see Fig. 1). Her lab values returned to normal, and the patient was tolerating oral intake without issues, but she again developed a metabolic acidosis consistent with EDKA once the IV insulin drip was stopped 2 days later. Despite restarting the basal/bolus insulin regimen, her β-hydroxybutyrate remained elevated for 5 days postsurgery, with persistent urinary glucose and ketone excretion.

Outcome and Follow-up

After 5 days of monitoring and treatment, the patient's labs indicated resolution of the EDKA, including negative β-hydroxybutyrate, closed anion gap, and normal bicarbonate. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 but still showed signs of ketones and glucose on serial urinalyses. The patient was discharged on metformin with a reduced dose of 500 mg daily and started on glargine 14 units daily. Empagliflozin was discontinued given the patient's preference. On her follow-up appointment, her hemoglobin A1c was 7.6% (SI: 60 mmol/mmol), and lab values were otherwise unremarkable.

Discussion

This case provides evidence that there is an increased risk of glucosuria, ketonuria, and EDKA that exists beyond 3 days of stopping SGLT2 inhibitors in the setting of surgery and prolonged fasting. This raises the question as to whether the recommendation to stop SGLT2 inhibitors preoperatively should be extended beyond the currently established 3 days, especially for high-risk procedures.

Currently, many national health organizations have published guidelines on the importance of discontinuing SGLT2 inhibitors prior to scheduled surgeries. For example, the American Diabetes Association [4] and the American College of Cardiology [7] recommend stopping empagliflozin, canagliflozin, and dapagliflozin 3 days prior to surgery while ertugliflozin is recommended to be stopped 4 days prior to surgery to reduce the risk of postoperative ketoacidosis. This longer discontinuation period for ertugliflozin is attributed to its longer half-life of 16.6 hours as compared to the other SGLT2 inhibitors (∼8-13 hours) [8]. However, there is significant evidence that SGLT2 inhibitors’ effects can persist for longer periods of time compared to their plasma concentration. After administration of 100 mg of empagliflozin to healthy volunteers, urinary glucose excretion remained significant after 60 hours, despite undetectable plasma levels of empagliflozin [6]. Indeed, SGLT2-inhibitors also have a slow dissociation (“off”) rate from their renal target, which indicates that they can continue to act on the transporter even at low plasma concentrations, explaining their prolonged effects [6]. Based on this information, cases of EDKA occurring long after SGLT-2 inhibitors are stopped could be expected even when, based on half-lives, drug concentrations should be very low. For example, a patient with T2D developed persistent EDKA for 8 days after discontinuing dapagliflozin [9]. As SGLT2-inhibitors are increasingly used in metabolic conditions, it is important to consider that their therapeutic effects may persist beyond the medication's plasma half-life, especially in the setting of interacting medications, prolonged fasting, or surgery.

In September 2023, the Food and Drug Administration updated the SGLT-2 inhibitors prescription label to indicate that urinary glucose excretion persists for up to 3 days after discontinuing the medication but also warned that glucosuria and ketoacidosis may last longer than previously anticipated [10]. Indeed, the label states that there were postmarketing reports of ketoacidosis and/or glucosuria lasting more than 6 days (and up to 2 weeks) after discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors [10].

As more cases of persistent glucosuria and ketogenesis are identified, this raises concern regarding whether this medication should be stopped even sooner before major scheduled surgeries. A retrospective study in an academic hospital found that EDKA developed in 15% of patients on SGLT2 inhibitors undergoing cardiac surgery, although no information was provided about when they were stopped before surgery [11]. In a systematic review of SGLT2-inhibitor related perioperative ketoacidosis, it was found that the number of related cases has increased significantly in recent years [12]. Importantly, more than 25% of EDKA cases related to SGLT-2 inhibitors were diagnosed 4 or more days after surgery.

It is clear that our case is not an isolated one. Therefore, there is an important need to identify risk factors, which could help identify which patients may benefit from stopping SGLT2 inhibitors more than 3 days before surgery. In our case, there were many perioperative risk factors that could have contributed to the development of EDKA, such as prolonged fasting, major cardiovascular surgery, use of steroids and vasopressors, and insulin adjustments. Indeed, patients undergoing gastric bypass and cardiac surgery appear to be at particularly high risk for EDKA [12].This decision should additionally weigh the potential risks of treatment interruption in patients taking these medications. It is also important in individuals taking SGLT2 inhibitors to have a low threshold to consider EDKA in cases of acidosis and/or consistent symptoms. Early initiation of dextrose and insulin can also help mitigate risks of EDKA in patients that require prolonged fasting.

In summary, we present a case that shows EDKA developed even after stopping the SGLT2 inhibitor 5 days prior to a major surgery. Moreover, we showed the presence of persistent ketonemia at least 8 days after the last dose of empagliflozin and glucosuria that was still present at the time of discharge 10 days after the last dose of the medication. The clinical implications of this case are that EDKA may occur even after stopping the medication for the recommended amount of time, and a longer washout period may be needed for specific surgeries or for certain patients. More thorough research is needed to determine which patients are at particularly high risk of EDKA and the appropriate number of days for discontinuing these medications in preparation for surgeries.
