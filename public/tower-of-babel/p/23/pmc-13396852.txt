Criteria and enabling conditions for assessing cash-releasing interventions: An evidence review in the context of the NHS ʻthree shifts’
Authors: Freddie Finlay, Wayne Smith, Tim Shaw, Oliver Carey, Akkshata Ahire, Amardeep Rai, Nigel Edwards, Gwyn Bevan, Charles Tallack, Andi Orlowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The NHS faces unprecedented financial pressure, requiring the identification of cash-releasing interventions (CRIs). To support evaluation of this, we established five criteria based on relevant frameworks. We conducted an evidence review of published and grey literature (2019–2025), identifying and evaluating CRIs aligned with NHS England’s ʻthree shifts’. 186 papers (filtered from 5,175 papers through eight stages) and 151 grey literature documents were assessed against the criteria. No sources contained enough information to determine whether they were cash releasing. There were 332 interventions with potential across community (n = 88), digital (n = 123) and prevention (n = 121), with most requiring 1–5-year implementation. Conditions that would enable cash release were identified. Numerous interventions improved the way that the NHS uses resources. It suggests that the NHS can improve population health and financial sustainability by reallocating resources into higher-value and more cost-effective interventions. Cash release should therefore be a means to improve value for populations, rather an objective itself.

Introduction

NHS financial context

The NHS in England operates within an environment of sustained and intensifying financial pressure that requires identification of interventions that release cash from existing budgets. A £6.6 billion deficit for 2025/26 was projected for NHS systems before the 2025 budget,1 and this shortfall is expected to persist despite the £29 billion funding increase announced subsequently.2 Analysis shows that many organisations face efficiency targets between 5% and 11%, substantially exceeding historically achievable rates of approximately 0.9% annually.3

The King’s Fund warns that some services are becoming financially unsustainable, and calls for greater realism from both government and the public about what the NHS can deliver in the short term. Their analysis demonstrates that tight budgets force NHS leaders to make difficult decisions to balance their books and protect patient care, with measures including freezing recruitment, reducing agency spend and delaying capital investments.1

Many studies of new models of care and other approaches to improving efficiency are economic evaluations and interested in cost-effectiveness rather than cost-saving. As such, analysis is based on the full cost effect of the intervention. However, from a financial rather than an economic perspective, this will not reflect the savings available. In most cases, many fixed and semi-variable costs cannot be significantly reduced and realised savings are often only the variable elements unless other actions are taken.

Within this context, cash-releasing interventions represent essential mechanisms for reducing waste, improving value, releasing resources and creating financial headroom for future transformation. Without releasing cash through targeted interventions, the NHS risks worsening access, declining performance, constrained capacity for innovation and not meeting financial targets.

NHS England’s 10-Year Health Plan articulates three fundamental shifts required for sustainability: moving care from hospitals to communities, from analogue to digital systems, and from treating sickness to preventing ill health.4 These shifts were incorporated into the screening framework, to ensure that all included sources were relevant to at least one.

Defining cash-releasing interventions

Despite widespread discussion of efficiency and cost-effectiveness in healthcare literature, the concept of ʻcash-releasing’ interventions lacks a consistent definition. ʻCash releasing’ is a distinct financial process that follows a clear set of criteria. Drawing on frameworks from the Government Efficiency Framework,5 the Healthcare Financial Management Association,6 and HM Treasury’s Green Book guidance on public sector business cases,7 we developed five criteria that distinguish cash release from efficiency gains or cost-effectiveness:1.Direct budget reduction is explicit: The change reduces actual spend or budget allocation, not merely ʻsaves time’ or ʻimproves efficiency’ without tangible financial impact.2.Clear route to remove cash from baseline: The intervention identifies specific mechanisms for cash extraction, such as lower contract prices, headcount reduction or estate cost reductions.3.Has baseline and counterfactual: A stated baseline cost or ʻwhat would have happened otherwise’ scenario enables verification of savings.4.Realised in-year (or time-phased) and sustainable: Savings materialise within defined timeframes rather than through cost deferral, and are recurring rather than one-off.5.No hidden cost shifting or performance harm: Savings do not create new costs elsewhere in the system and do not degrade clinical outcomes or service performance.

Research aim and hypotheses

This review aimed to identify published and grey literature detailing cash-releasing interventions implemented in UK health or healthcare settings relevant to the three shifts, and to explore why cash release remains elusive despite numerous cost-effective interventions.

We hypothesised that few, if any, interventions would meet all five criteria, reflecting the structural reality that even cost-effective interventions often fail to result in tangible cash release. This hypothesis draws on recognition that NHS funding mechanisms, demand pressures and organisational behaviours often prevent efficiency gains from translating into budget reductions. Additionally, we recognised that most studies within the evidence base were likely to be economic evaluations rather than an exploration of financial transactions. As a result, we did not expect to see a significant volume of research showing cash release.

Methodology

Research design and protocol development

We adopted a literature review methodology to identifying and evaluating cash-releasing interventions, drawing on established methods for evidence synthesis while incorporating novel AI-assisted screening procedures. The research question was developed through relevant stakeholder engagement.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across multiple databases and information sources. Published literature was identified through searches of EMBASE, MEDLINE and Cochrane databases. Grey literature sources searched included; NHS Futures platforms, NHS England publications, integrated care system reports, and health think tank outputs from organisations including The King’s Fund, Nuffield Trust, Health Foundation and NHS Confederation.

Search terms combined concepts of financial impact (eg ʻcash releasing,’ ʻcost benefit,’ ʻcost saving,’ ʻreturn on investment’) with general terms for interventions and healthcare settings. The search was deliberately broad to capture the full range of potentially relevant interventions without premature exclusion.

Screening process and inclusion/exclusion criteria

Screening of journal articles identified in the database searches proceeded through eight sequential stages with progressively stringent criteria (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Journal article sequential screening stages.

AI-assisted screening and validation

Large language model technology was employed at each screening stage to enhance efficiency and consistency while maintaining rigorous human oversight. The AI system was provided with explicit instructions defining each criterion, examples of inclusions and exclusions, and structured output formats. Human validation was conducted through dual review processes, with a random sample of 361 articles undergoing independent human review to assess AI accuracy. Agreement rates exceeded 77% across all stages, with disagreements resolved through discussion and refinement of AI instructions. All sources advancing to final cash-releasing assessment underwent full human review.

It also supported the identification and assessment of the relevance of grey literature sources, which included a final round of human screening to ensure only sources that met the inclusion criteria were included in the final evidence map.

Data extraction and synthesis

The final evidence map included 186 journal articles and 151 grey literature sources. For interventions not meeting all five criteria but demonstrating potential for cash release, we identified conditions required for full cash realisation.

Findings

Overview of cash-releasing evidence

None of the 337 sources reviewed met all five criteria for cash release. Although only one source explicitly used the term ʻcash releasing’ in its discussion, even that did not meet all criteria.

332 interventions were classified as conditionally cash releasing, because they demonstrated strong potential for cash release subject to specific enabling conditions being met. These interventions showed evidence of cost-effectiveness, cost savings or positive return on investment, often at local implementation levels, but lacked complete evidence of all criteria. The conditional nature reflects that these interventions have resulted in efficiencies or demonstrated economic value, but required further organisational action to transition from efficiency to tangible cash release. Criteria achievement rates

Analysis of how frequently each criterion was met reveals differential achievement across requirements:

Direct budget reduction: Approximately 45% of interventions showed actual budget reductions rather than just potential savings. The remaining 55% described projected or modelled savings without formal budget rebasing, often because evaluations occurred during pilot phases before full financial impact was captured.

Explicit cash removal mechanism: Approximately 70% of interventions described plausible routes to cash extraction, though many remained theoretical rather than operationalised. Common mechanisms included reduced admission rates, shortened length of stay, avoided procedures and staff time savings. However, these require further actions to ensure that cash release is realised, such as reconfiguration to ensure that beds are closed/repurposed or staff redeployed/made redundant.

Baseline and counterfactual: Approximately 60% included meaningful baseline costs and comparison scenarios. However, substantial variation existed in methodological rigour, with some relying on projected savings from modelling rather than measured outcomes against historical baselines.

Realised and sustainable: Only 30% demonstrated in-year realisation of savings with evidence of sustainability. Most interventions were prospective, pilot-stage or recently implemented, with savings modelled but not yet verified across multiple financial years.

No cost-shifting or performance harm: Approximately 50% provided sufficient assurance that savings did not create new costs elsewhere or degrade outcomes. The remainder either lacked comprehensive system-wide cost analysis or raised concerns about potential cost transfer.

These achievement rates highlight that evidence of cost-effectiveness is substantially more common than evidence of cash release. The gap between efficiency (doing more with less) and cash release (reducing actual budgets) represents a critical challenge for NHS financial sustainability.

Shift 1: hospital to community

89 interventions were primarily relevant to moving care from hospitals to communities. None met all criteria, while 88 were classified as conditionally cash releasing (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Temporal distribution of conditionally cash releasing interventions (Hospital to Community).

Temporal distribution of conditionally cash releasing interventions (Hospital to Community).

Interventions included Community First Responder services,8 community occupational therapy,9 peer-to-peer mentoring,10 link worker social prescribing,11 and home-based rehabilitation.12

Example: home-based rehabilitation after knee arthroplasty

The CORKA (Community-based Rehabilitation after Knee Arthroplasty) trial compared home-based rehabilitation to traditional outpatient physiotherapy for patients at risk of poor outcomes following knee arthroplasty.12 The study demonstrated that home-based rehabilitation was cost-effective with 75% probability at the £30,000/QALY threshold, being £66 cheaper per participant than usual care. Clinical outcomes were equivalent between groups, with home-based participants achieving comparable functional improvement while avoiding travel burden and hospital attendance.

However, this intervention was classified as conditionally rather than fully cash releasing. To release cash, CORKA would require widespread adoption reducing outpatient demand to levels enabling clinic operating hours, staffing, or estate costs to be reduced; workforce redesign enabling redeployment or reduction of senior physiotherapists; digital tools further reducing travel and appointment costs; commissioners restructuring contracts to reflect lower costs; and national scaling with consistent evidence allowing budget rebasing.

Shift 2: analogue to digital

126 interventions were primarily relevant to digital transformation of health and care. None met all five cash-releasing criteria, while 123 were classified as conditionally cash releasing (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Temporal distribution of conditionally cash releasing interventions (Analogue to Digital).

Temporal distribution of conditionally cash releasing interventions (Analogue to Digital).

Interventions included virtual wards,13 patient-facing apps/websites for self-management,14 smart operating theatres,15 shared electronic patient records16 and electronic job planning.17

Example: virtual heart failure ward

A study from a large rural county demonstrated the impacts of introducing a virtual heart failure ward, providing hospital-level monitoring and treatment in patients’ homes using remote monitoring technology and multidisciplinary team support.13 The virtual ward reduced heart failure admissions by 10%, released 1,147 acute bed days, and saved approximately £1 million, with high patient satisfaction.

However, to release cash, this model would require acute capacity reductions made permanent through closing or repurposing inpatient beds; commissioners restructuring contracts reflecting lower hospital utilisation; the model to be sustained and scaled; and costs of running the virtual ward remaining lower than avoided hospital costs with verified net savings.

Shift 3: treating sickness to preventing ill health

122 interventions were primarily relevant to prevention of ill health. None met all five cash-releasing criteria, while 121 were classified as conditionally cash releasing (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Temporal distribution of conditionally cash releasing interventions (Sickness to Prevention).

Interventions included health promotion,18 health checks,19 physical activity,20 prehabilitation,21 vaccinations,22 targeted screening23 and smoking cessation.24

Example: enhanced funding for footcare services

A case study examined the early impacts of expanding a foot protection team and implementing prevention strategies to reduce severe diabetic foot ulcers.25 The investment reduced severe ulcers from 46% to 36% of cases, saving £53,000 per 100 patients while improving quality of life and healing times.

However, to release cash, the intervention would require fewer hospital admissions, amputations or inpatient bed days translating into reduced commissioned activity; reduced demand sustained over time enabling permanent downsizing of high-cost specialist care; commissioners agreeing to realise savings through contract renegotiation; and preventive activities funded within existing resources.

Discussion

What conditions are required for cash release?

Analysis of the 332 conditionally cash-releasing interventions identified six thematic categories of conditions required for translation from efficiency to cash release:

Structural and financial

Cash release fundamentally depends on changing financial baselines rather than solely improving productivity. Without explicit mechanisms to remove funds from budgets, efficiency gains do not translate into savings. Required actions identified include:•budgets or contracts being explicitly reduced or withdrawn•funding streams restructured, so savings are captured•estate, workforce, or service lines rationalised with recurring costs removed•capital investment identified to enable estate and service reconfiguration.

Operational and workforce

Operational efficiencies, such as those releasing staff time, only generate cash if that freed capacity is reduced or redeployed. Freed capacity absorbed by other work, expanding scope or reducing waiting lists are valuable but do not release cash. Practical and political barriers

Even when technical conditions exist, practical and political barriers prevent realisation. Service closure creates access, equity, and reputational risks generating public opposition. Workforce reductions encounter contractual, ethical and operational constraints. System-level barriers include; fragmented budgets preventing savings realisation when benefits accrue to different organisations than costs, reinvestment pressures absorbing efficiency gains, and policy ambiguity about whether efficiency should fund improved access or cash extraction.

Additional barriers reflect the shifting landscape of NHS organisation. Current focus on frontline services and reduction of management capacity paradoxically undermines innovation capability. Identifying and implementing cash-releasing interventions requires analytical capacity, project management expertise and change management skills, typically concentrated in management roles. Potential changes to funding models create uncertainty that inhibits long-term commitments.

Should cash release be a primary objective?

None of the 337 studies contained evidence to show that all five criteria had been met, suggesting that releasing cash should not be the primary objective. Kay explains why some goals are best sought obliquely rather than directly.26 Kay sets out the pathological consequences for private enterprises of making profits for shareholders as overriding objective, which has resulted in the demise of a number of businesses.27 For the NHS, similarly setting a financial objective, releasing cash, may encounter the same consequences. Instead, interventions should be framed as the means to improve value through reallocation of resources. Only then will it be possible to engage the wide range of stakeholders needed to act as stewards and engage in a critical examination of how resources are currently allocated.28 These stakeholders must include patients and clinicians, as well as finance staff. One type of economic evaluation which sets out to maximise value and allocate resources efficiently is the STAR approach.29, 30

This is in line with a more measured approach to the implementation of the 10-year plan, that acknowledges the current financial constraints and key obstacles yet to be overcome.31

Limitations

Several methodological limitations of this study are acknowledged.

Firstly, the stringent definition of cash releasing excluded interventions achieving budget reductions, but that did not indicate whether they met all criteria. This was due to the focus of most published sources not being cash release. Health economics orthodoxy prioritises value (outcomes per pound spent) over absolute cost reduction, meaning that studies focus on cost-effectiveness ratios rather than budget impacts. Cash release also trails behind competing priorities like quality improvement and clinical effectiveness in NHS evaluation.

Secondly, interventions within the sources had to align with at least one of the three shifts, meaning that interventions that fell outside of this were not included. As a result, cash-releasing benefits relating to factors like estates, medicine optimisation etc were not captured. Additionally, interventions currently being implemented in alignment with the 10-Year Health Plan would not yet appear in literature, creating temporal lag between policy implementation and evidence availability.

Further restrictions to UK contexts and recent publications may have missed relevant international evidence or earlier pioneering implementations.

Conclusions

This literature review confirms the hypothesis that there have been no published interventions found in the UK healthcare literature published over 7 years (2019–2025) that meet our rigorous criteria for cash release: direct budget reduction; explicit removal mechanisms; clear baselines, in-year realisation with sustainability; and absence of cost-shifting or harm.

332 interventions demonstrated either gains in cost-effectiveness or efficiency: reduced resource consumption, superior outcomes with fewer inputs, or care delivery in lower-cost settings. However, none achieved full cash release. This highlights structural and operational challenges in expecting cash to be released through isolated changes within a system characterised by fixed costs, absorbed capacity and persistent demand pressure. It also reflects fundamental features of healthcare economics including supplier-induced demand, capacity absorption and organisational behaviours that prioritise service expansion over budget contraction.

The NHS can benefit from reallocating resources into more cost-effective interventions within its largely fixed budget. That means that financial sustainability will be achieved not through one-off cash savings, but through sustained value-based decision-making, supported by explicit disinvestment, service reconfiguration and rebasing of activity and budgets. Cash release should therefore be a means to the objective of improving value for populations rather an end in itself.

Recommendations

Based on findings, we recommend:1.Develop standardised NHS definition and classification of cash-releasing interventions to enable systematic identification and comparison.2.Encourage evaluations to assess cash releasing potential explicitly from intervention inception with dedicated assessment against cash-releasing criteria.3.Introduce routine monitoring of financial realisation following efficiency projects with variance analysis when savings fail to realise.4.Mandate joint financial and economic evaluation (utilising joint frameworks) during business case development with explicit assessment of workforce implications and capacity changes.5.Establish integrated care system-level governance for interventions with cross-organisational impacts, including authority to negotiate financial flows.6.Maintain realistic expectations about efficiency’s contribution while pursuing parallel reforms addressing workforce sustainability, capital investment and service scope.7.Commission research examining international approaches to cash release, longer-term impacts of UK interventions, and mechanisms translating efficiency into budget reductions. Researchers should go beyond a limited focus on cost-effectiveness and look systematically at cost impacts.
