Topological structure and influencing factors of the spatial CO₂ emission network in Chinese cities
Authors: Fengmei Yang, Xuefang Zhang, Nian Wang, Shudi Zuo, Meng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating the spatial correlation characteristics of CO2 emissions is crucial for promoting regional collaborative emission reduction. This study examines 334 Chinese cities, employing an adjusted gravity model to construct a carbon emission spatial correlation network (CESCN). Utilizing social network analysis (SNA) methods, it systematically examines the network structure characteristics, dynamic evolution patterns across 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020, and the driving factors for 2020. Key findings include: (1) China’s CESCN exhibits overall characteristics of “high resilience, low density, and weak hierarchy.” Inter-city carbon emission linkages are predominantly passively driven by macroeconomic activities, while networks based on proactive collaborative emission reduction remain underdeveloped. (2) The network demonstrates distinct node role differentiation and spatial clustering, with eastern coastal developed cities like Shanghai, Shenzhen, Hangzhou, and Suzhou serving as core hubs. (3) The network’s core-periphery structure underwent a clear evolution from being primarily geographically driven to being economically driven, with the core zone stabilizing in the more economically developed eastern region. (4) geographical distance, disparities in economic development levels, and differences in population size inhibit the formation of CESCN; conversely, variations in industrial structure and carbon intensity exert a positive promoting effect. By constructing a more comprehensive urban sample network, this research deepens the understanding of the overall spatial correlation patterns of China’s CO2 emissions, providing empirical evidence and decision-making references for optimizing regional low-carbon collaborative governance policies.Keywords: CO2 emissions, Spatial correlation, Network structure characteristics, Influencing factor, Social network analysisSubject terms: Complex networks, Complex networks, Development studies, Economics, Economics, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Background and motivation

Mitigating global warming through carbon emissions control has become a shared understanding in the international community1. In response to global warming, China has committed to peaking its carbon emissions by 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality by 2060. The public good characteristics of CO2 limit the effectiveness of local governance models that rely on administrative boundaries2. The regional collaborative emission reduction (RCER) model effectively enhances carbon reduction efficiency by mitigating information asymmetry among participating entities, making it a crucial pathway to achieving the “dual carbon” goals3,4.

The RCER emphasizes the efficient integration of resources and the full utilization of each party’s unique advantages5. Therefore, clarifying each region’s role and developmental trajectory within RCER initiatives is essential. Analyzing the structural characteristics of CO2 emissions spatial correlation networks (CESCN) and identifying their driving factors are essential steps. Such an analysis provides a basis for optimizing inter-regional emission reduction strategies and cooperation models, which, in turn, promote the transfer of best practices and strengthen the synergy and effectiveness of climate actions.

Literature review

Focus on research scale: a comprehensive portrait of Chinese cities’ CESCN

Research on the spatial relationships of carbon emissions has established a multi-scale analytical framework spanning national, provincial, and city levels. National-scale studies have revealed complex mechanisms of transnational carbon spillover, such as the spatial interaction effects of globalization and economic complexity on CO₂ emissions6, the global coupling patterns between air pollutants and carbon emissions7, and the spatial network characteristics in the coupling coordination of global industrial structure optimization and carbon emission efficiency8. Provincial-level studies focus on intra-China regional carbon transfer and coordination mechanisms. For instance, Li et al. (2025), based on a “provincial accounting-efficiency assessment-spatial synergy” framework, analyzed the spatiotemporal patterns of land-use carbon emissions and the driving factors and spatial spillover effects of their efficiency9. Sun et al. revealed the network structure of interprovincial carbon-reduction capabilities10. At the urban scale, as a critical level for further revealing the spatial linkage of carbon emissions, research initially focused on urban agglomerations as advanced forms of modern urban development. For instance, Yang & Su (2024) studied the Guanzhong Plain urban agglomeration, revealing that its spatial network of carbon reduction capacity exhibits a “core-periphery” structure driven by economic and technological disparities11. Similarly, Dong & Liu (2025) identified a similar structure in the carbon emission network of the Yangtze River Delta Urban Agglomeration, noting a mismatch between its emission reduction cooperation and the current network state12. However, beyond urban agglomerations, systematic investigations covering all individual cities nationwide remain insufficient. Although Wang et al. (2024) and Sun et al. (2024) conducted pioneering analyses of 260 and 284 Chinese cities, respectively5,13, these studies did not encompass all prefecture-level administrative units, potentially compromising the completeness and representativeness of the network structures. Therefore, constructing a spatial correlation network with a more comprehensive sample of cities is a fundamental prerequisite for accurately depicting the full picture of spatial interactions in carbon emissions across Chinese cities.

Methodological evolution: from correlation detection to network structure analysis

In spatial correlation studies of carbon emissions, methodological evolution has profoundly advanced our understanding of complex spatial relationships. Early research primarily relied on spatial autocorrelation analysis, such as employing the global Moran’s I index to detect overall patterns of carbon emission clustering or dispersion14,15, while Localized Spatial Association (LISA) metrics further identified local clusters of high values, low values, and spatial outliers16. However, while these methods effectively reveal patterns of spatial dependency, they struggle to quantify the strength and direction of associations between specific regions.

To address these limitations, spatial gravity models have been introduced to this field. Based on regional economic scale, emission levels, and distance decay effects, this model provides a classical framework for quantifying bilateral association strength17. It has been widely applied in studies of trade18,19, migration20,21, and other spatial interaction studies. However, traditional gravity models often implicitly assume symmetrical relationships, which contradicts the prevalent reality of bidirectional asymmetry in carbon emission flows22. To address this, scholars have refined the model by introducing directional correction coefficients, enabling more precise depictions of spatial flow network configurations for carbon emissions23. While the modified gravity model can more accurately quantify the strength of asymmetric linkages between specific city pairs, its analytical perspective remains fundamentally “point-to-point,” making it difficult to systematically reveal the intricate, interwoven relationship structures among all cities within an entire regional system. To overcome this limitation and fully leverage the rich “relational data” generated by the gravity model, research paradigms have evolved toward networked and systemic analysis24.

In this context, social network analysis (SNA) has become a mainstream paradigm for investigating the spatial correlations in carbon emissions. SNA represents a fundamental shift in research perspective: it treats different regions as network nodes and the directed correlation strength, calculated using a modified gravity model, as edges, thereby constructing a comprehensive directed and weighted spatial correlation network25,26. This paradigm transcends traditional “attribute” analysis by focusing on “relationships” themselves, enabling systematic examination of network-wide topological structures such as density and connectivity, as well as node centrality and control power5. Therefore, SNA provides a powerful analytical tool for understanding the spatial synergy and transmission mechanisms of carbon emissions.

Deepening research paradigms: from structural description to mechanistic analysis

Research based on SNA exhibits a clear progression of paradigms. The first category follows the “network construction - structural representation” paradigm, focusing on describing topological features such as network density and centrality24,27. A second, more advanced category expands on the “network construction - structural representation - influencing factor analysis” paradigm. This approach employs methods such as QAP regression to examine how factors such as geographic distance, economic level, and industrial structure drive network formation4,28. As a nonparametric permutation test, the Quadratic Assignment Procedure (QAP) enables the simultaneous analysis of regression relationships among multiple independent variable matrices and a single dependent variable matrix while reducing reliance on variable interpretation29. It has become a popular method for analyzing correlations between spatial association matrices and their driving factors4,28. Compared to the first paradigm, the second category of research emphasizes the importance of considering multifaceted influencing factors within carbon emission networks, shifting from simple structural characterization to exploring complex interactions that shape spatial associations. It underscores the significance of integrating multidimensional analysis to provide a more nuanced understanding of carbon emission dynamics and support effective policy interventions.

In summary, existing research has made significant progress at theoretical, methodological, and practical levels, laying a solid foundation for understanding spatial correlation networks of carbon emissions. However, through a systematic review, the following key gaps remain identifiable, collectively forming the starting point and innovative positioning of this study.

First, in terms of research breadth, a comprehensive city-level panoramic network characterization covering the entire nation remains incomplete. Although urban cluster studies and some large-sample investigations offer valuable insights, their limited sample sizes may fail to capture the intricate interconnections among Chinese cities fully. A more comprehensive and complete urban sample network is essential for accurately assessing the overall structure of national carbon linkages and the roles of key nodes.

Second, methodological depth requires advancement from network structure description to dynamic mechanism and multidimensional attribute analysis. While many studies follow the “structural representation”-“influencing factor analysis” paradigm, systematic, in-depth, integrated analysis is lacking regarding the dynamic evolution of core-periphery structures within networks, the differentiated roles of city nodes, and the comprehensive driving mechanisms underlying these structural characteristics.

Position and contributions

Based on the research gaps revealed by the above literature review, this study positions itself as follows: building a long-term time series of China’s CESCN on a more comprehensive urban sample, while deepening the “structure-mechanism” analytical paradigm. Specific contributions are as follows:

Sample Expansion and Network Completeness: By expanding the research sample to 334 Chinese cities, this study constructs one of the most comprehensive city-level CESCNs to date, providing a more reliable empirical foundation for understanding the holistic nature and complexity of spatial carbon emission linkages nationwide.

Dynamic Evolution and Deep Structural Analysis: Beyond tracking temporal shifts in aggregate network metrics, this study emphasizes analyzing the dynamic reconfiguration of core-periphery structures and functional evolution within the network. This reveals patterns of change in power and dependency relationships within spatial carbon emission linkages.

Determination of CESCN

CESCN is a sophisticated, multi-level, and interconnected network shaped by the interplay among production activities, consumer behavior, trade dynamics, and environmental performance31. SNA’s ability to consider both ‘attribute’ and ‘relational’ effects makes it more suitable for assessing complex, multi-level carbon emissions network structures5. Therefore, the research employs SNA to construct CESCN for 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020, using gravity values derived from CO2 emissions data across 334 cities. Core‑periphery structure

The core-periphery structure is a basic framework in network analysis, highlighting two distinct components within a network: a concentrated “core,” where nodes have tight connections, and a dispersed “periphery,” where nodes have weaker ties to the core and to one another. Numerous algorithms exist for identifying the core-periphery structure. For this study, the absolute core-periphery model within UCINET software is selected for its ability to classify nodes into “core” and “periphery” based on the intensity of their connections.

Data sources

The China City Greenhouse Gas Working Group provided data on CO2 emissions, carbon intensity, population, GDP, and GDP per capita for this study. This dataset, constructed using a unified accounting framework and emission factor methodology, provides a foundational socio-economic database, including CO₂ emissions at the prefecture-city level in China. It is widely recognized and extensively cited as an authoritative source within the field. The data were downloaded from the official CCG website1.

Industrial Structure and Urbanization Rate: Data on industrial structure and urbanization rate for each city were sourced from the corresponding annual editions of the China City Statistical Yearbook and city-level Statistical Bulletins on National Economic and Social Development.

Geographical Distance: The spatial distance between cities is measured as spherical distance. First, we obtained the longitude and latitude coordinates of each prefecture-level city’s seat of government using authoritative geographic data from the National Geomatics Center of China. Subsequently, the spherical distance between each pair of city coordinates was calculated using the standard formula with the Earth’s mean radius.

Result

Overall network characteristics

Using the modified gravity model, a spatial correlation matrix of CO2 emissions from 334 cities was derived. This matrix was then imported into Gephi to generate a spatial correlation network diagram (Fig. 2). The network exhibits a typical structure, where node size indicates cities’ prominence within the network, and node colors signify modularity classes. Shanghai, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Hangzhou, Suzhou, and Wuxi are central to the network, exhibiting strong correlations with other cities, thereby marking them as the core cities of the CESCN. These cities are notably advanced with robust economic growth, underscoring the critical role of economically prosperous regions in the formation of CESCN.

Diagram of the CESCN across 334 cities. The figures were created by us using Gephi (version 0.10.0, available at https://gephi.org).

Figure 3 illustrates the evolution of network connectedness, network density, network efficiency, and network hierarchy in the CESCN across 334 cities during the period under review. Throughout this timeframe, the network’s connectedness consistently maintained a value of 1, indicating a robust, resilient network structure. The structure of the CESCN has strong connectivity and robustness, transcending the limitations of geographical adjacency, and it exhibits universal spatial association and spatial spillover effects. The network density shows an upward trend (with a fluctuation in 2015), indicating that the connections between cities gradually strengthen. Still, the maximum value is only 0.1559, indicating that the tightness of the CESCN is not high. The network efficiency values in the sample observation years are all above 0.75, and the overall trend is downward (there are fluctuations in 2015). It shows that the CESCN is highly efficient. As connections increase, the multiple superposition effect becomes increasingly significant, and the structure of the CESCN will become more complex. The network hierarchy showed no substantial change during the sample observation period, and all were less than 0.012, indicating that the network hierarchy structure was not strict and that no significant relationship gradient had formed among cities in the CESCN. The spatial impact of core nodes on edge nodes is not sufficient. Therefore, strengthening the spatial spillover effects of central node cities on edge node cities becomes an essential direction for future low-carbon development between cities.

Figure 6 illustrates the spatial distribution of betweenness centrality. The ranking of the betweenness centrality of cities remained relatively the same during the study period. Cities exhibiting elevated betweenness centrality tend to cluster modestly within the Yangtze River Delta area. These cities possess a heightened capacity to exert influence over and regulate the movement of labor, capital, and various resources and technologies among cities across the CESCN, thus helping to regulate and limit the CO2 emissions of other cities and being conducive to the balanced allocation of resources in the network. Cities boasting elevated betweenness centrality frequently assume pivotal roles as stakeholders or policymakers in the realms of carbon quota allocation and carbon trading initiatives. They act as crucial “bridges,” serving as resource distribution conduits and intermediaries within the CESCN.

Closeness centrality

Figure 7 illustrates the spatial distribution of closeness centrality. The rankings of cities in terms of closeness centrality remained relatively stable throughout the study period. Cities with higher closeness centrality show a moderate clustering tendency within the Yangtze River Delta region. In 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020, the average closeness centrality was 56.81, 57.21, 57.15, and 57.53, respectively, and the number of cities greater than the average closeness centrality was 70, 71, 70, and 68, respectively. Dongying, Shenzhen, Suzhou, Wuxi, Guangzhou, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Ningbo, Beijing, Nanjing, Qingdao, Foshan, Dalian, Changzhou, Changsha, and Zhenjiang are among these 16 cities that have been in the top 20 for at least three out of the four years and are in the leading position of closeness centrality. This suggests that these cities are more likely to be spatially related to other cities, exhibit higher carbon flow efficiency, and offer greater convenience in tracking the direction of CO₂ emissions within the network. Consequently, they wield stronger influence and authority in decision-making and regulation related to carbon emissions. Not only do these cities lead the country in economic competitiveness, but they also demonstrate robust vitality in scientific and technological innovation and talent attraction. As key allocation points for resources such as capital, technology, and energy, they are central in controlling, managing, and disseminating carbon reductions. This implies the presence of a “siphon effect” within the CESCN, originating from the competitive edge that certain cities hold due to their advanced economic development, strategic geographic positioning, efficient regional transportation networks, allure of resource elements, and prowess in energy technology. Their advanced practices and experience in CO₂ emissions management can guide emission management efforts in other cities and promote green, low-carbon development nationwide.

Core-periphery structure

Figure 8 illustrates the spatial distribution of the core-periphery structure of the CESCN. Over time, the core position has moved from the central to the eastern regions, forming a more stable pattern after 2015. This shift is attributed to the fact that, as economic development progresses, the dominance of geographical distance advantages in the network’s core-periphery division is gradually replaced by economic advantages. Central cities have a geographical distance advantage when connecting with other cities, which was crucial in the CESCN in 2005 when economic development was slower. As the economy rapidly develops, the economic level increasingly plays a leading role in the CESCN. Compared with other regions, eastern China has experienced more rapid economic growth, becoming a key engine of the nation’s economy, leading to the eastward shift of the core in the CESCN.

The geographical distance (GD) regression coefficient is -1.164599, indicating a statistically significant impact at the 1% confidence level. This suggests that a reduction in geographical distance fosters the development of the CESCN. When cities are closer in distance, the exchange of diverse resource elements becomes more accessible and cost-effective, promoting inter-city collaboration and, consequently, the formation of the CESCN33.

The regression coefficient for population difference (POP) is -0.000080, which is statistically significant at the 1% level. This suggests that the population gap inhibits the formation of CESCN. A larger gap in population size may lead to uneven development rates between cities, thereby significantly increasing the complexity involved in allocating emission reduction responsibilities. Such complexity not only hampers the achievement of synergistic emission reduction but also consistently hinders communication and cooperation among cities in reducing CO₂ emissions.

The per capita GDP differences (PC-GDP) regression coefficient is -0.022922, indicating a statistically significant impact at the 1% confidence level. This suggests that the existence of the economic development gap is not conducive to forming CESCN. More essential differences in financial development levels imply more significant trade barriers, mismatches between market demand and supply, barriers to investment and cooperation, and barriers to technology and knowledge transfer between cities. Therefore, compared with cities with the same level of economic development, the greater the difference in economic growth, the less economic interaction between cities, and thus the relatively minor correlation of CO2 emissions.

The regression coefficient for industrial structure differences (IS) is 0.001763, statistically significant at the 1% level. This suggests that variation in industrial structure facilitates the formation of CESCN. Differences in industrial composition typically reflect the varying reliance of cities on different industry types and their relative weights in economic development. Such industrial specialization encourages the flow of factors such as high-carbon goods and low-carbon services, thereby strengthening intercity carbon emission trading34. Furthermore, industrial structural disparities contribute to phenomena such as industrial relocation, enhanced cross-city investment and technological cooperation, and labor mobility—all of which promote the interconnectedness of CO₂ emissions across regions.

The regression coefficient for carbon intensity differences (CI) is 0.003712, which is statistically significant at the 1% level. This indicates that greater disparities in carbon intensity are associated with stronger correlations in CO₂ emissions between cities. Differences in industrial structure, production efficiency, and technological levels among cities may lead to variations in carbon intensity, thereby influencing the correlation with CO2 emissions. When a city does a better job of improving its productivity and technology levels, it may have a lower carbon intensity and a stronger link to cities with higher CO2 emissions, as these cities seek to improve their productivity and technology levels through cooperation, thereby reducing CO2 emissions.

Urbanization level differences (UL) do not significantly affect the formation of CESCN. Urbanization is a long-term process, and the impact on the spatial linkage of carbon emissions takes a longer period to emerge5. The study period (2020) selected in this study did not show a significant influence.

Discussions and policy implications

Discussions

This study reveals a series of structural characteristics within Chinese cities’ CESCN. These features do not exist in isolation but are deeply rooted in China’s specific developmental stage, regional strategies, and governance models, carrying profound practical significance.

First, the characteristics of high resilience, low density, and weak hierarchy in the network reflect the current mismatch between the breadth and depth of regional linkages in China. The consistently high network resilience, as indicated by a measure that remains at 1, demonstrates that cities have established extensive and fundamental carbon emission connections that transcend geographical adjacency, facilitated by the cross-regional distribution of domestic industrial chains. However, the relatively low absolute network density and weak hierarchy collectively reveal an insufficient depth in these connections, reflecting long-standing institutional challenges such as administrative regional economic barriers35 and local protectionism36. Carbon emission connections between cities are largely passively driven by macroeconomic activities such as trade and investment. While tightly knit, efficient institutional cooperation networks based on proactive, coordinated emission reduction goals have not yet matured. Therefore, strengthening network density and establishing a reasonable functional hierarchy are key breakthroughs for achieving nationwide coordinated low-carbon governance.

Second, the functional differentiation of core nodes and the eastward shift of the core region reflect the evolving trajectory and shifting drivers of China’s regional development. The study identifies cities such as Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Hangzhou as “bidirectionally active” hubs. Their centrality stems not merely from their economic scale but, more critically, from their strategic positions within national industrial, innovation, and capital chains. This observation supports the view that in the stage of high-quality development, factors such as economic sophistication, technological capacity, and energy efficiency have become new determinants of network power5. Concurrently, the core region has shifted steadily from central inland areas, which relied more on geographical centrality around 2005, to the eastern coastal zone. This process exhibits a high degree of alignment with the state’s priority development strategy for coastal regions, indicating that the core structure of the CESCN is fundamentally shaped by patterns of economic agglomeration and radiation guided by national policy. Consequently, future efforts toward low-carbon regional synergy must be deeply integrated with national strategies for regional economic development, leveraging existing economic centers to establish corresponding centers of emission reduction.

Moreover, the influencing factors revealed by QAP analysis highlight the underlying tensions and potential pathways for regional synergistic emission reduction in China. The significant negative impact of geographical distance confirms the foundational role of geographical proximity in reducing transaction costs and fostering industrial collaboration, thereby providing empirical support for the policy approach of prioritizing the integration of emission reduction within city clusters and metropolitan areas as primary units37. The negative impact of per capita GDP disparities underscores the systemic constraints that regional development imbalances impose on synergistic efforts to reduce emissions. Significant economic development gaps may create substantial divides between developed and less developed regions in terms of perceived responsibility for emissions reduction, technological capacity, and willingness to invest. Therefore, establishing robust cross-regional mechanisms for ecological compensation and technology transfer becomes essential under the principle of “common but differentiated responsibilities.” Notably, the positive impacts of differences in industrial structure and carbon intensity indicate that regional industrial division of labor based on comparative advantage inherently generates significant carbon-emission linkages. If guided by policy to redirect such linkages toward enhanced low-carbon collaboration—for example, through co-building green supply chains or joint research and development in low-carbon technologies—it would be possible to transform existing economic connections into effective synergies for emission reduction.

Policy Implications

Based on the above discussion, the following policy recommendations are proposed:

Strengthen the radiation and leading role of core cities: Encourage core network cities such as Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Hangzhou not only to achieve low-carbon transformation themselves but also to actively enhance positive spatial spillovers to peripheral cities through industrial collaboration, technology transfer, carbon market linkage, and talent training, turning their network centrality into a driving force for emission reduction.

Leverage industrial differences to build complementary emission reduction alliances: Acknowledge carbon interdependencies arising from structural disparities, establish comprehensive carbon accounting and responsibility-sharing systems covering entire industrial chains, and promote the transfer of low-carbon technologies alongside industrial relocation to prevent pollution transfer without technology transfer.

Strengthen Monitoring and Evaluation of Spatial Carbon Networks: Integrate CESCN metrics into regional low-carbon development assessment frameworks. Dynamically track shifts in cities’ roles and interconnections within networks to provide scientific foundations for tailoring coordinated regional carbon reduction policies.

Conclusions and limitations

Conclusions

This study employs a modified gravity model and SNA to systematically examine the structural characteristics, evolution patterns, and driving factors of the CESCN across 334 Chinese cities. Key findings are as follows:

First, Chinese cities’ carbon emissions have formed a structurally stable, extensively interconnected, and increasingly cohesive complex spatial network. Its connectivity transcends geographical adjacency, demonstrating strong spatial spillover effects. However, the network overall exhibits characteristics of high resilience, low density, and weak hierarchy. Inter-city collaborative emission reduction relationships remain spontaneous and loosely organized, with core nodes yet to fully leverage their influence and regulatory capacity over peripheral nodes.

Second, cities within the network exhibit pronounced role differentiation and distinct regional clustering. Developed eastern coastal cities like Shanghai, Shenzhen, Hangzhou, and Suzhou serve not only as key carbon emission sinks and hubs but also as centers for resource flow and control within the network. Most cities demonstrate net carbon emission outflow (outdegree > indegree), reflecting the fundamental pattern of economic activity and carbon emissions spreading from core areas to peripheries.

Third, the core-periphery structure of CESCN has undergone a clear evolution from being primarily driven by geographic location to being dominated by economic potential. With economic development, the network core has steadily shifted and concentrated in the eastern regions, which exhibit faster economic growth and stronger factor attractiveness. The growing economic concentration has thus become the primary force shaping the spatial distribution of carbon emissions.

Fourth, geographical distance, differences in economic development levels, and differences in population size inhibit the formation of CESCN. Conversely, variations in industrial structure and carbon intensity exert a positive facilitating effect. The impact of differences in urbanization levels has not yet become significantly apparent during the study period.

Limitations and outlook

Methodological robustness requires further refinement: While the specific functional form of the modified gravitational model and the selection of the threshold are conventional and involve subjectivity. Future research could employ sensitivity analysis to systematically compare how different model settings and threshold schemes affect network core characteristics, thereby enhancing the robustness of conclusions.

The underlying mechanisms require further exploration: While QAP regression reveals statistical correlations among multiple factors and network connectivity, it fundamentally constitutes a correlation analysis that does not elucidate the specific pathways by which these factors influence carbon emission linkages. Subsequent research may employ alternative methodologies to elucidate the mechanisms underlying each factor’s influence, thereby providing policymakers with greater value in developing more targeted and effective CO2 reduction strategies.

Limitations of the representative case area: Nationwide analysis may obscure structural differences across regions. Future studies should conduct comparative analyses at the sub-regional or metropolitan-cluster level to reveal the spatial heterogeneity of CESCN characteristics and drivers, thereby supporting more targeted regional collaborative emission-reduction policies.
