Barriers and Facilitators to Providing Women Postpartum Care by Health Care Providers in the First 12 Months After Childbirth: A Systematic Qualitative Meta‐Aggregation Review
Authors: Maryam Rouhi, Silvana Bettiol, Sarah Prior, Narges Rouhi, Theodora Dedo Azu, Christine Stirling, Lynne Staff, Linda Jones, Antigoni Leftherioti, Patricia Nicholson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPostpartum care is crucial for maternal and infant health but remains inconsistently administered. This systematic qualitative review aimed to synthesize health care providers’ perspectives by exploring the barriers and facilitators experienced by health care providers in administering optimal maternal care during the first 12 months after childbirth.MethodsA systematic qualitative meta‐aggregation review was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. Peer‐reviewed qualitative studies from January 2010 to April 2024 were retrieved from MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science. Findings were mapped onto the Behavioral Model of Health Services Use.ResultsTwenty‐three studies were included. Four key themes emerged: (1) professional barriers associated with administering quality health care, (2) organizational and systemic challenges in postpartum care (structural, policy, and resources barriers), (3) facility levels of services and their influence on decision‐making, and (4) client's factors impacting their postpartum. Facilitators included interprofessional teamwork, training, and continuity of care. Key facilitators that supported health care providers in administering postpartum maternal care included interprofessional teamwork, targeted training, and continuity of care.DiscussionPostpartum care continues to be fragmented and underprioritized at individual, organizational, and systemic levels, limiting the quality and continuity of maternal care. Addressing these challenges requires strengthened provider training, improved referral systems, culturally sensitive care, and integrated, team‐based approaches. Policy reforms that prioritize postpartum care as a core component of the maternal health care continuum are essential to improving maternal outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The postpartum period is a critical time for family health, emphasizing the need for effective care to support the physical and mental well‐being of individuals who are childbearing, newborns, and families. 1 Maternal well‐being following childbirth plays a critical role in shaping the health outcomes of both women and their families over time. 2 Poor practices in postpartum care can impair physical and emotional recovery, leading to lasting health effects. 3 Furthermore, inadequate postpartum care can result in issues such as postpartum depression (PPD), anxiety, chronic pain, and even long‐term complications, such as pelvic floor dysfunction or cardiovascular disorders. 2 To mitigate these risks, evidence‐based practices are essential to improve postpartum health care quality and reduce long‐term morbidity. 4 QUICK POINTS ✦Health care providers face professional, organizational, systemic, and client‐related barriers in administering postpartum care, including inadequate staffing, limited training, fragmented referral pathways, cultural barriers, and low maternal health literacy.✦Effective postpartum care is supported by interprofessional teamwork, provider training, continuity of care, family involvement, and culturally sensitive practices.✦Strengthening training, improving referral systems, promoting integrated, team‐based approaches, and prioritizing postpartum care in health care policy are essential to improve maternal outcomes.

Findings from a previously conducted systematic review identified a broad range of challenges and enablers that influenced women's engagement with health care professionals during the first year after childbirth. 5 Normalizing women's postpartum health problems was a key challenge resulting in women not seeking professional help. 5 , 6 Other researchers highlighted a significant gap in the provision of postpartum care due to a lack of knowledge among health care providers (HCPs) regarding postpartum maternal morbidities. 7 , 8 Several other factors prevented women from seeking necessary postpartum help. These included knowledge deficits, low health literacy, fear of judgment, reliance on informal support networks, limited access to professional care and reliance on familial advice, and cultural influences. 5 These challenges have compounded women's reported dissatisfaction with postpartum care compared with their more positive experiences during prenatal care. 9

Beyond women's experiences, HCPs also face multiple system‐level challenges in administering optimal postpartum care. Studies have reported that limited staffing, time constraints, fragmented care models, lack of continuity, and insufficient training in managing postpartum complications hinder the ability of HCPs to provide comprehensive follow‐up and support. 10 Moreover, institutional priorities often focus more on antenatal and intrapartum care, leaving the postpartum period underresourced and undervalued within health care systems. 11 Understanding these barriers from the providers’ perspective is crucial to designing effective interventions and improving service provision.

METHODS

A systematic qualitative meta‐aggregation review was conducted, following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) process. 12 Grounded in pragmatism, meta‐aggregation distills findings from qualitative studies into practical “lines of action” that can inform policy and practice. 13 This approach systematically synthesizes evidence into actionable, evidence‐based statements while preserving participants’ perspectives. To ensure a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthesis, the review included an extensive search of relevant studies, minimizing bias and directly addressing the research question. The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD42021266376).

Conceptual Framework

The Behavioral Model of Health Services Use (BMHSU) was used as a framework for this study. 14 The BMHSU (Figure 1) proposes that health outcomes are influenced by a combination of contextual and individual characteristics and health behaviors. For this review, contextual and individual characteristics were most relevant and further categorized into predisposing variables: enabling and need‐related variables. 14 Contextual characteristics include family, society, and the health care system, whereas individual characteristics refer to personal beliefs about health care services, education level, and demographics such as age. 15 Health behaviors, shaped by personal practices and the process of medical care, directly influence the use of personal health care services. 14

The Behavioral Model of Health Services Use Including Contextual and Individual Characteristics

Findings were deductively organized according to the BMHSU domains—predisposing, enabling, and need‐related factors 14 —providing a structured framework to interpret evidence and clarify how individual, organizational, and client‐level factors may interact to influence postpartum care.

Search Strategy

This review included English‐language qualitative studies published between January 2010 and April 2024, sourced from MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science. Qualitative methods capture the depth and context of HCP experiences, which quantitative approaches cannot. Including a broad publication range allowed identification of historical and contemporary trends, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of postpartum care provision by HCPs. The search strategy also intentionally included studies from diverse countries in order to capture a wide range of HCP experiences, organizational contexts, and cultural influences. Geographic diversity enhances transferability, identifies common and context‐specific barriers and facilitators, and strengthens the robustness and applicability of the findings for informing policy and practice.

The inclusion criteria were formulated according to Participants, Phenomenon of Interest, Context, and Outcome format adapted for qualitative research. 16 For this review “Participant” was defined as all HCPs providing postpartum care, including general practitioners, midwives, maternal and child health nurses, obstetricians, and gynecologists in both private and public practice. “Interest” encompassed any factors hindering or influencing health care administration of PPC. “Context” included all international settings where postpartum care is provided. The primary “Outcome” was identification of barriers and facilitators to the provision of postpartum care (Supporting Information, Table S1).

Eligible studies containing qualitative data from any methodology or research design, including mixed‐methods studies with qualitative data as the primary data source, were considered for inclusion. All studies reporting quantitative data, including mixed‐methods studies with quantitative data as the primary data source, grey literature, abstracts, reviews, editorials, commentaries, and conference procedures, were excluded.

Data Extraction and Quality Appraisal

Articles were selected based on rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. The following data were extracted: author(s), publication date, country, aims, methodology, method, phenomenon of interest, setting, participants, population ethnicity, reporting of morbidity, data analysis, and findings (Table 1).

All articles retrieved from the databases were imported into Covidence 38 with duplicates removed prior to selection of studies (Figure 2). Screening was conducted in 2 stages: first by title and abstract, followed by a full‐text review. Two reviewers (M.R., T.D.A.) independently assessed the full text of eligible articles using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. 39 This checklist consists of 10 questions organized into 3 main sections: filtering, technical appraisal, and theoretical appraisal. Studies were considered for inclusion if both reviewers answered “yes” to at least 7 of the 10 questions. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus after a thorough review of the checklist criteria.

Flow Diagram of Trial

In addition to the critical appraisal, qualitative findings relevant to postpartum care provision were independently extracted by the same 2 reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted data included participant quotations and interpreted themes directly related to barriers and facilitators. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus, and all extracted data were cross‐checked to ensure accuracy and consistency prior to synthesis. Critical appraisal tools were applied to assess the trustworthiness, relevance, and rigor of included studies. The credibility of qualitative research was emphasized, and high‐quality articles without apparent bias were prioritized. 40 Multiple interpretations of qualitative findings were considered to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the aims of the included studies.

Data Synthesis

Meta‐aggregation was used to synthesize data and generate a set of statements that represent the findings. It provides a structured, transparent, and practical method to synthesize qualitative evidence across studies, turning diverse findings into clear, actionable insights that can directly address the research question about HCP experiences and system‐level factors. These were then categorized based on similarity in meaning. These categories were synthesized to produce a single comprehensive set of findings that can be used to inform decision‐making and evidence‐based practice.

RESULTS

The initial search strategy yielded 972 articles, with 23 articles included in the review (Figure 2). Barriers and facilitators to HCPs’ provision of postpartum care were collated and interpreted using the JBI Qualitative Assessment and Review Instrument (JBI‐QARI) within the BMHSU framework. 14 This framework categorizes factors influencing postpartum care into 3 domains: (1) predisposing factors, encompassing institutional conditions that may encourage or limit HCPs’ service provision regardless of women's use; (2) enabling factors, including conditions that support or hinder HCPs in providing timely and effective care; and (3) need‐related factors, representing recognized health needs or conditions identified by HCPs and women.

Twelve categories of barriers and facilitators were extracted from the included studies and aggregated into 4 synthesis statements (Table 2). The importance of each category was determined based on the number of supporting studies and the methodological rigor assessed via JBI‐QARI, with categories supported by greater evidence prioritized in the synthesis. This approach provides a transparent and structured interpretation of key contextual factors influencing access to postpartum maternal care and highlights the central role of HCPs in ensuring care accessibility and quality.

Synthesis Statement 1. Professional Barriers Associated With Providing Quality Health Care

Category 1: Inadequate Staffing Levels and Support Resources

Inadequate staffing and resourcing were frequently cited as a major obstacle to providing comprehensive postpartum care for women in 11 out of 23 studies. 18 , 20 , 24 , 28 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 36 , 42 For example, in Pallangyo et al, 28 one HCP stated, “We will not be enough to provide proper PPC. We are already overwhelmed so we suggest also for the increased number of HCP and especially specifically assigned to the role.” 28

Additionally, a shortage of specialist doctors was reported as a barrier to providing postpartum care: “There are many challenges that are preventing us [healthcare providers] from providing basic and essential health services to pregnant women. We don't have adequate female doctors and midwives, drugs.” 32

Living in rural areas and staff shortage were also reported as barriers to the provision of postpartum care and supported in one study: “Recruitment is usually an ongoing issue due to the hospital being in a rural area.” 34

Two studies reported the lack of diagnostic facilities as a barrier to providing postpartum care. 33 , 34 For example, “There is lack of investigation services [diagnostic] on twenty‐four‐hour basis.” 33

A lack of financial support for education and limited access to professional development opportunities due to various factors, such as poor internet connectivity in low‐resource settings, were reported as barriers to professional growth and career advancement: “After completion of course our skills improve but financial raise [funding?] should be there it will sustain our motivation.” 33

HCPs reported that excessive clerical tasks, such as paperwork, overwhelmed them and reduced time for direct woman care. 19

The results from Alnuaimi et al 31 and Pope et al 36 highlighted key challenges in maternal mental health care, including insufficient organizational investment and resources, such as inadequate referral pathways, long wait lists, and insufficient public services tailored to women’ needs, leaving them without prompt access to adequate care.

Category 2: Limited Knowledge and Skills for Providing Mental Health Support

Eight studies reported that HCPs lacked adequate knowledge regarding mental health disorders and treatments. Here, one provider admitted, “Here in our community, we don't have any cases of postpartum depression. I mean, either we don't have any or we don't know how to observe [diagnose it].” 25

Mental health disorders were often misunderstood or considered normal, delaying diagnosis and care. 36 Reliance on laboratory tests rather than clinical judgment was common: “HCPs relying on laboratory results to assess and confirm the presence of PPD.” 37

One study highlighted that breastfeeding support often overshadowed mental health care: “The biggest, biggest, biggest thing is always the breastfeeding.” 20

Eight studies highlighted a lack of knowledge regarding mental health disorders and effective support and treatment options among HCPs. 17 , 18 , 24 , 25 , 29 , 34 , 36 , 37

Category 3: Compromised Care Because of Time Constraints

Short consultation times were a widespread problem. HCPs described their workload and limited time per patient. 21 , 22 , 29 , 31 , 35 , 36 , 37 One participant mentioned: While we would love to handle every patient well, this is not feasible because of time constraints. Here we see very many mothers and when you think of giving each one enough time to talk, the numbers pile up. 37

While we would love to handle every patient well, this is not feasible because of time constraints. Here we see very many mothers and when you think of giving each one enough time to talk, the numbers pile up. 37

Fragmented, limited, and hard‐to‐access postpartum support services led to unmet needs and feelings of isolation among women, with some HCPs explicitly admitting they “pass[ed] the buck” rather than taking ownership of care coordination. 36

Time pressures also affected the ability of HCPs to address complex postpartum issues such as perineal tears. 21

Category 4: Gender‐Specific Challenges

Gender preferences influenced postpartum consultations, with women favoring female HCPs for intimate examinations. One participant noted, “Some of them, they prefer a female doctor instead of a male doctor, especially if it involves a lot of checking (of) the breast and also the private part(s).” 35

Synthesis Statement 2. Organizational and Systemic Challenges in Postpartum Care: Structural, Policy, and Resources Barriers

Significant organizational and systemic challenges affecting the accessibility and quality of postpartum care were identified and supported by 22 findings. These challenges were grouped into 4 key categories: unclear referral pathways, high operational costs, social and cultural barriers, and issues within the health care professional‐patient relationship.

Category 5: Lack of Clear Referral Pathways

Seven studies highlighted a persistent gap in health care systems regarding the availability and clear referral pathways, particularly for mental health and psychosocial support. 21 , 22 , 29 , 31 , 35 , 36 , 37 HCPs often identified women's needs but lacked clear and accessible pathway for timely referrals: “[W]e're telling these patients all the time… ‘talk to your GP,’… If they have taken those steps, …we have to have… people to refer them on to.” 36

HCPs reported that women felt “lost in the system” because of inadequate referrals, leading to sentiments of being a “cog in a wheel” or HCPs “passing the buck.” Consequently, delaying referrals was viewed as a means to protect women from complex care systems and foster trusting relationships. 36

Category 6: Operational Costs of Services

High out‐of‐pocket expenses associated with health care services in many facilities deterred women from attending follow‐up visits or seeking support 10 , 17 , 32 : “The cost of some health care services in the facilities prevent mothers from visiting the reproductive healthcare centres frequently.” 32

Category 7: Social and Cultural Barriers

Social and cultural factors further limited access to postpartum care. Language barriers and low socioeconomic status hindered effective communication between women and HCPs, especially when English was a second language and interpreter services were unavailable. 36 Cultural values, taboos, and the fear of judgment often prevented women from discussing postpartum issues, such as breastfeeding, hygiene, or sexual health 17 , 31 , 41 : Sometimes our culture and norms inhibit the communication between our clients and us as they considered opening this issue is a shame and forbidden…so I think if we start the chat, women will accept the discussion and be encouraged to ask questions. 31

Feelings of shame and distrust contributed to social isolation, depriving women of needed postpartum support. 10 , 19 , 36 To address this, some HCPs emphasized strategies such “listening,” “clarifying expectations,” “providing relevant information,” and “facilitating a restful environment” to support the women's emotional and practical needs. 19 One provider emphasized, “The biggest skill that I feel I bring to a mother as…a health care professional, is my ability to listen, and to acknowledge her feelings.” 36

Category 8: Health Care Professional‐Patient Dyad

The quality of the health care professional‐patient relationship played a crucial role in shaping postpartum care experiences, particularly in relation to continuity of care. One HCP noted, “Patients who have been [seen] by the same provider and they've been lucky enough to get that continuity… that's an increased likelihood that they're going to return.” 10

Strong, continuous relationships between providers and postpartum women encouraged active participation in care and improved outcomes. 36 Another HCP stated, “Patients who have been [seen] by the same provider and they've been lucky enough to get that continuity …, that's an increased likelihood that they're going to return.” 10

Synthesis Statement 3: Facility Levels and Their Influence on Decision‐Making Processes

Category 9: Limited Training Opportunities and Support for HCPs

Midwives and other HCPs faced substantial challenges in providing comprehensive postpartum care, especially for PPD and other mental health issues, because of insufficient formal education and training. Many providers lack the necessary skills to effectively use postpartum mental health screening tools, limiting early identification and intervention. 36 The role of hospital management is crucial in prioritizing postnatal care, but, without robust institutional backing, providers struggled to implement best practices when caring for women who were postpartum. 24 , 28

Although breastfeeding education remained a key priority, it was often overshadowed by other important aspects of postnatal care, including mental health and relationship support 17 , 20 : [Y]ou'd be learning about…postpartum depression and puerperal psychosis and things that are as rare as hen's teeth…[but] when you come out into the real world, you realise: …that isn't the reality of most of the problems that people face…it's all the normal stuff.

Category 10: Care Provision Model Incorporating Family Participation

Family involvement in postpartum care was recognized as beneficial. Providing written educational materials to women and their families supports learning and empowers them to manage care at home. 31 These resources can be crucial in reinforcing key information about infant and maternal health. One provider explained, “I think offering written materials about sexuality would be a good idea, then they can discuss the issue with their husbands at home.” 31 Inclusive postpartum education sessions helped equip family members to support both woman and infant, enhancing continuity of care. 27

Synthesis Statement 4: Client Factors and the Impact on Postpartum Care

Category 11: Health Literacy Gap and Service Access Barriers

Low health literacy among women who are postpartum, defined as limited capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health care information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions, significantly hindered HCPs’ capacity to follow up on postpartum complications. 10 Women with low health literacy were less likely to access antenatal care, which increased the likelihood of home births without prior medical booking. Births occurring at home, where women had limited or no engagement with formal prenatal services, left them particularly vulnerable to poor postnatal outcomes. 22 , 26 Improving postpartum care accessibility and acceptability therefore requires raising awareness about available services, fostering flexibility in service provision, and promoting shared understanding between women and providers. 41 Family support was also found to be limited, especially for women experiencing mental health issues, reflecting a broader health literacy gap within families, wherein family members’ limited understanding of health needs restricted their capacity to support women who were postpartum effectively. One participant noted: Just even knowing that the services exist, or you know, the way in which mental health is spoken about within the home, or is recognized in itself, probably places a barrier, in terms of those women accessing support, knowing when they need to access support, and knowing when things are becoming, you know, unsafe, or becoming a problem. 41

DISCUSSION

The study highlights critical barriers and facilitators that HCPs encounter when providing postpartum care, offering valuable insights into areas for improvement and intervention that can be further understood through the BMHSU domains of enabling, predisposing, and need‐related factors. These results demonstrate that some challenges are rooted in systemic issues, whereas others reflect professional limitations and societal biases, emphasizing when targeted support could improve care provision.

Enabling Factors

One of the most prominent barriers identified was inadequate staffing and resources, an enabling factor within the BMHSU. HCPs frequently reported feeling overwhelmed by patient volume and role demands, which constrained their capacity to provide comprehensive, individualized care during the postpartum period. Although underresourcing in health care is widely recognized, 43 insufficient allocation of resources—specifically in postpartum care—critically affects both the quality and timeliness of interventions provided to women.

Time constraints also emerged as a recurring challenge. Increasing patient loads and limited consultation times prevented many HCPs from engaging meaningfully with patients, thereby affecting the quality of care. 44 , 45 , 46 This was particularly concerning in the postpartum period, when women required additional attention to address their emotional, physical, and practical recovery concerns.

Predisposing Factors

Another significant barrier was the lack of specialized knowledge and skills in managing specific postpartum conditions, particularly mental health issues such as PPD. 47 This gap highlights the need for targeted education and professional development to ensure HCPs are equipped to address the complex needs of women who are postpartum. Samuel et al 46 also noted that factors such as cost, time, and perceived relevance influenced HCPs’ capacity and capability to undertake further training.

A more nuanced predisposing factor was gender‐related preferences. Many women requested female HCPs for intimate consultations related to breastfeeding, vaginal health, and mental health. When these preferences could not be met because of staffing constraints, it affected patient comfort and engagement in care. Although not addressed in the included studies, similar challenges may also arise for transgender and gender‐diverse individuals seeking postpartum care, wherein provider gender, inclusivity, and culturally safe communication may influence care engagement and experiences.

Despite these challenges, there were several facilitators that HCPs identified as contributing to more effective postpartum care. Strong teamwork among HCPs was frequently cited as a key factor in overcoming barriers to care. This is consistent with Selix et al, 48 who suggested that the complexity of maternal care requires good teamwork and effective communication to formulate safe care plans. Collaborative efforts between obstetrician‐gynecologists, midwives, mental health professionals, and general practitioners allowed for more holistic care plans, ensuring that both the physical and emotional needs of women were addressed. 49

Need‐Related Factors

The need‐related domain represents the actual or perceived health needs that prompt postpartum care. HCPs reported that women often present with complex physical, emotional, and psychosocial needs, such as PPD, anxiety, perineal trauma, breastfeeding challenges, or complications after home births. 5 When these needs were unrecognized or delayed, women experienced gaps in care and delayed interventions. 21 , 25 , 27 , 37 Additionally, cultural practices, including reliance on traditional healers, sometimes led to inappropriate or delayed care, highlighting the importance of timely assessment and culturally sensitive engagement by HCPs to ensure women's needs are addressed.

Organizational and Systemic Barriers

Organizational and systemic challenges significantly influenced care quality and decision‐making in the current study. These included limited resources, gaps in policies addressing the unique needs of women who are postpartum, and a lack of coordinated referral pathways. Such barriers are common across health care services, with ongoing efforts to mitigate risks and improve patient outcomes. 50 Our findings suggest that these barriers may contribute to poorer outcomes for women, particularly those from culturally diverse backgrounds, with low health literacy, or living in rural and remote areas.

Fragmented care, delays, and missed follow‐up appointments often resulted from poorly defined referral pathways, disproportionately affecting women with complex health needs. 51 Operational cost constraints further limit access to essential services, prioritizing acute care over preventive postpartum support such as home visits and specialist services. 52 Social and cultural barriers, including stigma, language difficulties, and distrust in health care systems, exacerbate disparities in care. 53

Finally, the health care professional‐patient dyad remains central to effective care but is often strained by time pressures, insufficient systemic support, and lack of service integration. 45 Expecting individual providers to meet the multidimensional needs of women who are postpartum without multidisciplinary or coordinated support places an unrealistic burden on HCPs and highlights the need for systemic interventions.

Themes of professional challenges, organizational and systemic issues, and client‐level barriers exist across all settings; however, the intensity and nature differ. Higher‐resourced countries report more professional and continuity‐related challenges, whereas lower‐resourced countries are dominated by structural, financial, cultural, and health literacy barriers. 54 Thus, policy priorities and implementation strategies need to be tailored to the specific health care system context, ensuring that interventions address the dominant barriers within each setting.

Implications for Policy and Practice

To strengthen postpartum maternal care, policy and service reforms are required that position the postpartum period as a core component of the maternity care continuum rather than an optional extension of care. National and institutional policies should establish clear, standardized frameworks that define the roles and responsibilities of HCPs—including midwives, general practitioners, nurses, and obstetrician‐gynecologists—in providing ongoing maternal follow‐up during the first 12 months after childbirth. Such frameworks should support coordinated, multidisciplinary care and promote continuity across maternity and primary health care services.

Health systems should also invest in structured postpartum care pathways that include routine follow‐up, early identification of physical and mental health complications, and timely referral mechanisms. Strengthening access to mental health resources, particularly screening and management of postpartum mental health conditions, is essential to ensure that HCPs can respond effectively to women's needs during this period.

In addition, workforce capacity must be supported through targeted training and professional development programs that equip HCPs with the knowledge and skills required to identify and manage postpartum morbidities. Addressing systemic barriers, such as staff shortages, limited consultation time, and unclear referral pathways, will be critical to enabling providers to provide consistent, evidence‐based postpartum care.

Finally, strengthening institutional support and policy accountability mechanisms can help reduce variability in postpartum service provision and improve the accessibility, quality, and continuity of maternal care across diverse health care settings and populations.

Strengths and Limitations

This review has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first systematic qualitative meta‐aggregation examining the barriers and facilitators experienced by HCPs providing maternal care during the first 12 months postpartum. The use of meta‐aggregation, aligned with the JBI methodology, enabled the synthesis of high‐quality qualitative evidence while preserving the original meaning of participants’ experiences. In addition, a comprehensive and transparent search strategy across multiple databases ensured broad coverage of the literature. The application of standardized critical appraisal tools further enhanced the methodological rigor and credibility of the review. Importantly, the synthesis generated practice‐oriented insights by identifying actionable themes that can inform policy development, workforce support, and health care system improvements aimed at strengthening postpartum care.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, restricting the review to English‐language studies may have excluded relevant evidence and introduced language bias. Second, the inclusion of only peer‐reviewed literature may have resulted in the omission of valuable grey literature, such as government reports, policy documents, and conference proceedings, which could provide important contextual insights. Third, heterogeneity across study designs, participant populations, and health care settings posed challenges for synthesis and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, many included studies relied on self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially affecting the validity and reliability of the findings.

CONCLUSION

This systematic qualitative meta‐aggregation review highlights the complex interplay of barriers and facilitators influencing HCPs’ ability to provide maternal care during the first 12 months after childbirth. Organizational, systemic, and relational factors significantly shape care experiences and outcomes in the postpartum period. Addressing these challenges through clearer referral pathways, culturally responsive practices, adequate resourcing, and stronger structural support for HCPs is, therefore, essential for improving postpartum care. By synthesizing HCPs’ experiences across multiple studies, this review offers actionable insights for policy, workforce planning, and health care system redesign. Grounded in a pragmatic approach, qualitative meta‐aggregation preserves the context of individual studies while generating practical, evidence‐based recommendations. Therefore, this systematic review aims to identify the barriers and facilitators experienced by HCPs in providing optimal maternal care during the first 12 months after childbirth. Ultimately, the findings of this review provide a roadmap for targeted interventions that can enhance the quality, accessibility, and effectiveness of postpartum maternal care.
