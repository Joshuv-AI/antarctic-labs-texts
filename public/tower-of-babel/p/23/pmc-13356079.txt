Tacrolimus has better performance in B cell reconstitution after allo-HSCT compared to cyclosporine A: a real-world study
Authors: Hao Zhou, Guangyu Zhou, Wenqiong Xiang, Guilin Ren, Xi Dou, Jianbin Chen, Xiaoqiong Tang, Hongbin Zhang, Xin Wang, Xiaohua Luo, Lin Liu, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractB-cell reconstitution is closely associated with prognosis, infection risk, and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). However, the impact of different calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) on B-cell recovery remains unclear. We retrospectively analyzed 312 allo-HSCT recipients receiving cyclosporine (CsA, n = 211) or tacrolimus (FK506, n = 101). B-cell proportions in bone marrow and peripheral lymphocyte subsets were compared, and propensity score matching was applied to balance confounders. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that FK506 was independently associated with superior B-cell reconstitution at 3 months (OR 2.93, 95% CI 1.49–5.76, p = 0.010) and 4 months post-transplant (OR 3.53, 95% CI 1.11–11.15, p = 0.024). Higher B-cell proportions at 3–5 months were associated with a significantly lower risk of viral infection. Mediation analysis further revealed that improved B-cell reconstitution at day + 90 partially mediated the protective effect of FK506 against viral infection. These findings suggest that FK506 promotes improved B-cell recovery and may reduce viral infection risk after allo-HSCT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02176-6.Keywords: Immune reconstitution, B cells, Allo-HSCT, Calcineurin inhibitor, Glucocorticoid

Introduction

Immune reconstitution is closely associated with various transplant-related complications following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), including infections, GVHD, and relapse. This complex, dynamic process is influenced by multiple factors [1, 2]. Existing research primarily focuses on T-cell and NK-cell reconstitution, while studies on B-cell reconstitution remain limited due to its comparatively longer recovery period. Our previous study found that the recovery of B cells strongly correlated with the prognosis of the patients who underwent allo-HSCT. Additionally, other studies have highlighted the relationship between B cell reconstitution and the incidence of infections as well as secondary autoimmune diseases. These findings collectively revealed the critical significance of B cell reconstitution for the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing allo-HSCT [3–5]. Therefore, further research is needed to fully understand the dynamic of B-cell reconstitution after transplantation and its influencing factors.

Calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), including Cyclosporine A (CsA) and Tacrolimus (FK506), are widely used immunosuppressive agents in allo-HSCT, primarily preventing graft rejection by inhibiting T-cell activation [6, 7]. While CsA and FK506 are considered interchangeable in many clinical contexts, they still have some differences in their mechanisms of immune suppression [8, 9]. Our previous research suggested that the use of different CNIs may impact B cell reconstitution; however, further investigation has not been conducted [5]. Given the widespread clinical application of CsA and FK506, clarifying their impact on B-cell reconstitution may offer valuable theoretical foundations for the precise use of CNIs in clinical practice.

In the study, we retrospectively evaluated the dynamic reconstitution of bone marrow B cells at different time points in patients receiving CsA and FK506 for GVHD prophylaxis. Additionally, peripheral blood B-cell, NK-cell, and T-cell counts at each time point were compared between two groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to minimize the impact of confounding factors across the two groups. Furthermore, a mediation analysis was performed to explore potential interactions between FK506, B cell reconstitution, and viral infection. Our findings assist clinicians in selecting the appropriate type of CNI to achieve better B cell reconstitution and prognosis in patients undergoing allo-HSCT.

Methods

Patients

A total of 312 patients were included in this retrospective cohort study. All patients were from the Department of Hematology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and underwent allo-HSCT between January 2016 and June 2023. Two peripheral blood samples were obtained from two patients who underwent allo-HSCT at the Department of Hematology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University under the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was signed by all patients.

Survival

Patient survival data were retrospectively collected from the Electronic Medical Record system of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University or through telephone follow-ups. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from transplantation to death or last follow-up, while relapse-free survival (RFS) was defined as the period from transplantation to disease relapse.

Conditioning regimen

Conditioning regimen was categorized into myeloablative conditioning (MAC) and reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC). MAC was defined as using adequate dose of busulfan/cyclophosphamide-based regime to achieve completely myeloablative effect. RIC was defined as the substitution of cyclophosphamide with fludarabine or cladribine, thereby decreasing cyclophosphamide-associated toxicity while maintaining the minimum immunosuppression level necessary for HSCT. All preconditioning regimens were guided by the Consensus of Chinese Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation Experts (Table S1) [10].

GVHD prophylaxis

All cases of acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD) and chronic GVHD (cGVHD) were diagnosed and graded by specialized physicians according to the NCCN Guidelines for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation [11]. All patients underwent GVHD prophylaxis both during and post-transplantation. Prophylaxis regimens included either tacrolimus (FK506, administered at 0.05 mg/kg twice daily intravenously, adjusted to maintain a trough level of 5–15 ng/mL) combined with mycophenolate mofetil (0.5–1.0 g daily for 30 days) and methotrexate (MTX, 15 mg/m² intravenously on day + 1, followed by 10 mg/m² on days + 3, +6, and + 11), or cyclosporine (CsA, administered at 3.0 mg/kg/day intravenously from day − 1, adjusted to maintain a trough level of 200–300 ng/mL) combined with MMF and MTX [12–14]. Certain patients additionally received prophylactic anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG; Sanofi) administered intravenously over three days [15].

Infections

Viral reactivation was defined as EBV-DNA, CMV-DNA, HSV-DNA content in plasma higher than 50 copies/mL. Bacterial infection was defined as a positive peripheral blood culture, positive next-generation sequencing (NGS) results, or other reliable diagnostic findings during the transplantation period, with the diagnosis made by specialized physicians following evaluation. The diagnosis of fungal infection was carried out in accordance with the guidelines from the Chinese Invasive Fungal Infection Working Group, Society of Hematology, the Diagnostic Criteria and Treatment Principles of Invasive Fungal Disease in Patients with Hematological Diseases/Malignant Tumors (fifth and sixth revisions), the sixth European Conference on Infections in Leukemia, and the NCCN Guidelines for hematopoietic cell transplantation [16, 17].

Glucocorticoid

Glucocorticoids (0.8 mg/kg/day-2 mg/kg/day of methylprednisolone intravenously and orally) are administered guide by NCCN Guidelines for hematopoietic cell transplantation [11]. The types of glucocorticoids used include methylprednisolone, prednisone, dexamethasone, and hydrocortisone. The total glucocorticoid dose was defined as the sum of all types of glucocorticoids used by the patient from day 0 to day + 90 post-transplant, converted to an equivalent dose of methylprednisolone [18, 19]. The cut-off value for methylprednisolone was defined as a cumulative dose of 7 mg/kg, corresponding to administration at 1 mg/kg/day for 7 days. This dosage represents the standard initial steroid regimen commonly used for the treatment of aGVHD in our center. The duration of glucocorticoid therapy is defined as the total number of days commencing from day 0 and extending to the cessation of any form of glucocorticoid administration.

Flow cytometric analysis

Bone marrow and peripheral blood samples from patients were collected during routine monthly follow-ups and were subjected to flow cytometry to determine the status of relapse or sustained remission, continuing until 24 months post-transplant.

Bone marrow samples were subjected to staining with CD3 FITC/CD16 + 56 PE/CD45 PerCP CY5.5/CD4 PC7/CD19 APC/CD8 APC-CY7 antibodies, and the resulting data were analyzed using a decolor flow cytometer (Beckman NAVIOS). Peripheral blood samples from recipients were analyzed using flow cytometers (BD Canto II and Beckman NAVIOS) and were stained with IgD FITC/CD27 PE/CD14 ECD/CD3 PC5.5/CD19 PC7/CD24 APC/CD20 A750/CD38 V450/CD45KO. The resulting data were subsequently analyzed using Kaluza software. All analyses were conducted at the Center for Clinical Molecular Medical Detection and Department of Laboratory Medicine of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

The percentage of CD19 + B cells was determined by gating on the lymphocyte population based on forward and side scatter characteristics, followed by selecting the CD19 + cell population. The proportion of CD19 + B cells was calculated as the ratio of the number of CD19 + cells to the total number of lymphocytes. Total lymphocyte count was calculated based on forward and side scatter gating. The absolute counts of CD19 + B cells were calculated by multiplying the percentage of CD19 + B cells by the total lymphocyte count. The proportion and absolute counts of CD16/56 + NK cells and CD3 + T cells were calculated in the same way. The lymphocyte count was measured per microliter (µL) of blood.

RNA preparation and B-cell receptor repertoire sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from whole blood using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd) RNA. Samples were analyzed by High-throughput sequencing of IGH using the Lc-Bio® B-cell receptor (BCR) profiling system at a deep level (Lc-Bio, Hangzhou, China). Briefly, a 5’ RACE unbiased amplification protocol was used. This protocol uses unique molecular barcodes (UMBs) introduced in the course of cDNA synthesis to control bottlenecks and to eliminate PCR and sequencing errors. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq®/NextSeq® system with PE150 mode (Illumina). One common adaptor with UMB was added on the 5’ of cDNA during the first-strand cDNA synthesize and one reverse primer corresponding to the constant (C) regions of each of the IGH were designed to facilitate PCR amplification of cDNA sequences in a less biased manner. The UMB attached to each raw sequence reads were applied to correct PCR and sequencing errors correction and PCR duplicates removing. Map V, D, J and C segments with NCBI and then extract CDR3 regions and assemble clonotype for all clones. The resulting nucleotide and amino acid sequences of CDR3 of IGH were determined and those with out-of-frame and stop codon sequences were removed from the identified IGH repertoire. We further defined the amounts of each IGH clonotype by adding numbers of IGH clones sharing the same nucleotide sequence of CDR3.

Statistical analysis

The differences of clinical characteristics between two groups were compared using chi-square tests. The Mann-Whitney U test or Wilcoxon test was used to evaluate significant differences in the distribution of continuous data. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationship between bone marrow B-cell frequencies and other continuous variables. The optimal cut-off values for B cell frequencies at different time points based on OS and RFS were defined by log-rank test, dividing B cells into high and low groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted to eliminate the baseline unbalancing using R package “MatchIt” [20].

The Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to demonstrate OS and RFS of the CsA and FK506 groups, with the log-rank test used to assess differences between the two groups. Survival analysis and visualization were performed using the R software packages “survival,” “survminer,” “ggpubr,” and “ggplot2.”

Mediation analysis was conducted to describe the causal relationships among FK506, B-cell reconstitution, and viral infection. The models were established by Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and Bayesian mediation analysis with the R packages “lavaan” and “brm,” and the results were visualized using the R package “DiagrammeR“ [21]. A Sankey diagram created at https://www.sankeymatic.com was employed to illustrate the relationships among the three variables [22]. All statistical analyses and visualization were conducted by R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 9.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 312 patients were enrolled in the study, with a median age of 36 years (Table 1). 76% of the patients were under 50 years of age. The most patients were diagnosed with leukemia, accounting for 87.5% of the cohort. Males made up 52.9% of the population, while females constituted 47.1%. Most of the patients received allo-HSCT at CR1/steady status (82.1%). Haploidentical donors served as the primary source (67.3%) of stem cells. All patients received CNIs for GVHD prophylaxis after allo-HSCT, with 67.6% treated with CsA and 32.4% with FK506, and 45.2% of patients received ATG. Infections following allo-HSCT were prevalent, with viral infections occurring in 56.7%, bacterial infections in 92%, and fungal infections in 45.2% of patients. Glucocorticoids were administered to 84.3% of patients for the prevention or treatment of GVHD.

Additionally, we compared the counts of B cells, NK cells and T cells in the peripheral blood of both groups. It was found that the FK506 group demonstrated superior B-cell reconstitution at 4-month and 12-month (Fig. 1F, G). Glucocorticoids affect B-cell reconstitution

We collected data on the total glucocorticoid dose used within the first three months post-HSCT for each patient, as well as the total duration of glucocorticoid therapy following transplantation. A total of 263 patients received glucocorticoids (Table S5). Of all patients, 181 patients were categorized into the high cumulative dose group, and 131 patients were categorized into the low cumulative dose of glucocorticoid group. Forty-seven patients received glucocorticoids for more than 90 days. Correlation analysis revealed that B-cell recovery was negatively correlated with the dose and duration of glucocorticoid use post-transplantation, with significant correlations observed only within the first six months after transplantation (Figure S4). A line graph illustrating the monthly cumulative glucocorticoid dosage (Fig. 2A) demonstrated that peak glucocorticoid usage was observed within the first three months post-HSCT. The boxplot showed that higher glucocorticoid dose was associated with poorer B-cell recovery at the 3-month and 4-month post-transplantation. (Fig. 2B). Further subgroup analysis revealed that patients using FK506 had higher B-cell proportions than those in the CsA group at day + 90 (Fig. 2C). However, this finding was observed only in the high cumulative glucocorticoid dose group, with no statistically significant difference in the low cumulative dose of glucocorticoid group (Fig. 2D).

Effect of CNIs on B-cell reconstitution after PSM

After grouping patients based on the selection of CNIs, significant differences were observed in various clinical characteristics between the two groups (Table S6), including the status at HSCT (p = 0.049), the use of ATG (p < 0.001), the dosage (p < 0.001), and the duration of glucocorticoid therapy (p = 0.002). To mitigate these confounding factors, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed, incorporating relevant covariates. A matched cohort comprising 101 patient pairs was established (Table S7). Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) revealed that most of the differences were attenuated. However, a residual imbalance in ATG usage persisted after PSM (p = 0.003). A re-evaluation of B-cell frequencies within the matched cohort confirmed that the FK506 group exhibited elevated B-cell frequencies compared to patients with CsA at days + 90 and + 120 post-HSCT (Fig. 3). This finding was congruent with the pre-matching analysis.

B-cell reconstitution is correlated with the viral infections

We compared the viral infection status in patients grouping by B-cell reconstitution status (Table 3.). Patients with higher levels of B-cell percentage at 3-month, (51.8% vs. 69.9%, p = 0.034), 4-month (45.5% vs. 72.4% p = 0.018) and 5-month (51.7% vs. 73.2, p = 0.035) post-HSCT were less likely to experience viral infections. Further subgroup analysis was conducted to clarify the relationship between specific viral types and B-cell reconstitution (Table S8). Similarly, patients with better B-cell reconstitutions at 3-month showed a lower probability of CMV infection (35.3% vs. 53.6% p = 0.028), while those with better B-cell reconstitution at 18-month had a reduced probability of EBV (21.1% vs. 56.3% p = 0.039). However, no difference was observed between the groups regarding HSV infection.

Impact of CNIs on early B-cell reconstitution and viral infections after allo-HSCT

We found that the selection of CNIs was closely related to early B-cell reconstitution. Furthermore, early B-cell reconstitution was shown to influence the probability of viral infection/reactivation in patients. These results suggest a potential relationship among these three variables. To elucidate the connections between them, we conducted a mediation analysis to assess the direct effect of CNI on viral infection and the indirect effect mediated by B-cell reconstitution. The model indicated a causal relationship among the three variables: compare to CsA, FK506 positively influenced B-cell proportions (95%CI [0.07, 0.32], p = 0.003) at 3-month, thereby reducing the probability of viral infection (95%CI [-0.34, -0.03], p = 0.017), while no direct effect of FK506 on viral infection (95%CI [-0.10, 0.19], p = 0.526) was observed (Fig. 4A). To validate these findings, we performed Bayesian mediation analysis, which confirmed our results (Figure S5), demonstrating consistency across both models. A Sankey diagram was utilized to visualize the relationships among the three variables (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

The immune reconstitution process of post-HSCT is closely associated with prognosis and infection risk. This process is dynamic, with B-cell recovery considered to be gradual, requiring approximately 1–2 years for function and counts to return to normal levels [23]. In our study, we retrospectively included 312 patients who underwent allo-HSCT to analyze the association between B-cell reconstitution and the choice of CNIs. Our findings indicate that patients receiving FK506 exhibit superior B-cell reconstitution post-transplant compared to those receiving CsA.

Cyclosporine A and FK506 are widely used immunosuppressants in patients undergoing allo-HSCT. In this study, we conducted a detailed analysis of the effects of CsA and FK506 on bone marrow B-cell proliferation for the first time. After adjusting for baseline differences through PSM, it was observed that B-cell reconstitution between days + 90 and + 120 post-transplant was more robust in the FK506 group compared to the CsA group. Multivariate regression analysis indicated that FK506 exerted a weaker suppressive effect on B cells at 3-month and 4-month post-transplant compared to CsA. This phenomenon may be attributed to the direct inhibitory effect of CsA on IL-6, which could suppress the Tfh-IL-6-B cell positive feedback loop and subsequently impair B-cell recovery [24, 25]. However, in comparing peripheral blood immune cells, there was no significant difference in B-cell counts between the two groups around day + 90 post-transplant, with a marked difference observed at 4-month after allo-HSCT. This observation may reflect the time required for B cells to mature within the bone marrow and subsequently migrate to the peripheral circulation [26–28]. Furthermore, the NK-cell counts in the FK506 group were significantly higher at 3-month, 4-month and 12-month post-HSCT in comparison to those in the CsA group. Through Gene Ontology enrichment analysis, Qin et al. identified that both FK506 and CsA downregulate genes associated with NK-cell activation, with CsA exhibiting a more substantial downregulation [29]. This could explain the superior NK-cell reconstitution observed in the FK506 group. Moreover, peripheral T-cell counts in patients receiving FK506 were significantly higher at day + 90 and day + 120 post-transplant compared to those in the CsA group. This finding appears contrary to the stronger inhibitory effect of FK506 on T cells. The discrepancy may be attributed to the greater proportion of ATG usage in the CsA cohort, which is likely to have contributed to the lower T-cell counts observed in the CsA group relative to the FK506 group [30].

This study also investigated the impact of glucocorticoids on B-cell reconstitution following allo-HSCT. Group comparisons and correlation analyses indicated that the use of glucocorticoid suppressed B-cell recovery, consistent with findings from previous research. This phenomenon may be attributed to the induction of apoptosis in early-stage bone marrow B cells by glucocorticoids [31, 32]. However, the suppression effect of glucocorticoid was primarily observed within the first 6 months post-transplant. One possible explanation is that most patients in this study received glucocorticoid treatment for less than 90 days, and the suppression of B cells ceased upon discontinuation of glucocorticoids, with no prolonged impact on B-cell recovery after treatment cessation [32, 33].

In addition, when considering glucocorticoid dosage, the relatively protective effect of FK506 on B cells compared to CsA was evident only in the higher-dose treatment group. Muhetaer et al. reported the combination of FK506 with glucocorticoids may enhance the glucocorticoid-mediated suppression of lymphocytes, while higher glucocorticoid doses can impair regulatory T (Treg) cells [34, 35]. Thus, when used with FK506, the glucocorticoids may enhance their suppression of Treg cells, consequently releasing the inhibition on B cells, resulting in significantly better B-cell reconstitution in FK506-treated patients under high-dose glucocorticoid therapy than in the CsA group. However, the immunomodulatory effects of glucocorticoids are complex and remain contentious, necessitating further investigation.

We observed that the occurrence of aGVHD does not correlate with B-cell reconstitution in the univariate and multivariate analyses, which seems to be not reasonable. Therefore, we explored the specific role of aGVHD in B-cell recovery. The findings show that patients developing aGVHD achieve superior B-cell reconstitution at 3-month post-transplantation compared to those without aGVHD (Figure S6A). This phenomenon likely originates from higher level of B-cell activating factor in patients with aGVHD compare to their counterparts [36]. Although aGVHD impairs B-cell neogenesis in the bone marrow, it simultaneously induces a transient but intense increase in the replication frequency of B cells, contributing to the enhanced B-cell reconstitution observed in the aGVHD group [37]. We further compared the differences in glucocorticoid application between the cohorts. The data revealed that patients in the aGVHD group receive greater cumulative doses and undergo longer treatment durations of glucocorticoids than the rest of the cohort (Figure S6B, C). This extensive glucocorticoid exposure explains the lack of association between aGVHD and B-cell reconstitution in our regression models, as the profound B-cell suppression caused by glucocorticoids overshadows the underlying effects of aGVHD. We then compared B-cell recovery between patients receiving FK506 and CsA strictly within the subgroup that never develops aGVHD. The results indicate that the FK506 group maintains superior B-cell reconstitution at three and four months post-transplantation (Figure S6D, E). This confirms that FK506 exerts an independent promoting effect on B-cell recovery.

In our study, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the CsA group received ATG for GVHD prophylaxis compared to the FK506 group. This difference may be attributed to the relatively weaker suppressive effect of CsA on T cells, potentially necessitating additional immunosuppression with ATG to prevent GVHD [38, 39]. Since the use of PSM did not eliminate the intergroup differences in ATG administration and given that ATG may impair B-cell reconstitution, we compare the B-cell frequencies among patients who did not receive ATG [40]. The result shows that the B-cell reconstitution remains superior in patients treated with FK506 compared to those treated with CsA (Figure S7).

A phase III study by Hiraoka et al. reported that FK506 has a stronger effect on GVHD prevention with lower incidence of grade II-IV aGVHD in HLA-matched transplant patients compared to CsA, with no significant difference observed in OS and RFS between the two treatments [41]. We compared the effects of CNIs on bone marrow B-cell reconstitution among HLA-matched patients (Figure S8). Similarly, B-cell reconstitution at + 90 and + 120 days was found to be superior in the FK506 group compared to the CsA group. Given that lower bone marrow B-cell levels have been associated with the occurrence of aGVHD, this may explain why FK506 provides superior prophylactic effects against aGVHD in HLA-matched patients compared to CsA [42].

Viral infection/reactivation is a common complication in patients undergoing allo-HSCT. Due to incomplete immune reconstitution and the use of immunosuppressants, patients are particularly susceptible to viruses within 100 days after transplantation, which may increase the risk of death and relapse [43, 44]. Thus, exploring strategies to reduce viral infection risk during allo-HSCT has been a significant focus of research. Our data suggested that patients with poorer B-cell reconstitutions at days + 90, +120, and + 150 post-transplant were more likely to experience viremia. Mediation analysis indicated that, compared to CsA, FK506 may facilitate better B-cell reconstitution, thereby reducing the risk of viral infections during the transplantation. Therefore, for patients at high risk of viral infections, selecting FK506 as an immunosuppressant may be a better choice [44–46].

The key findings of our study are as follows. First, our study was the first to comprehensively investigate the effects of FK506 and CsA on B-cell reconstitution after allo-HSCT. Our findings indicated that patients in the FK506 group showed superior B-cell recovery compared to the CsA group, both in bone marrow and peripheral blood, with this difference predominantly occurring between days + 90 and + 120 post-transplant. The first 100 days after allo-HSCT were considered critical, as immune cell reconstitution during this period was closely associated with the incidence of aGVHD, viral infections, and overall survival [47, 48]. Although B-cell reconstitution was incomplete within the first 100 days, it significantly impacted patient outcomes. Troullioud Lucas et al. suggested that poor B-cell reconstitution within the first 100 days could result in higher NRM and an increased likelihood of grade II-IV aGVHD, underscoring the importance of B-cell reconstitution within this period [49].

Second, we conducted a detailed analysis of post-HSCT glucocorticoid use and found a higher cumulative dose of glucocorticoids combined with CsA within the first 100 days post-transplant led to poorer B-cell reconstitution, whereas FK506 did not have the same effect. It is therefore recommended to reduce the use of glucocorticoids when using CsA as a GVHD prophylaxis regimen. We also conducted PSM to balance baseline differences between the FK506 and CsA groups, which further clarified the distinct effects of these CNIs on B-cell reconstitution.

Additionally, we observed a potential association between bone marrow B-cell recovery around day + 100 and viral infections. Through mediation analysis, we further explored the relationship between FK506, B-cell reconstitution, and viral infection, revealing that B cells played a mediating role in the impact of FK506 on post-transplant viral infection. Based on our findings, we suggested a more targeted selection of CNIs for GVHD prevention in clinical practice. Since the use of FK506 may reduce the risk of viral infection during transplantation, we recommend using FK506 for GVHD prevention in patients with viral infection risk factors, such as advanced age or stem cells from unrelated donors [44, 50]. Previous studies suggested that male recipients, older donors, extensive aGVHD and viral infection could delay or impair B-cell reconstitution [5, 23, 51]. For patients with these risk factors, selecting FK506 may promote better B-cell recovery.

Our study also has some limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study, the limited data size along with potential confounding factors may have compromised the generalizability of the results, and the limited sample size of BCR sequencing precludes any definitive statistical conclusions. Further well-designed, large-sample, prospective, and multi-center clinical studies are needed for clarification. Additionally, the extensive use of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) during the transplantation at our center interfered with the measurement of immunoglobulin, preventing further analysis of the impact of different CNIs on the level of antibody. This may require animal experiments for further investigation or the design of well-planned prospective trials.

In summary, our study is the first to comprehensively investigate the impact of different types of CNIs on B cell reconstitution after allo-HSCT. After conducting PSM to match patients based on clinical characteristics, our study revealed that FK506 has a more positive effect on B-cell reconstitution compared to CsA. Furthermore, our study explored the relationship between different CNIs, B-cell reconstitution, and viral infections, demonstrating that FK506 may reduce the risk of viral infections during the transplantation. Our findings provide new evidence to guide the precise selection of CNIs in clinical practice.
