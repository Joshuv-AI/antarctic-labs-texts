Examination of the Relationship Between Foot Care Behaviour, Self‐Efficacy, and Diabetes Self‐Care Among Individuals With and Without Diabetic Foot Wounds
Authors: Elif Boz, Eylem Topbaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to examine the relationship between foot care behaviours, self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care activities among individuals with and without diabetic foot wounds and to identify associated sociodemographic and disease‐related factors.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted with 120 patients presenting to the internal medicine, endocrinology, and diabetic foot outpatient clinics and wards of three different hospitals. Data were collected using a demographic and clinical characteristics form, the Foot Care Behaviour Scale, Diabetic Foot Care Self‐Efficacy Scale, and Diabetes Self‐Care Scale. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 22, with Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal‐Wallis H, independent samples t‐test, ANOVA and correlation analysis applied as appropriate.ResultsPatients with and without diabetic foot wounds did not differ significantly in foot care behaviours or diabetes self‐care scores (p > 0.05). However, diabetic foot care self‐efficacy scores were significantly higher among patients without diabetic foot wounds (p < 0.05). In patients with diabetic foot wounds, prior foot care education was associated with significantly higher diabetic foot care self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care scores (p < 0.05). Older age was negatively correlated with scale scores in both groups, whereas higher education level and certain preventive practices (e.g., foot self‐examination, checking footwear) were significantly associated with better scores. Strong positive correlations were identified among foot care behaviours, diabetic foot care self‐efficacy, and diabetes self‐care across all participants (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe strong positive associations among foot care behaviours, diabetic foot care self‐efficacy, and diabetes self‐care underscore the critical role of patient empowerment in disease management. The higher self‐efficacy scores in patients without diabetic foot and those who were previously educated on their prevention demonstrate the importance of early, targeted nursing interventions. Implementing structured foot care education prior to the onset of complications may strengthen patient self‐efficacy, improve self‐care adherence and ultimately prevent the development of diabetic foot wounds.Keywords: diabetic foot, Foot care behaviours, nursing, self‐care, self‐efficacy

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic disease associated with various complications that seriously impact life. One of the most serious complications is diabetic foot, defined as infection, ulceration, and/or destruction of tissues of the foot associated with neuropathy and/or peripheral arterial disease in individuals with DM [1, 2]. Diabetic foot commonly develops as a consequence of long‐standing hyperglycemia, which leads to peripheral neuropathy, vascular impairment and delayed wound healing. It can result in infection, ulceration, and ultimately, amputation of the affected tissues [3, 4]. The estimated lifetime risk of developing a diabetic foot wound among individuals with DM ranges from 19% to 34% [5]. Globally, the prevalence of diabetic foot wound is approximately 6.3%, with regional variations reported across North America (13.0%), Africa (7.2%), Asia (5.5%) and Europe (5.1%) [6]. National data from the UK indicate that over 120,000 cases were recorded in England and Wales between 2018 and 2023, underscoring the persistent burden of this complication [7].

Several factors increase the risk of developing diabetic foot wounds, including foot deformities; medical conditions such as peripheral neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease; behaviours such as smoking, poor personal hygiene and suboptimal glycemic control; and limited knowledge about foot care [8, 9]. Thus, the prevention of diabetic foot wounds depends on patient education, early detection of neuropathy, and effective blood glucose control, which are essential components of comprehensive diabetes management. These elements are most effectively implemented through a multidisciplinary team approach, with healthcare professionals maintaining primary responsibility for treatment decisions and professional care [10, 11, 12].

The management of diabetic foot primarily relies on healthcare professionals, including nurses, who assess lesions, control risk factors, provide appropriate treatment (e.g., debridement and dressings), and monitor for complications. However, patients are also encouraged to actively practice self‐care behaviours and collaborate with healthcare professionals throughout the treatment process [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Self‐efficacy is recognized as a key factor in chronic disease management, supporting individuals in maintaining acquired skills and managing diabetes‐related complications effectively [18, 19].

According to Orem's self‐care theory, self‐care involves the deliberate actions individuals undertake to maintain health and well‐being, while the capacity to perform these actions is referred to as self‐care agency [20, 21].

In the context of diabetes, self‐care encompasses adherence to prescribed medications, consistent blood glucose monitoring, dietary regulation, and engagement in physical activity. Effective diabetes management, particularly the prevention of complications such as diabetic foot disorders, depends on patients' proficiency in self‐care behaviours and their motivation to implement them consistently [22, 23]. Regular foot care is another key strategy for reducing the risk of diabetic foot ulceration. However, integrating such practices into daily routines can be challenging for many patients, underscoring the essential role of nursing support.

Several studies have examined the relationships between diabetes education, self‐efficacy and self‐care behaviours [24, 25]. Recent research has further explored determinants of foot care behaviours and their associations with health literacy, self‐efficacy and glycaemic control [26, 27, 28]. It is believed that individuals with high levels of self‐efficacy and good self‐care behaviours manage diabetes and its complications more effectively. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have specifically compared these relationships between patients with and without diabetic foot wounds. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationship between foot care behaviours, self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care activities among individuals with and without diabetic foot wounds and to explore sociodemographic and disease‐related factors associated with these outcomes.

Methods

Study Design, Population and Sample

This descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted to examine the associations between foot care behaviours, self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care activities among individuals with and without diabetic foot wounds. The sample consisted of 120 patients aged ≥ 18 years who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate. Participants were referred or admitted to the diabetic foot outpatient clinics and wards of the Department of Internal Medicine and the Department of Endocrinology of three public hospitals (Samsun Vezirköprü Public Hospital, Ondokuz Mayıs University Medical Faculty Hospital, and Çorum Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital) between December 24, 2020, and July 1, 2022. Convenience sampling was applied, and all eligible patients were approached consecutively. Patients were excluded if they tested positive for COVID‐19 at hospital admission or declined participation.

Data Collection Tools

Data were collected using a demographic and clinical characteristics form and validated Turkish versions of the Foot Care Behaviour Scale, Diabetic Foot Care Self‐Efficacy Scale and Diabetes Self‐Care Scale.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics Form

This form was developed by the researchers in accordance with the relevant literature [29, 30, 31]. It consisted of 26 items in total, including 10 questions addressing sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, education level, place of residence, financial status, smoking status and frequency and alcohol consumption status and frequency), nine questions addressing disease‐specific characteristics (comorbidities, diabetes duration and treatment, family history of diabetes, presence of vascular disease, presence and duration of diabetic foot, number of diabetic foot wounds and history of surgery for foot wounds), and seven questions addressing diabetic foot care education and behaviors (previous education, adequacy and source of the education, performance and frequency of self‐foot examination, inspection of footwear before use and criteria for shoe selection).

Foot Care Behaviour Scale

The Foot Care Behaviour Scale was developed by Borges in 2007 as the Foot Self‐Care Observation Guide to assess foot self‐care behaviours in individuals with diabetes [32]. Borges validated the scale but did not conduct a reliability study [32]. Biçer [33], who conducted Turkish language validation, adapted this guide according to expert opinion. The scale measures the foot self‐care behaviours of individuals with diabetes by assessing how consistently they perform recommended foot care practices. It is a 15‐item, 5‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = never, 2 = occasionally, 3 = sometimes, 4 = frequently, 5 = always) with a total score ranging from 15 to 75 points. Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.83 in the Turkish validity study and 0.86 in the present study.

Diabetic Foot Care Self‐Efficacy Scale

This scale was developed by Quarles in 2005 to evaluate how patients with diabetic foot perceive their power in care. Biçer performed the Turkish validity and reliability of the scale [33]. It consists of nine items scored on an 11‐point visual scale (0 = feel not adequate at all to 10 = feel very adequate), yielding a score ranging from 0 to 90. In the Turkish validity study, Cronbach's α was determined as 0.86. The Cronbach's α coefficient obtained in our study was 0.953.

Diabetes Self‐Care Scale in Turkish

This scale was developed by Lee and Fisher (2005) to assess self‐care activities of individuals with type 2 diabetes, and the Turkish validity and reliability study was performed by Karakurt (2008) [34]. It is a Likert‐type scale consisting of 35 items. The Turkish version is consistent with the original except for the response options. Based on expert opinion and the suggestion of Lee and Fisher, the original 6‐point Likert‐type scale was converted to a 4‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = always). As a result, the total score of the Turkish version ranges from 35 to 140. Higher scores are associated with better self‐care behaviours, with a score of 92 or higher considered the minimum acceptable level of self‐care according to the Turkish validity study [34]. The Cronbach's α of the scale was 0.81 in the Turkish validity and reliability study and 0.87 in the present study.

Statistical Analysis

The data obtained in this study were analysed using the IBM SPSS version 22 package program. There were no missing data. For non‐normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for comparisons between three or more groups. For normally distributed data, independent samples t‐test was employed for comparisons between two groups, and ANOVA was utilized for comparisons between three or more groups. Correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between variables. The level of statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

The Declaration of Helsinki was adhered to in the study. Written permission was obtained from Amasya University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (E‐15386878‐044‐12,489, E‐76988455‐044‐67,030), Samsun Vezirköprü Public Hospital (date: 22.12.2020, number: 48066310‐000‐1139; date: 03.05.2021) and Ondokuz Mayıs University Faculty of Medicine Hospital (number: E‐15374210‐622.03‐23078; number: E‐15374210‐804.01‐155100), Çorum Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital (date: 26.04.2022, 85322798–799‐340/164182890). Necessary permissions were obtained from the authors all scales used in the study. In addition, written informed consent was obtained from the patients who agreed to participate in the study.

Results

Sociodemographic and Disease‐Related Characteristics of the Groups

Patients with diabetic foot wounds had a mean age of 65.00 ± 10.64 years, 80% were male, 73.3% were married, 56.7% had income equivalent to their expenses, and 70% were primary school graduates. Additionally, 61.7% of the patients lived in urban areas, 86.7% were non‐smokers, 100% were non‐drinkers and 50% had diabetes for 15 years or more (Table 1). In the group without diabetic foot wounds, the mean age was 59.85 ± 12.12 years, 60% were female, 83.3% were married, 60% had income equivalent to their expenses, and 36.7% were primary school graduates. Similar to the diabetic foot wound group, 61.7% of patients without diabetic foot wounds lived in urban areas, 81.7% were non‐smokers, and 100% were non‐drinkers, and 30% had diabetes for 0 to 5 years (Table 1).

In the group with diabetic foot wounds, 55% of patients had an additional chronic disease, 51.7% were receiving insulin therapy, and 55% reported a family history of diabetes (most commonly maternal, 57.6%). Vascular disease was present in 13.3% of the patients, with deep vein thrombosis accounting for most of these cases (87.5%). Regarding foot care behaviours, 68.3% of the patients reported performing foot self‐examinations, with 41.3% examining their feet weekly. In addition, only 40% of patients checked their shoes before wearing them and 43.3% paid attention to proper shoe selection (Table 1).

In the group without diabetic foot wounds, 63.3% had a chronic comorbidity, 61.7% were using oral antidiabetic medication, and 56.7% had a family history of diabetes (most commonly maternal, 67.6%). Vascular disease was less prevalent (3.3%). Of the two patients in this group with vascular disease, one had deep vein thrombosis and one had varicose veins. In terms of foot care practices, 58.3% of patients performed self‐foot examinations (45.7% examined their feet daily), 51.7% checked the inside of their shoes before wearing them, and 50% selected soft‐soled orthopedic shoes that fit the foot properly (Table 1).

Intergroup Comparison of Foot Care Behaviour, Diabetic Foot Care Self‐Efficacy and Diabetes Self‐Care Scale Scores

There was no significant difference between the groups regarding foot care behaviour and diabetes self‐care scores (p > 0.05). However, self‐efficacy scores were significantly higher among patients without diabetic foot wounds (p < 0.05; Table 2).

Patients with and without a history of undergoing surgery for diabetic foot wound showed no significant difference in foot care behaviour or self‐efficacy scores (p > 0.05), although diabetes self‐care scores were significantly higher among patients with a history of surgery (p < 0.05).

Prior education on diabetic foot care was associated with significantly higher self‐efficacy and self‐care scores (p < 0.05), although the difference in foot care behaviour score did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Comparison of Scale Scores According to Sociodemographic Characteristics

Among patients with diabetic foot wounds, age showed moderate to strong negative correlations with foot care behaviour score (r = −0.531, p < 0.001), diabetic foot care self‐efficacy score (r = −0.622, p < 0.001), and diabetes self‐care score (r = −0.453, p < 0.001). Among patients without diabetic foot wounds, age also showed a moderate‐to‐strong negative correlation with diabetic foot care self‐efficacy score (r = −0.523, p < 0.001) and a weak‐to‐moderate negative correlation with diabetes self‐care score (r = −0.292, p = 0.023). These results suggest that older age is associated with declines in foot care activity, self‐efficacy, and diabetes self‐care.

In both groups, no significant differences in foot care behaviour, self‐efficacy, or self‐care scores were observed based on gender, marital status, or financial status (p > 0.05). Education level was not associated with significant differences in any scale score in patients with diabetic foot wounds, whereas in patients without diabetic foot wounds, scale scores increased with education level. Scale scores were significantly lower in illiterate patients compared to those with primary, secondary and higher education levels, and significantly lower in primary school graduates compared to those with secondary and higher education levels (p < 0.05; Table 4).

Presence of a chronic comorbidity was not associated with any difference in scale scores among patients with diabetic foot wounds (p > 0.05). In the group without diabetic foot wounds, self‐efficacy scores were significantly lower among patients with comorbidities compared to those without (p < 0.05).

Among patients with diabetic foot wounds, scale scores showed no statistically significant differences according to the patients' diabetes treatment (p > 0.05). Among patients without diabetic foot wounds, there was no significant difference in foot care behaviour and self‐care scores (p > 0.05), whereas diabetes treatment was associated with significant differences in self‐efficacy score (p < 0.05). Post hoc analysis revealed significant differences between patients receiving oral antidiabetic drugs and those receiving oral antidiabetic therapy with insulin or using insulin alone. However, no significant difference was observed between patients receiving both oral antidiabetic drugs and insulin and those using insulin only (Table 5).

Patients with diabetic foot wounds who reported performing foot self‐examination had significantly higher scores on all three scales compared to those who did not (p < 0.05). Among patients without diabetic foot wounds, self‐care scores did not differ significantly (p > 0.05), whereas patients who performed foot self‐examination had significantly higher foot care behaviour and self‐efficacy scores (p < 0.05; Table 5).

In terms of footwear‐related practices, checking inside shoes before wearing them was associated with significantly higher foot care behaviour, self‐efficacy, and diabetes self‐care scores in both groups (p < 0.05; Table 5). Additionally, footwear selection criteria significantly impacted scale scores. In the diabetic foot wound group, patients who prioritized appropriate footwear (shoes that fit well, have soft soles, and are orthopedic) had significantly higher self‐efficacy scores than patients who preferred open‐toe and thin‐soled footwear. Foot care behaviour, self‐efficacy, and self‐care scores were significantly lower among patients who lacked caution in their shoe selection. Similarly, patients without diabetic foot wounds who prioritized appropriate footwear had higher scores on all three scales, although statistical significance could not be determined due to the small sizes of the subgroups (Table 5).

Relationships Between Foot Care Behaviour, Diabetic Foot Care Self‐Efficacy and Diabetes Self‐Care Scores

A strong positive correlation was observed between foot care behaviour and diabetic foot care self‐efficacy scores (r = 0.722; p < 0.05). The foot care behaviour score accounted for approximately 49% of the self‐efficacy score. Similarly, there was a strong positive correlation between foot care behaviour and diabetes self‐care scores (r = 0.618; p < 0.05). The foot care behaviour score accounted for approximately 36% of the self‐care score. There was also a strong positive correlation between diabetic foot care self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care scores (r = 0.719; p < 0.05), with the diabetes self‐care score accounting for approximately 49% of the self‐efficacy score (Table 6).

Discussion

Education emerged as an important determinant associated with improved outcomes. Patients with diabetic foot wounds who received structured education on foot care demonstrated significantly higher foot care self‐efficacy and diabetes self‐care scores. Although a difference in self‐reported foot care practices was not observed, educational interventions appear to enhance patients' confidence and overall self‐management capacity. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that education not only provides knowledge but also improves individuals' ability to translate knowledge into sustained behavioural practice [35, 36].

In the group with diabetic foot wounds, a history of surgery due to foot wounds did not significantly affect foot care behaviours or diabetic foot care self‐efficacy, but patients who had undergone surgery had significantly higher overall diabetes self‐care scores. This may reflect increased awareness, perceived vulnerability, and greater engagement in disease management following a serious complication.

In both patient groups, age was inversely associated with scale scores, indicating that older age was linked to less favourable foot care behaviour, self‐efficacy, and self‐care. This finding is consistent with previous reports of better diabetic foot self‐care practices among younger patients [37]. Age‐related declines in physical capacity, motivation or confidence may help explain this pattern.

Education level demonstrated differential effects between groups. Among patients with diabetic foot wounds, no significant association was observed between education level and scale scores. In patients without diabetic foot wounds, however, higher educational attainment was significantly associated with better foot care behaviours, greater self‐efficacy and stronger overall diabetes self‐care. Previous studies have similarly reported a positive association between education level and both foot care behaviours and diabetic foot self‐efficacy [35, 38].

Among patients without diabetic foot wounds, those residing in urban areas demonstrated higher self‐efficacy and self‐care levels. Montazeri et al. (2012) [39] similarly reported higher self‐care scores among individuals living in urban settings. This may be attributed to better access to healthcare services, more regular follow‐up and improved availability of structured disease management resources. Conversely, individuals with additional chronic diseases exhibited lower self‐efficacy levels, suggesting that the comorbidity burden may limit their capacity to consistently maintain foot care practices.

In both groups, specific preventive behaviours were strongly associated with improved outcomes. Individuals who performed regular foot self‐examination had significantly higher scale scores. Likewise, those who checked the inside of their shoes before wearing them demonstrated higher foot care behaviour, self‐efficacy and self‐care scores. These findings are consistent with previous literature emphasizing the importance of daily preventive practices in diabetic foot management [40, 41].

Patients with diabetic foot wounds who prioritized properly fitting, soft‐soled, orthopaedic footwear exhibited higher scores across all scales. This finding suggests that careful shoe selection may reflect greater awareness of foot health and recognition of the protective role of appropriate footwear in preventing foot injuries. These results are consistent with previous research highlighting the protective value of suitable footwear in reducing foot‐related complications [42].

Furthermore, significant positive correlations were identified among foot care behaviours, diabetic foot care self‐efficacy and overall diabetes self‐care. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating that self‐efficacy functions as a psychological mechanism that strengthens behavioural adherence and long‐term disease management [35, 43, 44, 45].

Although healthcare providers remain primarily responsible for professional care, individuals with diabetes should be encouraged to adopt self‐care behaviours and actively collaborate with healthcare professionals throughout the treatment process. Strengthening self‐efficacy through structured education, individualized counselling and regular follow‐up may represent a critical strategy for reducing the risk of diabetic foot complications and improving overall disease management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected from only three centres, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the groups were not homogeneously distributed in terms of gender, education level and type of diabetes treatment. Although these factors were partially controlled through statistical analyses, caution is warranted when interpreting the results. Furthermore, comparisons based on diabetes type and HbA1c levels were not conducted in this study. These variables could be included in future studies. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings, and further research should be conducted to evaluate a broader range of clinical parameters in more homogeneous samples.

Conclusion

Foot care behaviours, diabetes self‐care activities, and diabetic foot care self‐efficacy were strongly interrelated in both groups. Among patients without diabetic foot wounds, specific sociodemographic factors such as higher education level and urban residence were linked to better foot care behaviours, self‐efficacy, and diabetes self‐care. As increased literacy and prior education on foot care were associated with improved outcomes, targeted educational programs should be developed to enhance foot care behaviours, promote smoking cessation and optimize the management of comorbidities before diabetic foot wounds develop. Furthermore, given that patients residing in suburban and rural areas exhibited lower scale scores, proactive outreach programs are necessary. Providing preemptive training and distributing essential supplies to these populations can fulfil early care needs and ensure timely intervention. Therefore, implementing new health policies and nursing quality‐improvement initiatives focused on non‐urban populations is critical for elevating overall self‐care and self‐efficacy. Finally, routine foot self‐examination, shoe inspection and appropriate footwear selection were associated with higher clinical scores across both groups. Therefore, it is essential that nursing interventions explicitly train patients in these practical, foot‐protective skills.
