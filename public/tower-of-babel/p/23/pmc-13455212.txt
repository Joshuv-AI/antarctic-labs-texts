Admission lipid profile and outcomes in sepsis requiring early invasive source control
Authors: Juan Antonio Brito Piris, Sandra Parra Pérez, Pitter Francisco Cueto Quintana, Pedro Manuel Garrido Benedicto, Xavier Gabaldó Barrios, Lydia Cabau Parra, Xavier Navarro Segarra, Conxita Rovira Anglès, Immaculada Vallverdú Perapoch, Antoni Castro Salomó

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipid metabolism is profoundly altered in sepsis, but its prognostic utility is underused in clinical practice. We evaluated lipid profiles performed within 24–48 h of ICU admission in patients with sepsis or septic shock who had already undergone surgical or interventional source control and explored associations with organ support and hospital mortality.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective observational cohort study at the intensive care unit of Hospital Universitari Sant Joan de Reus (2016–2023). We included adults meeting Sepsis-3 criteria who underwent surgical or interventional source control within 48 h of hospital arrival. Only patients with available lipid profiles were included, which may introduce selection bias. Hospital mortality was defined as the primary clinical outcome. Secondary outcomes included organ support requirements, ICU and hospital length of stay, correlations with SOFA severity scores, and inflammatory biomarkers. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed as an exploratory analysis to investigate the independent associations between lipid parameters and hospital mortality.ResultsOf 266 screened patients, 150 met the inclusion criteria, comprising 61.3% males, with a median age of 72.1 years (IQR, 61.7–79.8), and an overall hospital mortality rate of 18.7% (n = 28). Non-survivors exhibited significantly lower concentrations of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and total cholesterol levels, and higher triglyceride levels than survivors. In ROC analyses, HDL-C showed the highest accuracy (AUC 0.966, 95% CI 0.904–1.000), with an optimal cutoff of 15 mg/dL, sensitivity of 96.4%, and specificity of 98.3%. This was followed by total cholesterol (AUC 0.963, 95% CI 0.901–1.000) and LDL-C (AUC 0.920, 95% CI 0.851–0.989). In contrast, triglycerides demonstrated a more modest predictive performance (AUC 0.849, 95% CI 0.772–0.926). In multivariable analysis, lower HDL-C levels (OR 0.802, 95% CI 0.727–0.885; p < 0.001) and lower albumin levels (OR 0.241, 95% CI 0.066–0.879; p = 0.031) remained independently associated with hospital mortality in the final parsimonious model derived using backward stepwise selection from an initial candidate model including chronic kidney disease, CRRT requirement, vasopressor use, LDL-C, VLDL-C, and triglycerides.ConclusionsEarly hypolipidemia was independently associated with hospital mortality in septic patients undergoing timely source control. Post-source control lipid alterations may reflect residual immunometabolic vulnerability and warrant further evaluation as exploratory prognostic biomarkers. Background

Sepsis is a major global health concern, characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection, and remains a leading cause of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and mortality worldwide [1–3]. Early and accurate risk stratification is essential to guide timely clinical decisions and optimize outcomes in critically ill patients.

In this context, infections requiring invasive or surgical source control, such as intra-abdominal infections, necrotizing skin and soft tissue infections, and obstructive pyelonephritis, are associated with rapid clinical deterioration, high inflammatory burden, and substantial mortality [2–6, 11, 12]. These patients often experience persistent pathogen load, ongoing endotoxin exposure, and delayed resolution of systemic inflammation, making early prognostic assessment particularly challenging. Despite advances in sepsis management, commonly used biomarkers lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity to fully capture the complexity of the host response in this high-risk subgroup.

Accumulating evidence indicates that lipid metabolism is tightly interconnected with innate immunity and the inflammatory response during infection. Sepsis is frequently accompanied by profound alterations in circulating lipoproteins, including marked reductions in high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), and total cholesterol, together with variable changes in triglyceride concentrations [8, 9, 16, 17, 31]. These changes occur early in the course of the disease and reflect a combination of decreased hepatic synthesis, increased vascular permeability, accelerated catabolism, and redistribution into inflamed tissues. Several clinical studies have demonstrated that early declines in HDL levels are associated with greater disease severity, organ dysfunction, and mortality in septic patients [18, 19, 29].

Beyond their role in lipid transport, lipoproteins, particularly HDL, play a key immunomodulatory role in sepsis [20, 21, 22, 33, 34]. During sepsis, HDL undergoes profound structural and functional remodeling, characterized by depletion of protective components such as ApoC-I, enrichment with acute-phase proteins, and loss of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, resulting in dysfunctional, pro-inflammatory particles [23–25]. In parallel, reductions in LDL, while potentially limiting endotoxin transport, may reflect severe metabolic dysregulation and have been associated with increased in-hospital mortality [16, 24].

Although hypocholesterolemia and dyslipidemia have consistently been linked to adverse outcomes in critically ill patients [16, 24, 32], most available studies have assessed lipid profiles at emergency department presentation or in heterogeneous cohorts of medical sepsis. Data remain scarce in patients requiring early surgical or interventional source control, particularly regarding lipid profiles obtained after operative intervention and initial hemodynamic stabilization. This distinction is clinically relevant, as surgical stress, resolution of infectious burden, and early resuscitation may substantially modulate metabolic and inflammatory pathways, potentially influencing the prognostic value of lipid measurements.

Recent studies suggest that integrating lipid variables into composite risk assessments may be correlated with mortality, the need for vasopressor therapy, mechanical ventilation, or renal replacement therapy [19]. Nevertheless, lipid profiling is rarely incorporated into routine sepsis evaluation and is not currently included in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines [30].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between early lipid profiles obtained within the first 24–48 h after ICU admission, following surgical or interventional source control, and hospital mortality in adults with sepsis or septic shock. We hypothesized that lower circulating lipid fractions, particularly HDL-C and LDL-C, would be independently associated with increased hospital mortality after adjustment for established severity markers.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a single-center retrospective observational cohort study at Hospital Universitari Sant Joan de Reus (Catalonia, Spain). Adult patients admitted with sepsis or septic shock, defined according to Sepsis-3 consensus criteria, between January 2016 and June 2023, were screened. Eligible cases were community-acquired infections requiring invasive or surgical source control, including intra-abdominal infections, obstructive pyelonephritis, and skin and soft tissue infections. Patients were admitted either directly from the Emergency Department to the ICU or first stabilized in the ICU after initial assessment in the Emergency Department.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

age ≥ 18 years; diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock according to Sepsis-3; requirement for surgical or percutaneous source control within 24–48 h of hospital arrival (defined as “time zero”); subsequent ICU admission; and availability of a lipid profile obtained within the first 24–48 h after ICU admission, after completion of source control and initial resuscitation. All patients had already received fluid resuscitation and empiric or targeted antibiotic therapy by the time the lipid profile was collected. Lipid profiles were obtained at the discretion of the treating physician before the conception and design of the present study. Only patients with available lipid profiles were included, which may introduce selection bias.

Exclusion criteria

Patients without a lipid profile performed within the first 24–48 h after ICU admission, or who underwent source control procedures outside our institution.

Patients with hospital admission within the preceding three months were excluded to reduce potential confounding from recent hospitalization-related inflammatory states, acute metabolic alterations, or lipid-modifying interventions, thereby improving cohort homogeneity.

Case identification and data collection

Patients were identified using ICD-10 codes A41.x and R65.2. Two independent investigators abstracted data, blinded to the study’s hypotheses and outcomes.

Collected variables included demographics, BMI, comorbidities, vital signs, physiological parameters at admission (including need for mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support), and imaging modalities used to identify the infectious source (ultrasound, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or other).

Laboratory data were obtained within the first 24–48 h of ICU admission, after source control and hemodynamic stabilization, and included leukocyte count, serum lactate, creatinine, C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, albumin, prealbumin, and lipid profile (HDL, LDL, VLDL, triglycerides, and HDL-to-triglyceride ratio). Disease severity was assessed using the SOFA score at diagnosis and categorized into tertiles as mild (0–3), moderate [4–6], or severe [7–15]. Our cohort of patients showed a range of SOFA scores from 0 to 15. Additional data included the type of source control, microbiological culture results, antibiotic therapy, organ support requirements, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and mortality. Adequacy of empirical antibiotic therapy was determined according to microbiological culture and susceptibility results.

Study objectives

The primary clinical outcome of the study was hospital mortality. Based on this outcome, patients were classified into survivors and non-survivors for comparative analyses.

Secondary outcomes were predefined and included organ support requirements (mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, and renal replacement therapy), ICU and hospital length of stay, correlations between lipid parameters and SOFA severity scores, and associations with inflammatory biomarkers.

Lipid parameters were analyzed as predefined exposure variables. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was selected as the main lipid variable, while low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C), triglycerides, total cholesterol, and derived lipid ratios were considered secondary exploratory variables.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the IRB CatSud (Institut d’Investigació Sanitària Pere Virgili; IISPV; reference 067/2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study was observational.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test for continuous variables. To compare proportions and study relationships between qualitative variables, we used the Chi-square test (χ²) and Fisher’s exact test, depending on the size and characteristics of the variables. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman correlations. Patients were grouped according to hospital mortality and SOFA categories: mild (0–3), moderate [4–6], and severe (≥ 7).

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the independent association between lipid variables and hospital mortality. Given the limited number of outcome events (n = 28 deaths), the number of covariates included in the model was restricted to clinically relevant predictors based on biological plausibility and previously reported associations with sepsis outcomes.

Candidate baseline variables included age, chronic kidney disease, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) requirement, vasopressor use, albumin, HDL-C, LDL-C, VLDL-C, and triglycerides. Candidate variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and biological plausibility, while univariable analyses were used as a supportive exploratory assessment. ICU and hospital length of stay variables were excluded from the final model because of their post-baseline nature and potential for reverse causality bias. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals and two-sided p-values; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Backward stepwise selection was performed to derive the final parsimonious model, and model performance was compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values, accepting variables with VIF < 5 and tolerance > 0.2. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Given the relatively small number of outcome events, the multivariable analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. No internal validation methods (such as bootstrapping or cross-validation) were performed due to the retrospective design and sample size constraints.

Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and graphical representations were generated with ggplot2 in R.

Results

Patient flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 266 patients were initially identified. Nine patients were excluded after infection was ruled out. All included patients were recorded as Caucasian in the clinical records. An additional 96 patients were excluded because they were directly admitted to a general ward and underwent source control more than 48 h after hospital admission. Six patients were further excluded due to missing critical data. The final study cohort consisted of 150 patients who met all the inclusion criteria. Clinical characteristics at ICU admission are shown in Table 1. Among the 150 included patients, 28 (18.7%) died during hospitalization. Non-survivors were older than survivors (median age 75.8 [IQR 69.9–80.8] vs. 71.1 [66.1–71.8] years; p = 0.035), with no significant differences in sex distribution or BMI. Non-survivors had shorter ICU and hospital lengths of stay, likely reflecting early mortality during hospitalization. Chronic kidney disease was significantly more frequent among non-survivors (25% vs. 8.2%; p = 0.011). No other comorbidities showed significant differences between groups.

Source-control indications were similarly distributed between survivors and non-survivors, with intestinal perforation being the most common diagnosis. Microbiological cultures were obtained in 78.7% of patients, and the distribution of bacterial, mixed bacterial–fungal, and fungal infections did not differ between groups. The adequacy of empirical antibiotic therapy was similar between survivors and non-survivors (p = 0.294). In the overall cohort, 54% of patients received adequate empirical therapy, 12.7% received inadequate empirical therapy, and in 33.3% of cases, cultures were not obtained or were negative, with no significant differences between groups.

Time from emergency department admission to the operating room was comparable between non-survivors and survivors (8.2 [4.5–21.1] vs. 11.6 [7.2–24.8] hours; p = 0.115). The median interval between source control and lipid measurement was 19 h in survivors and 17.5 h in non-survivors, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.908).

Regarding organ support, non-survivors more frequently required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) (14.3% vs. 3.3%; p = 0.019) and vasopressor support (67.9% vs. 42.6%; p = 0.016). The use of invasive mechanical ventilation was similar between groups. Overall, 71 of 150 patients (47.3%) fulfilled Sepsis-3 criteria for septic shock, including 19/28 non-survivors (67.9%) and 52/122 survivors (42.6%).

SOFA scores did not differ significantly between survivors and non-survivors at either ICU assessment (6 [IQR 4.75–9.25] vs. 5 [3–7]; p = 0.070) or ED assessment (6.5 [4–9] vs. 5 [3–8]; p = 0.104).

Differential laboratory profiles of survivors and non-survivors

Non-survivors exhibited a distinct metabolic–inflammatory profile, as summarized in Table 2. Compared with survivors, non-survivors had lower serum albumin levels (1.7 vs. 3.1 g/dL; p < 0.001), lower arterial pH (7.29 vs. 7.35; p = 0.023), and higher lactate levels (4.7 vs. 3 mmol/L; p = 0.009). The lipid profile was markedly altered, with reductions in total cholesterol (78 vs. 158 mg/dL; p < 0.001), LDL-C (41 vs. 79 mg/dL; p < 0.001), HDL-C (8.5 vs. 39.0 mg/dL; p < 0.001), and VLDL-C (28.5 vs. 37 mg/dL; p = 0.002), alongside elevated triglycerides (226.5 vs. 157 mg/dL; p < 0.001), and the HDL-to-triglyceride ratio was substantially reduced (0.03 vs. 0.27; p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between groups in C-reactive protein, platelet count, bilirubin, creatinine, or PT and fibrinogen levels.

Associations between lipid fractions and inflammatory reactants

ICU SOFA score (SOFA-ICU) showed significant correlations with HDL-C (r = − 0.287, p < 0.001), triglycerides (TG; r = 0.227, p = 0.008), and the HDL/TG ratio (r = − 0.298, p < 0.001). Among inflammatory biomarkers, CRP was the only variable significantly inversely correlated with SOFA-ICU (r = − 0.211, p = 0.014).

SOFA score assessed in the emergency department prior to source control and ICU admission (SOFA-ED) showed similar associations with lipid fractions, including HDL-C (r = − 0.193, p = 0.018), TG (r = 0.170, p = 0.038), and the HDL/TG ratio (r = − 0.182, p = 0.026). Among inflammatory and hematologic markers, leukocyte count (r = − 0.189, p = 0.021) and platelet count (r = − 0.324, p < 0.001) were inversely correlated with SOFA-ED.

Albumin demonstrated consistent associations with multiple lipid parameters, including total cholesterol (TC; r = 0.375, p < 0.001), LDL-C (r = 0.413, p < 0.001), HDL-C (r = 0.461, p < 0.001), TG (r = − 0.350, p < 0.001), and the HDL/TG ratio (r = 0.484, p < 0.001). Albumin levels were also positively associated with leukocyte count (r = 0.188, p = 0.021). Fibrinogen showed no significant correlations with lipid parameters.

Clinical and laboratory features across SOFA-based severity categories

Patient distribution by admission SOFA score (0–3, 4–6, ≥ 7) is summarized in Table 3. Systemic arterial hypertension was more frequent in the SOFA ≥ 7 group (80.5%) compared with lower categories (55.2% and 54.9%; p = 0.017), whereas the distribution of other comorbidities did not differ significantly across SOFA strata.

The spectrum of surgical diagnoses was similar among SOFA groups, and the rate of positive microbiological cultures showed a non-significant upward trend with higher SOFA scores (51.7% → 52.9% → 63.4%; p = 0.471).

Among laboratory variables, total bilirubin was significantly higher in the SOFA 4–6 and SOFA ≥ 7 groups compared with SOFA 0–3 (0.9 and 0.9 vs. 0.5 mg/dL; p = 0.015). VLDL-C was also significantly higher in the SOFA ≥ 7 group (39 [32.5–59] mg/dL; p = 0.009), whereas other laboratory parameters, including HDL-C, triglycerides, LDL-C, total cholesterol, serum albumin, CRP, and lactate, did not differ significantly across SOFA categories.

Organ support requirements increased with severity. The need for invasive mechanical ventilation rose from 41.4% in SOFA 0–3 to 70.6% in SOFA 4–6 and 78.0% in SOFA ≥ 7 (p = 0.001). Vasopressor use differed significantly across SOFA groups (34.5%, 60.8%, and 48.8%; p = 0.023), whereas CRRT showed a non-significant upward trend (1.7%, 5.9%, and 9.8%; p = 0.211).

In this cohort, we did not find any significant statistical association between mortality and SOFA classification. No progressive increase in mortality across SOFA categories was observed (ICU mortality: 17.2% → 9.8% → 17.1%; p = 0.482; hospital mortality: 22.4% → 13.7% → 19.5%; p = 0.503).

Prognostic performance of lipid variables

The discriminative capacity of early post-source control lipid parameters for hospital mortality was evaluated using ROC curves as an exploratory assessment. HDL-C showed the highest accuracy (AUC 0.966, 95% CI 0.904–1.000) with a sensitivity of 0.964 and a specificity of 0.983 (Fig. 2A), followed by total cholesterol (AUC 0.963, 95% CI 0.901–1.000) with a sensitivity of 0.964 and a specificity of 0.942 (Fig. 2B) and LDL-C (AUC 0.920, 95% CI 0.851–0.989) with a sensitivity of 0.892 and a specificity of 0.877 (Fig. 2C).

In contrast, triglycerides demonstrated a more modest predictive performance (AUC 0.849, 95% CI 0.772–0.926) with a sensitivity of 0.857 and a specificity of 0.819 (Fig. 2D). The ROC curve analysis yielded an optimal cutoff of 15 mg/dL, selected at the point that maximized Youden’s J index, resulting in a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 98%. These high AUC values may be overestimated due to the retrospective single-center design and limited sample size, and therefore require external validation before clinical application.

ROC curve analysis: (A) HDL-C, (B) total cholesterol, (C) LDL-C, and (D) triglycerides

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the independent association between lipid parameters and hospital mortality (Fig. 3A). Candidate covariates included age, chronic kidney disease, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) requirement, vasopressor use, HDL-C, LDL-C, VLDL-C, triglycerides, and albumin.

In the final parsimonious model, lower HDL-C and albumin levels remained independently associated with hospital mortality.

Backward stepwise selection supported by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) yielded the same final parsimonious model.

Model calibration assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test suggested suboptimal agreement between observed and predicted outcomes, which should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and low number of outcome events.

As shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3B), lower HDL-C levels remained independently associated with hospital mortality after multivariable adjustment (OR 0.802, 95% CI 0.727–0.885; p < 0.001). Lower albumin levels were also independently associated with mortality (OR 0.241, 95% CI 0.066–0.879; p = 0.031). Overall, the adjusted model identified HDL-C as the variable showing the strongest association with outcome among the analyzed laboratory parameters.

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of adults with sepsis or septic shock requiring invasive source control, non-survivors displayed a distinct immunometabolic profile characterized by profound hypolipidemia, particularly very low HDL-C and LDL-C with depressed total cholesterol, along with hypertriglyceridemia, hypoalbuminemia, and elevated lactate. Non-survivors had HDL-C levels more than four times lower than survivors, which is consistent with the biological alterations previously described in severe systemic inflammation and critical illness [4–6]. In our retrospective study these abnormalities were associated with higher illness severity and need for organ support and remained independently associated with hospital mortality in the multivariable model. ROC curves showed excellent discrimination for HDL-C, total cholesterol, and LDL-C, with more modest performance for triglycerides.

The very high discriminative performance observed for HDL-C and total cholesterol (AUC > 0.95) should be interpreted with caution. These values may reflect, at least in part, the specific characteristics of our cohort, including its clinical homogeneity, the timing of lipid assessment after initial resuscitation, and the limited sample size. Therefore, an overestimation of the predictive performance cannot be excluded, and external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before clinical application.

It is important to highlight that these lipid measurements were obtained 24–48 h after source control, in a context where the infectious source had already been controlled. Marked post-source control hypolipidemia in this context may reflect a high-risk phenotype, characterized by persistent inflammatory, metabolic, and oxidative stress dysregulation and increased vulnerability to organ dysfunction and subsequent complications.

The weak association between lipid variables and SOFA scores is consistent with the hypothesis that lipid metabolism reflects broader immunometabolic stress not fully captured by conventional organ dysfunction scores. While SOFA score reflects acute organ failure at a single time point, lipid alterations may integrate residual inflammatory, endothelial, and metabolic pathways. The absence of a clear stepwise increase in mortality across SOFA strata should be interpreted cautiously, likely reflecting limited sample size, low event rates, and loss of granularity due to categorization. In addition, in patients undergoing early source control, admission SOFA may incompletely reflect subsequent clinical trajectories, including delayed organ failure, secondary infections, or post-source control complications.

From a clinical perspective, post-source control lipid profiles may provide complementary prognostic information for future risk stratification studies. However, lipid parameters should be considered adjuncts to established severity scores such as SOFA rather than standalone decision-making tools.

Lower HDL-C and albumin levels remained independently associated with hospital mortality after multivariable adjustment, supporting their potential role as complementary markers of residual biological vulnerability following source control and initial resuscitation. Among the analyzed laboratory variables, HDL-C showed the strongest and most consistent association with outcome, suggesting that marked lipid alterations observed after source control may reflect ongoing immunometabolic dysregulation beyond the acute infectious phase. Albumin was also independently associated with hospital mortality. Beyond its role as a marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, albumin is one of the major endogenous antioxidants. Therefore, the lower albumin levels observed in non-survivors may reflect a state of systemic inflammation, increased capillary leakage, oxidative stress consumption, and impaired hepatic synthetic function, all hallmarks of severe septic illness and adverse prognosis [35].

Our findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that HDL-C and LDL-C decrease rapidly during sepsis and that these reductions correlate with morbidity and mortality [7, 17–19, 29]. Mechanistically, HDL-C particles undergo structural and functional impairment during severe inflammation, losing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and reducing endotoxin-neutralizing capacity [23–26, 33]. LDL-C reduction reflects decreased hepatic synthesis, capillary leak, and accelerated clearance, without implying a direct causal effect on outcomes [8, 27, 31, 33, 34].

From a clinical perspective, post-source control lipid alterations may reflect residual biological vulnerability following sepsis and source control and could represent complementary biomarkers for future risk stratification studies. However, these findings should be considered exploratory and are not currently sufficient to support clinical decision-making or therapeutic interventions targeting lipid metabolism. Post-source lipid trajectories could provide insights into metabolic recovery and the evolution of organ dysfunction, representing a promising avenue for prospective studies investigating dynamic lipid changes during critical illness and their association with clinical outcomes [26–28].

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies specifically evaluating lipid profiles obtained after surgical or interventional source control in patients with sepsis. This timing is clinically relevant, as it reflects the residual host response after initial infection control and resuscitation, rather than the early acute phase alone.

Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings, refine cutoff thresholds, and evaluate whether integrating lipid biomarkers with established clinical scores improves risk stratification.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design and the inclusion of only patients with available lipid measurements may introduce selection bias, as lipid profiling was performed according to clinical discretion rather than systematically across all eligible patients. Second, despite adjustment for relevant clinical and laboratory covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly with respect to unmeasured factors such as pre-hospital lipid-lowering therapy, baseline nutritional status, albumin supplementation and subtle variations in hepatic function. Third, the relatively small sample size and limited number of outcome events increase the risk of model overfitting, which may have led to optimistic estimates of association strength and predictive performance. The significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test result also supports cautious interpretation of model calibration and predictive estimates. Fourth, the absence of external validation limits the generalizability of the findings; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating until confirmed in independent cohorts. Finally, this study was not pre-registered due to its retrospective observational design, which constitutes an additional methodological limitation.

In conclusion, post-source control lipid parameters were independently associated with hospital mortality; however, their role in individual risk prediction remains uncertain. These findings suggest that lipid alterations following source control and resuscitation may reflect residual immunometabolic dysfunction and could represent complementary biomarkers for future risk stratification studies. Nevertheless, given the retrospective observational design and limited sample size, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, requiring prospective validation in independent cohorts before any clinical application can be considered.
