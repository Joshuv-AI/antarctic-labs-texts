Integrated cross-sectional study and functional validation indicate the association of lactobacillus crispatus-derived D-lactic acid with cervical gene expression and precancerous cervical lesions
Authors: Wenkui Dai, Xin Jiang, Yu Liu, Yingjuan Yu, Jun Hou, Jianguo Xia, Shuai Cheng Li, Changzhong Li, Hui Du, Ruifang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) persistence and progression to high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) involves complex interactions between the cervicovaginal microbiome, metabolome, and host immune responses. While Lactobacillus crispatus has been associated with the risk of cervical lesion progression, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 74 HPV-positive Chinese women diagnosed with low-grade SIL (LSIL) or HSIL. Cervicovaginal swabs were analyzed using 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted LC-MS metabolomics, while cervical exfoliated cells underwent RNA-sequencing. Community state types (CSTs) were determined by hierarchical clustering, and differentially expressed genes were identified across CSTs and SIL grades. Bayesian network analysis was employed to model molecular interactions between CVM compositions, metabolites, host gene expression, and HSIL status. Key findings were validated through in vitro experiments using HPV16-infected cervical epithelial cells (Ect1/E6E7) co-cultured with Lactobacillus supernatants and D-lactic acid.ResultsL. crispatus-dominated communities (CST_I) were significantly enriched in LSIL compared to HSIL patients (16.22% vs 2.70%, p < 0.05). CST_I exhibited 2.2–2.8-fold higher D-lactic acid levels compared to L. iners-dominated (CST_III) and non-Lactobacillus-dominated (CST_IV) communities. Transcriptomic analysis identified 116 DEGs varying across both CSTs and SIL grades, with functional enrichment revealing HSIL-associated genes involved in axonogenesis and pattern specification, while LSIL-associated genes were linked to immune responses. Bayesian network modeling suggested that CST_I-derived D-lactic acid inhibits FOXD1 expression, which directly correlates with HSIL status. In vitro validation confirmed that L. crispatus supernatant and D-lactic acid significantly reduced FOXD1 expression (p < 0.05), decreased HPV16 viral load, and inhibited Ect1/E6E7 cell growth.ConclusionsThis study elucidates a novel protective mechanism whereby L. crispatus-derived D-lactic acid modulates cervical gene expression, particularly through FOXD1 downregulation, conferring negative links to precancerous cervical lesions. These findings provide mechanistic insights into microbiome-mediated cervical cancer prevention and suggest potential therapeutic targets for intervention strategies aimed at reducing HSIL progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07982-w.

Background

Persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) is a well-established risk factor for the development of squamous intraepithelial lesions (SIL) and cervical cancer [1]. Despite this knowledge, the complex interplay of factors influencing the risk of persistent HPV infection and SIL progression remains incompletely understood. Recent research has underscored the potential role of the cervicovaginal microbiome (CVM) in modulating these risks [2–11].

Healthy women typically exhibited a CVM dominated by Lactobacillus species [12, 13]. Prospective studies have further suggested a causal relationship between vaginal Lactobacillus and the risk of persistent HPV infection as well as SIL progression [7–11]. Notably, women with a CVM dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus have shown a positive association with the natural regression of SIL, whereas those with other CVM structural patterns have an increased risk of SIL progression [7, 8]. These mechanistic connections between specific microbial taxa and cervical carcinogenesis progression remain to be elucidated.

The influence of the CVM on the risk of SIL progression can be partly attributed to its modulation of host immune responses [14–18]. Recent investigations have revealed associations between the CVM and various immune mediators, including a negative correlation between Lactobacillus and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as LAG-3 and PD-L1 [19, 20]. Certain vaginal Lactobacillus strains have been shown to modulate epithelial innate immune responses and activate Langerhans cells [15, 21], which play a crucial role in detecting HPV antigens and initiating cellular immunity. Furthermore, in vitro studies have demonstrated the inhibitory effect of vaginal Lactobacillus on the growth of cancer-related Ect1/E6E7 and CaSKi cells, thereby potentially influencing SIL progression and carcinogenesis [15].

Small compounds are important effectors or mediators in the interactions between microbiome and its host. Recent studies have highlighted differences in metabolite profiles between HPV-negative and HPV-positive women, as well as between low-grade SIL (LSIL) and high-grade SIL (HSIL) [2, 22–24]. For instance, Ilhan ZE et al. observed an enrichment of lipid metabolites and a depletion of amino acid and nucleotide metabolites, linking vaginal dysbiosis to HPV infection and precancerous cervical dysplasia [2]. Interestingly, the levels of anti-inflammatory nucleotides were found to correlate with the dominance of Lactobacillus, irrespective of SIL severity. Moreover, women diagnosed with cervical cancer exhibited an elevated level of amino acids compared to other HPV-positive groups, regardless of SIL status. Other researchers have reported higher concentrations of biogenic amines and phospholipids in HPV-positive women compared to those without HPV infection.22 These studies collectively suggested important roles played by metabolites in the interactions between CVM and SIL grades. Large cross-sectional studies have the potential to help further validate and refine the complex relationships between Lactobacillus, the metabolome, and cervical gene expression across different grades of SIL. In this study, we enrolled 74 HPV-positive Chinese women diagnosed with either LSIL or HSIL. Our primary objectives were to elucidate the variations in the CVM, metabolome, and cervical gene expression associated with different SIL grades, and to explore their interaction networks. Key findings were further validated through in vitro experiments. Our results provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the association between the CVM and SIL grades.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to investigate the associations between CVM, CVMeta, and cervical gene expression profiles in HPV-positive women with varying grades of SIL. A total of 74 women diagnosed with LSIL or HSIL were recruited through interviews conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, China. The enrollment criteria, as previously described [25], required participants to be over 18 years of age, premenopausal, and free from common genital infections, as evidenced by negative vaginal smear tests for H₂O₂, leukocyte esterase, neuraminidase, β-glucuronidase, and acetylaminoglucosidase. Exclusion criteria included a history of cervical ablation, resection surgery, hysterectomy, pelvic radiotherapy, sexual intercourse within three days, douching, or vaginal medication use within seven days, antibiotic exposure within one month, hormone replacement therapy, or GnRH-a use within three months, as well as autoimmune diseases or HIV infection. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Data and sample collection

Metadata, including information on smoking habits, sexual activity history, contraceptive use, gestation and abortion history, HPV genotypes, and vaccination records, were meticulously recorded for each participant by a research assistant. To ensure sample homogeneity, swabs were collected ≥ 3 days post-menstruation. During colposcopy, a single physician obtained posterior fornix swabs, which were immediately preserved in 2 ml sterile tubes on ice and transferred to −80 °C storage within 30 minutes of collection. For host transcriptome analysis, cervical exfoliated cells were gently collected using Pap brushes, suspended in RNAlater (QIAGEN), and stored at −80 °C within 30 minutes of collection.

HPV testing and pathology diagnosis

HPV screening was performed using the Roche Cobas® 4800 HPV system, which detects HPV16/18 and 12 additional high-risk genotypes (HPV 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68). Cervical biopsy specimens were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists to confirm SIL diagnosis. Cytologically diagnosed cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or worse (CIN2+) cases were classified as HSIL, while CIN1 cases were classified as LSIL [26, 27].

Microbial DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, and data processing

Microbial DNA was extracted from vaginal swabs using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis on an Agilent 5400 platform (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, USA). The 16S rDNA V4–V5 hypervariable regions were amplified using primers 515-FR (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 926-RR (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′). Amplicon libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a read length of 250 bp. Raw sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 [28] to generate CVM profiles.

CVM CST analysis

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering with average Euclidean linkage was employed to identify distinct CVM groups. CST nomenclature was based on the dominance of a single bacterial genus or species (relative abundance > 50%). For Lactobacillus-dominated CSTs lacking a single dominant species, the group was designated as CST_MixedL, indicating co-dominance by two Lactobacillus species with total relative abundance ≥ 50%.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolome profiling was conducted using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) on a Thermo Fisher Scientific platform (Ottawa, Canada). Raw intensity data were converted to mzXML format, and ion features were extracted using Progenesis QI software (v0.2.2). Features missing in >50% of quality control samples or >80% of test samples, or with a relative standard deviation > 30%, were excluded. Metabolite identification was performed using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, v0.5.0) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, v0.96.0).

RNA-Seq and data processing

Total RNA was extracted from cervical exfoliated cells using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit. RNA-Seq libraries were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw sequencing reads were filtered using Cutadapt (v0.2.5) [29] to remove low-quality sequences and adapter contaminants. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v0.2.1.0) [30]. Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (v0.1.3.3) [31] and normalized using DESeq2 (v0.1.20) [32]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across distinct CSTs and SIL grades were identified using DESeq2. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v0.4.16.6) [33].

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test was used to assess CST differences between LSIL and HSIL populations (p < 0.05). The R package MaAsLin2 (v0.7.3–64) [34] was applied to analyze CVM species differences between groups (padj < 0.05). Distinct metabolic profiles between LSIL and HSIL were analyzed via R package limma (v0.3.62.2) [35]. The synergy between CVM and CVMeta was evaluated using O2PLS analysis, implemented in the OmicsPLS R package (v0.2.1.0) [36].

For this exploratory study with moderate sample size, we applied a multi-criteria approach combining adjusted p-value (padj < 0.25), initial p-value (p < 0.05), and fold-change thresholds (≥2-fold) to identify differentially abundant metabolites and genes. The relaxed adjusted p-value threshold is appropriate for hypothesis-generating research and helps prevent the exclusion of potentially important biological signals that may be missed with overly stringent criteria in moderate-sized cohorts. This balanced statistical framework prioritizes biological relevance while maintaining scientific rigor, consistent with established practices in exploratory multi-omics discovery studies. Thus, metabolites and cervical genes with p < 0.05, padj < 0.25 and |log2FC|≥1 were considered as statistical different across distinct CSTs or SIL grades. All statistical analysis were performed using R software (v0.4.0.5), with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction applied to generate adjusted p-values (padj).

Bayesian network analysis

Among 116 DEGs with statistical differences across both distinct CSTs and SIL grades, a random forest classifier (100 trees, leave-one-out cross-validation) was applied to select the top 15 genes using the mlr R package (v0.2.19.2) [37]. Then the R package bnlearn (v0.5.0.2) [38] was applied to infer molecular interactions between L. crispatus-dominated CST_I, non-Lactobacillus-dominated CST_IV, 24 CST_I-associated metabolites, those 15 DEGs and HSIL state. In the final model, metabolites and DEGs data were discretized using above/below median prior to the network creation. To avoid local maxima, 1000 networks were bootstrapped and averaged across 1000 random restarts. Network structures were learned using the Hill-Climbing algorithm with the likelihood-equivalence Bayesian Dirichlet (BDe) scoring method. The final consensus network was visualized using Cytoscape (v0.3.10.3) [39].

Vaginal Lactobacillus strains and supernatant collection

For supernatant production, L. crispatus (CGMCC 1.2743) and L. gasseri(CGMCC 1.3396) were obtained from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center. Partially referring to previous reports, a 1 mL aliquot of bacterial suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was inoculated into 10 mL de Man-Rogosa-Sharpe (MRS) broth and cultured under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 hours. Post-cultivation, bacterial density was quantified via spectrophotometry. The culture was subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes to pellet cellular debris, after which the supernatant was aseptically filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and stored at −80 °C. D-LA concentration within bacterial supernatant was quantified by using the D-LA assay kits (Elabscience, China).

Co-culture with Ect1/E6E7 cells

The human immortalized cervical squamous cell line Ect1/E6E7 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). Ect1/E6E7 cells were starved in serum-free DMEM medium for 24 hours. Lactobacillus supernatant was then added to DMEM medium at a 20% (v/v) ratio, which was used to re-culture the starved cells for 24 hours. Control groups were re-cultured in DMEM medium without Lactobacillus supernatant. A total of four experimental groups were designed in this study: Control group: Ect1/E6E7 cells cultured normally; L. crispatus group: Cells co-cultured with L. crispatus supernatants with 42.6 mmol/L D-LA concentration; L. gasseri group: Cells co-cultured with L. gasseri supernatants with 11.8 mmol/L D-LA; D-LA group: Cells treated with 42.6 mmol/L D-LA (Sigma). Post-treatment analyses included HPV16 viral load detection by immunofluorescence, detection of cell vitality by CCK-8 experiment and mRNA expression levels of FOXD1 via qPCR.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells (5 × 105/well) were cultured on sterilized cover glasses (22 × 22 mm) in 6-well plates until 80% confluent. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), blocked (30 min), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with HPV16 E7 primary antibody (1:500 dilution). Following washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200 dilution, 1 h, dark). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (400× magnification).

CCK-8 assay for cell vitality

Cells (1 × 104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates (three replicates per group). After 24 h of treatment, the medium was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. Following 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

qPCR analysis of FOXD1 expression

After bacterial supernatant exposure, cells were washed with 1× PBS and lysed in RNAlater (QIAGEN). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent, followed by chloroform-isopropanol precipitation. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000. cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg RNA using a reverse transcription kit (25 °C for 5 min, 46 °C for 20 min, 95 °C for 1 min). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix with primers for FOXD1 (Forward: 5′-CTTTTCTCGTCTTGGTGGTT-3′; Reverse: 5′-ATAGGTCGGCTTTGCATAAA-3′) and GAPDH (Forward: 5′-CCACATCGCTCAGACACCAT-3′; Reverse: 5′-AGTTAAAAGCAGCCCTGGTGA-3′). Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2− ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the reference gene.

The demographic characteristics were comparable between LSIL and HSIL populations, with no significant differences in average age (Table 1). A large proportion of women in both groups tested positive for HPV16/18-excluded high-risk HPV genotypes. Approximately 16.7% and 23.7% of women exhibited HPV16 positivity in the LSIL and HSIL populations, respectively. Although the proportion of HPV18-positive women was higher in the HSIL group compared to the LSIL group, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Among the 74 participants, 4, 14, and 9 women had received the 2-valent, 4-valent, and 9-valent HPV vaccination, respectively. No significant differences were observed in smoking status between the LSIL and HSIL populations.

CVM differences between LSIL and HSIL populations

Based on 137 identified operational taxonomic units (OTUs), we generated profiling tables for 29 genera and 45 species, representing relative abundance for each taxonomic level. Using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity analysis, we observed partial but notable separation of microbial communities between the two populations (Fig. 2A). All microbial samples were classified into five community state types (CSTs): L. crispatus-dominated (CST_I, 18.92%, n = 14), L. gasseri-dominated (CST_II, 4.05%, n = 3), L. iners-dominated (CST_III, 52.70%, n = 39), L. jensenii-dominated (CST_V, 1.35%, n = 1), mixed Lactobacillus species with total representing > 50% relative abundance (CST_MixedL, 4.05%, n = 3), or non-Lactobacillus-dominated (CST_IV, 18.92%, n = 14) (Fig. 2B). Fisher’s exact test revealed significant differences in CST distribution between the LSIL and HSIL populations (p = 0.0024) (Fig. 2C). Specifically, we observed a decreased proportion of CST_IV and CST_III in the LSIL group (CST_III: 18.92%, 14/74; CST_IV: 5.41%, 4/74) compared to the HSIL group (CST_III: 33.78%, 25/74; CST_IV: 13.51%, 10/74). Further analysis revealed a higher proportion of CST_I in the LSIL group (16.22%, 12/74) versus the HSIL group (2.70%, 2/74). MaAsLin2 analysis confirmed a significant enrichment of L. crispatus in the LSIL group compared to the HSIL group (padj < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). Despite no statistical significance (padj > 0.05), the levels of L. iners, Gardnerella, and Fannyhessea species showed positive correlations with HSIL.

CVMeta variations across distinct CSTs

Untargeted metabolomics analysis identified 1372 metabolites with known identities annotated at mass spectrometry level 2. Our two-way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) analysis demonstrated a synergistic relationship between CVM and CVMeta profiles (Fig. S1). Assessment of corresponding loading values revealed that the top 5 CVM contributors included Alloscardovia omnicolens, L. crispatus, Fannyhessea vaginae, Gardnerella vaginalis, and an unknown Lactobacillus species (Table S1).

Further analysis identified 361, 40, and 261 differentially produced metabolites in CST_I-vs-IV, I-vs-III, and III-vs-IV, respectively (p < 0.05, padj < 0.25, |log₂FC|≥1) (Fig. 3A–C). Among these, 122 metabolites were specific to the CST_I-vs-IV, primarily comprising enriched amino acids, peptides and analogues, and depleted nucleosides, nucleotides and analogues in CST_I (Fig. 3D, Table S2). We further identified 24 metabolites notably enriched in CST_I compared to both CST_III and IV, including 22 distinct metabolites shared between CST_I-vs-IV and I-vs-III, and 5 metabolites shared among all three comparisons (Fig. 3D–E, Table S2). These included D-lactic acid (D-LA), which showed 2.2-fold and 2.8-fold higher levels in CST_I compared to CST_III and IV, respectively. The remaining 23 CST_I-enriched metabolites primarily comprised organic acids and derivatives, such as indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), as well as amino acids and peptides.

Additionally, we identified 10 metabolites enriched in CST_IV compared to both CST_I and III (Fig. 3E, Table S2). Specifically, glycocholic acid exhibited 4.5-fold and 2.4-fold higher levels in CST_IV compared to CST_I and III, respectively. Analysis also indicated 2.7-fold and 2.3-fold higher levels of 1-Oleoyl-L-alpha-lysophosphatidic acid in CST_IV compared to CST_I and III, respectively. Other notable differences included 11.7-fold and 23.7-fold higher levels of styrene oxide, and 3.5-fold and 2.9-fold higher levels of uridine in CST_IV compared to CST_I and III, respectively. For CST_III, analysis identified 4 enriched metabolites, including N-lactoyl-tyrosine and triamterene, when compared to both CST_I and IV.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across distinct CSTs and their associations with SIL grades

Transcriptome analysis of cervical exfoliated cells generated expression information for 21,082 genes across CST_I, III, and IV. Following the criteria of p < 0.05, padj < 0.25, and |log₂FC|≥1, we identified 546, 137 and 245 DEGs in CST_I-vs-IV, I-vs-III, and III-vs-IV, respectively (Tables S3-5). Of these DEGs, 116 exhibited distinct expression patterns across SIL grades (Fig. 4A, Table S6). Specifically, among 450 DEGs specific to CST_I-vs-IV, 40 were differentially expressed between LSIL and HSIL populations. Other DEGs with distinct expression patterns across SIL grades included 24 of 90 DEGs specific to CST_I-vs-III and 25 of 166 specific to CST_III-vs-IV. Among these 116 DEGs, functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct patterns between 55 HSIL-enriched and 61 LSIL-enriched DEGs (padj < 0.1) (Fig. 4B). HSIL-enriched DEGs were primarily associated with 17 biological processes, including axonogenesis, pattern specification processes, and epidermis development. In contrast, LSIL-enriched DEGs were mainly associated with immune responses and transmembrane transport. Using the random forest model, we identified the top 15 contributors to SIL grade differentiation among these 116 DEGs, with 7 enriched in HSIL and 8 enriched in LSIL (Fig. 4C). Further analysis indicated that FOXD1 and TP63, among the 7 HSIL-enriched DEGs, were involved in 8 HSIL-enriched biological processes such as axonogenesis, axon guidance, and pattern specification (Table S7). For the 8 LSIL-enriched DEGs, CD19 and CD79B were primarily involved in LSIL-enriched biological processes.

To further understand associations between DEGs and SIL grades, we analyzed expression patterns across both CSTs and SIL grades (Fig. 4D–G, Fig. S2). For CST_I-vs-IV, DEGs enriched in CST_I were primarily enriched in the LSIL group, while those enriched in CST_IV were mainly enriched in the HSIL group (Fig. 4D). Further analysis showed that 6 of 40 DEGs specific to CST_I-vs-IV were included in the top 15 contributors to LSIL/HSIL differentiation, with the top 3 being HSIL-enriched FOXD1 and LSIL-enriched GYS2 and SPON1. Among the 24 DEGs in CST_I-vs-III (Fig. 4E), those enriched in CST_I were also enriched in the LSIL group, such as FCER2, which was included in the top 15 contributors to HSIL-vs-LSIL differences. In contrast, those enriched in CST_III were mainly enriched in the HSIL group, including PHYHIPL, which was among the top 15 contributors to HSIL-vs-LSIL differences. Among 9 shared DEGs between CST_I-vs-IV and I-vs-III (Fig. 4F), CST_I-enriched DEGs were primarily enriched in the LSIL group, while CST_I-depleted DEGs were mainly enriched in the HSIL group, such as TP63, which ranked 10th among the top 15 contributors to LSIL/HSIL differentiation. For shared DEGs between CST_I-vs-IV and III-vs-IV, CST_I-enriched DEGs also represented enrichment in CST_III communities and the LSIL group (Fig. 4G).

Interestingly, we found that several CST_IV-enriched DEGs in the CST_III-vs-IV, such as CD19 and CD79B, were enriched in LSIL compared to the HSIL group (Fig. S2A). Moreover, these two DEGs were included in the top 15 contributors to LSIL/HSIL differentiation (Fig. 4C). However, CD19 and CD79B exhibited 2.9-fold and 1.3-fold higher levels in CST_I compared to CST_IV, respectively, though without statistical significance under our criteria. This may explain the enrichment of CD19 and CD79B in the LSIL group, despite their enrichment in CST_IV compared to CST_III.

We identified 331 edges in the generated consensus network, with CST_I directly linked to 15 nodes. As expected, CST_I directly linked to CST_IV and D-LA, a well-known beneficial metabolite produced by L. crispatus. Further analysis indicated direct links from D-LA to 9 metabolites (e.g., LSIL-enriched ILA) and 4 DEGs, including HSIL-enriched FOXD1 and TP63, with TP63 directly linked to FOXD1. CST_I also directly linked to FOXD1, which in turn directly linked to HSIL status. Besides mediation by FOXD1, both CST_I and D-LA also directly linked to HSIL status. Analysis also indicated that ILA, potentially regulated by D-LA, directly linked to HSIL status (Fig. 5). Additionally, ILA, CST_I, and CST_IV directly linked to LSIL-enriched SPON1, suggesting their influence on cervical SPON1 expression. Moreover, HSIL-enriched TP63 and LSIL-enriched CD19 directly linked to SPON1, implicating protein-protein interactions in cervical gene expression profiles. Interestingly, HSIL directly linked to 8 metabolites and 4 DEGs, including LSIL-enriched SPON1 and GYS2, and HSIL-enriched PHYHIPL and GOLGA6B. This is partially consistent with previous findings indicating modulation of cervical gene expression by HPV infection. These results collectively suggest bidirectional interactions between CVM, host responses, and HPV-induced SIL.

Discussion

Accumulated evidence has highlighted associations between SIL grades, CVM compositions, and the cervicovaginal metabolome [2, 3, 22–24]. However, the intricate interactions and their combined influence on cervical host responses remain incompletely understood. This study provides compelling evidence for coordinated alterations in CVM, CVMeta, and cervical gene expression patterns across different SIL grades. Specifically, it elucidates mechanisms underlying negative correlation of L. crispatus and its derived D-LA with FOXD1 expression and HSIL development.

Consistent with previous findings, our study revealed a lower prevalence of L. crispatus-dominated communities in HSIL compared to LSIL [2–6]. Furthermore, prospective studies have indicated a protective role of L. crispatus against SIL progression [7, 8]. Beyond microbial compositions, our study demonstrates varied CVMeta profiles across distinct CSTs. Specifically, we identified significantly elevated levels of D-LA in CST_I compared to CST_III and CST_IV. As expected, CST_I directly links to D-LA and CST_IV in our inferred interaction networks. As previously documented, D-LA is predominantly produced by L. jensenii, L. crispatus, and L. gasseri, contributing to the maintenance of an acidic vaginal environment that confers resistance to genital pathogens like G. vaginalis [40, 41]. Recent studies have further implicated L. crispatus and its produced D-LA in enhancing epithelial barrier integrity and junctional molecule expression [42, 43]. Conversely, both G. vaginalis and HPV infection have been shown to disrupt the epithelial barrier [44–46], thereby facilitating HPV invasion and increasing the risk of persistent infection. These findings provide insights into the negative correlations between L. crispatus-derived D-LA and SIL progression [47–50].

Consistently, we identified direct links of both CST_I and D-LA to HSIL status, suggesting their regulatory roles in decreasing HSIL risk. Moreover, CST_I and D-LA directly link to HSIL-enriched FOXD1, which directly links to HSIL status. To validate inferred interactions between CST_I, D-LA, FOXD1, and HSIL status, we assessed the inhibitory effects of L. crispatus-derived D-LA on both FOXD1 expression and the growth of precancerous cervical cells. Our results indicated that L. crispatus and L. gasseri culture supernatants, enriched with D-LA, as well as purified D-LA inhibited the growth of precancerous cervical epithelial cells. However, only L. crispatus culture supernatant and D-LA significantly downregulated FOXD1 expression. This suggests that L. gasseri achieves growth inhibition through FOXD1-independent mechanisms, potentially involving alternative metabolites or direct antimicrobial effects. In contrast, L. crispatus uniquely combines growth inhibition with specific downregulation of FOXD1 expression via D-LA production, establishing a novel microbiota-metabolite-host axis. While the role of FOXD1 in SIL progression remains to be fully elucidated, accumulating evidence implicates FOXD1 in promoting tumor cell proliferation, tumor progression, and poor prognosis [51–54]. In the cervical epithelial context, FOXD1 upregulation may compromise epithelial barrier integrity by promoting mesenchymal characteristics that facilitate HPV-driven transformation. Specifically, FOXD1 can transcriptionally activate genes involved in cell migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis while suppressing epithelial adhesion molecules. The observed downregulation of FOXD1 by L. crispatus-derived D-LA suggests a mechanism whereby beneficial microbiota maintain epithelial homeostasis by preserving differentiated epithelial characteristics and suppressing pro-invasive transcriptional programs. This represents a novel microbiome-mediated mechanism for maintaining cervical epithelial integrity in the presence of oncogenic HPV infection. Therefore, our findings, together with previous studies, collectively suggest that L. crispatus-derived D-LA associate negatively with precancerous cervical lesions via the directional interaction pathway D-LA→FOXD1→HSIL. This implicates the potential of L. crispatus-derived D-LA in topical interventions targeting SIL progression. Additionally, D-LA links to TP63, which then directly links to FOXD1. The TP63 gene is one of the most frequent HPV integration sites, and its overexpression can serve as a biomarker for SIL progression risk [55–57]. Therefore, the D-LA→TP63 interaction provides another plausible explanation for the beneficial effect of D-LA in lowering the risk of SIL progression.

Besides D-LA, our analysis also indicates enrichment of amino acids, peptides, and analogues in CST_I compared to CST_III and IV communities. These metabolites have been identified as depleted in precancerous cervical dysplasia compared to HPV-positive women with lower SIL grades [7, 22, 24]. Despite the need for further understanding of the protective effects of CST_I-enriched amino acid metabolism against precancerous cervical lesions, our findings provide some insights. Specifically, ILA, a product of tryptophan metabolism, was notably enriched in CST_I compared to both CST_III and IV communities. Bayesian network analysis further suggested the modulation of D-LA on ILA, which then influenced HSIL risk. Despite limited reports validating the inferred ILA→HSIL interaction, previous studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus-derived ILA improves epithelial barrier function and inhibits tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [58–61]. These findings offer a plausible explanation for the beneficial roles of CST_I-enriched ILA in lowering the risk of SIL progression.

Bayesian network analysis also identified other molecular interactions associated with SIL grades. For instance, CST_I and ILA directly link to LSIL-enriched SPON1, which is also potentially regulated by HSIL status and TP63. However, the specific role of cervical LSIL-enriched SPON1 expression needs further research, as recent studies suggest its complex roles. Specifically, researchers using public databases have identified upregulation of SPON1 expression in cervical cancer and its potential application in predicting cervical cancer survival [62, 63]. Additionally, CST_I-enriched CD19 directly modulated both SPON1 and TP63. This is partially consistent with previous findings indicating a positive association between CD19 and survival in HPV-related cancers [64–66].

Despite the interesting findings in this study, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 74) limits deeper understanding of these data, though our cross-sectional design included comparatively more samples than previous studies [2, 19, 20]. Several important limitations caused by sample size should include: limited ability to detect small-to-moderate effect sizes, particularly in stratified analyses by CST and SIL grade, potentially leading to type II errors; reduced reliability of complex multi-omics integration methods and Bayesian network inference, particularly for rare taxa or metabolites; limited representation of population diversity, restricting external validity across different demographic groups and geographic regions; increased risk of false positive findings despite adjustment procedures, particularly concerning given the extensive feature space in omics analyses. These limitations informed our choice of exploratory statistical thresholds, emphasis on effect sizes alongside p-values, and conservative interpretation of findings. Validation in larger, multicenter cohorts with longitudinal follow-up is essential to confirm these preliminary associations and establish clinical utility. However, these limitations were partially mitigated by the application of rigorous inclusion criteria, specifically excluding participants with common vaginal infections and menopause, which are known to significantly influence CVM composition [67, 68]. Furthermore, in vitro experiments were conducted to validate key findings, enhancing the robustness of our conclusions. Second, while 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing provided valuable insights into CVM compositions, it precluded detailed analysis of intra-group heterogeneity and inter-group variations among vaginal Lactobacillus species at the strain level, which may represent distinct functional communities [12, 69]. Future studies incorporating shotgun metagenomics and metabolomics approaches are needed to address these gaps. Nevertheless, our in vitro validation experiments, based on specific Lactobacillus strains, partially offset the limitations of 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing. Lastly, although Bayesian network analysis suggested plausible linkages associated with SIL grades, causality remains to be definitively established. While our in vitro validation experiments provided valuable mechanistic insights, those results do not fully recapitulate the complex in vivo cervical microenvironment. For instance, there is absence of immune cell populations that mediate microbiota-host interactions, lack of three-dimensional tissue architecture and cell-cell contacts and missed hormonal regulation and menstrual cycle influences. Therefore, while our in vitro findings provide mechanistic plausibility for the D-LA/FOXD1 interaction, extrapolation to human clinical outcomes requires substantial caution and validation in more physiologically relevant model systems, including primary cervical epithelial cultures, organoid models and ultimately longitudinal human studies. Additionally, longitudinal studies and intervention trials based on animal models are essential to further establish causality and fully elucidate the clinical implications of these findings.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for the modulation of CVM and associated metabolome on cervical gene expression across SIL grades. Integrated Bayesian network analysis and in vitro validation experiments suggest the benefical role of L. crispatus-derived D-LA in preventing precancerous cervical lesions. While causality remains to be definitively established, these findings open several translational avenues: (1) development of targeted probiotic formulations enriched with D-LA-producing L. crispatus strains for cervical health maintenance; (2) investigation of topical D-LA applications as adjuvant therapy for managing low-grade cervical lesions; (3) evaluation of FOXD1 expression as a biomarker for assessing microbiota-mediated protection against SIL progression. These applications could ultimately inform precision medicine approaches for HPV-positive women at varying risks of cervical disease progression.
