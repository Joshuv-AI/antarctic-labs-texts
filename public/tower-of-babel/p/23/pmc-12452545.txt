Value of dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI in the diagnosis of acute radiation-induced rectal injury in patients with rectal cancer: A comparison with endoscopy
Authors: Wen-jie Fan, Yu-ru Ma, Quan-meng Liu, Ning Zhang, Yi-yan Liu, Zi-qiang Wen, Bao-lan Lu, Jian-peng Yuan, Shen-ping Yu, Yan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the diagnostic value of dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) quantitative parameters in acute radiation-induced rectal injury (RRI) among patients with rectal cancer.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients confirmed to rectal cancer who underwent rectal MRI (including a DCE-MRI sequence) and endoscopy after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy from November 2014 to July 2022. The enrolled patients were divided into an acute RRI group and a non-acute RRI group based on Vienna rectoscopy score. Two radiologists independently measured DCE-MRI quantitative parameters (including the forward volume transfer constant [Ktrans], rate constant [kep], and fractional extravascular extracellular space volume [ve]) and thickness of rectal wall. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to analyze statistically significant parameters.ResultsForty-nine patients (median age, 58 years; interquartile range, 14 years; 34 men) were enrolled, 28 of whom were in the acute RRI group. Ktrans in patients with acute RRI was significantly lower compared to those without acute RRI (0.049 min−1 vs 0.107 min−1; P < 0.001). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of Ktrans was 0.80. With a Ktrans cutoff value of 0.079 min−1, the sensitivity and specificity were 93 % and 67 %, respectively.ConclusionKtrans demonstrated moderate performance in diagnosing acute RRI, providing a non-invasive and objective basis for managing and treating rectal cancer patients with acute RRI.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Radiation injuries, Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, Magnetic resonance imaging, Perfusion imaging

Introduction

The incidence and mortality of rectal cancer are higher, with an earlier age of onset and a more advanced stage at the time of diagnosis in recent years [1]. Total mesorectal excision following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy is an important treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer [2]. However, radiotherapy can easily cause rectal injuries and impact patients’ quality of life. For patients with rectal cancer who received preoperative radiotherapy, the occurrence of acute radiation-induced rectal injury (RRI) within 3 months after completing radiotherapy will increase the risk of complications such as anastomotic leakage and stenosis following anal preservation radical surgery [3]. Additionally, it will aggravate symptoms of severe postoperative low anterior resection syndrome and affect long-term rectal and anal function of patients [4]. Due to the self-limiting nature of acute RRI [5], surgeons may appropriately delay surgery for patients with acute RRI after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy based on its status. Additionally, extended resection can be considered for patients with acute RRI to maintain blood supply at the proximal incision and reduce the risk of anastomotic fistula [6], [7], [8]. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the presence of acute RRI before surgery to determine the overall treatment strategy.

Endoscopy is currently the most common method for diagnosing RRI. However, it is an invasive procedure that may cause mucosal bleeding, perforation, fistula, and other adverse reactions. Moreover, it is challenging to visualize deep lesions of the intestinal wall and pass through huge masses or narrow intestinal cavities caused by chemoradiotherapy [9], [10], [11]. Non-invasive MRI can not only provide a comprehensive assessment of rectal cancer but also detect the presence of secondary RRI after chemoradiotherapy. The typical manifestation of acute RRI is intestinal wall thickening due to submucosal edema, which appears as submucosal hypersignal on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and layered enhancement on enhanced scan [12], [13], [14].

The main pathological feature of acute RRI is a submucosal vascular lesion [11], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) is a widely used functional imaging technique in rectal cancer imaging, which can reflect tissue blood flow and microvascular permeability through its quantitative perfusion parameters. This ability is of great value in evaluating tissue microcirculation [20], [21]. Therefore, we speculate that DCE-MRI has the potential to detect radiation-induced microvascular changes in the rectal wall and thus maybe detect acute RRI more sensitively than T2WI.

This study aimed to investigate the diagnostic value of DCE-MRI quantitative parameters in acute RRI.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was waived. From November 2014 to July 2022, 135 consecutive patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for rectal cancer and had post-treatment MRI within 3 months after completing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy were considered for inclusion. The exclusion criteria were as follows: MRI sequences lacking DCE-MRI sequence (n = 2), did not undergo endoscopy within 3 months after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (n = 80), and involvement of the entire rectal wall by tumor on DCE-MRI (n = 4). Finally, 49 patients (median age, 58 years; interquartile range, 14 years; 34 men) were included (Fig. 1).

Flow chart for patients’ enrollment.

Therapeutic schedule

All patients received long-term radiotherapy with a total dose of 50–60 Gy for the gross tumor volume (primary tumor and positive lymph nodes), delivered in 25 daily fractions, along with concurrent chemotherapy. The chemotherapy regimens included mFOLFOX (leucovorin + fluorouracil + oxaliplatin), CapeOX (capecitabine + oxaliplatin), or capecitabine monotherapy.

MRI acquisition

An appropriate amount of coupling gel (20–80 mL) was administered into the rectum prior to MRI examination. To inhibit intestinal peristalsis, 20 mg of raceanisodamine hydrochloride was intramuscularly injected approximately 10 min before imaging, unless contraindicated.

MRI was conducted on a 3.0-T scanner (Magnetom Verio; Siemens Healthcare) using a 6-channel phased-array surface coil in combination with the built-in spine coil. The rectal MRI protocols included sagittal, coronal, and oblique axial (perpendicular to the tumor base) high-spatial-resolution T2WI acquired via turbo spin-echo sequence, as well as oblique axial T1 mapping and DCE-MRI. Before administration of the contrast agent, an oblique axial T1 mapping was obtained using a volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination sequence with two flip angles (2° and 14°) to determine the T1 relaxation time in the tissue before the arrival of contrast agent. Subsequently, a three-dimensional dynamic T1-weighted sequence based on a time-resolved technique with interleaved stochastic trajectories was used to perform DCE-MRI. Following five pre-contrast phases, DCE-MRI was initiated simultaneously with the intravenous bolus injection of 0.1 mmol/kg gadopentetate dimeglumine at a rate of 3.0 mL/s, followed by a saline flush of 25 mL. A total of 75 sequential dynamic phases were acquired with a temporal resolution ranging from 4.25 s. The details of the MRI protocols are provided in Table E1.

Image analysis

The DCE-MRI data were post-processed using Tissue 4D software on an MRI workstation (Syngo MultiModality Workplace, VE40A, Siemens Healthcare). Two radiologists (with 2 and 7 years of experience in rectal MRI, respectively) who were blinded to the endoscopy information jointly determined the optimal rectal wall on DCE-MRI guided by oblique axial T2WI. The selection of the optimal intestinal wall necessitates fulfilling two criteria simultaneously: it must allow for clear delineation of the intestinal wall while ensuring a distance from tumor. Subsequently, the DCE-MRI quantitative parameters (including forward volume transfer constant [Ktrans], rate constant [kep], and fractional extravascular extracellular space volume [ve]) as well as rectal wall thickness of the optimal intestinal wall were independently evaluated by two radiologists. Before the study, the two readers underwent a training session involving five MRI cases (not part of the study sample), aimed at standardizing the quantitative measurements. Each radiologist performed all steps of DCE-MRI data postprocessing separately, including the specific steps (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). First, T1 map was reconstructed using the variable flip angle method. Automatic motion correction was performed for DCE-MRI. And if excessive rectal motion occurred during the scanning process, the affected phases of DCE-MRI would be manually removed. The T1 map was aligned to the motion-corrected DCE-MRI. Second, a volume of interest (VOI) was manually delineated on the DCE-MRI to include the target rectum and mesorectum while excluding adjacent bladder regions. The arterial input function of the VOI, selected from the three population-averaged options (fast, intermediate, and slow) based on the smallest chi-square value, was subsequently employed to generate a Ktrans map within the defined VOI by fitting the Tofts model. Third, a region of interest (ROI) encompassing at least one-third of the circumferential rectal wall was manually drawn with guidance from oblique axial T2WI. Finally, the ROI was copied to the Ktrans map for computation of mean values of Ktrans, kep, and ve. For all measurements, the average between the two radiologists was used for data analyses.

Endoscopic evaluation

The typical endoscopic manifestations of RRI include mucosal hyperemia, telangiectasia, ulceration, stenosis, and necrosis. During the acute phase, mucosal hyperemia and telangiectasia are the predominant features [22]. The severity and extent of endoscopic lesions play a crucial role in evaluating the condition. Currently, the Vienna rectoscopy score is widely used as an endoscopic scoring system (Table E2 and E3) [22]. Based on this score, an experienced endoscopist (with 19 years of experience) assessed the status of acute RRI and categorized the enrolled patients into two groups: those with acute RRI (score > 0) and those without acute RRI (score = 0) (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), as the aim of our study was to determine the presence of acute RRI before surgery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM) and MedCalc statistical software (version 15.8; MedCalc Software bvba; https:// www. medca lc. org; 2015). The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as means ± standard deviations and compared using independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as numbers with percentages and analyzed using either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed to evaluate the statistically significant parameters, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated along with a 95 % confidence interval (CI). Interobserver agreement for quantitative parameters measurements between the two radiologists was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a 95 % CI (ICC < 0.50, poor agreement; ICC = 0.50–0.75, moderate agreement; ICC = 0.76–0.90, good agreement; ICC = 0.91–1.00, excellent agreement) [23]. Taking into account one-sided α = 0.05, (1-β) = 0.8, and effect size = 0.8, it was determined that the minimum required sample size was 42 (group 1 = 21, group 2 = 21) using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University).

Diagnostic value of Ktrans in acute radiation-induced rectal injury

The AUC was 0.80 (95 % CI: 0.66, 0.90), indicating that Ktrans had a moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing acute RRI from non-actue RRI. Using a Ktrans cutoff value of 0.079 min−1, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing acute RRI were 93 % (26 out of 28) and 67 % (14 out of 21), respectively. Discussion

The presence of acute RRI not only diminishes patients’ quality of life but also hampers anastomotic healing following surgery [16], [20], [24], [25]. Accurate preoperative diagnosis of acute RRI is crucial for optimizing management and treatment strategies [26].

In this study, we utilized DCE-MRI quantitative parameters to assess the status of acute RRI in patients with rectal cancer following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Our results revealed a significantly lower Ktrans value in the acute RRI group compared to the non-acute RRI group (0.049 min−1 vs 0.107 min−1; P < 0.001), with an AUC of 0.80. These results indicated that Ktrans was a reliable diagnostic tool for identifying the occurrence of acute RRI after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Ktrans demonstrated high sensitivity (93 %) and relatively low specificity (67 %), indicating that acute RRI was less likely to be missed but more prone to misdiagnosis. However, diagnosis of acute RRI is of great significance to reduce postoperative anastomotic complications and improve patients’ quality of life. Our previous study demonstrated that Ktrans varied between mild and severe acute RRI, as measured by pathological score [27]. However, a comparison of Ktrans values between these two groups was not feasible due to the predominance of patients with a Vienna rectoscopy score of 1, only three patients with a Vienna rectoscopy score of 2, and no patients with a Vienna rectoscopy score ≥ 3 in the acute RRI group. Therefore, it is imperative to include more patients across different Vienna rectoscopy scores for further study.

The transfer rate of contrast agent from plasma to extravascular extracellular space, known as Ktrans, is influenced by microcirculation blood flow and vascular permeability [28], with microcirculation blood flow being the primary determinant [29]. In acute RRI, there is a reduction in microvessels and a high proportion of narrow blood vessels within the submucosa, while a compensatory increase in capillaries within the mucosal layer (manifested as mucosal edema and capillary dilation during endoscopy) [15], [16]. We speculated that submucosal vascular lesions outweighed mucosal vascular hyperplasia, resulting in decreased blood flow to the rectal wall. Therefore, patients with acute RRI exhibited lower Ktrans compared to those without acute RRI.

Kep reflects the rate at which contrast agent returns from extravascular extracellular space to plasma, and this is influenced by microvascular permeability and surface area [28]. In patients with acute RRI, decreased submucosal microvessels and narrowed vessels in this layer may result in a reduction of the microvascular surface area [17]. We expected kep to be lower in the acute RRI group than in the non-acute RRI group. Our results showed that kep exhibited a decreasing trend in the acute RRI group, but it was not significant (0.318 min−1 vs 0.404 min−1; P = 0.254), possibly due to the new formation of mucosal microvessels [15]. Compared to mature microvessels, newly formed vessels have tortuous structures and increased permeability [19], thereby weakening the impact of microvascular surface area on kep.

Ve represents the fraction of extravascular extracellular space [28]. The results from this study showed that the ve in the acute RRI group was lower than that in the non-acute RRI group, but no statistical difference was observed (0.213 vs 0.250; P = 0.072). The reason might be that the extravascular extracellular space is affected by various factors of the tissue microenvironment, such as cell density, low oxygen fraction, and interstitial fluid pressure. These factors lead to the change of ve [30].

The thickness of rectal wall was observed to be greater in the acute RRI group compared to the non-acute RRI group; however, this difference did not achieve statistical significance (4.129 mm vs 3.550 mm; P = 0.275). Both acute RRI and non-acute RRI patients exhibited submucosal edema in 71 % of cases, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 1.000). The change in morphology occurred subsequent to the alteration in hemodynamics, which may explain this observation [31]. As RRI is a progressive and destruction disease, Wu et al. and Cao et al. found that rectal wall thickness was greater in patients with severe chronic RRI than those with non-severe chronic RRI at the later stage of the disease (> 3 months after completing radiation) [14], [16], [32]. It should also be noted that the application of coupling gel may exert some influence on rectal wall thickness.

The above results are in line with our previous study [27], which showed that the Ktrans of the severe acute RRI group was significantly lower than that of the mild acute RRI group (0.032 min−1 vs 0.054 min−1; P = 0.008); however, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of kep, ve, rectal wall thickness, and submucosal edema (P > 0.5). Although this study grouped the patients based on the severity of acute RRI rather than whether it was acute RRI or not, the submucosal microvessels in patients with severe acute RRI were fewer, and the degree of microvascular stenosis was more severe compared to those with mild acute RRI [15], [33], just as in the comparison between acute RRI and non-acute RRI.

Our study had several limitations. First, the quantitative parameters derived from DCE-MRI were based on a single section far away from rectal cancer rather than based on the volume analysis. However, it should be noted that the DCE-MRI quantitative parameters on the rectal wall near the rectal cancer may be influenced by tumor-induced neovascularization [34]. On the other hand, the volume analysis was labor-intensive and did not easily applicable in the clinical setting. Second, this was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size from a single center and lacked a validation cohort. Further studies with larger sample sizes across multiple centers should be conducted to validate our results and may enhance the statistical power.

In conclusion, the quantitative parameter Ktrans obtained by DCE-MRI demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance for acute RRI in patients with rectal cancer. Therefore, Ktrans can serve as a simple, non-invasive, and objective imaging method to diagnose acute RRI and provide an objective basis for preoperative management and treatment strategies, such as delaying surgery or performing extended resection. Future large-scale prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate our findings.
