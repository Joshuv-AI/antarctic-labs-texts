Development of a Classifier for Metabolic Subtypes of Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma to Guide Personalized Immunotherapy Strategies: Biomarker Analysis of the Phase III CONTINUUM and DIPPER Trials
Authors: Sai-Wei Huang, Ye-Lin Liang, Ya-Lan Tao, Wen-Fei Li, Guan-Qun Zhou, Yan-Ping Mao, Rui Guo, Lei Chen, Sha Xu, Xu Liu, Ning Zhang, Fang Liu, Liang-Fang Shen, Yang-Ying Zhou, Ya-Wei Yuan, Guo-Rong Zou, Feng Jin, Ling-Long Tang, Ying Sun, Ying-Qin Li, Jun Ma, Na Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEPersonalized immunotherapy strategies are urgently needed for patients with locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). We aim to identify biomarkers predictive of immunotherapy benefits, using data from the phase III CONTINUUM (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03700476) and DIPPER (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03427827) randomized clinical trials.PATIENTS AND METHODSTumor samples from 407 patients in the CONTINUUM (discovery cohort) and DIPPER (validation cohort) trials were subjected to RNA sequencing. In the discovery cohort, metabolic gene-based consensus clustering was performed to determine subtypes. A machine learning–based classifier was subsequently developed in the discovery cohort and then applied to the validation cohort to assign metabolic subtypes. Gene set enrichment analyses were used to characterize the biological features of each metabolic subtype. The clinical end point was event-free survival (EFS).RESULTSIn the discovery cohort, three metabolic subtypes were identified with distinct tumor-intrinsic and immune features as well as differential EFS benefits from adding anti–PD-1 to chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Specifically, the MS1 subtype exhibited a significant improvement in 3-year EFS in the anti–PD-1 plus CRT arm compared with the CRT-alone arm (3-year EFS, 90.2% v 69.6%; hazard ratio, 0.27 [95% CI, 0.11 to 0.67]), whereas MS2 (3-year EFS, 94.1% v 93.8%) and MS3 subtypes (3-year EFS, 75.0% v 75.0%) derived no significant survival benefit. The subtype features were preserved in the validation cohort, with consistent prognostic and predictive value. A pooled analysis of both cohorts demonstrated the significant interaction between metabolic subtypes and the treatment effect (Pinteraction = 0.0074).CONCLUSIONIn this biomarker study, we defined metabolic subtypes of NPC that predicted the EFS benefit from immunotherapy. This novel molecular classification provides a promising predictive biomarker for personalized treatment decision for patients with locoregionally advanced NPC.

INTRODUCTION

Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has revolutionized the paradigm of cancer treatment. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is characterized by abundant immune cell infiltration, with over 80% of patients showing PD-L1 expression,1-3 making it a candidate for ICI treatment. Two multicenter phase III randomized trials, CONTINUUM and DIPPER,4,5 have demonstrated a remarkably improved event-free survival (EFS) with the addition of PD-1 blockades to standard chemoradiotherapy (CRT) in locoregionally advanced NPC (LA-NPC), supporting this combination as a promising new standard of care. However, approximately 15% of patients failed to show durable responses and suffered from relapse after anti–PD-1 therapy, whereas 4%-10% of patients experienced severe immune-related adverse events,4,5 underscoring the urgent need for robust biomarkers to guide treatment selection.

While various biomarkers, including PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI), have been proposed to predict response to ICIs in several cancers,6,7 their utility in NPC remains inconclusive. Notably, survival benefits from PD-1 blockade therapy have been consistently observed across all PD-L1 expression subgroups.2,4,5,8 Moreover, hotspot gene mutations are hardly found in NPC,9 and the prevalence of mismatch repair deficiency is also quite rare,10 limiting the clinical application of biomarkers such as TMB and MSI. Our previous transcriptomic analysis in patients with NPC treated with CRT, but not immunotherapy, identifies three distinct immune subtypes: active immune subtype (A-IS), evaded immune subtype (E-IS), and nonimmune subtype (non-IS). In an exploratory cohort of 32 patients receiving PD-1 blockade therapy, the A-IS subtype is associated with better short-term radiographic response.11 However, short-term response does not consistently correlate with long-term survival outcomes,12 prompting further evaluation of the prognostic and predictive value of these immune subtypes and investigation into additional potential biomarkers of survival benefit from immunotherapy.

Metabolic reprogramming is one of the hallmarks of cancer, and it affects not only cancer cell function but also immune cell activity, comprising the complicated metabolic interaction in the tumor microenvironment (TME).13,14 Metabolic products also participate in epigenetic and post-translational modifications, thereby regulating gene expression and cellular functions.15 NPC harbors specific metabolic alterations, including enhanced glycolysis, nucleotide synthesis, and lipid metabolism,16 which contribute to immunosuppressive TME.17 However, the full metabolic landscape of NPC remains unclear, highlighting the need for comprehensive research into the metabolic heterogeneity and the crosstalk between tumor and immune cells. Such studies could be crucial for understanding the mechanisms of immunotherapy responses and may help with the stratification of patients for efficacious immunotherapy.

In this biomarker analysis of the CONTINUUM and DIPPER trials, we characterize three metabolism-based subtypes of NPC and present, to our knowledge, the first clinically validated classifier that could predict EFS benefit from immunotherapy for each patient with LA-NPC, paving the way for more personalized and effective immunotherapy strategies.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Participants

We prospectively collected pretreatment tumor samples from two multicenter, phase III, randomized controlled trials in LA-NPC, the CONTINUUM (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03700476) and DIPPER (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03427827) trials, which served as discovery and validation cohorts, respectively. Patients were histopathologically diagnosed with T4N1M0 or T1-4N2-3M0, according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition staging system. The CONTINUUM trial evaluated anti–PD-1 plus CRT versus CRT alone, whereas the DIPPER trial assessed adjuvant anti–PD-1 versus observation after curative CRT. Treatment arms were summarized as CRT with additional anti–PD-1 immunotherapy (aPD1-CRT arm) or standard CRT alone (CRT arm). Both trials incorporated biomarker analyses based on pretreatment paraffin-embedded tumor samples. We identified metabolic subtypes from the CONTINUUM cohort and then validated these subtypes in the DIPPER cohort by developing a machine learning–based classifier. The protocols for both trials and biomarker analysis were approved by the institutional ethics review board at each participating center (B2018-107, B2017-097, B2025-231-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study is reported following the REMARK guidelines.

Specimen Processing and Development of the Molecular Subtype Classifier

All available samples underwent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). In the discovery cohort, the subtypes were defined by metabolic gene-based consensus clustering using the partitioning around medoids method. A machine learning–based classifier was trained to classify tumors into distinct subtypes. For each patient in the validation cohort, the classifier was applied individually to predict the metabolic subtype. Details of specimen processing and development of the molecular subtype classifier are given in the Data Supplement (Supplement Methods).

Outcomes

The primary end point was EFS, defined as the time from random assignment to disease relapse (distant or locoregional) or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Treatment responses were evaluated using laryngopharyngeal endoscopy and magnetic resonance imaging according to RECIST 1.1. A complete biological response was defined as undetectable Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-DNA after induction therapy.18,19 Biomarker analyses were designed to assess the prognostic and predictive value of metabolic subtype in terms of EFS.

Statistical Analysis

Details of bioinformatics analysis are given in the Data Supplement (Supplement Methods). Wilcoxon tests or t tests were used for two-group comparisons. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare three or more independent groups. Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were used to examine association and dependency among categorical variables. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson's or Spearman's coefficients. The predictive performance was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of multiclass classifications using the R package multiROC (v1.1.1).

EFS was estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test using survminer (v0.4.9) and survival packages (v3.5-5). Unstratified hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated using an unstratified univariable Cox model. A pooled analysis of the two trials was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model stratified by trial. The model was adjusted for clinical covariates, including the T category, N category, and baseline EBV-DNA level. Interaction effects were assessed using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the treatment-by-subtype interaction term. All P values were two-sided, and P values < .05 were considered statistically significant, except for interaction tests, where P < .1 was used.20-22 P values for multiple comparisons were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. All analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.3).

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

In the CONTINUUM trial, a total of 187 patients (aPD1-CRT, n = 99; CRT, n = 88) were available as the discovery cohort. In the DIPPER trial, 220 patients (aPD1-CRT, n = 116; CRT, n = 104; Fig 1) were designated as the validation cohort. In the CONTINUUM trial, compared with non–biomarker-evaluable population (BEP) patients, the BEP patients were younger and had a higher proportion of N3 disease and a lower proportion of T4 disease, but an overall similar distribution of stage IV (70%). In addition, the BEP patients had lower rates of unknown EBV-DNA levels and PD-L1 expression. In the DIPPER trial, baseline characteristics were largely comparable between BEP and non-BEP patients. In both trials, 3-year EFS and treatment effects were consistent between BEP and non-BEP patients (Data Supplement, Table S1), supporting the representativeness of the BEP populations in this study.

Identification of Metabolic Subtypes

We first evaluated the efficacy of our previous immune classification11 and found that the immune subtyping could not predict long-term survival benefit from anti–PD-1 therapy in both CONTINUUM and DIPPER trials (Data Supplement, Fig S1). Given that patients who experienced tumor progression predominantly exhibited metabolic dysregulation (Data Supplement, Fig S2), we sought to establish metabolic subtypes for predicting survival benefit from ICIs. An unbiased consensus clustering based on metabolism-related genes was used to identify NPC subtypes using 187 patient samples of the CONTINUUM trial. This analysis identified three distinct metabolic subtypes (MS1-MS3; Data Supplement, Fig S3 and Table S2), accounting for 63.1% (118 of 187), 17.6% (33 of 187), and 19.3% (36 of 187) of the patients, respectively.

To characterize the molecular features of each subtype, we performed single sample gene set enrichment analysis by metabolism pathways from the KEGG database (Data Supplement, Fig S4). The MS1 subtype was metabolism-intermediate, exhibiting moderate activity in metabolic pathways and enriched in genes associated with amino acid catabolism, such as glutaminase (GLS; Fig 2A). The MS2 subtype was metabolism-specialized, characterized by strong enrichment in unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, particularly those involving linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig 2B). The MS3 subtype was metabolism-hyperactivated, displaying a globally activated metabolic transcriptional profile, with higher expression of genes associated with amino acid (methionine, serine, and arginine) and energy (glycolysis, citrate cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation) metabolism, suggesting a highly proliferative nature of these tumors (Figs 2A and 2B; Data Supplement, Fig S4). To evaluate the reproducibility of the metabolic subtypes, clustering analysis was additionally performed using k-means and non-negative matrix factorization. Both methods captured subtypes with highly concordant metabolic pathway profiles (Data Supplement, Fig S5), underscoring the robustness of these metabolic patterns across alternative clustering algorithms.

Across the three metabolic subtypes, no significant differences were observed in clinical features including age, sex, N category, or TNM stage. However, patients classified under the MS3 subtype exhibited a higher proportion of T4 disease, indicating greater tumor burdens. The expression of PD-L1 showed no significant difference among these three subtypes (Table 1), indicating that our metabolic subtypes were independent of PD-L1 expression. A significant association between prognosis and metabolic subtypes was identified in the CONTINUUM cohort, with MS2 demonstrating the longest survival (3-year EFS, 93.9% [95% CI, 86.1 to 100]) and MS3 exhibiting the shortest survival (3-year EFS, 75.0% [95% CI, 62.1 to 90.6]; Fig 2C). Intriguingly, in the CRT arm, MS2 had better prognosis, whereas MS1 and MS3 had similar and poorer outcomes. However, in the aPD1-CRT arm, MS1 showed improved outcomes compared with MS3 and similar to MS2 (Data Supplement, Fig S6).

Biological Characteristics Under Each Metabolic Subtype

We next focused on tumor-intrinsic pathways. Remarkably, MS3 tumors exhibited elevated activity in oncogenic pathways, such as EGFR, MAPK, and VEGF, as measured using the PROGENy algorithm.23 In addition, TGF-β signaling, which is related to tumor cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and enhances immunosuppressive function of regulatory T cells (Tregs),24,25 was enriched in MS3 tumors. Conversely, immune activation pathways, including NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and TRAIL, were more active in MS1 or MS2 subtypes (Data Supplement, Fig S7A). Similarly, enrichment analysis of Hallmark pathways showed that the MS1 subtype had increased enrichment scores corresponding to immune response (interferon pathways), whereas the MS3 subtype was highly enriched in malignant pathways such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition, MYC, and hypoxia signaling (Data Supplement, Fig S7B).

Immune deconvolution analysis revealed distinct TME components (Fig 3A). Compared with MS1 and MS2 subtypes, MS3 tumors exhibited broadly reduced immune cell infiltration, such as T, B, and NK cells (Data Supplement, Fig S8 and Table S3), resulting in the lowest immune score and highest tumor purity (Fig 3B). Although MS1 and MS2 subtypes showed immune-rich TMEs, MS2 tumors had more mast cells, aligning with previous studies highlighting the antitumor role of mast cells.26 By contrast, the MS1 subtype displayed a higher abundance of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and CAFs (Data Supplement, Fig S9A) and higher expression of chemotactic factors, including CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCR4, and CSF1R (Data Supplement, Fig S9B). Notably, MS1 tumors exhibited elevated N-glycan associated pathways while showing decreased O-glycan–associated pathways compared with MS2 tumors (Data Supplement, Fig S4). Consistent with previous studies linking myeloid cells to T-cell suppression via N-glycan signaling,27-29 the N-glycan pathway was positively associated with myeloid-derived suppressor cells, TAMs, and exhausted T cells (Data Supplement, Fig S9C). These results suggest that despite the abundant immune cell infiltration, MS1 tumors exhibit a more immunosuppressive TME.

To delineate gene expression patterns underlying the immune-desert MS3 subtype, weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify subtype-related gene modules (Data Supplement, Fig S10A). Genes from the MS3-specific module were enriched in DNA methylation and chromatin modification pathways (Data Supplement, Fig S10B). This was consistent with the enrichment of methylation-related metabolic pathways, including methionine, serine, and one-carbon metabolism15 in the MS3 subtype (Data Supplement, Fig S10C). MS3 tumors exhibited high expression of DNA methyltransferases and low expression of demethylases (Data Supplement, Fig S10D). In line with the transcriptional repression associated with DNA methylation,30,31 we observed downregulation of immune-related pathways in MS3 tumors (Data Supplement, Fig S11A). Gene set enrichment analysis also demonstrated decreased activity of innate immunity pathways such as those associated with viral response (Data Supplement, Fig S11B). Gene ontology network analysis further highlighted suppression of T-cell differentiation, proliferation, and lymphocyte-mediated immunity in MS3 tumors (Data Supplement, Fig S11C).

Validation of the Tripartite Metabolic Subtypes

To independently validate the metabolic subtypes, we followed the same clustering pipeline using data from the DIPPER cohort and an external public data set.32 In the DIPPER cohort, metabolic gene-based clustering consistently identified three distinct subtypes, with biological characteristics similar to those observed in the CONTINUUM cohort (Data Supplement, Fig S12). The metabolic subtypes were also successfully recapitulated in the cohort in the study by Yeo et al (Data Supplement, Figs S13A and 13B). Furthermore, the MS3 subtype was significantly enriched for ascending/ascending-descending phenotypes (indicating T3-4 primary tumors), similar to our observations in the immunotherapy cohorts (Data Supplement, Figs S13C and 13D). The independent discovery of three metabolically coherent subgroups in external data sets provides strong support for the biological robustness of this tripartite classification.

Development of a Classifier for Metabolic Subtypes

To ensure reproducibility and support clinical application, we trained a machine learning–based classifier in the training set and evaluated the performance of the classifier in the test set using data from the CONTINUUM cohort (Figs 4A and 4B; Data Supplement, Fig S14 and Table S4). The classifier performed well for metabolic subtyping in the training (accuracy, 96.9%; average AUROC, 0.98) and test (accuracy, 91.1%; average AUROC, 0.94) sets.

We then implemented this classifier to the independent DIPPER cohort to predict metabolic subtypes in single individuals. Pathway enrichment analyses confirmed that biological features of each subtype were preserved across cohorts (Data Supplement, Figs S15A and 15B), indicating that the classifier effectively captured the underlying biological features. The association of metabolic subtypes with prognosis was also observed (Data Supplement, Fig S15C).

Predictive Performance of the Classifier for Metabolic Subtype

We then investigated whether immunotherapy conferred differential treatment outcome across metabolic subtypes. With respect to short-term outcomes, we observed no significant differences in radiologic tumor response (assessed by RECIST) or biological response (evaluated by EBV-DNA clearance) across the three subtypes in the CONTINUUM cohort (Data Supplement, Fig S16). For long-term outcomes, patients with MS1 tumors demonstrated a notable improvement in EFS with anti–PD-1 therapy (3-year EFS, 90.2% v 69.6%; log-rank P = .0024; HR, 0.27 [95% CI, 0.11 to 0.67]), whereas MS2 and MS3 subtypes showed limited benefit compared with CRT alone (Pinteraction = 0.073; Figs 4C-4E). Consistent findings were observed in the DIPPER cohort, where MS1 patients receiving additional anti–PD-1 therapy also showed significantly improved EFS compared with CRT alone (3-year EFS, 92.5% v 74.3%; log-rank P = .0015; HR, 0.25 [95% CI, 0.10 to 0.63]). No significant EFS benefit was observed in MS2 or MS3 subtypes (Pinteraction = 0.090; Figs 4F-4H).

We then performed a pooled analysis of all BEP patients from the two trials. MS1 patients displayed significant EFS improvement in the aPD1-CRT arm compared with CRT alone (stratified HR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.14 to 0.50]), whereas no EFS benefit was observed in MS2 and MS3 patients (Fig 5; Data Supplement, Fig S17). Further examination of the predictive utility of the metabolic subtype indicated a significant interaction between the metabolic subtype and anti–PD-1 immunotherapy (Pinteraction = 0.0074; Fig 5), which remained predictive after multivariable adjustment (adjusted Pinteraction = 0.005; Data Supplement, Table S5). Notably, we also conducted interaction tests between the treatment arm and baseline clinical features and found no factors predictive of EFS benefit (Fig 5). Similarly, despite a differential distribution of our previous immune subtypes across metabolic groups (Data Supplement, Fig S18A), no significant interaction was observed between metabolic and immune subtypes (Pinteraction = 0.4). Furthermore, the previously defined immune subtypes did not confer additional predictive value beyond that provided by the metabolic subtypes (Data Supplement, Fig S18B). Taken together, the metabolic subtype can significantly and independently predict differential survival benefit from the addition of anti–PD-1 immunotherapy to standard CRT in patients with LA-NPC.

DISCUSSION

In this biomarker study of the phase III CONTINUUM and DIPPER trials, we proposed a novel metabolic classification of NPC with distinct biological features and differential benefits from immunotherapy. To enhance clinical applicability, a machine learning–based classifier was developed for direct application to individual patients and was validated to effectively capture the biological variations and differentiated treatment benefits.

Induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent CRT is the recommended treatment for LA-NPC.33,34 Recently, the CONTINUUM and DIPPER studies have demonstrated that adding PD-1 blockade to CRT prolongs EFS. However, not all patients benefit from immunotherapy, and its use may increase treatment-related toxicity. There is an urgent need to identify biomarkers to guide patient selection for immunotherapy. In this study, we identified three metabolic subtypes of NPC with distinct metabolic and immune features, leading to different outcomes and immunotherapy benefits. MS1 tumors displayed intermediate metabolic activity but high GLS expression, which is essential for CD8+ T-cell activation in response to PD-1 blockade.35 MS2 tumors featured unsaturated fatty acid metabolism and mast cell enrichment, indicating the role of mast cells in mediating favorable prognosis in NPC. MS3 tumors reflected a globally activated metabolic state, suggesting rapid proliferation and high metabolic demand. Diverse genomic and epigenomic mechanisms may underlie distinct phenotypic manifestations. In MS1 tumors, heightened NF-κB pathway activity likely represents a genomic hallmark of NPC as mutations within this pathway lead to sustained activation, thereby fostering an inflammatory and immunosuppressive microenvironment.36-38 Conversely, elevated DNA methylation and chromatin modification pathways in MS3 tumors suggest that epigenetic dysregulation serves as the primary phenotypic driver.39-41 Integrated analysis of paired whole-exome sequencing and DNA methylation data in future studies will help clarify the molecular etiologies of these subtypes.

Moreover, this novel classification potentially informs individualized treatment strategies for patients with NPC regardless of concurrent or adjuvant anti–PD-1 therapy. Patients with MS1 tumors derived benefits from immunotherapy and are thus suitable for the combination of anti–PD-1 and standard CRT. Patients with MS2 tumors, who exhibited favorable prognosis regardless of immunotherapy, may be adequately managed with standard CRT alone. These patients do not require immunotherapy, thus avoiding potential side effects. MS3 tumors exhibited a high enrichment of methylation-related metabolic pathways, elevated expression of DNA methyltransferases, and resistance to immunotherapy. DNA hypermethylation is one of the key mechanisms contributing to immune exclusion across multiple cancer types, potentially underlying the immune desert phenotype observed in MS3 tumors. Epigenetic therapies, including DNA-demethylating agents and histone deacetylase inhibitors, have been shown to promote immune infiltration into immune desert tumors, thereby boosting the efficacy of immunotherapy in both preclinical models and clinical trials.42-44 Therefore, for patients with MS3 tumors, combining PD-1 blockade with epigenetic drugs may overcome immune resistance and offer a promising therapeutic strategy.

Finally, the classifier developed in this study holds potential for clinical translation. Given the increasing integration of next-generation sequencing into clinical diagnostics and treatment decision making across various cancer types, gene expression–based subtyping is becoming increasingly feasible. To bridge the gap between research and clinical application, we have developed a user-friendly web-based tool45 that allows researchers to obtain metabolic subtype classifications for individual patients by inputting gene expression matrices. Moreover, our classifier operates independent of cohort-based clustering, enabling predictions at the individual patient level. This feature will facilitate in-depth research in precision oncology. For instance, it can help explore potential shifts in metabolic subtypes between primary and recurrent or metastatic tumors. Furthermore, the flexibility of the classifier will enhance its applicability in prospective clinical trials, where it can be used for patient stratification in the study design. Such applications will be essential for validating its role in guiding individualized treatment strategies and ultimately supporting its integration into routine clinical practice.

Limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the classifier was developed in patients from endemic regions, and its generalizability to nonendemic populations needs further validation. Second, whether the metabolic subtypes can identify patients who would benefit from other immunotherapeutic agents requires further investigation. Third, the performance of the classifier based on data from other sequencing platforms needs further validation, and a more streamlined classifier is warranted. Finally, the underlying mechanisms by which metabolism influences response to immunotherapy, regardless of the treatment sequence—whether concurrent or adjuvant—remain unclear and warrant further studies.

In conclusion, in this biomarker study of two phase III randomized controlled trials, a novel metabolic classifier of NPC was established to classify patients into three subtypes with distinct biological characteristics and differential survival benefits from immunotherapy. This machine learning–based classifier may serve as an effective tool for guiding treatment selection and advancing personalized therapy in NPC.
