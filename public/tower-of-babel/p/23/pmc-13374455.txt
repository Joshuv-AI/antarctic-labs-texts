What is the impact of modest increases in physical activity on the future burden of diabetes in Brazil? A modelling study and projection analysis from 2026 to 2050
Authors: Natan Feter, Karoline dos Santos Rodrigues, Danilo De Paula, Lauren Scherer, Jayne Feter, Natália Schröeder, Yohana Vieira, Juliana Q Rocha, Larissa Cunha, Luísa S Da Silva, Gabriela W Lopes, Eduardo L Caputo, Maria Inês Schmidt, Samuel C Dumith, Airton J Rombaldi, Bruce B Duncan, Daniel Umpierre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is a major global public health challenge, with rapidly rising incidence, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Physical activity is a well-established protective factor against type 2 diabetes (T2DM); however, the population-level impact of realistic modest increases in physical activity remains unclear.ObjectivesTo estimate the burden of T2DM attributable to physical inactivity in Brazil and project the impact of modest increases in physical activity.Research design and methodsWe calculated the population attributable fractions (PAFs) and economic costs of diabetes due to physical inactivity in Brazil. We estimated the incidence of T2DM from 2026 to 2050 using data from the National Health Survey (2013 and 2019) and the Global Burden of Disease Study. The relative risk of incident diabetes across the physical activity continuum was extracted from a previous meta-analysis. We modelled scenarios simulating gradual increases in leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) and resistance exercise (RE) and projected economic impacts through 2050.ResultsFrom 2026 to 2050, 11.7% (95% uncertainty interval, UI 9.5% to 14.1%) of incident T2DM cases in Brazil (~3.7 million) might be attributable to physical inactivity, with higher PAFs among adults with lower education (PAF: 13.7%; 95% UI 11.1% to 16.4%) and in Mixed Race (12%; 95% UI 9.6% to 14.3%) and Black (11.9%; 95% UI 9.5% to 14.2%) populations. In a nonlinear dose-response association, increasing LTPA by 10, 20 or 30 min/day among inactive individuals could prevent 7.0% (2.20 million), 12.9% (4.05 million) and 16.0% (5.02 million) of incident cases, respectively, by 2050. For RE, the same daily increases could prevent 12.0% (3.80 million), 18.7% (5.87 million) and 22.5% (7.07 million) of cases. These increments could significantly reduce healthcare costs, starting at R$1.9 billion for the 10 min LTPA scenario, with the greatest impact observed from RE.ConclusionsPromoting small increases in physical activity could yield substantial health and economic benefits. Equitable public health strategies are essential to address disparities and halt the growing burden of diabetes in Brazil.

Background

Diabetes is a major global public health issue, with high prevalence, substantial disease burden and escalating healthcare costs.1 2 In Brazil, diabetes prevalence has increased markedly in recent decades and is projected to continue rising between 2019 and 2050.1 3 Most of this increase is driven by modifiable risk factors rather than demographic change.3 Notably, modifiable exposures account for more than half of the diabetes burden in Tropical Latin America, including Brazil, with physical inactivity ranking as the third leading contributor based on population attributable fraction (PAF), following only high body mass index and dietary risks.4

A growing body of evidence demonstrates an inverse association between physical activity and the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).57 Meta-analyses have characterised this relationship as nonlinear, with the greatest risk reductions occurring at the lower end of the activity spectrum.6 7 Furthermore, changes in physical activity levels have been shown to improve glycaemic control in a dose-response manner, and supervised aerobic exercise at low-to-moderate volume yields significant clinical benefits.8 9 This biological plausibility, where even small increases in physical activity volume may meaningfully improve glycaemic control and lower diabetes risk, supports investigating modest, scalable doses for population-level prevention.

Globally, up to 13% of T2DM cases in men and 29% in women have been attributed to physical inactivity.10 11 Because eliminating physical inactivity is unrealistic at the population level, estimating the impact of feasible behavioural changes, such as modest increases in daily activity, may better inform public health strategies. This approach is particularly relevant for low- and middle-income countries, where diabetes incidence is projected to rise by up to 83% over the next 25 years.1

Despite well-established evidence supporting physical activity for T2DM prevention,57 the population-level impact of different physical activity modalities on diabetes incidence remains poorly quantified. Resistance exercise has been associated with T2DM prevention12 13 and improvements in glycaemic control14 15 and muscle mass,16 all of which are key mechanisms for reducing diabetes risk. By targeting skeletal muscle adaptations, resistance exercise enhances glucose uptake, oxidation and storage,17 18 yet its potential impact at low, scalable doses on the future burden of T2DM has not been examined.

Beyond individual behaviour, social determinants of health play a critical role in shaping T2DM risk. Socioeconomic position, neighbourhood characteristics, food environments, healthcare access and social context all contribute to disparities in diabetes incidence and outcomes.19 As one of the most socially unequal countries in the world,20 Brazil offers a unique context to examine how physical inactivity and social inequalities interact to drive the future burden of T2DM. Effective and equitable strategies to curb the projected diabetes burden in Brazil are urgently needed. Accordingly, we aimed to: (1) quantify the impact of physical inactivity on T2DM incidence from 2026 to 2050; (2) estimate the associated direct and indirect economic costs and (3) model the potential impact of feasible increases in physical activity, including resistance exercise, on future diabetes burden.

Methods

Study design

This study is part of the IMPACT-D (Impact of Physical Activity and Practices on the Analysis of Behavior and Social Determinants in Type 2 Diabetes) study, which investigates the impact of 24-hour movement behaviours (ie, physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep) and their interaction with social determinants of health on the risk of T2DM and related complications. The present analysis focused specifically on estimating the impact of physical inactivity on the burden of T2DM in Brazil. To achieve this, the study was divided into three phases: (1) data extraction, (2) PAF calculation and (3) estimation of the burden on current and projected T2DM cases.

Data extraction

As illustrated in figure 1, the estimation of PAF requires two key inputs: the prevalence of exposure (ie, physical inactivity) and the relative risk (RR) of the outcome (ie, T2DM) associated with the exposure.

IMPACT-D, Impact of Physical Activity and Practices on the Analysis of Behavior and Social Determinants in Type 2 Diabetes study; LTPA, leisure-time physical activity; PAF, population attributable fraction; RR, relative risk.

We determined the prevalence of physical inactivity (ie, <150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity)21 among Brazilian adults using the 2019 National Health Survey,22 the most recent nationally representative survey in Brazil. To estimate the proportion of adults participating in resistance exercise and leisure-time physical activity (LTPA), we used two questions from the survey. The first question asked whether the respondent had participated in any LTPA in the 3 months before the survey. If the answer was no, we used this percentage to estimate how potential increases in LTPA could affect T2DM incidence. If yes, respondents were asked about the type of LTPA they most often engaged in during that period, with options including resistance exercise, biking, swimming and others. We used this question to determine the proportion of adults not engaging in resistance exercise as the most common LTPA by analysing the surveys’ microdata to estimate the potential impact of increases in resistance exercise on T2DM incidence. The prevalence of physical inactivity and the proportion of people not engaging in LTPA or resistance exercise in 2019 in Brazil are described in online supplemental tables 2 and 3, respectively.

To account for disparities in physical activity, prevalence estimates were stratified by sex (women, men), race and ethnicity (Black, Brown and White), and education level (less than elementary school, university degree or higher). The term ‘Brown’ was referred to as ‘Mixed Race’ hereafter to adhere to the guidelines on the reporting of race and ethnicity.23 We also used ‘race and ethnicity’ terms to align with recent guidelines.23 We focused on physical inactivity during leisure-time, which is more susceptible to influence by public health policies and yields a stronger beneficial association with health outcomes than physical activity in other domains (ie, occupational, household, commuting).24

RR were obtained from a published meta-analysis that included data from 1.8 million participants and 151 677 incident cases of T2DM across 55 prospective cohort studies.7 The authors reported the RR of T2DM for 600 MET-min/week (ie, cut-off for physical inactivity based on WHO guidelines21 versus zero MET-min/week of LTPA) (RR: 0.84; 95% CI 0.81 to 0.87), as well as sex-specific estimates. Additionally, they provided the RR per 1 hour per week (ie, 8.6 min/day) increase in LTPA and resistance exercise per week, which we used to model the potential impact of incremental increases in these activities among those reporting zero hours of LTPA in the 2019 survey. We also extracted the lower and upper limits of 95% CIs for each RR to calculate uncertainty intervals (UI) for the PAFs.

PAF calculation

We calculated the PAF of T2DM due to physical inactivity using the semi-adjusted PAF formula,25 as illustrated in figure 1. This approach combines the prevalence of exposure with RR estimates from a meta-analysis that included studies adjusted for major confounding variables, including age, sex, smoking status and body mass index. This methodology allows for estimating the disease burden in the Brazilian population while maintaining the internal validity provided by controlled risk estimates from large-scale prospective studies.25 PAFs were estimated overall and stratified by sex, race and ethnicity, and education.22 Additionally, we calculated PAFs under a counterfactual scenario, assuming a 1 hour per week increase in LTPA and resistance exercise among individuals who reported zero hours/week in the 2019 survey. Burden of T2DM

This stage included four steps: (1) estimating the number of incident cases of T2DM in Brazil from 2013 to 2019, (2) estimating the number of incident T2DM cases from 2026 to 2050, (3) projecting the economic cost of diabetes from 2026 to 2050 and (4) estimating the reduction of the cost from 2026 to 2050 by theoretical increases by 1 hour per week of LTPA and resistance exercise in the population. All estimates were calculated overall and across different strata, including sex, race and ethnicity and educational achievement.

To estimate 2025 cases, we calculated incident cases between 2013 and 2019 by subtracting prevalence and adding estimated deaths among people with diabetes during the period. Prevalence data came from the 2013 and 2019 National Health Surveys,3 combined with population sizes.26 Mortality data (2013–2019) came from the Mortality Information System via TABNET (http://tabnet.datasus.gov.br/cgi/deftohtm.exe?sim/cnv/obt10uf.def), which offers data stratified by sex, race/ethnicity and education.

T2DM prevalence rose from 6.2% (95% CI 5.9% to 6.6%) in 2013 to 7.7% (95% CI 7.4% to 8.0%) in 2019: 8.5 to 11.4 million cases, respectively. We divided the 2013–2019 incident cases by seven to estimate annual incidence, then multiplied by four and added to 2019 totals to project 2025 prevalence. This approach reflects available data constraints. Online supplemental methods 1 details the process.

To estimate the number of incident cases between 2026 and 2050, we calculated the difference in the number of cases between 2025 and 2050 and added the estimated number of deaths among people with diabetes during that period (Supplementary Methods 1). The projected prevalence in 2050 was extracted from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021.1 Projections indicate that Brazil will have 37.2 million diabetes cases by 2050.1 The projected number of deaths from 2026 to 2050 was also extracted from the GBD Foresight tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-foresight/).

The National Health Survey and the GBD estimated the prevalence of type 1 diabetes and T2DM in 2013, 2019 and 2050, respectively. However, the RR used to estimate the PAF pertains specifically to T2DM. Considering T2DM accounted for 94.0% of all diabetes cases in Brazil in 2019,1 we used 94% of the T2DM cases in 2013, 2019, and 2050 as a strategy to focus our findings on T2DM. Online supplemental table 1 describes the estimated number of people with diabetes in 2025 and 2050 in Brazil, the estimated number of deaths in people with diabetes, and the number of incident cases between 2026 and 2050.

The annual per-patient total cost of diabetes (including direct and indirect costs) was estimated at R$4178.83,27 equivalent to US$709.14, in April 2025 (https://www.bcb.gov.br/conversao). Of this total, R$2073.15 (49.6%) was paid by Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS). Online supplemental materials provide a detailed description of the annual per-patient cost estimation.

Statistical analysis

We estimated the number of incident T2DM cases and related costs attributable to physical inactivity by multiplying the PAFs by the number of incident cases and economic costs. We also modelled the theoretical impact of small increments in physical activity (any LTPA and resistance exercise specifically) on the burden of T2DM. For this estimation, we multiplied the prevalence of survey participants who reported zero minutes of LTPA or resistance exercise in 2013 and 2019 by the corresponding PAFs, assuming a 1 hour per week (ie, 8.6 min/day) increase. The same modelling strategy was applied for both the 2019 and 2050 incidence and economic burden estimates, and stratified analyses were conducted by sex, race and ethnicity and education level.

We conducted simulation-based uncertainty analyses using bootstrapping and Monte Carlo techniques. First, point estimates and 95% CI for diabetes cases in 2013, 2019 and 2050 were used to parameterise log-normal distributions, from which 100 000 random samples were drawn to simulate the distributions of case counts each year. To estimate the number of diabetes cases attributable to physical inactivity, we employed a probabilistic approach by combining simulated distributions of incident T2DM cases with PAFs. Normal distributions were defined based on reported means and SEs derived from 95% UI for both parameters. After excluding negative values to maintain validity, 10 000 simulations were generated and multiplied to yield distributions of attributable cases.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the planning, design, data collection, analysis or interpretation of results for this study

Results

The burden of T2DM due to physical inactivity in Brazil from 2026 to 2050

We estimated that 11.7% (95% UI 9.5% to 14.1%) of incident T2DM cases in Brazil from 2026 to 2050 could be attributable to physical inactivity, corresponding to approximately 3.7 million cases (95% UI 2.8 to 4.6) (online supplemental table 2). Estimated PAFs were similar between women (11.1%, 95% UI 8.9% to 13.4%) and men (12.3%, 95% UI 9.9% to 14.7%). The highest PAFs were observed among individuals who self-identified as Mixed Race (12.0%; 95% UI 9.6% to 14.3%) and Black (11.9%; 95% UI 9.5% to 14.2%), followed by White individuals (11.5%; 95% UI 9.3% to 13.8%). The PAF due to physical inactivity was highest among individuals with incomplete primary education or lower educational attainment (PAF: 13.7%, 95% UI 11.1% to 16.4%). In contrast, among those with a university degree or higher, the PAF was 8.8% (95% UI 7.1% to 10.7%)

Future diabetes burden and cost implications of physical activity in Brazil

By 2050, the GBD projects that 35 million people will be living with T2DM in Brazil, an increase of 20.3 million (95% UI 16.7 to 24.2) compared with 2025.

We observed a nonlinear dose–response association between PA increases and the number of preventable T2DM cases, with more substantial reductions in incidence as resistance exercise time increased. The effect plateaued after ~30 min/day of added resistance exercise. For LTPA, the increase in preventable cases was more linear, though with diminishing returns beyond 20 min/day. Resistance exercise produced greater reductions in diabetes incidence than any LTPA, especially between 20 and 40 additional minutes/day, where the difference was most pronounced.

Adding 10 min/day of any LTPA in adults who reported no activity in 2019 would prevent approximately 7.0% of incident T2DM cases by 2050 (95% UI 5.5 to 9.2), equivalent to 2.20 million cases (95% UI 1.7 to 2.9). Projections for higher doses showed that increasing LTPA by 20 and 30 min/day would prevent 12.9% (4.05 million) and 16.0% (5.02 million) of incident cases, respectively.

Increasing resistance exercise yielded even greater preventative potential at lower doses: a 10 min/day increase would prevent 12.1% of cases (95% UI 8.3% to 14.9%), or 1.6 million more cases than the same increase in LTPA. Furthermore, adding 20 and 30 min/day of resistance exercise would prevent 18.7% (5.87 million) and 22.5% (7.07 million) of incident cases, respectively (figure 2; online supplemental figure 1). The relative difference in preventative impact between modalities narrowed as physical activity duration increased, consistent with the nonlinear nature of the dose-response relationship.

The projected economic impact of these PA gains is substantial. If all adults who reported no activity in 2019 achieved 150 min/week (ie, 3 hours/week in our analysis) of LTPA, the projected healthcare and societal savings would total R$3.4 billion (95% UI 2.2 to 5.4) by 2050. This figure increases to R$6.6 billion (95% UI 4.1 to 9.1) if the added time is spent on resistance exercise. Of these savings, the Brazilian healthcare system (ie, SUS) would save R$1.7 billion and R$3.3 billion, respectively.

Impact of increasing physical activity on T2DM in Brazil by sex

Sex-stratified analyses revealed consistent trends, with greater absolute benefits observed in women. Among women, increasing any LTPA by 10 min/day would prevent approximately 1.5 million diabetes cases (95% UI 1.2 to 1.9), while the same increase in resistance exercise would prevent 2.4 million cases (95% UI 1.7 to 3.0) a 63% increase. Among men, the addition of 10 min/day of LTPA or resistance exercise would prevent 1.0 million (95% UI 0.8 to 1.3) and 1.9 million (95% UI 1.3 to 2.3) cases, respectively—an 86% increase for resistance exercise compared with LTPA (online supplemental figure 1).

Economic savings mirrored these patterns. For women, 10 and 20 min/day of resistance exercise would yield cost reductions of R$1.2 billion and R$2.9 billion, respectively, while for LTPA, savings were R$457 million and R$1.5 billion (figure 3). Among men, resistance exercise and LTPA were associated with savings of R$963 million and R$277 million, respectively, for 10 min/day increases.

The proportion of cases that could be prevented by adding approximately 10 min/day of LTPA and resistance exercise was similar among Black (7.2% and 12.2%, respectively), Mixed Race (7.2% and 12.3%, respectively), and White adults (6.8% and 11.9%, respectively).

Impact of increasing physical activity on T2DM in Brazil by educational attainment

PAFs were higher in individuals with less than an elementary school education than those with a university degree (online supplemental figure 3). Adding 20 min/day (ie, 2 hours/week) of LTPA or resistance exercise was associated with 2.7 million (95% UI 2.1 to 3.2) and 3.6 million (95% UI 2.7 to 4.3) preventable cases of diabetes in individuals with less than an elementary school education. Among individuals with a university degree, the same increases in physical activity were associated with 26 499 (95% UI 20 184 to 31 415) and 45 124 (95% UI 33 566 to 54 509) preventable cases, respectively.

Regarding avoidable economic costs, we observed a substantial impact of increasing LTPA and resistance exercise, especially for those with less than an elementary school education (figure 4). If adults with less than elementary school and zero minutes/week of LTPA could reach the recommended physical activity levels (ie, 150 min/week) through any LTPA or resistance exercise alone, the total avoidable cost would sum to R$2.6 billion (95% CI 1.7 to 4.1) and R$4.2 billion (95% CI 2.6 to 5.7), respectively. Increasing LTPA or resistance exercise levels by 10 min/day yielded a theoretical reduction of R$520.9 million (95% CI 330.8 to 890.5) and R$1.2 billion (95% CI 0.6 to 1.9). Among individuals with a university degree, LTPA and resistance exercise also provide significant savings, but to a lesser extent.

Discussion

This study highlights the significant burden of T2DM and its economic costs attributable to physical inactivity in Brazil, demonstrating that even modest increases in physical activity, particularly resistance exercise, can help slow the projected growth of the diabetes epidemic. By 2050, Brazil is expected to have 35 million T2DM cases, representing 3419 new cases per day since 2025 and an additional R$150 billion in healthcare costs. These alarming projections reinforce the urgent need for equitable public policies that promote physical activity across the population.

Furthermore, the potential for synergistic interactions between diet and physical activity cannot be overlooked. While physical activity independently improves insulin sensitivity, its effects are often amplified when combined with dietary improvements, such as increased fibre intake or reduced glycaemic load.28 29 For instance, physical activity-induced glucose uptake in skeletal muscle may be more efficient in the context of a high-quality diet that minimises systemic inflammation.28 Conversely, the ‘small doses’ of physical activity modelled in this study may serve as a behavioural catalyst, where individuals who successfully adopt 10–20 min of daily activity may be more inclined to improve dietary habits, leading to a compounding effect on T2DM prevention that exceeds the sum of each intervention in isolation.30 31

We have combined three major sources (ie, National Health Survey, GBD and peer-reviewed meta-analysis) of data and found evidence that small, achievable increases in physical activity, such as just 10 additional minutes/day, could prevent 2.9 million diabetes cases and save R$1.5 billion in associated costs by 2050. Unlike the unattainable goal of eliminating risk factors, this approach offers a more realistic and actionable public health strategy. Notably, recent global estimates show that we are not on track to meet the WHO’s 2030 target of a 15% relative reduction in insufficient physical activity.32 By quantifying the impact of modest increases in activity, our study presents a feasible and equity-enhancing pathway to reduce the burden of diabetes.

Importantly, the burden of diabetes attributable to physical inactivity is not evenly distributed. It is more pronounced among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, who are both more likely to be physically inactive and more susceptible to diabetes.3 22 33 Urban environments in Brazil increasingly favour designs that reduce walkability and perceived safety, which are key barriers to LTPA, especially in low-income neighbourhoods.34 35 Furthermore, resistance exercise remains largely inaccessible to these populations due to limited facilities, high costs and perceived difficulty.36 37 Data from the 2019 National Health Survey showed 92% of Brazilian adults did not engage in resistance exercise; among those with lower educational attainment, this figure rose to 99%, underscoring education as a strong correlate of resistance exercise participation.36 Public and private sector initiatives are urgently needed to reduce these disparities. Health insurance providers could integrate structured resistance exercise programmes as preventive services, potentially lowering long-term healthcare expenditures. Simultaneously, Brazil’s SUS could offer brief, supervised resistance exercise sessions and health education initiatives within primary care settings, especially considering the substantial burden of diabetes driven by physical inactivity in the public sector.

We also found that nearly one in ten diabetes cases in Brazil could be attributed to physical inactivity, with only slight differences across racial and ethnic groups. Underdiagnosis of diabetes likely affects all groups, particularly when estimates are based solely on self-reported diagnosis. However, structural inequalities may exacerbate this issue among racially marginalised populations. Data from the ELSA-Brasil study, a large, ethnically diverse cohort, revealed that the prevalence of diabetes is underestimated when based only on self-reported diagnosis, which may reflect existing barriers to timely diagnosis and care.38 These findings emphasise how race and ethnicity, under the underlying structural racism in Brazil, remain powerful indicators of unequal access to care and adverse social determinants of health in Brazil.39

Our sex-stratified analyses revealed that women would experience greater absolute health and economic benefits from modest increases in physical activity compared with men. This disparity is likely driven by a combination of biological and structural factors; biologically, women often exhibit distinct skeletal muscle metabolic profiles and hormonal influences on insulin sensitivity that modulate the protective effects of exercise.40 41 Culturally, women in Brazil frequently face higher barriers to LTPA due to the double-burden of domestic labour and professional shifts, resulting in a higher prevalence of physical inactivity.33 42 Consequently, women exhibit a more pronounced preventative potential at the population level; the substantial latent health gains suggest that mitigating these structural impediments could yield a disproportionately high reduction in disease incidence. These findings underscore the need for gender-responsive public health frameworks that integrate the specific biosocial determinants of health specific to the Brazilian context.

The education gap also plays a crucial role. The proportion of diabetes cases and associated costs attributable to physical inactivity was approximately 50% higher among individuals with only elementary education or less. These individuals are more likely to hold informal or precarious jobs, often characterised by long working hours, low wages and limited flexibility, conditions that hinder the adoption of healthy behaviours. Furthermore, national studies show that among non-white women with low levels of education and income, the prevalence of LTPA is below 10%. In contrast, among white men with higher education and income, it exceeds 45%.33 These findings illustrate the impact of social inequalities on health behaviours and reinforce that LTPA is often a social privilege. Still, our projections indicate that interventions specifically targeted at individuals with low educational attainment can provide a high return on investment. For example, increasing LTPA by 6 hours per week among individuals with higher education would prevent 527 695 incident T2DM cases, approximately three times lower than the reduction of 1.5 million incident cases achieved with just 1 hour per week among those with lower educational attainment. These findings underscore the need for equity-guided physical activity promotion strategies that account for the intersections of education, race/ethnicity and gender.

Resistance exercise emerges as a particularly potent strategy for reducing diabetes risk. It improves glycaemic control by increasing muscle mass and enhancing insulin sensitivity, mechanisms that are especially beneficial in preventing and managing diabetes.4345 Skeletal muscle is a major site for glucose disposal; therefore, increases in muscle mass and strength directly contribute to improved metabolic function.46 47 Our findings support this physiological rationale and align with two recent meta-analyses showing that high-intensity resistance exercise is associated with significantly greater reductions in glycated haemoglobin and fasting insulin levels compared with lower-intensity interventions and with other exercise modalities.45 48 Moreover, resistance exercise may offer unique advantages for populations with mobility limitations or lower aerobic capacity, expanding the reach of diabetes prevention strategies. Nevertheless, LTPA remains a cornerstone of public health promotion. Its lower entry barrier, higher potential for spontaneous engagement and broader appeal across age and fitness levels make it an essential component of scalable and inclusive physical activity interventions.

The study has limitations. First, self-reported questionnaires to measure physical activity are vulnerable to recall and social desirability bias. Also, we acknowledge that physical activity was assessed in 2019 due to the absence of more recent nationally representative data. The prevalence of physical inactivity has changed little in recent years, with a slight increase between 2010 and 2020,32 followed by a further increase during the COVID-19 pandemic.49 In addition, COVID-19 led to significant disruption in diabetes care, including in Brazil.50 51 Therefore, our findings are more likely to be underestimated than overestimated. Similarly, our projections do not explicitly account for the temporal disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). While the lack of nationally representative longitudinal data matching the National Health Survey structure precluded specific baseline adjustments, the pandemic likely exacerbated sedentary behaviour and disrupted routine diabetes screening and care. Consequently, the actual burden of diabetes in 2026 may exceed our projections. Our findings should be interpreted as conservative lower-bound estimates based primarily on prepandemic population conditions. Second, T2DM prevalence among more vulnerable groups may be underestimated due to limited access to diagnosis. Third, our calculation of PAFs was based on a formula that considers only one risk factor at a time and does not account for potential joint effects resulting in reductions in other risk factors. Fourth, a specific limitation of our modelling approach is that the effects of small increments in physical activity (eg, 10 min per day) were extrapolated from larger effect estimates (eg, 60 min per day), assuming a linear dose-response relationship. However, the actual protective effect may not scale proportionally with time, and thus our estimates may not fully reflect non-linearities in the exposure-risk relationship. Consequently, because the most substantial risk reductions typically occur when moving from total inactivity to even minimal levels of movement, our findings likely represent a conservative estimate. The true preventative impact of small doses of physical activity on future diabetes burden may be even greater than our projections suggest. Future studies should explore non-linear modelling approaches to provide more precise estimates of the impact of modest increases in physical activity on diabetes incidence and economic burden. Fifth, we used the proportion of adults not engaging in resistance exercise as the most common form of LTPA as a proxy for low participation in this specific type of activity. While this estimate is consistent with previous estimates reported in Brazil (91.8% compared with 92.1% in the present study),52 it may not accurately reflect the true prevalence of resistance exercise and could overestimate our findings on resistance exercise. Finally, projections of diabetes prevalence assume a trajectory that extrapolates current trends, while in fact major progress, too recent to be reflected in these trends, has been made in the past few years in medications to treat obesity and prevent diabetes.

One of the main strengths of this study is its long-term outlook, offering projections through 2050 that are essential for strategic public health planning. The analytical approach is reinforced by composite RR and PAFs, which enhance the reliability of the estimated preventable costs. Additionally, integrating diverse data sources, including global projections of diabetes prevalence, national-level cost estimates and large-scale meta-analyses on physical activity, adds depth and rigour to the overall analysis. The study also underscores the importance of equitable strategies to promote physical activity, recognising the central role of environment, access and social conditions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the burden of diabetes attributable to physical inactivity in Brazil is substantial, particularly among socially vulnerable groups. Rather than focusing on the unrealistic goal of eliminating physical inactivity, our results support a more feasible and equitable public health strategy: promoting small, achievable increases in physical activity, especially in resistance exercise. As Brazil faces a growing burden of diabetes and widening health disparities, implementing structural interventions can generate both health and economic benefits. Prioritising policies that support physical activity at the population level is not just a matter of individual behaviour change: it is a vital step toward reducing preventable disease and advancing health equity across the country.
