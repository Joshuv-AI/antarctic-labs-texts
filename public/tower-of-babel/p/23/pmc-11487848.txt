Funding multinational investigator-initiated clinical studies in Europe: why and how?
Authors: Marta del Álamo, Sabrina Lémeret, Cristina Nieto, Lara Pandya, Hans Hagen, Saul Walker, Jacques Demotes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigator-initiated clinical studies (IICSs), also referred to as non-commercial, academic or independent clinical studies, address important research questions that are usually neglected by industry despite their high societal value. Indeed, industry may direct their focus and resources on studies that will yield results and products that can ultimately generate revenue for the company. Conversely, IICS research questions include (a) refining or getting new indications of available treatments (drug repurposing); (b) optimisation, by comparing various health products or treatment regimens; and (c) innovation, especially for advanced therapies. Multinational IICSs increase the scientific quality of the data by exchange of research ideas, scientific techniques and tools. Participation of patients from different geographical, social and ethnic backgrounds equally adds to the value of study results and yields more generalisable evidence than a study confined to a single geographical location. Multinational IICSs are generally sponsored by non-profit/academic organisations and publicly funded. Funding has been already identified as a main challenge for the conduct IICS and especially for clinical trials (IICTs, IICS where a medical intervention is directly tested). Background

Clinical research involves testing new discoveries by carrying out carefully controlled investigations on patients, known as clinical studies. Commercially sponsored clinical studies are responsible for bringing most new drugs to the market, whereas non-commercial or academic studies have their own specific objectives, addressing research questions on clinically relevant topics that are usually neglected by industry—mostly due to the lack of market value—despite their high societal value. Indeed, IICS priorities are many times based on pressing clinical and public health problems that might be solved or mitigated through solutions with no or limited commercial value. This includes (a) refining or getting new indications of available treatments (drug repurposing) and (b) optimisation, by comparing various health products or treatment regimens (combination of drugs, different treatment dosage or schedules), comparing medical devices, comparing therapies without medicinal products (e.g. radiation or procedural interventions) and comparing diagnostic procedures and prevention measures. Indeed, many of these studies and trials that have low financial gain for industry are initiated by investigators and therefore referred to as investigator-initiated clinical studies (IICSs), including trials (IICTs), where the investigator conceives the research and develops the protocol.

IICTs, also referred to as non-commercial, academic or independent clinical trials, are generally sponsored by non-profit/academic organisations and are publicly funded. IICTs are essential when evaluating the efficacy and safety of all interventions, exploring new indications for authorised drugs (repurposing), comparing the efficacy and safety of approved healthcare strategies and looking for solutions for health conditions with a missing industry market (rare diseases, tropical diseases).

Even though they represent almost half of the clinical research activity in Europe, IICTs are mostly conducted in a single country [1]. Taking advantage of Europe’s half-billion population size, its medical expertise, high-quality healthcare systems, harmonised regulatory framework and scientific potential, enhanced multinational cooperation in clinical studies would boost clinical research and its impact in Europe. It would also (a) foster rapid patient recruitment and spread best practice, thus enhancing Europe’s competitiveness and equity for the benefit of patients and healthcare systems, and (b) allow countries with small populations and/or research communities to participate in trials for which their demography and epidemiological situation might not be sufficient to enable robust trial designs.

During the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, most of the academic clinical research response efforts were initiated, conducted and funded at the national level [2]. Later during the pandemic, more international cooperation and coordination was established [3]. In the EU, an unprecedented number of national academic clinical trials were launched to accelerate COVID-19 treatment and prevention. Despite these efforts, the lack of coordination of clinical research across the member states led to a chaotic landscape with numerous underpowered trials that could not provide meaningful results as well as a duplication of research activities [4, 5], highlighting the need to better align investments. In May 2022, the Seventy-fifth WHA (World Health Assembly) adopted resolution WHA75.8 [6] on strengthening clinical trials to provide high-quality evidence on health interventions and to improve research quality and coordination.

Recognising the key role of academia in the clinical research landscape in Europe, the EMA ACT-EU programme intends, among others, to propose pragmatic and harmonised solutions to support non-commercial sponsors setting up and conducting multinational clinical trials across the EU.

In this article, we discuss the value of IICS, what should be the objectives of publicly funded multinational IICS and what the current funding mechanisms for IICS in Europe are.

Main body

What should be the objectives of multi-country IICS supported by public funding?

Academic clinical studies address issues that are usually neglected by industry. Main examples include academic innovation, drug repurposing and comparative-effectiveness. In this section, we discuss both their suitability for public funding and the importance of the multinational component.

Academic innovation

Academic innovation focuses on the development of innovative health products (medicinal products, medical devices) intending to attain a first marketing authorisation.

The discovery and development of new therapies has and will likely continue to require contributions from academic institutions and the biopharmaceutical industry. Most new insights arise in academia, funded by public grants, foundations and institutional funds. Academic institutions identify promising discoveries and seek to initiate their development and commercialisation, eventually partnering with industry [7].

Adequate public/private funding mechanisms (Innovative Health Initiative—IHI, others), fostering early industry-academia interaction, would promote the generation of regulatory-compliant research results in academic-driven developments for novel therapeutics, leading to more efficient market access.

Drug repurposing

Drug repurposing aims to identify new uses for approved medicines in indications outside the scope of the original approval. The perspective for repurposing is different depending on the nature of the drug [8]:

Pharmaceutical companies might find a commercial interest in repurposing/repositioning in the first two cases, but they will be less prone to pursue non-clinical/clinical development in the third case.

Academic institutions are more willing to explore repurposing options for medicines out of protection. However, although involved in generating data, academic organisations may lack the expertise and resources to navigate regulatory routes and requirements, including finding the relevant Marketing Authorisation Holder (MAH) of the finished product with whom to collaborate in order to bring the indication “on label”. It has therefore been recommended to find solutions to facilitate bringing new uses for medicines “on label” by developing a collaborative framework between not-for-profit and academic organisations, patient organisations, the pharmaceutical industry, health technology assessment bodies, payers and regulators.

Comparative-effectiveness research (CER)

Comparative-effectiveness research (CER) is the generation and synthesis of evidence that compares the benefits and harms of alternative methods to prevent, diagnose, treat and monitor a clinical condition or to improve the delivery of care [9]. The goal of CER is to provide stakeholders (patients, caregivers, providers, payers, policy makers) with information that can be used to make decisions about the benefits/tradeoffs between two or more tests, treatments/interventions, care delivery systems or policies.

Indeed, pragmatic practice-oriented publicly funded clinical trials can provide answers to highly relevant research questions in healthcare, both in terms of clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, which are less likely to be answered by trials funded by the pharmaceutical and medical device industry [10].

Funding of well-selected clinical studies, especially in the above-mentioned areas/conditions, should therefore be done in the context of high-quality and well-financed multinational programmes where authorities collaborate with experts in the field to answer questions that are relevant for patients and society.

In this scenario, we expect that strategies for funding multinational IICS will evolve to mitigate these barriers, facilitating research that can provide answers to highly relevant questions in healthcare which are less likely to be answered by studies funded by the pharmaceutical and medical device industry.

Current models of public funding for multinational IICS in Europe

Most national research programmes fund clinical studies at country level. However, funding opportunities for multinational studies are limited. In general, national research funding agencies are not able to allow funds to cross borders, inhibiting international collaboration. Notable exceptions include Innovation Fund Denmark and the German Research Foundation (DFG) that consider the provision of some budget for recruiting sites out of the country.

We describe here organisations/initiatives funding multinational IICS in Europe, including some programmes that allow international collaboration beyond Europe. These funding programmes have been identified through surveys/interviews as part of the work developed by the ERA4Health Partnership, a public-public partnership bringing together 27 funding organisations from 22 countries and the European Commission (EC) through joint transnational calls.

European commission funding

The EC funds various types of research through its framework programmes, with the most recent one being Horizon Europe (2021–2027). The EC supports a wide range of research activities, including basic research, applied research, technological development and innovation.

The funding rules for Horizon Europe calls vary depending on the specific programme, action and type of project being funded. However, there are some general principles and guidelines that apply across most Horizon Europe calls. Here are some key aspects of the funding rules:

Funding rates. Horizon Europe provides funding for eligible costs incurred by project participants. Funding rates may vary depending on factors such as the type of activity (e.g. research, innovation, coordination and support), the type of organisation and the nature of the costs (e.g. personnel, equipment, travel).

Funding rates may vary depending on factors such as the type of activity (e.g. research, innovation, coordination and support), the type of organisation and the nature of the costs (e.g. personnel, equipment, travel).

Financial contribution. Horizon Europe projects are typically funded through grants, which are non-repayable financial contributions provided by the EC to project participants. Grants may cover a portion of the eligible costs incurred by participants, and recipients are usually required to contribute matching funds or co-finance a portion of the project.

Financial contribution. Horizon Europe projects are typically funded through grants, which are non-repayable financial contributions provided by the EC to project participants. Grants may cover a portion of the eligible costs incurred by participants, and recipients are usually required to contribute matching funds or co-finance a portion of the project.

Grant Agreement and Consortium Agreements involving members of the awarded consortium and the EC define what activities will be undertaken, the project duration, overall budget, rates and cost, the EU budget’s contribution and all rights and obligations.

Current (Horizon Europe) and previous (Horizon 2020 and 7th Framework Programmes) EC research programmes have funded multinational clinical studies, including clinical trials over the last years. Since 2011–2012, the EC has been launching regular calls for clinical trials with a pivotal budget of 6 million euros per study and a minimum of 3 participating countries.

Joint transnational calls (JTCs)

A joint transnational call (JTC) is a coordinated effort among multiple countries or regions to fund collaborative research projects in a specific field or topic. Typically, each participating country or region contributes funds to support researchers from their respective areas who collaborate on a shared research project. In Europe, JTCs are usually launched in the context of European Partnerships, and previously in H2020, through ERA-NET co-funds. European Partnerships are initiatives in which the EC and private and/or public partners commit themselves to jointly support the development and implementation of a research and innovation programme. The EC co-financing rate is typically 30% (and can be up to 50% in exceptional cases). In some cases, JTCs are not co-funded by the EC.

To fund studies through a JTC, funders commit voluntarily a certain amount of money in a “virtual common pot”. This means that funds stay within the country of the funder that commits them. Selection of awarded projects, including lead investigator, is based on an independent evaluation procedure agreed by all participating funders. When the JTC is co-funded, the EC provides additional funding, the so-called EC co-fund. This JTC model, commonly used to fund multinational pre-clinical research by most European public funders, is not well suited to fund multinational IICS, especially trials (IICT). Indeed, only a few multinational academic trials have been funded through this scheme [15] and they have all reported that funding lines (both amount and timing) and funding rules were the main obstacles hindering the development of the research. The ERA4Health Partnership is currently exploring how to adapt the JTC model to better support multinational IICS. The partnership aims to launch specific calls to fund multinational IICS, through the strong collaboration and commitments of its consortia of funding organisations not only at European level, but also all over the world.

BeNeFIT programme: BeNeFIT calls for comparative effectiveness

Created in 2006, KCE Trials is a department of the Belgian Health Care Knowledge Centre (KCE), an independent research centre that provides scientific advice on topics related to health care. KCE Trials selects and funds non-commercial large multi-centre pragmatic IICTs. ZonMw is the public funder of clinical research in the Netherlands. Since 2017, KCE and ZonMw have collaborated through the BeNeFIT programme funding non-commercial comparative-effectiveness trials, for a total budget of 9 million € per call. Both organisations had to work outside of their usual procedures for the success of the programme. Although the programme does not allow cross-border funding, the BeNeFIT model relies on a strong feasibility evaluation and follow-up of selected projects. Three BeNeFIT calls have been organised so far.

ATTRACT: international call for research proposals on rare cancer drug development

ATTRACT (https://www.kwf.nl/en/attract) brings together 6 European charities (Belgium, France, Spain, Netherlands, Switzerland) to fund multinational clinical research to develop better treatments for rare cancers. ERDERA (Rare Diseases Partnership) joint transnational calls for IICS on rare diseases

ERDERA, public–private European partnership, on rare diseases will fund phase I/II multinational trials following the JTC model but using the EC contribution as a “real common pot”. This “central budget” will be managed by a private foundation (Italian Telethon). The involvement of a private foundation facilitates the circulation of funds across borders, increases the flexibility through the management by the third party (Telethon) and allows time savings on the implementation of trial as no public procurement is necessary for subcontracting another entity. The partnership is currently exploring the possibility of adding funding from other funding sources outside ERDERA.

NordForsk community research

Founded by the Nordic Council of Ministers, NordForsk funds clinical studies, including clinical trials, following both the “virtual and real common pot” models or mixed models combining both. NordForsk can enter into collaboration with funding organisations/institutions outside the Nordic Region when this is expected to result in high scientific quality and Nordic added value. The virtual common pot model is the preferred funding model for partners from outside the Nordics where NordForsk can act as a broker between national funding organisations in the Nordics and organisations outside the Nordics.

Current models of pooled public funding for multinational IICS in Europe for international cooperation

There are national research agencies (e.g. INSERM/ANRS, DHSC/MRC UK) able to fund international clinical research, including in low- and middle-income countries through Official Development Assistance (ODA). In addition, there are examples of pooled funding models, some of which combine public and private funding, de-risking funding investment and enabling investments in large clinical trials that no single funder could fund alone, thus offering the potential for greater impact.

GloPID-R: global research collaboration for infectious disease preparedness

Launched in 2013, and funded by the EC since 2015, GloPID-R brings together funding agencies globally, who are investing in research related to new or re-emerging infectious diseases. The aim is to better align and coordinate research investments by its members to increase preparedness and speed up a research response to outbreaks with pandemic potential. One of the major projects is to develop common funding platforms intended to reduce the level of duplication of research investments for both, pandemic response and preparedness. Currently, GloPID-R is working towards its first coordinated funding call, envisaged to be opened during the latter half of 2024.

CEPI: new vaccines for a safer world

CEPI (Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations) is an innovative global partnership launched in 2017 to develop vaccines to stop future epidemics. CEPI is funded by international governments, the EC and a private foundation. CEPI’s mission is to accelerate the development of vaccines and other biologic countermeasures against epidemic and pandemic threats. CEPI funds clinical trial networks and clinical trials themselves via calls for proposals and strategic partnerships. CEPI also funds enabling science, clinical laboratory networks and manufacturing partnerships. The majority of CEPI funds from government and non-government sources are provided on a non-restricted basis to a common fund managed by CEPI; hence, they are not tied and can cross borders. In addition, the European Commission has provided co-financing for specific projects that has allowed for multi-country studies (e.g. support to establish a multi-centre Controlled Human Infection Model network).

EDCTP: the european and developing countries clinical trials partnership

The Global Health EDCTP3 Joint Undertaking is a partnership between the EC, representing the European Union (EU), and the EDCTP Association, representing the governments of European and sub-Saharan African countries. Global Health EDCTP3 aims to deliver new solutions to reduce the burden of infectious diseases in sub-Saharan Africa and to strengthen research capacities to prepare for and respond to re-emerging infectious diseases. It funds clinical trials and clinical studies conducted by highly collaborative European-African consortia with global partners, which always include capacity development or networking, through a pooled funding mechanism. Global Health EDCTP3 combines in one common pot, managed by the Joint Undertaking, financial contributions from three main sources of funding: the EU, the African and European participating states of the EDCTP Association, and third parties (including private partners) participating as ad hoc contributing partners. This pooled funding supports centrally managed internationally collaborative activities, such as research and innovation actions (RIAs), coordination and support actions (CSAs) and other programme activities. Contributing partners provide financial or in-kind contributions towards joint calls on research and development on strategic topics of mutual interest. The European and African EDCTP Association participating states also provide in-kind contributions to additional activities that are managed and implemented by one or more participating state according to their own national rules, but which are well aligned with the scope and strategy of Global Health EDCTP3.

Supporting clinical research infrastructures and networks

Finally, designing, planning and conducting a multi-country investigator-initiated clinical trial is a complex enterprise requiring a broad, cross-border cooperation both on the trial management side and on the investigation side.

The single sponsor (as requested by the EU 536/2014 Clinical Trial Regulation) of an IICT is an academic institution, responsible for the legal, financial and scientific management of the trial. ECRIN (European Clinical Research Infrastructure Network) is the European research infrastructure federating national clinical trial unit (CTU) networks to support the single sponsor in the trial management tasks (regulatory and ethical approvals, monitoring, vigilance, data management and data sharing), provided at non-profit rates to the sponsor. ECRIN sustainability is granted through the national financial contribution of its member countries.

On the investigation side, disease-specific investigation networks are being established at national level through public or charity funding. EU funding from the Health programme or the IHI partnership act as an incentive to structure, in specific disease areas, the investigation communities at pan-European level, facilitating the design of multinational IICS, as well as the patient recruitment and investigation.

Designing and conducting investigator-initiated multinational clinical studies is challenging and funding has been described as one major obstacle. The above-mentioned funding mechanisms, although offering good opportunities to support IICS in Europe remain, in some cases, still subject to complex funding rules, heavy administrative burdens and lack flexibility.

Conclusion

Multinational IICSs are designed and conducted by independent clinicians and academic researchers to generate clinical evidence to improve health care. The benefits are numerous and not restricted to the discovery of new therapies. Much of the benefit comes from identifying and addressing uncertainty in existing practices; evaluating a range of treatment options that address key unanswered questions free of commercial imperatives, identifying alternative and potentially more efficient diagnostic strategies; and identifying more effective models of care or lower cost alternatives that are equally effective as expensive interventions. IICSs play a vital role in the generation of evidence that can eventually drive policy.

WHO Resolution WHA78.5 encourages research funding agencies to prioritise and fund clinical trials that are well-designed and well-implemented in order to generate the scientifically robust and actionable evidence needed to inform public health policy, regulatory decisions and medical practice.

Conducting multinational trials can be challenging for academic sponsors but can be achieved through appropriate governance arrangements, funding mechanisms and strong collaborations with international collaborative trials groups, and industry where applicable. Strategies for funding multinational IICS should evolve to mitigate identified barriers by (a) prioritizing public funding towards specific multinational IICS objectives, (b) implementing flexible public funding mechanism/calls (cross-border funding) and (c) sustaining clinical research infrastructures and networks.
