Examination of factors associated with mental health of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan: A cross‐sectional study based on the two‐continua model
Authors: Adel Stoyanova, Faye Foster, Pavel Savin, Nurali Amanzholov, Raushan Alibekova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHuman immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a significant global health problem, and the number of new cases is increasing in Central Asia, including Kazakhstan. This study aimed to examine the association of a range of demographic, physical health and psychosocial factors with the mental health of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan, applying the two‐continua model of mental health, which holds that mental wellbeing and mental illness are two distinct continua that are interrelated in their contributions to overall mental health. The study findings can inform future interventions aimed to prevent mental illness and promote the mental wellbeing of people living with HIV.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted in collaboration with the Kazakhstan Union of people living with HIV. The online survey link was disseminated via social networks of people living with HIV in various regions of Kazakhstan from January to February 2024. The questionnaire included questions on sociodemographic characteristics, general health information and standardized validated scales to measure flourishing, depressive and anxiety symptoms, HIV‐related stigma, and social support. Descriptive, bivariate and multivariate multinomial regression analyses were performed in STATA software version 18.ResultsA third of the sample was flourishing (33.53%) without depressive or anxiety symptoms, while 15.29% of participants were flourishing despite mental illness, 30.59% were not flourishing and had no mental illness, and 20.59% were experiencing mental illness and not flourishing. Participants with higher social support were more likely to flourish and have no symptoms of depression or anxiety, while flourishing despite mental illness was associated with the unemployment status of people living with HIV. High internalized stigma was associated with the increased risk of having depression or anxiety symptoms and low mental wellbeing, while better physical health and older age had a protective effect against mental illness and languishing.ConclusionsStudy findings suggest that enhancing access to social support and reducing HIV‐related stigma are key to improving mental wellbeing among people living with HIV in Kazakhstan, especially among individuals of younger age, and those with worse physical condition. Applying the dual continuum model of mental health and integrating mental wellbeing scales in physical and psychological examinations of people living with HIV is recommended to better determine their service needs and provide tailored interventions to those most in need.Keywords: flourishing, HIV, languishing, mental health, positive mental health, wellbeing

INTRODUCTION

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global health challenge [1], with actively growing new cases in Central Asia, including Kazakhstan [2]. UNAIDS estimates show that 35 000 people are living with HIV in Kazakhstan [3]. The health‐related quality of life of people living with HIV is often worse than that of the general population due to poorer mental and physical health.

Previous research on the mental health of HIV‐positive people mainly focused on mental illnesses such as depression and anxiety, which are common among people living with HIV [4]. Studies showed that depression was associated with poor treatment adherence [5] and impaired the recovery of people living with HIV, posing a global public health problem [6]. In addition, HIV‐related stigma is a significant stressor contributing to mental health issues [7]. In Kazakhstan, people living with HIV often face high levels of both internalized stigma and discrimination regarding their HIV status [8, 9], as well as fear of disclosure of HIV status, which, according to previous studies, correlates with a greater propensity for mental illness among people living with HIV [10].

Mental health is often defined as the absence of mental illness [11], however, the World Health Organization describes it as ‘a state of wellbeing in which a person is aware of his or her abilities, can cope with the ordinary stresses of life, work productively and fruitfully, and contribute to his or her society’ [12]. Despite the predominance of research examining mental health from a pathological perspective, there is a rising recent trend to study positive mental health and flourishing [13]. Positive mental health is defined as a state of balance between subjective wellbeing and full functioning [14], and flourishing is defined as the process of living actively in accordance with personal beliefs, visions for the future, and responsibility as an individual, within a community perspective to which one belongs [15]. Previous studies of general population showed that positive mental health correlates with increased longevity and better physical health [16, 17], whereas in HIV‐positive populations, research in this area is limited [18]. However, there is evidence that optimism correlates with slower HIV progression, better response to treatment [19], and reduced perceptions of HIV‐related stigma [20].

According to a contemporary theoretical model of the two continuums, mental health and mental illness are related but separate concepts: ‘Mental health is not merely the absence of mental illness; it is not simply the presence of high levels of subjective wellbeing’ [21]. Thus, individuals with mental illness can still flourish if they experience high emotional, psychological and social wellbeing, while those without mental illness may languish if wellbeing is low. This model supports categorizing individuals as flourishing, moderately mentally healthy, or languishing, regardless of mental illness diagnosis [22]. A scoping review discovered that the dual‐continua model has more explanatory power than the traditional bipolar approach, and that the model was tested using a variety of methods, including confirmatory factor analysis, longitudinal observational designs, and differential predictor assessment [23]. Independent socio‐demographic factors such as gender, age, education level and occupational status can affect complete mental health categories but show varying results [11, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27].

The lack of research on positive mental health and flourishing among people living with HIV in Kazakhstan hinders the development of programmes to improve quality of life in people living with HIV. The purpose of this study is to examine the association of some demographic, physical and psychosocial factors with mental health and mental illness (such as depression and anxiety) in adults with HIV in Kazakhstan. The results of this study may inform future interventions aimed not only to prevent mental illness, but also to promote mental health and wellbeing of HIV‐positive people.

METHODS

Study participants and sample collection

The present cross‐sectional study was performed using the Qualtrics platform for online surveys. The collection of data was carried out between January and February 2024. The study included 170 individuals (18 years and older) living with HIV in Kazakhstan who agreed to participate by signing electronic informed consent. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling, primarily via the ‘Kazakhstan Union of PLHIV’. The Union's staff distributed an electronic invitation with a link to the survey through social networks of people living with HIV across different regions of Kazakhstan. This approach aimed to reach a broad range of participants, although no specific stratification criteria beyond the general eligibility requirements were applied.

Study instruments

This online survey consisted of a total of 48 questions and four parts:

Sociodemographic information: age, sex, gender identity, ethnicity, education, region, relationships, employment status, financial satisfaction and concern about living conditions;

General health information: overall physical health was assessed using a 2‐item Global Physical Health scale [28], duration of HIV diagnosis, chronic diseases, health status, physical functioning, adherence and access to antiretroviral treatment (ART), reasons for ART refusal (if any), number of missed ART days in the last month, satisfaction with healthcare services provided by the medical staff at the HIV Prevention Center, support from any organization;

Mental health: mental wellbeing was assessed by Flourishing Scale [29], a brief 8‐item measure of key aspects of psychological wellbeing, including life purpose and meaning, positive relationships, competence, self‐esteem, engagement, and optimism. Each item is assessed on a 7‐point Likert‐type scale, ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. It provides a single score reflecting overall life functioning. The original English versions of the standardized questionnaires were translated using the forward and back translation method into Kazakh and Russian languages. To ensure the instrument's validity and reliability, the translation was adapted culturally. The questionnaire was pre‐tested and adjusted for clarity and comprehension on a small group of HIV‐positive adults; responses of pre‐tested subjects were excluded from the analysis.

Study variables

Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics, general health status of respondents, HIV‐related stigma and social support.

The main outcome variable of complete mental health was composed of flourishing mental health and mental illness based on a dual‐continua model. For mental health, participants who scored above the median on the flourishing scale (>48) were categorized as flourishing (Yes = 1; No = 0). Mental illness was defined if participants screened positive on the PHQ‐2 or GAD‐2 scales (score ≥3), indicating possible cases of major depression or generalized anxiety disorder. These three dichotomous variables were then combined in one outcome variable of complete mental health with four categories: (1) flourishing without mental illness, (2) flourishing with mental illness, (3) not flourishing and no mental illness, and (4) not flourishing and having mental illness.

Ethics statement

Nazarbayev University Institutional Research Ethics Committee approved the study (NU IREC #811/27112023). Prior to the survey, all respondents received an online informed consent form outlining the study's aim, procedures, voluntary nature, data confidentiality measures, and contact details for the researchers. No personal information, including identifiers, IP addresses or location, was accessible to the researchers before, during or after the study. Participants were informed of their rights and the study details in the recruitment letter.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA/SE 18.0. For descriptive analysis, categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions, while continuous variables were described with mean values and standard deviations. Bivariate tests (Chi‐squared/Fisher's exact test and ANOVA/Kruskal‐Wallis) were used to examine associations between the outcome variable of complete mental health and independent variables. A p‐value threshold of <0.25 was applied to bivariate analyses to ensure no significant associations were overlooked due to the relatively small sample size.

Significant variables from bivariate analyses were entered into multivariate multinomial logistic regression models, focusing on three groups of independent variables: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) general health information, and (3) stigma and social support. The final multivariate model incorporated significant variables from three models, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. The final model was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test and a deviation goodness‐of‐fit test and linearity with the continuous exposure variable was assessed. Additional AIC/BIC statistics were used to compare model performance.

Among the participants, 64.85% reported their biological sex as female, 35.15% as male, while 65.87% identified as women, 31.14% as men, with almost 3% preferring not to disclose their gender identity. The largest age group was 35–44 years (40.59%), followed by 45–64 years (33.53%), with 18–34 years being the least represented (25.88%). In terms of ethnicity, the majority were Russian (54.17%) and Kazakh (35.12%). Most participants (66.47%) had secondary or special secondary education, and 90% resided in urban areas. Table 5 displays the findings of the final multivariate multinomial logistic regression model. The likelihood ratio chi‐square test was found to be significant, χ 2 (18) = 89.59, p < 0.0001. The pseudo R2 was 0.2488 for the given model, specifying that approximately 25% of the variance of the mental health categories was captured for by the predictors included in the model. Compared with the reference group, people with higher perceived social support were more likely to be flourishing without mental illness (OR = 1.60, p < 0.0001), controlling for the effects of age, employment status, physical health and internalized stigma. Those who were not employed, were 8.76 times more likely to be flourishing even in the presence of mental illness, than those who worked full‐time or part‐time. As for the category ‘not flourishing and having mental illness’, those in the 45–64 years age category were 94% less likely to fall into this category compared with people aged 18–34 years. Furthermore, people with better physical health were 51% less likely to fall into this category. However, participants with higher internalized stigma were 1.55 times more likely to be languishing and experiencing mental illness.

DISCUSSION

This study is one of the first to explore how demographic, physical health and psychosocial factors relate to the overall mental health of adults with HIV in Kazakhstan, using a two‐continua model to assess both mental wellbeing and mental illness. The findings of our study revealed that four categories of complete mental health were associated with distinct factors, confirming the validity of the two‐continua model. Participants with higher social support were more likely to flourish without symptoms of depression or anxiety, while flourishing even in the presence of mental illness was associated with the unemployment status of people living with HIV. High internalized stigma was associated with the increased risk of languishing and experiencing symptoms of depression or anxiety, while better physical health and older age had a protective effect against languishing and mental illness.

In our study, 33.53% of adults living with HIV were flourishing without mental illness. The prevalence of complete mental health in our study was higher compared with the rates among the adult population in the United States (18%) [22] and the population of older adults with chronic diseases in China (18.3%) [35]. However, it is comparable to the rates found in population‐based studies from Canada among adults with a lifetime experience of depression (39%) [36] or suicidal ideation (38.2%) [37]. By contrast, studies from the US conducted among young adults and students showed higher rates of flourishing, at 61.4% [38] and 57%, respectively [39].

We recognize that these cross‐national comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Differences in age distribution, sampling methods, cultural context, and levels of stigma, as well as differences in health systems and access to care, can affect prevalence rates. For example, while comparing young US adults with a more demographically diverse Kazakhstani population, key differences in life stage, socioeconomic status, and mental health literacy may influence a self‐perception of wellbeing.

Our results also showed that higher social support was associated with the category of flourishing without any mental illness, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [17, 36, 37]. However, more research is needed to determine the most effective types of support for improving the wellbeing of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan. Future programmes aimed at promoting mental wellbeing should consider specific forms of support that promote flourishing among HIV‐positive adults.

The prevalence of people living with HIV who were flourishing even in the presence of mental illness in our study was 15.29%. This prevalence is slightly lower than among sexual minority individuals (24%) in Australia [40], but higher than in previous studies, which found 4.8% [38] and 13% [39] among young adults in the United States. Furthermore, our results indicated that unemployed individuals with HIV were more likely experience flourishing even in the presence of mental illnesses compared to those who were employed. The association of unemployment with depression was demonstrated in previous studies, suggesting the bidirectional nature of this relationship [41, 42], whereas participation in employment was positively associated with HIV outcomes [43], quality of life [44] and flourishing [17]. However, research on the relationship of employment with flourishing is complex, and it may vary across cultures. A study on occupational stress among workers in Japan showed that full‐time employment and mental workload were inversely associated with flourishing mental health [45]. Previous studies suggested that hostile work environments and anticipated stigma may limit willingness to seek out employment in people living with HIV, or may cause them to leave employment [46], while the longer duration of unemployment was found to be associated with improved psychological wellbeing [47]. The findings of our study can also be explained by the significant external stigma and discrimination faced by people living with HIV in Kazakhstan. As noted by Amanzholov, Musina [8], these problems include denial of employment, blackmail, social isolation, and bullying. Further investigation of the relationship between employment and positive mental health is recommended, which can help to develop more comprehensive care for people living with HIV, including a supportive environment for employment.

The prevalence of people living with HIV in the third group, who were neither flourishing nor experiencing any mental illness, was 30.59%. From the perspective of the Two‐Continua Model, which sees mental health as a complete state, namely, not merely the absence of mental illness but also the presence of mental health, this cohort could be considered to be vulnerable as they are susceptible to developing mental illness due to lack of flourishing [48, 49]. That is a large portion of people who are still not covered by the system of mental health services. Thus, there is a need to develop comprehensive screening and management patterns that incorporate all four categories of overall mental health, including assessments of mental illness and mental wellbeing.

The ‘not flourishing and having mental illness’ group comprised 20.59% of our sample. This is higher than the 15.1% and 17% found in similar studies of young people in the United States, 16.2% among older adults in China, and 10.9% among sexual minority population in Australia. One reason for this higher prevalence could be that people living with HIV experience greater rates of mental illness compared with the general population [50, 51]. Our study also found that individuals older than 44 years were less likely to be in the group of people experiencing both mental illness and lack of flourishing, which is consistent with previous studies [26, 35], and can be explained potentially by presence of more effective coping mechanisms in older adults due to their greater life experience. Poorer physical health was also associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing mental illness and lack of flourishing in our study, aligning with the bio‐psychosocial model, which emphasizes the significant influence of physical health on mental health and vice versa [52, 53]. HIV can impact both physical and social functioning, which in turn affects the onset of depression and anxiety [54]. Comprehensive support systems are essential to address both the mental and physical health needs of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan.

Furthermore, our analysis of this group presented a significant association with internalized stigma. Specifically, people with HIV who reported higher levels of internalized stigma were more likely to be languishing and experiencing mental illness compared with the reference group. This finding is in line with previous studies which showed that internalized stigma negatively affects mental health [55, 56]. Despite improved access to free antiretroviral treatment in Kazakhstan, people living with HIV continue to face significant stigma, discrimination and inadequate services [8]. Given the evidence from a previous study that the effects of perceived public and experienced stigma on depression and anxiety were mediated by self‐stigma [57], efforts to reduce HIV‐related stigma and improve the quality of life of people living with HIV should include strategies specifically addressing self‐stigma.

Study strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in being the first to explore the association of a wide range of demographic, health and psychosocial factors with mental health among individuals with HIV in Kazakhstan. By utilizing a dual continuum model, the study examined both flourishing mental health and mental illness. The scales used are widely validated and reliable, and the study included a diverse population from various regions of Kazakhstan, enhancing its generalizability. This research raises awareness about mental health issues among people with HIV, and can inform the development of future intervention programmes.

However, the study has several limitations. Its cross‐sectional design prevents the establishment of causality, and further longitudinal research is needed to explore the origins of mental illness and mental health in the context of HIV. The non‐random sampling limits the generalizability of the results, and the exclusion of youth and adolescents with HIV means the findings do not apply to these groups. Our sample also over‐represents women (65%) compared with the national people living with HIV data (33% women), likely due to higher female participation in online surveys, which may have introduced gender‐related bias. In addition, the online questionnaire may have excluded certain key population groups from the study, such as injecting drug users and sex workers, who often experience symptoms of mental illness. Their participation may have been limited by barriers such as low computer literacy, lack of Internet access, and concerns about confidentiality. Furthermore, the study did not collect data on HIV transmission routes, although these could be relevant to mental health outcomes. The use of screening instruments rather than documented diagnoses of depression or anxiety may affect the accuracy of the findings; however, the use of brief validated screening tools is helpful for detection of symptoms on a broad population level and understanding of mental health trends and needs within a population. Screening tools can also improve the consistency and reliability of findings in research and clinical settings by providing standardized measures and reduce stigma by normalizing the conversation around mental health. Finally, the limited sample size, overall, and especially in some of the subgroups of variables (e.g., other non‐binary categories of gender identity, rural and suburban areas of living, not employed) may have reduced the power of our study to reveal significant associations. These small cell sizes reduce statistical power and stability of estimates, limiting the interpretability of the results. Although we retained these categories for inclusivity and transparency, we advise caution in interpreting these subgroup comparisons. Future studies should aim for larger and more balanced samples to confirm the observed relationships and better explore factors affecting the mental health of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan.

Practical implications/recommendations

Increased access to social support and health services is recommended to improve the mental health and wellbeing of people living with HIV in Kazakhstan. It is critical to continue and expand HIV awareness campaigns in Kazakhstan, with a particular focus on reducing discrimination and its impact on internalized stigma and the mental health of people living with HIV.

Our recommendation is to integrate mental health services into routine HIV treatment, applying a dual continuum approach to both prevent mental illness and promote mental wellbeing. We recommend adding structured psychosocial interventions like peer‐led support groups, mental health literacy initiatives, and stigma‐reduction campaigns to current HIV services because of the importance of social support in improving wellbeing. These initiatives can reduce internal stigma, promote emotional support and help people living with HIV strengthen their resilience. Training healthcare workers on mental health issues related to HIV and strengthening referral networks between HIV and mental health services will also contribute to strengthening these efforts.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of our study confirmed that mental illness and mental wellbeing are related but distinct dimensions, and factors associated with positive mental health and flourishing were different from factors associated with psychological distress symptoms, or factors related to the overlapped dimensions. Higher social support was linked to flourishing without depression or anxiety, while flourishing despite mental illness was associated with unemployment. High internalized stigma increased the risk of depression, anxiety and poor mental wellbeing, whereas better physical health and older age provided protection against languishing and psychological distress.

It is recommended to increase access to social support and health services for people living with HIV, and promote community‐based interventions to reduce stigma and discrimination. Further research is needed to assess the feasibility of integrating mental wellbeing scales into the routine physical and psychological examinations of people living with HIV in the context of the Kazakhstani healthcare system. Such integration may enhance the identification of service needs and inform the development of tailored interventions for individuals experiencing mental illness, diminished mental wellbeing, or those at elevated risk of mental health disorders.
