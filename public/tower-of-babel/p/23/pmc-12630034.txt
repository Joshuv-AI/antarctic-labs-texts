Electronic structures and Zeeman splitting in GaAs1-xBix nanowires under axial magnetic fields
Authors: Zhiying Hu, Jiarui Zhang, Wen Xiong, Xin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCombining the effective-mass theory with valence-band anticrossing model, we investigate the electronic structures and Zeeman splitting characteristics of dilute bismuth GaAs nanowires under axial magnetic fields. Calculated results demonstrate that absorption spectra of GaAs1-xBix nanowires exhibit a linear relationship of Zeeman splitting energy with magnetic field regardless of the diameter and Bi composition, due to lifting of degeneracy in both electron and hole states. Importantly, we find that enhanced Zeeman splitting energies and effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires with larger diameters and lower Bi concentrations can be obtained, which principally arises from splitting energy of the hole states involved in first peaks of σ+ and σ− spectra. Further, normalized magnetic circular dichroism spectra corroborate the Zeeman splitting features observed in the absorption spectra. Our research manifests that GaAs1-xBix nanowires exhibit promising potential for quantum information processing applications because of their large and tunable effective g factors.Subject areas: physics, condensed matter physics

Introduction

In recent decades, low-dimensional semiconductor materials have garnered significant research interest owing to their distinctive physical properties and promising applications across diverse technological domains.1,2 As prototypical one-dimensional nanostructures, semiconductor nanowires feature remarkable quantum confinement effects and exceptional charge carrier transport characteristics, making them as excellent candidates for optoelectronic devices including solar cells,3 lasers,4 and light-emitting diodes.5 Among various nanowire materials, III-V semiconductors are characterized by their direct-bandgap structure and high electron mobility,6 which facilitate more efficient light-matter interactions and consequently demonstrate superior performance in photoelectric conversion devices.7,8,9 Notably, GaAs material, as a representative III-V semiconductor, has attracted considerable attention in both fundamental research and practical applications due to its outstanding electronic and optical properties.10,11,12

For GaAs material, both theoretical and experimental studies have confirmed that the bandgap can be effectively tuned by incorporating small amounts of nitrogen (N) or bismuth (Bi).13,14,15,16 Specifically, the interaction between impurity states introduced by dilute nitrogen doping and conduction band edge (CBE) of GaAs material leads to the decrease of bandgap, which can be depicted by conduction-band anticrossing (CBAC) model. However, the electron mobility in nitrogen-doped semiconductors was suppressed, limiting their applications in high-performance devices.17 In comparison, the introduction of dilute bismuth (typically ≤10% concentration18) shows unique advantages: First of all, incorporating isoelectronic Bi into GaAs material leads to a significant reduction in the bandgap, which is mainly achieved through the interaction between the Bi impurity states and the valence band edge (VBE) of GaAs material, a mechanism well governed by the valence-band anticrossing (VBAC) model.19,20 Secondly, it is worth noting that Bi doping has almost no effect on the conduction band, which makes it possible to achieve bandgap regulation without significantly affecting the electron mobility.17 Finally, due to the large atomic number of Bi (Z = 83), Bi incorporation in GaAs material can generate a giant spin-orbit splitting energy (ΔSO).21,22 With the increase of Bi concentration, ΔSO will surpass the bandgap (Eg), leading to effective suppression of both Auger recombination and intervalence band absorption (IVBA) processes in the near-infrared wavelength range.23,24,25 Moreover, the enhanced spin-orbit coupling (SOC) can induce significant spin splitting in momentum space, generating spin electronic states. When an external magnetic field is applied, the electron spins will align preferentially along the field direction, establishing a well-defined global spin polarization.26 This characteristic renders GaAs1-xBix materials particularly promising for quantum information processing.27,28 Importantly, as a key parameter characterizing the coupling between the spin and the magnetic field, the precise regulation of the g factor is crucial for achieving the manipulation of the spin.29,30 Notably, the unique one-dimensional geometry and quantum confinement in nanowires allow for tunable g factors through diameter variation and enable pronounced Zeeman splitting at low magnetic fields.31,32 In addition, nanowires can provide a promising platform for quantum information processing, since they enable scalable qubit registers and flexible tailoring of material composition during growth.33 Therefore, investigating the phenomena of GaAs1-xBix nanowires in a magnetic field is of great significance for revealing their fundamental physical mechanisms and promoting the development of quantum information technology. However, current studies on the physical properties of GaAs1-xBix related materials in the magnetic field are primarily limited to bulk systems and low-dimensional quantum well structures,34,35,36 leaving the magnetic response of GaAs1-xBix nanowires completely unexplored. In the recent work, we have developed a 14-band effective-mass Hamiltonian to explore the electronic structures and optical properties of GaAs1-xBix nanowires,37 which provides an effective theoretical framework for investigating the magnetic field-dependent properties of GaAs1-xBix nanowires.

In this work, the studies on the electronic structures and Zeeman splitting of GaAs1-xBix nanowires under axial magnetic fields were performed. The calculated results demonstrate a linear relationship between the Zeeman splitting energy and the magnetic field irrespective of the diameter and Bi concentration. Additionally, the Zeeman splitting energy depends on both the nanowire diameter and the Bi concentration, with obvious values at large diameters and low Bi contents. The remaining part of this paper is arranged as follows: firstly, the theoretical model used to study the electronic structures and absorption spectra of GaAs1-xBix nanowires in a magnetic field is introduced; secondly, based on the computational results, we analyze the Zeeman splitting features and effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires under the magnetic field; finally, we summarize the content of the full paper.

Compared with the zero-field case, because the cylindrical symmetry of GaAs1-xBix nanowires remains unchanged in an axial magnetic field, the total angular momentum J is still a good quantum number for labeling individual electron state and hole state. As shown in Figure 1A, the states e01/2 and e0−1/2 represent the electron ground states with the superscript J = 1/2 and J = −1/2, respectively. While e11/2 and e1−1/2 correspond to the first excited states of the electron, also with J = 1/2 and −1/2. Similarly, in Figure 1D, h01/2 and h0−1/2 describe the hole ground states with J = 1/2 and −1/2, whereas h11/2 and h1−1/2 represent the first excited states of the hole with the same J. Meanwhile, the annotations of other sub-figures in these two figures stand for the electron and hole states for different J values. As illustrated in Figures 1 and 2, the splitting energy of each electron state or hole state in the magnetic field becomes more significant with variations in the angular momentum |J|, which reflects its strong dependence on |J|. Interestingly, as shown in Figures 1A and 2A, it can be found that under the same magnetic field, the splitting energy of electron states remains nearly unchanged with increasing Bi concentration, while that of hole states exhibits a slight decreasing trend in Figures 1D and 2D. This behavior is due to the coupling between Bi impurity states and the GaAs VBE, while their interaction with the conduction band edge is almost negligible. Furthermore, in addition to mainly affecting the splitting energy of hole states, this coupling also reduces the bandgap of GaAs1-xBix nanowires. The bandgap reduction primarily originates from the upward shift of the valence band maximum (VBM) caused by Bi doping, which is well described by the VBAC model mentioned above. For instance, for a Bi concentration of x = 0.02 (Figure 1D), the energy of VBM is located at −0.230 eV. When the Bi content increases to x = 0.06 (Figure 2D), the energy of VBM will elevate to −0.120 eV, resulting in a bandgap reduction from 1.515 eV to 1.406 eV.

To systematically investigate the Zeeman splitting features in GaAs1-xBix nanowires, we calculated the absorption spectra under different magnetic fields. As shown in Figure 3A, the absorption spectrum exhibits degenerate characteristic at zero magnetic field (B = 0 T). According to the optical transition selection rules in nanowires, the right circularly polarized light σ+ and left circularly polarized light σ− must obey the rule with Δ J = +1 and Δ J = −1, respectively, where Δ J is the difference between the total angular momentum of the hole states and electron states involved in the optical transition. Following the rule, the σ+ and σ− absorption spectra can be separated by the applied magnetic field. We emphasize that, in our calculations, the energy difference of the first peaks in the σ+ and σ− spectra can be defined as the Zeeman splitting energy ΔEzee, expressed as ΔEzee = E(σ+)-E(σ−) = geffμBB, where geff is the effective g factor of GaAs1-xBix nanowires. As shown in Figure 3C, under the condition of B = 10 T, D = 6 nm, and x = 0.02, we analyze that the first peak in the σ+ spectrum originates from e0−1/2⇒h01/2 transition, while the first peak in the σ− spectrum corresponds to e01/2⇒h0−1/2 transition. Therefore, the corresponding Zeeman splitting energy is associated with the splitting energies of the electron states (e01/2, e0−1/2) and hole states (h01/2, h0−1/2). Comparing Figures 3C and 3E, a slight enhancement in ΔEzee is observed as the magnetic field increases from 10 T to 20 T. In Figures 3D and 3F, when x = 0.06 and D = 6 nm are fixed, the similar dependence of ΔEzee on the magnetic field is also achieved. The absorption spectra of GaAs1-xBix nanowires with 12 nm diameter is presented in Figure 4, and it is found that ΔEzee in Figures 4C and 4E demonstrates an obvious enhancement with rising magnetic field. In addition, by comparing Figure 3C with Figure 4C, we can see that the nanowires with D = 12 nm exhibit larger ΔEzee than that with D = 6 nm at the same magnetic field and Bi content, and the similar result can also be acquired in other three groups of sub-figures: Figures 3D and 4D, 3E and 4E, 3F and 4F. According to the analysis, in the case of D = 12 nm, the first peaks in the σ+ and σ− spectra chiefly originate from e01/2⇒h03/2 and e0−1/2⇒h0−3/2 transitions, respectively. Therefore, ΔEzee is primarily determined by the splitting energies of the electron states (e01/2, e0−1/2) and hole states (h03/2, h0−3/2). As analysis aforementioned, the splitting energy of the hole states with |J| = 3/2 is greater than that of the hole states with |J| = 1/2. Consequently, ΔEzee for D = 12 nm is larger than that for D = 6 nm. When the diameter increases to 18 nm, the absorption spectra in the magnetic field is shown in Figure 5. Through the analysis, we find that the first peaks in σ+ and σ− spectra also arise from e01/2⇒h03/2 and e0−1/2⇒h0−3/2 transitions, respectively, which is the same as the case of D = 12 nm in Figure 4. Nevertheless, our calculations reveal that the increased diameter can enhance the splitting energy of h03/2 and h0−3/2 states, which leads to larger ΔEzee when the diameter increases from 12 nm to 18 nm. Significantly, this diameter-dependent of Zeeman splitting energy enables GaAs1-xBix nanowires to have potential applications in quantum information processing. Moreover, through comparative analysis of Figures 1, 2, and 3, it is clearly found that under the same magnetic field, the enhanced VBAC effect induced by increasing Bi content can result in the obvious red-shifting of the absorption peaks.

Discussion

Figure 6 presents the variation of Zeeman splitting energy with the magnetic field B and Bi concentration x in GaAs1-xBix nanowires. As shown in Figure 6A, the Zeeman splitting energy exhibits an approximately linear increase with the magnetic field, indicating that the effective g factor (geff = ΔEzeeμBB) remains nearly constant for a fixed diameter and Bi content. Based on the analysis of the absorption spectra, we know that the Zeeman splitting energy is closely related to the splitting energy of the corresponding hole states. In Figures 3, 4, and 5, the splitting energy of hole states increases with the diameter of GaAs1-xBix nanowires, thus strengthening the Zeeman splitting energy. Specifically, in Figure 6A with B = 20 T and x = 0.02, we obtain that the Zeeman splitting energy ΔEzee increases from −4.2 meV to −9.9 meV as the diameter grows from 6 nm to 12 nm, and further rises to −11.1 meV at D = 18 nm. Here, the absolute values of these energies (|ΔEzee|) represent the magnitude of the splitting. Analogously, this diameter dependence of the Zeeman splitting energy can be observed across different magnetic fields and Bi contents in Figure 6A. Moreover, we also calculated the Zeeman splitting energy at a fixed magnetic field of B = 20 T for various Bi concentrations, as presented in Figure 6B. According to the previous analysis, the increase in Bi content can reduce the splitting energy of hole states, which consequently decrease the energy difference between the first peaks observed in the σ+ and σ− spectra, thereby reducing the Zeeman splitting energy. For nanowires with the diameter D = 6 nm, the splitting energy of the hole states h01/2 and h0−1/2 shows a slight reduction with increasing Bi concentration, resulting in a corresponding but minor decrease in Zeeman splitting energy. When the diameter of nanowires is 12 nm, the Zeeman splitting energy is mainly governed by the splitting energy of the hole states h03/2 and h0−3/2. With rising Bi concentration, a notable reduction occurs in the splitting energy between h03/2 and h0−3/2 hole states. In this case, the Zeeman splitting energy decreases from −10.8 meV to −7.8 meV as the Bi concentration increases from x = 0.01 to 0.08. At a larger diameter D = 18 nm, the Zeeman splitting energy drops from −12.3 meV to −9.3 meV over the same Bi composition range. For the case of D = 24 nm, the similar trend can be observed, with the Zeeman splitting energy decreasing from −15.7 meV to −10.9 meV with rising Bi concentration from x = 0.01 to 0.08. Through the above analysis, we conclude that GaAs1-xBix nanowires clearly exhibit the enhanced Zeeman splitting energy at larger diameters and lower Bi contents, accompanied by a great effective g factor. For instance, Figure 6B shows that under the condition of B = 20 T, x = 0.08, and D = 24 nm, the calculated Zeeman splitting energy ΔEzee = −10.9 meV, which corresponds to an effective g factor of geff = −9.42. When the Bi content decreases to x = 0.01, ΔEzee and geff will increase to −15.7 meV and geff = −13.56, respectively. These results demonstrate that the effective g factor of GaAs1-xBix nanowires can be tuned by varying both the nanowire diameter and Bi composition. Notably, under conditions of low Bi concentration and large diameter, the nanowires exhibit geff values that are larger than the theoretical value of geff = −2.1 for bulk GaAs material reported in ref.47. Moreover, our calculations reveal that the majority effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires with large diameters and low Bi concentrations exceed those reported for bulk systems and quantum wells in ref.34,35,36. In particular, under the case of x = 0.01, B = 20 T, and D = 24 nm, we obtained a value of geff = −13.56, which is markedly greater than the values reported for bulk systems and quantum well structures. The above results indicate that GaAs1-xBix nanowires offer unique advantages over bulk systems and quantum well structures, including the relative ease of synthesis and the ability to tune the g factors through diameter control. Therefore, this large and tunable effective g factors makes GaAs1-xBix nanowires a promising candidate for quantum information processing applications. It should be noted that, in calculating the Zeeman splitting energies and effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires, we adopted the effective-mass parameters of bulk GaAs materials. According to ref.48,49, the effective-mass parameters of bulk GaAs materials include contributions arising from the nonlocal character of the self-consistent potential. However, such nonlocal effect is absent in low-dimensional nanowire structures. In order to more accurately and intuitively elucidate the Zeeman splitting behavior, we calculated the normalized MCD spectra of GaAs1-xBix nanowires under varying magnetic fields and Bi concentrations, as shown in Figure 7, and the theoretical results can be directly verified through experimental measurements of the MCD spectra or their normalized counterparts. As seen in Figures 7A–7C, the normalized MCD spectral intensity demonstrates a distinct enhancement with increasing magnetic field from 0 T to 20 T. For detailed analysis, we focus on Figure 7B to investigate the underlying causative factor. Regarding the nanowires with D = 12 nm in Figure 7B, the first peaks in σ− and σ+ spectra basically correspond to e0−1/2⇒h0−3/2 and e01/2⇒h03/2, respectively. The |S⟩↓ component in e0−1/2 and |S⟩↑ component in e01/2 show negligible variation with increasing magnetic field, whereas the 12|X−iY⟩↓ component in h0−3/2 and the 12|X+iY⟩↑ component in h03/2 will decrease and rise, respectively. With increasing magnetic field, the absorption coefficient for σ− spectrum progressively decreases while the σ+ component correspondingly increases, thereby enhancing the absolute value of their difference. Since the normalized MCD signal is defined as proportional to ΔA = α−(E)-α+(E), this leads to the observed intensity enhancement in Figure 7B. Similarly, the strengthening of the normalized MCD spectral intensity in Figures 7A and 7C also follows the previous trend. Figures 7D–7F demonstrate the normalized MCD spectra at different Bi concentrations under the fixed magnetic field of B = 20 T. As shown in Figure 7D, the normalized MCD spectral intensity remains nearly constant with increasing Bi concentration. According to the analysis, for GaAs1-xBix nanowires with a diameter of D = 6 nm, the hole states corresponding to the first peak in σ− and σ+ spectra are h0−1/2 and h01/2, respectively. In this case, with the Bi content increases, the 12|X−iY⟩↑ component in h0−1/2 and 12|X+iY⟩↓ component in h01/2 keep nearly invariant, resulting in σ− and σ+ absorption coefficients that remain almost the same. When the diameter increases to D = 12 nm, Figure 7E demonstrates that the normalized MCD spectral intensity decreases with rising Bi concentration from x = 0.02 to 0.08. In this situation, the first peaks in σ− and σ+ spectra originate from the transitions involving the hole states h0−3/2 and h03/2, respectively. As a matter of fact, both 12|X−iY⟩↓ component in h0−3/2 and 12|X+iY⟩↑ component in h03/2 exhibit the reduction with the increase of Bi content, which stems from the stronger VBAC effect. However, the absolute difference between σ− and σ+ absorption coefficients will decrease accordingly which makes the normalized MCD spectral intensity go down with increasing Bi content. Let’s focus on Figure 7F, the same trend is also observed for GaAs1-xBix nanowires with D = 18 nm. In addition, as presented in Figures 7D–7F, it is found that the normalized MCD spectra exhibit a redshift similar to that observed in the absorption spectra in Figures 3, 4, and 5, both resulting from the bandgap narrowing caused by increasing Bi concentration.

In conclusion, considering the VBAC effect, the electronic structures and absorption spectra of GaAs1-xBix nanowires under axial magnetic fields are calculated by taking the 14-band effective mass theory, and the results reveal that the splitting of both electron and hole states induces a pronounced polarization-resolved splitting in the absorption spectra, evident as separate σ+ and σ− absorption features. By analyzing the splitting energy of hole states involved in the first peaks of σ+ and σ− spectra, we obtained the following meaningful results: Firstly, the Zeeman splitting energy scales linearly with the magnetic field regardless of the diameter and Bi content of nanowires. Secondly, the increasing diameter of GaAs1-xBix nanowires improves the hole state splitting energy while simultaneously altering the hole states determining the first peaks in σ+ and σ− spectra, resulting in a size-dependent Zeeman splitting energy. Finally, the elevated Bi concentration in GaAs1-xBix nanowires can reduce the splitting energy of hole states, thereby diminishing the Zeeman splitting energy under the magnetic field. Notably, the VBAC model remains applicable even when the nanowire diameter increases to D = 24 nm and the Bi concentration reaches x = 0.08. These dependencies indicate that low Bi concentration together with large nanowire diameter can collectively enhance the Zeeman splitting, corresponding to a large effective g factor. Importantly, we evaluated the corrections to the effective-mass parameters and determined that they have a negligible effect on the calculated Zeeman splitting energies and effective g factors. Moreover, by calculating the normalized MCD spectra and analyzing the components in hole states, we further confirmed these characteristic regularities in GaAs1-xBix nanowire. The large and tunable effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires lead to pronounced Zeeman splitting energies even at low magnetic fields, thereby enhancing spin resolution and enabling precise spin manipulation. These features position GaAs1-xBix nanowires as a promising platform for quantum information processing applications. Beyond this, such effective g factors are broadly significant for spintronics and topological quantum devices, as well as for exploring fundamental spin-related phenomena, such as spin-driven phase transitions and spin interactions.

Limitations of the study

In calculating the Zeeman splitting energies and effective g factors of GaAs1-xBix nanowires (x ≤ 10%), we adopted the effective-mass parameters of bulk GaAs materials. Since the nonlocal effects inherent in the bulk material are absent in low-dimensional structures, the application of bulk GaAs effective-mass parameters to nanowire systems requires appropriate corrections. However, our analysis indicates that these corrections have a negligible effect on the calculated Zeeman splitting energies and effective g factors.
