Correlates of Patient and Nurse Missed Care: A Multi‐Hospital Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Dilay Hacıdursunoğlu Erbaş, Evin Korkmaz, Güneş Feyizoğlu, Cansu Kaçmaz, Fatma Eti Aslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo determine the prevalence of missed care in inpatient surgical wards and to examine the causes and consequences of missed care from the perspectives of patients and nurses.DesignThis study employed a descriptive, correlational, cross‐sectional design.MethodsThis study was conducted over 3 days in November 2023 and included 354 patients and 89 nurses working in surgical wards of city hospitals. Data were collected using the Patient and Nurse Identifying Information Form, the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient, and the MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse. The data were analysed using SPSS. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist.ResultsPatients reported that oral care, body care and mobilization were care activities that were most frequently not performed. Patients' educational level and age did not affect the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient scores, whereas duration of hospitalization and gender had a significant effect on the scores. According to nurses, the most frequently missed nursing care need was turning the patient every 2 h. Nurses also reported that an insufficient number of auxiliary and technical personnel, as well as unexpected increases in patient numbers and workload intensity in the unit, were major reasons for missed care. Gender, educational level, job satisfaction, age, years of employment, and weekly working hours did not significantly affect the MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse scores of the nurses.ConclusionThe results indicate that missed nursing care is a significant problem from both patient and nurse perspectives. Communication deficiencies and insufficient staffing negatively affect the quality of care. Health institutions should focus on improving communication, staffing levels, and teamwork to enhance care quality.Implications for Clinical Nursing Practice and Patient CareThis study highlights the persistent issue of missed care from both patient and nurse perspectives. The findings demonstrate that basic care needs, such as oral hygiene, mobilization, and personal care, are frequently unmet. Contributing factors including communication breakdowns, insufficient staffing, and workload imbalance were identified. These results underscore the need for nursing managers to reassess resource allocation, strengthen interdisciplinary communication, and support care planning strategies that prioritize fundamental care. The study offers valuable guidance for clinical nurses and nurse leaders aiming to enhance patient safety and improve the quality of care in surgical wards.Impact StatementThe findings of this multi‐centre study may inform nursing management practices in acute surgical settings, particularly in middle‐ and low‐resource health systems where staffing shortages are common. The dual‐perspective approach, incorporating both patient and nurse views, provides a more comprehensive understanding of care gaps. This perspective may influence policy decisions, staff allocation and training programs. The results may also guide future interventions aimed at reducing adverse patient outcomes associated with missed care.Patient or Public InvolvementPatients participated in this study by completing the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient tool and sharing their experiences of missed care needs during hospitalization. Their perspectives were essential in identifying gaps in basic nursing care and potential adverse events associated with care omissions. Although patients were not involved in the study design or data analysis, their contributions informed the interpretation of the findings and supported the development of practice recommendations aimed at improving person‐centred care in surgical wards.Reporting MethodThis study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist for cross‐sectional studies.

Introduction

Unmet care, a concept that has been frequently discussed in recent years, is defined in the literature as the ‘neglect or postponement of all or part of the required care’ (Kalisch and Xie 2014; Kalisch and Lee 2010). In addition, instead of the concept of unmet care, concepts such as ‘missed care’ and ‘incomplete care’ are also used in the literature (Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal 2021).

Care is a core concept of the nursing profession and should not be neglected (Karaca and Durna 2018). In daily clinical practice, however, required care may sometimes be overlooked, and threats to patient safety may occur (Kalisch et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2015). Missed nursing care is a major concern in healthcare environments, involving tasks that are either not performed or delayed (Kalisch and Xie 2014; Kalisch and Lee 2010). Research indicates that the primary causes of missed care are staffing shortages, lack of resources, and communication failures (Al‐Faouri et al. 2020; Alsalem et al. 2023; Papathanasiou et al. 2024). Insufficient staffing is frequently identified as the main reason for missed nursing care, with fewer nurses per shift associated with increased instances of missed care (Al‐Faouri et al. 2020). The specific types of care that are most often missed differ among studies. Some research highlights routine tasks such as turning, ambulating, and feeding as the most omitted activities (Gravlin and Phoenix Bittner 2010), whereas other studies suggest that basic care interventions and patient discharge education are more frequently neglected (Moreno‐Monsiváis et al. 2015). This variation may stem from differences in healthcare settings or cultural contexts. Furthermore, the work environment plays a significant role in missed care, as nurses often feel torn between curative tasks and activities such as communication and emotional support (Papathanasiou et al. 2024; Mandal and Seethalakshmi 2023). In summary, missed nursing care is a multifaceted issue shaped by multiple factors, including staffing levels, organizational challenges, and characteristics of the work environment (Papathanasiou et al. 2024).

Failure to provide complete care may result in decreased patient satisfaction, an increased frequency of medical errors, prolonged hospitalization, and higher healthcare costs (Kalisch and Xie 2014; Chaboyer et al. 2021; Duffy et al. 2018). It may also contribute to increased turnover of nurses and reduced job and professional satisfaction (Jones et al. 2015). Although several studies have examined missed care from the perspectives of patients and nurses (Duffy et al. 2018; Karadaş et al. 2024; Eskin Bacaksiz et al. 2020; Palese et al. 2015; İlaslan and Yıldırım Şişman 2019; Kalisch et al. 2011; Kalish et al. 2009), no previous study has investigated these perspectives within the same research framework. Therefore, this study aims to examine missed care from both patient and nurse perspectives within a single study, rather than directly comparing their responses.

Aim

This study was conducted to determine the prevalence of missed care in inpatient surgical wards and to identify factors associated with missed care from the perspectives of both patients and nurses.

Methods

Design

This study employed a descriptive, correlational, cross‐sectional design.

Sampling and Participants

The population of the study consisted of patients who underwent surgery and were hospitalized in the inpatient surgical wards of city hospitals in Istanbul (n = 4) during a 3‐day period in November 2023, as well as the nurses working in these wards. There are four city hospitals in Istanbul. Because one of these hospitals did not grant permission for the study, the research was conducted in three city hospitals. The first hospital is a large‐scale tertiary care facility with a capacity of 2682 beds, including 456 intensive care unit (ICU) beds. The second hospital is a high‐capacity teaching and research hospital with approximately 800 beds. The third hospital is a major city hospital with 827 beds, offering a wide range of surgical and medical services. All three institutions are state‐affiliated, high‐volume tertiary care centres serving diverse patient populations. The population of the study consisted of hospitals and participants who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate during the study period. During the data collection period, all patients who met the inclusion criteria and were hospitalized in the participating surgical wards, and all eligible nurses working in these wards, were invited to participate in the study. No additional sampling method was applied. The study aimed to reach the entire accessible population during the specified dates without any restrictions in the three city hospitals in Istanbul. The study was completed with participants from three city hospitals and included a total of 354 patients and 89 nurses.

Data Collection Methods and Tools

The data collection entailed a patient and nurse survey administered by the researchers. Patient data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews using the Patient Identifying Information Form and the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient. Nurse data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews using the Nurse Identifying Information Form and the MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse. Data were collected by researchers who were trained to ensure the comfort and privacy of both patients and nurses. Patients' physical condition was considered during the administration of the surveys, and interviews were paused when necessary. Data collection with patients was conducted in patient rooms in a manner that ensured privacy and minimized the presence of third parties. Nurses' working hours were considered during survey administration, and data were collected during periods when nurses were available. Voluntary participation was emphasized to all participants. Completing the data collection forms required approximately 15 to 20 min.

The Patient Identifying Information Form

The patient identifying information form consisted of four questions including age, gender, educational level, and length of hospitalization (days).

MISSCARE Survey‐Patient

MISSCARE Survey‐Patient originally developed by Kalisch et al. (2014), was adapted into Turkish by Sönmez et al. (2019). Patients' perceptions of missed nursing care were assessed using this instrument. The scale consists of 13 items and three sub dimensions related to the frequency and duration of care interventions. The sub‐dimensions of the scale are communication, time, and basic care. The communication sub‐dimension consists of five items scored on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The communication sub‐dimension includes questions related to how frequently patients communicated with nurses, whether they were informed about tests, procedures, treatment, and care, whether they were listened to, and whether their opinions were considered (e.g., ‘How often did your nurse talk to you about your treatment and care?’). The time sub‐dimension consists of four items, each of which is scored between 1 (shorter than 5 min) and 5 (longer than 30 min). These items assess the time required for nurses to respond to patient needs, including requests related to urination, alarms from monitoring devices, and the call signal (e.g., ‘When you pressed your call light/bell (or called the nurse), how long did it take the nurse to respond on average?’). The basic care sub‐dimension includes four questions scored on a five‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). These questions assess the provision of basic care needs such as bathing, oral care, and exercise (e.g., ‘How often did the nurse check on you during your hospital stay to make sure you bathed or that your body was clean?’). In two items within the basic care sub‐dimension and four items within the time sub‐dimension, a sixth response option is available to indicate that the patient did not require the care or assistance in question (e.g., ‘I could not walk’, ‘I never pressed the call light or bell’). Responses marked with this sixth option (‘not applicable’) were treated as missing values and were excluded from the score calculation. Higher scores indicate that nursing care activities were performed more consistently, whereas lower scores indicate a higher level of missed nursing care. The Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient for the original scale was 0.83 (Kalisch et al. 2014) and in a study by Sönmez et al., the Cronbach's alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.78 (Sönmez et al. 2019). In this study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the scale was found to be 0.84. At the end of the scale, six additional questions assess whether nurse‐sensitive quality indicators improved during hospitalization.

The Nurse Identifying Information Form

The nurse identifying information form consists of six questions that assess nurses' age, gender, educational level, years of employment, job satisfaction and weekly working hours.

The MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse

The MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse was developed by Kalisch and Williams (2009) and adapted into Turkish by Kalisch et al. (2012). The MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse is used to determine nurses' perceptions regarding the frequency and reasons for missed nursing care. The survey consists of 37 items and two sections. The frequency of missed nursing care activities was measured using MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse (Part 1). This section consists of 21 items assessing nursing care activities that should be provided but may be missed. Nurses indicate how often each care activity is missed using response options ranging from ‘always’ to ‘rarely’. If a care activity is not relevant to the unit in which the nurse works (e.g., assisting the patient to stand), respondents may select the ‘not applicable’ option. The results of this section were evaluated using percentage calculations for each item. A higher percentage indicates that the specific nursing care activity is more frequently missed, whereas a lower percentage indicates that the activity is more consistently performed. Another variable measured in the study was the reasons for missed nursing care, which was assessed using MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse (Part 2). This section consists of 16 items examining the reasons for missed nursing care. These reasons are grouped into three sub‐dimensions: labor resources, material resources, and communication/teamwork. The labor resources sub‐dimension includes questions related to staffing and workload issues, such as insufficient personnel, unexpected increases in patient numbers, and unit overcrowding (e.g., ‘Inadequate number of staff’). The material resources sub‐dimension addresses shortages or unavailability of necessary materials and equipment, or malfunctioning devices required for patient care (e.g., ‘Medications not available when needed’). The communication/teamwork sub‐dimension includes questions related to interpersonal and interprofessional communication, including lack of team support, conflicts among nurses, and difficulties in coordinating care with other healthcare professionals (e.g., ‘Unbalanced patient assignments’). Each reason is evaluated on a four‐point Likert scale consisting of the options ‘important’, ‘moderately important’, ‘minor’, and ‘not a reason for failure to provide care’. Higher scores indicate that the factor is perceived as a more important reason for missed nursing care. Overall, the responses provide information on both the frequency of missed nursing care and the perceived reasons for these omissions (Kalisch and Williams 2009; Kalisch et al. 2012).

Data Analysis

The study data were analysed using the IBM SPSS v21.0 statistical program. The Kolmogorov Smirnov normality test was used to determine whether the data followed a normal distribution, and the results indicated that the data were not normally distributed. In the analysis of the data, numbers, percentages, means and standard deviations were used to determine descriptive characteristics of the sample. The Mann Whitney U Test was used for comparisons between independent groups, and Spearman's correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from ISTANBUL MEDIPOL UNIVERSITY Non‐Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision no. 562, dated 13.07.2023). Permission to use the scales was obtained from the scale developers via email before the study. Before data collection, the patients and nurses were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and their consent was obtained. During the face‐to‐face interviews, privacy was ensured to prevent patients and nurses from feeling under pressure. Participants were also informed that their personal information would not be shared with any third parties and that the principle of confidentiality would be strictly maintained. The principle of anonymity and data security was ensured by keeping all collected information confidential and by protecting the identity of the respondents. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patient and Nurse Characteristics

Although the target population included surgical wards from four city hospitals in Istanbul, only three hospitals participated (response rate 75%) because one hospital did not grant study permission. A total of 354 patients hospitalized in surgical wards and 89 nurses working in these units were included in the study. The mean age of the patients was 53.2 years, and their average length of hospitalization was 6.4 days. Most patients had a non‐graduate level of education. Among nurses, the majority were female, with an average age of 28.4 years and an average professional experience of 4.9 years. Nurses reported working an average of 57.2 h per week, and approximately half stated that they were not satisfied with their current position (Table 1).

From the patient perspective, the most frequently missed care activities were oral care (51.7%), body care (51.1%) and mobilization (37.6%). In contrast, the most frequently performed activities were informing patients about examinations (70.1%), listening to patients (68.9%), and talking to the patient about the treatment care (67.8%). When the missed care activities reported by nurses were examined, several care practices were identified as being inconsistently performed. Regular assessment of patients' vital signs was reported by many nurses (73%) as a care activity that was rarely completed as scheduled. Similarly, providing emotional support to patients and their relatives (27%) and repositioning patients every 2 h (22.5%) were also reported as care activities that were not consistently carried out. In contrast, activities such as responding to emergency medication requests within 15 min and providing discharge‐related patient education (7.9%) were reported less frequently as missed care.

The Cronbach's α values of the three sub‐dimensions and the total scale score of the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient were high (Table 2). The overall MISSCARE Survey‐Patient score indicated a moderate level of missed care. Among the sub‐dimensions, communication had the lowest scores, whereas basic care had the highest scores. This finding suggests that basic care activities were more frequently missed compared to communication‐related care (Table 2). No significant relationship was found between MISSCARE Survey‐Patient scores and patients' age or educational level. However, gender and length of hospitalization were significantly associated with missed care scores (Table 3).

When the reasons for missed care were analysed, organizational and staffing‐ related factors emerged as the most prominent contributors. In particular, an insufficient number of auxiliary and technical personnel and unexpected increases in patient numbers or unit overcrowding were identified by most nurses as major reasons for missed care (78.7%). In addition, lack of support from other healthcare professionals was reported as a moderately important factor (34.8%). In addition, 27% of nurses reported imbalance in patient distribution as a minor contributing factor to missed care, while 34.8% stated that insufficient personnel was not a reason for missed care.

Discussion

Missed nursing care refers to care that is delayed, partially completed or not completed at all. In their systematic review, Chaboyer et al. reported that missed nursing care leads to lower quality patient care and reduced patient satisfaction, increased adverse patient events, decreased nurse job satisfaction, increased stress among nurses and longer hospital stays and readmissions (Chaboyer et al. 2021). In this context, identifying the types and causes of missed care is important. The findings of this study, which were conducted to determine the extent of missed care in inpatient surgical wards, and to examine the causes and consequences of missed care from the perspectives of patients and nurses, were discussed in relation to the current literature.

In the present study, the mean score of the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient was 2.4 ± 0.5. This score was reported as 1.6 ± 0.4 in the study by Sönmez et al. (2019) and 3.2 ± 0.8 in the study by Yayla et al. (Sönmez et al. 2019; Yayla et al. 2023). These results suggest that patients in our study reported a moderate level of missed care. Similar to the study by Kalisch et al. (2014), patients in our study reported the highest level of missed care in the basic care dimension. Studies by Zarate‐Grajales et al. (2022) and the systematic review by Gustafsson et al. (2020) also indicated that patients most frequently reported problems related to basic care (Kalisch et al. 2014; Zarate‐Grajales et al. 2022; Gustafsson et al. 2020). However, unlike some findings in the literature, patients in our study reported missed care primarily in the basic care sub‐dimension, followed by the time and communication sub‐dimensions, respectively (Sönmez et al. 2019; Yayla et al. 2023; Gustafsson et al. 2020). Undergoing surgery often limits patients' ability to perform activities independently (Baksi and Genç 2020). From this point of view, it can be suggested that patients in our study perceived the concept of time dimension as particularly important because they expected their needs to be met promptly following surgery.

In our study, the three most frequently missed care activities reported by patients were oral care, body care, and mobilization. Similarly, Kalisch et al. (2014) reported that oral care, mobilization, assistance with transferring from bed to chair, providing information about tests and procedures, and body care interventions were commonly missed (Kalisch et al. 2014). In addition, a systematic review examining missed care from the patient perspective reported that interventions such as oral care, mobilization, assistance with sitting in a chair, and body care were frequently identified as missed care (Gustafsson et al. 2020). In this context, the findings of our study are consistent with the literature. Hospitalized surgical patients often require greater assistance with basic care because they may be unable to meet their needs independently.

In the present study, there was no significant relationship between the educational level and age of the patients and the MISSCARE Survey‐Patient scores. However, Dabney and Kalisch (2015) reported that age was a predictor of missed care, and Kalisch et al. (2014) found that patients with lower educational levels reported higher levels of missed care (Kalisch et al. 2014; Dabney and Kalisch 2015). Orique et al. (2017), similar to our findings, reported no significant relationship between the educational level of patients and missed care (Orique et al. 2017). In contrast to some findings in the literature (Kalisch et al. 2014; Orique et al. 2017), a significant relationship was found in our study between missed care and both patient gender and length of hospitalization (Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal 2022). However, there are also studies in the literature that report similar findings regarding the gender variable (Zarate‐Grajales et al. 2022). The differences among study findings may be related to variations in study samples and healthcare settings.

When the adverse events reported by the patients in our study were examined, it was observed that the most common adverse event was occlusion in the vascular access as in the studies of Yayla et al. (2023) and Sönmez et al. (2019). In addition, the missed care scores of patients who reported vascular access occlusion were higher than those of patients who did not report this event, and a statistically significant difference was found between the two groups.

In our study, nurses most frequently reported that turning the patient every 2 h was the care requirement that was not consistently met. Similar findings were reported in the study by Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal (2022). However, in the studies of Çimen and Aslan (2021) and Eskin Bacaksiz et al. (2020), mobilization was identified as the most frequently missed nursing care activity (Eskin Bacaksiz et al. 2020; Çimen and Aslan 2021). In addition, Çimen and Aslan (2021) reported that turning the patient every 2 h was the second most frequently missed care. Again, in a study conducted in Italy, the most frequently missed care was listed as mobilization, turning the patient every 2 h, and giving medication at the right time (Palese et al. 2015). In this respect, the findings of our study are generally consistent with the existing literature. In our study, nurses rarely reported assessment of patients' vital signs as missed care, which is consistent with the findings of Eskin Bacaksiz et al. (2020). However, the literature also presents some differing results. For instance, Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal (2022) reported that bedside blood glucose monitoring upon request was among the least frequently missed care activities, while Çimen and Aslan (2021) found that hand hygiene practices were rarely omitted (Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal 2022; Çimen and Aslan 2021). These differences between study findings may be attributed to variations in institutional care policies and differences in the characteristics of the study samples.

In our study, nurses stated that the insufficient number of auxiliary and technical personnel and an unexpected increase in the number of patients and/or intensity in the unit where they worked were important reasons for not providing care. Similarly, Edfeldt et al. (2024) determined the most important reasons for missed care as ‘insufficient number of personnel’ and ‘unexpected increase in the number of patients and/or intensity in the unit’ (Edfeldt et al. 2024). Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal (2022) reported that the most important reason for not providing care was labor resources and that insufficient personnel and an unexpected increase in the number of patients and/or intensity in the unit where they worked was an important reason for not providing care (Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal 2022). Mandal and Seethalakshmi (2023) found that understaffing was the most frequently cited reason for skipped care during periods of increased workload (Mandal and Seethalakshmi 2023). Overall, these findings demonstrate that the results of our study are consistent with the existing literature.

In our study, no significant relationship was found between nurses' MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse scores and the variables of gender, education level, job satisfaction, age, years of professional experience, and weekly working hours. Similarly, Çimen and Aslan (2021) reported that educational level did not have a significant effect on questionnaire scores (Çimen and Aslan 2021). Eskin Bacaksiz et al. (2020) found that nurses with non‐graduate/graduate and high school graduates had higher survey scores. We believe that the difference between the findings in the literature and those of our study may be related to the characteristics of our sample group, as 94.4% of the nurses included in our study held a bachelor's degree or higher. In the study conducted by Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal (2022), a negative correlation was found between nurses' education level, years of professional experience, and job satisfaction and missed care scores, while a positive correlation was observed with weekly working hours (Taşkıran Eskici and Baykal 2022). The differences between our findings and those reported in the literature may be explained by variations in the sample groups and institutional characteristics of the healthcare settings where the studies were conducted.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the strengths of this study is that it contributes to raising awareness among both patients and nurses regarding the concept of missed nursing care, which has recently gained increasing attention in the literature. By examining missed care from the perspectives of both patients and nurses, the study provides a broader understanding of unmet care needs in inpatient surgical wards. However, this study also has several limitations. First, the study data were collected over a 3‐day period. Although these days were assumed to represent the routine functioning of the hospital, this period may not fully capture temporal variations in care delivery, such as weekend staff shortages, fluctuations in patient volume, or organizational changes that may occur on different days of the week. Second, some subgroup sample sizes were relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power of the Mann–Whitney U test used in the analysis. Third, the Cronbach's alpha value for the MISSCARE Survey‐Nurse Part 1 (Frequency of Missed Care Needs) was very high (α = 0.94). Although this value indicates excellent internal consistency, it may also suggest that some items are highly similar and may contain a degree of redundancy. Therefore, this aspect of the measurement tool should also be considered a limitation of the present study. These limitations should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

This study identified missed care needs and the reasons for missed nursing care from the perspectives of both patients hospitalized in inpatient surgical wards and nurses working in these units. In addition, it provides insight into the potential adverse outcomes experienced by patients as a result of missed care needs. The findings of the study offer important implications for nursing practice and the quality of patient care. Based on the results, healthcare institutions should place greater emphasis on effective communication, adequate staffing, and teamwork to improve the quality‐of‐care delivery. Furthermore, since vascular occlusion was identified as the most common adverse event in this study, we recommend the development of clinical algorithms and preventive protocols to reduce its occurrence. Additional research should also be conducted to investigate the underlying causes of this complication. Finally, when planning patient care, particular attention should be given to basic care needs, which were reported by patients as the most frequently missed aspects of care. Addressing these fundamental care requirements may contribute significantly to improving patient outcomes and overall care quality.
