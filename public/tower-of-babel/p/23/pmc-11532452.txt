Traumatic anterior fracture dislocation of the humeral head. A rare case report

Introduction

Anterior glenohumeral dislocation of the shoulder is a common injury, with an incidence of 1.7 %, making it the most frequently encountered shoulder dislocation in emergency departments [1,2]. In contrast, fracture-dislocation of the anatomical neck of the humerus is rare [1]. The risk of avascular necrosis associated with these injuries is significant, yet literature on the management and long-term outcomes is scarce [3]. The aim of reporting this case is to enhance awareness and recognition of these injuries, guide their management, and improve understanding of the long-term complications associated with them. We report a case of a 20-year-old female presenting with an anterior fracture dislocation of the humerus with an anatomical neck fracture. The injury was managed with open reduction and internal fixation using a plate and screws. Considering the rarity of this injury, this case provides valuable information regarding the management and prognosis of such injuries. This case report was written by following the Surgical Case Report (SCARE) [4].

Case report

A 20-year-old female presented to our emergency department 15 h after sustaining an injury to her left shoulder in a vehicle crush as a pillion rider. The patient's gown became caught in the motorcycle wheel, leading to a fall on her left side. The injury was accompanied by severe pain and an inability to use the limb. Physical examination revealed a swollen shoulder with a loss of contour, tenderness, and an adducted upper limb supported by the contralateral hand. Neurovascular examination was intact, with motor strength and sensation preserved. The patient was given an analgesic injection, and the limb was immobilized, before undergoing radiological evaluation. An X-ray (Fig. 1) of the left shoulder showed an anterior fracture-dislocation of the proximal humerus. Given the displacement of the humeral head, emergency surgical intervention was deemed necessary.

Operative procedure

It took 34 h from the time of injury to the operating room. Thirty minutes before induction, the patient was given an injection of antibiotics. Under general anesthesia in the supine position, she was aseptically draped. The deltopectoral approach was used to access the fracture site. The dislocated head was extracted and anatomically reduced (Fig. 2a and b) to the proximal humerus and temporarily held with K-wires (Fig. 2c). Reduction of the dislocated shoulder joint was then successfully completed. A PHILOS plate was placed slightly posterior to the bicipital groove, and one screw was placed in the oblong hole to allow adjustment of the final position, approximately 5 mm distal to the tip of the greater tuberosity. The plate was aligned properly over the shaft and held in place with a clamp. Using a 2.5-mm drill bit and sleeve under C-arm guidance, the head was drilled, and 3.5-mm screws were inserted carefully in multiple directions to reach the subchondral bone without damaging the cartilage. After measuring the appropriate length, the K-wires were removed (Fig. 3a and b). The wound was irrigated with normal saline, and the skin was closed in layers with interrupted sutures, achieving hemostasis. The operating time was approximately 2 h, with an estimated blood loss of 120 mL.

Postoperative care

The limb was immobilized in a triangular arm sling. After 24 h, the neurovascular status was assessed and found to be intact. Pumping and isometric exercises of the limb were initiated, performed four times a day for 3 min per session. On the third day, a control X-ray was done, and the patient was discharged with the shoulder immobilized for two weeks. Instructions were given to continue with isometric exercises to restore muscle strength.

In the second week, the wound was inspected with no complications, the stitches were removed, and the patient was sent to the physiotherapy unit for rehabilitation. The regimen consisted of shoulder pendulum exercises and active-assisted exercises including flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation.

At three weeks, the arm was allowed to move freely, and the patient was instructed to perform a full range of movements as tolerated. Weekly physiotherapy sessions were scheduled.

At six weeks, the patient was assessed for progress and range of motion. Radiological follow-up was performed to evaluate the hardware and healing status (Fig. 4a and b).

At the three-month follow-up, the DASH score was 18. The shoulder elevation reached 110° (Fig. 5a), internal rotation extended above the wrist (Fig. 5b), and external rotation reached 40°. Follow-up radiographs (Fig. 6) showed no implant failure.

Discussion

Proximal humeral fractures are common injuries, accounting for 5 to 9 % of all fractures [1]. Fractures at the level of the anatomical neck of the humerus are uncommon injuries encountered in orthopedic traumatology, accounting for around 0.5 % of all proximal humerus fractures, and are considered complicated injuries [2]. Glenohumeral dislocation associated with an anatomical neck fracture poses a devastating complication due to the risk of avascular necrosis of the humeral head, as it is a poorly vascularized area. Additional risks include stiffness from prolonged immobilization, as well as potential brachial plexus and axillary artery injury [6,8].

In decision-making between non-operative and operative management, factors such as fracture pattern, patient age, pre-injury activity level, soft tissue status, and comorbidities play a major role [5]. Non-operative management has been reported in selected cases of proximal humeral fracture with anterior dislocation, particularly in older patients [1]. Although shoulder hemiarthroplasty has been advocated as a treatment for complex displaced intra-articular fractures of the humeral head due to the risk of subsequent osteonecrosis, head-preservation treatments are more commonly recommended [7]. In young patient this option was associated reoperation, progressive glenoid wear and deteriorating function [8].

For most displaced or unstable proximal humerus fractures, other operative methods available to today's orthopedic surgeons include closed reduction with percutaneous pinning, intramedullary nailing, locking plate fixation, or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty [9].

Time to surgery is critically important, and early operative management of anatomical neck fractures of the humerus is associated with a significantly lower risk of osteonecrosis [2]. Evidence suggests that late surgery (>48 h) after injury and poor fracture reduction significantly increase the risk of fixation failure and avascular necrosis [10]. In our case, surgery was performed 34 hour post-injury with good reduction and stable fixation.

Due to the patient's age, bone quality, and functional demands, we decided to fix this injury rather than opting for arthroplasty, as several studies have reported unfavorable outcomes for replacement due to rotator cuff dysfunction and a high rate of revision surgery [11]. Initial immobilization in a sling for two weeks is recommended; however, this recommendation is based on clinical experience in general or uncontrolled case series [12].

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient had regained near-full range of motion, no surgical site infection or implant failure and she was able to carry out daily activities. This case highlights the importance of early surgical intervention and structured rehabilitation in achieving good functional outcomes. The rarity of this injury in a young adult female underscores the need for reporting such cases to enhance the understanding and management of similar injuries in the future.

Conclusion

We report this case to highlight the importance of early surgery, good anatomic reduction with stable fixation, and early mobilization of the shoulder in order to achieve a favorable outcome and minimize complications, such as stiffness, for patients with this type of injury.

Recommendations

Further studies are needed to assess the prevalence, functional outcome and factors associated with long term complications due to proximal humerus fracture dislocation that could lead to early identification of at-risk individuals upon encounter and evidence-based management of these injuries.
