Real‐world evaluation of a population‐based vaginal self‐sampling program for cervical cancer prevention in Region Skåne, Sweden
Authors: Caroline Hellsten, Ola Forslund, Christer Borgfeldt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCervical cancer represents a significant public health challenge worldwide. To improve screening accessibility and participation, we implemented vaginal human papilloma virus (HPV) self‐sampling as part of an organized cervical cancer prevention strategy. This study aimed to evaluate the implementation and outcomes of the self‐sampling program in a real‐world setting.Material and MethodsBetween September 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024, a total of 557 976 vaginal self‐sampling kits were distributed. Follow‐up with cervical sampling continued through April 2025. Data were collected using the Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS) and the Melior Journal system.ResultsOf the distributed kits, 208 386 were returned, yielding an adherence rate of 37% (208 386/557 976). Among the valid samples, 19% (39 697/204 763) tested positive for HPV. Follow‐up adherence among HPV‐positive women was 81% (32 305/39 697), defined as attendance at follow‐up clinics by the end of the fourth subsequent calendar month. Cervical samples were HPV‐positive in 39% (12 495/32 305) and HPV16 or HPV18/45 were detected in 2157 cases (17%). Cytological analysis identified high‐grade cervical dysplasia in 759 women (6.1%, 759/12 505) and histopathology confirmed its presence in 980 cases (13%, 980/7641). Cervical cancer was diagnosed in 32 cervical biopsies (0.4%, 32/7647).ConclusionsThe vaginal self‐sampling program was effective in detecting HPV. Its capacity to identify HPV types and significant cytological abnormalities underscores its potential for facilitating early detection, enhancing screening coverage and timely clinical intervention, thereby reducing the incidence of cervical cancer.Keywords: cervical cancer, cervical cancer screening, organized screening, vaginal HPV self‐sampling

Key message

HPV self‐sampling is an effective approach in reducing cervical cancer incidence among a screening population.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical cancer continues to represent a significant global public health burden, despite progress in both screening initiatives and vaccination programs. 1 In Sweden, screening has traditionally involved midwives collecting cervical smears during clinical visits. Recently, vaginal self‐sampling has emerged as a viable alternative for HPV testing, offering increased accessibility and convenience—especially for women facing barriers to clinic attendance due to geographic, personal, or socioeconomic factors. 2 Self‐sampling allows women to collect specimens privately at home, thereby improving participation rates—particularly in under‐screened populations, where most cervical cancer cases occur. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 HPV vaccination uptake among women aged 23–29 is increasing but remains incomplete. Future analyses should stratify outcomes by vaccination status to evaluate its impact on HPV prevalence and the detection of CIN2+. 7

In accordance with recommendations from Swedish health authorities, women should be offered a vaginal self‐sampling device or the option of cervical HPV sampling performed by a midwife beginning at age 23 and continuing until their final screening, which may occur between the ages of 64 and 70, depending on the timing of their most recent screening. At the time of the study, the screening intervals were every third year for women aged 23–50 and every fifth year for women aged 50 to 64 until 2022, when the recommendation changed to every fifth year for women aged 23–50 and every seventh year for women aged 50–64. Women testing HPV positive on vaginal self‐sampling are routinely invited for cervical HPV testing followed by reflex cytology if the cervical sample tests positive for HPV. Women diagnosed with Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASCUS), or more severe cytological abnormalities, are referred for colposcopic evaluation and biopsy. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the implementation and outcomes of the self‐sampling program in a real‐world setting.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The performance of the HPV self‐sampling program was assessed between September 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024 in the county of Region Skåne, Sweden, with a population of 1 400 000. The follow‐up period for women who tested HPV‐positive via self‐sampling at the end of 2024 was extended to the end of April 2025 to include the results of the analysis of their follow‐up cervical sample. Women received self‐sampling kits by mail, and we assessed the acceptability and effectiveness of home‐based vaginal HPV testing in a real‐world setting. Key outcomes included return rates, HPV positivity, follow‐up compliance, and cytological and histopathological findings. The primary endpoint was the number of histopathologically confirmed cervical dysplasia cases (high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion [HSIL], atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion [ASC‐H], adenocarcinoma in situ, carcinoma in situ, or cancer). Secondary endpoints included kit return adherence, follow‐up testing within 5 months, HPV positivity rates, and cytological abnormalities.

Cervical cancer screening is provided free of charge. Invitations and follow‐up data related to the screening program are managed by the Regional County Administration in Region Skåne, in collaboration with the Laboratory of Pathology. Data collection was conducted using the Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS) database. Follow‐up cervical sample results obtained after a positive HPV finding via vaginal self‐sampling were included only if performed at the end of the fourth subsequent calendar month of the self‐sampling date, with the first cytology result considered for analysis. Histopathological outcomes were included only if obtained at the end of the 12th subsequent calendar month following the cervical cytology, and only one diagnosis per individual (the highest‐ranked) was included in the analysis. The dataset did not allow differentiation as to whether the worst histopathological outcome was derived from cervical biopsies or from conization specimens. Screening history among the cervical cancer cases was categorized as either irregular or regular screening. Irregular screening was defined as having missed the most recent screening round, having participated in the most recent round but not the preceding one, or having missed two of the most recent rounds. Women with a documented screening history covering at least two consecutive rounds prior to diagnosis were classified as regularly screened.

The self‐sampling kits (Multitest Swab Specimen Collection kit, Hologic) containing 2.9 mL of the Multitest Swab Transport Media (STM) (Hologic Inc.) and an information leaflet on how to perform the self‐sample were mailed to the home addresses of women. Use of the multitest swab for patient‐collected vaginal specimen collection has not been validated by the manufacturer for the Aptima HPV assay. If the women wanted a healthcare worker to collect the cervical sample, there were instructions to contact the Administrative Office and obtain an appointment with a midwife at an outpatient clinic for collection of cervical samples that would be tested for HPV (reflex cytology performed if the sample was found to be HPV‐positive). Participants returned their self‐samples by prepaid surface mail to the Department of Pathology in Lund.

In Region Skåne, the Aptima mRNA assay is used for HPV detection. A meta‐analysis by Arbyn et al. reported that, when applied to vaginal self‐samples, the Aptima mRNA assay demonstrated a reduced sensitivity (relative sensitivity 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI] [0.74–0.96]), compared with other DNA‐based gold standard assays on cervical samples, although specificity remained noninferior. 8 To address this limitation and enhance the clinical sensitivity of the assay, we implemented a preheating step (90°C for 75 min) prior to commencing the assay. This modification increased the clinical sensitivity for the detection of high‐grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2+) from 85.3% to 95.3%. 9 The self‐sampling approach combined with the preheating step has been in use since September 2021 for all women eligible for cervical cancer screening. The APTIMA HPV assay is FDA‐cleared for clinician‐collected ThinPrep cervical specimens; its use on self‐collected vaginal samples (and with a pre‐heating step) constitutes off‐label use within a regional quality‐assured program.

All HPV‐positive women were invited for a follow‐up appointment with a midwife at various outpatient clinics, where cervical samples were collected for liquid‐based cytology in ThinPrep vials containing 20 mL of PreservCyt® Solution (Hologic Inc.). If the participant tested positive for HPV via cervical screening, a renewed HPV analysis (using the AHPV‐GT assay) was conducted, incorporating partial genotyping for HPV types 16, 18, and 45, along with reflex cytological examination. Partial HPV genotyping (HPV16 and HPV18/45) was performed on HPV‐positive cervical samples. Although the HPV detection system used can simultaneously identify HPV16 and HPV18/45, it does not detect concurrent infections involving other HPV genotypes. Therefore, in samples positive for HPV16 or HPV18/45, no information on the simultaneous presence of non‐HPV16/18/45 types could be obtained. Cytology assessments were categorized according to the Bethesda system. 10 Women exhibiting the presence of ASCUS or worse, were referred for further gynecological follow‐up at outpatient colposcopy clinics. At these clinics, biopsies were collected via cervical sampling and sent for histological evaluation at the Department of Pathology in Lund. Colposcopies are performed by certified residents or specialists, and cervical biopsy sampling is recommended irrespective of the colposcopic findings. All HPV samples, as well as cytological and histopathological samples, were analyzed at the Pathology Laboratory in Region Skåne. Additional information on screening attendance and FIGO stage for women with cervical cancer was retrieved from the Melior medical journal system.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using binomial‐distribution‐based comparisons and exact CI values. A two‐tailed p‐value of <0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Additionally, positive predictive value (PPV) calculations were undertaken to assess the diagnostic performance of the HPV self‐sampling program.

RESULTS

A total of 557 976 vaginal self‐sampling kits were distributed between September 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024. Of these, 37% ([95% CI, 37.22%–37.47%], 208 386/557 976) were returned, with 3623 (1.7%) deemed invalid upon initial testing and subsequently referred to midwives for cervical sample collection. Among the valid samples, the HPV prevalence was 19% ([95% CI, 19.22%–19.56%], 39 697/204 763) (Figure 1). The median age for participants conducting self‐samples was 41 (range 23–70), and the mean age was 42 (SD 13). The proportions at each step were as follows: HPV‐positive self‐samples (n = 39 697, 19%), attendance at follow‐up (n = 32 305, 81%), and HPV‐positive cervical samples (n = 12 495; 39%).

Follow‐up at midwife clinics was conducted at the end of the fourth subsequent calendar month for 32 305 women, representing 81% of HPV‐positive women (Figure 1, Table 1). The mean duration from vaginal HPV sampling to the follow‐up appointment for cervical sampling was 83.4 days (SD: 21.7), with a median of 85.0 days (range 0–144). Cervical samples were HPV positive in 39% (12 495/32 305) of the study cohort. Among cervical HPV‐positive women, HPV16 was detected in 8.5% (1063/12 495), and HPV18/45 in 8.8% (1094/12 495), while the remaining 82.7% were classified as positive for other hrHPV types. Cervical cytology results are shown briefly in Figure 1 and in more detail in Table 2. Among women diagnosed with cervical cancer, prior cytology revealed CIN2+ lesions in 79% (12/15) of those aged 50–70 years, in 92.7% (12/13) of those aged 30–49 years, and in 100% (4/4) of those aged 23–29 years. In women aged 50–70, 105 women had histologically confirmed high‐grade dysplasia, and cytology revealed low‐grade abnormalities in 52%, high‐grade in 46%, and suspected adenocarcinoma in 2%. Detection of cytological ASCUS/LSIL differed by age group (23–29: 86.0%; 30–49: 80.6%; 50–70: 76.6%), with a significant decreasing trend with age (p trend < 0.001). The corresponding proportions of benign cytology results were 8.3%, 11.0%, and 17.0% in the respective age groups.

The PPV of the HPV test performed on self‐collected samples was 2.4% (781/32 305), whereas the PPV for the follow‐up cytology samples was 6.2% (781/12 505), both calculated for the cytological detection of high‐grade cervical dysplasia or cervical cancer (Table 2). The PPV of the HPV test performed on clinician‐collected cervical samples was notably higher, at 13.7% (1044/7647), when evaluated against histopathological confirmation of high‐grade cervical dysplasia or cancer (Table 4).

Between September 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024, the cervical cancer screening program utilizing vaginal HPV self‐sampling detected 32 cases of cervical cancer. Of these, 25 were squamous cell carcinomas, 6 adenocarcinomas, and 1 adenosquamous carcinoma. The mean age at diagnosis was 46 years. HPV genotyping revealed HPV16 as the most prevalent type, identified in 17 cases. HPV18/45 was detected in 6 cases, other high‐risk HPV types in 7 cases, and co‐infection with HPV16 and HPV18/45 in 2 cases. The most common cytological result prior to cancer diagnosis in histopathology was HSIL (n = 18), followed by ASC‐H (n = 4), suspicion of squamous cell carcinoma (n = 3), LSIL (n = 3), AIS (n = 2), suspicion of adenocarcinoma (n = 1), and ASCUS (n = 1). The mean interval between cytology and biopsy‐confirmed cancer was 3.5 months. Ten women with cervical cancer had undergone regular screening, whereas 22 had irregular screening histories. Among the 10 women who had been screened regularly prior to cancer diagnosis, five women had normal cytology results in their previous screening round, and three women had a negative HPV test. In two cases, cervical cancer was diagnosed following their initial screening at the age of 23. According to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification, microinvasive (stage IA1) and localized tumors (stage IA2–IB2) were significantly more common (n = 24) than advanced‐stage tumors (stage IB3–IV; n = 7) (p = 0.003). One tumor was unstaged due to nonparticipation in follow‐up procedures.

The proportion of cervical cancer detected among women who returned self‐collected HPV samples was 0.00015 (32 cases out of 208 386), corresponding to an incidence of 15.3 cervical cancer cases per 100 000 HPV‐screened women. Based on a study period of 3.5 years, this translates to an annual incidence rate of approximately 4.4 cases per 100 000 women (calculated as 0.00015 × 100 000 ÷ 3.5).

DISCUSSION

This study showed that the implementation of a vaginal HPV self‐sampling program resulted in a 37% return rate, with 19% of valid samples testing positive for HPV. Among HPV‐positive women, 81% adhered to follow‐up, leading to the detection of high‐grade cervical dysplasia in 6.1% of cytology samples and 13% of histopathology‐confirmed biopsies. These results indicate promising program performance in case finding; however, the higher HPV positivity in self‐samples by the off‐label assay we use and resource implications warrant cautious interpretation and continued optimization.

During the initial 2 years following the implementation of the primary HPV‐based screening program in the Netherlands, women were offered the option of either self‐sampling for HPV testing or undergoing clinician‐collected sampling. Only 7% of screening‐eligible women opted for the self‐sampling method during this period, with uptake increasing to 16% by 2020. 11 The return‐rate of kits in our study was notably higher than that observed in the neighboring Capital Region of Denmark, where a large‐scale study involving direct mail distribution reported a return rate of 25% among previous nonattenders. 12 However, the return rate in Region Skåne remains lower than that achieved in the Stockholm‐Gotland region of Sweden, where self‐sampling has been implemented earlier and more extensively. In that region, return rates have reached approximately 50% (Elfström M, Personal Communication, Autumn 2025), likely reflecting both greater public familiarity with the method and more established infrastructure for self‐sampling initiatives. These regional differences in return rates may be influenced by factors such as the duration and scale of implementation, public awareness campaigns, and healthcare system integration.

The National Cervical Prevention Quality Register reported that in 2024, 64% of women in Region Skåne had undergone a screening test within the past 1 year. The overall screening coverage in Region Skåne—measured over one screening interval plus an additional 6 months—was 83% during 2022–2023, rising slightly to 85% in 2024. These figures are consistent with previous years, indicating a modest but positive trend in screening participation. 13 A caveat was that no short‐term reminders were sent to women, which may have otherwise improved adherence to the self‐sampling. However, after 1 year, a second self‐sampling kit was sent to those who had not returned the initial kit.

Among all returned self‐samples, the majority were from women aged 30–49 years (n = 98 473), with the number declining to 61 569 among women aged 50–70 years. One possible explanation is that older women may prefer to adhere to established screening routines and may be less inclined to adopt new health‐related innovations compared with younger women. In addition, information regarding self‐sampling may not have reached older women as effectively, and the procedure itself may be perceived as more challenging to perform in this age group. Recent studies have reported high acceptability of self‐sampling among both nonattenders 14 and regular participants in the screening program. 15 Although many women accept self‐sampling, concerns regarding test accuracy are more pronounced for self‐collected samples compared to clinician‐collected samples. 15 Optimizing kit packaging and the accompanying information materials may further improve attendance rates. As public awareness and acceptance of self‐sampling increases, compliance rates are expected to improve. In the interim, healthcare professionals and screening program authorities and administrators play a pivotal role in supporting its integration into routine healthcare through patient education about the safety and efficacy of the procedure.

The 19% HPV positivity exceeds rates reported with DNA‐based assays; this difference may reflect assay platform characteristics and, potentially, the added preheating step, which requires prospective validation before causal inference. For instance, in the Stockholm region, where the HPV‐DNA‐based assay (BD Onclarity) was used, HPV prevalence in self‐collected samples was 16% (20 272/126 601) between 2022 and 2024. 16 In our region with a self‐sampling response rate of 37% and an HPV prevalence of 19%, we estimated that about 11 900 women require follow‐up with clinician‐collected cervical sampling each year. If an HPV‐DNA‐based assay with an HPV prevalence of 16% had been used instead, the corresponding number would be approximately 10 000 women. Compared with HPV‐DNA testing, the 19% increase in women requiring follow‐up cervical sampling after an HPV‐positive Aptima self‐sample is expected to result in a modest increase in healthcare resource utilization. However, overtreatment associated with the use of Aptima self‐sampling, compared with HPV‐DNA testing, is unlikely, as referral for colposcopy has thus far required both cervical HPV‐positivity and ASCUS+ cytology.

The higher HPV prevalence observed in our study compared to those using DNA‐based assays is likely attributable to the inclusion of the pre‐assay heating step prior to Aptima mRNA analysis. The primary task of the Aptima assay is to detect E6/E7 mRNA transcripts of HPV‐infected cells; however, HPV DNA can also be detected. 17 The preheating step in our method probably results in an increased number of single‐stranded HPV DNA molecules, which are then captured with the Aptima. In PCR‐based methods, DNA is separated into single‐stranded templates at temperatures around 95°C. 18 mRNA is a less stable molecule and starts degrading at temperatures around 40°C. 19 However, in the protocol developed by Forslund and Borgfeldt, the preheating step did not appear to degrade HPV mRNA in the Aptima (STM) solution. In three of the five original HPV‐positive samples, the HPV signal (S/CO) values increased significantly following incubation at 90°C for 1 h. The authors suggest that it is possible that heat treatment may disrupt molecular interactions between vaginal tract components and HPV mRNA, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of the assay when applied to vaginal self‐samples. 9 This phenomenon warrants further investigation to clarify the molecular integrity and diagnostic relevance of heat‐treated samples. Such evaluations should also adhere to the VALidation of HUman papillomavirus assays and DEtection Systems protocol, 20 including non‐inferiority testing of the Aptima mRNA assay (with the pre‐assay heating step) against established DNA‐based methods in real‐world screening settings.

At the follow‐up at the end of the fourth subsequent calendar month, where cervical samples were collected by midwives, HPV was detected in 39% (12 495/32 305) of the vaginal HPV positive women, with a high follow‐up adherence rate of 81%. This relatively high adherence to follow‐up procedures reflects effective communication and coordination with healthcare providers, ensuring continuity of care. The attendance rate was, however, lower than expected. In our previous study, attendance at follow‐up appointments after a positive HPV self‐sampling test was 91.2% within the same time interval. 21 It is also noteworthy that during the initial implementation of self‐sampling in 2021, most enquiries from women participating in the screening program in southern Sweden concerned the sampling procedure itself. By 2022, however, the most common reason for contacting the screening coordinator was related to testing HPV‐positive, with women requesting prompt follow‐up appointments with a midwife. The 19% loss to follow‐up observed after an HPV‐positive test result in our study is an important finding that warrants further evaluation. This level of attrition may reflect limited understanding of the significance of an HPV‐positive result among women in the screening population, and the information provided in the notification letters following an HPV‐positive result might be unclear, underscoring the need for improved communication strategies.

The high HPV positivity observed in self‐samples, coupled with lower HPV positivity in cervical samples, underscores the need to refine triage algorithms to reduce the risk of overdiagnosis and unnecessary follow‐up. These results are in concordance with our earlier study where about two‐thirds of the HPV‐positive women from self‐sampling had no detectable HPV in the cervical sample, and the HPV positivity rate on self‐samples was 17.1%. 21 In Scotland, HPV detection showed 67.1% concordance between two vaginal self‐samples, but only 30% between initial self‐samples and clinician‐collected cervical samples using the Aptima mRNA assay. 22 Another study reported that 30% of women cleared HPV infection within 4.4 months. 23 Additionally, non‐HPV16/18 high‐risk types have been shown to be more frequently detected in self‐collected vaginal samples compared to clinician‐collected cervical samples. 24 Since the material from self‐sampling is diluted in 2.9 mL and the clinical cervical sample in 20 mL, this might partly explain why a larger proportion of vaginal samples turn out to be HPV positive compared with cervical samples. The high prevalence of HPV detected in vaginal self‐samples when using the Aptima mRNA assay with the additional pre‐assay heating step, leads to more unnecessary follow‐up procedures causing extra healthcare costs and increased workload for clinics. Although previous studies have reported negative emotional reactions following a positive HPV result, 25 , 26 no evidence of long‐term anxiety was identified in another study. 27 As follow‐up appointments for women testing HPV‐positive via self‐collected vaginal samples are generally scheduled within 3 months, the risk of long‐term psychological harm attributable solely to a positive HPV result from self‐sampling appears limited.

Our findings are encouraging among women who participated in self‐sampling screening, with an observed annual incidence of cervical cancer of approximately 4.4 cases per 100 000 women. However, given that an estimated substantial proportion (~60%) of all cervical cancer cases occur in women who do not attend regular screening, 28 it is imperative to intensify efforts to both promote HPV vaccination and engage nonattending populations.

In our study, cervical cytology results revealed a high prevalence of ASCUS (50%) and LSIL (33%), which are typically associated with transient HPV infections. In 2017, with the introduction of primary HPV‐based screening using cervical samples in southern Sweden, 37% of women had cytological findings of ASCUS or LSIL, while 53% had benign cytology results. 29 Our results from southern Sweden represent the highest proportion of low‐grade cytology compared with other laboratories in the country (Edvardsson H, Personal Communication, September 2025). The reason for this marked increase is unknown. It is unlikely that these findings reflect a true population‐based difference compared with other regions in Sweden; rather, they are more likely to indicate overdiagnoses which may lead to unnecessary referrals for colposcopy and eventually conization treatment. The increased number of referrals to colposcopy units for ASCUS+ cytology results leads to inefficient allocation of health resources, failing to ensure that efforts are directed where they yield the greatest public health benefits, and resulting in a cost inefficient strategy. To reduce overtreatment and minimize the associated harm, robust quality assurance is essential.

Prior cytology detected CIN2+ lesions in most women who were subsequently diagnosed with cervical cancer, with detection rates increasing from 79% in women aged 50–70 years to 100% in those aged 23–29 years. Among the 105 women aged 50–70 years with histologically confirmed high‐grade dysplasia, prior cytology revealed low‐grade abnormalities in 52%, high‐grade abnormalities in 46%, and suspected adenocarcinoma in 2%. These findings highlight cytology's limitations in accurately reflecting disease severity, especially in older women. Age‐related hormonal changes often shift the transformation zone higher within the endocervical canal (TZ3), complicating biopsy sampling and make colposcopic evaluation impossible. Self‐sampling for HPV analysis presents a molecular alternative that may enhance early diagnosis and intervention. In total, 980 women were diagnosed with biopsy‐verified high‐grade cervical dysplasia through the end of the 12th subsequent calendar month of cervical sampling, of whom 44% (427/980) had prior cytology results indicating high‐grade abnormalities or suspicion of cervical cancer. This finding is consistent with national data from 2023, which reported prior high‐grade cytology results, suspicion of cancer or cancer in 46% (5927/13 014) of women with histological diagnoses of HSIL, AIS, or cervical cancer. 30 The proportion of biopsy‐verified high‐grade cervical dysplasia or cancer was 0.5% (1012/204 763) of all eligible self‐samples, which is consistent with our previous study, where the proportion was 0.5%. 21

This large‐scale, population‐based study, involving over 557 000 distributed self‐sampling kits, offers robust and generalizable insights into the real‐world implementation of vaginal HPV self‐sampling. Key strengths include centralized analysis and comprehensive data collection from validated systems. Targeted interventions—such as automated reminders, clearer communication, and streamlined follow‐up access—could enhance compliance to the self‐sample device. Local information campaigns and activities delivered by health communicators could be implemented to improve outreach and increase adherence to self‐sampling for HPV analysis, particularly in areas with low screening coverage. Limitations include potential selection bias, as responders may have been more health‐conscious than nonresponders, and the absence of a comparator group receiving both clinician‐ and self‐collected samples, which limits direct comparisons. Another limitation is that our dataset lacked various baseline characteristics of the study population, such as missing calendar time for self‐sampling, previous abnormalities, and screening history, which may impair direct comparisons to other studies. The higher observed HPV positivity rate may have been influenced by the preassay heating protocol employed, though its clinical implications require further validation. Another issue was the slightly higher rate of invalid self‐collected samples (1.7%) when using the Aptima mRNA assay, compared to DNA‐based HPV assays, which reported an invalid rate of 0.6% (789/127 390). 16 Potential contributing factors include sample dilution in 2.9 mL STM, mailing and handling procedures, and laboratory processing, together with our use of the APTIMA assay on self‐collected samples. We also observed an increased proportion of invalid samples among women aged 50 years and older (4.6%), consistent with findings reported by Auvinen et al. 31 The underlying reason for the overall higher proportion of invalid samples is unclear, but it may be related to age‐associated changes in the vaginal microbiota that could interfere with the Aptima mRNA assay. Nonetheless, we consider the observed rate of invalid samples to be manageable by offering clinician‐collected cervical sampling to this subgroup of women. Further studies are, however, needed to minimize the proportion of invalid self‐samples when the APTIMA HPV assay is used. Additionally, the time between kit receipt and sample collection could not be measured, as only the number of kits distributed and samples received were recorded. Moreover, it remains possible that some women outside the organized screening program who submitted a self‐sample were included in the data, such as women who had been vaccinated against HPV and were subsequently offered an HPV self‐sampling kit. Overall, our findings support the feasibility and effectiveness of vaginal self‐sampling in increasing screening coverage, while highlighting areas for improvement in follow‐up adherence and clinical validation. Future studies should compare Aptima mRNA with DNA‐based HPV assays to assess sensitivity and specificity trade‐offs. Such evaluations are crucial to determine whether the higher HPV positivity observed with Aptima, and its preheating step reflects true clinical benefit or unnecessary follow‐up. In Sweden (2024), two large regions using DNA‐based assays reported 12.7% HPV positivity (10 395/81 584 self‐samples), whereas Skåne using Aptima showed 19.3% (13 513/69 828 self‐samples). 7 This difference raises concerns about efficient use of healthcare resources. Comparative studies and optimized triage strategies, including HPV genotyping or molecular markers, are needed to balance sensitivity with specificity and ensure cost‐effective, clinically relevant screening outcomes. APTIMA is FDA‐cleared for clinician‐collected ThinPrep cervical specimens; therefore, our self‐sample application (including preheating) is off‐label and requires formal validation. We further note that the 19% HPV positivity in self‐samples and the high proportion of ASC‐US at follow‐up cytology call for strengthened quality assurance across triage and colposcopy pathways. In our region, Lindroth et al. reported that, in 2017, 23% of HPV‐positive women participating in cervical screening had cytological findings of ASC‐US. 29 In the present study, the markedly higher proportion of ASC‐US (50%) among HPV‐positive cervical samples raises concerns about the quality of cytological diagnostics in our region. To strengthen diagnostic accuracy, weekly cytology conferences have now been implemented at the Regional Cytology Laboratory (Köster J, Personal Communication, November 2025).

CONCLUSION

We have demonstrated that mailing self‐sampling kits directly to women's homes proved effective in detecting HPV and contributing to cervical cancer prevention. Despite limited adherence, overall participation and follow‐up rates were high. The program's ability to identify HPV types and significant cytological abnormalities supports its potential for early detection and timely intervention. Continued research and monitoring are essential to assess long‐term impact and optimize implementation.
