Homozygous FDXR variant in twin sisters with spastic paraparesis followed by acute progressive flaccid quadriparesis
Authors: Abbas Tafakhori, Zahra Sarvestani, Ariana Kariminejad, Homa Tajsharghi, Go Hun Seo, Seung Woo Ryu, Sanaz Heydari Havadaragh
Published: 2026

Introduction

Neurological disorders arising from genetic variants present complex challenges for diagnosis and treatment. Among these, variants affecting mitochondrial function have emerged as significant contributors to neurodegenerative diseases [1, 2], often complicating diagnosis and management [3]. The FDXR gene, encoding mitochondrial membrane-associated ferredoxin reductase enzyme, along with FDX1 and FDX2 proteins [4] is critical for iron-sulfur cluster [5] biogenesis, a process essential for mitochondrial function and cell survival [6, 7]. Disease-associated variants in this gene lead to disrupted iron-sulfur cluster [5] formation, mitochondrial iron accumulation, and progressive neurodegeneration [1, 8–10].

To date, about 50 cases of FDXR-related disorders (FRD) have been reported [5, 9, 11–21] and only 35 of the 182 known variants are classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants known to cause FDXR-associated disorders [22].

The clinical spectrum of FDXR-related disease (FDR) is wide-ranging and evolving. These patients may present with optic (93.5%) and/or acoustic (50.0%) neuropathies as the initial reports on the disease mainly focused on these clinical signs [9]. However, recent findings reveal a broader array of symptoms, such as global developmental delays and neuro-regressions, peripheral neuropathy, hypotonia, ataxia, and severe motor dysfunction [9]. Despite these advances, genotype-phenotype correlations remain poorly defined, particularly in acute-onset cases.

Here, we report identical twin sisters with consanguineous parents who developed a post-infection acute flaccid quadriparesis, later linked to a homozygous FDXR variant (FDXR (NM_024417.5): c.463 C > T, p.Arg155Trp). This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of FRD by highlighting: (1) a mimic of inflammatory neuropathy (AIDP or Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy), and (2) discordant clinical manifestations in monozygotic twins despite shared genetics.

Case Presentation

In this case report, we present the distinctive clinical profile of twin sisters, A. and B., both 22 years old and residing in Tehran, Iran.

Twin A., a 22-year-old woman, was referred to our hospital due to the development of progressive limb weakness following an upper respiratory infection, which was initially suspected as acute inflammatory neuropathy. She received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasmapheresis for a provisional diagnosis of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS). Despite initial improvement, her symptoms gradually worsened, leading to a diagnosis of chronic polyneuropathy. Alongside the ongoing progression, she also experienced episodic exacerbations of weakness and ultimately developed flaccid quadriplegia over the course of her disease. Beginning 1 year prior to her last admission, she developed dysarthria, dysphagia, urinary and later fecal incontinence, and fluctuating hearing impairment. Furthermore, she experienced progressive visual deficits culminating in blindness.

Twin B., the twin sister of A., presented at the age of 22 with a similar, almost concurrent initial course. she also experienced progressive limb weakness post-URI, resulting in flaccid quadriplegia and had visual deficit; however, this patient did not develop blindness, dysarthria, dysphagia, or hearing impairment.

Both individuals were results of premature birth and had low birth weights (A.: 1250 gr, B.: 1500 gr). They also achieved developmental milestones normally. Although formal genetic testing to confirm zygosity or detailed documentation of chorionicity and amniocity was not available, the twins were presumed to be monozygotic based on their same sex, simultaneous birth, highly concordant clinical course, and the presence of a rare, well-defined FDXR-associated phenotype affecting both individuals. Nevertheless, we acknowledge a small possibility that the twins may be dizygotic, and this represents a limitation of the study. The parents had a consanguineous relationship (first cousins), with no mention of additional offspring. However, similar neurological symptoms were reported in the maternal cousin (without a definite diagnosis).

A noteworthy point in the past medical history of the twins was the onset of early motor difficulties around the ages of 10–11 years old, when both began to experience fatigue and lower limb spasm while walking. These symptoms gradually interfered with their mobility and led to their enrollment in physiotherapy programs for management of spastic paraparesis. Despite these interventions, the cause of their lower limb spasticity remained undiagnosed at that time, and both subsequently received repeated botulinum toxin injections in an attempt to relieve muscle tightness and improve ambulation.

Electromyography and nerve conduction studies (EMG-NCV) on all four limbs performed for both cases were consistent with severe length-dependent axonal sensory and motor polyneuropathy with active denervation in either of them.

No pathologic findings were detected on the brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the patients. However, spinal MRI was indicative of multilevel disc bulging and dehydration in the lumbar segment of the spine.

Additionally, throughout the investigation for the etiology of the twins’ condition, many supplementary tests and analyses were carried out regarding the autoimmune, infectious, and metabolic causes (including serum and CSF lactate and pyruvate, plasma folate, vit B12, homocysteine, tissue transglutaminase IgA, endomysial antibody IgA, antiganglioside antibodies, amino acids, fatty acids, and viral markers such as EBV, CMV, HIV, VZV, HTLV, HSV 1 and 2). However, none of the test results showed any significant disruption responsible for the disease (CSF analysis showed elevated protein in both cases with normal glucose/lactate; serum studies revealed elevated lactate in A., normal B12/folate, and negative viral/autoantibody panels). Whole exome sequencing

Considering the suspicion of a genetic origin, Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) was performed for Twin A. It was performed in accordance with the CAP/CLIA validated standard operating protocol. Exome capture was performed using xGen Exome Research Panel v2 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, Iowa, USA). Sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as 150 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing data were aligned to either GRCh37/hg19 human reference genome using BWA-MEM and processed for variant calling by GATK v4.2.14 [23, 24]. Copy number variants (CNVs) were called using 3bCNV, an internally developed tool that uses depth-of-coverage information of each exon [25]. Variants were annotated, filtered, and classified using EVIDENCE v4, which incorporates Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) for annotation and the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guideline for variant classification [26, 27]. The splicing prediction was performed using spliceAI [28]. The filtered and classified variant list was then manually reviewed by medical geneticists and physicians. The most likely variants that can explain the patient’s phenotype were selected for reporting.

As demonstrated in Fig. 1, WES results of twin A. revealed a homozygous variant [29, 30] on the FDXR gene (FDXR (NM_024417.5): c.463 C > T, NP_077728.3: p.Arg155Trp). This variant is of an extremely low frequency (1.18e-3%) based on gnomAD v4.1.0 dataset [31] and results in the substitution of cytosine nucleotide with thymine on the 463rd locus of chromosome 17, leading to the substitution of arginine with tryptophan in the protein product of the gene (ferredoxin reductase). Also, a missense change is the probable mechanism of this variant.

Identification of c.463 C > T, a heterozygous variant in FDXR (NM_024417.5). The BAM file displayed in The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) shows c.463 C > T

The FDXR (NM_024417.5): c.463 C > T, NP_077728.3: p.Arg155Trp variant was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP 2015 guidelines [30]. Supporting evidence includes its absence from population databases (PM2), homozygosity in affected individuals in a consanguineous family consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance (PM3), location at a highly conserved, functionally important residue (PM1), occurrence as a missense variant in a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation (PP2), and deleterious predictions by multiple in silico tools (PP3).

Given the WES result of twin A., it was recommended to perform a co-segregation analysis for the parents and the other sister to determine the carriers and other potential genetic variants; which was not carried out.

During the subsequent follow-ups, regrettably, we learned about the passing of both sisters at the age of 22 (in the early 2024) less than one year after development of flaccid quadriplegia. The cause of death in both patients was assumed to be cardiorespiratory arrest due to aspiration pneumonia.

The twins’ medical history and clinical presentation suggest a complex neurological condition with both shared and distinct features, highlighting the importance of studying such cases to enhance our understanding of rare neurologic disorders, especially those with genetic origins.

Discussion

The central nervous system is one of the most energy-demanding systems in the human body [32], and any dysfunction affecting the supplementation of energy to this system may cause mild to severe neurological impairment. Mitochondrial disorders, whether caused by nuclear (nDNA) or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variants, frequently manifest with neurological symptoms due to disrupted oxidative phosphorylation [33]. Among these, disorders associated with defective iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster biogenesis and homeostasis represent an important subgroup, as Fe–S clusters are essential cofactors for numerous mitochondrial enzymes. Deficiencies in genes involved in Fe–S cluster formation—such as FDXR, FXN, ISCU, GLRX5, and LYRM4—can lead to diverse neurodegenerative phenotypes, including optic atrophy, ataxia, spasticity, and peripheral neuropathy [34].

FRD is an autosomal recessive condition caused by disease-associated variants in the FDXR gene, which encodes ferredoxin reductase, an enzyme critical for iron–sulfur cluster biogenesis. This process is essential for mitochondrial function, and its disruption primarily affects the nervous system [35]. Two main sets of clinical phenotypes have been described, though there is significant overlap between them (OMIM: 103270): (1) auditory neuropathy–optic atrophy syndrome and (2) optic atrophy–ataxia–peripheral neuropathy–global developmental delay [35]. Despite these predominant patterns, phenotypic variability is noted among patients.

A recent report by Wang et al. (2025) [18] introduced the case of a 16-year-old female with compound heterozygous FDXR variants who developed optic atrophy followed by ataxia, lower limb pain, and peripheral neuropathy during childhood.

The FDXR variant identified in our patient (FDXR (NM_024417.5): c.463 C > T, NP_077728.3: p.Arg155Trp) has been previously reported in the literature in three cases [9, 13]. Slone et al. first reported this variant in a nearly 4-year-old male who exhibited early signs of developmental delay, ataxia, and visual impairments, and was revealed to be compound heterozygous for the mentioned FDXR variant through WES [13]. In a separate study, Masnada et al. described two siblings—a brother and a sister from a Gambian family—who developed the disease at an older age compared to the previous study. The brother first displayed symptoms at 9 years of age, and his younger sister at age 7. The brother has experienced nocturnal enuresis, gait disturbance, pain, and optic neuropathy throughout the disease, while his sister presented with more atypical features, such as abdominal pain, flaccid paralysis, and dysesthesia. Both siblings were found to be homozygous for the variant, as indicated by their WES results [9]. Table 2 summarizes the clinical features of the patients with FDXR variants discussed earlier.

As described earlier, the first-emerging clinical feature observed in our study subjects was insidious spastic paraparesis, which later progressed to acute flaccid paralysis and subsequently to progressive visual, hearing, and speech deficits. Notably, the presentation of acute-onset flaccid quadriparesis following an upper respiratory infection represents a novel finding, diverging from the classic insidious course of FRD. Furthermore, spastic paraparesis, as observed in our patients, is a distinguishing manifestation. Masnada et al. [9], in their comprehensive review of FRD cases reported up to 2023, indicated that spastic paraparesis has been an uncommon finding, with almost no prior reports documenting it as a prominent feature.

The marked phenotypic discordance between the twins in our study underscores the clinical variability of FRD, even among individuals sharing an identical genetic material. This disparity aligns with prior reports of intrafamilial variability in FRD, where siblings with even similar FDXR variants exhibited differing ages of onset and symptom profiles [9]. Such variability may arise from a combination of epigenetic modifications and environmental factors. Although monozygotic twins share the same genome, epigenetic marks—including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs—can diverge over time [36] due to non-shared environmental exposures, stochastic developmental events, and differences in intrauterine conditions such as placentation or minor hypoxic episodes [37]. These epigenetic differences can modulate the expression of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes, including FDXR, contributing to phenotypic discordance. Also, studies reported that FDXR expression shows significant differences in response to radiation among individuals, indicating its sensitivity to environmental triggers [38].

Furthermore, many studies have reported premature births among FDR patients [9, 13–15], although some cases were delivered at full term [5, 12, 14]. The birth condition of the twins in this study, however, may be attributed to multi-gestational pregnancy, but was more consistent with instances of preterm delivery, suggesting that the birth situation may follow a common pattern in FDR patients.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, genetic testing was only performed for one twin (A.), leaving the genetic status of the other sibling (B.) unconfirmed (due to the prohibitive cost of genetic testing in our clinical setting in Iran). Second, co-segregation analysis was not conducted in the parents or extended family, which could have provided additional evidence for the pathogenicity of the identified FDXR variant. Additionally, we acknowledge that the lack of formal determination of chorionicity/amniocity or molecular confirmation of zygosity is another limitation of our study.

Conclusion

This case report expands the clinical spectrum of FDXR-related disorders by describing twin sisters who presented with acute-onset flaccid quadriparesis following a respiratory infection, an atypical presentation for this typically progressive neurodegenerative condition. The identification of a homozygous FDXR variant (c.463 C > T) in one twin, coupled with their shared clinical course, strongly supports the genetic etiology of their condition while highlighting intriguing phenotypic variability between genetically identical individuals. Our findings underscore three key implications for clinical practice: (1) FDXR variants should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute neuropathies, particularly when inflammatory etiologies are excluded; (2) the c.463 C > T variant may be associated with both acute and chronic neurological presentations, suggesting potential modifier effects; and (3) systematic genetic testing of family members is crucial for understanding phenotypic expression in rare disorders. Future studies should focus on the functional characterization of FDXR variants and the exploration of targeted therapeutic strategies for this devastating condition.
