Higher-order metal–organic halogen-bonded cocrystals relying on a single supramolecular synthon
Authors: Lidija Posavec, Anuj Tripathi, Stefano Canossa, Martin Lutz, Katarina Pičuljan, Vinko Nemec, Mihails Arhangelskis, Dominik Cinčić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A proof-of-principle study is outlined for the construction of multicomponent metal-containing solids, i.e. three- and four component metal–organic halogen-bonded cocrystals (MOXB) based exclusively on I⋯S halogen bonds. This research represents a curiosity-driven exploration that yielded highly significant observations regarding the ability of the metal–organic multitopic acceptor to simultaneously engage in halogen bonding with a large number of halogen bond donors (up to five) due to the presence of an acceptor species, the isothiocyanate sulfur atom, which forms a robust I⋯SCN synthon. The effectiveness of this exploration is proven by the synthesis of a unique family of four ternary and one quaternary cocrystal using a Ni(ii) coordination compound, Ni(4-methylpyridine)4(NCS)2, and three perfluorinated iodobenzenes. All cocrystals were obtained as crystallization products from a solution of three (or four) reactants. Periodic density-functional theory calculations have been conducted to assess the formation energies of the obtained cocrystals and the strengths of the established intermolecular interactions. The presented research indicates significant potential of MOXB cocrystals in the efficient synthesis of higher-order cocrystals based solely on halogen bonds and could serve as the basis for an approach to designing higher-order cocrystals in general.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, rational design and targeted synthesis of higher-order cocrystals (i.e. ternary or more complex cocrystals) has become one of the most exciting and challenging areas of crystal engineering.1,2 Strategies and systematic construction of binary cocrystals (comprising two distinct molecules) are well-studied,3–10 and represent an important supramolecular solid-state approach in developing solid materials with new or improved properties, especially in the context of the pharmaceutical industry11–14 and materials chemistry, e.g. luminescent,15–17 photomechanical,18–20 optical21,22 or energetic materials.23–25 In contrast, fewer synthetic approaches have been developed for the synthesis of higher-order cocrystals. The first example of a general strategy for the synthesis of hydrogen-bonded ternary cocrystals, namely synthon hierarchy, was used by C. B. Aakeröy and co-workers.26 They cocrystallized isonicotinamide with equimolar amounts of two different carboxylic acids of different acidity, wherein the stronger acid engages in hydrogen bonding with the best acceptor to form an acid⋯pyridine synthon and the weaker acid forms an acid⋯amide synthon. In general, hydrogen bonds between molecules usually form in a hierarchical mode, in that the best donor connects to the best acceptor, the second best donor to the second best acceptor, etc.5,27–30 Later studies on ternary cocrystals have been expanded towards the use of various types of hydrogen bonds and other noncovalent interactions (i.e. halogen bonds), mostly in an orthogonal manner.31–40 A significant number of reports dealing with synthetic strategies for the preparation of higher-order cocrystals have been reported by G. Desiraju and co-workers.41–47 They expanded the synthon hierarchy approach by using a combination of hydrogen and halogen bonds and also devised other strategies: long-range synthon Aufbau modules,41 shape-size mimicry,42 structural inequivalence43 and the combinatorial approach.1,45 Overall, most reported ternary cocrystals (hydrogen-bonded, hydrogen/halogen-bonded and halogen-bonded) are based on small or medium-sized organic molecules that contain one or several donor/acceptor sites and act as multifunctional di- or tritopic building blocks.48 Molecules carrying bifurcated or trifurcated halogen or hydrogen bond acceptor sites, which can participate simultaneously in two or more hydrogen or halogen bonds, have only rarely been used. Furthermore, an overview of the Cambridge Structural Database48 reveals there has been little work done on higher-order cocrystals that are based exclusively on robust halogen bond synthons. There are only a few deposited data sets corresponding to this type of ternary cocrystals49–51 and most of them are reported by G. Desiraju and co-workers.49 In their study they used different approaches for cocrystal synthesis by a combination of simple aromatic nitrogen or/and oxygen-containing acceptors of different Lewis basicity with four halogenobenzenes of different acidity. Interestingly, halogen-bonded cocrystals with four or more components have not been reported in the literature.

In recent years, metal–organic halogen-bonded (MOXB) cocrystals have become an important area within crystal engineering, as they enable the incorporation of metal-related properties (e.g., magnetic or catalytic)52,53 into self-assembled materials, while metal complexes used as building blocks of multicomponent solids offer geometries not usually accessible with purely organic molecules.10,48,54–57 Therefore, this area of solid state chemistry bridges inorganic and organic chemistry and provides exciting possibilities for advanced material design. Our group has previously demonstrated a general strategy for the design and synthesis of MOXB binary cocrystals based on coordination complexes that can engage in halogen bonding via chloride or isothiocyanate ions coordinated to the metal centre as acceptors.58,59 Herein, we report highly significant observations that established a novel synthetic strategy for higher-order MOXB cocrystals. This strategy relies on a single halogen bond synthon and overcomes the limitations of previously established approaches, particularly the reliance on donors and acceptors with differing acidity or basicity, which often requires molecules containing multiple donor or acceptor sites. Our approach is focused on the ability of the metal complex to act as a multitopic acceptor that simultaneously participates in halogen bonding with multiple halogen bond donors, up to five, which surpasses the average case wherein individual organic or metal–organic acceptors engage two to three donors. This is facilitated by the presence of the isothiocyanate sulfur atom, which is predisposed as a bifurcated or trifurcated halogen bond acceptor, thereby enabling the formation of a robust I⋯SCN synthon. The sulfur atom of the isothiocyanate species remains underexplored and lacks recognition as an effective halogen bond acceptor.

Here, based on our strategy we report the synthesis of a family of four ternary and one quaternary MOXB cocrystals using the previously researched Ni(4-methylpyridine)4(NCS)2 metal complex (1) and three perhalogenated iodobenzenes, 1,4-diiodoteraflurobenzene (14tfib), 1,3-diiodoterafluorobenzene (13tfib) and 1,3,5-triiodotrifluorobenzene (135tfib) (Fig. 1). Calculated molecular electrostatic potential (MEPmax) values on donor iodine atoms of 135tfib (163 kJ mol−1 e−1), 13tfib (168 kJ mol−1 e−1), and 14tfib (173 kJ mol−1 e−1) are in a narrow range, which should ensure that each molecule interacts with the acceptor species in a similar manner. Similar bonding strength of halogen bond donors in cooperative mode is essential to prevent competitive bonding, where only one type of molecule with significantly stronger halogen bond donor potential preferentially interacts with the acceptor. Another important aspect of our strategy for incorporating a third molecule into the system is the topicity and structural similarity of the selected halogen bond donors.

The chosen perfluorinated iodobenzenes predominantly act as ditopic halogen bond donors in cocrystals with nitrogen or oxygen-containing acceptors.48,61 Notably, even 135tfib forms three halogen bonds only in cocrystals with more basic acceptors (i.e. pyridines), while in cocrystals with other acceptors it mostly acts as a ditopic donor.48,61 The selected compound 1 contains four 4-methylpyridine ligands and two isothiocyanate groups, and the calculated MEP for 1 confirmed the strong acceptor potential of both isothiocyanate sulfur atoms, with an MEPmin value of −158 kJ mol−1 e−1. By deliberately incorporating only a single acceptor type, we established a design strategy that minimizes the number of possible competing synthons, in contrast to previously used synthetic strategies for ternary cocrystals that rely on acceptor molecules carrying multiple acceptor types. Restricting the acceptor site to one single atom type limited the interactions to one specific I⋯S halogen bond synthon, competing only with other I⋯S synthons of similar strength. To the best of our knowledge, this approach is the first to demonstrate that a single halogen-bond synthon can be employed in the design and synthesis of higher-order cocrystals. Furthermore, it is particularly advantageous, as it enables a direct comparison of interaction strengths between different donor molecules within the resulting cocrystal structures.

Results and discussion

In order to achieve our goal, we first attempted cocrystallization of 1 with 14tfib and 135tfib. Since several studies have indicated that a mechanochemical route can be more effective in ternary cocrystal synthesis,62–64 we first performed cocrystallization by liquid-assisted grinding (LAG)7,65,66 of components in a 1 : 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio (see SI). The measured powder X-ray diffraction pattern (PXRD) of the obtained product did not match with those calculated from single crystal data of previously reported binary cocrystals (1)(14tfib)2 or (1)(135tfib)2. To confirm the potential formation of the ternary cocrystal, we performed cocrystallization experiments by crystallization from a solution of components and obtained purple plate-like crystals. Single-crystal X-ray structure determination confirmed the formation of the ternary cocrystal, (1)(14tfib)(135tfib), which crystallizes in the P21/c space group (Fig. 2). Unexpectedly, we found out that the PXRD pattern calculated from single crystal data does not match that of the LAG product. After a second series of solution cocrystallization experiments, we obtained blue plate-like crystals. Structure analysis revealed a new crystal form of the (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) cocrystal, which crystalizes in the C2/c space group, and whose calculated PXRD pattern is in good agreement with that of the LAG product (see SI). The supramolecular architecture based on halogen bonds remains similar in both forms, with each halogen bond donor engaging in I⋯S halogen bonding with 1. As anticipated, each sulfur atom acts as an acceptor of two I⋯S halogen bonds, one with a 14tfib and the other with a 135tfib molecule (Fig. 2). This arrangement of halogen bonds connects molecules of 1 and the halogen bond donors into a two-dimensional network, which is further extended into three dimensions through C–H⋯S interactions. A key distinction between the two polymorphic forms lies in the geometry of the halogen bond motifs. In (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c, the two donor molecules bonded to the same sulfur atom are positioned at an angle of approximately 90° to each other, whereas in the P21/c form, the donors are positioned in an approximately linear (∼180°) arrangement (Fig. 2). To explain the formation of ternary systems and gain a better understanding of driving forces that are responsible for the formation of higher-order cocrystals, periodic density-functional theory calculations (DFT) were performed, with the aid of plane-wave DFT code CASTEP 22.11,67 using the PBE functional68 with Grimme D369 dispersion correction. The aim of these calculations was to quantify the thermodynamic stability of the synthesized binary and ternary cocrystals with respect to their individual components, as well as to gain molecular-level insights into structure stability via individual intermolecular dimer interaction energies found in each cocrystal structure. An additional purpose of these intermolecular interaction energy calculations was to provide insights into the overall thermodynamic driving force associated with cocrystal formation. The molecular dimers selected for these calculations all included I⋯S halogen bonds. It needs to be emphasized, however, that dimer interaction energies cannot be strictly equated with halogen bond energies, since multiple supramolecular interactions contribute to the overall dimer interaction energies. In particular, the 4-methylpyridine ligands of 1 engage in π–π stacking interaction with the halogen bond donor molecules in multiple cases.

Crystal packing (left) and part of the crystal structure showing the halogen bonding motifs (right) in the prepared polymorphic ternary cocrystals: (a) (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c, (b) (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form P21/c.

First, we calculated formation energies for both binary cocrystals, (1)(14tfib)2 and (1)(135tfib)2, which are −41.3 kJ mol−1 and −36.7 kJ mol−1, respectively, indicating that the formation of binary cocrystals from their respective individual components is thermodynamically favorable. However, the results of mechanochemical reaction experiments suggested that (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c is the most stable product in the studied cocrystal system. Indeed, calculated formation energies for both (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c and (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form P21/c are −58.1 kJ mol−1 and −39.0 kJ mol−1, respectively. This also indicates that the most favorable outcome of the reaction of 1, 14tfib and 135tfib in a 1 : 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio is the C2/c polymorph of the ternary (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) cocrystal, as opposed to equimolar mixture of the binary cocrystals (1)(135tfib)2 and (1)(14tfib)2. Furthermore, the P21/c form is only slightly more stable than the (1)(135tfib)2 binary cocrystal and is less stable than the (1)(14tfib)2 binary cocrystal. Similarities in crystal packing become apparent when we compare the obtained ternary cocrystals with binary cocrystals that contain only a single type of halogen bond donor molecule. The crystal packing of (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c exhibits a layered structure, where donor molecule layers are inserted between layers of the coordination compound, closely resembling the packing observed in the binary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)2. The crystal packing evolution from binary to ternary cocrystals is evident, as the structure appears to incorporate an additional layer of 135tfib donor molecules. This results in an alternating arrangement of 14tfib and 135tfib molecular layers within the crystal lattice (Fig. 2a). Since the crystal packing of the ternary cocrystals closely resembled that of the binary cocrystals, we conducted a detailed analysis of all halogen bonding interactions to further elucidate the observed trend (Fig. 3). In the binary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)2, both crystallographically independent 14tfib molecules interact with a single sulfur atom, forming two dimer interactions of differing strengths, one stronger (−54.6 kJ mol−1) and the other weaker (−47.6 kJ mol−1). In the ternary cocrystal, only the stronger halogen bonds with 14tfib are retained, while the weaker halogen-bonded 14tfib molecules are replaced by competing donor molecules, in this case, 135tfib (Fig. 3). Guided by the observation that the stronger I⋯S halogen bonds with 14tfib are maintained in the structure of the ternary cocrystals, while weaker are displaced by competing donor molecules, we anticipated observing a similar trend in the ternary cocrystal containing 14tfib and 13tfib. To confirm our hypothesis, we performed cocrystallization experiments of 1 with 14tfib and 13tfib. Since 13tfib is a liquid at room temperature, we bypassed the LAG approach and conducted cocrystallization experiments from a solution of components in a 1 : 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio and obtained purple needle-like crystals concomitantly with other components. Single-crystal X-ray structure determination revealed that the cocrystal composition is (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5. When we repeated the cocrystallization experiment with a stoichiometric ratio of components as in the crystal structure (1 : 0.5 : 1.5) we again obtained the same ternary cocrystal, only this time in a higher supramolecular yield. Its asymmetric unit contains two crystallographically independent molecules of 1, three 13tfib molecules and one 14tfib molecule. As in both polymorphs of (1)(14tfib)(135tfib), in the crystal structure of (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 each isothiocyanate sulfur atom of 1 also engages in two I⋯S halogen bonds (Fig. 3). A similar progression of intermolecular interactions is observed when transitioning to the ternary cocrystal from the binary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)2. One crystallographically independent molecule of the coordination compound conforms to the previously observed trend, where stronger I⋯S halogen bonds (−54.6 kJ mol−1) with 14tfib are retained in the structure, while the other two are replaced by 13tfib donor molecules. On the other hand, the second crystallographically independent molecule of 1 only engages in I⋯S halogen bonds with 13tfib molecules (Fig. 3). Crystallographically independent molecules of 1 differ slightly in molecular geometry due to the flexibility of the isothiocyanate group which can bend to accommodate favorable intermolecular interactions. The calculated formation energy for (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 is −49.7 kJ mol−1, indicating that this cocrystal is only slightly more stable than the (1)(14tfib)2 binary cocrystal and less stable than the (1)(13tfib)2 binary cocrystal with a formation energy of −54.7 kJ mol−1.

Intermolecular interaction energies between XB donor molecules and the coordination compound in binary (1)(14tfib)2 and ternary cocrystals (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c and (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 with accompanying cocrystal formation energies.

We next performed cocrystallization experiments from solutions for the remaining combination of components, 135tfib, 13tfib and 1, in a 1 : 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio. We obtained dark purple plate-like crystals. Single-crystal X-ray structure determination confirmed that it is a ternary cocrystal, (1)(135tfib)(13tfib), which exhibits a nearly identical supramolecular architecture based on halogen bonds to the (1)(135tfib)2 binary cocrystal. In both cocrystals, each molecule of 1 engages in I⋯S halogen bonding with five neighboring halogen bond donors, where one isothiocyanate species of 1 acts as a tritopic acceptor, and the other one as a ditopic acceptor. Evaluation of interaction energies in (1)(135tfib)2 shows that one dimer interaction is significantly stronger (−72.6 kJ mol−1), whereas the remaining four exhibit similar strength, varying within ±3 kJ mol−1 (Fig. 4). A similar progression of intermolecular interactions is observed when transitioning from the binary to ternary cocrystal, where the strongest dimer interactions (−73.9 kJ mol−1) are retained in the structure. Of the five 135tfib molecules bonded to 1 in the binary cocrystal, three molecules are tritopic, with all three iodine atoms participating in I⋯S halogen bonds, while the remaining two molecules act as a ditopic halogen bond donor forming two I⋯S halogen bonds. These two ditopic 135tfib molecules are the ones replaced by 13tfib in the ternary cocrystal structure. This observation emphasizes the importance of donor molecule topicity as a crucial factor for introducing a second type of halogen bond donor molecule into the system. When the system contains ditopic halogen bond donor molecules, only molecules with the same or higher topicity can be incorporated to preserve the supramolecular architecture or enhance the complexity of the system. The topicity of donor molecules in the crystal structure also serves as a way to identify ‘weak spots’ and determine which donor molecules could be replaced to form a ternary cocrystal.

Intermolecular interaction energies between XB donor molecules and the coordination compound in binary (1)(135tfib)2 and ternary (1)(135tfib)(13tfib) cocrystals with accompanying cocrystal formation energies.

In all prepared ternary cocrystals containing the 14tfib donor molecule, very similar bonding patterns are observed. This can be attributed to 14tfib being the strongest halogen bond donor in the system based on ESP values. Additionally, molecules of 14tfib interact with the acceptor by forming a robust 1⋯14tfib⋯1 halogen bond motif that remains intact even after incorporation of other types of halogen bond donor molecules into the system (Fig. 5).

Preserved supramolecular motifs (I⋯S halogen bonds with 14tfib) in ternary cocrystals shown in purple.

Based on these results, we next explored the possibility of synthesizing a quaternary cocrystal containing all three halogen bond donors. Guided by the crystal structure of the (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5, we assumed that one or two molecules out of three crystallographically independent 13tfib molecules could be replaced with 135tfib. As both 13tfib and 135tfib share similar geometries we expected to obtain a quaternary cocrystal by preserving the supramolecular architecture of ‘predecessor’ ternary cocrystal. Accordingly, we performed a cocrystallization experiment of 1 with 14tfib, 13tfib and 135tfib in a 2 : 1 : 2 : 1 stoichiometric ratio, from a solution in the 1 : 1 (v/v) methanol/acetone mixture. We obtained purple needle-like crystals which, upon single-crystal X-ray structure determination were confirmed to be a quaternary solid solution cocrystal of crystallographically estimated composition (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.2(135tfib)0.3, featuring an almost identical halogen-bonded architecture to that of the ternary ‘predecessor’. Of the three crystallographically independent 13tfib molecules, two are partially replaced by 135tfib, sharing the same crystallographic sites with refined occupancies of 0.506(10)/0.494(10) and 0.895(8)/0.105(8), respectively (Fig. 6). For the purpose of the calculations, the stoichiometry of the quaternary cocrystal was modeled as (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)(135tfib)0.5, since using the structure with the crystallographically estimated composition would require generating a large supercell structure, significantly increasing the computational cost of the periodic DFT calculation. The calculated formation energy for the quaternary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)(135tfib)0.5 was found to be −49.1 kJ mol−1, very similar to that of the structurally-similar ternary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 (−49.7 kJ mol−1), justifying our strategy for generating the quaternary cocrystal from thermodynamic principles. Crystal structure determination of this quaternary system proved remarkably challenging due to very unique single crystal twinning, with many domain boundaries and diffuse scattering from the disorder (see SI), features that appear intrinsic to this cocrystal and characteristic of its crystallization behavior (based on crystallization experiments listed in SI) which could be a consequence of its similarity to the ternary ‘predecessor’ in both halogen-bonded architecture and formation energies. Despite these, single-crystal X-ray diffraction data of moderate quality afforded a reliable model refined in the non-centrosymmetric space group Pc. Furthermore, the repeated cocrystallization experiment, this time performed using the crystallographically determined stoichiometric ratio of components, yielded the same cocrystal (see SI), for which 13C NMR spectroscopy confirmed the presence of all three halogen bond donors within a single solid (see SI).

Parts of the crystal structures showing present halogen bonding in ternary (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 and quaternary (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.2(135tfib)0.3 solid solution cocrystal.

To understand the formation of the obtained ternary and quaternary cocrystals, we compared their formation energies and supramolecular architecture formed by I⋯S halogen bonds (Fig. 7) to ‘predecessor’ binary cocrystals. By substituting 14tfib in the binary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)2 with 135tfib, the supramolecular organization evolves from one-dimensional (1D halogen-bonded chains) to two-dimensional (2D halogen-bonded network) for both polymorphs of (1)(14tfib)(135tfib). A similar progression from 1D halogen-bonded chains in (1)(14tfib)2 toward a three-dimensional halogen-bonded architecture is observed for (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5. For all cases, this is a result of the introduction of a second type of halogen bond donor molecule which can form halogen bonds in a similar, although not identical manner to 14tfib, due to the different positions of iodine atoms in the molecule. Somewhat expectedly, no such progression is observed in the case of the (1)(135tfib)(13tfib) cocrystal, as both 13tfib and 135tfib share similar geometries and primarily act as ditopic halogen bond donors. Consequently, both binary and ternary cocrystals form nearly identical two-dimensional halogen-bonded networks. The same can be observed for the ternary (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.5 and the quaternary (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.2(135tfib)0.3 which exhibit identical supramolecular halogen-bonded architectures.

Crystal structure landscape of the studied system showing crystal packing of binary and higher-order MOXB cocrystals with accompanying cocrystal formation energies (relative to starting compound 1 and halogen bond donors) and cocrystal decomposition temperatures (Td).

Thermal analysis revealed that TG curves of the prepared higher-order cocrystals are of the same profile as those of ‘predecessor’ binary cocrystals.59 By evaluating the inflection point temperature for the first step of thermal degradation (Td, Fig. 7, see SI) it was determined that all ternary cocrystals, similar to their ‘predecessor’ binary cocrystals, show higher degradation temperatures compared to the pure compound 1 and their thermal stability is corelated with the nature of the halogen bond donor molecules. Despite differences in supramolecular architectures of individual cocrystals and in the melting points of the corresponding halogen bond donors, the thermal degradation temperatures of all prepared ternary cocrystals were remarkably similar. The halogen bond donor 13tfib is a liquid at room temperature with melting point at ∼23 °C, while 14tfib, and 135tfib are solids with melting points at ∼108, and ∼155 °C, respectively. Consequently, the ternary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)(135tfib) form C2/c exhibits thermal degradation at a temperature 3 °C higher than that of (1)(14tfib)2, at 108 °C, while (1)2(14tfib)(13tfib)3 degrades at a temperature 3 °C lower, at 102 °C. Similarly, (1)(135tfib)(13tfib) exhibits thermal degradation at a temperature 11 °C lower than (1)(135tfib)2, at 104 °C, which is 34 °C higher than (1)(13tfib)2. Finally, it has been shown that the quaternary cocrystal (1)(14tfib)0.5(13tfib)1.2(135tfib)0.3 is the most thermally stable of the higher-order cocrystals, with a decomposition temperature of 110 °C.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our work provides a proof-of-principle demonstration of a thus far unique approach to the synthesis of higher-order MOXB cocrystals that employs a metal coordination compound as a multitopic acceptor, a species that is continually overlooked by the crystal engineering community. In the context of materials science and supramolecular chemistry, the presented results are, to the best of our knowledge, the first reported ternary as well as quaternary cocrystals containing metal–organic building blocks, prepared by a strategy based exclusively on halogen bonds. This strategy relies on an isothiocyanate ligand whose pendant sulfur atom enables the simultaneous formation of multiple halogen bonds, functioning as either a bifurcated or trifurcated halogen bond acceptor, and is based on the ability of the metal–organic multitopic acceptor to engage simultaneously in halogen bonding with four or five halogen bond donors utilizing a single, reliable I⋯SCN halogen bond synthon. The robustness of our approach is evident by the synthesis of five higher-order cocrystals featuring two- or three-dimensional halogen-bonded architectures. Despite significant differences in component solubilities, all cocrystals were obtained as crystallization products from a solution of three (or four) reactants. DFT calculations confirm that all prepared ternary cocrystals exhibit greater stability than at least one of the ‘predecessor’ binary cocrystals. Overall, these findings show how halogen bonding, more specifically a single halogen bond synthon, can be utilized in the design and synthesis of higher-order MOXB cocrystals. This strategy addresses limitations of previously established approaches, particularly the reliance on donors and acceptors with differing acidity or basicity, which often requires molecules containing multiple donor or acceptor sites. Further systematic investigations of other higher-order MOXB cocrystal systems, employing diverse metal–organic complexes and the I⋯SCN halogen bond synthon, are ongoing. We anticipate that these results will serve as a basis for a general approach for the design and efficient synthesis of higher-order cocrystals, whether based solely on halogen bonds, on other directional intermolecular interactions, or on their combinations.
