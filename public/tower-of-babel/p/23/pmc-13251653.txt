Toward a Rigorous Framework for Studying the Gut–Brain Axis, Environmental Exposures, and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder
Authors: Tamar L Gur
Published: 2026

Interest in the gut–brain axis as a determinant of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders has rapidly expanded, driven by growing recognition of both environmental influences on brain development and by technical advances in microbiome profiling. In attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), this enthusiasm has generated an expanding literature but has also produced heterogeneity, variable methodological quality, and increasing translational claims (1, 2, 3). Against this backdrop, the systematic review and mechanistic synthesis by Wu et al. (4) in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science provides a timely and necessary recalibration of the field. By integrating evidence across environmental epidemiology, microbiome science, and neurodevelopment, the authors establish both a conceptual framework and a methodological standard for advancing gut–brain axis research in ADHD.

Drawing on 127 observational, experimental, and interventional studies, the authors evaluate the hypothesis that the gut–brain axis mediates the relationship between exposure to environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EEDs) and ADHD (4). Three convergent findings emerge. First, prenatal and early-life exposure to multiple EED classes, including phthalates, bisphenol A, pesticides, and persistent organic pollutants, is consistently associated with increased risk of ADHD diagnoses or symptom dimensions (4, 5, 6). Second, individuals with ADHD exhibit reproducible alterations in gut microbiota structure and function, most notably reduced microbial diversity, depletion of beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, and impairments in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (1, 2, 3,7,8). Third, EED exposure itself induces similar patterns of gut dysbiosis in both human and preclinical models, suggesting a shared biological substrate linking environmental exposure and neurodevelopment (4, 5, 6). Rather than treating these associations in isolation, Wu et al. A defining strength of this review is its methodological rigor. The authors closely adhere to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, including prospective protocol registration, transparent and reproducible search strategies, clearly articulated inclusion and exclusion criteria, independent screening and data extraction, systematic risk-of-bias assessment, and presentation of a PRISMA flow diagram (4). In a field where heterogeneity often undermines synthesis, this level of transparency substantially enhances confidence in the conclusions. The authors’ explicit justification for a narrative rather than quantitative meta-analytic approach, coupled with a structured qualitative sensitivity analysis, further underscores a commitment to methodological appropriateness over analytic convenience.

Conceptually, the review advances the field by positioning the gut microbiota as a plausible, potentially modifiable mediator linking environmental exposures to ADHD risk. The proposed framework highlights immune–inflammatory signaling, microbial metabolite pathways (particularly SCFAs), and neuroendocrine regulation via the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis as interacting mechanisms through which EED-induced dysbiosis may influence neurodevelopment (4). While each of these pathways has been examined independently in prior studies, their integration within a single developmental model represents an important step forward, particularly for ADHD, a disorder marked by etiological complexity and clinical heterogeneity (1, 2, 3).

Wu et al. (4) are careful to distinguish biological plausibility from established causality, which is to be commended. Although interventional studies, including probiotic and synbiotic trials and fecal microbiota transplantation in animal models suggest that microbiota modulation can influence ADHD-related behaviors, the authors appropriately characterize this evidence as preliminary. They highlight the urgent need for longitudinal designs, precise and harmonized ADHD phenotyping, repeated exposure assessment across developmental windows, and deeper functional microbiome profiling. This emphasis on rigor serves as a critical corrective in an area where clinical enthusiasm for microbiome-based interventions risks outpacing the evidence.

The review is particularly notable for its attention to the complexity of real-world exposures. By emphasizing emerging mixture-analysis approaches such as weighted quantile sum regression and Bayesian kernel machine regression, the authors outline a clear path toward analytic frameworks that better reflect human environmental biology. Integrating these approaches with longitudinal microbiome and neurodevelopmental data will be essential for identifying causal pathways and tractable intervention points (4,5).

From a translational and public health perspective, the implications are 2-fold. First, synthesis strengthens the rationale for policies that reduce early-life exposure to EEDs during sensitive neurodevelopmental periods (5,6). Second, it supports continued, but cautious, exploration of microbiota-targeted strategies as preventive or adjunctive treatments for ADHD (1,3,8). Crucially, the authors frame such strategies not as stand-alone therapeutics but as components of a broader systems-level approach to neurodevelopmental risk.

In summary, this systematic review represents a substantive and methodologically robust contribution to the literature. It makes clear that progress toward microbiome-based interventions in ADHD depends on careful, disciplined interrogation of the gut–brain axis, grounded in rigorous study design and restrained interpretation. By setting a high bar for both conceptual integration and methodological transparency, Wu et al. provide a credible road map for determining whether and how the microbiome can be responsibly leveraged in the prevention or treatment of ADHD.
