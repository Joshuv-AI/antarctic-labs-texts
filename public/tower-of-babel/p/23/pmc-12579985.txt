Transverse Tibial Transport: Surgical Technique and Insights From First International Case Series
Authors: Jing Yuan Wong, Matthew Sebastian, Qikai Hua, Chin Yik Tan, James H P Hui, Yongsheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic limb ischemia (CLI) is a significant health issue, particularly among patients with diabetes who are at elevated risk of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) due to peripheral neuropathy and ischemia. In Singapore, approximately one in six adults is affected by diabetes, with a lifetime risk of developing DFU ranging from 15% to 25%. This paper examines the indications, surgical techniques and postoperative protocols for transverse tibial transport (TTT), an innovative limb salvage procedure implemented in a general hospital in Singapore and presents our early experience with this technique. All six patients in our study successfully achieved limb salvage with complete wound healing, with a median healing time of 4 months. Four patients required additional wound debridement and skin coverage to facilitate healing. Importantly, none of the patients experienced pin site infections during the procedure or throughout the postoperative healing phase. Our study demonstrates favourable outcomes and underscores the clinical utility of TTT in augmenting the multimodal treatment of recalcitrant DFUs.Keywords: chronic limb ischaemia, diabetic foot ulcer, distraction angiogenesis, non‐healing ulcer, tissue repair and regeneration, transverse tibial transport

Introduction

Approximately one in six adults aged 21–69 years is affected by diabetes [1], and projections indicate that by 2050, half of the adult population will be affected [2]. Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) emerge from a complex interaction between peripheral neuropathy and ischaemia, frequently leading to ulceration and Charcot neuropathy, which increases the risk of infection [3]. The lifetime risk of developing DFU in individuals with diabetes ranges from 15% to 25%, with a 20% incidence of osteomyelitis [4].

The management of DFUs requires a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach, involving endocrinologists, vascular surgeons, orthopaedic and plastic surgeons, podiatrists and wound care specialists. The primary therapeutic objective in managing DFUs is to restore blood flow and regenerate blood vessels, along with optimal blood sugar control to reduce the risk of infection and promote healing [5].

Transverse tibial transport (TTT) is a surgical technique based on the law of tension‐stress, originally introduced by Ilizarov. Ilizarov et al. demonstrated that gradual bone distraction not only promotes osteogenesis but also stimulates neovascularisation, known as distraction angiogenesis [6, 7], and regeneration of adjacent soft tissues including specialised structures such as sweat glands—a process termed distraction histogenesis [8, 9].

In TTT, a medial tibial corticotomy is performed, and the bone segment is transversely mobilised using an external fixator. Gradual distraction applies the tension‐stress effect, enhancing local cellular metabolism, tissue regeneration and microcirculatory restoration.

The aim of this study is to introduce surgical technique and postoperative protocols for TTT as a novel limb salvage technique for recalcitrant DFUs that fail to respond to standard wound management modalities. This study showcases early experience with this technique implemented in a general hospital in Singapore, as well as the first international case series conducted outside of China. We also aim to further refine the indications of TTT in a new unit looking to adapt TTT as an adjunct therapy to treat recalcitrant DFUs.

As this was our first time performing the procedure, we aimed to ensure that TTT was not conducted on patients who were likely to recover with standard treatment alone. This approach was taken to avoid subjecting patients to unnecessary intervention.

All patients received comprehensive standard multidisciplinary care, which included vascular assessment and intervention (such as ABI/TBI measurements, duplex ultrasound and angioplasty) as necessary, surgical debridement and ray amputations performed by the orthopaedic team as needed, systemic antibiotic therapy, meticulous wound management, dietician and endocrinologist review. We offered TTT to patients who had shown evidence of failure or poor response to standard treatment.

Each patient was provided with comprehensive care by a multidisciplinary limb salvage team consisting of vascular surgeons, plastic surgeons, orthopaedic surgeons, wound care nurses and podiatrists. In terms of medical management, an endocrinologist assessed each patient, and nutritional supplementation was administered as recommended by a qualified dietician. To achieve seamless coordination and communication among the multiple disciplines, encrypted instant messaging service group was utilised.

Results

Six consecutive patients underwent TTT surgery between May 2024 and January 2025. Following the acquisition of written informed consent, comprehensive data on patient demographics, clinical presentation, treatment course and outcomes were prospectively collected from the hospital's electronic medical record system.

All six patients were male, with ages ranging from 42 to 79 years (mean: 57.67; median, 53 years). Each patient was diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, and five of the six patients had poor glycaemic control, as indicated by HbA1c levels greater than 6.4% (mean: 8.42%; median: 7.1%). Three out of six patients had complicated diabetes as evidenced by at least one manifestation of end organ damage (chronic kidney disease, retinopathies, neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, heart attack and stroke). Additionally, all patients had some degree of peripheral arterial disease, with 50% having undergone conventional vascular interventions before TTT. Two of our patients had extensive below‐the‐knee vessel calcification prior to TTT and none of them had desert foot. Two patients were smokers, and one was on haemodialysis. In terms of limb involvement, four of six patients had right‐sided lower limb involvement, and one had osteomyelitis. Five patients had previously undergone ray amputation, with a median of two toes lost. The total number of prior surgeries (excluding vascular procedures) ranged from one to 20, with an average of seven surgeries per patient.

On average, each patient required 2.83 cycles of TTT. All six patients successfully achieved limb salvage with complete wound healing, with a median healing time of 4 months. Notably, one of the patients' limbs was successfully salvaged with TTT performed on the contralateral limb, which was intended to stimulate the body's healing process and support the recovery of the at‐risk limb. Four patients required additional wound debridement and skin coverage to facilitate healing. Importantly, none of the patients experienced pin site infections during the procedure or throughout the postoperative healing phase.

Among the six cases examined, one patient exhibited a particularly complex instance of osteomyelitis, further complicated by implant infection and impaired wound healing after surgical fixation of a right ankle trimalleolar fracture. Prior to undergoing tibial transport surgery, the patient required 17 wound debridement procedures and multiple applications of negative‐pressure wound therapy in the operating theatre. Intraoperative angiography of the right lower limb revealed concomitant peripheral vascular disease. Given the severity of the condition, the patient presented with the option of limb salvage versus major amputation of the right lower limb. The patient opted for limb salvage and was subsequently transferred to our facility for TTT surgery on the contralateral limb, in conjunction with further wound management. After TTT, the patient underwent four additional wound debridement surgeries in the operating theatre, followed by wound coverage with a free flap and skin graft. However, the flap failed after 2 weeks, necessitating removal. The wound was thereafter managed with ongoing debridement and negative‐pressure wound therapy. The patient later underwent great toe ray amputation with exchange of an antibiotic rod and absorbable antibiotic carrier. Owing to the patient's medical condition and persistent postoperative hypotension, which required inotropic support and intensive care monitoring, further surgical interventions were not feasible. Nonetheless, the patient's right lower limb was successfully salvaged through meticulous wound care, bedside wound debridement and the TTT technique. The wound was ultimately healed by secondary intention over an 8‐month period.

Overall, all six patients, who had previously demonstrated evidence of failed conventional therapy that would likely have led to major amputation if left unaddressed, were successfully salvaged. Following TTT, the majority of patients did not require further debridement in the operating theatre, necessitating only limited bedside debridement during dressing changes. Discussion

TTT builds on the law of tension‐stress, first introduced by Ilizarov, which underscores the importance of controlled bone distraction in promoting tissue regeneration [7]. Expanding upon these theories, Chinese researchers developed the TTT technique as a limb salvage strategy for severe recalcitrant DFUs, aiming to restore microcirculation and accelerate wound healing [10, 11, 12, 13]. Early clinical outcomes from China demonstrated promising results, particularly in wound healing and limb preservation, with recent international studies echoing these findings [14, 15, 16, 17].

Wound healing involves three phases: inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Chinese researchers demonstrate that TTT accelerates wound healing and limb salvage in DFU patients [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] Animal studies further reveal increased HIF‐1α expression, which promotes secretion of angiogenic factors, thereby supporting neovascularisation and tissue healing [24].

It is well recognised that TTT may enhance the systemic recruitment of healing stem cells and modulate local inflammatory response [25]. This observation is supported by a case in our study, in which contralateral limb TTT was performed due to extensive wounds and compromised soft tissue on the right lower extremity. Following five cycles of TTT over a 3‐month period, the wound size was reduced by approximately 75%. Such application highlights the potential for broader therapeutic applications of this technique.

In our current study, we also like to emphasise the critical importance of early TTT intervention in improving wound outcomes and enhancing the likelihood of limb salvage. In Case 4, the patient exhibited progressive gangrene despite undergoing all conventional treatments. With the implementation of early TTT, we were able to arrest the ascending limb infection and successfully avoid major limb amputation. Another notable advantage of the TTT technique is its adaptability, allowing the duration and number of distraction cycles to be individualised based on each patient's wound condition and inflammatory marker trends, as long as there are no pin site infections. Once the wound demonstrates clinical stabilisation—evidenced by the presence of healthy granulation tissue and a downtrend in inflammatory markers indicating controlled infection—the TTT external fixator can be safely removed. Subsequent wound management may be continued with standard dressing or debridement, followed by definitive wound coverage in collaboration with plastic surgeons, if necessary.

In terms of surgical pearls, we improvise the technique by employing a Kirschner wire to assist in locating adjacent drill holes during corticotomy, thereby avoiding stripping of the entire periosteum and preserving soft tissue integrity. Apart from that, postop pin track bleeding is frequently encountered, and this issue could be addressed by applying a Prolene stitch on either side of the second and third pins. Additionally, we also used adrenaline‐soaked gauze to dress the pin sites and added a bulky compression bandage at the end of surgery to tamponade the bleeding at the osteotomy sites.

The majority of our patients are presented in the acute setting; therefore, concurrent multidisciplinary support is essential to optimise outcomes following TTT. Upon admission, nutritional status and glycaemic control are managed in collaboration with certified dietitians and the diabetes care team, with ongoing follow‐up provided through outpatient polyclinics or endocrine clinics after hospital discharge. Equally important is patient and caregiver education, which enables active participation throughout the treatment process and contributes significantly to improved overall outcomes.

While evidence supports TTT's efficacy in enhancing perfusion and promoting healing in recalcitrant DFU, it should not be regarded as solitary therapy. Optimal outcomes could only be achieved when integrated with conventional interventions such as revascularisation procedures, debridement, infection control, for example, antibiotic‐embedded bone cement and negative‐pressure wound therapy [26, 27].

In conclusion, our international case series recognises TTT as a promising adjunctive approach for the treatment of complex DFUs by addressing vascular insufficiency, tissue regeneration and systemic recruitment of healing stem cells. We would also like to highlight the importance of earlier implementation of TTT to improve overall outcome and limb salvage rate of patients. Continued research is warranted to refine indications, optimise protocols, timing of TTT application and clarify long‐term outcomes. TTT is not anticipated to replace conventional treatment methods, such as efficient surgical debridement, medical management, meticulous postoperative wound care and revascularisation. Instead, this technique should be incorporated as part of the existing multidisciplinary strategy to improve limb salvage rates in patients with severe, treatment‐resistant DFU.

Conclusion

Traditional treatments for severe DFUs exhibit inherent limitations, highlighting the necessity for alternative strategies to enhance healing and avert amputation. The TTT technique has emerged as an innovative limb salvage method, demonstrating promising outcomes in the present case series. Nevertheless, this technique is associated with several risks, including tibial stress fractures, iatrogenic tibial fractures, cross‐contamination between the infected foot wound and clean tibial pin wounds, pin site infections and skin necrosis at the surgical site. Notably, the duration of postoperative follow‐up in this study was relatively short, rendering the long‐term impact of TTT on the reduction of future major limb amputations uncertain.

While the findings suggest that TTT may contribute to wound healing and the prevention of major amputations in patients with DFU, it is crucial to acknowledge that managing this condition requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach. This includes the expertise of infectious disease specialists, podiatrists, orthopaedic surgeons, vascular surgeons, wound care nurses, endocrinologists and dieticians.

It is important to highlight that the indications for TTT at our institution differ from those outlined in the Chinese Association of Orthopaedic Surgeons (CAOS) clinical guidelines [5], which recommend TTT for patients with dry DFUs without signs of active infection. At our centre, TTT is not offered as a first‐line intervention for DFUs, nor is it considered for wounds with a high likelihood of healing through conventional methods. Instead, TTT is reserved for patients with DFUs that are either worsening or showing no signs of improvement, despite the exhaustion of standard treatment options.

The application of TTT has garnered increasing attention from clinicians globally due to its promising outcomes and relatively short learning curve [25, 28]. However, the absence of standardised protocols and a comprehensive understanding of their mechanisms of action has resulted in variability in their application, management and efficacy. Future research should focus on refining the patient selection criteria for surgery and further investigating the underlying mechanisms of action of TTT.
