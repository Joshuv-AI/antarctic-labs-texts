Thioredoxin-interacting protein as a crucial regulator in asthma exacerbation induced by copper oxide nanoparticles in a mouse model
Authors: Woong-Il Kim, So-Won Pak, Sin-Hyang Park, Ba-Reun Jin, Junhyeong Lee, Min-Jung Park, Changjong Moon, Won-Kee Yoon, Sung-Hwan Kim, In-Sik Shin, Jong-Choon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper oxide nanoparticles (CuONPs) are increasingly used across various industrial applications, raising concerns about their potential toxicity and necessitating comprehensive safety evaluations. In this study, we first evaluated the respiratory toxicity of CuONP exposure in a mouse model of asthma. CuONP exposure alone exacerbated asthma symptoms, as evidenced by increased airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory cell infiltration, and elevated cytokine production with increasing thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) expression. We then investigated whether TXNIP functions as a critical regulator of asthma exacerbation by examining the effects of TXNIP on the same experimental models using induced TXNIP-overexpressed mice induced by intratracheal injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV)2/8 and TXNIP knock-out (KO) mice. In CuONP-exposed asthmatic animals, TXNIP overexpressed animals significantly increased airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory cell counts and the production of helper type 2 cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-5 and IL-13, as well as the IL-1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-10 compared with the green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing controls. These responses were accompanied by the elevation of inflammatory infiltration, mucus production and the expression of apoptotic markers in lung tissues. By contrast, TXNIP KO animals markedly reduced the pathophysiological factors, inflammatory responses, mucus production and the expression of apoptotic markers in lung tissue compared with the wild-type (WT) animals. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TXNIP plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses and cell death in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation.Keywords: Copper oxide nanoparticles, Asthma exacerbation, Thioredoxin-interacting protein, Apoptosis, Inflammation

Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory condition, currently affecting an estimated 330 million individuals worldwide, with its incidence continuing to rise [1]. Its development is influenced by a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures, including common allergens (e.g., pollen, pet dander), psychological stress, air pollutants, and respiratory pathogens [2,3]. These factors initiate immune responses that contribute to sustained airway inflammation and subsequent structural alterations, such as smooth muscle hypertrophy and excessive mucus secretion—hallmarks of airway remodeling [4]. In addition, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), characterized by increased airway sensitivity, can lead to bronchoconstriction and airflow limitation, resulting in symptoms such as coughing and shortness of breath [5]. Recent advances in nanotechnology have increased the risk of exposure to nanoparticles, which can exacerbate symptoms of respiratory diseases such as asthma, highlighting the importance of assessing the impact of exposure to nanoparticles [6,7]. However, the toxic mechanisms through which nanomaterial exposure affects asthma are still not fully understood.

Nanotechnology is an emerging field focused on the characterization and application of nanomaterials across various disciplines [8]. Over the past few decades, advances in nanotechnology have increased the risk of nanoparticle exposure, prompting extensive research into their potential effects on human health [9,10]. Among the many types of nanoparticles, copper oxide nanoparticles (CuONPs) are extensively utilized in the manufacture of solar cells, the development of antimicrobial agents, and as catalytic agents in several reactions, necessitating a thorough evaluation of their toxicity in humans [11,12]. Recent studies have demonstrated that inhalation exposure to CuONPs induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [13,14]. The thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) signaling pathway is known to be activated upon exposure to nanoparticles such as titanium dioxide and silica dioxide [15,16]. This suggests that exposure to CuONPs could also activate TXNIP, triggering oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, which may exacerbate underlying lung diseases. However, research in this area remains scarce.

TXNIP is a key physiological inhibitor of the thioredoxin (TRX) redox system, which regulates cellular redox homeostasis by inhibiting the antioxidative function of TRX [17]. One of the essential biological roles of TRX is to protect cells from oxidative damage by scavenging intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) [18]. Excessive accumulation of intracellular ROS can cause fatal damage to cellular components, making the regulation of ROS balance essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis, with TRX playing a key role in this process [19]. Under oxidative stress, TXNIP disrupts the interaction between TRX and apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1), leading to ASK1 phosphorylation and subsequent activation of apoptotic signaling pathways [20,21]. This stress-induced activation and translocation of TXNIP can promote the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, resulting in cellular damage [22,23]. Recent studies have shown that cells from TXNIP knock-out (KO) mice produce less ROS compared to wild-type (WT) cells, while TRX downregulation in TXNIP-overexpressing cells leads to increased ROS production [24,25]. Additionally, exposure to CuONPs in an ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma mouse model enhanced the inflammatory response [26]. However, definitive evidence supporting the role of TXNIP as a critical regulator in CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation remains limited.

In this study, we exposed an OVA-induced asthma mouse model to CuONPs to evaluate asthma exacerbation and TXNIP expression. Furthermore, we investigated the effects of TXNIP overexpression and deletion on CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation to determine whether TXNIP functions as a key regulator and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving the inflammatory response.

Materials and methods

Nanoparticles

CuONPs (particle size <50 nm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Before intranasal instillation, the particles were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and sonicated for 3 min to ensure dispersion. The morphology and primary particle size of CuONPs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Gemini500, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany), operated at accelerating voltages of 150 kV and 15 kV, respectively. Surface charge was evaluated by measuring the zeta potential using a zeta potential analyzer (ELSZeno, Otsuka Electronics, Tokyo, Japan). Elemental composition and purity were assessed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using a Zeiss Gemini500 SEM equipped with an X-maxN 150 mm2 silicon drift detector (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK).

Endotoxin assay for CuONPs

To determine whether CuONPs were contaminated with endotoxins, a recombinant factor C (rFC)-based endotoxin detection assay was performed. All glassware used in the assay was depyrogenated by dry heating at 250 °C for 1 h prior to use. The assay was conducted according to the manufacturer's instructions (AcroBiosystems, Newark, DE, USA). Endotoxin standards and CuONP samples were incubated at 37 °C, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 380 nm and 440 nm, respectively.

Animals and experimental design

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Damool Science (Daejeon, Republic of Korea) and acclimated for one week prior to the experiments. All mice were housed in a SPF facility during the experimental period. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chonnam National University (CNU IACUC-YB-2022-21 and CNU IACUC-YB-2023-112) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

To evaluate the effects of CuONP exposure on asthma development, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6): NC, CuONPs (0.5 mg/kg), OVA, and OVA + CuONPs. Asthma was induced by intraperitoneal injection of OVA (20 μg/mouse, Sigma-Aldrich) and aluminum hydroxide (2 mg/mouse, Sigma-Aldrich) in 200 μL PBS on days 0 and 14. Airway challenge was performed using 1 % (w/v) OVA aerosol for 1 h on days 21, 23, and 25 with an ultrasonic nebulizer (NE-U12, Omron, Kyoto, Japan). CuONPs were administered via intranasal instillation on days 20, 22, and 24. On day 26, AHR was measured using the FlexiVent system (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada) following tracheostomy, with PBS or methacholine (10, 20, and 40 mg/mL) as stimuli (Fig. S1A).

To investigate the role of TXNIP in CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation, both TXNIP-overexpressing and TXNIP KO models were established. TXNIP-overexpressing mice and green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing controls were generated by intratracheal instillation of adeno associated virus (AAV)2/8 into WT C57BL/6J mice one week prior to the experiment. TXNIP KO mice were obtained from the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (Cheongju, Republic of Korea), and WT C57BL/6J controls were purchased from same supplier. Mice in both genetic models (overexpression and KO) were assigned to the following groups (n = 6 per groups): NC, OVA, and OVA + CuONPs. The asthma induction, CuONP exposure, and AHR evaluation protocols were identical to those described above (Fig. S1B).

DNA cloning, preparation, and purification of AAV

A recombinant AAV plasmid was constructed to express mouse Txnip (NM_001009935.2) fused with a FLAG tag under the control of the CAG promoter, and the vector was synthesized using a commercial gene synthesis service (VectorBuilder, Chicago, IL). For virus packaging, the target construct (pAAV2/8), along with the packaging plasmid and helper plasmid pAdDeltaF6 (both from Addgene, Watertown, MA), were co-transfected into HEK293 cells at an equal molar ratio. After 72 h of incubation, the cells were harvested and lysed in a buffer containing 150 mM NaCl and 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Benzonase (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to degrade residual nucleic acids, and cell debris was removed by centrifugation. The resulting viral particles were purified using an iodixanol step-gradient ultracentrifugation (15 %, 25 %, 40 %, and 60 %) in QuickSeal tubes (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), followed by ultracentrifugation at 280,000×g for 3 h at 14 °C using a Himac CP80WX with a P90AT rotor (Hitachi). The viral fraction was collected from the interface between the 40 % and 60 % layers, concentrated with Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (Sigma-Aldrich), and washed three times. Mice were then administered purified AAV-TXNIP or AAV-GFP control via intratracheal instillation at a dose of 2 × 1011 viral particles per animal.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopic evaluation

Immunofluorescence analysis was performed as previously described [27] using anti-GFP antibodies (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and FITC-conjugated rabbit IgG (1:100; Sigma-Aldrich). Fluorescence images were captured using a Leica TCS SP5 AOBS laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) with a Leica 200 × (N.A. 1.4) objective.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and serum analysis

Two days after the final OVA challenge, mice were euthanized, and tracheotomy was performed. To collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), 700 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was instilled into the lungs through a tracheal cannula and gently aspirated; this procedure was repeated twice. The recovered lavage fluid was centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was stored in microcentrifuge tubes for subsequent cytokine analysis. Inflammatory cytokines in the BALF, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The remaining cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of PBS, and total cell counts were determined using an automated cell counter (Cell Countess III, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). For differential cell analysis, cells were cytocentrifuged onto slides using a CytoSpin device (Hanil Science Industrial Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and stained with Diff-Quik (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Inflammatory cell subtypes were counted under a light microscope at 200 × magnification. The proportions of eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes were calculated based on total and stained cell counts. Following BALF collection, whole blood was obtained via the caudal vena cava and centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min to isolate serum. Total immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels were measured using ELISA kits (BioLegend, San Diego, CA).

Histopathology

After BALF collection, the left lung lobe was immersed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin and fixed for 72 h. Subsequently, the tissues were processed for paraffin embedding, sectioned at 4 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Sigma-Aldrich) to assess airway inflammation. To evaluate mucus secretion, periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was performed using a commercial kit (IMEB Inc., San Marcos, CA).

Immunohistochemistry and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling assay

For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections were prepared as previously described [28]. Cleaved caspase-3 expression was detected using a rabbit monoclonal anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (1:200 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). Quantification of staining was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.51). Apoptotic cells were identified by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining, carried out using a commercial assay kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Positive cells were counted in four randomly selected fields per section at 200 × magnification.

Western blot analysis

Immunoblotting was performed as previously described [28]. The following primary antibodies and dilutions were used: p-ASK1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), t-ASK1 (1:1000; Abcam), Bax (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), Bcl-2 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), and β-actin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology). The secondary antibodies and dilutions used were: goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative protein expression levels were quantified using densitometric analysis with Chemi-Doc (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using analysis of variance followed by Dunnett's test for multiple comparisons. P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Physicochemical characteristics of CuONPs

As observed by SEM and TEM, CuONPs exhibited a predominantly spherical morphology. The average primary particle size was 43.48 ± 10.36 nm (Fig. 1A). Zeta potential analysis using the ELSZeno analyzer revealed a surface charge of −25.63 mV (Fig. 1B). EDS showed an atomic composition of 47.81 % copper and 52.19 % oxygen, yielding an atomic ratio close to 1:1, which confirms the material to be CuO (Fig. 1C).

Physicochemical characteristics of CuONPs. (A) Morphology of CuONPs analyzed by scanning electric microscopy (scale bar = 100 nm) and transmission electron microscopy (scale bar = 200 nm). (B) Zeta potential of CuONPs measured using a zeta potential analyzer. (C) Elemental composition of CuONPs determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, showing atomic ratios of Cu (47.81 %) and O (52.19 %) (scale bar = 250 nm).

The endotoxin level of CuONPs

To determine whether endotoxin contamination contributed to the observed effects of CuONPs, we measured endotoxin levels using a rFC endotoxin detection assay, both in the absence and presence of externally added endotoxin (0.5 EU/mL) as a positive control (Fig. S2). The results showed that endotoxin levels in all concentrations of CuONPs were below the detection threshold (0.005 EU/mL), indicating that the CuONPs used in this study were free from detectable endotoxin contamination.

Protein overexpression via intratracheal administration of AAV2/8

To achieve protein overexpression, AAV2/8 was delivered intratracheally as previously described [29]. To verify the efficiency of gene transfer in the lung tissue, GFP fluorescence was quantified. A significant increase in GFP expression was observed in the AAV-GFP group compared to the NC group (Fig. S3A). Additionally, Western blot analysis confirmed the upregulated GFP expression levels in the lung tissue (Fig. S3B).

Effects of CuONP exposure on asthma exacerbation in a mouse model

Compared to the NC group, the OVA group exhibited significantly increased AHR, total inflammatory cell counts, inflammatory cytokine levels in the BALF, and serum IgE levels. Similarly, the CuONPs group showed a significant increase in BALF inflammatory cell counts and cytokine levels relative to the NC group (Fig. 2A–K). In asthmatic mice, CuONP exposure further elevated the numbers of inflammatory cells—including eosinophils, neutrophils, and lymphocytes—as well as the levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-5, IL-13, IL-1β, and IL-6, compared to the OVA group. These findings were consistent with histological observations, which revealed enhanced inflammatory cell infiltration and mucus production in lung tissues following CuONP exposure. Similarly, immunohistochemical analysis showed markedly elevated TXNIP expression in the lungs of CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice, further supporting the pro-inflammatory effect of CuONP exposure (Fig. 2L).

Effects of TXNIP overexpression on asthma exacerbation-related pathophysiological factors in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice

Compared to the GFP control, TXNIP overexpression significantly increased AHR in both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice (Fig. 3A). TXNIP overexpression significantly increased eosinophil-dominated inflammatory cells in the BALF of asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls. In CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice, TXNIP overexpression also led to a significant rise in inflammatory cells, particularly eosinophils, neutrophils, and macrophages, compared to GFP controls (Fig. 3B–F). TXNIP overexpression significantly increased the key CD4+ T helper type 2 (Th2) cytokines IL-5 and IL-13 in both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls (Fig. 3G and H). Additionally, TXNIP overexpression led to a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, particularly in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice (Fig. 3I–K). Interferon gamma (IFN- γ) and IL-10 levels were significantly elevated in the CuONP-exposed TXNIP-overexpressing asthmatic group compared to control group (Fig. 3L and M). Total IgE levels were also significantly elevated in these groups (Fig. 3N).

Effects of TXNIP overexpression on histopathological alterations and TXNIP-related signaling in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice

Histopathological analysis using H&E and PAS staining was performed to assess inflammatory cell infiltration and mucus production (Fig. 4A). TXNIP overexpression significantly increased the accumulation of inflammatory cells in both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls (Fig. 4B). Similarly, mucus secretion was markedly higher in the TXNIP overexpression groups compared to the respective control groups (Fig. 4C). The expression of cleaved caspase-3 was confirmed through IHC. TXNIP overexpression significantly increased cleaved caspase-3 expression in both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls (Fig. 4D and E). TUNEL assays conducted to assess lung parenchymal cell death revealed that TXNIP overexpression markedly increased apoptotic cell death in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls (Fig. 4F). Apoptosis-related signaling pathways were also analyzed using Western blotting (Fig. 4G). TXNIP overexpression significantly increased the expression levels of p-ASK1, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 in both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice. In contrast, the expression level of Bcl-2 showed an opposite trend (Fig. 4H–K).

Effects of TXNIP deletion on asthma exacerbation-related pathophysiological factors in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice

TXNIP KO significantly decreased AHR in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 5A). TXNIP KO significantly reduced the numbers of eosinophils, macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes in the BALF of both asthmatic mice and CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 5B–F). TXNIP KO remarkably reduced the key Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-5 in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice compared to GFP controls (Fig. 5G and H). Additionally, TXNIP KO led to a significant decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, especially in CuONP-exposed asthmatic mice (Fig. 5I–K). IFN- γ and IL-10 levels were significantly reduced in the CuONP-exposed TXNIP KO asthmatic group compared to control group (Fig. 5L and M). Total IgE levels were also significantly decreased in these groups (Fig. 5N).

Discussion

The increasing use of nanomaterials has raised growing concerns about their potential toxicity [30,31]. CuONPs have been extensively utilized across various industries, and consequently, their inherent toxicity and impact on underlying diseases have been the subject of extensive research [32,33]. However, the mechanisms underlying CuONP-induced toxicity in pulmonary diseases remain poorly understood. In our previous study, we demonstrated that exposure of OVA-induced asthmatic mice to CuONPs exacerbated the asthmatic inflammatory response through activation of the TXNIP signaling pathway [26]. In this study, we further analyzed the mechanisms of toxicity using TXNIP-overexpressing and KO mice to determine whether TXNIP is indeed a crucial regulator of asthma exacerbation induced by CuONP exposure. In TXNIP-overexpressing asthmatic mice, CuONP exposure markedly enhanced allergic responses, as evidenced by increased AHR, inflammatory cell infiltration, cytokine secretion, and IgE levels, compared to control mice, while TXNIP KO asthmatic mice exhibited attenuated responses under the same conditions.

The generation of oxidative radical species is widely recognized as the predominant toxicological mechanism underlying cellular damage induced by nanoparticle exposure [34]. Specifically, CuONP exposure induces ROS production, which subsequently promotes the infiltration of inflammatory cells into damaged tissues [35]. Notably, even at low concentrations, cellular internalization of CuONPs can trigger substantial oxidative stress [36]. This excessive ROS accumulation can compromise the ability of cells and organs to detoxify ROS, ultimately resulting in the progression of cell death [37]. Consistent with these findings, our previous study demonstrated that CuONP exposure significantly increased AHR, mucus production, and inflammatory cell infiltration in asthmatic mice [26]. Collectively, these observations suggest that exposure to CuONPs exacerbates allergic asthma by generating ROS and enhancing inflammatory cell infiltration. Along with these changes, asthmatic mice exposed to CuONPs exhibited increased TXNIP expression, which in turn led to enhanced phosphorylation of ASK1 and the activation of downstream apoptotic signaling pathways. These results imply that TXNIP may play a key role in the mechanism underlying CuO NP-induced asthma exacerbation.

To elucidate how the degree of TXNIP expression influences CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation, we employed TXNIP-overexpressing mice—generated via the AAV system for targeted pulmonary expression [29]—and TXNIP KO mice. TXNIP acts as a key physiological inhibitor of the TRX redox system [17]. TRX plays a crucial role in protecting cells from oxidative stress by scavenging intracellular ROS [18,38]. Excessive ROS accumulation due to TXNIP upregulation leads to severe cellular damage and activation of the apoptotic pathway [19,39]. Under oxidative stress conditions, TXNIP expression is elevated, which suppresses TRX activity, thereby promoting ASK1 phosphorylation and subsequent caspase activation, ultimately resulting in apoptosis within lung tissue [40]. In this study, CuONP exposure in TXNIP-overexpressing asthmatic mice significantly enhanced ASK1 phosphorylation and downstream signaling activity compared to GFP-expressing controls, accompanied by increased apoptosis, destruction of parenchymal lung tissue, and inflammatory responses. Conversely, in TXNIP KO asthmatic mice, CuONP exposure resulted in a marked reduction in ASK1 phosphorylation and its downstream signaling, along with decreased apoptosis, tissue damage, and inflammation, thereby mitigating asthma exacerbation. Although TXNIP KO has been reported to affect hematopoietic stem cell differentiation and platelet production [41], our findings suggest that the attenuated inflammatory response observed in TXNIP KO mice following CuONP exposure is not due to a generalized immune defect. In the absence of exposure, both the total number and differential composition of inflammatory cells in BALF were comparable to wild-type mice, indicating preserved baseline immune homeostasis in the lung. Therefore, while systemic hematopoietic abnormalities may exist in TXNIP KO mice, our results imply that the protective phenotype observed is primarily attributable to local lung-specific mechanisms. Moreover, the observed reductions in ASK1 phosphorylation and cleaved caspase-3 expression in TXNIP KO mice suggest an overall suppression of the TXNIP/ASK1/p38 apoptotic axis. This is consistent with previous reports describing TXNIP as an upstream modulator of redox-mediated inflammatory and apoptotic signaling through ASK1 and p38 pathways [42,43]. Together, these findings imply that the protective effect observed in TXNIP KO mice primarily reflects the disruption of TXNIP-dependent oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling, rather than being a consequence of systemic hematopoietic or platelet abnormalities. In line with these observations, a similar role of TXNIP was demonstrated in our previous study using a COPD model, in which CuONP exposure aggravated disease severity through TXNIP overexpression via the same experimental design [44]. Collectively, these findings support the role of TXNIP as a central mediator of CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation.

While these findings are significant, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Our study primarily focused on the acute exposure to CuONPs; however, the potential effects of chronic exposure may be more pronounced, underscoring the need for further research. Although this study did not investigate chronic exposure effects, the data from acute exposure provide valuable baseline information to guide the design of future studies. Additionally, in the TXNIP KO experiments, CuONP-induced asthma exacerbations were significantly reduced, but a slight increase in inflammation was observed within the KO groups. This suggests that mechanisms other than the TXNIP/ASK1 pathway may also contribute to CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation, indicating that inhibiting TXNIP alone is insufficient to fully prevent the exacerbation. Furthermore, to explore the possibility of TXNIP influencing immune polarization, we measured IL-10 and IFN-γ levels in BALF samples from TXNIP-overexpressing and KO mice. These markers were chosen as surrogates for regulatory and Th1 responses, as shown in previous studies [45,46]. Although these data provide only a limited view of the immune landscape, they offer preliminary insights into the broader immunological context of TXNIP-mediated effects. Future studies incorporating comprehensive immune profiling-such as flow cytometry or single-cell RNA sequencing-are warranted to fully elucidate the immunomodulatory role of TXNIP in asthma exacerbation. Nevertheless, by focusing on the TXNIP pathway, our study identified, through various assays, that CuONPs significantly contribute to asthma exacerbation.

Beyond these mechanistic insights, recent studies have proposed inverse vaccination as a promising strategy to suppress autoreactive T cell responses [47,48], which may hold potential for mitigating exaggerated type 2 immunity and airway inflammation induced by nanoparticle exposure. Furthermore, considering the possibility of genetic polymorphisms in the human TXNIP gene, individual susceptibility to nanoparticle-induced respiratory toxicity may vary. These factors highlight the need for personalized risk assessment approaches and the refinement of current occupational exposure standards to ensure adequate protection for vulnerable populations. In particular, future studies evaluating the association between TXNIP genetic variants and individual susceptibility to nanoparticle-induced respiratory toxicity in human populations are warranted to enhance the precision of human health risk assessments.

In conclusion, CuONPs exacerbated the inflammatory response associated with asthma by increasing proinflammatory cytokine levels and mucus production, with TXNIP signaling playing a key role in this process. Upregulation of TXNIP led to increased phosphorylation of ASK1, activating downstream signaling pathways and resulting in more severe cellular apoptosis. Conversely, TXNIP KO attenuated asthma exacerbations by inhibiting ASK1 phosphorylation and downstream apoptotic signaling. Collectively, our results demonstrate that TXNIP serves as a critical regulator of CuONP-induced asthma exacerbation in various mouse models. Thus, therapeutic modulation of the TXNIP pathway may provide a novel approach for mitigating nanoparticle-induced asthma exacerbation and improving clinical outcomes in environmentally triggered respiratory diseases.
