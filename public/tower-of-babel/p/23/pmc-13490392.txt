Reconstitution of Ras-PI3Kγ membrane communication and feedback using light-induced signaling inputs
Authors: Sophia Doerr, Andrés Olavarrieta Colasurdo, Scott D Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReactions involving small GTPases and phosphatidylinositol phosphate (PIP) lipids serve essential roles in signal transduction at the plasma membrane. In cells, these distinct classes of molecules are linked through positive and negative feedback loops that give rise to emergent properties such as excitability and polarization. Here, we reconstitute communication and feedback between Ras GTPase and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase gamma (PI3Kγ)-mediated PIP3 production on supported membranes using purified proteins. We employ light-induced membrane recruitment to rapidly shift steady-state conditions and observe the spatiotemporal response of the signaling module. Alone, the Ras-PI3Kγ module exhibits transient and reversible activation due to global inhibition. The introduction of GEF-mediated positive feedback enables sustained threshold crossing and local amplification of Ras(GTP) and PIP3, resulting in a traveling, bistable wave of activity with characteristics of an excitable network. Spatial coupling between Ras(GTP) and PIP3 lipids depends on lateral diffusion and feedback circuit architecture. This work illuminates the roles activation thresholds, membrane diffusion, and positive feedback play in regulating the dynamics of Ras-PI3Kγ membrane signaling reactions in the presence of global inhibition.Subject terms: Lipids, Membrane structure and assembly, Biochemical networks

Introduction

To execute their specialized functions, cells must sense and rapidly respond to environmental signals. Two classes of molecules that are ubiquitously important for signal transduction at the plasma membrane of eukaryotic cells are phosphatidylinositol phosphate (PIP) lipids and small GTPases (Fig. 1a, b)1,2. Together, these molecules function downstream of plasma membrane receptors to regulate essential cellular processes like growth, trafficking, and cytoskeletal reorganization. Interconnected networks of positive and negative feedback loops regulate the spatial distribution and abundance of PIP lipids and active small GTPases3–6. Positive feedback in plasma membrane-associated signaling events functions to amplify external signals and break spatial symmetry through the rapid generation of molecular concentration gradients inside cells7–9. It is challenging to decipher how specific feedback circuits control the spatiotemporal dynamics of individual signaling modules in vivo due to redundancy and crosstalk within signaling networks. To study feedback circuitry in membrane signaling, a bottom-up reductionist biochemistry approach is necessary to complement a large body of existing cell biological research.

Phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) lipids are critical signaling molecules that are generated through the phosphorylation of phosphatidylinositol (4,5)-bisphosphate (PIP2) by Class I phosphoinositide 3-kinases at the plasma membrane10. In hematopoietic cells, the PI3K gamma (PI3Kγ) isoform serves critical functions in regulating chemotaxis, phagocytosis, cytokine production, and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)11,12. PI3Kγ is activated at the plasma membrane downstream of G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) and the GTP-bound form of Ras GTPase13–15. Since the Ras-PI3Kγ signaling module is broadly important for immune cell function, it is unsurprising that dysregulation of this pathway plays a role in numerous inflammatory diseases and cancers11,16,17. Studying the mechanisms that control communication between Ras and PI3Kγ has broad implications for our understanding of plasma membrane signal transduction.

Recent advances in fluorescence microscopy and supported lipid bilayer (SLB) technology have enabled researchers to directly visualize the membrane binding and activity of many peripheral membrane proteins in vitro with single-molecule resolution18–20. This includes studies deciphering mechanisms of RasGEF (guanine nucleotide exchange factor) autoinhibition and synergistic activation of PI3Kγ by Ras(GTP) and GβGγ13,21–23. Despite technical innovations, most kinetic measurements rely solely on monitoring the conversion of substrate to product in a single, irreversible direction. By contrast, biochemical reactions involving Ras and PI3K are frequently transient and reversible in living cells due to global inhibition imposed by GTPase activating proteins (GAPs) and lipid phosphatases (Fig. 1c)24,25. It is the dynamic interconversion between substrate and product that gives rise to distinct emergent properties, such as bistability26, excitability8, and cell polarity27. Although the Ras-PI3K signaling module exhibits membrane oscillations characteristic of cortical excitability in cells28–30, the molecular basis of this spatial patterning remains unclear. Due to the limitations of current biochemical reconstitutions of membrane signaling, it has not been feasible to reconstitute the Ras-PI3Kγ signaling module in the presence of feedback circuitry and global inhibition. As a result, there are gaps in knowledge concerning how Ras and PI3Kγ activity overcomes global inhibition to drive signal amplification and excitability.

Here, we reconstitute Ras and PI3Kγ activation reactions on SLBs and observe their spatiotemporal dynamics using Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence microscopy (TIRF-M). To mimic early membrane signaling events, we utilize the improved Light-Inducible Dimer (iLID) system31 to reversibly control the localization of peripheral membrane binding proteins and rapidly shift steady-state reaction conditions. By combining this light-inducible system with spatial control granted by a digital micromirror device (DMD), we locally trigger signaling reactions with varying input strengths and visualize the dynamic response of the Ras-PI3Kγ module. To investigate the impact of feedback on Ras(GTP) and PIP3 production, we introduce positive feedback loops mediated by GEFs to this experimental system. Using this approach, we determine the requirements for the Ras-PI3Kγ signaling module to overcome negative regulation imposed by a GAP and the lipid phosphatase PTEN (Fig. 1a, b). We demonstrate how positive feedback results in the spatiotemporal amplification of both Ras activation and PI3Kγ-dependent PIP3 synthesis. The dynamics of the resulting activity wave depend on a Ras activation threshold, feedback loop architecture, and the membrane diffusion of Ras(GTP) and PIP3 molecules. This work establishes an experimental framework for systematically studying how positive feedback enables membrane signaling reactions to locally outpace global inhibition and drive spatial patterning programs that control excitability in vivo. Deciphering how PIP lipids and small GTPases function in concert provides insight into the mechanisms that govern signal transduction across cell types.

Results

Spatial control of light-induced membrane recruitment

To study communication and feedback between small GTPases and PIP lipid-modifying enzymes, we adapted the iLID (improved Light-Inducible Dimer) system31 to spatially and temporally control the localization of purified proteins on supported lipid bilayers (SLBs). For these experiments, purified his10-mNeonGreen-iLID (referred to as iLID) was stably tethered to SLBs using nickel-chelating lipids and visualized by TIRF microscopy (Fig. 2a). In the absence of 488 nm (blue) light, iLID adopts a conformation that sterically occludes its SsrA peptide from interacting with its soluble binding partner, SspB (stringent response protein B) (Fig. 2a)31. Although iLID is considered closed in the dark, it is still conformationally dynamic and displays a weak affinity for some SspB variants31. To measure the degree of light-independent association between the two proteins, we compared the membrane absorption kinetics and relative equilibrium membrane densities of three previously isolated SspB variants (i.e., nano, micro, and milli)32 in the presence of membrane-tethered iLID (Fig. 2b). In the absence of 488 nm light, equilibrium membrane density of the high-affinity SspB(nano) variant was ≥ 10-fold greater than those of SspB(micro) and SspB(milli).

After allowing the SspB variants to reach equilibrium in the presence of membrane-tethered iLID, we compared their light-induced membrane recruitment. For these experiments, we used a single, 50 ms pulse of 488 nm light (at ~50 µW) to shift iLID into the open conformation and allow it to associate with SspB. In all cases, this resulted in an immediate increase in AF647-SspB membrane localization that peaked within 10 s of the 488 nm activating signal (Fig. 2b, c). SspB(nano) displayed the highest total membrane recruitment, SspB(micro) exhibited a modest increase in membrane intensity, and the change in the SspB(milli) signal was nearly undetectable. All three SspB variants dissociated from bilayers with exponential kinetics based on the reversion of iLID to the closed, dark-state conformation (Fig. 2b).

To mimic the polarized membrane signaling that occurs in vivo, we established an approach to spatially control the blue light-induced activation of membrane-tethered iLID on SLBs using a digital micromirror device (DMD) (Fig. 2a, d). For different 488-nm illumination patterns, we visualized the corresponding SspB(micro) membrane localization and defined the limits of our ability to detect iLID-SspB heterodimerization (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3). For all light patterns, lateral membrane diffusion of iLID caused SspB(micro) membrane localization to expand slightly beyond the initially defined region of activation (Fig. 2f and Movie 1). We could repeatedly activate iLID in the same membrane region without observing a decrease in SspB recruitment (Suppl. Fig. 4a).

Using spatially patterned light, we next sought to tune SspB membrane recruitment by modulating the intensity of blue light used to activate iLID. Visualization of SspB membrane recruitment as a function of blue light intensity revealed that iLID was maximally activated in the presence of light at 10% LED power ( ~ 15 µW, Fig. 2g and Suppl. Fig. 4b, c). Lower levels of activating blue light resulted in less SspB membrane recruitment. To quantify the fold change in SspB membrane recruitment, we accounted for both the basal level of light-independent recruitment and the maximum light-induced membrane recruitment (Fig. 2h). SspB(nano) and SspB(micro) both displayed much larger fold changes in membrane recruitment than SspB(milli) with SspB(nano) slightly outperforming SspB(micro) across a range of light intensities (Fig. 2i). Comparing dissociation kinetics revealed that the dissociation half-times for SspB(nano, micro, milli) were relatively insensitive to the strength of the activating blue light although the three variants displayed different dissociation half-times that were correlated with their respective affinities (Fig. 2j). Based on its relatively low level of light-independent membrane recruitment and moderately large increase in light-dependent membrane localization, we determined SspB(micro) to be the most versatile for reconstituting membrane signaling reactions.

Rapid and reversible Ras activation with spatial control

GTPases are toggled between their active, GTP-bound state and their inactive, GDP-bound state by GEFs and GAPs, respectively (Fig. 1b). To measure the kinetics of GAP- and GEF-mediated regulation of Ras, we visualized the change in membrane localization of a fluorescently labeled Ras(GTP) binding domain (RBD) derived from c-Raf kinase (Fig. 3a). Ras activation is often studied using GAPs and GEFs in isolation to drive reactions in a single irreversible direction. However, GAPs and GEFs can compete to establish distinct steady-state levels of Ras(GTP)33–35. The time scale of Ras activation can also lead to different cell signaling fates36. In many cases, receptor activation is followed by signal adaptation mechanisms that restore cellular Ras(GTP) concentrations to a basal level5 (Fig. 1c). To mimic the dynamic rise and fall in Ras activity that is commonly observed in cell signaling, we established conditions to trigger the transient light-induced activation of Ras in the context of global inhibition imposed by the soluble RasGAP NF1 (Neurofibromatosis type 1, subsequently referred to as GAP) (Fig. 3b). We conjugated purified Ras to SLBs in a physiologically relevant orientation using maleimide lipids, as previously described in ref. 21. To spatially control Ras activation, we designed and purified the catalytic domain of the SOS1 (Son of Sevenless homolog 1) RasGEF fused to SspB(micro) (subsequently referred to as GEF-SspB). Similar to SspB(micro) alone, we observed robust and transient recruitment of the GEF-SspB fusion to membrane-tethered iLID following patterned illumination with 488 nm light (Fig. 3c, Suppl. Fig. 1a, b, and Suppl. Fig. 4d–h). Immediately following light-induced membrane recruitment of GEF-SspB, we detected a local increase in AF546-RBD (active Ras) fluorescence intensity, indicating that we were successful in spatially controlling Ras activation (Fig. 3c, d and Movie 2). This local Ras(GTP) signal decayed back to baseline following membrane dissociation of GEF-SspB and GAP-mediated GTP hydrolysis (Fig. 3b–d). Importantly, the timescale of Ras activation and deactivation was comparable to signal dynamics observed in cells25,37.

In vivo, the total RasGAP concentration can range from approximately 50 to 200 nM38,39. However, these inhibitors are structurally variable and exist within complex regulatory networks making it difficult to estimate the total cellular concentration of active RasGAP. To determine the relationship between GAP concentration and steady-state Ras(GTP) levels, we tested our light-inducible Ras activation system over a range of total soluble GAP concentrations (Fig. 3e, f). For these experiments, we allowed reactions containing membrane-tethered Ras and GEF-SspB to reach an initial steady-state of Ras activation in the presence of global GAP-mediated inhibition. Then, we used a transient pulse of 488 nm light guided by the DMD to drive membrane recruitment of GEF-SspB. We quantified the corresponding fold change in Ras(GTP) on membranes (Fig. 3e, f). In the absence of GAP, we observed no light-induced Ras activation because all the available Ras molecules were already GTP-bound due to unopposed global GEF activity. Conversely, when GAP was present at the high concentration of 3.2 µM, most Ras molecules remained GDP-bound and the change in Ras activation upon illumination was undetectable. In the intermediate GAP concentration range of 400–800 nM we measured robust increases in Ras(GTP) following light-induced activation with GEF-SspB. These Ras(GTP) signals decayed back to the baseline in minutes. We used GAP concentrations between 400 and 810 nM for all subsequent optogenetic experiments. By combining transient, light-mediated Ras activation and global GAP inhibition, we established an in vitro assay that resembles the spatiotemporal dynamics of Ras activation in vivo.

Positive feedback drives Ras(GTP) amplification in the presence of global inhibition

In cells, GEFs bind activated GTPases or downstream products to generate recruitment-based feedback loops that amplify membrane signaling reactions4,40–42. We hypothesized that GEF-mediated positive feedback would alter the spatiotemporal dynamics of light-inducible Ras activation in our reconstituted system. To test this, we designed a simple feedback circuit that relies on a minimal RasGEF catalytic domain derived from SOS1 fused to a Ras(GTP) binding domain (RBD) (Fig. 4a). This chimeric GEF, which we refer to as GEFRas(GTP), binds with high specificity to Ras(GTP). Although SOS1 naturally exhibits positive feedback based on differential Ras binding to its catalytic and allosteric binding sites43, our engineered positive feedback circuit allowed us to bypass complexity associated with SOS1 autoinhibition and receptor-mediated activation. Measurement of Ras nucleotide exchange in the presence of GEFRas(GTP) and the absence of GAP inhibition produced a sigmoidal kinetic trace consistent with strong recruitment-based positive feedback (Fig. 4b). Moreover, in the presence of GAP and high GEFRas(GTP) concentrations ( > 30 nM), we occasionally observed the spontaneous formation of periodic bistable patterns of Ras(GDP) and Ras(GTP) on supported membranes (Fig. 4c). These patterns were reminiscent of previously reported phosphatidylinositol phosphate (PIP) lipid compositional patterns that form due to competing lipid kinases and phosphatases that exhibit positive feedback based on product binding18. Depending on the balance of GAP and GEF activity, we could shift the system to steady-state conditions that either eliminated Ras(GTP) or caused Ras to be activated across the entire bilayer. The kinetics of Ras activation and the spontaneous symmetry breaking observed in the presence of GEFRas(GTP) demonstrate that we were successful in introducing GEF-mediated positive feedback to our system.

Membrane signaling reactions often require a threshold concentration of activator to overcome global inhibition44–47. To determine the requirements to amplify and persistently outpace GAP-mediated inhibition following local production of Ras(GTP), we varied the intensity of activating light to recruit a range of GEF-SspB (i.e. initiator) membrane densities (Fig. 4d–f). These experiments were performed under initial steady-state conditions that eliminated spontaneous Ras(GTP) pattern formation through high GAP activity. Following a low, 2.2-fold membrane enrichment of GEF-SspB, we found that GEFRas(GTP) in solution was unable amplify local Ras(GTP) enough to overcome GAP-mediated inhibition (Fig. 4d–f and Movie 3). Instead, the local Ras(GTP) signal rapidly disappeared. Repetitive local stimulation with low-intensity light was similarly unable to trigger the Ras(GTP)-mediated positive feedback loop (Suppl. Fig. 5). By contrast, when GEF-SspB membrane enrichment was greater than 3.7-fold, the Ras(GTP) signal not only persisted long after GEF-SspB dissociated but also resulted in the fluorescent region expanding across the bilayer (Fig. 4d, f). Based on these results, this in vitro system requires a threshold density of ~35% activated Ras molecules ( ~ 350 molecules/µm2) to overcome global inhibition and amplify via Ras(GTP)-mediated positive feedback.

To confirm that the spatial amplification of Ras(GTP) was due to the presence of GEF-mediated positive feedback, we performed two identical Ras activation experiments with either GEFRas(GTP) or an equivalent concentration of GEF lacking positive feedback (Fig. 5a and Movie 4). Whereas Ras activation following light-mediated activation (30% LED power) in the control experiment remained local and transient due to global GAP-mediated inhibition, the presence of the GEFRas(GTP)-mediated positive feedback loop allowed the Ras activation reaction to locally outcompete negative regulation. The resulting AF546-RBD (active Ras) signal grew in intensity and expanded across the bilayer with an average velocity of 0.06 µm/sec (Fig. 5a, b).

The sharp boundaries and rapid expansion of the Ras(GTP) region we observed in the presence of GEFRas(GTP) are reminiscent of a Fisher-KPP wave48,49. This type of reaction-diffusion system has historically been used to describe gene flow in population genetics50. In its simplest form, the Fisher-KPP equation describes a propagating wave that arises from a combination of diffusion and logistic growth that depends on the maximum product density of the system. The relationship between wave velocity (v≈2D*knet) and wave front width (w≈D/knet) can be used to estimate the diffusion coefficient of the species that constitutes the Fisher-KPP wave. Based on an experimental wave velocity of 0.06 µm/sec (Fig. 5c) and a wave width of 10.5 µm (Fig. 5d), we calculated a diffusion coefficient of 0.35 µm2/sec for membrane-bound Ras(GTP). This estimate was in good agreement with the diffusion coefficient of ~0.5 µm2/sec measured by single particle tracking of AF647-RBD bound to Ras(GTP) (Fig. 5e).

In contrast to the original Fisher-KPP model, our reaction-diffusion system also contains a decay term due to global GAP-mediated inhibition of Ras(GTP). To estimate the decay rate, we quantified the kinetics of reversible light-induced Ras activation measured in the absence of GEF-mediated positive feedback (Fig. 5a, b). In this experiment, the decay in Ras(GTP) signal is due to a combination of diffusion away from the initial site of Ras activation and GAP-mediated inhibition (Fig. 5a). By quantifying the disappearance of the Ras(GTP) signal based on the total integrated membrane intensity of AF546-RBD, we eliminated the contribution of Ras(GTP) diffusion to the overall decay rate. Fitting the AF546-RBD signal decay to a single exponential allowed us to calculate a decay rate of 0.02 sec–1 (Fig. 5f).

Using experimentally measured kinetic parameters (Fig. 5c–f), we simulated the spatial distribution of membrane-bound Ras(GTP) as a Fisher-KPP wave in the presence of GAP-mediated decay. Like our biochemical reconstitutions, our simulations required positive feedback for signal amplification and wave expansion (Fig. 5g). Simulations based solely on Ras(GTP) diffusion produced broad wave fronts that lacked two distinct steady states (Suppl. Fig. 6a–c). Although diffusion is an essential parameter for wave expansion, logistic growth is required to create a sharp boundary between Ras(GDP) and Ras(GTP) bilayer regions. Comparing our simulations to the experimental data revealed discrepancies, including a ~ 5-fold increased wave velocity and a more diffuse wave front (Fig. 5g). To reconcile these differences, we considered that wave propagation in our experimental system requires a threshold density of Ras(GTP) to stimulate amplification and overcome global GAP-mediated inhibition following light-induced activation (Fig. 4d–f). Ras(GTP) molecules that diffuse outside the boundaries of the wave are rapidly inactivated by GAP. When we added a Ras activation threshold to our model, the simulated bistable Ras(GTP) wave displayed a sharper wave boundary, slower velocity, and more distinct steady-states that mirrored our experimental data (Fig. 5g, h). Similar wave dynamics were observed in simulations using diffusion coefficients ranging from 0.3-0.6 µm2/sec (Suppl. Fig. 7). However, simulations with faster Ras(GTP) diffusion (i.e. >0.7 µm2/sec) could not support signal amplification and wave expansion since the positive feedback circuit could not sustain activity above the activation threshold and outpace GAP-mediated inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Reconstitution of Ras(GTP)-PI3Kγ communication with global inhibition

Cell surface receptors and GTPase signaling are critical for the activation of Class I phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3Ks), which phosphorylate PIP2 at the plasma membrane to generate PIP31,16. The PI3K gamma (PI3Kγ) variant is highly expressed in the immune system and is activated by GTP-bound Ras and GβGγ subunits (Fig. 6a)12–15,51. We set out to determine how these factors localize and activate PI3Kγ using purified proteins on SLBs containing PIP2. We visualized kinase localization by imaging AF647-labeled PI3Kγ and simultaneously monitored lipid phosphorylation activity using a fluorescently labeled PIP3 lipid binding domain (LBD) derived from Btk (Bruton’s tyrosine kinase). We found that Ras(GTP) alone did not facilitate PI3Kγ membrane localization, whereas membrane-bound GβGγ could recruit and activate PI3Kγ. Notably, when both Ras(GTP) and GβGγ were present, we observed a ~ 2-fold increase in PI3Kγ membrane localization and a corresponding ~2-fold increase in the rate of PIP3 generation compared to with GβGγ alone (Fig. 6b and Suppl. Fig. 8a, b). These results are consistent with the findings of previous biochemical studies of PI3Kγ activation13.

In cells, PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog) lipid phosphatase limits spurious PI3K signaling by converting PIP3 back to PIP224. By including soluble PTEN in our reconstitution, we were able to balance GβGγ-dependent, Ras-independent generation of PIP3 by PI3Kγ. When we then rapidly activated a high density of Ras molecules by introducing GEF to this reaction, we found that PI3Kγ activity was able to overcome PTEN-mediated lipid dephosphorylation (Suppl. Fig. 8c, d). PI3Kγ, membrane-bound Ras, membrane-bound GβGγ, and PTEN comprise a switchable, reconstituted signaling module that only leads to PIP3 generation when Ras is activated by a GEF.

Diffusion modulates spatial coupling between Ras(GTP) and PIP3 synthesis in the presence of positive feedback

To study the spatial coupling between Ras(GTP) and PIP3 production on membranes, we combined our reconstituted Ras-PI3Kγ module in the presence of global inhibition (i.e., GAP and PTEN) with iLID-SspB-dependent light-inducible Ras activation (Fig. 6c). First, we examined the spatiotemporal amplification of Ras(GTP) and PIP3 following transient membrane recruitment of the GEF-SspB initiator in the presence of GEFRas(GTP)-mediated positive feedback (Fig. 6c–e). As previously observed, light-induced recruitment of GEF-SspB resulted in the local production of Ras(GTP), which was then spatially amplified by GEFRas(GTP). Compared to reconstitutions performed in the absence of membrane-anchored GβGγ, these Ras(GTP) waves expanded more slowly due to molecular crowding (Suppl. Fig. 9). However, the presence of GβGγ was critical for stimulating local PI3Kγ activity. Although PIP3 generation lagged behind that of Ras(GTP), the region of AF546-LBD biosensor fluorescence tracked the Ras(GTP) signal and expanded beyond the original light-activated region (Fig. 6d, e and Movie 6). These results demonstrate communication between light-inducible Ras activation and PI3Kγ signaling. A minimal GEF-mediated positive feedback loop allows both reactions to overcome global GAP- and PTEN-mediated inhibition.

In vivo, GEFs can also mediate feedback loops that drive GTPase activation based on PIP3-dependent membrane recruitment and activation4,40. Having successfully reconstituted Ras(GTP)-PI3Kγ communication in the presence of GEF-mediated positive feedback, we aimed to reconstitute a GEF-mediated feedback circuit that could amplify Ras downstream of PI3Kγ-mediated PIP3 production (Fig. 6c). Much like the GEFRas(GTP)-mediated feedback loop, we engineered this second feedback loop by fusing a Btk-derived PIP3 lipid binding domain (LBD) to a SOS1 GEF domain to generate a chimera we refer to as GEFPIP3. Following transient, light-induced membrane recruitment of the GEF-SspB initiator, GEFPIP3 was indeed able to drive the spatial expansion of Ras(GTP) and PIP3 generation on membranes (Fig. 6f, g and Movie 7). These amplification patterns exhibited similar dynamics to those we observed previously in the presence of GEFRas(GTP). The results of introducing GEFRas(GTP) and GEFPIP3 to our reconstituted pathway provide molecular evidence for the sufficiency of simple, GEF-mediated feedback circuits to produce spatiotemporal PIP3 signal amplification, an essential step in numerous cell signaling pathways.

Although the PIP3 signal was spatially correlated with Ras(GTP) in the presence of both GEFRas(GTP) and GEFPIP3, we observed a pronounced difference in the boundary steepness of Ras(GTP) waves when comparing the two feedback circuits (Fig. 6d–g). Positive feedback mediated by GEFRas(GTP) invariably resulted in a uniformly bright Ras(GTP) region with a steep wavefront (Fig. 6d, e). By contrast, the PIP3 wavefronts were less defined than those of Ras(GTP). Importantly, when Ras activation depended on the generation of rapidly diffusing PIP3 lipids due to GEFPIP3, the Ras(GTP) wavefront more closely resembled the gradual boundary of the PIP3 region. Overall, these observations are consistent with the markedly different diffusion coefficients of membrane-tethered Ras ( ~ 0.5 µm2/sec) and lipids within the supported membrane ( ~ 3 µm2/sec) (Fig. 5e and Suppl. Fig. 10). To model the effect of diffusivity on wavefront expansion and steepness, we simulated Fisher-KPP waves using a range of diffusion coefficients for the membrane-bound species (i.e., Ras or PIP3) (Fig. 6h, i). Following spatially patterned activation of Ras(GTP), simulated Fisher-KPP waves expanded at rates proportional to the diffusion coefficient. However, the steepness of the wave boundary decreased as diffusivity increased (Fig. 6h), a result that is consistent with the spatial coupling observed between Ras(GTP) and PIP3 in our reconstitution. These results support a model in which the lateral membrane diffusion of reacting species together with regulatory circuit architecture modulate the spatial coupling and evolution of membrane signaling reactions (Fig. 6j).

Discussion

This study establishes a framework for reconstituting communication and feedback between membrane signaling reactions involving small GTPases, PIP lipids, and the enzymes that regulate them. We devised an approach to reconstitute distinct steady-state levels of Ras activity on membranes in the presence of both positive and negative regulators. By suppressing spurious Ras activation through global, GAP-mediated inhibition we mimic a cellular state prior to receptor agonist-induced pathway activation. Unlike previous studies that relied on stochastic activation of a RasGEF to trigger Ras bistability on micropatterned membranes35, we utilize light-induced membrane recruitment of a GEF to locally trigger Ras activation on unconstrained bilayers. We demonstrate that reactions starting in a predominantly Ras(GDP) steady-state can be rapidly shifted to a Ras(GTP) steady-state in a spatially controlled manner. The light-based signaling inputs and corresponding responses occur over a biologically relevant time scale (i.e., seconds to minutes).

Using the iLID-SspB system, we reconstituted two GEF recruitment-based positive feedback circuits and identified regimes in which a reconstituted Ras-PI3Kγ signaling module could overcome global inhibition and amplify in space and time. We found that an initial threshold density of membrane-localized GEF is required to transiently outcompete GAP-mediated inactivation of Ras. When this threshold is met, positive feedback gives rise to an expanding bistable wave of Ras(GTP). Considering the relatively large number of GEFs recruited to supported membranes (i.e., 100–1000 molecules/µm2) via the iLID-SspB system, we have characterized a relatively deterministic form of Ras bistability. A similar type of reaction wave has been shown to stochastically emerge on reconstituted membranes when Rab5 GTPase activation is mediated by a Rabex5:Rabaptin5 positive feedback loop52. Although the reconstituted Rab5 waves travel at a similar velocity, the wave front is approximately five times wider (i.e., more diffuse) compared to the Ras(GTP) waves reported here. This can be rationalized based on differences in GTPase membrane diffusion, GAP/GEF reaction kinetics, and activation thresholds.

When Ras(GTP) activation is linked to PIP3 production, the spatiotemporal coupling of the two reactions is regulated by differences in the lateral membrane diffusion of Ras(GTP) and PIP3. The relatively rapid diffusion of PIP3 produces waves with less defined boundaries. PIP3 waves also grow in concentration at a slower rate compared to bistable Ras(GTP) waves mediated by GEFRas(GTP). When positive feedback is mediated by GEFPIP3, the spatial distribution of Ras(GTP) and PIP3 lipids becomes more similar. These observations are consistent with previous stochastic kinetic modeling showing that molecules with slower diffusion coefficients exhibit more robust polarity in two-component reaction-diffusion systems with positive feedback based on product binding7. Our simulations indicate that Ras diffusion in the range of 0.2–0.6 µm2/sec is critical for signal amplification and wave propagation (Suppl. Fig. 7). Based on our single molecule analysis of the Ras binding domain (RBD), diffusion of membrane-tethered Ras(GTP) is slower than expected for Ras alone (Fig. 2e). Although single molecule analysis of the GEF was not performed here, GEF binding is expected to reduce Ras membrane diffusion based on previous reports19,21,22. Molecular crowding caused by membrane-anchored GβGγ also slows Ras diffusion, which decreases the speed of Ras(GTP) wave expansion (Suppl. Fig. 9). Overall, slower Ras membrane diffusion is predicted to stimulate positive feedback and increase the probability of threshold crossing needed for bistability. In the future, a more comprehensive single-molecule analysis of the enzymes and effectors will help decipher which sets of parameters cause Ras(GTP) wave expansion to become a diffusion-limited versus reaction-limited process.

In vivo, an extensive network of positive and negative feedback loops establishes communication between Ras and PIP3. Historically, these signaling networks have been challenging to dissect due to redundancy, network crosstalk, and signal adaptation5,53. Positive feedback within the Ras-PI3K signaling pathway can at least in part be attributed to GEFs that are recruited to the plasma membrane by active Ras itself and direct interactions with PIP3 lipids54,55. These GEFs function analogously to the chimeric GEFs used in our assay although they contain additional layers of regulation, such as reversible autoinhibition. In nature, processive membrane association of RasGEFs also plays a role in increasing local Ras(GTP) density and promiscuous GEFs that bind multiple classes of small GTPases via their allosteric or catalytic domains amplify signaling events beyond direct Ras signaling19,21,56,57. There is also a large body of evidence supporting Ras-independent feedback circuits mediated by downstream effectors in the PI3K signaling pathway. For example, PIP3 generation promotes the recruitment of GEFs such as Vav, P-Rex, and the DOCK/ELMO complex, which activate Rac GTPase40,58–62. GTPases including Rac, Cdc42, and RhoG, which are activated in parallel and downstream of Ras, can also stimulate PI3K activity. PI3K-mediated PIP3 generation and Rac activation lead to the formation of branched actin networks which deform the membrane and produce force at the leading edge of migratory cells63,64. Dendritic actin networks also promote PIP3 production by delivering signaling molecules including PI3K and various GEFs to the cell cortex in a polarized manner53,65–69. Cell biological evidence points to the presence of myriad positive feedback circuits in signaling networks, and computational modeling has led to speculation about considerably more. Few of these circuits have been studied through biochemical reconstitution. Our study represents an exploration of a two regulatory nodes that are known to modulate hundreds of signaling molecules in living cells.

Small GTPases are understood to be key activators of Class I PI3Ks, and the relationship between these molecules is at the core of numerous signaling cascades57,67. Best known for its role in myeloid cells, the PI3Kγ isoform has been shown to be activated by GPCR-derived GβGγ subunits and GTP-bound Ras GTPases in vivo and in vitro12,14,15,51. In this study, we directly observed PIP3 generation by PI3Kγ in the presence of membrane-bound Ras and GβGγ. Using light-induced membrane recruitment of a GEF to initiate the local activation of Ras, we reconstitute communication between this GTPase and PI3Kγ-mediated production of PIP3 lipids. In agreement with previous biochemical studies13, we demonstrate that Ras(GTP) alone is unable to activate PI3Kγ. However, the presence of both Ras(GTP) and GβGγ leads to enhanced PI3Kγ membrane localization and PIP3 generation. In the presence of global PTEN phosphatase activity, we found that Ras activation is required for PI3Kγ-dependent lipid phosphorylation. Cells establish precise, system-level steady-state signaling conditions by balancing complex networks of positive and negative regulators. The total concentrations of cell surface receptors, small GTPases, GEFs, GAPs, lipid kinases, and lipid phosphatases vary between cell types, creating thresholds for activation, rates of signal amplification, and cellular outcomes70. The total cellular concentration of RasGAPs and RasGEFs can range from 50 to 200 nM38,39. However, the system-level concentrations and activities of GAPs and GEFs are difficult to estimate due to our incomplete understanding of their regulatory mechanisms (e.g., autoinhibition). This makes it challenging to approximate biologically relevant strengths of global Ras activation and inhibition. In our assay, we use a single soluble GAP (NF1) at concentrations between 400 and 800 nM which we experimentally determined to be sufficient to suppress most GEF-mediated Ras activation. This global, GAP-driven inhibition stands in for the intricate, system-level Ras regulatory modules that exist across cell types.

Steady-state cellular conditions can be altered by mutations to the molecules that comprise signaling network reconstituted here. Dysregulation of key nodes like Ras and PI3K has long been known to have severe cellular consequences, which has flagged these molecules and their associated regulators as important cancer therapeutic targets2,11,16,17,34,47,71,72. The global inhibitors used in our in vitro assay, NF1 RasGAP and PTEN phosphatase, are well-characterized tumor suppressors that prevent aberrant Ras and PIP3 signaling73,74. Cancer cell lines that have lost NF1 RasGAP expression exhibit elevated steady-state levels of Ras(GTP)75. Conversely, enhancing the membrane binding of PTEN in cells expressing constitutively active PI3K leads to a marked decrease in membrane PIP376. Biochemical reconstitution of GTPase and PIP lipid signaling in the presence of mutated GTPases, kinases, and their respective regulators will shed light on how the relationships between these molecules determine the properties of healthy and diseased cells. In addition to influencing steady-state conditions, alterations to regulatory modules via mutations or relative protein concentrations can affect how signaling networks transition between steady-states. For this reason, it is important to monitor the spatiotemporal dynamics of membrane reactions in addition to measuring steady-state conditions following perturbations to these signaling networks.

One aspect of membrane signaling that was not explored in this study is the molecular basis of negative feedback and the role it serves in regulating excitable reaction-diffusion systems. Negative feedback can broadly be categorized as either local or long-range. Long-range negative feedback mediated by cortical membrane-cytoskeletal tension allows for the establishment and maintenance of asymmetry over the entire length of a polarized cell77,78. By contrast, local negative feedback relies on the products of specific biochemical reactions to recruit inhibitors and is fundamental for signal adaptation. In the context of directed cell migration, local negative feedback prevents cells from becoming polarity-locked, thus maintaining their flexibility and sensitivity to changes in environmental signals3,5,9,58,79,80. GAPs, which directly oppose GEF activity, are natural candidates for mediating local delayed negative feedback53,81. Unsurprisingly, various GAPs have been shown to serve functions in directed cell migration82,83 and signal adaptation58. Like GEFs, some GAPs may be directly recruited to the membrane by small GTPases83. Additionally, there is evidence for different classes of GAPs binding to PIP3 and a downstream product, PI(3,4)P284–87. In addition to regulating GAP membrane localization, PI(3,4)P2 also promotes negative feedback by regulating receptor endocytosis at the plasma membrane88. Interestingly, actin networks, which promote PI3K activity and mediate long-range negative feedback, also participate in local negative feedback by inhibiting Rac and Rho GTPases58,89,90. Filamentous actin is hypothesized to deliver GAPs directly to the membrane and may also physically drive the dissociation of signaling molecules through network treadmilling53,91. Deciphering the role that GAPs, PIP lipids, and the actin cytoskeleton serve in mediating delayed negative feedback will be critical for reconstituting more complex excitable systems in the future.

Preparation of supported lipid bilayers

The following lipids were used to generate small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) and subsequently supported lipid bilayers: 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero- 3-phosphocholine (18:1 DOPC, Avanti #850375 C), L-α-phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (Brain PIP2, Avanti #840046X), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[4-(p-maleimidomethyl) cyclohexane-carboxamide] (18:1 MCC-PE, Avanti #780201 C), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-[(N-(5-amino-1-carboxypentyl)iminodiacetic acid)succinyl] (18:1 DGS-NTA(Ni), Avanti #790404 C), egg L-α-Phosphatidylethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (Ammonium Salt) (Liss Rhod PE, Avanti # 810146). To make SUVs, 2 µmoles total lipids were combined in a 35 mL glass round bottom flask containing ~2 mL chloroform.

Lipids were dried to a thin film using rotary evaporation with the glass round-bottom flask submerged in a 42 °C water bath. After evaporating the chloroform, the round bottom flask was flushed with nitrogen gas or placed in a vacuum desiccator. The lipid film was resuspended in 2 mL PBS [pH 7.4] to make a final concentration of 1 mM total lipids. All lipid mixtures expressed as percentages (e.g., 98% DOPC, 2% PIP2) are equivalent to molar fractions. To generate 50 nm SUVs, 1 mM total lipid mixtures were extruded through a 0.05 µm pore size 19 mm polycarbonate (PC) membrane (Whatman, Cat #WHA800308) with filter supports (Avanti #610014) on both sides of the PC membrane. To create supported lipid bilayers, coverglass (25×75 mm, IBIDI, Cat #10812) was initially cleaned with 2% Hellmanex III (Fisher, Cat #14-385-864) that was heated to 60–70 °C in a glass coplin jar and incubated for at least 30 min. After rinsing the Hellmanex cleaned glass with MilliQ water, the coverglass was etched with Piranha solution (1:3, hydrogen peroxide:sulfuric acid) for 10–15 min. Etched coverglass that was rinsed and stored in MilliQ water, was removed from the Coplin jar and rapidly dried with nitrogen gas before adhering to a 6-well, sticky-side chamber (IBIDI, Cat #80608). To create a supported lipid bilayer, a total lipid concentration of 0.25 mM SUVs in PBS [pH 7.4] was flowed into the IBIDI chamber. After 30 min of incubation, supported membranes were washed with 5 mL PBS [pH 7.4] to remove non-absorbed SUVs. Membrane defects were then blocked for ~5 min with 1 mg/mL β-casein (ThermoFisher, Cat #37528) diluted in PBS [pH 7.4]. To reduce the fraction of immobile proteins on SLBs, the 10 mg/mL β-casein solution was centrifuged in a TLA120.2 at 100,000 rpm (434,513 x g) for 30 min at 4 °C using an Optima MAX-TL ultracentrifuge. After blocking bilayers with β-casein, membranes were washed again with 1 mL PBS [pH 7.4] and 1 mL PBS [pH 7.4], 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µM GDP, 0.1 mM TCEP prior to H-Ras conjugation. Prior to imaging, supported membranes were washed with TIRF-M imaging buffer.

Membrane conjugation

For experiments involving membrane-conjugated H-Ras, β-casein-blocked supported lipid bilayers containing MCC-PE lipids were incubated for 2 h with 30–35 µM H-Ras at 23 °C in buffer containing PBS [pH 7.4], 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µM GDP, 0.1 mM TCEP. Bilayers were quenched in PBS [pH 7.4], 5 mM βME for 10 minutes before washing with PBS [pH 7.4], 1 mM MgCl2, 100 µM GDP, 0.1 mM TCEP. The resulting Ras membrane density ranged from 1000 to 2000 molecules/µm2 as previously described in ref. 21. For experiments involving iLID, bilayers containing NiNTA lipids were incubated with 20–50 nM his10-mNG-iLID for 20 min at 23 °C. Unbound his10-mNG-iLID was flushed out of the chamber with TIRF-M imaging buffer. For experiments involving GβGγ (i.e. Figure 6), bilayers were incubated with 25 nM GβGγ (native farnesyl lipid anchor) for 20 min. Unbound GβGγ was flushed out of the chamber with TIRF-M imaging buffer.

TIRF microscopy imaging buffer

All supported lipid bilayer TIRF-M experiments were performed in 200 µl reaction volumes using the following reaction buffer: 20 mM HEPES [pH 7.0], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ATP (Sigma, Cat #A2383-10G), 100 µM GTP, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EGTA, 20 mM glucose, 200 µg/mL β-casein (ThermoScientific, Cat #37528), 20 mM βME, 320 µg/mL glucose oxidase (Serva, Cat #22780.01 Aspergillus niger), 50 µg/mL catalase (Sigma, Cat #C40-100MG Bovine Liver), and 2 mM Trolox (Cayman Chemicals, Cat #10011659). For experiments shown in Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 7b, ATP was initially omitted from buffer and 1 mM ATP was spiked in at indicated timepoint. Perishable reagents (i.e., glucose oxidase, catalase, and Trolox) were added 5–10 min before initiating biochemical reconstitutions on SLBs and acquiring images. All reactions in this study were reconstituted and visualized on a TIRF microscope at 23 °C.

Microscope hardware and imaging acquisition

Imaging experiments were performed using an inverted Nikon Ti2 microscope using either a 60x (1.49 NA) or 100x (1.49 NA) oil immersion Nikon TIRF objective. The x-axis and y-axis positions were controlled using a Nikon motorized stage, joystick, and NIS-elements software. Fluorescently labeled proteins were excited with either a 488, 561, or 637 nm OBIS laser diode (Coherent Inc. Santa Clara, CA) controlled with a Vortran laser launch (Sacramento, CA) and acousto-optic tuneable filters (AOTF) control. Excitation and emission light were transmitted through a multi-bandpass quad filter cube (C-TIRF ULTRA HI S/N QUAD 405/488/561/638; Semrock) containing a dichroic mirror. For bulk excitation of fluorescent biosensors, laser power measured through a 60x TIRF objective ranged from 0.5 to 2 mW. Fluorescence emission was detected on an iXion Life 897 EMCCD camera (Andor Technology Ltd., UK) after passing through a Nikon Ti2 emission filter wheel containing the following 25 mm emission filters: ET525/50 M, ET600/50 M, ET700/75 M (Semrock). All experiments were performed at room temperature (23 °C). Microscope hardware was controlled with Nikon NIS elements.

Spatially patterned light

A Mightex Polygon400 digital micromirror device (DMD) controlled by NIS elements was used for patterned illumination. For activation of membrane-bound iLID, excitation light from a SOLA SE light engine (Lumencor, 380–680 nm range of wavelengths) was filtered using a Chroma 488 nm long-pass dichroic mirror (25.5 × 36 x 2 mm; Cat #ZT488rdc-UF2). The dichroic mirror was mounted at 45° angle in a Nikon Ti2 LAPP (Cat #Ti2-LA-BM-E) prior to entering the microscope filter turret. This setup reflects iLID activating light from the Polygon400 DMD to the sample. The 488LP dichroic allowed transmission of 561 nm and 647 nm laser excitation light to image fluorescently labeled SspB, GEFs, and activity biosensors following activation of iLID. Following repetitive illumination of the same membrane region with 488 nm light, we observed a very slight increase in light-independent basal SspB recruitment. This potentially represents an accumulation of iLID protein that does not transition back to its closed, dark-state conformation. For this reason, we changed the field of view between light-induced activation steps in the same reaction chamber for subsequent experiments.

Image analysis

To calculate the fold difference membrane enrichment comparing the SspB fusion proteins in the dark (Fig. 2b), we compare the equilibrium membrane intensity at 280 seconds. The background intensity measured prior to adding AF647-SspB was subtracted from each data point before calculating the fold difference.

To calculate the fold membrane enrichment of SspB fusion proteins (Fig. 2h, I, Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4g) we took into account both the basal level of light-independent recruitment and the maximum light-induced membrane recruitment, as previously described100. Fold membrane enrichment of AF647-SspB fusion proteins was calculated using (basal light-independent recruitment + peak 488 nm light dependent recruitment)/basal light-independent recruitment. The same equation was used to calculate fold Ras activation in Fig. 3e, f: (steady-state light-independent Ras activation + peak 488 nm light dependent activation)/steady-state light-independent activation.

To calculate the decay halftime of the AF647-SspB membrane signal (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 4h), kinetic traces were fit to a non-linear one phase decay curve using Prism GraphPad 11: Y = (Y0 - Plateau)*exp(-K*X) + Plateau where Y0 is the Y value at time 0, Plateau is the Y value at infinite times, and K is the rate constant, expressed in reciprocal of the X axis time units.

Ras(GTP) wave radius (µm) (Fig. 4f) was calculated as a function of time based on AF546-RBD fluorescence using the manual thresholding Fiji/ImageJ plugin101. Starting with an 8-bit image stack, images were globally thresholded at a pixel value of 40. Outlier fluorescent speckles caused by immobilized proteins and membrane defects smaller than 9 pixels were removed. The images were subsequently eroded using Fiji/ImageJ. These thresholded image stacks were further analyzed using Python code executed in Spyder 6 software. For each image in the time stack, radii were measured from the centroid of the Ras(GTP) region at 15-degree intervals (24 total) and averaged.

To generate the intensity line scans in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6, images were first blurred in Fiji/ImageJ101 using a Gaussian equation with a variance of 1.5 µm. Intensities were measured along a horizontal line at the center of each image. To determine the steepness of Fisher-KPP waves (Fig. 6h) we calculated the derivative at the wave front inflection point using Prism GraphPad 11.

Single particle tracking

Single molecules of Rhodamine PE (Supplementary Fig. 10) and AF647-RBD bound to Ras(GTP) (Fig. 4e) were identified and tracked using the ImageJ/Fiji TrackMate plugin102. As previously described in ref. 99, image stacks were loaded into ImageJ/Fiji as.nd2 files. Using the LoG detector in the TrackMate plugin, fluorescent particles were identified based on their size (i.e., 6-pixel diameter), fluorescence intensity, and signal-to-noise ratio. The LAP tracker was used to generate trajectories that followed single-molecule displacement (or step size in µm) as a function of time. Particle trajectories were filtered based on Track Start (particles at start of movie were removed), Track End (particles at end of movie were removed), Duration (particles present for less than 2 frames were removed), and Track displacement. Immobilized particles were filtered out of the data at a frequency of 1–2% of all trajectories. The output files from TrackMate were then analyzed using Prism 10 graphing software.

Step-size distributions of single-particle trajectories were plotted in Prism. For all analyses presented in this article, the bin size for the step size distribution was 0.01 μm. For curving fitting, the step size distributions were plotted as probability density versus step size (μm). This was achieved by dividing the frequency distribution (i.e., y-axis values) by the bin size (0.01 μm). The probability density versus step size plots were fit to one- or two-species distributions as previously described in ref. 99.

Statistics & Reproducibility

Information about statistics and sample size is stated in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. Experiments were performed 3-10 times for reproducibility and statistics. For experiments that lack statistics, representative experimental results are shown. For example, montages presented in Figs. 4d, 5a, 6d, and 6f. For single molecule tracking of Ras(GTP) and lipids in supported lipid bilayers (i.e. Figure 5e and Supplementary Fig. 10), statistics were collected for > 6000 trajectories with two technical replicates. Simulations of Ras(GTP) waves were performed 2–3 times for reproducibility. Statistics for simulations were not collected because we used a deterministic model.
