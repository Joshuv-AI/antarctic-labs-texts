Iron Accumulation and Metabolic Alterations in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
Authors: Gardenia Pacheco, Joao A Gomes, Kevin Cho, Daniel L Morris, Maddison I Segal, Kayla R Adkins‐Travis, Ethan Stancliffe, Gary J Patti, Christopher J Ziegler, Leah P Shriver
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a severe type of stroke that is associated with poor outcome. A subset of patients with aSAH will develop secondary complications, most notably delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), which potentiates neurological injury. In this study, we investigate the relationship between cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) iron accumulation, brain metabolism, and neuronal injury in patients with aSAH with or without DCI.MethodsWe collected longitudinal CSF samples of patients immediately after hospitalization and 5 to 8 days after onset of ictus. CSF was analyzed with electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy and metabolomics to determine the presence of redox‐active iron species and metabolic alterations associated with aSAH and DCI. Neuronal pathology induced by iron overload was characterized in neuronal and meningeal cell models.ResultsElectron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy identified higher levels of an Fe(III) protoporphyrin IX (hemin)‐like molecule in the CSF of patients who developed DCI compared with patients who did not show secondary ischemic injury after aSAH or controls without neurological disease. Treatment of a human neuronal cell line with Fe(III)‐containing hemin resulted in the disruption of the axonal mitochondrial network and loss of viability. This was cell‐type dependent as a meningeal cell line was resistant to hemin treatment, despite both cell types upregulating the iron ferroxidase ceruloplasmin. Metabolomic profiling of the same CSF samples uncovered significant dysregulation of metabolic pathways associated with energy generation and amino acid utilization, consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction. Using machine learning, we identified a set of metabolites that predicted intensive care unit length of stay.ConclusionaSAH leads to the accumulation of an Fe(III)‐containing heme species in the CSF of a subset of patients who subsequently develop DCI. The accumulation of an Fe(III) protoporphyrin induces axonal mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to cell death. aSAH alters the CSF metabolome involved in mitochondrial function and a subset of these metabolites are predictive of intensive care unit stay. These results identify potential biomarkers for mitochondrial pathology and provide insight into alterations in brain iron metabolism triggered by aSAH.Keywords: aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, delayed cerebral ischemia, iron, metabolomics

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is responsible for 25% of stroke‐related deaths and significant disability in >50% of survivors. 1 In addition, secondary complications such as delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) contribute to poor outcome after aSAH. 2 Multiple pathophysiological processes have been characterized in aSAH and DCI, including secondary neurological dysfunction induced by the products of hemolysis 3 , 4 and hypoxia‐induced rewiring of cerebral metabolism. 5 The timing and relationship between these pathologies remain incompletely defined, hindering the development of new therapeutics to treat neuronal injury after aSAH.

In this study, we sought to investigate the relationship between dysfunctional iron handling and central nervous system (CNS) metabolism in patients with aSAH with and without DCI, by using a combination of electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy and longitudinal metabolomic profiling of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). We find the appearance of an Fe(III) protoporphyrin IX species in the CSF of patients with aSAH that can induce mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons. Furthermore, we identify metabolic signatures of aSAH that implicates early mitochondrial changes and sustained defects in nitrogen balance. Finally, we applied machine learning to this data set and identified a panel of metabolites that were predictive of the length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay and altered in both patients with DCI and patients without DCI. Together, this study identifies metabolic parameters in aSAH that are important for iron homeostasis and disease outcome.

Methods

Raw data are deposited in Metabolomics Workbench (study number ST003303).

Subjects

Consecutive patients admitted to the neurointensive care unit between May 2017 and June 2018 with the diagnosis of aSAH were screened for enrollment in an observational prospective cohort study seeking to assess oxidative stress in this population. Subjects were approached for participation in the study if they were aged between 18 and 80 years, admission Hunt and Hess grade was 2 to 5, had a clear time of onset of symptoms, and required insertion of an external ventricular drain (EVD) as part of their clinical management. High‐grade patients not expected to survive the index admission were not approached for participation. Patients were similarly excluded if the time of ictus onset was unclear, if no EVD was present, or if consent could not be obtained. All the patients approached for enrollment (or their next of kin/legal representatives) agreed to participate in the present study.

Demographic, Clinical, and Radiographic Data

Relevant data from subjects enrolled in this study were abstracted from the electronic medical record for the duration of the hospitalization. Age, sex, race, aneurysm location, admission Glasgow Coma Scale, EVD duration, ICU length of stay (LOS), hospital LOS, discharge disposition, blood gas analysis, and routine CSF analysis were recorded. The Hunt and Hess grading scale on admission (before EVD insertion) was abstracted from the electronic medical record. This metric is closely scrutinized as part of the comprehensive stroke center certification, and the various clinical teams undergo ample training to ensure accuracy in documentation. The modified Fisher scale was adjudicated by a member of the research team (J.A.G.) upon review of admission computed tomography scan.

The 90‐day modified Rankin scale score was obtained in either a subsequent outpatient visit or via telephone by a member of the research team. The simplified modified Rankin scale questionnaire was used. 6

Screening for DCI and clinical management were dictated by the clinical team and in accordance with current management guidelines. 7 Development of DCI was defined as a focal neurologic impairment (ie, hemiparesis, neglect, apraxia, aphasia, or hemianopia) or unexplained reduction in Glasgow Coma Scale score of >2 points lasting at least 1 hour, not apparent immediately following aneurysm occlusion and not attributable to other conditions (hydrocephalus, seizures, infections, etc), in accordance with the International Multi‐disciplinary Consensus Conference on the Critical Care Management of Subarachnoid Hemorrhage. 8

CSF Collection

Samples of CSF were obtained from the EVD within the first 24 hours (early sample), and once between days 5 and 8 (late sample) following the onset of ictus. Fluid was obtained from the burette attached to the EVD system, allowing only sampling of fresh CSF. A total of 6 mL were collected at each time point and immediately centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 minutes in the Cleveland Clinic Genetics core laboratory. The (noncellular) supernatant was aliquoted in small polypropylene cryovials and stored in liquid nitrogen to avoid auto‐oxidation of samples. 9 The time elapsed from sample collection to storage in liquid nitrogen was kept under 30 minutes.

Control CSF was obtained from patients with suspected neurological disease and seen at the “lumbar puncture clinic” that resulted normal after testing analysis and imaging studies. This study was conducted in accordance with all local institutional review board guidelines (#17‐271), and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants or their next of kin/legally authorized representatives. Additional CSF samples from patients with aSAH and controls were obtained as diagnostic remnants from Tissue for Research, Inc. (institutional review board waiver #202404115).

EPR Spectroscopy

EPR samples were prepared by combining 350 μL of patient CSF with 150 μL of polyethylene glycol within a nitrogen glove box to prevent oxidation. Samples were then kept frozen under liquid nitrogen before data collection. EPR experiments were conducted at the Ohio Advanced EPR Laboratory at Miami University (Oxford, OH). Continuous‐wave EPR spectra were collected at X‐band on a Bruker EMX continuous‐wave EPR spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA) coupled with an Oxford continuous flow cryostat (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK). Each continuous‐wave EPR spectrum was acquired by signal averaging ten 42‐second field scans with a central field of 2600 G and sweep width of 5000 G, modulation frequency of 100 kHz, modulation amplitude of 10 G, and microwave power of 10 mW at 297 K. Spectra were subsequently processed using the OAEPRL EPR processing package (NetSuite, Austin, TX).

Cell Culture

The SH‐SY5Y (ATTC CRL‐2266) cell line was cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The IOMM‐Lee (ATTC CRL‐3370) cell line was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 11.2% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% carbon dioxide. SH‐SY5Y cells were plated on poly‐L‐lysine–coated vessels and allowed to adhere overnight before differentiation using a modified protocol. 10

Animal work was approved by the Institutional Care and Use Committee at Washington University (protocol 22–0304). Mouse primary neurons were isolated from P0 pups. In brief, brains were isolated and placed in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) and then dissociated into a single‐cell suspension (neural tissue dissociation kit – postnatal neurons; Miltenyi Biotech, Auburn, CA) followed by magnetic bead separation (neuron isolation kit, Miltenyi Biotech). Cells were cultured at 37°C in MACS Neuro Media (Miltenyi Biotech) containing 1X MACS NeuroBrew‐21 (Miltenyi Biotech) and .5mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher) on poly‐D‐lysine–coated plates for 1 week before experiments. Media was changed by half every other day.

Treatment With Hemin

Hemin stock solutions were prepared in neat dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vehicle control) and diluted in media to treatment doses. Dose–response experiments were performed in 96‐well plates at varying concentrations of hemin and respective vehicle (% DMSO) for 24 hours. Cell viability was determined with a resazurin‐based in vitro toxicology assay (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Live Cell Mitochondrial Imaging

Differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells were treated with 24 μM of hemin or 0.12% DMSO for up to 3 hours and then stained with MitoTracker Red CMXRos dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes. Cells were imaged as top to bottom 0.5 μm step Z‐stacks on a Nikon TI‐E inverted confocal microscope. Images were compiled as maximum intensity Z projections in the National Institutes of Health Fiji open‐source software.

Primary neurons were treated with CSF isolated from subarachnoid hemorrhage patients and controls for 12 hours followed by staining with MitoTracker Red dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes. Live cell imaging on a Leica THUNDER imager (NCI Inc., Brooklyn Park, MN).

Immunofluorescence

Differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells were treated with 24 μM of hemin for 1 to 2 hours. IOMM‐Lee cells were treated with 250 μM of hemin for 1 to 2 hours. Cells were subsequently fixed, stained with anti‐ceruloplasmin and DAPI, and imaged on a Nikon TI‐E inverted confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry Analysis and Data Processing

Metabolite extraction from patient CSF samples was performed using a modified method. 11 Ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) was performed with an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC System interfaced with an Agilent QTOF 6545 mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Hydrophilic interaction LC was conducted with a HILICON iHILIC‐(P) classic hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 5 μm; HILICON, Umeå, Sweden). Mobile‐phase solvents were composed of A=20 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 0.1% ammonium hydroxide and 2.5 μM medronic acid in water:acetonitrile (95:5) and B=2.5 μM medronic acid in acetonitrile:water (95:5). The column compartment was maintained at 45°C for all experiments. The following linear gradient was applied at a flow rate of 250 μL/min: 0 to 1 minute: 90% B, 1 to 12 minutes: 90% to 35% B, 12 to 12.5 minutes: 35% to 25% B, 12.5 to 14.5 minutes: 25% B. The column was reequilibrated with 20 column volumes of 90% B. The injection volume was 4 μL for all samples. Data were collected with the following settings: capillary voltage, −3 kV; gas, 200 °C at 10 L/min; nebulizer, 44 psi; sheath gas, 300°C at 12 L/min; fragmentor voltage, 100 V; scan rate, 1 scan per second; mass range, 67 to 1500 Da; polarity, negative. LC‐MS data were processed and analyzed with the open‐source Skyline software 12 and XCMS. 13

Metabolomics data analysis and visualization was completed in MetaboAnalyst 5.0. 14 Features were putatively identified via DecoID by matching MS/MS fragmentation to library standards 15 and identifications confirmed with level 1 or 2 confidence according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative. 16 Heatmaps of row Z‐score values for the 49 metabolites identified were generated with the open‐source Morpheus (Broad Institute, https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus) software. Hierarchical clustering of the patients (columns) was completed using the 1 minus Pearson correlation metric with an average linkage. Graphs were made using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Machine Learning

Machine learning was performed as previously described. 17 Five different machine learning models: logistic regression, ElasticNet linear regression, partial least squares discriminant analysis, support vector machine, and random forest were tested on CSF samples collected at 24 hours and 5 to 8 days after aSAH ictus using a leave‐1‐out cross validation. The significance of the model fit was evaluated with a permutation test.

Statistical Analysis

Cell viability data are expressed as mean±SD. ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric tests were performed using Prism 9 software. The original false discovery rate method of Benjamini and Hochberg test for correction of multiple comparisons was used with a family‐wise significance value of 0.05 and a 95% CI. 18 Results of P≤0.05 are considered statistically significant. Statistical test used is specified in figure caption.

Study Participants

A total of 20 patients with aSAH and 10 controls are included in this exploratory report. Baseline demographics, disease severity at presentation, clinical course, and outcomes are outlined in Table 1. Control subjects were overall younger than patients with aSAH and included fewer women. Early CSF samples could only be obtained in 11 of 20 patients (55%), while late samples were available for the whole cohort. Criteria for DCI were met by 7 of 20 (35%, Table 2). A combination of hemodynamic augmentation, intra‐arterial vasodilator therapy, and angioplasty were used to treat DCI in this cohort. The majority of patients were discharged home or to an acute rehabilitation facility, while 2 patients died during the first 30 days.

Accumulation of a Hemin‐Like Porphyrinoid in the CSF of Patients With aSAH With DCI That Impacts Mitochondrial Organization and Cell Viability

Using continuous‐wave EPR, we first examined the CSF of patients with aSAH (n=10) for the presence of Kramer's doublet redox‐active iron species 5 to 8 days after onset of hemorrhage. Within this cohort, a subset of patients (n=6) developed DCI. Control samples from individuals without evidence of neurological disease (n=6) were used to establish the baseline for individuals without aSAH. In patients with DCI, EPR spectra show a peak characteristic of the presence of an Fe(III)‐containing species at 1180 gauss that is not as prominent or absent in patients with aSAH without DCI and control patients (Figures 1A and S1). To determine the identity of this iron species, we also analyzed a hemin sample and found that it matched the iron signature observed in the patient group with DCI. These findings suggest that a hemin‐like Fe(III) bound porphyrinoid accumulates in the first 5 to 8 days after hemorrhage in the CSF of patients with aSAH that develop DCI.

Next, we sought to determine the functional significance of the accumulation of Fe(III) in the CNS. Increased hemin levels have previously been shown to occur after intracerebral hematoma and can induce neurotoxicity. 19 To investigate this, we exposed 2 cell lines, SH‐SY5Y differentiated in retinoic acid, which models postmitotic neurons, 10 and the meningeal cell line IOMM‐Lee to hemin for 24 hours (Figure 1B and C). The viability of differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells rapidly declined with increasing concentrations of hemin with an IC50 value of ≈12 μM, but IOMM‐Lee cells retained ≈75% viability even after treatment with larger concentrations of hemin (up to 300 μM). We subsequently determined the time course for hemin‐induced toxicity by treating differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells with 24 μM hemin for 24 to 96 hours. At every time point tested, hemin induced a significant loss of viability compared with vehicle‐treated cells. In contrast, IOMM‐Lee cells remained viable even after treatment with higher concentrations (250 μM) of hemin for up to 96 hours (Figure 1D).

Iron overload can impact cell viability through a number of different mechanisms, including ferroptosis, 20 disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, 21 and mitochondrial fragmentation. 21 We used MitoTracker Red CMXRos dye to perform live cell imaging of mitochondrial dynamics in differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells after treatment with hemin or vehicle (% DMSO) for 30 minutes to 3 hours (Figures 1E and S2). Staining with this dye is dependent on the maintenance of the mitochondrial membrane potential in viable cells. 22 In vehicle‐treated cells, mitochondria are long and spindle‐like and extend into the axons forming an interconnected network (top row). Following treatment of cells with 24 μM of hemin over the course of 3 hours, adherent cells relocalized mitochondria toward the cell body and this was accompanied by a fragmented mitochondrial network within axons (bottom row). Similarly, treatment of primary neurons with CSF isolated from patients with aSAH lead to beading in the axonal network compared with controls, although less severe than seen with hemin treatment (Figure S3). Overall, these results suggest that excessive accumulation of heme containing Fe(III) adversely impacts the axonal mitochondrial networks and leads to cell death.

The cellular responses to oxidative stress induced by iron overload depends on the balance of antioxidant defenses, including expression of proteins for iron handling and storage such as ceruloplasmin. 23 , 24 We observed significant differences in the viability of differentiated SH‐SY5Y and IOMM‐Lee cells after exposure to hemin (Figure 1). This led us to examine the expression of ceruloplasmin in both cell types in response to hemin treatment. We treated differentiated SH‐SY5Y cells with 24 μM of hemin and IOMM‐Lee cells with 250 μM hemin for 1 and 2 hours, respectively, and then stained with anti‐ceruloplasmin antibody (Figure 1F, left). In both neurons and meningeal cells, hemin treatment for 1 hour led to an induction of ceruloplasmin expression (Figure 1F, right). However, by 2 hours of hemin treatment, more variable expression is seen in both cell types. Additionally, the increased expression of ceruloplasmin in IOMM‐Lee cells was observed only when the cells were exposed to a higher concentration of hemin (250 μM versus 24 μM for neurons). This suggests that iron overload can stimulate ceruloplasmin expression in cells but that neurons remain vulnerable to hemin‐induced toxicity despite this increased capacity for iron handling.

Time‐Dependent Metabolic Changes in the CSF After aSAH

To investigate altered metabolism associated with iron overload and mitochondrial dysfunction during aSAH, we performed untargeted metabolomics on the longitudinal samples of CSF isolated from the same patients with aSAH examined with EPR. To determine metabolic dysfunction associated with aSAH, we first performed unsupervised analysis of the metabolic profiles obtained from CSF collected from patients with aSAH compared with control CSF samples. Principal component analysis shows separation of all patients with aSAH from controls on the basis of their metabolic profiles (Figure 2A). We could further stratify patients on the basis of the time of collection, with the 24‐hour time point (early) metabolic profiles being distinct from the samples collected 5 to 8 days after aSAH (late, Figure 2B). Pathway enrichment of the most significantly dysregulated processes identified carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism as highly altered in aSAH compared with controls (Figure S4A). Of note, glycolytic intermediates and lactate were upregulated across both collection time points (Figure 2C and D), and this is consistent with the induction of a hyperglycolytic phenotype after aSAH. 25 Interestingly, when comparing metabolites that separated early versus late samples, the relative concentrations of CSF amino acids were consistently increased 5 to 8 days after aSAH compared with the 24‐hour sample. CSF collected at this later time point showed an accumulation of branched amino acids (leucine/isoleucine/valine) as well as alterations in serine, methionine, arginine, and glutamine (Figure 2E). Additionally, a paired analysis of CSF samples isolated from the same patients at both the early and late time points identified enrichment in pathways involved in amino acid utilization (arginine and serine metabolism) in the late time point samples compared with CSF collected 24 hours after aSAH (Figure S4B–D).

We subsequently sought to assess the predictive power of our longitudinal metabolite profiles by developing a machine learning model of disease outcome (ICU LOS). The predictors in our model were composed of 367 metabolites, and we evaluated the performance of different models including random forest, support vector machines, and linear models using a leave‐1‐out cross‐validation. A linear ElasticNet regression model using metabolite profiles collected 5 to 8 days after aSAH was the most effective model at successfully predicting ICU stay (R 2=0.24) (Figure 2F). The top 20 most important predictor metabolites included amino acids such as glutamine and valine as well as tricarboxylic acid intermediates and small‐molecule osmolytes such as glycine betaine (Table S1).

Metabolic Alterations in Patients With aSAH With and Without DCI

Since we saw an association between metabolic dysfunction and ICU LOS, we next examined whether CSF metabolic changes could distinguish patients with and without DCI. Patients with aSAH, regardless of whether they developed DCI or not, had increases in metabolites involved in energy generation and nitrogen metabolism when compared with control samples (Figure 3A). Interestingly, not all tricarboxylic acid intermediates were changed in patients with aSAH as citrate, succinate, and aconitate were similar to controls, while fumarate and malate accumulated in the CSF of both —patients with and patients without DCI. Additionally, increases in both tryptophan and kynurenine, 2 metabolites that have been linked to inflammatory responses in the CNS, 26 were also observed in patients with aSAH regardless of DCI status (Figure 3B). Finally, we examined whether our top 20 predictor metabolites associated with ICU stay were differentially regulated in patients with versus patients without DCI. Although there was a trend toward increases in phenylalanine and the antioxidant ascorbic acid in our patients with DCI, the majority of predictor metabolites had similar changes in both individuals with individuals without DCI (Figure 3C).

Discussion

The complex, multifactorial pathology of aSAH has limited the development of effective therapies for this condition. 27 , 28 In the current investigation, 35% of the enrolled aSAH cohort developed DCI, with the onset of symptoms ranging from 2 to 7 days after the initial ictus. While only a subset of patients with aSAH develop DCI, from a clinical standpoint DCI as a delayed complication is amenable to therapeutic intervention and early treatment could limit further neurological deficits. Here, we used complementary technologies, including EPR spectroscopy and LC‐MS–based metabolomics, to examine small molecules involved in metabolic dysfunction and oxidative stress that could be potential therapeutic targets.

The extravasation of blood into the CNS induces a cascade of reactions, occurring in the days and weeks following aSAH, that result in red blood cell lysis and heme breakdown that are thought to play a role in aSAH pathogenesis. 29 , 30 However, studies examining heme oxygenase‐1 in aSAH have pointed to both pathogenic 31 and therapeutic roles 32 for the breakdown of hemoglobin, indicating that the timing and efficiency of this process may be critical to altering the balance between oxidative injury and repair. Using EPR, we identified increases in ferric iron species coordinated by heme in the CSF of aSAH patients with DCI. This suggests an imbalance between ferric and ferrous iron species or incomplete degradation of heme that may potentiate oxidative injury. Consistent with our results, changes in the Fe(II)/Fe(III) ratio and accumulation of ferric iron in the brain have been linked to neurodegeneration. 33 We subsequently confirmed that this early accumulation of hemin‐like species could impact CNS function in cell models. Neuronal cells were highly susceptible to hemin toxicity, not only reducing overall cell viability, but also inducing dynamic reorganization of the mitochondrial networks within axons. These changes occurred despite increases in ceruloplasmin expression similar to meningeal cells that are more resistant to iron overload. Our results are in line with recent work demonstrating that dysregulation of mitochondrial morphology contributes to neuronal damage in aSAH at the early injury phase 34 and points toward a potential role for iron accumulation in mediating this pathology.

Coinciding with increases in ferric iron species in the CSF of our patient population are changes in glycolytic intermediates in the CSF consistent with altered cerebral glucose metabolism, which was maintained days after ictus onset. This hyperglycolytic phenotype has been suggested to serve a protective role after aSAH. 35 In contrast, amino acid accumulation within the CSF is a delayed feature of aSAH. Additionally, our machine learning model identified several amino acids as top predictor metabolites for length of ICU stay. Negative nitrogen balance is associated with poor outcome after aSAH. 36 However, more research is needed to determine the role of amino acid metabolism in aSAH.

A limitation of our study is the small patient number. Therefore, metabolite biomarkers associated with LOS must be validated in a wider patient population. Interestingly, a study using ElasticNet machine learning to predict poor modified Rankin scale score on discharge also identified metabolites that are shared with our study, 37 and these would be potential candidates that could be tracked in a broader patient population.

Overall, our study uses a set of complementary technologies to define the metabolic state of patients after aSAH. Intriguingly, Fe(III)‐containing porphyrin species are associated with DCI, suggesting a role for perturbed iron homeostasis leading to metabolic derangements. Identifying the interplay between these factors may point toward new therapeutic modalities to prevent secondary brain injury after aSAH.
