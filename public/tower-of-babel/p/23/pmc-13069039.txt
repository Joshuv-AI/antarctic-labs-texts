Enhancing depression diagnosis with augmented brain signal driven decorrelated graph neural networks
Authors: Jyotismita Barman, Mohammad Yusuf, Sandeep Kumar, Tapan Kumar Gandhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a leading global neuropsychiatric disorder, requiring precise diagnosis for effective intervention. Developing accurate diagnostic models for MDD remains a critical but challenging task. This study introduces a graph-based deep learning framework that addresses the issue of limited training data and facilitates robust training for identifying MDD across diverse episode patterns.MethodsWe introduce Brain Augmented-Decorrelated Network (BrainADNet), a framework designed to address data scarcity by augmenting brain signal inputs. BrainADNet builds upon the Skip-Graph Convolutional Network to aggregate informative multi-layer features, enriching its representational capacity. Recognizing the clinical relevance of demographic factors such as age, education, and gender in depression, we incorporate these attributes into the training process and examine their effect on diagnosis. To further improve feature diversity and reduce overfitting, we use a decorrelation regularizer to the model training. This encourages GCN embeddings to learn complementary, non-redundant representations from input graphs.ResultsAs far as we are aware, the framework surpasses existing models in accurately identifying MDD cases across depressive stages. We present a detailed ablation study demonstrating the contribution of each component to diagnostic precision. Our study highlights the top-10 brain regions influential in diagnosing MDD in males and females, addressing a crucial gap in understanding gender-specific neural mechanisms. We also uncover distinct patterns in latent-space brain connectivity, derived from GCN embeddings, between individuals experiencing single versus multiple depression episodes.ConclusionsThis study underscores the potential of graph methods to advance diagnostic precision for MDD. By integrating gender-specific and stage-wise insights, our framework equips medical professionals and researchers to design personalized and targeted therapeutic strategies, offering transformative implications for patient care.

Introduction

The World Health Organization’s (WHO) Mental Health Action Plan 2013-2030 and Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) prioritize addressing mental disorders, particularly depression and self-harm, including suicide prevention1. Major depressive disorder (MDD), a complex condition influenced by biological, environmental, and psycho-social factors, was the third largest contributor to the global disease burden in 2008 and is projected to become the leading cause by 20302,3. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-V), diagnosis requires persistent symptoms like low mood, sleep disturbances, difficulty concentrating, and thoughts of self-harm, all of which severely impact social and personal life4. Currently, MDD affects 3.8% of the global population, with higher prevalence in women (6%) compared to men (4%), and in older adults (5.7% of those over 60)5. Women are ~50% more likely to experience MDD than men. Recurrent MDD (rMDD), a more severe and persistent form, involves repeated depressive episodes and has been recognized in the WHO’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11)6. Alarmingly, individuals with a single depressive episode face a 50% risk of recurrence, which increases to 80% after two episodes7. Despite the availability of effective treatments, over 75% of people in low- and middle-income countries remain untreated due to limited resources and the stigma surrounding mental illness. Misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis of MDD is common, as its symptoms are often mistaken for normal emotional fluctuations. Accurate and timely identification of MDD is crucial for delivering effective treatments, reducing the risk of recurrence, and alleviating the long-term psychological, social, and economic burden on healthcare systems.

Among neuroimaging techniques, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has emerged as a critical tool for capturing neuronal activity in specific brain regions8,9. Studies reveal that depression significantly impacts structural neuroplasticity, altering both structural and functional connectivity in key brain areas such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and gray and white matter9–11. Early investigations into classifying MDD patients versus Healthy Controls (HCs) have uncovered distinct alterations in functional brain networks, especially in resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data12–14. The default mode network (DMN), typically active during rest, exhibits functional connectivity and heightened metabolic activity in healthy individuals15,16. However, large-scale analyses reveal marked reductions in DMN connectivity in recurrent MDD (rMDD) patients (t = −3.737, p = 0.0002, d = −0.326), while only minimal alterations are observed in first-episode MDD patients (t = −0.914, p = 0.361, d = −0.076)17. These findings underscore the profound effects of depression on brain connectivity and suggest that DMN disruptions may serve as a biomarker for recurrent MDD, offering valuable insights for diagnosis and intervention.

Traditional machine learning and deep learning based fMRI classification methods, such as support vector machines (SVM), logistic regression, and decision trees, rely on feature extraction but often fail to capture the critical topological relationships between brain regions18,19. Although deep learning techniques have shown promise in medical imaging, they are not designed to handle the intricate connectivity architecture of the human brain20–22. This limitation leads to suboptimal performance in disease classification, as accurately mapping the complex connectivity of the brain network is essential for understanding neurological disorders. Graph-based framework offers a solution by modeling brain regions as nodes and their functional connections as the edges23–25. Graph neural networks (GNNs) build on this foundation by integrating graph structures with neural networks, enabling the simultaneous processing of node attributes and their interconnections. This approach allows GNNs to effectively capture complex brain network patterns, significantly enhancing brain disorder classification and cognitive state prediction26,27. Recent works employ GCNs to capture topological properties of brain connectivity, achieving favorable performance in identifying MDD28,29. Additionally, GNNs have been investigated for interpretable diagnostics via linear models leveraging global network measures, as well as for multimodal integration and multi-task learning, showing potential to enhance diagnostic precision and adaptability30–32.

Existing graph-based approaches face a critical limitation in generalizing under limited annotated data. Generative architectures, such as variational autoencoders and diffusion-based models, have shown strong potential for data augmentation in various domains. However, these methods face several critical limitations when applied to brain connectivity analysis. These models often rely on large and diverse sets of unlabeled data, require substantial computational resources, and typically struggle to preserve biologically meaningful connectivity patterns33,34. Again, diffusion-based methods often lack domain-specific inductive biases and involve complex optimization procedures, making their direct application to brain graph analysis challenging35. In parallel, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning are two active research paradigms for learning under low-label conditions. In semi-supervised learning, the model is trained to minimize error on the labeled data while enforcing consistency on the unlabeled data. In contrast, self-supervised learning focuses on learning invariant features by solving pretext tasks, followed by fine-tuning on labeled data. Both paradigms typically require enough task-specific data to achieve desirable performance and are often computationally intensive. We argue that effective augmentation strategies for labeled data can substantially improve the performance of any learning paradigm.

Motivated by this, we propose BrainADNet, an efficient and interpretable framework to diagnose individuals with depressive disorder and identify their stages. To address the issue of data scarcity, we first implement a sliding-window technique on the original time-series brain signals, also known as blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signals, as a data augmentation step. This approach not only mitigates the issue of limited data but also standardizes the BOLD time series to a fixed dimension across all subjects from different sites. In this work, unlike conventional approaches that focus solely on brain signals, BrainADNet integrates demographic information, including age, gender, and years of education, along with brain connectivity features, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how this personal information influences the training and performance of the model. Following ref. 36, a core component of BrainADNet is Skip-GCN, which is designed to mitigate overfitting while enhancing classification accuracy and reliability. The dynamic weight fusion block in Skip-GCN aggregates information from each layer. To mitigate overfitting, which is a common issue when training neural networks on limited data, we use a decorrelation regularizer along with the traditional GCN loss function. By reducing correlations between hidden units, the regularizer promotes independent and diverse graph representations, improving both training efficiency and model performance. Since graph classification in neuroimaging is typically formulated as a supervised learning problem37, we present our results in a supervised setting. However, the techniques we propose are not limited to supervised frameworks. In preliminary evaluations, we find that the same augmentation strategies yield consistent benefits when incorporated into SSL frameworks, highlighting the flexibility and broad applicability of our approach.

We validate the effectiveness of BrainADNet using rs-fMRI data from the Rest-meta-MDD consortium (https://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD)38, which contains data from 25 research groups across 17 hospitals in China17. Extensive experiments on the benchmark dataset demonstrate that BrainADNet consistently outperforms existing methods in classifying depressive disorders. We also investigate gender-specific biomarkers and the stage-wise evolution of functional connectivity networks associated with depression.

Materials and methods

Data description

The dataset used in this study comprises preprocessed rs-fMRI from 1300 patients diagnosed with MDD and 1128 HCs17. All participants provided written informed consent. The study from which the dataset was derived was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (No. H21102)39. All the primary information, such as diagnosis, age, sex, and education, as well as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) corresponding to each participant, is also provided in the dataset. After a comprehensive quality assessment, the dataset was refined to include 848 individuals diagnosed with MDD and 794 HCs from 17 distinct hospitals/sites. In the multiclass classification framework, we aimed to differentiate between three distinct groups: First-episode drug-naive (FEDN) patients, representing individuals experiencing their first depressive episode without prior psychiatric medication; rMDD patients, characterized by individuals with multiple depressive episodes; and HCs with no history of psychiatric disorders. Participants for the multiclass classification analysis were drawn from nine hospitals/sites (Sites 7–9, 11, 14, and 19–21, 23), comprising a total of 937 individuals: 529 HCs, 219 FEDN patients, and 189 rMDD patients. Detailed demographic and clinical information of the participants are provided in the sheet entitled Dataset_details in the Supplementary Data. Additionally, our analysis categorized participants into three pairwise comparison groups to further investigate distinctions: MDD vs. HC, rMDD vs. HC, and FEDN vs. HC. In the MDD category, we include both rMDD and FEDN individuals. The data statistics for binary classification are as follows: for the MDD vs. HC classification, the dataset comprises 1570 participants from 16 sites (Sites 1, 2, 7–11, 13–15, 17, 19–23), including 756 HCs and 814 individuals diagnosed with MDD. For the FEDN vs. HC classification, data are obtained from 5 sites (Sites 8, 9, 14, 20, 23), comprising 598 participants (379 HCs and 219 FEDN). For the rMDD vs. HC classification, the dataset consists of 615 participants from 6 sites (Sites 7, 9, 11, 19–21), including 426 HCs and 189 rMDD patients.

Data preprocessing

To eliminate variability, the T1-weighted rs-fMRI images were preprocessed using a standardized preprocessing pipeline, data processing assistant for resting-state fMRI (DPARSF) at each local participating site40. The following preprocessing steps were performed: (i) Slice timing correction: to synchronize all slices to a reference time point, (ii) Realignment: to correct the functional data for a movement that has occurred during scanning, (iii) removing covariates: used data with global signal regression, (iv) Spatial normalization: to ensure consistent anatomical coordinates across all the subjects, (v) Co registration: used to put the structural and functional data in alignment, (vi) Band-pass filtering: performed using a 0.01-0.1 Hz filter, (vii) Normalization: normalized to a symmetric template and finally smoothened to enhance the signal and cancel out any noise. Following preprocessing, time series BOLD signals corresponding to specific brain atlases are extracted. Subsequently, poor-quality and incomplete BOLD signals are also excluded across the various atlases: anatomical automatic labeling (AAL) - 116 ROIs41, Dosenbach - 160 functional ROIs42, Craddock - 200 ROIs43, where ROI refers to the region of interest.

Ethics statement

In this work, we propose a graph-based machine learning framework for the diagnosis of depression, with the goal of improving mental health outcomes. We utilize a controlled access dataset38 (http://rfmri.org/REST-meta-MDD) and implement model development and evaluation procedures to ensure scientific integrity. This research was conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines and reflects our commitment to inclusion, equity, and responsible research practices.

BrainADNet framework

Figure 1 illustrates the proposed framework of our study. Our first step towards feature extraction is data augmentation, where each original BOLD signal is segmented into multiple fixed-length time series. This is achieved through a sliding-window technique, with a window length of W = 60 and a stride of S = 30 for ensuring consistent time series BOLD signals across all the participants. This data augmentation strategy addresses the limited availability of brain signals by generating multiple signals from a single input. This augmented time series data is then used to derive the brain graph, where initially we calculate the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between every pair of ROIs across all the subjects using Equation (1). After applying Fisher’s z-transformation to stabilize the correlation coefficients, we incorporate demographic attributes to derive the final graph features. The KNN graph is constructed using Equation (2) on Fisher’s z-transformed features, where we set κ = 10. The application of KNN is pivotal for preserving the local connectivity structure of the data, as it captures the most relevant relationships between nodes while mitigating the impact of noise and sparsity. All the augmented graphs and features are labeled according to their original BOLD signals. Finally, we train the Skip-GCN model using a decorrelation loss function along with the traditional Cross-Entropy (CE) loss, effectively preventing overfitting and enhancing classification accuracy.Fig. 1Overall pipeline of BrainADNet for classifying depressed individuals across different stages and healthy controls.a, b The original BOLD signal is segmented using a sliding window (W = 60, S = 30) to generate multiple augmented time-series segments. c Pearson’s correlation matrices are computed from each segment, followed by Fisher’s Z-transformation, and demographic information is early-fused with the resulting connectivity matrices to define graph features. d Graphs are constructed using a KNN algorithm (κ = 10). Skip-GCN

The framework of the GCN model with skip connections between layers is shown in Fig. 2. Specifically, we adopt a ChebyNet architecture composed of three Chebyshev (Cheb) convolutional layers. Each Cheb layer sequentially processes node features from the previous layer to compute updated representations. Within each layer, the input features are passed through a series of operations, including a ChebConv layer, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and dropout.Fig. 2Skip-GCN architecture used in our experiment.The model consists of three convolutional layers with ReLU as the activation function, followed by a dropout layer. Decorrelation for generalization

To improve model generalization, it is important to learn representations that are both diverse and non-redundant. Minimizing activation correlation is a well-established strategy to achieve this45,46. Results

In this section, we present a comprehensive analysis of the results, highlighting both the classification performance and the underlying neurobiological insights. Beyond classifying individuals across different stages of depression, we also investigate the influential brain regions that contribute to the diagnosis of depression in both male and female individuals. Furthermore, we examine the evolution of brain functional connectivity with varying levels of depression severity, providing a deeper understanding of how these changes correlate with the progression of the disorder. We also conduct a series of comprehensive ablation studies to evaluate the contribution of each component of our method to the overall classification performance. For the ablation study and subsequent analysis, we consider the AAL atlas.

Precision-recall analysis for class imbalance

Class imbalance is a well-recognized challenge in medical imaging problems, as it can bias models toward minority classes and obscure their true predictive performance. The precision-recall (PR) curve captures the trade-off between positive predictive value (precision) and sensitivity (recall), making it particularly well-suited for evaluating potential model bias. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) summarizes the trade-off between precision and recall across all decision thresholds. Higher AUPRC values indicate a better balance between these two metrics, reflecting more reliable detection of the minority class. Figure 5 presents the PR comparative analysis for three binary classification tasks: rMDD vs. HC, MDD vs. HC, and FEDN vs. HC. The results demonstrate that BrainADNet consistently maintains favorable precision-recall trade-offs in both balanced (MDD vs. HC, FEDN vs. HC) and imbalanced settings (rMDD vs. HC). Spatial vs. temporal graph analysis

While our previous experiments focused exclusively on spatial graph representations, prior studies have also explored temporal graphs for modeling brain connectivity. To provide a more comprehensive analysis, we evaluate our framework using both spatial and temporal graphs. Identical sliding-window augmentation and training procedures are applied to both to ensure a fair and consistent comparison. The spatial graph models functional connectivity, where nodes represent brain regions and edges encode statistical dependencies between their BOLD signals, capturing interactions across the entire scanning period. Temporal graphs are generated by taking the transpose of the augmented BOLD signal matrices to compute correlations, capturing similarities between time points and tracking whole-brain activation. As shown in Fig. 6b, spatial graphs consistently outperformed temporal graphs, demonstrating their superior ability to capture clinically informative connectivity patterns for MDD classification.

The observed performance difference likely arises from key neurobiological and modeling considerations. MDD involves persistent disruptions in functional connectivity across large-scale brain networks, especially the default mode network (DMN), salience network (SN), and fronto-limbic circuits17,62. Spatial graphs are better at modeling these connections, making them more suitable for detecting patterns associated with the disorder. While temporal graphs capture time-varying global activation patterns and may be valuable for studying transient brain dynamics. Moreover, spatial graphs integrate information across the entire available time series, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio and capturing better connectivity patterns. This analysis highlights the importance of spatial connectivity in understanding brain network alterations relevant to MDD diagnosis.

Sensitivity towards gender

Substantial evidence highlights gender differences in the occurrence of depression, with research consistently indicating that women are more susceptible to depression than men63–65. Given this established fact, it is essential to evaluate whether our model exhibits any bias toward a specific gender when incorporating gender information alongside fMRI data. Specifically, we assess the ability of our model to accurately classify male and female individuals separately while ensuring balanced performance across both groups. Fig. 6c illustrates the classification performance of our model for males and females separately, along with its overall performance across all data in classifying MDD individuals. Here, Accuracy represents the proportion of all depressed individuals (both male and female) correctly classified by the model; Accuracy (M) denotes the percentage of accurately classified male individuals; while accuracy (F) indicates the percentage of correctly identified female individuals. The results demonstrate that our model achieves consistent performance across both genders, with minimal variations in classification metrics. Despite well-established gender differences in depression prevalence, the model exhibits marginal gender-specific performance disparities. Its ability to integrate gender information while maintaining largely balanced classification performance makes it a valuable tool for identifying depression in diverse populations, supporting its potential applicability in clinical settings.

In Fig. 7a, the baseline model (Model 1) uses a spectral Chebyshev GCN, which serves as the core classifier. In Model 2, we add skip connections with a dynamic weight fusion block. Skip connections enhance training stability by enabling effective reuse of lower-layer features, which empirically results in smoother convergence and more stable optimization, as shown in Fig. 7d. The fusion block learns to assign adaptive importance weights to outputs from different layers, allowing the model to emphasize discriminative representations while mitigating overfitting through selective feature aggregation. In Model 3, we incorporate demographic information to facilitate more personalized and context-aware representations. Even when added to a vanilla GCN, these attributes lead to noticeable improvements in classification accuracy, as shown in Fig. 7e. This underscores the importance of these features and demonstrates that incorporating demographic information enhances the representational capacity of the model and contributes to improved diagnostic performance. In Model 4, we use a decorrelation regularizer to promote diversity among the learned representations. To assess its effect, we analyze the degree of correlation in the representations learned by a vanilla GCN, as illustrated in Fig. 7f. In the absence of this regularizer, the model tends to produce highly similar features. Although its impact on convergence is modest, the regularizer effectively increases feature diversity, which is essential for improving generalization. Finally, Model 5 (i.e., BrainADNet) augments the training data using a sliding-window approach, generating multiple overlapping time segments per subject. This increases the number and variability of training samples, enabling the model to generalize across dynamic fluctuations in the BOLD signal. In Fig. 7g, we further assess the effect of the augmentation strategy on self-supervised models. Specifically, the GCDA48 model is pretrained under two conditions: (i) using the original pretraining dataset and (ii) using the same dataset after applying the sliding-window augmentation. After completing pretraining in both settings, we fine-tune each model on the target site and evaluate its downstream performance. The results presented in Fig. 7a, g demonstrate that employing a proper augmentation strategy is model-agnostic, yielding consistent performance improvements across both supervised and self-supervised learning frameworks. Collectively, the ablation study shows that each module contributes uniquely and complementarily to overall model performance by enhancing convergence, diversity, personalization, and temporal generalization.

Ablation study with decorrelation objective

To empirically evaluate the advantage of decorrelation loss over conventional regularizers, we perform a systematic ablation study. Several variants of BrainADNet are trained to classify MDD patients and HCs, with one or more regularizers (dropout, weight decay, and decorrelation) selectively removed. As shown in Fig. 7h, removing only the decorrelation loss results in an average accuracy drop of ~2%. The best performance is achieved when decorrelation is combined with dropout and weight decay, highlighting their complementary roles in improving generalization and robustness.

Training stability and convergence with decorrelation objective

To assess optimization behavior, we perform controlled ablation experiments where each regularizer (dropout, weight decay, decorrelation) is selectively removed. As shown in Fig. 7i, the model trained with the decorrelation loss exhibits faster and smoother convergence, indicating improved training stability. BrainADNet, which integrates all three regularizers, converges even more quickly and smoothly, highlighting the potential effectiveness of the proposed method.

Discussion

This study aims to advance mental health assessment by improving disease prediction for individuals across different stages of depressive disorder. To this end, we propose an efficient graph-based machine learning framework designed to perform reliably even under limited-data conditions. Our comparative experiments demonstrate consistently strong performance, and a comprehensive ablation study highlights the contribution of each model component to the final prediction. We also examine several practical considerations relevant to real-world applicability, ranging from model performance across different demographic groups to its generalization across diverse clinical and acquisition settings. Finally, we investigate whether depressive disorder affects different demographic groups differently and how functional connectivity patterns evolve across clinical stages. All analyses are conducted using the AAL atlas, and the extended analyses supporting these findings are presented below.

Firstly, we analyze the responsible brain areas in male and female individuals suffering from depressive disorder. Understanding the root cause and the affected regions is essential for the proper treatment of any disorder. Additionally, when treating individuals from different demographic groups, it is critical to consider the unique physiological and neurological differences, as these factors influence both the manifestation of disorders and the effectiveness of treatments. For instance, fundamental differences between males and females, from blood count to brain function, necessitate customized treatment to ensure optimal results. In this study, we analyze the brain regions affected by depressive disorder in both male and female individuals, highlighting gender-specific patterns that could guide more targeted and effective treatment approaches.

In this study, we use gradient weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) with Skip-GCN, allowing localization of the brain areas exerting the greatest influence on the classification performance. Grad-CAM generates importance scores that quantitatively assess the contribution of specific characteristics to predictive accuracy of the model66. Grad-CAM was originally developed for convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in the computer-vision domain67. Its recent adaptation to GCNs allows the identification of discriminative nodes in non-Euclidean graph structures beyond image space68. In this work, we follow the Grad-CAM strategy described in ref. 54 to highlight brain regions that uniquely contribute to classification outcomes across male and female groups. We compute Grad-CAM at the GCN input layer using a Gradient × Input approach. The input feature matrix is set to require gradients, and a forward pass generates class scores. We select the softmax probability of the MDD class and backpropagate to obtain its gradients with respect to the input. These gradients are then multiplied element-wise with the original input features to produce a Grad-CAM map highlighting influential input positions. Averaging the resulting values across the feature dimension yields a contribution score for each ROI. This procedure is applied separately for models trained on male and female subjects to identify sex-specific discriminative brain regions.

As shown in Fig. 8, distinct regions unique to each gender have also been identified. Research highlights the angular gyrus as strongly associated with MDD69–72. According to studies, in MDD patients, the left angular gyrus often exhibits reduced regional homogeneity (ReHo) and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), indicating weakened connectivity that correlates with the severity of depressive symptoms. Also, gray matter volume reductions in the left angular gyrus have been observed, with these structural changes associated with the duration and recurrence of depressive episodes71. A study showed increased ReHo values and enhanced connectivity in the left angular gyrus after electroconvulsive therapy in MDD patients to better memory recall70. As highlighted by various studies, the cingulum also plays a significant role in MDD73–75. Studies show that MDD patients who have attempted suicide exhibit increased ReHo values in the cingulum compared to those who have not, suggesting heightened brain activity in this region among at-risk individuals. Conversely, these same patients show decreased ReHo in the right cingulate gyrus compared to healthy controls, highlighting altered brain function73. While numerous studies have rigorously validated the remaining responsible brain regions associated with MDD patients as identified by our proposed model, there is still much to discover about how these regions may function differently across genders76–78. Conclusion

In this study, we introduced BrainADNet, a framework designed to address critical challenges in diagnosing depressive disorders across diverse episodes. By employing a sliding-window technique, BrainADNet enriched and standardized neuroimaging data, thereby overcoming limitations posed by the scarcity of datasets. The integration of a decorrelation regularizer ensured robust learning by promoting independent and diverse feature representations, effectively mitigating overfitting. BrainADNet enhanced the quality of learned representations by leveraging skip connections to reuse lower-layer features and integrating them through a learnable fusion mechanism. Our framework also incorporated subject-specific demographic attributes with brain connectivity features during model training, enabling unified representation learning that captured both network-level neurobiological patterns and individual variability. This holistic approach not only improved diagnostic accuracy but also revealed how depression affects men and women differently, addressing a critical gap in mental health research. Furthermore, BrainADNet revealed how functional connectivity in early, non-medicated, and recurrent stages of depression deviated from that of healthy individuals, thereby offering valuable insights into the evolution of functional connectivity networks. By addressing existing limitations in diagnostic methodology and enabling patient-specific analysis, BrainADNet serves as a valuable tool for advancing depression management.
