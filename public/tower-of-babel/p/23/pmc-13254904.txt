Choroidal melanoma edge recurrence 13 years after I125 plaque brachytherapy
Authors: Maria Lee, Elham Khanafshar, Adrianna E. Eder, Kavita K. Mishra, Armin R. Afshar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe vast majority of posterior uveal (choroidal or ciliary body) melanoma local recurrences treatment with plaque brachytherapy occur within 5 years. We report an unusual case of a patient with choroidal melanoma edge recurrence 13 years after I125 plaque brachytherapy, without evidence of distant metastasis.CaseA 70-year-old male was referred 13 years after I125 plaque brachytherapy with an amelanotic mass along the posterior edge of an atrophic choroidal melanoma scar. Ultrasound showed a dome-shaped choroidal mass with low internal reflectivity. Optical coherence scan showed a choroidal tumor with irregular surface contour (possible ‘lumpy-bumpy’ appearance) and associated subretinal fluid. Vitrector-assisted biopsy confirmed choroidal melanoma with cells with variable size, enlarged nuclei and occasional prominent nucleoli with SOX-10 positivity. Genetic testing revealed BAP1 mutation, GNA11 mutation, chromosome 3 loss and 8q gain, all associated with increased metastatic risk. The patient underwent proton beam radiotherapy.ConclusionsThis case illustrates a rare late local recurrence of choroidal melanoma with different appearance from the original tumor. It also highlights the diagnostic and prognostic role of transretinal biopsy in confirming the nature of the lesion as a recurrence and the option of proton beam radiation as salvage therapy.

Introduction

Uveal melanoma is the predominant primary intraocular cancer among adults, with an incidence of approximately 5.1 cases per million per year.1 The most common treatment options include radiation (plaque brachytherapy, proton beam radiotherapy) and enucleation. Plaque brachytherapy is commonly utilized, with a 6.6% local recurrence rate of posterior uveal (choroidal and ciliary body) melanomas within 5 years of treatment, which accounts for 90.5% of recurrences overall.2 Recurrences present at a median of 18 months after plaque brachytherapy, and 57.1% are located at the tumor margin.2 In this case report, we describe the unusual presentation of a patient with local edge recurrence of choroidal melanoma 13 years after I125 plaque brachytherapy.

Case

A 70-year-old man with choroidal melanoma treated with I125 plaque brachytherapy 13 years prior was referred with a new mass along the tumor margin. Examination revealed an amelanotic lesion located along the posterior edge of the atrophic, melanocytic tumor scar (Fig. 1A and B). The lesion appeared dome-shaped on ultrasound with low internal reflectivity, and measured 9.5 mm circumferentially x 7.0 mm radially x 2.4 mm thickness, with distance of 6.05 mm between the posterior margin of the lesion and the optic disc (Fig. 1C and D). Optical coherence tomography through the tumor showed a potentially ‘lumpy-bumpy’ appearance the choroid (Fig. 1 E, F, G). Given the amelanotic lesion and melanotic primary tumor scar and to rule out metastasis from another primary tumor given his 50 pack-year smoking history, the patient underwent CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, all of which were unremarkable. The patient desired salvage therapy with proton beam irradiation. 27-gauge vitrector-assisted transretinal biopsy during tantalum marker insertion in preparation for radiation (Fig. 2A) showed cellular pleomorphism, enlarged nuclei, and occasional prominent nucleoli with SOX-10 positivity (Fig. 2B), confirming choroidal melanoma local recurrence. Genetic testing was performed using the UCSF 500 next generation sequencing cancer gene panel, which interrogates the full coding region of over 500 cancer genes. Results revealed a pathogenic GNA11 initiating oncogenic mutation, confirming the tumor was of uveal melanocytic origin, pathogenic BAP1 tumor suppressor gene splice-site mutation, chromosome 1q loss, chromosome 3 loss, and chromosome 8q gain. The patient underwent proton beam radiotherapy. Discussion

Diagnosis of a new lesion along the margin of a treated choroidal melanoma can often be informed by its clinical appearance and imaging biomarkers. Choroidal melanoma typically appears melanocytic (pigmented and brown) with a smooth, dome-shaped surface, although amelanotic melanomas can also be seen 3. On the other hand, choroidal metastases are usually amelanotic and irregular with a ‘lumpy-bumpy’ contour on optical coherence tomography.3 The most common sources of uveal metastases are breast and lung cancer.4 This male patient's lesion demonstrated mixed features–dome-shaped yet amelanotic in the setting of a pigmented/melanocytic primary tumor scar–and, despite a 50 pack-year smoking history, systemic imaging unremarkable, making choroidal metastasis unlikely.

If clinical features are ambiguous, transretinal biopsy can provide additional histopathological information to aid in diagnosis. The most sensitive marker for uveal melanoma is SOX-105, which is usually negative in epithelial carcinomas,5 and macro-nucleoli and multiple nucleoli are strongly linked to epithelioid uveal melanoma.6 Thus, this patient's histopathological findings confirmed diagnosis of choroidal melanoma. Proton beam radiation has been shown to be an effective method of salvage therapy for uveal melanoma recurrence, achieving 92.1% local tumor control after 10 years.7 Thus, this patient underwent proton beam therapy, with promising short-term outcomes.

Unfortunately, nearly 50% of patients with uveal melanoma develop metastatic disease despite successful local treatment,8 with the liver as the initial site of metastasis in the vast majority (90%) of cases.9 Genetic testing can help inform metastatic risk and prognosis. For example, activating mutations affecting the Gα11/Q pathway promote tumor growth, and SF3B1 and loss-of-function BAP1 mutations are associated with metastatic progression, while EIF1AX is associated with lower metastatic risk.8 In addition, high-risk uveal melanoma is associated with loss of chromosome 3 and gain in chromosome 8q.8 This patient was found to have pathogenic GNA11 (involved in Gα11/Q pathway) and BAP1 mutations, in addition to chromosome 3 loss and 8q gain; thus, he is at high risk for metastasis. However, imaging showed no signs of metastatic disease.

In summary, this case report describes a unique presentation of an isolated, late local recurrence of choroidal melanoma in a patient with increased genetic risk for metastasis, but without evidence of distant metastasis. The new tumor was amelanotic, appearing different from the primary tumor. The report also underscores the utility of transretinal biopsy in providing key histopathological and molecular information for diagnosis, and it highlights the use of proton beam radiation as salvage therapy in patients with atypical recurrence. Future research should be performed to assess risk of further recurrence and/or delayed metastasis in patients with high genetic risk, as well as long-term outcomes of secondary salvage treatment with proton beam radiation.
