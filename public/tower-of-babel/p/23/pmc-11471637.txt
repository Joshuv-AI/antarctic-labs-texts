Vaccines coverage and associated factors among children aged 12–23 months in the Pawie district, Ethiopia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Fekiahmed Salah, Chernet Tafere, Lemessa Jira Ejigu, Fikir Tadesse, Azeb Gedif, Sileshi Mulatu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough access to vaccines for children is increasing at healthcare facilities, outbreaks of vaccine preventable diseases and deaths have been reported in different areas of the Benishangul Gumuz region, in Ethiopia. Various interventions have been designed to provide vaccines for each child at an appropriate age. Still there is limited information on full vaccination coverage and associated factors among children aged 12–23 months in the Benishangul-Gumuz region, particularly in the Pawie district.MethodA community-based cross-sectional study design was used from April, 1–30, 2022 among 750 participants. Study populations were selected using a simple random sampling technique. Data was collected using structured questionnaires. Data was coded and entered into the SPSS Statistics 23.0. Bi-variable and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to assess factors associated with the children vaccination status. Variables with p-value <0.05 were considered significantly associated with the outcome variable.ResultThe results of this study revealed that 542 (72.3 %) (95 % CI: 68.9, 75.2) of the children were fully vaccinated. Factors significantly associated with full vaccination coverage included employed mothers (AOR = 0.53, 95 % CI: 0.29, 0.95), antenatal care (ANC) follow-up (AOR = 1.74, 95 % CI: 1.00, 3.03), post-natal care (PNC) follow-up (AOR = 6.19, 95 % CI: 3.62, 10.56), health institution delivery (AOR = 4.70, 95 % CI: 1.90, 11.66), birth order of children (AOR = 3.63, CI: 2.00, 6.59), maternal tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccination (AOR = 3.05, CI: 1.36, 6.86) and knowledge of vaccination schedule (AOR = 2.49, 95 % CI: 1.19, 5.21).ConclusionsThis study revealed that among children aged 12–23 months, full vaccination coverage was notably low. Factors significantly associated with higher rates of full vaccination included mothers’ occupation, attendance at ANC visits, PNC visits, birth order of children, place of delivery, maternal awareness of vaccination schedules, and maternal TT vaccination status. These findings highlight the multifaceted influences of maternal characteristics, healthcare use patterns, and maternal knowledge on childhood vaccination in this population.

Background

Immunization is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions to prevent deaths from childhood infectious diseases. Currently, it prevents 2–3 million deaths annually with a potential additional 1.5 million avoidable deaths.1

Vaccine-preventable diseases contribute substantially to the less than five year-old mortality as well as morbidity.2 Hence; immunization has become one of the major contributors to public health globally as it prevents communicable diseases, particularly in children. Evidence shows that 29 % of deaths among under five children are vaccine preventable,3 and being fully vaccinated is associated with a 22 % lower rate of child mortality.4 Despite the benefits of childhood immunization, routine vaccination coverage for all the recommended Expanded Program on Immunization vaccines has remained poor in some African countries, such as Nigeria (31 %), Ethiopia (43 %), Uganda (55 %) and Ghana (57 %). While immunization is one of the most successful public health interventions, coverage has plateaued over the last decade,5

Worldwide, 22.7 million children (17 % of the target population) were not vaccinated with the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis DTP3 in 2020 compared with 19.0 million (14 %) in 2019.6,7 Children who did not receive the first diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis dose (DTP1) by the age of 12 months (zero-dose children) accounted for 95 % of them.8 Among those who did not receive DTP3 in 2020, approximately 17.1 million (75 %) were zero-dose children. Global coverage decreased in 2020 compared with the 2019 estimates for the completed vaccine course of Haemophilus influenza type b (Hib), hepatitis B vaccine (HepB), human papillomavirus vaccine (HPV), and rubella-containing vaccine (RCV).9, 10, 11

About 3 million children die from infectious diseases every year in the African region. While many of these deaths could be prevented by prompt immunization, an estimated 20 % of children do not receive the recommended vaccines against preventable diseases.4,12,13 Besides, more children are vulnerable to vaccine-preventable diseases when a high percentage of children in the African region do not receive timely immunization.12,14

Although access to vaccines for children is increasing at healthcare facilities, outbreaks of vaccine preventable diseases and deaths have been reported in different areas of the Benshangul Gumuz region in Ethiopia. Various interventions have also attempted to provide vaccines for each child at an appropriate age. Still there is limited information on the full vaccination coverage and associated factors among children aged 12–23 months in the Benshangul-Gumuz region, particularly in the Pawie district.

Methods

Study area, design and period

The Pawie district is in the Metekel zone, Benshangul Gumuz region, northwest Ethiopia and 568km away from Addis Ababa, the capital city. There are 23 kebeles in the district with a total population of 71,853, In this district there are 3 health centers, 23 health posts, one general hospital, with 47 Health Extension Workers enrolled. In the district 2206; (3.07 % of the total population) was under 2 years of age; the source of information was obtained from the Pawie district Health Office 2022/23 annual plan which is unpublished. A community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted to assess the full vaccination coverage and associated factors among children aged 12–23 months in this study area. The study period was April, 1–30, 2022.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was determined by using the single population proportion formula and considering 66.7 % of full vaccination coverage rate as per the study in Benshangul Gumuz Region,15 95 % confidence interval, 5 % marginal error, 10 % of non-response rate and design effect of 2 which gave 750 respondents. By using a simple random sampling technique, 10 kebeles out of 23 were selected and the sample size was distributed proportionally to the children age (12–23 months). By considering children registration numbers from the Health Extension Worker registration book as a sampling frame, a systematic random sampling technique was used to select children from selected kebeles. Study participants were selected using every third household intervals, by dividing the total number of children to the allocated sample size.

Data collection

Data was collected by face-to-face interview using structured and pretested questionnaires. Questionnaires embodied socio-demographic characteristics and service delivery patterns. Four BSc nurses took part in the data collection and supervision process.

Data processing and analysis

Data was coded and cleaned up manually before being entered into the computer. Data from completed questionnaire was then entered into Epi-Info 7 and exported into SPSS 23.0 for analysis. Descriptive analysis was carried out for each of the variables to check frequency, distribution and missing values. A bivariate analysis was used to check crude associations between full vaccination coverage and independent variables.

Variables with a p value < 0.25 on the bivariate analysis were entered into a binary logistic regression to identify factors that affect full vaccination coverage. Adjusted odds ratio and corresponding 95 % confidence intervals were used to quantify degrees of association between independent and outcome variables. Variables with p-value ≤0.05 were considered as being statistically significant.

Factors associated with full vaccination coverage among children aged 12–23 months

Among a number of socio-demographic, maternal/caretaker and child-related factors included in this study, only few variables were identified as significantly associated with a child full vaccination status.

In the bi-variable analysis, mother occupation, ANC follow-up, PNC visit, maternal TT vaccination, place of delivery, birth order of the indexed child, travel time to reach the vaccination site of the health facility, mothers who knew of the vaccination schedule of the catchment area, and those who knew of the benefit of vaccination and of vaccine-preventable childhood diseases were factors significantly associated with a full vaccination status of children aged 12–23 months.

However, the results show in the multi-variable logistic regression analysis with the adjusted model, that employed mothers, NC follow up, PNC visit, birth order of children, place of delivery, mothers who knew of the vaccination schedule of the catchment area and maternal TT vaccination were found to be statistically significantly associated with a full vaccination status. The odds of a full vaccination for the children of housewife mothers were lower by 0.53 compared to those of employed ones (AOR = 0.53, 95 % CI: 0.29, 0.95).

The odds of a full vaccination status among children born to mothers who had a ANC and PNC follow-up were three and four time higher as compared to their counterparts (AOR = 1.74, 95 % CI: 1.00, 3.03) and (AOR = 6.19, 95 % CI: 3.62, 10.56), respectively.

Children born in health institutions were four times more likely to be fully vaccinated than those born at home (AOR = 4.70, 95 % CI: 1.90, 11.66). Birth order of children showed a significant association with a full vaccination status, being the first child in the family had nearly three times higher odds of full vaccination than being the second one and above (AOR = 3.63, CI: 2.00, 6.59). The odds of a full vaccination among mothers who had been vaccinated for TT were higher as compared to their counterparts (AOR = 3.05, CI: 1.36, 6.86).

This study also revealed that children born from mothers who had a knowledge of the vaccination schedule were twice more likely to be fully vaccinated than their counterparts (AOR = 2.49, 95 % CI: 1.19, 5.21). Discussion

The study findings showed that 72.3 % (95 % CI: 68.9, 75.2) of the children aged 12–23 months were fully vaccinated. This result is comparable with the studies conducted in Ethiopia (Debre Birhan town 73 %16 Arba Minch (73 %)17 and Malawi (72 %).18 The full vaccination coverage in this study was lower as compared to studies conducted in the Lay Armachiho district, North Gondar Zone (76 %),19 Eritrea (83 %),20 Kenya (77 %),21 Ghana (89 %),22 Cameroon (86 %),23 Nepal (85 %),24 and lower than the recommended targets set by the WHO and UNICEF to achieve at least 90 % by 201525, 26, 27, 28 which reinforces the need for continuous staff motivation, regular supervision, monitoring and evaluation to detect early declines in vaccination coverage. The level of full vaccination coverage observed in this study was higher than the one found in the report of the National Demographic Health Survey (EDHS 2016) (39 %) and EDHS 2019 (43 %), study conducted in the northwest part of Ethiopia Mecha district (49.3 %),25 North West Gojjam, Ethiopia (58.4 %),29 in a systematic review and meta-analysis in Ethiopia (58.92 %),30 and Mozambique in the two districts of the Zambezia province - Gurue and Milange (49.7 % and 48 %, respectively).31,32 The possible explanations might be differences in the study periods and variations in the implementation and outcome of the vaccination program among study areas and across the regions. The top three vaccine full vaccination coverage among children were 85.3 % for the pentavalent vaccine first dose, 84.8 % for the PCV first dose and 84.4 % for the OPV first dose.

As indicated above, the measles vaccination coverage was lower than all other types of vaccines; because of the relatively longer interval between the third dose of pentavalent and the measles one, a number of children may not return for the measles vaccine and this will make its coverage lower than for other vaccines.

Full vaccination was higher in children who were from a community with a high proportion of ANC as compared to those who were from community with low ANC utilization.33 This finding is in line with the study done in Bangladesh and in Ethiopia in Ambo, the study conducted in the Mecha district, the Amibara district, in Senegal and Nigeria.25,28,34,35 This showed that children of mothers who had received appropriate ANC were significantly more likely to be fully immunized than those of mothers who had not.

Compared to mothers who had not had a PNC visit, those who had were more likely to have a fully immunized child. Thess findings were in line with those of other similar studies in the city of Gondar administration, Ethiopia,36 and the Democratic Republic of Congo.37 This could be due to the fact that the mothers who had visited a PNC center had received guidance and support on the benefits of child immunization from health professionals. The place of delivery is another factor which showed a significant association with a full vaccination status. Mothers who delivered in a health institution were more likely to complete the child vaccination schedule when compared to those who had delivered at home. In this study; children who were born in a health institution were 4.7 times more likely to complete their vaccination schedule. This is in agreement with the results of previous studies conducted in Ambo,35 and the Mecha districts,25 North west Ethiopia,38 and Mozambique.32

These findings indicate that mothers who had delivered at health institutions were more aware of the benefits of vaccination than those who had delivered at home which might be related to the fact that they were nearer to a health service centre and, when giving birth at a health institution, most of the time the first doses of vaccination were given after birth at the health institution, therefore women might have had adequate counseling about the importance of childhood vaccination and adhere better to vaccination schedules. Some of the vaccinations are started on the first day of the newborn's life and there is also a more regular availability of vaccines at health institutions than outreach posts.

The likelihood of getting a child fully vaccinated was shown to be reduced as the birth order increased. Children of a higher birth order were more likely not to be not fully vaccinated when compared with those of a lower birth one. In this study birth order showed a statistically significant association with a full vaccination status, the first child in the family having nearly four times higher odds of a full vaccination than being the second one and above. This is in line with findings reported by studies from Ethiopia,16,36 Nigeria,27 Kenya,21 and India,39 but in contrast to a survey in Ambo Woreda.35 The first child in many cultures is the favorite one, gets more attention and time which is associated with him/her completing the full course of vaccination. The children of employed mothers had an increased rate of full vaccination compared to those of unemployed ones.

The findings of this study show that the odds of a full vaccination coverage for the children of housewife mothers were 53 % lower compared to those of employed ones which is congruent with other studies in Ethiopia16,29 and might be due to a lack of access to useful information about childhood vaccination and as a consequence an absence of vaccination.

Vaccination schedule of a catchment area was one of the factors related to full vaccination status. Mothers’ knowledge of vaccination schedules of a catchment area had a positive association with a fully-vaccinated child. This study show that children born from mothers who had knowledge of vaccination schedules were twice more likely to be fully vaccinated than their counterparts. This finding agrees with the results of previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and Kenya.19,21,40 This greatly reduced missing vaccination doses.

Tetanus toxoid vaccination is one of the factors associated with full vaccination coverage. The present study revealed that mothers/caregivers who were immunized with this vaccine during pregnancy were 3.05 times more likely to fully vaccinate their children compared to those who did not.

In agreement with the studies carried out in the Amibara district,Ethiopia28 and Bangladesh,34 mother/caregiver TT immunization acceptance was found to be the most important factor for full immunization of children. The possible explanation could be that mothers who had visited health facilities for their vaccination might have had exposure to information about the importance of completing routine childhood vaccination.

Conclusions

This study revealed that among children aged 12–23 months, full vaccination coverage was notably low in the Pawie district in Ethiopia. Factors significantly associated with higher rates of full vaccination included mothers’ occupation, ANC visits, PNC visits, birth order of children, place of delivery, maternal awareness of vaccination schedules, and maternal TT vaccination status. These findings highlight the multifaceted influences of maternal characteristics, healthcare utilization patterns, and maternal knowledge of childhood vaccination outcomes in this population.
