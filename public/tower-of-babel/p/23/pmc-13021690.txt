Comprehensive Immune-Phenotyping of Peripheral Lymphocytes in Coronary Artery Disease Reveals Potential Roles of Effector Memory and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T Cells
Authors: Xin Huang, Jiaxi Cheng, Leidan Zhang, Lei Jia, Chao Wu, Liyuan Zheng, Fada Wang, Kefei Dou, Taisheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of coronary artery disease (CAD), yet the peripheral immune profile in CAD remains insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to assess circulating lymphocyte subsets in CAD patients and their potential clinical relevance. Peripheral blood from 48 patients with angiographically confirmed CAD and 14 age- and sex-matched controls was analyzed using flow cytometry. A comprehensive antibody panel was used to characterize T, B, and NK cell populations. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Q values represent false discovery rate–adjusted p values. Among subsets analyzed, only effector memory (EM) CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were significantly elevated in CAD patients. EM CD4⁺ cells showed higher counts (147 vs. 102 cells/µL) and proportions (23.9% vs. 15.2%) compared to controls, and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ cells showed similar increases (160 vs. 95 cells/µL; 23.6% vs. 13.5%). After adjusting for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), both subsets remained positively correlated with monocyte counts and inversely associated with total bilirubin levels. Furthermore, EM CD4⁺ T cells showed positive associations with circulating IL-1β (r = 0.487, p < 0.001, q = 0.035) and IL-8 (r = 0.471, p < 0.001, q = 0.04) levels, whereas PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells demonstrated negative associations with IL-4 (r = − 0.537, p < 0.001, q = 0.018) and IL-10 (r = − 0.522, p < 0.001, q = 0.026). In conclusion, EM and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells are selectively enriched in CAD patients and show consistent associations with monocytes, total bilirubin, and circulating cytokines independent of hsCRP, reflecting immune alterations beyond systemic inflammation. Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is increasingly recognized as a chronic inflammatory condition, driven by both systemic immune activation and local vascular inflammation that contribute to plaque formation and destabilization [1, 2]. Anti-inflammatory therapies targeting cytokines such as IL-1β [3] and IL-6 [4], as well as broader agents like colchicine [5], have demonstrated potential in reducing cardiovascular events. However, outcomes across clinical trials remain variable [6, 7], highlighting the complexity of immune involvement in atherosclerosis and the need to better define patient-specific immune profiles to guide targeted anti-inflammatory strategies.

While innate immune cells, particularly monocytes and macrophages, have long been recognized as central to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis [8, 9], the role of adaptive immunity is gaining increasing attention. T and B lymphocyte subsets exert both protective and pathogenic effects depending on their phenotypes and activation states. For instance, cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells producing IFN-γ and granzyme B have been linked to plaque destabilization [10, 11], whereas regulatory CD4⁺ T cells (Tregs) may suppress vascular inflammation via IL-10 and TGF-β signaling [12]. Pro-inflammatory Th1 CD4⁺ T cells, on the other hand, promote atherosclerosis through IFN-γ production [13], and activated B cells have been associated with increased cardiovascular risk [14]. Natural killer (NK) cells may also contribute by releasing cytotoxic granules that exacerbate vascular injury [15].

While immune cells within atherosclerotic plaques have been well studied, the peripheral immune landscape remains insufficiently characterized. It is unclear to what extent circulating lymphocyte profiles reflect underlying vascular inflammation or relate to clinical features of CAD. This study aimed to comprehensively profile peripheral lymphocyte subsets in CAD patients using flow cytometry and assess their associations with clinical and biochemical parameters. The results may help identify immune signatures relevant to CAD pathogenesis and inform future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study Population

Patients with CAD and healthy controls were consecutively recruited at Fuwai Hospital (Beijing, China). Inclusion criteria for the CAD group required angiographic confirmation of ≥ 90% stenosis in at least one major epicardial coronary artery (left main, left anterior descending [LAD], left circumflex, or right coronary artery). During the same period, 14 age- and sex-matched individuals with at most mild atheroma and no stenosis on diagnostic coronary angiography were recruited as healthy controls.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included: (1) recent (< 3 months) or active infection, autoimmune disease, hematologic disorders, recent trauma or surgery, or malignancy; (2) current use of systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents (except low-dose aspirin); and (3) inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent. Controls were additionally screened to exclude any prior history or clinical evidence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, such as stroke, transient ischemic attack, peripheral artery disease, or previous revascularization.

All participants gave written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study. The research protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical data were obtained by independent study personnel from electronic medical records and catheterization lab registries, without access to the study hypothesis. A centralized data coordination team supervised data processing to ensure completeness and reliability.

Sample Collection and Flow Cytometry

Peripheral venous blood (3 mL) was collected into standard EDTA tubes according to clinical presentation. For patients undergoing elective PCI, samples were obtained in the early morning after an overnight fast, within 24 h of hospitalization and before the procedure. For patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction requiring emergency PCI, samples were collected upon hospital admission and prior to coronary angiography or intervention, without delaying urgent clinical care. All samples were processed within 4 h of collection. For lymphocyte immunophenotyping, 100 µL of whole blood was stained with predefined antibody panels, followed by red blood cell lysis using BD Pharm Lyse™ (BD Biosciences, Cat# 555899). For intracellular Ki-67 staining, fixation and permeabilization were performed using the BD IntraStain™ Kit (BD Biosciences, Cat# 641776) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was carried out using a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer, calibrated daily with BD CS&T beads. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (10.9.0, BD Biosciences). Gating strategies for the identification of T cell, B cell, and NK cell subsets were established based on canonical lineage and functional markers, which are illustrated in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1. All assays were conducted under standardized conditions to ensure consistency across samples.

Clinical, Biochemical, and Angiographic Measurements

For additional measurements, hematologic parameters (including total leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes) were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (XE-2100, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan).

Plasma lipid profiles, including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), and apolipoprotein B (ApoB), were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi LABOSPECT 008, Tokyo, Japan). TG, TC, and HDL-C were assessed by enzymatic assays, and LDL-C was calculated using the Friedewald formula. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] concentrations were determined by immunoturbidimetric assay (LASAY Lipoprotein(a) Auto; SHIMA Laboratories Co., Ltd).

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels were measured using a clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU680). Plasma N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was quantified by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography. Circulating cytokine levels were analyzed on a FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using a commercially available Cytokine Multiplex Detection Kit (Hangzhou Cellgene Biotechnology Co., Ltd), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Angiographic data were obtained during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and were assessed by the operating interventional cardiologist at the time of the procedure according to standard clinical practice. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed by transthoracic echocardiography using standard clinical protocols.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Propensity scores based on age and sex were calculated and applied as a post hoc statistical adjustment during data analysis to improve comparability between CAD patients and healthy controls. Following adjustment, differences in lymphocyte subsets and clinical variables were assessed. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for two groups or the Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Data distributions and between-group differences were visualized using box plots.

Given the large number of immune subsets analyzed, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to account for multiple comparisons. Absolute counts and proportional measures were treated as separate families of tests. Both raw p values and FDR-adjusted q values are reported, and statistical significance was defined as q < 0.05.

Partial spearman’s rank correlation analyses were used to evaluate associations between lymphocyte subsets and clinical, biochemical, or angiographic features in CAD patients while adjusting for hsCRP. In total, 85 variables, including 12 cytokines, were assessed for correlation with four predefined lymphocyte subsets (absolute counts and proportions of PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ cells and Effector memory [EM] CD4⁺ T cells). FDR adjustment was applied across all correlation analyses, and results are presented with both raw p values and FDR-adjusted q values. Correlation results were visualized using both heatmaps and scatter plots with overlaid linear regression lines. Radar plots were used to illustrate immune profiles in healthy controls and across different CAD presentations, including stable angina (SA), unstable angina (UA), non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline demographics were comparable between groups, with no significant differences in age, age distribution, or sex (Table 1).

CAD patients had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, including hypertension (47.9%), hyperlipidemia (62.5%), prior myocardial infarction (35.0%), and prior PCI (58.3%). HDL-C levels were lower in CAD patients, while LDL-C levels were similar between groups, potentially due to universal statin use.

Regarding clinical presentation, UA was most common (39.6%), followed by SA (22.9%), NSTEMI (20.8%), and STEMI (16.7%). Angiographic findings showed LAD involvement in 87.5% of CAD patients and calcified lesions in 52.1%. All PCIs were performed via radial artery access, with post-procedural Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow achieved in 91.7% of cases. Stents were placed in 79.2% of patients, with a mean stent length of 25.4 ± 25.6 mm (Supplementary Table 1).

Among all subsets analyzed, only two CD4⁺ T cell populations showed significant differences between CAD patients and healthy controls (Tables 2 and 3).

Immunologic Characterization of CAD by Clinical Phenotype

Comparisons across clinical presentations revealed distinct immune cell profiles among SA, UA, NSTEMI, and STEMI patients (Supplementary Tables 4–6). Key differences in immune subsets are summarized in radar plots (Supplementary Fig. 2).

EM CD4⁺, assessed by both absolute counts and proportions, were significantly elevated in patients with UA (155 cells/µL vs. 102 cells/µL, p = 0.001, q = 0.023; 24.0% vs. 15.2%, p < 0.001, q = 0.011) and NSTEMI (219 cells/µL vs. 102 cells/µL, p = 0.001, q = 0.031; 26.3% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.001, q = 0.030) compared to healthy controls. For PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ cells, significant elevations were observed in patients with SA (172 cells/µL vs. 95 cells/µL, p = 0.001, q = 0.031; 27.8% vs. 13.5%, p < 0.001, q = 0.020) and UA (159 cells/µL vs. 95 cells/µL, p < 0.001, q = 0.019; 23.6% vs. 13.5%, p = 0.001, q = 0.030) relative to healthy controls.

Notably, no significant differences in EM CD4⁺ or PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cell subsets were observed in the STEMI group compared with healthy controls. In contrast, STEMI patients presented a distinct immune profile characterized by a significant increase in EM CD8⁺ T cells, both in absolute counts (130 cells/µL vs. 53 cells/µL, p < 0.001, q = 0.022) and proportions (31.1% vs. 20.8%, p = 0.001, q = 0.030). Additionally, STEMI patients demonstrated a reduced proportion of HLA-DR⁺ NK cells (12.7% vs. 22.3%, p = 0.001, q = 0.030) alongside an increased proportion of Ki-67⁺ NK cells (3.5% vs. 2.0%, p = 0.002 q = 0.048).

Discussion

In this study, we applied a multicolor flow cytometry to comprehensively profile lymphocyte subsets in CAD patients and healthy controls confirmed by coronary angiography. The main findings were: (1) Among the lymphocyte subsets analyzed, only EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were significantly elevated in CAD patients, both in absolute counts and proportions; (2) these differences remained consistent across subgroups stratified by sex, age, hypertension, and diabetes; (3) EM and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were significantly correlated with monocytes, selected cytokines, and total bilirubin after adjustment for hsCRP; (4) exploratory analyses stratified by clinical presentation suggested heterogeneous immune patterns across CAD subtypes.

In atherosclerosis, antigen-primed CD4⁺ T cells differentiate into effector subsets that mediate pro- or anti-inflammatory responses [8, 16]. Among these, EM CD4⁺ T cells are antigen-experienced cells with retained effector functions, long-term memory, and the ability to rapidly secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ upon reactivation [17]. They express homing receptors like CCR5 and CXCR3, enabling recruitment to inflamed vascular tissue [18, 19]. In our study, both the absolute count and proportion of circulating EM CD4⁺ T cells were significantly increased in CAD patients compared to healthy controls. This finding aligns with previous reports of EM CD4⁺ T cell expansion in patients with atherosclerosis and acute cardiovascular events [20]. EM CD4⁺ T cells have also been detected at higher levels within carotid plaques of stroke patients, suggesting a role in local plaque inflammation [21]. Blockade of CXCR3 reduces atherosclerotic lesion formation in animal models [22], further supporting their involvement in the inflammatory process of atherosclerosis. While CD28⁻ CD4⁺ T cells, a terminally differentiated subset of EM T cells, have been previously implicated in plaque instability and myocardial infarction [23], no significant increase in this subset was observed in our cohort.

PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were also significantly elevated in CAD patients, both in absolute counts and proportions. PD-1 is a well-established marker associated with T cell exhaustion, a functional state induced by chronic antigen exposure and sustained inflammation [24]. Through attenuation of T-cell receptor signaling and effector function, PD-1 signaling serves as an endogenous negative feedback mechanism to limit immune-mediated tissue injury [25–27]. In our cohort, the expansion of PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells likely reflects an adaptive immunoregulatory or exhaustion-associated phenotype resulting from prolonged antigenic and inflammatory stimulation. Although PD-1 signaling serves to restrain immune-mediated tissue damage, increased PD-1 expression is a well-recognized consequence of chronic immune activation, as demonstrated in experimental atherosclerotic models [28]. Accordingly, the higher PD-1 expression observed in CAD patients may reflect the intensity and chronicity of the underlying inflammatory milieu. This interpretation is further supported by prior studies reporting elevated PD-1 expression on CD4⁺ T cells both in the peripheral blood and within the atherosclerotic plaques of CAD patients [21, 28]. In cancer patients, PD-1 blockade has been associated with aggravated plaque inflammation, likely through reactivation of exhausted T cells [29]. Recent studies suggest that Fcγ receptor–binding anti–PD-1 antibodies can act as surrogate PD-1 ligands to reduce plaque burden, highlighting PD-1 signaling merits further investigation as a marker of immune dysregulation in atherosclerosis [30].

Monocytes play a central role in atherogenesis by infiltrating the intima, taking up oxidized LDL, and differentiating into foam cells that drive plaque formation [31]. EM CD4⁺ T cells have been shown to activate monocytes through direct contact, inducing the release of inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, while patrolling monocytes can present antigens to EM CD4⁺ T cells within the vasculature [32, 33]. In our study, both the absolute counts and proportions of EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were positively correlated with circulating monocyte levels after adjusting for hsCRP, suggesting a potential bidirectional interaction. Given that inhibiting monocyte recruitment has been shown to attenuate plaque development in animal models [34], the observed associations suggest that EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cell subsets may serve as accessible biomarkers reflecting monocyte-associated inflammatory activity in CAD.

In addition, these subsets are associated with key inflammatory cytokines. IL-1β, a pro-inflammatory cytokine implicated in atherosclerosis progression, is found in human plaques, and its inhibition with canakinumab reduced recurrent cardiovascular events in the CANTOS (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study) trial [35–37]. In our study, EM CD4⁺ T-cell proportions were positively associated with circulating IL-1β levels, indicating co-variation within an inflammatory milieu. Conversely, IL-10 and IL-4 are anti-inflammatory cytokines that offer vascular protection. IL-10 is expressed in atherosclerotic plaques, and its deficiency leads to larger, unstable lesions in animal models [38]. IL-4 levels are often reduced in severe CAD patients, suggesting a stage-dependent protective role [39]. We found that PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells were negatively correlated with both IL-4 and IL-10 levels, implicating that increased PD-1 expression may be associated with a diminished anti-inflammatory environment. Taken together, these findings suggest that EM and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells are closely linked to the systemic inflammatory state in CAD and warrant further investigation in mechanistic and longitudinal studies.

While our analyses suggested potential trends toward distinct immune signatures across CAD clinical subtypes, the limited sample sizes within individual subgroups may have substantially influenced the observed immune profiles. Accordingly, all subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory and require validation in larger, prospectively standardized cohorts. Within these constraints, EM CD8⁺ T cell counts increased progressively from UA to NSTEMI and peaked in STEMI, potentially reflecting heightened cytotoxic activation in acute presentations [40]. In contrast, EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells, subsets associated with chronic immune activation and exhaustion, were elevated in SA, UA, and NSTEMI but not in STEMI, potentially due to transient redistribution or temporoary suppression during acute myocardial injury.

STEMI patients also demonstrated a distinct NK cell phenotype characterized by reduced HLA-DR⁺ NK cell proportions alongside elevated Ki-67⁺ NK cell proportions, suggesting impaired NK cell function with compensatory proliferation [41]. Prior studies have reported impaired NK receptor expression and functional alterations in MI compared to SA and healthy controls [42, 43], although distinctions between STEMI and NSTEMI have rarely been examined.

In contrast, NSTEMI exhibited an intermediate immune profile, sharing elevated EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells seen in SA/UA and increased EM CD8⁺ T cells observed in STEMI. NSTEMI was also associated with higher total and HLA-DR⁺ NK cell counts, consistent with prior reports linking HLA-DR⁺ NK cells to enhanced activation [44] and sustained perforin-mediated inflammatory responses in ACS [45].

Together, these findings suggest that EM and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells may preferentially reflect chronic immune activation in SA and UA, whereas NK cell dysregulation and cytotoxic T-cell expansion may be more prominent in acute coronary presentations. NSTEMI appears to represent an immunologic intermediate between chronic and acute phenotypes. However, given the exploratory nature of these analyses, no definitive subtype-specific mechanisms can be inferred.

Finally, while the present study provides a broad overview of lymphocyte alterations in CAD, the roles of specific NK cell subsets, distinct CD4⁺ T-helper lineages, as well as plasmablast and memory B-cell populations remain incompletely defined. Future prospective studies incorporating more granular immunophenotyping panels and functional markers will be essential to determine whether these subpopulations serve as more precise indicators of CAD progression or immune dysregulation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power to detect subtle immunophenotypic differences and limits the generalizability of subgroup analyses across the spectrum of CAD. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents inference of temporal or causal relationships between immune cell alterations and disease progression. Third, although associations were identified between lymphocyte subsets and inflammatory cytokines, the absence of functional validation or longitudinal assessment precludes causal inference, and these correlations may reflect coordinated immune responses to systemic inflammation rather than subset-specific biological effects. Fourth, immune profiling was limited to peripheral blood, which may not fully capture the immunologic landscape within atherosclerotic plaques or vascular tissues where local cues, such as hypoxia or lipid accumulation, modulate immune cell function. Fifth, differences in ischemia-to-sampling intervals between elective and emergency PCI patients may have influenced circulating immune profiles and should be considered when interpreting subgroup analyses. Sixth, there were inherent differences in baseline comorbidities between the CAD and control groups; although these differences reflect real-world clinical populations, they may introduce residual confounding and could influence immune phenotypes independently of CAD status. Seventh, our immunophenotyping was constrained by a predefined set of markers. The absence of additional markers (such as CD39, LAG3, and TIM-3 to more definitively characterize T-cell exhaustion; markers to distinguish specific T-helper subsets; and intracellular cytokine staining to functionally validate IL-1β, IL-4, and IL-10 production) may have limited the resolution of certain functional immune states. Consequently, some nuanced immune alterations may not have been fully captured, and the cellular sources of circulating cytokines could not be definitively identified. Finally, medication use, especially statins and antiplatelet agents in the CAD group, may have modulated immune parameters and confounded the observed associations.

Conclusion

Peripheral EM CD4⁺ and PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells are selectively elevated in CAD and correlate with monocytes, total bilirubin, and circulating cytokines independent of hsCRP, reflecting immune alterations beyond systemic inflammation. Immunophenotyping may provide complementary information on inflammatory status and clinical heterogeneity in CAD.
