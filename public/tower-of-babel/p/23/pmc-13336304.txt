Ex vivo-generated conventional dendritic cells type 1 and type 2 from blood progenitors induce potent antigen-specific T-cell immunity
Authors: Jorge Cuenca-Escalona, Kevin Bos, Johanna Bödder, Tom van Oorschot, Tjitske Duiveman-de Boer, Georgina Flórez-Grau, I. Jolanda M. de Vries
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile existing cancer therapies manage to reduce tumor burden, they often fail to produce durable or curative outcomes. Alternatively, cell-based approaches – particularly dendritic cell (DC) vaccines conceal the potential to elicit robust tumor-specific T-cell responses, equipping patients with T-cells capable of eradicating tumor cells and preventing future relapses. To date, most DC vaccines have been generated with monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs), nowadays considered less potent at priming T-cells than blood-derived DC subsets, arguing for the usage of blood DCs for vaccine development. However, their scarcity in blood has hindered their clinical application, limiting their use in DC-based vaccination trials. To overcome this limitation, we developed a protocol to generate clinically relevant quantities of conventional DC type 1 (cDC1s) and DC2-like cells (cDC2s) from progenitor cells isolated from peripheral blood without the need for G-CSF mobilization, enabling their use for autologous vaccinations. The protocol is feeder layer free, yields clinically relevant amounts of cDC1s and cDC2s-like cells, and can be translated into a GMP-compliant setup. The ex vivo-generated DCs resemble their natural counterparts, acquire a robust pro-inflammatory phenotype (e.g., CD83, CCR7, and IL-12p70), and efficiently expand pro-inflammatory T-cells (e.g., IFN-γ). Notably, in an in vitro immunization model, these DCs primed naive T-cells for the expansion of tumor-antigen-specific CD8 T-cells exhibiting strong immune-stimulating features (e.g., IFN-γ, CD107a). In summary, this study demonstrates that blood progenitors differentiate into cDC1 and cDC2-like cells capable of eliciting potent antigen-specific T-cell subsets, offering a promising avenue for the development of more effective DC-based cancer therapies.

Introduction

Cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, can manage to reduce patient’s tumor burden and prolong patient survival. However, these therapies often fail to achieve durable or curative outcomes in the majority of patients. Alternatively, immunotherapies pose a promising option, as they specifically target the tumor while harnessing the patient’s own immune system in the fight against the tumor. Among these, dendritic cell (DC)-based therapies, commonly called DC vaccines, conceal the potential to launch tumor-specific T-cell responses, thereby providing the patient with the necessary T-cell repertoire to eliminate tumor cells and prevent future relapses. In this strategy, patient’s DCs are isolated, exposed ex vivo to tumor antigen(s) and subsequently re-infused to the patient, where they prime the development of tumor-specific T cells. 1‐3 Despite extensive clinical efforts, however, DC vaccination has yielded modest clinical responses in only a minority of patients. 4 , 5

Historically, moDCs have been used in DC vaccination trials because they are well-tolerated and obtainable in large amounts in autologous settings. 4 However, mounting evidence indicates that the naturally occurring human DC subsets outperform the function of moDCs. While efficient in antigen uptake, moDCs lack the T-cell stimulatory capacity of the human blood DC subsets. 6‐8 In human blood, three major groups of DCs are identified: plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and the conventional DCs type 1 (cDC1) and type 2 (cDC2). 9 , 10 Noteworthy, these DCs differ in multiple attributes, including abundance, ontogeny and more importantly, functional specialization. 11‐14 Compared to pDCs, cDC1s and cDC2s are known for their superior capacity to prime antigen-specific T-cell immunity. 15‐17 Notably, cDC1s excel in antigen cross-presentation and subsequent CD8 T-cell activation, 6 , 18 , 19 a key process for effective tumor cell clearance. Collectively, these features support the notion that blood DCs, and cDC1s particularly, rather than moDCs, represent a promising cellular platform for the development of more effective DC vaccines.

The scarcity of these DCs circulating in the blood has limited their usage for DC vaccine manufacturing. Especially the scarcity of CDC1s, of which only (1–3) x 106 cells are theoretically obtainable from a patient apheresis product, emphasizes the need for alternative strategies to generate high amounts of these DCs for DC-based therapies. To address this limitation, multiple protocols for the ex vivo generation of human DCs from hematopoietic stem cells and progenitor cells have been developed. 20‐26 However, many of these approaches exhibit challenges that still hinder their clinical implementation for autologous DC vaccine development, including the reliance on mouse-derived cellular feeder layers or the requirement for G-CSF mobilization to obtain sufficient progenitor cell yields.

To circumvent these limitations, we have developed a GMP-compliant protocol for the generation of clinically relevant quantities of human cDC1s and cDC2-like cells from CD34+ progenitor cells isolated from non-G-CSF mobilized peripheral blood. The ex vivo-generated cDC1s and cDC2s recapitulate the phenotype and lineage marker expression of their blood counterparts. Functionally, CD34-derived DCs acquire a robust mature phenotype following TLR challenge (e.g., CD83, CCR7, and IL-12p70) and are able to induce potent pro-inflammatory CD4 and CD8 T-cell responses. Importantly, CD34-derived DCs are able to endocytose environmental cargo and present antigen-derived peptides, leading to autologous T-cell activation. Furthermore, in a vaccination-mimicking setting, these ex vivo-generated DCs were able to prime naive CD8 T-cells and expand pro-inflammatory MART1–specific CD8 T-cells. Together, these findings demonstrate that cDC1 and cDC2-like cells generated from peripheral blood progenitors elicit strong antigen-specific T-cell responses, representing a promising strategy for advancing the power of DC-based cancer therapies.

Materials and methods

Cell isolation

CD34+ cells, pan T-cells, naive CD4, and naive CD8 T-cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (Lymphoprep, Serumwerk Bernburg) from healthy donor’s buffy coats (Sanquin, the Netherlands). CD34+ cells were isolated with the MACS CD34 Microbead kit Ultrapure (130-100-453, Miltenyi Biotec). CD34+ cells were first eluted through an LS-column (130-042-401, Miltenyi Biotec), followed by an enrichment step on an MS-column (130-042-201, Miltenyi Biotec). Purity was assessed by flow cytometry (see Supplementary Figure 1A) using antibodies against CD34 FITC (1:50, 343604, BioLegend) with an average mean ± SEM of 92,66 ± 1,92. After positive CD34 isolation, the negative fraction containing the lymphocytes was used for the isolation of either autologous pan T-cells or Naive CD8 T-cells. Pan T-cells were isolated using the MACS Pan T-cell isolation kit (130-096-535, Miltenyi Biotec). Pan naive T-cells were isolated using the MACS Naive Pan T-cell isolation kit (130-097-095, Miltenyi Biotec) and the MACS Naive CD8 T-cell isolation kit (130-093-244, Miltenyi Biotec). Pan T-cells, pan naive T-cells, and naive CD8 T-cells were cryopreserved until further use in freezing media composed of 50% X-VIVO 15 medium (Lonza), 40% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 10% CryoSure DMSO (WAK-Chemie).

Generation of cDC1s and cDC2s from human adult blood CD34+ cells

CD34+ cells were cultured at a cell concentration ranging from 5–10 × 104 cells/ml in 24-well plates in StemMACS™ HSC Expansion Media (130-100-473, Miltenyi Biotec) supplemented with 2% Human-Serum (HS, Sanquin, The Netherlands), 1 µM SR1 (72344, StemCell Technologies), 50µg/ml of ascorbic acid (Centrasam), 50U/ml of GM-CSF (130-093-867, Miltenyi Biotec) and further supplemented with the StemMACS™ HSC Expansion Cocktail (130-100-843, Miltenyi Biotec) according to manufacturer´s instructions. On days 5 and 8, the expanding cells of each well were split into two different wells, and equal volumes of the above-mentioned expansion media were added. On day 10, expanding cells were subjected to a DC skewing phase. To this end, expanding cells were washed and replated at a cell concentration ranging from 6–8 × 105 cells/ml in T75 flasks using CellGenix® GMP DC-medium (Sartorius CellGenix) supplemented with 2% HS, 1 µM SR1, 50µg/ml ascorbic acid, 100 U/ml FLT3L (130-096-477, Miltenyi Biotec), 800U/ml GM-CSF (130-093-867, Miltenyi Biotec), 1000 U/ml IFN-a2 (130-093-874, Miltenyi Biotec), and 100 U/ml IL-4 (130-093-924, Miltenyi Biotec). On day 14, cultures were supplemented with fresh media (25% of the volume used on day 10) prepared with CellGenix® GMP DC medium containing 2% HS, 50 µg/ml ascorbic acid, 200 U/ml FLT3L, 1600 U/ml GM-CSF, 2000U/ml IFN-α2, and 200 U/ml IL-4. On day 17, the resulting cells were characterized by flow cytometry, and DCs were sorted for follow-up phenotypical and functional studies.

DC enrichment

CD34-derived DCs were sorted using the FACSMelody Cell-Sorter or magnetic cell-sorting (MACS). FACS-based cDC2 and cDC1 sorting was performed based on CD1c expression and double CD141/CLEC9A expression, respectively (see Supplementary Figure 1B). Alternatively, DCs were enriched by MACS using CD141-Microbeads (130-090-512, Miltenyi Biotec) and CD1c Isolation kit (130-119-475, Miltenyi Biotec) sequentially (see supplementary figure 1C). On day 17, cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS containing 0.1%BSA, 0.2 mM EDTA (5 µL/1 × 106) and incubated with biotinylated anti-CD1c (10µL/1 × 106 cells) and CD141 microbeads (10µL/1 × 106 cells) for 25 min at 4ºC. Next, cells were washed, and CDC1s were positively isolated with an LS-column. Negative fraction was incubated for 20 min with anti-biotin microbeads (20µL/1 × 106 cells). cDC2s were isolated positively with an LS-column.

DC culture and stimulation

Sorted cDC1s and cDC2s were cultured at 0.5 × 106 cells/ml in X-VIVO15 medium (Lonza) with 2%HS. To induce maturation, DCs were overnight stimulated with 5 µg/ml R848 and Poly(IC) (InvivoGen).

cDC1 and cDC2 characterization

Lineage marker expression was determined by flow cytometry. Cells were first stained with the viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS) for 15 min at 4ºC. Then, cells were incubated with blocking buffer composed of PBA (PBS, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, and 0.01%NaH3) with 5%HS. After 15 min, cells were washed and incubated with antibodies against human HLA-DR-PerCP (1:40, 307628, BioLegend), CD1c-BV421 (1:50, 331526, BioLegend), CD141-PE-Cy7 (1:40, 344110, BioLegend), CLEC9A-VioBrightFITC (1:50, 130-126-029, Miltenyi Biotec), XCR1-PE (1:50, 372604, BioLegend), HLA-ABC-APC (1:50, 555553, BD Biosciences), CD5-BV711 (1:100, 300644 BioLegend), CD163-BV605 (1:100, 333616, BioLegend), CD14-BV785 (1:100, 301839, BioLegend), SIRPα-APC (1:50, 144013, BioLegend), and CD11b-Pe-Cy7 (1:75, A54822, Beckman). BD Horizon™ Brilliant Stain Buffer (563794, BD Biosciences) was used according to manufacturer´s instructions. Flow cytometry was performed with BD FACSLyric™ (see Supplementary Figure 2A–C) and data analyzed with FlowJo software v10 (BD).

In addition, DCs' phenotype was determined after TLR stimulation. DCs were first incubated with the fixative viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS, 15 min, 4 ºC). Cells were then washed and incubated with blocking buffer (PBA containing 5%HS, 15 min, 4 ºC). Next, DCs were washed and incubated with antibodies against CD40-FITC (1:50, 334306, BioLegend), CD86-Pe-Cy7 (1:75, 561128, BioLegend), CD80-Alexa700 (1:75, 561133, BD Biosciences), CD83-BV785 (1:75, 305338, BioLegend), HLA-DR-BV510 (1:40, 307646, BioLegend), and CCR7-APC (1:100, 130-120-466, Miltenyi Biotec). Flow cytometry was performed with BD FACSLyric™ (see Supplementary Figure 2F), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software v10 (BD).

Cytokine and chemokine determination

For cytokines and chemokines quantification, supernatants from overnight TLR-stimulated DCs were analyzed with Human Essential Immune Response LEGENDplex™ kit (740929, BioLegend) according to the manufacturer´s instructions.

Mixed lymphocyte reaction

To determine CD34-derived DCs’ capacity to induce T-cell activation, DCs were cocultured with allogeneic pan T-cells stained with CFSE (C34554, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as previously described. 27 10 × 103 DCs were either left untreated or TLR-matured and then cocultured at a 1:10 ratio (DC:T-cell) with CFSE-labeled T-cells for 6 d in a final volume of 200 µL in a 96 U-bottom plate. Then, T-cells were harvested for flow cytometry analysis, and supernatants were collected for cytokine determination. Supernatant was analyzed by ELISA for IFN-γ detection (88-7316-77; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For flow cytometry characterization, T-cells were incubated with the viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS, 15 min, 4 ºC). T-cells were then incubated with antibodies against CD8-APC (1:50, 555369, BD Biosciences) and CD4-PE (1:50, 555347, BD Biosciences). T-cell proliferation was determined by flow cytometry with BD FACSVerse™ (see Supplementary Figure 3A) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10. Additionally, we determined the ratio of proliferating CD8/CD4 T-cells, calculated as [% proliferated CD8 T-cells per condition]/[% proliferated CD4 T-cells per condition].

Naive T-cell polarization assays

The ability of CD34-derived DCs to skew naive T-cell polarization was determined by coculturing DCs with allogeneic pan naive T-cells. 10 × 103 either untreated or TLR-stimulated DCs were cocultured with 50 × 103 pan naive T-cells (1:5 ratio) in a volume of 200 µL in a 96 U-bottom plate. After 6 d, half the media was removed, an equal volume of fresh media was added and then supplemented with 20U/ml IL-2 (130-097-773; Miltenyi Biotec). From day 6 onwards, medium replacement with IL-2 supplementation was performed every 2 d until day 12 of coculture. Then, the phenotype of the expanded CD8 T-cells, frequency of regulatory T-cells (Treg, CD4+CD127-CD25+FOXP3+ T-cells), and cytokine production capacity were determined.

For CD8 T-cell phenotype and Treg frequency identification, T-cells were first stained with the viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS, 15 min, 4 ºC). T-cells were then fixed with the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining kit (00-5523-00, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer´s instructions. After fixation, cells were incubated for 15 min at 4ºC with permeabilization buffer supplied by the kit supplemented with 5%HS. For Treg frequency determination, cells were incubated with antibodies against CD4-BV421 (1:50, 562424, BD Biosciences), FOXP3-A488 (1:50, 53-4776-42; eBioscience), CD25-APC (1:100, 555434; BD Biosciences), and CD127-PE (1:100, 12-1278-42; eBioscience). For the characterization of CD8 T-cells, cells were stained with antibodies against CD8-FITC (1:50, 555366, BD Biosciences), Perforin-Alexa647 (1:100, 308110; BioLegend), and Granzyme B-PE (1:100, 372208; BioLegend).

For T-cell cytokine production characterization, T-cell cocultures were stimulated with 10 ng/ml brefeldin-A (Cayman Chemicals), 25 ng/ml PMA (Calbiochem), and 0.5 mg/ml ionomycin (ThermoFisher) for the last 3 h of culture. Cells were stained with the viability-dye eFluor780, followed by fixation using the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining kit and later permeabilization in the presence of 5%HS (as described above). Intracellular cytokine production was determined with antibodies against TNFα-APC (1:75, 130-117-382, Miltenyi Biotec), IFN-γ-BV421 (1:100, 562988, BD Biosciences), CD4-PerCP (1:50, 300528, BioLegend), and CD8-Pe-Cy7 (1:50, 344712, BioLegend).

CD8 phenotype (See Supplementary Figure 3B), intracellular cytokine production (see Supplementary Figure 3C), and Treg frequency (see Supplementary Figure 3E) were determined using BD FACSVerse™ or BD FACSLyric™ and analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

Endocytosis assays

For endocytic capacity determination, DC suspensions (0.5 × 106 cells/ml in pre-warmed medium) were stimulated with 10 µg/ml of Dextran-Alexa647 (D22914, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at 37 ºC. To distinguish active from passive endocytosis, Dextran-treated DCs were incubated in parallel at 4 ºC for 10 min. After, DCs were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry using BD FACS Lyric (see Supplementary Figure 3F) and later analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

For the tumor cell endocytosis assay, 200 × 103 DCs were plated at a ratio of 1:4 with the target tumor cell (Human-lymphoma BJAB, ACC 757) in a final volume of 500 µl in a 48-well plate. Prior to coculture, tumor cells were heat-killed at 95 ºC for 30 min. Heat-killed tumor cells were cooled down on ice for 10 min and stained with Cell-Trace Violet (CTV, C34557, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 2 h of coculture at 37ºC, cells were stained to identify DC2s (CD1c-FITC, 1:25, 130-113-301, Miltenyi Biotec) and cDC1s (CD141 PerCP-Cy5.5, 1:25, 344112, BioLegend) prior to analysis using a BD FACSLyric™. To distinguish active from passive endocytosis, DCs cultured with tumor cells were in parallel incubated at 4 ºC for 2 h (see Supplementary Figure 3G). Data was then analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

Tetanus toxoid and CMV antigen-specific assay

To establish the CD34-derived DCs’ capacity to induce antigen-specific T-cell responses, DCs were cocultured with autologous T-cells in the presence of CMV-peptides (130-093-435, Miltenyi Biotec) or the tetanus toxoid (TT) (582231, Thermo Fisher Scientific). 10 × 103 DCs (in duplicates) were stimulated with 0.5 µg/ml TT or 0.1 µg/ml CMV-peptides. DCs were then cocultured at a 1:25 ratio with autologous pan T-cells previously labeled with CFSE as described earlier. To distinguish antigen-specific T-cell activation from baseline T-cell activation, cocultures were also performed in the absence of the antigen. Cells were cocultured for 6 d until analysis. T-cells were harvested for flow cytometry analysis, and supernatant was collected and analyzed with a LEGENDplex™ Human Essential Immune Response Panel (740929, BioLegend).

For flow cytometry characterization, T-cells were incubated with the fixable viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS, 15 min, 4 ºC). T-cells were then washed and incubated with antibodies against CD8-APC (1:50, 555369, BD Biosciences) and either a CD4-BV421 (1:50, 562424, BD Biosciences) or a CD4-PE (1:50, 555347, BD Biosciences). T-cell proliferation was determined by flow cytometry with BD FACSVerse™ (see Supplementary Figure 3A) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

De novo antigen-specific CD8 T-cell induction

To assess the CD34-derived DCs’ ability to prime de novo antigen-specific T-cell responses against a relevant tumor antigen, DCs were generated from CD34+ cells isolated from HLA-A2+ buffy coats and cocultured with autologous naive CD8 T-cells. For these induction experiments, 0.5 × 106 cDC1s and cDC2s were combined (pan-DC, approximate ratio 1:1) in a 48-well plate (0.5 ml final volume). DCs were matured overnight with 5µg/ml of R848 and Poly(IC) in the presence of 10 µM of MART1 peptide (ELAGIGILTV, Genscript). The next day, DCs were provided with a second dosage of 10 µM of the MART1 peptide prior to coculture with the autologous naive T-cells. After 1 h of incubation, 500 µL of medium containing 1 million autologous naive CD8 T-cells were added to the DC cultures (1:2 DC to naive CD8 T-cell ratio). On days 3, 6, 8, and 10, cocultures were supplemented with 10 ng/ml IL-7 and IL-15, and split into new wells with fresh media. On day 12, expanded T-cells were replated in fresh medium at 0.5 × 106 cells/ml. Replated CD8 T-cells were then kept for 24 hours in this cytokine-free media to facilitate a rested state.

Frequency of MART1-specific CD8 T-cells was determined using MART1 specific Dextramer-PE (1:25, WB02162, Immudex) for 25 min. As a negative control for the Dextramer staining, empty Dextramers-PE were used (WB02666, Immudex). MART1 specific CD8 T-cells were analyzed using BD FACSLyric™ (see Supplementary Figure 4A) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

T-cell responsiveness to tumor-antigen rechallenge was performed by coculturing MART1-loaded autologous moDCs (10 µM MART1 peptide provided 2 h prior to coculture) together with the pool of expanded CD8 T-cells. Coculture was performed at a ratio of 1:4 (moDC:CD8 T-cell) for 20 h prior to T-cell characterization. Cells were stained with the viability-dye eFluor780 (1:2000 in PBS, 15 min, 4ºC). Cells were then incubated with blocking buffer (PBA supplemented with 5%HS) for 15 min at 4 ºC. After, T-cells were stained with antibodies against CD8-FITC (1:25, 555366, BD Biosciences), CD25-PE-Cy7 (1:25, 302612, BioLegend), CD69-PerCP (1:25, 340548, BD Biosciences), and CD107a-Alexa700 (1:25, 561340, BD Biosciences), and CD137-APC (1:25, 550890, BD Biosciences). Marker expression was determined with BD FACSLyric™ (see Supplementary Figure 4B) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

For cytokine production determination, T-cell cocultures were provided with 10 ng/ml brefeldin-A for the last 3 h of coculture. Then, cells were stained with the viability-dye eFluor780, followed by fixation and permeabilization using the FOXP3/Transcription Factor Staining kit. T-cells were incubated with antibodies against TNFα-APC (1:50, 130-117-382, Miltenyi Biotec), IL-2-Alexa488 (1:50, 500314, BioLegend), IFN-γ-BV421 (1:50, 562988, BD Biosciences), and CD8-FITC (1:25, 555366, BD Biosciences). After 30 min of incubation, cytokine production was determined with BD FACSLyric™ (see supplementary figure 4B) and analyzed with FlowJo software v10.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 8 (version 8.0.2; GraphPad Software). Unless otherwise indicated, data are presented as mean ± SEM in either bar graphs or scatter dot plots with each dot representing a different donor. Statistical significance was determined as indicated in the legend of each figure after assessing whether the dataset followed a normal distribution or not. When comparing untreated and TLR-stimulated DCs, data was inquired using paired analysis, whereas comparisons between cDC1s and cDC2s were determined using an unpaired analysis. Graphs show the non-significant comparisons in the form of numerical P-values, while significant comparisons are displayed using asterisks. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

Blood CD34-derived cDC1 and cDC2 cells are generated in similar quantities and recapitulate their blood counterparts

CD34+ cells isolated from human peripheral blood were used as the starting material. Enriched CD34+ cells obtained without G-CSF mobilization underwent sequential expansion and differentiation into DCs over a 17-day culture period (Figure 1A). This protocol yielded similar frequencies of cDC1s (identified as CD141 and CLEC9A positive) and cDC2s (CD1c positive) (Figure 1B and C), with a high cellular viability (see supplementary figure 1D). This protocol yielded an average of 5–7 million cDC1s and cDC2s per 1 million CD34+ input-cells (Figure 1D). Further characterization of the non-cDC1/cDC2 compartment revealed that the majority of cells represented myeloid cell progenitors at various stages of differentiation (see Supplementary Figure 1E). Lineage-positive cells (e.g., CD14+), CD16 + HLA-DR + DCs, and transitional DCs (Axl + SIGLEC6+) were either negligible or undetectable within the cultures. Additionally, a consistent population of resting pDCs was not detected across donors, as cells expressing the canonical pDC markers CD123 and BDCA2 were absent from the resulting cultures (see Supplementary Figure 1F–G).

Further characterization of the generated subsets demonstrated that the differentiated cDC1-like cells express high levels of CD141 and CLEC9A, together with the antigen-presenting molecules HLA-DR and HLA-ABC, with a heterogeneous XCR1 expression (Figure 1E and Supplementary Figure 1I, J). Characterization of marker expression on cDC2s revealed a high expression of CD1c, HLA-DR, HLA-ABC, and a heterogeneous expression of the markers CD11b and SIRPα. Additionally, cDC2s exhibited negligible to no expression of the DC3(-like) markers CD14 and CD163 (Figure 1D and Supplementary Figure 1H and 1J). Overall, the protocol yields high numbers of cDC1s and cDC2s-like cells with lineage marker expression closely resembling that of naturally occurring human blood DC subsets.

CD34-derived cDC1s and cDC2s acquire a fully mature state upon TLR challenge

DCs require the acquisition of a mature phenotype, including the upregulation of lymph node homing chemokine receptors, co-stimulatory molecules, and antigen-presenting molecules, which enables the efficient and robust induction of T-cell immunity. 28 , 29 In addition, DC maturation is associated with the production of multiple cytokines and chemokines that facilitate the induction of robust pro-inflammatory immunity. Therefore, we assessed whether the generated cDC1 and cDC2-like cells were capable of acquiring a mature phenotype upon TLR stimulation. To this end, cDC1s and cDC2s were sorted from the day-17 culture (see Supplementary Figure 1B, 1C) and replated in fresh culture medium with or without a TLR cocktail for 24 hours prior to assessment of surface marker expression by flow cytometry.

In both DC subsets, TLR stimulation induced upregulation of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80, CD86, CD83, and CD40, together with increased expression of the lymph node homing chemokine receptor CCR7 and the antigen-presenting molecule HLA-DR (Figure 2A and B), observable in percentage of expression as well as gMFI (see Supplementary Figure 2G). A modest reduction in cDC2 viability was observed following TLR-induced maturation (see Supplementary Figure 1E). Of note, both DC subsets exhibited a partially mature phenotype in the absence of TLR stimulation. This phenotype was consistently observed across all maturation markers, with cDC1s displaying a more pronounced activated phenotype compared to DC2s (Figure 2A and B).

To further characterize the functional capacity of cDC1s and cDC2s to produce inflammatory mediators, culture supernatants were collected 24 h after TLR stimulation and analyzed using LEGENDplex™ cytometric bead array. TLR stimulation significantly enhanced the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines for both DC subsets, including IL-12p70, TNFα, IL-6, and IL-1β, as well as the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Figure 2C and Supplementary Figure 2H). Whilst both cDC1s and cDC2s did not secrete detectable levels of TGFβ, they produced high levels of various chemokines, including CCL2, CXCL8, and CXCL10 (Figure 2C and Supplementary Figure 2H), which are crucial for the recruitment of other innate immune cells and cytotoxic lymphocytes to the site of immune activation.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that CD34-derived DCs acquire a fully mature and functional pro-inflammatory phenotype upon TLR stimulation.

CD34-derived CDC1s and DC2s induce potent pro-inflammatory CD4 and CD8 T-cell responses

DCs play a central role in both the initiation and polarization of T-cell immune responses. To assess the capacity of generated CD34-derived DCs to induce T-cell expansion, allogeneic cocultures were established with pan T-cells that had been pre-labeled with the proliferation dye CFSE. After 6 d of coculture, T-cell proliferation was quantified based on CFSE dilution, and supernatants were collected for subsequent IFN-γ measurement by ELISA. Consistent with the acquisition of a mature DC phenotype, TLR stimulation resulted in a marked increase in T-cell proliferation in cocultures with both DC subsets (Figure 3A and B). In line with these findings, cocultures with either cDC1s or cDC2s led to substantially higher levels of IFN-γ (Figure 3C). Interestingly, cDC2 cocultures yielded significantly higher levels of IFN-γ compared with cDC1 cocultures. To determine whether cDC1s and cDC2s differentially influenced specific subsets of T-cells, the proliferation of CD4 and CD8 T-cells was analyzed. cDC1s preferentially promoted CD8 T-cell proliferation relative to CD4 T-cells, as reflected by an increased CD8/CD4 proliferation ratio compared with DC2 cocultures (Figure 3D).

To further examine the T-cell-skewing potential of CD34-derived CcDC1s and cDC2s, cocultures were established with allogeneic naive CD4 and CD8 T-cells. Intracellular cytokine staining demonstrated that both DC subsets, particularly following TLR stimulation, efficiently expanded CD4 T-cells producing high levels of IFN-γ and TNFα (Figure 3E and F). Analysis of individual cytokines showed a pronounced shift toward an IFN-γ-producing phenotype upon DC maturation (Figure 3G), whereas TNFα expression alone remained largely unchanged (see Supplementary Figure 3D). Additionally, regulatory T-cells (Tregs; CD4+CD127-CD25+FOXP3+) were quantified. Flow cytometric analysis revealed negligible Treg frequencies in all cocultures, particularly following DC maturation, regardless of the DC subset used (Figure 3H).

To further characterize CD8 T-cells, intracellular expression of the cytotoxicity effector molecules perforin (PRF) and granzyme-B (GRZB), as well as IFN-γ and TNFα production, was assessed. CD8 T-cells cocultured with TLR-matured DCs expressed high levels of both PRF and GRZB (Figure 3I and J). Baseline IFN-γ and TNFα expression was already substantial and was further upregulated upon DC maturation (Figure 3K). As for the individual cytokine expression, only a modest increase in IFN-γ-producing CD8 T-cells was observed (Figure 3L), with no major differences in TNFα expression alone (Supplementary Figure 3D). Comparison of DC subsets revealed a slightly greater capacity of cDC2s to induce IFN-γ-producing CD8 T-cells compared to CDC1s (Figure 3L). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CD34-derived cDC1 and cDC2-like cells efficiently promote expansion and polarization of pro-inflammatory CD4 and CD8 T-cell responses.

CD34-derived DCs exhibit distinct endocytic capacity

DCs continuously sample their environment for potential threats and associated antigens, a process that is crucial for the induction of antigen-specific T-cell responses. To determine whether the generated cDC1 and cDC2-like cells were able to effectively acquire cellular cargo via endocytosis, enriched cDC1s and cDC2s were cocultured for two hours with heat-killed tumor cells that had been fluorescently labeled with CTV (Figure 4A). Active tumor cell uptake was determined by comparison with DC-tumor cell cocultures incubated at 4 ºC (see Supplementary Figure 3G). Distinct endocytic capacities were observed between the two subsets, with cDC2s displaying superior uptake of tumor cell-derived cargo, as indicated by higher CTV signal intensity compared with cDC1s (Figure 4A and B). In addition, the capacity of cDC1s and cDC2s to internalize soluble protein via endocytosis was assessed using fluorescently labeled dextran. Cells were incubated for 10 minutes (Figure 4C), and active uptake was determined by comparison with cells incubated at 4 degrees (see Supplementary Figure 3F). Consistent with the tumor cell uptake experiments, cDC2s exhibited a higher endocytic capacity than cDC1s (Figure 4D). Together, these data demonstrate that both generated DC subsets actively engage in the uptake of environmental cargo, including cellular and protein-derived material, with cDC2s exhibiting a higher uptake capacity than cDC1s.

CD34-derived CDC1s and DC2s effectively present antigen and activate T-cells

Although CD34-derived cDC1s and cDC2s were shown to take up environmental cargo, their capacity to effectively present peptides via antigen-presenting molecules and induce T-cell activation remained to be determined. To address this, cDC1s and cDC2s were exposed to tetanus toxoid (TT) or cytomegalovirus (CMV) peptides, antigens for which most adults are considered to possess a memory T-cell response. DCs were cocultured for 6 d with autologous CFSE-labeled pan T-cells. Antigen-specific T-cell proliferation was assessed on day 6 by determining the frequency of CFSElow T-cells relative to cocultures performed in the absence of antigens (Figure 4E). Both cDC1s and cDC2s efficiently presented antigen and induced activation and expansion of antigen-specific T cells (Figure 4F). Further characterization of the CD4/CD8 compartment demonstrated the ability of CD34-derived cDCs to induce CD8 T-cell activation upon TT stimulation, indicative of cross-presentation of antigen-derived peptides (see Supplementary Figure 3H). Additionally, levels of the T-cell activation-associated cytokines IL-2 and IFN-γ were quantified in coculture supernatants. In line with the proliferation data, both cytokines were produced in an antigen-specific manner by cocultures containing either DC subset (Figure 4G). Interestingly, modest subset-specific differences were observed: cDC1s tended to induce stronger CMV-specific T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ production, whereas cDC2s preferentially promoted TT-specific T-cell proliferation and IL-2 production.

Although modest, these differences are consistent with the literature suggesting an intrinsic tendency of CDC1s and DC2s to preferentially support CD8 or CD4 T-cell responses, respectively. To further explore this, expression levels of the antigen-presenting molecules HLA-DR or HLA-ABC were assessed. Flow cytometric analysis revealed higher HLA-DR expression on cDC2s, whereas cDC1s expressed higher levels of HLA-ABC (see Supplementary Figure 2D, E). Together, these data demonstrate that CD34-derived DC subsets differentially express HLA molecules and are capable of antigen uptake, processing, and presentation, resulting in effective activation and expansion of autologous T-cells.

Ex vivo-generated pan-DCs induce de novo tumor-antigen-specific CD8 T-cell responses

DCs are highly effective at inducing antigen-specific T-cell responses from naive T-cell pools, a property that underpins their therapeutic potential in anti-tumor immunotherapy. Therefore, the ability of CD34-derived DCs to induce de novo tumor-antigen-specific CD8 T-cell responses from naive T-cells was evaluated.

For these experiments, CD34+ cells and autologous naive CD8 T-cells were isolated from HLA-A2+ buffy coats, with naive CD8 T-cells cryopreserved until use. CD34+ cells were differentiated into DCs as described above. Given that both cDC1s and cDC2s promoted pro-inflammatory CD8 T-cell responses, both subsets were combined into a single population referred to as pan-DCs and used as a platform for antigen-specific CD8 T-cell priming (Figure 5A). Enriched pan-DCs were matured overnight with R848 and Poly(IC) in the presence of the MART1 peptide. The day after, autologous naive CD8 T-cells were thawed and cocultured with matured pan-DCs at an approximate DC-to-naive T-cell ratio of 1:2. After 3 d, cocultures were supplemented with IL-7 and IL-15 every 2–3 d until day 13. At day 13, expansion of MART1-specific CD8 T-cells was assessed with dextramer staining (Figure 5B). CD34-derived pan-DCs consistently induced the expansion of MART1-specific CD8 T-cells across donors (Figure 5C), although inter-donor variability in expansion magnitude was observed (Figure 5C and Supplementary Figure 4A).

To further characterize the pan-DC-induced CD8 T-cells, responsiveness to antigen rechallenge was evaluated. (Figure 5D). MART1-loaded moDCs were cocultured with the expanded CD8 T-cell pool derived from the expanded peptide and non-peptide specific CD8 T-cells. After 24 hours, expression of activation markers was assessed, and intracellular cytokine production was measured following brefeldin A treatment during the final 3 hours of coculture. Antigen rechallenge resulted in upregulation of the activation markers CD25, CD137, and CD69 on CD8 T-cells (Figure 5E, F, and G). In addition, increased surface expression of CD107a was detected, indicating enhanced cytotoxic potential (Figure 5G). Consistent with this activated phenotype, rechallenged CD8 T-cells produced IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α (Figure 5G). Although MART1 responsiveness was observed across all donors (Figure 5D), considerable inter-donor variability was noted, likely reflecting differences in the abundance of MART1-specific CD8 T-cells per donor (see Supplementary Figure 4A). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CD34-derived DCs can induce de novo antigen-specific CD8 T-cells from naive precursors, that are sensitive and responsive to antigen rechallenge, acquiring a pro-inflammatory state desirable for anti-tumor immunity.

Discussion

Building on previous work and aiming to establish an autologous, clinically feasible approach, we developed a protocol that enables the ex vivo generation of human cDC1s and cDC2s from CD34+ progenitor cells isolated from non-G-CSF-mobilized adult peripheral blood. With this protocol, 1 × 106 blood progenitors (typically obtainable from a peripheral blood apheresis product corresponding to 1–2 liters of processed blood volume) yield an approximate of 5 × 106 cDC2s and 6 × 106 cDC1s. Although earlier reports have shown a comparable yield in CDC1 23 and DC2 25 quantities, our protocol is the first to achieve a similar cDC1 to cDC2 ratio in the output product. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that these previous protocols are based on cord blood or G-CSF-mobilized progenitors, known to preserve a higher expanding capacity compared to that of the adult blood progenitors used in this study. 30‐33 Importantly, and with clinical translation in mind, with this protocol, both cDC1 and cDC2-like cells can be efficiently enriched from the final cell product via magnetic sorting (see Supplementary Figure 1C), demonstrating the practicality of this approach for DC vaccine manufacturing.

To assess the resemblance of the ex vivo-generated cells to bona fide blood cDC1s and cDC2s, we evaluated the expression of established lineage markers for each subset. CD34-derived cDC1s recapitulate their blood counterparts (high CD141, CLEC9A, HLA-DR/ABC expression, along with a partial XCR1 expression). This heterogeneous XCR1 expression likely reflects the presence of multiple developmental stages within the cDC1 population, as XCR1-negative cDC1s have been identified as immediate precursors of terminally differentiated cDC1s. 34 CD34-derived cDC2s displayed robust expression of CD1c and HLA-DR/ABC, as well as partial expression of SIRPα, CD5, and CD11b, consistent with blood DC2s. 35 , 36 Importantly, with regards to anti-cancer therapies, the resulting cDC2 cells express negligible levels of the so-called DC3 markers CD14 and CD163 37 associated with suppressive DCs and cancer patients with a poor clinical prognosis. 13,36,38‐40

The resulting CD34-derived DCs displayed subset-specific HLA expression patterns. cDC1s expressed higher levels of HLA-ABC compared with cDC2s, whereas cDC2s expressed higher levels of HLA-DR, potentially reflecting their respective specializations in promoting CD8 T-cell or CD4 T-cell responses. This observation is consistent with prior reports describing intrinsic HLA-expression differences between human DC subsets. 12 , 19 , 41 , 42 Together, these data indicate that the generated CD34-derived cDC1 and cDC2-like cells resemble and preserve key phenotypical characteristics relevant to their immunological function.

In addition, we assessed the capacity of the CD34-derived cDC1s and cDC2s to acquire a mature phenotype upon TLR stimulation. Following TLR stimulation, both subsets upregulated key maturation and co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD86, and CD40), HLA-DR, and the chemokine receptor CCR7. Resembling their blood DC counterparts, CD34-derived DCs produced high levels of TNF-α, IL-12p70, and CXCL10. 12 , 19 , 25 , 43 , 44, 26 In agreement with earlier studies, CD34-derived cDC1s produced greater levels of CXCL10 compared to cDC2s. 19 With regards to their T-cell stimulatory capacity, CD34-derived DCs induced robust allogeneic T-cell proliferation accompanied by a strong IFN-γ production. cDC1 cocultures preferentially expanded CD8 T-cells over CD4 T-cells, consistent with the well-established specialization of CDC1s in priming CD8 T-cell responses, 6 , 19 , 45 potentially attributable to the higher expression of HLA-ABC on cDC1s relative to cDC2s. CD34-derived DCs drove a pronounced skewing toward IFN-γ-producing CD4 and CD8 T-cells, together with CD8 T-cells expressing high levels of the cytotoxic molecules (e.g., granzyme-B). To exclude the possibility of undesirable regulatory effects, an important consideration for prospective DC vaccines, 46 we assessed the induction of regulatory T-cells and detected negligible Treg frequencies. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that these CD34-derived DCs acquire a mature state capable of stimulating the expansion of pro-inflammatory T-cell subsets, crucial for potent anti-tumor immunity.

To assess their ability to induce antigen-specific responses, we first examined the capacity of CD34-derived cDC1s and cDC2s to take up exogenous cargo. CD34-derived cDC1s exhibited markedly lower cargo uptake compared with cDC2s, aligning with earlier studies conducted on their blood counterparts. 6 , 8 , 19 , 47 CD34-derived cDC1 and cDC2-like cells efficiently activated autologous T-cells in response to tetanus toxoid or CMV-derived peptides. In agreement with prior findings, CD34-derived cDC1s induced a more pronounced CD8 T-cell response upon CMV challenge compared to cDC2s. 19 Given that 15-mer CMV peptides were used, this enhanced CD8 T-cell activation may be attributed to the well-established cross-presenting capacity of cDC1s. 18

In the context of DC vaccination, the clinical benefit is regarded as deriving from the induction of de novo T-cell responses against tumor-derived antigens. 48‐51 Although our data shows that CD34-derived DCs can efficiently reactivate memory T-cells, it remained unclear whether these ex vivo–generated DCs could also prime naive T-cells against a given antigen. Mimicking a vaccination setup, CD34-derived DCs were challenged to induce tumor antigen-specific de novo T-cell responses. Across all donors, CD34-derived DCs primed naive CD8 T-cells, yielding the expansion of CD8 T-cells specific for the tumor-derived antigen MART1. Importantly, upon MART1 rechallenge, the induced CD8 T-cells acquired an active and pro-inflammatory state (e.g., expression of CD137, IFN-γ, and CD107a). Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that CD34-derived DCs initiate de novo antigen-specific CD8 T-cell responses and establish antigen-responsive CD8 T-cells, crucial for effective anti-tumor immunity.

While our findings highlight the strong therapeutic potential of these CD34-derived DCs, the major limitation of this study is that our culture system does not generate pDCs. However, given the limited T-cell stimulatory capacity of pDCs, 15‐17 we believe this protocol, capable of a high cDC1 and cDC2-like cell yield, holds great potential for DC vaccination purposes, where robust antigen-specific T-cell induction is key. This protocol offers major advantages, such as being feeder layer free, GMP-compliant, and capable of generating comparable quantities of cDC1 and cDC2-like subsets. More importantly, and in line with an autologous therapeutic strategy, the protocol relies on isolated CD34 progenitors present in peripheral blood without the need for G-CSF mobilization. To our knowledge, this is the first approach capable of generating clinically relevant numbers of both cDC1s and cDC2s from this progenitor source. Together, our results demonstrate that non-G-CSF mobilized blood progenitors can differentiate into cDC1 and cDC2-like cells eliciting potent T-cell subsets, underscoring their promise for the development of DC-based therapies capable of mounting durable and potentially curative anti-tumor responses.
