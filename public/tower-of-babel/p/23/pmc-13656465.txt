Diagnosing carcinoma origin in ultrasound-guided needle biopsies: a context-driven approach anchored in site-specific immunohistochemical markers
Authors: Gratiana Hermann, Raluca Ghica, Rares Buiga, Iunia Cebotaru, Daniela Coza, Zeno Sparchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn small-biopsy carcinomas, tumor-origin assignment often relies on limited tissue, clinical-radiologic context, morphology, and immunohistochemistry. Introduction

Assigning carcinoma origin in small biopsies represents a central task in diagnostic pathology, particularly in the evaluation of metastatic or poorly differentiated tumors. In routine practice, site-specific markers such as TTF1, CDX2/SATB2, GATA3, PAX8, napsin A, WT1, HepPar-1, and glypican-3 often provide high-confidence support for tumor origin when informative. In contrast, the CK7/CK20 tandem typically serves an orientational role, especially in metastatic carcinomas and workups for carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP), where it helps narrow the differential but rarely establishes origin independently [1–6], given its overlap and exceptions across tumor types [7].

The diagnostic gold standard for metastatic disease in patients with a known primary tumor remains side-by-side morphologic comparison between primary and metastatic lesions. However, when prior material is unavailable and/or morphology is equivocal, immunohistochemistry (IHC) can provide supportive evidence for tumor-origin assignment, as illustrated in organ-specific settings such as lung carcinoma [8].

Although the clinical information accompanying small biopsies may appear concise on submission forms, additional clinical and imaging information is often available in contemporary practice.

Together with morphology, biopsy site, patient age and sex, and epidemiologic tumor prevalence, these sources may provide sufficient diagnostic orientation to support a targeted IHC strategy rather than reflexive use of broader panels including the CK7/CK20 tandem. For some tumor origins outside CUP/MUO-centered workups, when the diagnostic context already supports a likely origin and highly informative site-specific markers exist for the suspected tumor origin, CK7/CK20 could be required less frequently for initial diagnostic orientation. In diagnostic practice, context-driven IHC interpretation and site-specific markers are widely used to support tumor-origin assignment; however, how often this support is achieved through site-specific-marker–anchored IHC without CK7/CK20 dependence in routine, context-rich, non-CUP/MUO small-biopsy cohorts has been less explicitly addressed. This practice has largely evolved through routine diagnostic practice and, in some settings, organ-specific recommendations [8], rather than through direct evaluation in defined context-rich, non-CUP small-biopsy settings. Although much of the formal evaluation of tumor-origin immunohistochemistry has focused on CUP/MUO, these cases represent only a small minority of cancers overall, generally below 5% [9]. By contrast, most routine small-biopsy carcinoma work occurs outside this setting, making this non-CUP small-biopsy practice pattern worth evaluating in its own right.

Against this backdrop, we audited a selected subset of small-biopsy carcinomas in which a single-site origin had been assigned at sign-out, to determine how often single-site tumor-origin assignment was adequately supported by IHC anchored on site-specific markers without dependence on CK7/CK20.

Materials and methods

Study design and objectives

This retrospective cohort study reviewed the immunohistochemical support for tumor-origin assignment in small-biopsy carcinomas, in a selected subset in which a single-site origin had been assigned at sign-out, within a context-driven immunohistochemical approach in routine diagnostic practice at Humanitas MedLife Hospital (Cluj-Napoca, Romania) between February 2022 and December 2024. The primary objective was to assess how often, in this selected context-rich carcinoma cohort, a diagnostically sufficient single-site tumor-origin assignment was supported by IHC anchored on site-specific markers without dependence on CK7/CK20. A secondary objective was to assess the frequency and incremental contribution of CK7/CK20 beyond morphology, context, and site-specific markers.

Cohort derivation and exclusions

From 154 consecutive, mostly ultrasound-guided tumor biopsies with IHC available, non-carcinomas were excluded (n = 17). From the resulting 137 carcinoma biopsies, additional exclusions were applied for carcinomas coded as uncertain origin (code 0) or compatible pancreatobiliary (PB) origin (code 2), as well as selected specific carcinoma subtypes - neuroendocrine neoplasms, adrenocortical carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, squamous cell carcinomas, primary renal cell carcinoma (RCC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worked up only with CD34, and prostatic carcinoma lacking NKX3.1 at the time of work-up. These exclusions yielded a final analytic cohort of 60 carcinomas. Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow and cohort derivation.

Abbreviations: code 0 uncertain tumor origin (PB-suspected, indeterminate/other); code 1 single-site origin assigned; code 2 compatible pancreatobiliary origin; HCC hepatocellular carcinoma; IHC immunohistochemistry; MUO malignancy of unknown origin; PB pancreatobiliary; provisional CUP, MUO submission remaining without a clear or suggested tumor origin after pathological evaluation; RCC renal cell carcinoma

Primary lung carcinomas and hepatocellular carcinomas were included alongside metastatic carcinomas because they still required single-site origin assignment within routine small-biopsy practice, using the same integrated assessment of morphology, clinical/imaging information, and site-specific IHC support. Their inclusion allowed review of this IHC support across both primary and metastatic biopsy settings.

Pancreatobiliary-compatible and pancreatobiliary-like carcinomas were excluded because their work-up usually requires integration of morphology and pattern-based immunophenotypic findings within the clinical/imaging context. In this setting, CK7/CK20 and other high-yield markers are often used mainly to exclude competing origins or support a compatible pancreatobiliary interpretation, rather than to confirm origin through a single highly sensitive and specific positive site-defining marker. They therefore represent a different diagnostic context from the one audited here.

Neuroendocrine neoplasms, adrenocortical carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinomas were excluded because their routine work-up follows distinct lineage- or organ-specific diagnostic pathways that do not directly address the present audit question. In squamous cell carcinoma, markers such as p40/p63/CK5/6 primarily establish squamous differentiation rather than site of origin, and CK7/CK20 generally does not play a central role in tumor-origin assignment within this diagnostic pathway. In neuroendocrine neoplasms, site-specific markers may show overlap and/or site infidelity, and tumor distribution together with clinical/imaging findings often plays a central role in origin assignment; CK7/CK20 is also generally not central to NEN origin assignment, apart from specific exceptions such as Merkel cell carcinoma, not represented here. Adrenocortical and thyroid carcinomas were also excluded because they represent separate organ-specific diagnostic contexts and, in this cohort, would have added heterogeneity without materially informing the evaluation of site-specific-marker–anchored support for carcinoma-origin assignment.

Biopsy procedures and evaluation

All but one specimen consisted of ultrasound (US)-guided core needle biopsies using an 18-gauge automatic TruCut Needle (Medax, Italy); one case was a peritoneal fluid cell block. Clinical histories and imaging data, including contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), were reviewed through institutional information systems. Tumor-origin assessment was based on information available at the time of biopsy evaluation. In one case, discrepancies between requisition information and electronic records were resolved by medical-record review for case coding and contextual classification.

Specimens were processed using standard formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded techniques. IHC marker selection followed routine diagnostic practice and was context-driven, integrating morphology, biopsy site, clinical and imaging findings, patient age and sex, epidemiologic tumor prevalence, and prior pathology when available. Panels included site-specific markers, CK7 and/or CK20, and additional markers as indicated by the differential diagnosis. A complete list of antibodies used is provided in Additional table file [see Additional file 1].

Tumor origin coding

Tumor origin was coded based on integrated morphologic, immunohistochemical, and clinical/imaging assessment. Code 1 indicated a single-site origin assigned at sign-out; code 2 indicated compatible pancreatobiliary origin; code 0 indicated uncertain origin when no single-site could be assigned. PB-like (code 0) refers to cases with pancreatobiliary-type morphology/working diagnosis that lacked adequate integrated support from morphology, IHC, and/or imaging for a compatible PB assignment (code 2) and therefore remained coded as uncertain origin. Only code 1 carcinomas were included in the analytic cohort.

MUO was used as a submission-status variable, indicating cases submitted without a known or suspected primary site, and was distinct from final tumor-origin coding. Among MUO submissions, cases that remained without a clear or suggested tumor origin after pathological evaluation were classified as provisional CUP; no provisional CUP cases were included in the present analytic cohort.

Independent external review

A subset (n = 37) of the broader 154-case IHC cohort underwent independent external review. Five of them were included in the present cohort, all showing concordant tumor-origin assignment.

Retrospective assessment of IHC panel sufficiency and CK7/CK20

IHC panel sufficiency was retrospectively assessed as the adequacy of the performed IHC panel (i.e., without adding further stains), interpreted in the biopsy-time clinical/imaging and morphologic context, to support a confident single-site tumor-origin assignment. Panels were classified as: sufficient (performed IHC adequately supported a confident single-site origin), borderline (a single-site origin was supportable, but additional IHC would reasonably increase confidence), insufficient (performed IHC did not adequately support a confident single-site origin and additional IHC would be required but was not performed/available), and appropriate but inconclusive on IHC alone (an extensive, appropriate panel was applied but IHC remained differential and single-site assignment required integration of additional information).

Theoretical need for CK7/CK20 (both stains) was assessed retrospectively across all cases, regardless of whether performed, based on whether inclusion of both markers would reasonably have been recommended given morphology and context.

CK7/CK20 contribution was evaluated only in cases in which both markers were performed and was defined as interpretive impact beyond morphology, clinical/imaging context, and site-specific markers.

Collected variables included age, sex, biopsy site, tumor morphology, primary versus metastatic status, tumor-origin coding, IHC markers performed, site-specific marker use, theoretical need for CK7/CK20, and contribution when applicable.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cohort characteristics, tumor-origin categories, IHC sufficiency, site-specific-marker–anchored support without CK7/CK20 dependence and CK7/CK20 application, theoretical need, and contribution. Continuous variables are presented as median and range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

Clinical correlation and ethics

Follow-up or downstream confirmation was available only in selected cases and was not used as a systematic external reference standard for validating diagnostic accuracy. All analyses reflect information available during the active diagnostic work-up up to final sign-out. For selected cases, additional clinical or imaging information that became available during the active diagnostic work-up and before final sign-out was incorporated into the contemporaneous interpretation. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards, all cases were anonymized prior to analysis, and the study protocol was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committees.

Cross‑sectional imaging beyond CEUS was available in 32/60 cases (53%) and included CT, MRI, and FDG PET‑CT; selected endoscopic evaluations were also available. The analytic cohort had a median age of 67 years (range, 46–89 years), comprised 32 females (53%) and 28 males (47%), and included 13 primary tumors (22%), 43 metastases (72%), and 4 malignancies of unknown origin (MUO) submissions (6%). The liver represented the most frequent metastatic biopsy site (29/47, 62%). Poorly differentiated morphology was observed in 26 carcinomas, predominantly in metastatic lesions but also in four primary lung carcinomas. Single-site origin was assigned in all cases (60/60, 100%).

Among the 60 carcinomas, tumor origin was assigned as breast in 25% (15/60), colorectal in 20% (12/60), lung in 18% (11/60), liver/HCC in 17% (10/60), and other origins (Müllerian, renal, gastric, urothelial) in 20% (12/60). All breast and colorectal cases were metastatic, whereas lung and HCC included both primary and metastatic biopsies.

Overall diagnostic IHC sufficiency was achieved in 95% (57/60), including 100% (13/13) of primary tumors and 94% (44/47) of metastases. Site-specific markers were applied in all cases. Discussion

This study examined a selected subgroup of small-biopsy carcinomas in which a single-site origin was assigned at sign-out; accordingly, the findings reflect IHC support for tumor-origin assignment within this resolved subgroup, not the broader spectrum of diagnostically unresolved carcinomas. In this cohort, known prior malignancy and/or cross-sectional imaging supported a suspected primary site in 50/60 cases (83%), together with biopsy site and morphology. In this context-integrated setting, IHC panels anchored in site-specific markers were sufficient to support tumor-origin assignment in 93% (56/60) of cases, with CK7/CK20 contributive in only one case. Published studies have shown that selected site-specific markers can be highly informative in metastatic tumor workup, including GATA3 in breast metastases and SATB2/CDX2-based gastrointestinal panels in appropriate differential settings [10–12]. Other work has emphasized that their diagnostic value is greatest when interpreted within an individualized framework integrating morphology and available clinical/imaging data [6, 13]. Our findings are in keeping with this approach.

Wang reported limited utility of CK7/CK20 in a broader biopsy-and-resection series enriched for CUP/MUO-type workups, largely because CK7/CK20 patterns were often discordant with the final diagnosis [14]. By contrast, our selected, context-rich small-biopsy cohort was not primarily centered on CUP/MUO, and CK7/CK20 appeared less contributive mainly because tumor-origin assignment was usually already supported by morphology, clinical/imaging context, biopsy site, and highly informative site-specific markers. In our setting, the limited contributive role of CK7/CK20 appeared to reflect mainly reduced incremental value beyond those elements. Consequently, in this selected cohort, routine initial reliance on CK7/CK20 was unnecessary in many cases.

Taken together, these findings support the feasibility of a site-specific-marker-first approach within this defined, context-driven small-biopsy cohort not primarily centered on CUP/MUO, while CK7/CK20 retained value in selected tumor-origin assignment scenarios in this audit.

Descriptively, these findings may suggest that a site-specific-marker-first approach could be more readily applicable in small biopsies, in the appropriate morphologic and clinical/imaging context, to breast and colorectal metastasis assignments, and to both primary and metastatic lung- and hepatocellular carcinoma-related assignments, than to more heterogeneous or diagnostically challenging groups.

A site-specific-marker-first approach, in settings similar to those found in our study, may also offer a practical advantage in small biopsies by helping conserve tissue and keeping IHC panels limited when no competing differential diagnosis or discordant result requires broader IHC work-up, particularly when morphology, biopsy site, and clinical/imaging context already support a likely primary site, but prior material is unavailable for direct comparison and/or morphology remains equivocal. More broadly, these findings may point toward the value of better defining minimal confirmatory IHC strategies for small biopsies submitted for downstream molecular testing.

In our cohort, all borderline, insufficient, or appropriate-but-inconclusive panels (3/47) occurred in the metastatic group, illustrating some of the practical diagnostic pitfalls encountered in small biopsies.

In one case, the IHC panel was considered borderline in a female patient with history of resected right-sided colorectal carcinoma, presenting with liver metastasis. Tumor morphology was enteric with dirty necrosis, and IHC showed focal weak CK20 expression, CK7 expression in isolated cells, heterogeneous CDX2 expression, and no SATB2 expression. The absence of additional intestinal or pancreatobiliary-discriminating markers limited diagnostic confidence. In the presence of supportive morphology and a known colonic primary, this IHC panel may be considered acceptable in context; however, from a stand-alone perspective, the findings remain borderline, and additional IHC and/or direct comparison with prior material, and/or clinical/imaging correlation would strengthen tumor-origin assignment.

This was the only colorectal carcinoma in our cohort showing non-homogeneous CDX2 nuclear staining and absent SATB2 expression. Overall, CDX2 was expressed in all colorectal cases, with strong homogeneous expression in 92% (11/12), and SATB2 in 82% (9/11), broadly comparable with reported sensitivities [15–20]. The borderline case is in keeping with prior reports that weak/heterogeneous CDX2, absent SATB2, reduced CK20, and/or aberrant CK7 expression may occur particularly in right-sided and/or poorly differentiated colorectal adenocarcinomas [21–24].

Although colorectal origin was assigned without reliance on CK7/CK20 in this cohort, the limited spectrum of colorectal carcinoma subtypes—mostly conventional adenocarcinomas, with only one mucinous carcinoma without competing primaries and no medullary or other uncommon subtypes—restricts extrapolation across the full colorectal carcinoma spectrum. In underrepresented colorectal carcinoma subtypes or in different competing differential diagnoses, CK7/CK20 or other markers could potentially be informative, depending on the differential diagnosis.

Another case with reduced confidence on IHC alone involved a female patient biopsied for a liver metastasis, with a contemporaneously diagnosed primary breast carcinoma at an outside institution. Because the carcinoma was poorly differentiated and the initial immunophenotype was conflicting (GATA3, mammaglobin, and ER all negative) in this clinical context, an extensive panel was applied, and alternative primaries were considered and largely excluded. During work-up, additional clinical/imaging information became available, including PET-CT showing bone metastases and the outside breast biopsy for direct morphologic comparison. These findings supported metastatic breast carcinoma despite absent GATA3, ER, and mammaglobin expression. This case was classified as appropriate but inconclusive on IHC alone. In our series, this was the only GATA3-negative breast-origin case.

In another case, the IHC panel was insufficient due to limited marker availability, with only focal mammaglobin expression and diffuse CK7 positivity; tumor origin remained supported mainly by CK7 in the absence of useful clinical/imaging information beyond a post-mastectomy state; further work-up was halted by patient retrieval of the tissue block.

In our cohort, GATA3 expression was present in 93% (13/14) of tested breast metastases, mostly with diffuse strong nuclear expression, in keeping with the reported high sensitivity of GATA3 (> 90%) in metastatic breast carcinoma [25, 26]. By contrast, mammaglobin was less consistently informative, being expressed in 27% (3/11) of tested known breast-origin metastases, with diffuse expression in only one case; this is consistent with its recognized limited sensitivity and potential focal/patchy expression in small biopsies and metastatic sites [25–31]. Negative mammaglobin does not exclude breast origin, and interpretation requires integration with morphology, other markers, and clinical/imaging context.

A separate case further illustrates the importance of clinical context. The tumor was poorly differentiated and showed diffuse GATA3 and ER positivity, but also WT1 expression, raising consideration of Müllerian origin. However, PAX8 was negative, and subsequent clinical information confirmed a prior breast carcinoma, with morphologic concordance on comparison with previous biopsies, and no evidence of a Müllerian primary on imaging. This case highlights that WT1 expression may be encountered in breast carcinoma and, in isolation, does not establish Müllerian origin.

In our hepatocellular and lung carcinomas, site-specific markers performed in line with established sensitivity profiles and allowed single-site origin assignment without reliance on CK7/CK20. In well-differentiated HCCs, morphology and hepatocellular markers were more informative than CK7/CK20. However, more challenging hepatic tumors may require broader panels, because hepatocellular markers such as HepPar-1 are not entirely specific and may be expressed in non-HCC mimics, including hepatoid adenocarcinoma; in such settings, CK7/CK20 may contribute as part of an expanded work-up guided by morphology and clinical/imaging context [32].

Case numbers were limited in several tumor categories, particularly renal, Müllerian, gastric, and urothelial carcinomas.

The single case in which CK7/CK20 was directly contributive was a metastatic urothelial carcinoma. In this poorly differentiated carcinoma, GATA3 was absent and p40 was positive; in this context, the CK7+/CK20 + profile, together with morphology and clinical/imaging information, supported urothelial origin. This illustrates that, although CK7/CK20 lacks specificity in isolation, it may retain value in selected scenarios when interpreted in context.

Given the limited number of cases in several subgroups within the heterogeneous “other tumor-origin” group, no formal statistical inference can be drawn.

No major unexpected immunophenotypic deviations were observed in the smaller subgroups, aside from isolated discordant marker expression in individual cases. In these subgroups, the observed marker performance was broadly consistent with published sensitivities.

Beyond its possible diagnostic implications, a site-specific-marker-first approach could also become increasingly relevant as limited formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue in small-biopsy workflows is expected to support downstream molecular analyses. This may be particularly important in routine, context-driven biopsy work-up outside more protocolized settings such as CUP/MUO or specific carcinoma settings such as breast and lung, even though immunohistochemical and downstream molecular analyses in other tumors may be similarly sensitive to pre-analytic conditions. Current guideline-recommended pre-analytic targets include cold ischemia of less than 1 h [33] and fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 6–72 h [34]. When pre-analytic conditions are suboptimal, they may affect the reliability of downstream assays themselves. This has been shown for HER2 assessment in gastric cancer [35], PD-L1 immunohistochemical expression [36], and RNA quantity and quality relevant to next-generation sequencing (NGS) workflows [37]. US-guided core needle biopsies are particularly amenable to pre-analytic standardization because their limited size may facilitate better control of these variables. In terms of tissue amount available for NGS, inadequacy persisted despite optimized workflows and varied by specimen type; notably, CT-guided liver and soft tissue core biopsies still showed inadequacy rates of 14.3% and 15.4%, respectively [38]. Together, these observations suggest that, in small biopsies, both tissue adequacy and downstream assay reliability may be shaped by how the specimen is handled upfront. In the longer term, these same pre-analytic advantages may also make US-guided core needle biopsies attractive for emerging FFPE-based proteomic applications [39], which may expand biomarker discovery and help identify new therapeutic targets.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective, single-institution audit with a modest overall cohort size and small tumor-origin groups, which limits generalizability and requires confirmation in larger cohorts; the findings should therefore be interpreted descriptively only. The heterogeneous “other tumor-origin” group, which included serous Müllerian, urothelial, renal, and gastric carcinomas, contained several tumor origins represented by few or single cases; therefore, conclusions regarding these tumor origins should not extend beyond the observed data. A central limitation is that the analytic cohort was intentionally restricted to carcinomas in which a single-site origin had been assigned at sign-out. This selection enriched the cohort for cases in which tumor-origin assignment was already supportable by context, morphology, and site-specific markers, and excluded many diagnostically uncertain, PB-like, CUP-like, or otherwise ambiguous settings in which CK7/CK20 may be more informative. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as an estimate of the general utility of CK7/CK20 in routine carcinoma-origin work-up. In addition, the retrospective assessment of CK7/CK20 “theoretical need” was based on case-by-case expert judgment rather than an objective and reproducible criterion and may be influenced by hindsight.

Because downstream confirmation by resection, direct comparison with prior material, or systematic clinical follow-up was not available for many cases, the audit assesses the ability of the performed work-up to support a single-site tumor-origin assignment in context, rather than the accuracy of that assignment.

Finally, the cohort did not include several diagnostic settings in which CK7/CK20 is expected to remain an important component of the work-up for tumor-origin assignment alongside site-specific and other IHC markers, particularly adenocarcinomas with intestinal, mucinous, or PB-like morphology and tumors arising in the urinary bladder or urachus. Examples include ampullary/periampullary adenocarcinomas, bladder/urachal adenocarcinomas, mucinous Müllerian carcinomas, enteric-type and colloid lung adenocarcinomas, and other upper gastrointestinal, small-bowel, appendiceal, or colorectal mucinous adenocarcinoma settings. Therefore, the present findings regarding the limited incremental contribution of CK7/CK20 should not be extended to these diagnostic settings.

Despite these limitations, the consecutive biopsy base and uniform diagnostic setting allowed descriptive evaluation of a context-driven, site-specific-marker-first approach in routine small-biopsy practice.

Conclusion

In this selected, context-rich, non-CUP/MUO small-biopsy cohort, tumor-origin assignment was supported in most cases (56/60, 93%) by IHC anchored on site-specific markers without dependence on CK7/CK20, while CK7/CK20 was retrospectively considered needed only in selected cases, and had limited observed incremental contribution beyond morphology, clinical/imaging context, and site-specific markers.

Considering the retrospective, single-institution design, the limited cohort size, the small tumor-origin groups, the heterogeneous “other tumor-origin” group with several origins represented by few or single cases, and the exclusion of several diagnostically challenging tumor categories, further studies are warranted to confirm these findings, assess their generalizability across broader biopsy populations and practice settings, and evaluate diagnostic accuracy against appropriate external reference standards.

As growing demands are placed on limited FFPE tissue in small-biopsy workflows expected to support immunohistochemical analyses, next-generation sequencing, and emerging proteomic applications, our findings highlight the potential relevance of tissue-conscious IHC marker selection, alongside the established importance of pre-analytic standards for downstream assay reliability. More broadly, the relevance of tissue sparing and pre-analytic standardization may extend to other small-biopsy tumor settings in which immunohistochemical and downstream molecular analyses are similarly sensitive to pre-analytic conditions and tissue availability.
