Psychosomatic medicine service competency training in China’s GP residency programs: a national survey from a CBME perspective
Authors: Jiahui Jiang, Zikai Zhang, Yiming Wu, Yingquan Luo, Chen Wang, Tengyang Fan, Jessica Thai, Si Lei, Yuan Gao, Lei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompetency-based medical education (CBME) emphasizes the acquisition of clearly defined, observable competencies aligned with clinical practice needs. Psychosomatic medicine service competency (PMSC) is increasingly recognized as essential for general practitioners (GPs) delivering holistic, biopsychosocial primary care. However, the extent to which PMSC is incorporated into China’s General Practice Standardized Residency Training Program (SRTP) from a CBME perspective remains unclear.MethodsA national cross-sectional survey was conducted from May to August 2024. Training base representatives (e.g., program directors, teaching coordinators, and core faculty) from general practice residency training bases across mainland China were invited to complete an online questionnaire using convenience sampling. The survey explored training content, teaching strategies, evaluation methods, and institutional challenges among training practices for PMSC in SRTP.ResultsA total of 200 questionnaires were distributed, and 193 valid responses were received (response rate: 96.5%). While basic PMSC such as communication skills and empathy were reportedly covered in most programs, only a minority offered structured training in managing specific conditions. Less than 40% of programs employed interactive learning methods such as role-playing, multidisciplinary collaboration, or situational teaching. Major barriers identified included a lack of standardized curricula, insufficient qualified faculty, and limited institutional prioritization. Despite these gaps, 94.3% of respondents expressed strong support for enhancing PMSC for GPs in SRTP.ConclusionPMSC training within China’s GP SRTP shows suboptimal alignment with CBME principles. Advancing psychosomatic education will likely require explicit competency definitions, structured workplace-based learning, and competency-oriented assessment. Given the convenience sampling design and reliance on institutional self-reporting by training base representatives, findings should be interpreted cautiously and may not directly reflect trainees’ experiences or actual competency attainment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09162-2.Keywords: Psychosomatic medicine, General practice, Standardized Residency Training Program, Medical education reform

Introduction

General practitioners (GPs) serve as the first point of contact for patients presenting with a wide range of physical complaints, many of which are closely intertwined with psychological distress, social adversity, and functional symptoms [1–3]. Effectively managing these presentations requires competencies extending beyond biomedical diagnosis to include patient-centered communication and integrative mind-body care [4–6]. However, a significant gap remains in how these competencies are integrated into China’s General Practice Standardized Residency Training Program (SRTP) [7, 8].

Psychosomatic medicine service competency (PMSC) in primary care provides a structured approach to the biopsychosocial interactions in health [9, 10]. Importantly, PMSC differs from specialist psychiatric care; it emphasizes GPs’ abilities to recognize psychosocial contributors to physical symptoms, provide basic psychoeducation, and manage common stress-related conditions within the scope of primary care practice [11, 12].

In this study, psychosomatic medicine is conceptually distinguished from—but related to—broader terms such as behavioral health. While behavioral health often serves as an umbrella term encompassing mental health conditions and health-related behaviors, psychosomatic medicine specifically emphasizes mind–body interactions, particularly functional somatic symptoms and psychological distress associated with chronic medical illness. By focusing on these interactions, PMSC offers a feasible and competency-oriented framework for GPs to act as clinical “gatekeepers,” supporting early recognition, initial assessment, and appropriate referral when specialist mental health care is required [13].

This study is conceptually grounded in the biopsychosocial model and the principles of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME). The former provides the theoretical foundation for holistic care [14, 15], while CBME frames training as the progressive acquisition of observable and assessable outcomes [16–18]. From this perspective, PMSC is shaped not only by curricular content but also by teaching strategies, faculty capacity, and institutional support.

While PMSC domains have been increasingly embedded in residency curricula in countries such as Germany and the United States through workplace-based teaching and defined milestones, where structured psychosomatic training requirements (e.g., 40–80 h in Germany) and integrated workplace-based models are implemented [19–22], China’s SRTP has yet to systematically integrate these elements. Existing Chinese research has predominantly focused on psychiatric rotations or general mental health education, offering limited insight into how psychosomatic-specific competencies—such as the assessment of functional somatic symptoms and psychosocial contributors to physical complaints—are conceptualized and assessed at the primary care level [23, 24].

Guided by this framework, this national survey evaluates the status of PMSC training within China’s GP SRTP, focusing on: (1) the scope of psychosomatic‑related competencies included in training programs; (2) teaching and assessment strategies used to support competency development; (3) faculty capacity and institutional prioritization; and (4) perceived barriers to effective implementation. By providing a nationwide, system‑level overview from the perspective of training institutions, this study seeks to generate evidence to inform curriculum development and policy‑oriented educational reform in general practice training.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to evaluate the current capacity for PMSC and related training in GP residency programs across mainland China. The survey was administered between May and August 2024. One authorized representative—the program director, teaching director, coordinators, or a core faculty member—from each general practice training base was invited to complete the questionnaire, with each base submitting one response.

Participants and sampling

A convenience sampling method was employed for this study. Training bases were first identified using the official Directory of Standardized Residency Training Bases published by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China. Based on this national registry, we reached out to approximately 30% of the 680 accredited General Practice (GP) residency training bases across mainland China. Potential participants were contacted and invited through the National Medical Education Collaboration network and affiliated teaching hospital partnerships.

Eligible respondents included program directors, teaching heads, pedagogical secretaries, or core faculty members who were directly involved in curriculum planning and implementation and had comprehensive knowledge of their institution’s residency training program. Although the sampling was non-randomized, participating training bases were distributed across 29 provincial-level administrative regions, providing substantial geographic diversity and representation of different socioeconomic contexts in mainland China.

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered via Wenjuanxing, a professional web-based survey platform widely used in China. Survey links were distributed electronically, and participation was voluntary. A total of 200 eligible participants were invited to complete the questionnaire.

To ensure data quality and integrity, the survey platform restricted submissions to one response per IP address, and institutional identifiers were cross-checked during data cleaning to prevent duplicate entries. Built-in logic checks were applied to identify internal inconsistencies. Questionnaires demonstrating insufficient completion time or logical contradictions were excluded. After quality control procedures, 193 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis, yielding a response rate of 96.5%.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) holding a managerial role and being familiar with training activities at the base; (2) working at a nationally accredited general practice residency training base; (3) voluntary participation with full completion of the online questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of direct involvement in training management; (2) part-time or temporary administrative roles; (3) refusal or withdrawal of consent; (4) prolonged absence preventing an accurate response.

Survey instrument development

The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review. Items in Sect. 2 (Assessment of psychosomatic and mental health service capacity) were adapted from previously validated instruments, with permission from the original authors, and supplemented by additional items designed by the research team [25]. All remaining sections (Sects. 1 and 3–5) were newly developed by the research team to reflect the specific structure, training content, and implementation context of China’s SRTP.

Content validity and pilot testing

Content validity was established through expert review by a multidisciplinary panel consisting of senior general practitioners, psychiatrists with expertise in psychosomatic medicine, and a medical education specialist. Panel members evaluated the relevance, clarity, and appropriateness of questionnaire items for assessing PMSC training within SRTP. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made to improve item wording and ensure alignment with GP-level training objectives.

Prior to nationwide distribution, the questionnaire was pilot-tested among a small group of GP educators and training coordinators from accredited training bases to assess clarity, feasibility, and completion time. Feedback from the pilot test led to minor refinements in wording and format. Data from the pilot phase were not included in the final analysis.

Reliability assessment

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha separately for each section comprising multiple related items. The questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency across domains, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.92 for Sects. 2, 0.95 for Sect. 3, and 0.84 for Sect. 4, indicating good to excellent reliability for each construct.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongji Hospital (approval number: K-W-2023-002). Participation was voluntary, and all respondents provided informed consent prior to participation. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all data were anonymized and stored securely in accordance with ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%), while continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). For Table 1, one-sample proportion tests and χ² goodness-of-fit tests were used for selected categorical variables to examine deviations from expected distributions. No formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied, as analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Results

Among the 200 surveys distributed, a total of 193 valid responses were obtained from program directors, teaching directors, coordinators, and core faculties across 29 provincial-level regions in mainland China. Most participants were aged 41–50 years (47.67%), with a female majority (67.36%). Analysis of the Sankey diagram indicates that 77.72% of respondents have longer professional experience, with many holding intermediate or higher titles (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Overall, 25.91% of the respondents characterized the training as a ‘high priority,’ while 39.9% viewed it as a ‘moderate priority’ (Table 2). Coverage of general PMSC-related competencies varied. Communication skills (75.13%) and respect for patient autonomy (90.16%) were frequently included in training programs (defined as fully or often utilized). In contrast, psychological education, understanding mental health legislation, emergency psychiatry, psychotherapy, and psychiatric medication use were reported as fully or often utilized in fewer than 75% of programs (Fig. 3).

Training content differed by condition. Sleep–wake disorders and anxiety disorders were more frequently reported as covered at a moderate or high level. In contrast, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and somatoform disorders were commonly reported as minimally covered or not included (Fig. 4).

Outpatient (91.19%) and inpatient (84.46%) clinical teaching were the most commonly reported training formats, followed by lectures (78.76%), shadowing (65.80%), and case discussions (60.10%). Interactive methods such as multidisciplinary team participation (39.38%), role-playing (29.02%), and situational teaching (37.82%) were less frequently reported. Only 40.41% of institutions indicated having a structured PMSC education plan. Shortages of faculty with formal psychosomatic training were commonly reported (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Discussion

This national survey provides an overview of the current status of psychosomatic medicine–related training within SRTP in mainland China. Although most respondents reported that psychosomatic medicine receives moderate to high institutional attention, our findings suggest a significant “perception-practice gap”: institutional recognition has not yet been translated into structured, competency-oriented curriculum design.

From a CBME perspective, a key implication of our findings is that current training may not sufficiently prepare GP residents to manage mental health conditions presenting with somatic complaints. In primary care, the ability to recognize serious mental illnesses, conduct initial risk assessments, and coordinate timely referrals is essential for patient safety. The observed training gaps in both SMI and common functional somatic symptoms are concerning, as they suggest that residents may lack the foundational “triaging” skills necessary for the primary care mental health burden in China [26, 27]. Without these, the risk of “over-medicalization” of functional symptoms and failure to identify psychiatric emergencies remains high [28, 29].

The predominance of informal, practice-based exposure observed in this study suggests that current training systems may rely heavily on opportunistic learning rather than structured competency development. This reliance is likely shaped by structural limitations in curriculum design and faculty capacity, as only a minority of institutions reported formal PMSC education plans. From a workplace learning perspective, the absence of structured supervision and feedback mechanisms may hinder the systematic acquisition of biopsychosocial competencies.

In contrast, countries like Germany and the United States have systematically embedded these principles into primary care training through formal mandates. For instance, all German physicians must complete at least 40 to 80 h of basic psychosomatic care training, including theoretical instruction, Balint groups, and case documentation [30]. Similarly, the U.S. family medicine model combines formal seminars and MDT discussions with daily patient encounters to ensure both breadth and depth of competency development [31, 32]. Without similar explicit frameworks and formative assessments, China’s reliance on informal clinical exposure may not translate into consistent competency acquisition—a core tenet of CBME [33].

The limited availability of structured PMSC training may pose potential clinical risks for patients. Without these competencies, GPs may overlook the psychosocial origins of physical complaints, leading to redundant diagnostic procedures and ‘over-medicalization’ of functional somatic symptoms [28, 34]. More critically, an inability to recognize underlying mental distress can result in the failure to manage psychiatric emergencies or identify self-harm risks, potentially posing risks to patient safety [29]. Furthermore, physicians who lack psychosomatic training often hold higher levels of stigma, which has been shown to reduce patient treatment adherence and significantly lower the overall quality of care [35, 36].

The barriers identified—non-standardized curricula, limited qualified faculty, and low institutional awareness—may reflect broader structural challenges in general practice training systems, consistent with previous research [24, 37]. Few studies have specifically examined how these systemic barriers manifest within the domain of psychosomatic education. By focusing on this underexplored area, our study is among the first to systematically investigate how these limitations constrain the development of PMSC in general practice training. This targeted perspective helps bridge a critical knowledge gap and underscores the centrality of psychosomatic care to holistic, community-based primary health services.

The strong consensus among respondents regarding the importance of psychosomatic medicine indicates a favorable institutional climate for future curriculum development. This shared recognition suggests that capacity-building efforts may be feasible even in the presence of existing structural and resource constraints. In this context, exploratory initiatives—such as psychosomatic training modules or optional postgraduate tracks (e.g., MSc-level programs) aligned with CBME principles—may represent feasible pathways for capacity building, provided they are accompanied by rigorous evaluation and do not replace core residency training requirements [38, 39].

Moving forward, potential strategies should include the development of national training modules aligned with standardized PMSC, embedding psychosomatic content into licensure and board certification assessments, and expanding the use of case-based learning and simulated encounters in both pre-service and in-service education [40]. From a CBME standpoint, these strategies emphasize the alignment of competencies with assessment and certification, ensuring that training outcomes are measurable and accountable. Faculty development is equally essential, especially in cultivating interdisciplinary teaching teams that include psychiatrists, psychologists, and experienced general practitioners [41]. Furthermore, expanding opportunities for trainees to engage in community-based psychosomatic medicine services such as primary mental health clinics or collaborative care programs would provide authentic clinical exposure and reinforce biopsychosocial thinking [42, 43].

To address these gaps, our team has proposed a competency-based PMSC training framework specifically for GP residents in China. This curriculum integrates three core domains: (1) Clinical Knowledge, focusing on functional somatic symptoms; (2) Psychosocial Skills, using Role-Playing and Standardized Patients to enhance communication; and (3) Integrated Management, emphasizing brief psychological interventions. While structural challenges remain within the current Chinese medical education system regarding specialized degree categories, future exploration into the establishment of an MSc in Psychosomatic Medicine for GPs merits consideration. As a potential long-term direction, such a program could help professionalize the field and support the development of lead clinicians capable of implementing integrated care models in primary health settings.

This study has several strengths, including wide geographic coverage and the inclusion of diverse roles within teaching bases. The inclusion of both directors and coordinators also provided a broader institutional perspective. However, limitations should be noted. First, the study relied on self-reported institutional data provided by a single informant from each training base, which may be subject to reporting bias and may not fully capture intra-institutional variation. Second, this study focused on program directors and core faculty rather than residents. While trainers provide a comprehensive overview of curriculum design and institutional resources, trainees’ subjective experiences may differ, which warrants future investigation. Third, the convenience sampling strategy limits generalizability, particularly to under-resourced or rural training settings.

Future research should consider mixed-methods approaches that incorporate perspectives from residents, faculty, and patients, and examine not only content coverage but also pedagogical quality, supervision models, and learning outcomes. Comparative case studies of training bases with stronger psychosomatic integration may also offer useful lessons for national reform.

Conclusion

From a CBME perspective, PMSC training within China’s General Practice SRTP exhibits areas for further development and systematic implementation. Although psychosomatic care is widely recognized as important, current training tends to emphasize general professional attributes rather than clearly defined, observable psychosomatic competencies. Strengthening PMSC training may be facilitated by explicit competency frameworks, structured workplace-based learning, and aligned assessment strategies, supported by enhanced faculty capacity and institutional commitment. Embedding psychosomatic education within a CBME framework may help support future general practitioners.
