CAF-derived PAI-1 serves as an EGFR ligand and fuels KRAS-mutant pancreatic cancer
Authors: Yu Wang, Chao Liu, Song Gao, Bo Wang, Hongwei Wang, Zijian Shi, Shunjin Xia, Jiayong Li, Xiaofeng Yao, Xiaofan Guo, Jianwei Zheng, Qianqian Zhou, Zhiqiang Chen, Lei Zheng, Qiang Zhang, Jiang Du, Tianxing Zhou, Zhaoyu Zhang, Weijie Song, Lisha Qi, Yalei Wang, Yuh-Pyng Sher, Min Li, Xuan Zhou, Mien-Chie Hung, Yu Ren, Jihui Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) activation contributes to pancreatic cancer etiology, yet anti-EGFR therapy offers minimal clinical benefits in patients with KRAS mutations. We report that plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) derived from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) is selectively elevated in KRAS-mutant tumors and functions as a ligand of EGFR, contributing to resistance to anti-EGFR therapy. Ablation of stromal PAI-1 breaks the CAF-tumor interaction, curbing tumor growth and enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of anti-EGFR therapy in both genetically engineered mouse models and patient-derived xenografts. Mechanistically, KRAS mutation in PDAC activates c-Myc to release IL-1α from PDAC cells to stimulate NF-κB signaling in CAFs to turn on the transcription of PAI-1. PDAC patients with KRAS mutations have higher plasma PAI-1 levels than those with wild-type KRAS. Our findings reveal a mechanism through which CAFs and tumor cells are regulated by the interaction between PAI-1 secreted by CAFs and EGFR on tumor cells. Importantly, the newly identified ligand-receptor interaction of PAI-1-EGFR in the tumor microenvironment of pancreatic cancer may open a new avenue for understanding receptor biology.Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Cancer therapeutic resistance

Introduction

For more than four decades, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) has served as the prototypical receptor-tyrosine-kinase (RTK) model1,2, and insights from this pathway have driven multiple FDA-approved therapies that prolong survival in several malignancies3,4. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a conspicuous exception, for which EGFR-targeted therapies have shown minimal clinical benefit5–7. A major reason is EGFR’s extensive ligand repertoire, EGF, TGF-α, amphiregulin and at least eight others, are secreted by tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immune infiltrates, and endothelial cells8–11. These ligands forge a niche-specific, highly redundant signaling web that can rapidly reengage EGFR when any single node is blocked. Compounding this problem, more than 90% of PDACs harbor oncogenic KRAS mutations12, and the dense fibroinflammatory stroma of these tumors functions as a persistent “ligand reservoir”. Together, these features allow KRAS-mutant PDAC to bypass single-agent EGFR blockade, highlighting the biological imperative to dismantle microenvironment-specific ligand-receptor circuits rather than targeting the receptor in isolation.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays important roles in promoting an aggressive phenotype and therapeutic resistance13. PDAC has a unique microenvironment characterized by an abundance of CAFs, known as fibrotic desmoplasia14,15. A direct relationship between tumor burden and CAF frequency has been demonstrated in patients affected by different tumors16–18, including PDAC19. Emerging studies have supported the role of CAFs in promoting immune evasion20, chemotherapy resistance21,22, and metastatic potential23,24 through the secretion of cytokines, growth factors, and metabolites. However, the function and underlying mechanism of the crosstalk between tumor cells and CAFs in PDAC with KRAS mutation remain elusive.

The serpin family is traditionally referred to as the serine protease inhibitor superfamily, but emerging evidence supports its paradoxical role in cancer progression25–27. SerpinB5 demonstrates either protumor or anti-tumor activity in pancreatic cancer28,29. SerpinE1, also known as PAI-1, promotes immunosuppression and CAF expansion in PDAC30. However, tumor cell-derived PAI-1 was reported to trigger autophagic degradation of the KRAS protein and restrict PDAC progression31. Its dual function depends on subcellular localization and epigenetic modifications. Whether and how the crosstalk between stromal and tumor cells impacts the divergent behaviors of serpin family members remain unknown.

Here, we show that PAI-1 functions as a ligand for EGFR to promote tumor progression in KRASmt PDAC. Oncogenic KRAS mutation increases the PAI-1 level through the c-Myc/IL-1α/NF-κB pathway. The absence of PAI-1 disrupts CAF-tumor interactions, increasing the susceptibility of PDAC to monoclonal antibodies against EGFR (EGFR mAbs). Targeting PAI-1 in conjunction with anti-EGFR agents represents a transformative opportunity to improve outcomes for patients with KRASmt PDAC, offering renewed hope against this challenging disease.

Results

PAI-1+ fibroblasts are upregulated in PDAC with KRAS mutation

To identify key mediators through which KRAS mutation drives PDAC progression, we performed proteomic analyses on KRASmt and KRASwt tumors from the Cancer Proteome Atlas (TCPA) PDAC cohort. We further analyzed proteins upregulated with increasing KRAS transcription levels in PDAC patients with KRAS mutations. Notably, intersectional analysis revealed that PAI-1 was the top candidate (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Higher plasma and stromal levels of PAI-1 were detected in KRASmt PDAC patients than in their KRASwt counterparts (Fig. 1b, c). We further analyzed murine PAI-1 expression patterns using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (GSE26988832 + GSE14062833 + GSE17984634) (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis revealed that PAI-1 levels increased in fibroblasts when KRAS mutation was activated, whereas no alteration was detected in other cellular subpopulations (Fig. 1d). We also reported a similar pattern of PAI-1 distribution in one external human PDAC cohort (CRA00116035) (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining revealed the colocalization of PAI-1, fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and PDGFRα in KRASmt PDAC, whereas PAI-1 was distributed in tumor cells from KRASwt tumors (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1e). We further validated these findings using RNA-scope in situ hybridization (Fig. 1g). These findings indicate that CAFs are likely responsible for elevated PAI-1 levels, which are dependent on KRAS mutation.

To assess clinical relevance, we found that the number of PAI-1-high (PAI-1H) CAFs (47.7% of KRASmt PDAC cases) correlated with increased tumor volume, more lymph node metastasis, advanced AJCC stage (Supplementary Table 1) and poor prognosis (Fig. 1h), in the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (TCIH) cohort. We also reported shorter overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in PDAC patients with higher PAI-1 levels than in those with lower PAI-1 levels, according to TCGA data (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Thus, these findings indicate that PAI-1 is mainly secreted by CAFs in KRASmt PDAC and is correlated with tumor progression.

PAI-1 in CAFs promotes tumor progression in PDAC

To elucidate the functions of PAI-1, we isolated human CAFs and then genetically engineered them to overexpress PAI-1 (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). We found that several pathways related with tumor progression were upregulated in PANC-1 co-cultured with the PAI-1 overexpressing CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 2e), suggesting an oncogenic role of PAI-1. To decipher the impact of CAF-derived PAI-1, tamoxifen‑inducible Acta2CreERT2/+; Serpine1flox/flox (CreER;Serpine1f/f) mice were generated to selectively delete exons 3–5 of Serpine1 (PAI-1) in CAFs prior to orthotopic implantation of syngeneic KPC cells (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2f). Flow-sorted CAFs from tumors displayed markedly reduced PAI-1 levels (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2g). Genetic fibroblast specific Serpine1 deletion resulted in significantly smaller tumors compared with CreER controls, underscoring the protumorigenic function of CAF-derived PAI-1 in vivo (Fig. 2d, e). The CreER; Serpine1f/f mice showed a strong reduction of Ki67+ proliferating tumor cells compared to that CreER mice (Fig. 2f). Additionally, we also found overexpressing PAI-1 in CAFs increased tumor growth of mice bearing subcutaneous xenograft tumors derived from PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2g, h).

Ablation of PAI-1 using neutralizing antibody (αPAI-1) in CAFs significantly impaired cell migration ability and increased the percentage of apoptosis in PANC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i). Co-culture of KRASmt patient-derived organoids (PDOs) with PAI-1 inhibitor (PAI-1i) pretreated CAFs triggered Caspase 3/7-mediated apoptosis (Fig. 2i). Testing of therapeutic efficacy in tumor-bearing KPC (LSL-KRASG12D/+;LSL-Trp53R172H/+;Pdx1-Cre) genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) revealed that compared with vehicle control treatment, PAI-1i induced significant tumor regression (Fig. 2j, k and Supplementary Fig. 2j, k). PAI-1i induced an approximately 50% tumor reduction in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Fig. 2l). Thus, these data indicate that stromal PAI-1 is necessary to promote tumor growth and the malignant phenotype of PDAC in human and mouse models.

PAI-1 triggers EGFR signaling activation

To elucidate how CAF-derived PAI-1 mediates the interaction between CAFs and tumor cells, we employed RNA-seq for gene profile analysis. The results revealed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) predominantly associated with EGFR activation pathways (Fig. 3a). Notably, flow-sorted tumor cells from CreER;Serpine1f/f mice displayed markedly reduced EGFR phosphorylation level, compared to the CreER controls (Fig. 3b, c).

Indeed, PAI-1i treatment led to a marked reduction in stromal PAI-1 expression and EGFR phosphorylation levels in both KPC GEMMs and PDX models (Fig. 3d, e). Treatment with recombinant protein PAI-1 (rPAI-1) increased phosphorylation of EGFR, AKT, and ERK in PANC-1 and KPC cells, demonstrating that PAI-1 is sufficient to activate EGFR signaling (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 3a). We generated KRAS mutant BxPC-3 cells (BxPC-3Kmt) as previously described31. Likewise, rPAI-1 activated EGFR signaling in these engineered KRAS-mutant PDAC cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Co-culturing CAFs with tumor cells activated EGFR signaling, which was inhibited upon PAI-1 neutralization, indicating that CAFs activated EGFR in tumor cells via PAI-1 secretion (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 3c). Abundant numbers of PAI-1+/phosphorylated EGFR+ PDOs were detected when co-cultured with PAI-1-overexpressing CAFs, which could be disrupted by αPAI-1 (Fig. 3h). Next, we investigated whether the ability of stromal PAI-1 to activate EGFR signaling is dependent on its kinase activity in vitro. Treatment with rPAI-1 increased EGFR phosphorylation at Tyr1068 in the EGFR wild-type cells but not in the EGFR kinase-dead mutant (K721M, EGFR KD) cells (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). Moreover, rPAI-1 induced the dimerization of HA-tagged and Myc-tagged EGFR, a requirement for EGFR activation (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Fig. 3g). These results indicate that CAF-derived PAI-1 induces EGFR activation to support tumor progression.

PAI-1 binds to EGFR and acts as a ligand

Encouraged by these findings, we sought to explore how stromal PAI-1 activates EGFR signaling. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) spectral counts indicated that EGFR is a potential binding protein of PAI-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays demonstrated that PAI-1 binds to EGFR in both PANC-1 and KPC cells (Fig. 4a, b). This interaction was verified using a GST pull-down assay (Fig. 4c). The mIHC staining demonstrated the colocalization of PAI-1 and EGFR on the cytomembrane of Pan-CK+ cells in PDAC patients with KRAS mutation (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Additionally, a proximity ligation assay (PLA) revealed extensive interactions between PAI-1 and EGFR across human and murine PDAC cell lines co-cultured with CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). PAI-1 primarily exerts its biological effects through a conserved serpin domain. We conducted Co-IP experiments to confirm that Serpin domain of PAI-1 is responsible for the interaction of EGFR (Supplementary Fig. 4f).

We further confirmed that PAI-1 directly interacts with the extracellular domain (ECD) of EGFR (Fig. 4d). EGFR-ECD comprises four functional subdomains, of which domains I and III mainly mediate oncogenic signal transduction (Fig. 4e). To determine whether these domains are essential for PAI-1 binding, we constructed four segmented EGFR ECD subdomains. Notably, Co-IP revealed that PAI-1 predominantly binds to D1 and D3, suggesting that PAI-1 may mimic the ability of EGF to activate EGFR (Fig. 4f). Following computational docking simulations, the top-ranked bound conformation for the EGFR-ECD-PAI-1 complex was selected, and 8 amino acids on the binding surface of both proteins were predicted (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4g). Co-IP assays indicated that the T165 residue of PAI-1 and the K396 residue of EGFR-ECD are critical residues within the contact surface, as T165A and K396A mutants lost their binding (Fig. 4h, i). To characterize this interaction quantitatively, we measured the binding kinetics between EGFR-ECD and PAI-1 using bio-layer interferometry (BLI). The results revealed a strong binding affinity between EGFR-ECD and PAI-1, with a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 21.92 nM, comparable to that of EGF (~7.73 nM) and markedly lower than that of amphiregulin (AREG, ~139.00 nM) (Fig. 4j–l). More importantly, BLI assay also demonstrated that PAI-1 was able to compete with and displace AREG pre-bound to EGFR-ECD in vitro (Fig. 4m). Besides, western blot confirmed that PAI-1 induced EGFR activation through direct binding to the ECD of EGFR, rather than via indirect transactivation routes. Blockade of LRP1, as well as inhibition of metalloproteases or the Src/ADAM9 axis, did not attenuate PAI-1-mediated phosphorylation of EGFR, indicating that these established indirect mechanisms are not involved (Supplementary Fig. 5a–f). These findings indicate a strong binding affinity between PAI-1 and EGFR-ECD, presenting PAI-1 within the high-affinity range typical of EGFR ligands.

High levels of stromal PAI-1 confer resistance to anti-EGFR therapy

Given the established function of PAI-1, we next examined whether PAI-1 regulates the response to anti-EGFR therapy in KRASmt PDAC. Notably, cetuximab (Cetux), a widely used monoclonal antibody against EGFR during cancer therapy, specifically targets domain 3 of the ECD of EGFR36. We next investigated whether stromal PAI-1 influences the response to Cetux in PDAC with KRAS mutation. Five PDX models derived from KRASmt or KRASwt PDAC patients were selected for further experiments (Fig. 5a). Our data revealed that KRASmt PDXs, with high PAI-1 expression levels, displayed significant resistance to Cetux-induced tumor regression compared to KRASwt models (Fig. 5b–f). Furthermore, we established animal models via orthotopic co-transplantation of PANC-1 cells with CAFs. Notably, PAI-1-overexpressing CAFs led to a minimal response to Cetux in KRASmt PDAC (Fig. 5g, h). Moreover, a higher ratio of CAF and tumor cell was responsible for the Cetux therapeutic failure in the co-transplantation system (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

To address whether stromal PAI-1 competes with Cetux for binding to EGFR, we conducted a PLA and found that rPAI-1 treatment significantly reduced PLA signals (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed decreased colocalization of FITC-tagged Cetux with EGFR in PANC-1 cells treated with rPAI-1 (Supplementary Fig. 6d). We next examined whether neutralizing PAI-1 sensitizes PDAC to Cetux. The KRASmt PDAC-derived PDOs were co-cultured with CAFs, and displayed a weak response to Cetux and a reduction in Caspase 3/7. However, αPAI-1 enhanced Cetux-induced apoptosis in these organoids, as indicated by increased Caspase 3/7 levels (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Furthermore, αPAI-1 treatment significantly increased the effectiveness of Cetux in both subcutaneous (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g) and orthotopic models (Fig. 5i, j and Supplementary Fig. 6h) generated by co-transplanting PDAC cells with CAFs. These findings indicate that neutralizing stromal PAI-1 effectively sensitizes PDAC with KRAS mutation to Cetux therapy.

PAI-1 inhibition enables anti-tumor effect of Cetux in KRASmt PDAC

To validate the critical role of stromal PAI-1 in regulating the response to anti-EGFR therapy, we constructed in vitro and in vivo models. As expected, KRASmt PDAC cells co-cultured with CAFs did not respond to Cetux alone, whereas the combination treatment with PAI-1i resulted in a synergistic therapeutic effect. In PDAC cells harboring wild-type KRAS, Cetux alone markedly inhibited cell growth in vitro (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Similarly, in PDAC cells bearing mutant KRAS, PAI-1i in combination with Cetux inhibited cell clone formation capacity (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Moreover, this comprehensive treatment significantly increased caspase-dependent apoptosis in PDOs with KRAS mutations when they were co-cultured with CAFs (Fig. 6b).

In the orthotopic model, Cetux treatment failed to inhibit tumor local growth. When combined with PAI-1i, the efficacy improved significantly, resulting in tumor regression and an extended survival time (Fig. 6c–e). Additionally, PDXs with KRAS mutations showed minimal response to Cetux. Moreover, the combination of Cetux and PAI-1i led to dramatic tumor regression and decreased Ki-67 expression in PDX tumors (Fig. 6f–h and Supplementary Fig. 7g). We also included CreER; Serpine1f/f mice to examine the combination therapy efficacy. Cetux treatment led to pronounced tumor regression and a significant reduction in Ki-67 index in CreER; Serpine1f/f, whereas CreER controls remained resistant to Cetux (Fig. 6i–l). These in vivo data demonstrate that loss of stromal PAI-1 sensitizes PDAC to Cetux therapy, providing evidence that CAF‑derived PAI‑1 is a key stromal determinant of drug resistance.

KRAS mutation induces CAF-derived PAI-1 via c-Myc/IL-1α/NF-κB signaling

We next explored the underlying mechanism of KRAS mutation-dependent upregulation of stromal PAI-1 expression. The protein, secretion and mRNA levels of PAI-1 were greater in CAFs co-cultured with BxPC-3Kmt cells than in KRASwt cells (Fig. 7a–c). Transcriptome analysis of data from scRNA-seq and various transcription factor (TF) databases revealed that NF-κB is a key upregulated TF in KRASmt PDAC (Supplementary Fig. 8a). NF-κB activation was found enriched in PDAC with high PAI-1 expression according to TCGA (Supplementary Fig. 8b), as confirmed by elevated p65-Ser536 phosphorylation in CAFs co-cultured with BxPC-3Kmt cells (Fig. 7d). NF-κB-overexpressing CAFs showed a significant upregulation of PAI-1 protein, secretion, and mRNA expression (Fig. 7e–g). NF-κB was further confirmed to bind to the PAI-1 promoter in CAFs using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay (Fig. 7h).

Considering that KRAS mutation induces malignant transition mainly via the ERK and AKT pathways37, we found that an ERK inhibitor (ERKi), but not an AKT inhibitor (AKTi), suppressed PAI-1, indicating the ERK is crucial for KRAS mutation-mediated PAI-1 upregulation in CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 8c). To explore how KRAS mutation promotes PAI-1 abundance, we conducted a proteomics analysis using PDAC tissues with and without KRAS mutation. Our meta-analysis revealed significant enrichment of “MYC target V1” and “MYC target V2” gene signatures (Fig. 7i). Engineered tumor cells also showed robust MYC activation with KRAS mutations (Supplementary Fig. 8d). The TCGA database revealed the upregulation of IL-1A in KRASmt PDAC tumors (Fig. 7j). Given that c-Myc transactivates IL-1A38, we next hypothesized that KRAS mutation augments stromal PAI-1 via the c-Myc/IL-1α regulatory network. In BxPC-3Kmt cells, we observed increased c-Myc phosphorylation at S62, along with elevated IL-1A mRNA, protein, and secretion levels (Fig. 7k and Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). This finding was corroborated by similar observations in KRASmt PDAC cells overexpressing c-Myc (Fig. 7l-n and Supplementary Fig. 8g, h). Silencing c-Myc expression significantly decreased the IL-1α level (Supplementary Fig. 8i-l). Notably, c-Myc inhibition compromised the upregulation of PAI-1 in CAFs induced by BxPC-3Kmt co-culturing (Supplementary Fig. 8m). Furthermore, we treated CAFs with recombinant human IL-1α (rIL-1α) and detected increased mRNA, protein and secretion levels of PAI-1 (Fig. 7o–q). Treatment with an anti-IL-1α neutralizing antibody (αIL-1α) significantly reduced PAI-1 and NF-κB phosphorylation levels in CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 8n–q). Loss‑of‑function experiments were performed to delineate the essential role of the c‑Myc/IL‑1α axis in the paracrine induction of PAI‑1 by KRAS (Fig. 7r and Supplementary Fig. 8r). The tumor weight was significantly reduced in mice treated with IL-1α neutralizing antibody (αIL-1α) (Fig. 7s, t). To dissect the mechanism within the TME, we isolated CAFs and tumor cells from the tumor tissue of mice using flow cytometry analysis. Treatment with αIL-1α induced a markedly reduction in PAI-1 expression and p-p65 activity in CAFs along with diminished EGFR phosphorylation in tumor cells (Fig. 7u). Therefore, these findings indicate that IL-1α/NF-κB signaling plays a critical role in KRAS mutation-induced PAI-1 expression in CAF.

PAI-1 expression is correlated with EGFR activation in KRASmt PDAC

To further elucidate the clinical relevance of the PAI-1/EGFR axis in PDAC, we analyzed the expression profile and correlation between PAI-1 and p-EGFR in 172 KRASmt PDAC patients in the TCIH cohort. In PAI-1-overexpressing PDAC specimens, we observed a robust expression of EGFR phosphorylation at Tyr1068 (Fig. 8a). Prognostic analysis revealed that elevated EGFR Tyr1068 phosphorylation was associated with shorter OS and PFS, and higher activation levels of EGFR and PAI-1 were linked to worse survival outcomes, as reflected by stronger hazard ratios (Fig. 8b). To further strengthen this observation, multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that high stromal PAI-1 represents a potential adverse prognostic factor for PDAC (Fig. 8c and Supplementary Table 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that stromal-derived PAI-1, induced by epithelial KRAS mutations, functions as a critical ligand for EGFR activation, promoting tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Clinically, targeting the PAI-1/EGFR axis may therefore offer a promising strategy to improve outcomes for patients with PDAC.

Discussion

Oncogenic KRAS mutation is a hallmark of PDAC, driving its aggressive behavior and posing challenges for effective clinical intervention12,39–42. Unfortunately, treatment options for this aggressive cancer remain limited43. Our research revealed that PAI-1, which is derived from CAFs and intricately upregulated by mutant KRAS, serves as a key mediator of cancer progression and is a compelling therapeutic target.

Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of PAI-1 in remodeling the TME of PDAC, inducing fibrosis, immunosuppression30, and EMT44. Our findings revealed that the mutant KRAS in PDAC cells increases PAI-1 expression in CAFs through the c-Myc/IL-1α/NF-κB pathway, promoting EGFR activation and cancer progression (Fig. 8d). In contrast, previous studies demonstrated that in epithelial cells, PAI-1 is transcriptionally repressed by ADAM9, facilitating resistance to gemcitabine by reducing KRAS degradation in PDAC31. Our findings elucidate the dualistic role of PAI-1 in PDAC and add complexity to its functional characterization. Therefore, our findings solve the puzzle of the paradoxical function of PAI-1 in PDAC. In this context, we hypothesize that the function of PAI-1 and its clinical relevance are contingent upon the cell type of the tumor.

Our study demonstrated that PAI-1 and EGFR are ligand-receptor pairs between the serpin family and receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). These results provide a mechanistic insight into the interplay between PAI-1 and EGFR in the remodeling of the PDAC microenvironment. In particular, SerpinE2 serves as a pertinent example. It enhances tumor metastasis by promoting the formation of pseudopodia and activating CAFs. This occurs via the interaction of SerpinE2 with the intracellular domain (ICD) of EGFR, which inhibits its ubiquitination and thus enhances its stability by competing with the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl45. These findings suggest that serpins may play non-canonical roles as ligands for RTKs, warranting further investigation into the ligand-like functions of other serpins.

EGFR is highly expressed in PDAC, yet therapeutic attempts to exploit this vulnerability have yielded limited success in KRASmt tumors46–50. Monoclonal antibodies against EGFR have become a frontline treatment for KRASwt PDAC51,52, but have shown little to no effectiveness in cases of KRAS mutation53–55. This disparity highlights the critical need to explore how KRAS mutations may inhibit or undermine the efficacy of these antibodies. Moreover, compared with those in patients with wild-type KRAS, PAI-1 plasma levels are increased in patients with mutated KRAS. This highlights the potential for patient stratification based on the use of CAF-derived PAI-1, a non-invasive biomarker. Furthermore, ablation of PAI-1 enhanced the sensitivity to Cetux in a PDX model of KRASmt PDAC, providing a promising strategy to combat KRASmt-driven cancer. While we provide evidence that CAF-derived PAI-1 activates EGFR, the exact molecular basis of their interaction is not yet fully resolved. Alternative mechanisms, such as indirect or transactivation through parallel signaling pathways cannot be entirely excluded, highlighting the need for future studies to explore this interaction in detail.

In summary, our findings establish PAI-1 as a critical modulator within the TME of PDAC, with CAFs serving as the primary source of this EGFR ligand in response to KRAS mutation. The dual roles of PAI-1 in promoting EGFR activation and facilitating tumor-stroma crosstalk underscore its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. Targeting PAI-1 in combination with anti-EGFR mAbs holds significant promise for overcoming resistance and improving outcomes in KRASmt PDAC. These findings offer a promising approach for treating KRAS-driven tumors, highlight a potential plasma biomarker for patient selection, and provide a new paradigm for understanding the biology of EGFR and its ligands in a specific environment, such as the TME of pancreatic cancer.

Methods

Statement about ethics

All human PDAC tissue specimens used in this study were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection, with informed written consent. All protocols related to patient specimen collection were approved by the Ethics Committee of TCIH (EK2020155, EK20240133) and in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. There was no bias in the selection of patients. All patients had been diagnosed independently by two pathologists. Written informed consents were obtained from all patients before collection of tissues.

All animal experiments were conducted following guidelines for the ethical treatment of laboratory animals after approval was obtained from the TCIH (NSFC-AE-2020161, NSFC-AE-2024242). The potential influence of sex on experimental outcomes was not evaluated, and mice of both sexes were used in this study. All mice were monitored every 3–5 days and euthanized when tumors reached the maximum volume of 2000 mm3, or earlier if clinical deterioration was observed, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines.

Cell lines

The human PDAC cell lines PANC-1, BxPC-3, and AsPC-1 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). KPC cells were kindly provided by Prof. Liang (Department of Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery, The First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China). For cell culture, RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 11875) and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, 11965118) medium were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Excell Bio, FSP500) and 1% antibiotics. Rigorous examination procedures were performed to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination in all the cultured cells.

Human and murine CAFs isolation and validation

Human CAFs and matched normal fibroblasts (NFs) were obtained from freshly resected PDAC specimens and adjacent normal pancreatic tissue as previously described56, under approved ethics protocols. Samples were transported on ice in DMEM + 10% FBS, minced into 2–3 mm³ fragments, and digested with collagenase type I at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by filtration through a 70 µm cell strainer (Corning, 352350) and centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in DMEM + 10% FBS, plated into 10 cm dishes, and incubated for 30 min to allow fibroblast attachment. Then, medium was replaced to remove non-adherent epithelial and immune cells. To ensure that the cells were free from cancer cell contamination, all human CAFs were expanded and passaged at least 10 times. Then fibroblast identity was validated at the protein level by flow cytometric detection of PDPN as positive marker, and EpCAM, CD31, and CD45 as negative markers. All CAFs underwent western blotting for Pan-CK (Cell Signaling Technology, 4545T) with tumor cells as positive controls.

Murine CAFs were isolated from KPC GEMM with confirmed PDAC, as previously described56. In detail, fresh tumors were transported in ice-cold DMEM supplemented with 1% FBS. The peripheral region of the tumor mass was dissected, minced into 2–3 mm³ fragments, and digested in DMEM containing 1% FBS, 0.125 mg/mL collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich, C0130), and 0.125 mg/mL dispase II (Life Technologies, 17105-041) at 37 °C for 30 min. The digested tissue was treated with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25300-062) for 10 min at 37 °C, quenched with DMEM containing 10% FBS, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the pellet containing residual tumor pieces plated into 10 cm dishes (Corning, 430166) for fibroblast outgrowth. To ensure that the cells were free from cancer cell contamination, murine CAF were expanded and passaged at least 10 times. Then fibroblast identity was validated at the protein level by flow cytometric detection of PDPN as positive marker, and EpCAM, CD31, CD45 as negative markers. Besides, the isolated murine CAFs were also confirmed to be CAFs by their spindle-shaped morphology and αSMA-/FAP-positive immunofluorescence staining57.

Murine CAFSerpine1−/− were isolated from orthotopic KPC (GFP-labeled) tumors generated in CreER;Serpine1f/f GEMM following tamoxifen induction. Fresh tumors were minced into 2–3 mm³ fragments and digested in DMEM containing 10% FBS, collagenase D (2.5 mg/mL; Roche, 11088882001), Liberase TM (0.5 mg/mL; Roche, 05401119001), and DNase I (0.2 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, DN25) for 30 min at 37 °C in a rotating shaker (200 rpm). The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm strainer (Corning, 352340), RBC-lysed with ACK buffer (Gibco, A1049201) for 4 min on ice, neutralized with PBS + 5% FBS, stained with Live/Dead (BioLegend, 423101, 1:1000), CD45-APC/CY7 (BioLegend, 103116, 1:100), CD31-APC (BioLegend, 102509, 1:100) and PDPN-PE (BioLegend, 127407, 1:100). Sorting was performed on a BD FACSAriaTM llu, gating for PDPN+CD31⁻GFP⁻CD45⁻ populations. The isolated CAFs were cultured in the Fibroblast Medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, #2301) with 2% FBS and 1% growth factor in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Human PDAC organoids

Human PDOs were established as previously described in laboratory of Prof. David Tuveson58. Briefly, fresh PDAC specimens were cut into small pieces (<10 mm³) and washed three times with cold PBS containing 10% penicillin/streptomycin. Tissues were then digested for 30 min at 37 °C with vigorous agitation in digestion buffer composed of 10% penicillin/streptomycin, collagenase type II, collagenase type IV, type XI collagenase and Y-27632. Following digestion, tumor pellets were washed in wash buffer containing HEPES, 10% penicillin/streptomycin, advanced DMEM/F12 and 1×GlutaMAX, then collected by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min. Tumor cells were embedded in Matrigel and seeded into 96-well plates. After Matrigel polymerization, organoids were cultured in complete medium. Medium was refreshed every 3 days and organoids were passaged approximately every 7 days, depending on growth rate.

Organoid apoptosis assay

Human PDAC organoids were cultured with Organoid Media in GFR Matrigel (Corning, 354234). Primary human CAFs were first pre-treated with PAI-1i, and then indirect co-culturing was performed by adding the treated CAFs on top of Transwell chamber (4 μm) without Matrigel at a ratio of 3:1 and allowing them to incubate for 72 h. To evaluate the effect of αPAI-1 on anti-EGFR efficacy, PDAC organoids were exposed to treatments including Cetux, αPAI-1, or a combination. Organoid apoptosis was visualized after the introducing of a green-fluorescent Caspase 3/7 probe (ThermoFisher, R37111). An Opera Phenix High-Content Imaging System (PerkinElmer) was used to monitor apoptotic events within the organoids.

Experimental animals

The BALB/c nude mice and the C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The KPC mice (LSL-KRASG12D/+; LSL-Trp53R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre, Cat. NO. NM-KI-210096), Serpine1flox mice (Cat. NO. NM-CKO-220176) and Acta2CreERT2 mice (Cat. NO. NM-KI-200074) were all purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc. (Shanghai, China). The NPIdKO mice (NOD-Prkdcem1IDMO-Il2rgem2IDMO) were purchased from Beijing IDMO Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Mice were housed with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. The ambient temperature was maintained at 22 °C, and relative humidity was controlled at 50–60%.

Pancreas cancer allograft mouse models

For human orthotopic model, 5 × 105 human PDAC cells (PANC-1, BxPC3Kwt or BxPC3Kmt) were orthotopically co-transplanted with 1.5 × 106 human CAFs in 50 μL culture medium into the pancreas of BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks). For murine orthotopic model, a total of 7.5 × 104 KPC cells (passage 15 or lower) in 50 μL culture medium were injected into the pancreas of C57BL/6 (6–8 weeks), CreER; Serpine1f/f or CreER mice. For human subcutaneous model, 5 × 105 PANC-1 cells were co-transplanted with 1.5 × 106 human CAFs in 100 μL culture medium subcutaneously into the flank of BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks).

Animal study

Mice were randomly assigned to groups once tumors reached the predefined size threshold, using a computer-generated randomization list to balance baseline tumor burden across groups. Investigators responsible for tumor monitoring and endpoint measurements (volume, weight, survival, and metastasis counts) were blinded to treatment allocation, and drug preparation was performed by a separate investigator to maintain blinding throughout dosing. For anti-EGFR therapy, Cetux was administered 7 days after surgery, via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at 20 mg/kg every 2 days. IgG served as a therapeutic control. For PAI-1 inhibiting treatment, PAI-1i (Tiplaxtinin, PAI-039) was orally administered through gavage at 10 mg/kg every 2 days. For PAI-1 neutralizing treatment, mice were given a tail vein infusion of anti-PAI-1 antibody (αPAI-1, 10 mg/kg) once a week on the first day of treatment. For IL-1α neutralization, anti-mouse IL-1α antibody (αIL-1α) was administered 7 days after surgery, via tail vein injection at 10 mg/kg once a week on the first day of treatment. IgG served as a therapeutic control.

For orthotopic models, the endpoint was reached when any mouse exhibited mortality, severe ascites, or marked body-weight loss, in accordance with animal ethics guidelines. Then, animals were humanely euthanized, and tumors were collected for volume and weight measurement and subsequent analyses. For PDX and subcutaneous models, tumor volume was calculated as: (L × W2)/2 (“L” represents the larger diameter, “W” denotes the smaller diameter). When subcutaneous tumors reached 100 mm³ in volume, mice were randomly assigned to the indicated groups for subsequent experiments.

PDX models

The fresh tumor tissue was collected from three KRASmt PDAC and two KRASwt patients at TCIH. Then, tumor tissue was sectioned into approximately 3–5 mm in diameter and was implanted subcutaneously into the NPIdKO mice (6–8 weeks, passage-1, P1). When P1 tumors reaching a size range of approximately 300–500 mm3, the mice were sacrificed to obtain tumor fragments for further transplantation into the subcutaneous regions of NPIdKO mice (P2). And tumor tissues from P2 mice were cut and implanted into P3 mice. When PDX tumors in P3 mice reached 100 mm³ in volume, animals were randomly assigned to the indicated groups for subsequent therapeutic experiments.

Acta2CreERT2/+;Serpine1flox/flox mice

Acta2CreERT2 (Cat. NO. NM-KI-200074, Shanghai Model Organisms Center) mice were crossed with Serpine1flox/flox (Cat. NO. NM-CKO-220176, Shanghai Model Organisms Center) mice to generate Acta2CreERT2/+; Serpine1flox/flox (CreER; Serpine1f/f) mice. These mice were aged 8–12 weeks and treated with tamoxifen (120 mg/kg, #T5648, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in corn oil (C8267, Sigma-Aldrich) via intraperitoneal injection every two days for five times. One week after their last injection, mice underwent KPC cells orthotopic tumor implantation surgery.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

In the analysis of human and murine scRNA-seq data, processed count data were obtained from the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) and GEO databases for the following accessions: CRA00116035, GSE26988832, GSE14062833, and GSE17984634.

The matrix was then analyzed using the Seurat (4.3.2) R package59. Low-quality cells were removed based on the basis of specific criteria, including gene count (<200), log10GenesPerUMI (<0.7), mitochondrial gene count ratio (>10%), erythrocyte gene count ratio (>5%), and UMIs (<1000). Subsequently, doublet removal was performed using the DoubletFinder package (v2.0.3)60. Data were logarithmically transformed using the NormalizeData function.

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

To identify genes with distinct expression patterns in the subcluster, we utilized the FindAllMarkers function provided by Seurat. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined based on the basis of the following criteria: log2 fold change (log2FC) > 0.5, and adjusted P value < 0.05. The top DEGs were visualized using the R package pheatmap (v1.0.12). To detect pathways showing divergent enrichment of DEGs, we performed GSEA using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.0.5).

IHC and mIHC staining

Human and murine pancreatic cancer tumor tissues were collected and embedded in paraffin. The slides were then deparaffinized and hydrated through an ethanol gradient. The tissue slides were then steamed for 20 min to retrieve antigen. Slides were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide and 5% bovine serum to quench endogenous peroxidase activity and minimize non-specific antigen binding. Then, the slides were incubated with block solution for 30 min and primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Wash the slides with PBS and then incubated with secondary antibody for 30 min. DAB (ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9019) was employed to achieve color development, while hematoxylin solution (Solarbio, G1080) served as the stain for the nuclei. The immunoreactive score (IRS) was used to grade the expression levels of different markers in tumor cells or in the stroma area.

In mIHC using a tyramide signal amplification kit (TSA) (TOCRIS, 7526), tissue sections are deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval to expose epitopes. After blocking nonspecific binding with 1.5% BSA in PBS, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS rinsing, TSA fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in PBS with 1.5% BSA were applied for 1 h at room temperature, allowing binding to the other primary antibodies. After another rinse, the TSA reagent is introduced to amplify the fluorescent signal. Finally, the sections are visualized and analyzed with CaseViewer 2.4.

Plasmids and RNA interference

Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfection of U6-sgRNA-EF1a-Cas9-FLAG-P2A-puro vector containing each sgRNA, VSVG and PAX8 into HEK293T cells. Approximately 3 × 104 cells were seeded in each well of a 6-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing lentiviral particles, and cells were incubated for 16 h. Following transduction, the medium was refreshed, and puromycin selection (1.5 μg/mL) was performed for 7 days to establish stable cell lines. Knockout efficiency of target genes was validated by immunoblotting. The information of plasmids is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Approximately 1 × 105 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight to a confluence of 70%. Then, cells were transfected with indicated siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Downregulation of target genes was validated by immunoblotting. Information of sgRNA and siRNA sequences is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Western blot and immunoprecipitation assays

To isolate the proteins, the cells were disrupted using a lysis solution (composed of 40 mM Tris-HCl, 120 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitors). After denaturing the proteins, the lysates underwent separation through sodium dodecyl sulfate-(SDS) polyacrylamide gel and transfer onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Merck, IPFL00010). Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at a temperature of 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the Omni-ECL™ Pico Light Chemiluminescence Kit. For immunoprecipitation, protein lysates were incubated overnight at a temperature of 4 °C with antibodies. Subsequently, they were subjected to Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck, B23202) for 2 h prior to electrophoresis. Immunoblotting was performed on immunoprecipitated samples after washing with lysis buffer.

GST pull-down assay

We expressed GST fusion protein in BL21 and extracted protein lysates with lysis buffer. Glutathione-magnetite agarose beads from Millipore were used to purify the protein. The proteins bound to the beads were resolved by immunoblotting to analyze protein interactions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

In this study, the levels of secreted PAI-1 and IL-1α were measured using commercially available PAI-1/IL-1α ELISA kits. Briefly, the samples (patient plasma or cell culture medium) and standards were loaded into 96-well plates and incubated for 90 min at room temperature. Then, biotin-labeled primary antibody was added to react at 37 °C for 1 h. This was followed by incubating Streptavidin-HRP solution for 30 min. Finally, the plates were thoroughly washed before initiating the TMB reaction. The termination solution was then added and optical density readings were obtained using a BioTek Synergy H1 instrument set at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Protein-protein docking

The 4UV7 crystal structure of human EGFR was selected for preparing the ligand file required for molecular docking, while the 7AQF crystal structure of human PAI-1 was chosen to generate the receptor file for molecular docking. The QuickPrep plugin within the MOE software was utilized to perform protonation and structural optimization, following the specific workflow outlined below: A rigid docking strategy was adopted, starting with coarse-grained treatment of the protein structures. The Fast Fourier Transform algorithm was employed to search for potential interaction patterns between the ligand and the receptor. Subsequently, the Induced Fit protocol was used to optimize the conformations of the coarse-grained model and amino acid side chains, with the optimization of amino acid side chains conducted via sampling from a rotamer library. Finally, the entire docking system was structurally optimized through energy minimization, and the Generalized Born/Violation of Impact (GB/VI) solvation model was applied for energy calculation.

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) assays

The binding affinities between human recombinant PAI-1 (R&D systems, 1786-PI-010) and EGFR-ECD (TargetMol, TMPK-00426) were measured by BLI assay using the Octet RH16 System (Sartorius). All proteins were diluted in PBST. In brief, human His-labeled EGFR-ECD protein was immobilized onto Ni-NTA (NTA) biosensors (Sartorius) and balanced with PBST. The biosensors were then exposed to full-length PAI-1, EGF (Abclone, RP01030), or AREG (Abclone, RP00898), followed by washing (dissociation) with PBST. To analyze sequential bindings of PAI-1 and AREG to EGFR-ECD, His-labeled EGFR-ECD was immobilized onto NTA biosensors and balanced with PBST. The loaded biosensors were first exposed to AREG, balanced again with PBST, and then exposed to PAI-1. Binding affinities (Kd) were calculated using the Data Analysis software (Sartorius).

Mass spectrometry

Fresh pancreatic tumor tissues from KRASwt (n = 4) and KRASmt (n = 10) patients were lysed in SDT buffer, reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and precipitated with acetone. Proteins were digested with trypsin. The resulting peptides were analyzed on a Thermo Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer coupled with a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. MS1 scan range was m/z 380-980 at 240,000 resolution, followed by 300 DIA windows (2-Th) with NCE of 25% for MS2 acquisition.

Raw data were searched using DIA-NN software (version 1.8.1) against the UniProtKB human proteome database. To improve the quality of analysis results, the DIA-NN software further filters the search results, retaining only peptides with a Global.Q-value < 0.01 and proteins with a PG.Q.Value < 0.01. Differentially expressed proteins were identified by two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test (|log2FC| > 0.5, P < 0.05). Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape.

Immunofluorescence assay

A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded on a cover glass and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110). The cells were then permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200) and non-specific antigens were prevented using 5% BSA (Solarbio, A8020). The tissue slides or cells were subjected to specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by the application of Alexa Fluor conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, A-11001, A-11012). DAPI (Solarbio, C0065) was used for nuclear staining and fluorescence signals were captured using a Zeiss laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900) as per the standard protocol.

Duolink in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA allows the detection and visualization of protein interactions in cells. We seeded 5 × 104 cells on a cover glass and incubated them for 24 h before fixing them with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100. The cells were incubated overnight at a temperature of 4 °C with specific antibodies, following a blocking procedure. The subsequent day, the cells were sequentially incubated with a probe solution for 1 h and ligation buffer for 2 h at 4 °C. This was followed by additional incubation with amplification buffer in the absence of light for a total of 100 min at the same low temperature. Finally, DAPI staining was used to visualize the cell nuclei, and fluorescence signals were captured using a Zeiss laser confocal microscope (LSM900).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism and R (version 4.4.0). The associations between PAI-1 expression and clinicopathologic parameters were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, unless otherwise indicated. Two-group comparisons were performed using two-sided Student’s t-tests. For group comparisons involving more than two groups, differences were evaluated using ANOVA and post hoc Tukey’s tests. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used for survival studies, and log-rank test was used to compare the groups. Detailed statistical information for each experiment can be found in the figure captions.
