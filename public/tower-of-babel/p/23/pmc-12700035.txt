Relationship between skeletal muscle indicators and clinical outcomes in cystic fibrosis is modified by adiposity and CFTR modulator use: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Lucia A Gonzalez Ramirez, Pichatorn Suppakitjanusant, Tasha M Burley, Swati Zaveri, Arlene A Stecenko, William R Hunt, Ryan A Harris, Virginia A Stallings, Elizabeth A Ivie, Chris Driggers, Jose N Binongo, Thomas R Ziegler, Jessica A Alvarez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMuscle mass predicts clinical outcomes in people with cystic fibrosis (PwCF), but muscle quality remains insufficiently characterized. Furthermore, although CFTR modulators promote weight gain, their impact on the relationship between muscle and clinical outcomes is unknown.ObjectivesTo quantify the relationships between skeletal muscle indicators and clinical outcomes in adults with cystic fibrosis (CF) and determine whether CFTR modulators and/or adiposity influence the relationships.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 75 adults with CF and 73 controls without CF. Body composition [appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), visceral fat, and total body fat] was measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Muscle quality (via phase angle) was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis. Clinical outcomes included physical functionality [hand grip (HG) strength and 1-min sit-to-stand test result], lung function, and an oral glucose tolerance test result. Sarcopenia was defined as having low muscle mass and low HG strength. Statistical analyses included testing for effect modification in the relationships between variables.ResultsFive PwCF had sarcopenia compared with none of the controls. Despite similar BMI, PwCF had lower ASMI [β: −0.46; 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.88, −0.05; P = 0.02], HG strength (β: −3.6; 95% CI: −6.6, −0.61; P = 0.02), and muscle quality (β: −0.57; 95% CI: −0.93, −0.21; P = 0.01) compared with controls. In PwCF, lung function was positively associated with ASMI among people not taking CFTR modulators but not among users (interaction; β: −9.21; 95% CI: −16.87, −1.55; P = 0.01). Muscle quality (r = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.04, 0.59; P = 0.02) and HG strength (r = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.05, 0.60; P = 0.02) were positively related to insulin sensitivity. The positive relationship between ASMI and physical functionality was only significant among PwCF with low adiposity (interaction; β: −1.9; 95% CI: −3.51, −0.35; P = 0.02).ConclusionsMuscle mass, strength, and quality are lower in PwCF than in controls, with muscle strength and quality linked to glucose tolerance. Lung function relationships with muscle mass differ by CFTR modulator use, and excess adiposity attenuates strength-related functional benefits. This study highlights the need for personalized approaches to CF management.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, leading to progressive airway destruction, recurrent infections, and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency that causes malabsorption [1]. Low muscle mass due to protein catabolism, malabsorption, chronic inflammation, and recurrent infections has been associated with muscle dysfunction, diminished exercise performance, decreased pulmonary function, and lower quality of life among people with CF (PwCF) [2].

CF therapy has particularly focused on mitigating life-threatening progressive airway destruction caused by CFTR mutation variants [3]. Over the past decade, CFTR modulator therapy has become available for different arrays of CFTR mutations and age groups. Phase III clinical trials have demonstrated that highly effective CFTR therapy (HEMT) improves lung function, reduces hospitalizations for acute pulmonary exacerbations, increases weight gain, and increases life expectancy. With increased life span, aging-related health concerns in PwCF, including musculoskeletal disorders (i.e., osteoporosis and sarcopenia), diabetes, and obesity, are becoming more urgent [4].

Sarcopenia, formally recognized as a distinct disease entity (M62.84) by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification, is a muscle disorder characterized by muscle depletion and reduced muscle function and is associated with frailty, advanced lung disease, and higher mortality [5]. Research consortia have acknowledged that, although sarcopenia is predominantly associated with older populations, it can also manifest in younger individuals and those with catabolic conditions [6]. Although hidden muscle depletion has been a common finding in PwCF [[7], [8], [9]], sarcopenia has not been extensively investigated.

Before the widespread availability of CFTR modulators, a strong relationship was established between low lean mass and adverse clinical outcomes, particularly poor lung function [7,[10], [11], [12]]. Whether sarcopenia persists and whether lean mass continues to influence clinical outcomes in the CFTR modulator era are yet to be determined [8].

BMI (in kg/m2), although widely used to screen nutritional status, does not capture compartment-specific changes in body composition [2]. More accurate and clinically validated measures, including appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), the ASM index (ASMI), and fat mass indices, are increasingly used to identify muscle depletion and other abnormal body composition phenotypes that may inform clinical decision making [6,10].

Sarcopenia can coexist with obesity; however, their combined effect on body composition and clinical outcomes remains unknown. In non-CF populations, adiposity-related declines in muscle function have been reported [13]. Furthermore, CFTR modulator–related weight gain is attributed to adiposity accretion [14], although compartmental body composition studies related to this phenomenon are scarce [15,16].

Given longer life expectancy, there is a need to further elucidate the role of lean mass and other body composition characteristics with clinical outcomes in adults with CF in the CFTR modulator era.

Objectives

This cross-sectional study aimed to assess muscle mass, muscle strength, muscle quality, and sarcopenia in PwCF compared with healthy adults and examine the interrelationships between skeletal muscle indicators and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that lower skeletal muscle mass, strength, and quality would be associated with worse clinical outcomes in adults with CF but that these relationships would be modified by CFTR modulator use and adiposity.

Methods

Study subjects

This cross-sectional study involved a convenience sample of 76 clinically stable PwCF from 3 cohort studies at Emory University and 75 participants without CF from 1 cohort. The experimental design and study participants’ flowchart is presented in Figure 1. The CF cohorts were the BioEnergetics and Metabolomics in Cystic Fibrosis (BEAM-CF, 2014–2018, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02225899), Role of Body Fat Distribution in Metabolic and Pulmonary Decline in Cystic Fibrosis (ORBIT-CF, 2019 to present, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04002882), and Georgia CF Center Longitudinal Phenotyping (P30-CF, 2021 to present, IRB00107055) studies. Healthy non-CF controls were from the Bioenergetic Characterization of Body Composition Subtypes (BioBods) study, conducted from 2016 to 2018 (IRB00085327). The Emory Institutional Review Board approved the studies, and all participants provided written informed consent at enrollment. Testing was conducted at the Georgia Clinical and Translational Science Alliance (Georgia CTSA) Clinical Research Center site at Emory University Hospital. Inclusion criteria for PwCF included confirmed CF diagnosis, age >18 y, and clinical stability, which is defined as no changes in the medical regimen for ≥21 d prior to the baseline study visit. Exclusion criteria for this group were forced expiratory volume in the first second, expressed as percent predicted (FEV1%), of <25% at the most recent stable clinic visit, use of intravenous or oral antibiotics in the last 3 wk before baseline visit, pregnancy or lactation, use of chronic corticosteroids, history of, or being on the waiting list for, a lung transplant, and chronic oxygen therapy. Participants in the control group were healthy individuals without CF, aged >18 y, and residing in the same geographic region as the participants with CF (Atlanta and the surrounding metropolitan area). Exclusion criteria for the control group included respiratory, autoimmune, endocrine, or other chronic diseases and acute illness and the use of corticosteroids or antibiotics within the 3 wk prior to study entry. Although formal matching by age, sex, or BMI was not performed, efforts were made to recruit a demographically comparable sample. Three individuals were excluded because of their self-reported status as competitive fitness athletes, which could substantially affect body composition and bias group comparisons.FIGURE 1Study subjects’ flowchart. Body composition and clinical data

Height and weight were measured barefoot using a manual stadiometer and digital scale. BMI was categorized on the basis of the WHO classification [17]. Whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (GE Lunar iDXA, GE Healthcare) was used to assess lean mass, visceral adipose tissue (VAT), and total body fat percentage (Fat%). ASM, calculated as the sum of lean mass measurements for both arms and legs, was adjusted for height squared (m2) to obtain the ASMI. Low muscle mass was defined as an ASMI of <7.0 kg/m2 in males and <5.5 kg/m2 in females [6]. VAT was measured using CoreScan DXA software (GE HealthCare Technologies Inc.).

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) was conducted with a multifrequency segmental analyzer (Quadscan 4000, Bodystat). Participants lay supine with limbs extended away from the midline to prevent contact. The BIA was conducted using a tetrapolar method after entering sex, age, height, weight, and activity level information obtained from the participant [18]. Measurement was performed after overnight fasting, 24-h strenuous exercise restriction, and bladder emptying. Phase angle (PhA), an indicator of cell membrane integrity and function (especially skeletal muscle cell quality), was also obtained. PhA was calculated using raw resistance (R) and reactance (Xc) values at 50 kHz current using the following formula: arctangent (Xc/R) × 180°/π [19].

Skeletal muscle strength was measured via handgrip (HG) using a calibrated Jamar (Preston Corporation) or Takei (Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.) handheld dynamometer. Low skeletal muscle strength was defined as HG strength of <27.0 kg in males and <16.0 kg in females [6]. Sarcopenia was assessed using the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2) operational definition [6], using muscle strength via HG strength and muscle quantity by ASMI.

Clinical data, including genotype, mutation severity, CFTR modulator use, CFTR modulator type, neonatal screening diagnosis, history of meconium ileus, cirrhosis diagnosis, prior diagnosis of CF-related diabetes (CFRD), and insulin use, were obtained from electronic health records. Mutations were classified as severe for classes I–III and mild for classes IV–VI [20]; individuals with 1 severe and 1 mild mutation were categorized as having a combined phenotype. Dietary data for PwCF were collected using a 3-d, 24-h food record (2 weekdays and 1 weekend day). Energy intake was reported in kilocalories and adjusted for total body weight (kcal/kg), and protein intake was reported in grams and adjusted for total body weight (g/kg). Data on supplementation with CF-specific fat-soluble vitamins, vitamin D, and calcium were obtained via self-report. Participants also self-reported their race and could select ≥1 of the following categories: White or Caucasian, Black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, Unknown or Not Reported, or Other. For analysis, participants were subsequently categorized into 3 groups: White or Caucasian, Black or African American, and Other.

Clinical outcomes

Glucose tolerance

An oral glucose tolerance test was conducted after a 10-h overnight fast. Participants consumed 1.75 g/kg (maximum 75 g) of glucose within 5 min. Blood samples were collected via an intravenous catheter at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose consumption to measure glucose and insulin concentrations. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-h plasma glucose (2-hPG), and corresponding plasma insulin levels were used to calculate the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (ISI-M) to measure insulin sensitivity [21]. Participants with CF were categorized according to prior CFRD diagnosis and current diagnostic criteria [22]: normal glucose (FPG <100 mg/dL); pre-CFRD (FPG 100–125 mg/dL or 2-hPG 140–199 mg/dL); and CFRD (FPG ≥126 mg/dL or 2-hPG ≥200 mg/dL).

Lung function

For PwCF, all spirometry data over the previous year were obtained from electronic health records. Baseline FEV1% was defined as the average of the best quarterly FEV1% values over the past calendar year. FEV1% was calculated using the original Global Lung Initiative equations [23].

Physical functionality

Lower-limb muscle strength and physical function were assessed via the 1-min sit-to-stand test (STST) [24]. Participants stood and sat repeatedly within 1 min at their own pace without relying on their arms for support. The total number of stands completed was recorded.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, for continuous variables and counts (percentage of group size) for categorical variables. Group differences—PwCF in contrast to healthy controls and PwCF with low ASMI compared with normal ASMI—were determined using 2 independent sample t test (or Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate) for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Nonnormally distributed variables (i.e., age, VAT, 2-hPG, and ISI-M) were log-transformed before linear regression analysis. Prevalence ratios were estimated via modified Poisson regression with robust error variance [25]. Simple linear regression and correlation analyses were used to quantify the relationships between PhA and skeletal muscle indicators (i.e., ASMI and HG strength) and between body composition indicators and clinical outcomes, respectively. By constructing multiple linear regression models, confounding and effect modification were assessed when examining the differences between groups and the relationships between clinical outcomes and body composition. Potential covariates and effect modifiers included biological variables (i.e., sex and age) and clinical variables (i.e., CFTR modulator use, VAT, Fat%, and energy and protein intake). For stratified analyses in the CF group, participants were also categorized into high and low VAT and Fat% groups using sex-specific median values as cutoff points: VAT >0.57 kg in males and >0.30 kg in females and Fat% >25% in males and >33% in females. Stratified analyses were also conducted based on CFTR modulator usage. Additionally, 2 exploratory analyses were performed. The first compared participants with CF categorized as having low or normal PhA, using either a published threshold of ≤5.0° [26] or the median CF value of ≤6.0°. The second analysis compared 3 categories (controls, non-CFTR modulator users, and CFTR modulator users) to assess differences in body composition and clinical outcomes. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted. The first was conducted to account for potential differences between study cohorts by including cohort membership as a covariate in the regression models, in addition to the primary variables and covariates already included. The second sensitivity analysis excluded participants with pancreatic sufficiency to determine whether their inclusion materially affected the results.

All analyses were conducted in SAS Studio (2024 SAS Institute Inc.) and R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024) using 2-sided tests with a 0.05 significance level.

Results

Body composition comparison between PwCF and healthy controls

Demographic and clinical characteristics from healthy controls (n = 75) and PwCF (n = 76) are presented in Table 1. Participants were predominantly White in both groups. There were no statistically significant differences in sex, BMI, or Fat% among groups. However, participants in the CF group were statistically older [30 (24–38) y in contrast to 26 (22–32) y; P = 0.02] and had higher VAT [0.40 (0.22–0.80) kg in contrast to 0.21 (0.07–0.46) kg; P = 0.001] compared with controls. PwCF had significantly lower muscle mass (ASMI 6.8 ± 1.3 kg/m2 as opposed to 7.3 ± 1.3 kg/m2; P = 0.02), HG strength (31.4 ± 9.5 kg as opposed to 35.0 ± 9.3 kg; P = 0.01), and muscle quality (PhA 6.4° ± 1.1° as opposed to 7.0° ± 0.99°; P = 0.001). Differences between healthy controls and PwCF remained significant after adjusting for sex or both sex and age. Although differences between study cohorts were observed, adjusting for cohort membership in our statistical models did not significantly alter the results. When participants were categorized into 3 groups (controls, non-CFTR modulator users, and CFTR modulator users), non-CFTR modulator users exhibited lower ASMI compared with controls (β: −0.73; 95% CI: −1.29, −0.16; P = 0.01). Body composition and clinical outcomes among PwCF

In a subanalysis of PwCF only, participants were categorized into normal muscle mass (n = 52) and low muscle mass (n = 24) groups. PwCF who exhibited low muscle mass had higher prevalence of CFRD (54% compared with 28%) and higher energy (50.1 ± 15.1 in contrast to 33.9 ± 12.8 kcal/kg) and protein (2.0 ± 0.68 in contrast to 1.5 ± 0.63 g/kg) intake compared with the normal muscle mass group (TABLE 2, TABLE 3). Participants in the low muscle mass group had lower BMI [19.9 (18.6–22.3) kg/m2 compared with 24.8 (22.2–28.1) kg/m2], no prevalence of overweight and obesity, and lower HG strength (26.8 ± 8.3 compared with 33.7 ± 9.3 kg; Table 3). Although no difference in PhA values was observed in either the low compared with normal ASMI subgroup analysis or the univariate linear regression analysis, PhA values were significantly lower in the low ASMI group after adjusting for protein intake (β: −0.76; 95% CI: 0.16, 1.08; P = 0.01). Discussion

This cross-sectional study evaluated muscle mass, muscle strength, muscle quality, and sarcopenia in adults with CF compared with healthy controls. Additionally, we explored the relationships between these indicators and clinical outcomes in PwCF. Participants with CF exhibited lower muscle strength and muscle quality compared with healthy controls, and one-third had low skeletal muscle mass. In PwCF, PhA and HG strength were positively associated with glucose tolerance. Furthermore, multivariate analysis revealed significant interactions involving body fat composition and CFTR modulator status, highlighting the importance of comprehensive and personalized nutrition assessment in PwCF.

Sarcopenia, based on EWGSOP2 criteria, was present in only 5 PwCF. In a cohort of young adults, the presence of a condition typically associated with aging is clinically significant. As life expectancy among PwCF continues to increase, early onset of sarcopenia raises concerns about the long-term effects of prolonged exposure, which may be compounded by aging and lead to worsening outcomes over time. Overall, the prevalence of low muscle mass was significantly higher in the CF group than in controls, even after adjusting for confounders. This aligns with previous findings reporting fat-free mass or muscle mass differences between CF and healthy cohorts [7,10,27]. Muscle strength was also lower in PwCF than in controls, as previous studies have described [9,44]. Reductions in muscle mass may explain this difference, although reduced muscle quality may also play a role. Longitudinal, multicenter studies are needed to determine whether low muscle mass progresses to sarcopenia in PwCF. However, it is important to identify those with CF at risk of sarcopenia to implement measures to prevent potential negative clinical outcomes related to muscle loss, including diminished quality of life.

Muscle quality, measured as PhA, was lower in the CF group than in controls. PhA is calculated independently of traditional regression equations for body composition that rely on demographic and clinical data, which may bias calculations [19]. In this study, PhA correlated with skeletal muscle quantity and strength, consistent with findings in other populations [28,29], yet it is understudied in pediatrics [30] and adults [31] with CF. Consequently, CF-specific cutoff points for PhA have not yet been established. Although further research is needed, the present findings suggest that PhA measurements using BIA are a simple, portable, and inexpensive method for evaluating skeletal muscle quality and potential surrogate for assessing muscle mass quantity and strength in PwCF.

Among those with CF, PhA and HG strength were related to insulin sensitivity and 2-hPG. Alterations in glucose metabolism are common in PwCF and have been primarily attributed to impaired insulin secretion due to pancreatic β-cell endocrine insufficiency [32]. Insulin deficiency in CF may contribute to a net catabolic state, leading to increased proteolysis and reduced skeletal muscle mass [33]. Concomitantly, decreased insulin sensitivity, especially in skeletal muscle, may further impair glucose metabolism [34]. Low skeletal muscle cell quality, the primary tissue responsible for glucose uptake, could interfere with the glucose transporter GLUT-4 trafficking from the intracellular compartment to the cell surface, contributing to glucose dysregulation and impaired muscle strength [33]. The present findings suggest that PhA and HG strength could play a role in a more comprehensive nutritional assessment for PwCF in future research and potentially in clinical practice. Similar to BIA, HG offers a simple, inexpensive, and portable surrogate for skeletal muscle mass and strength.

The multivariable analysis of body composition indicators and clinical outcomes revealed significant interactions with adipose measures. Studies have reported that PwCF exhibit higher VAT levels despite having Fat% similar to healthy controls [27,35]. CFTR modulator use is associated with weight gain [10], and recent longitudinal studies have identified fat mass increase as a major contributor to these body weight changes [14,31]. In our analysis, body fat composition modified the relationship between physical functionality and muscle mass and strength. These findings highlight the importance of an integrative, rather than a component-specific, approach when conducting nutritional assessments.

Low muscle mass in relation to worse lung function has been reported in pediatric [30,36] and adult [7,35] CF populations. However, most of these high-quality studies were conducted before the widespread use of CFTR modulators. In our study, CFTR modulator status modified the positive relationship between muscle mass and lung function such that the association was significant only in PwCF not taking modulators. To our knowledge, this interaction has not previously been described in PwCF. These findings suggest that PwCF not on modulators may benefit from more frequent assessments of lean mass and musculoskeletal health support compared with those already on modulator therapy.

The pathophysiology underlying reduced muscle quality and quantity in CF is not fully understood, but CFTR deficiency itself has been implicated. Human and animal models have demonstrated that CFTR knockout leads to impaired insulin-stimulated GLUT4 translocation in skeletal muscle fibers, contributing to altered glucose metabolism, dysregulated calcium homeostasis, inflammation, and greater skeletal muscle weakness [[37], [38], [39]]. Although CFTR modulators improve pulmonary function [40], their effects on glucose metabolism remain variable [41,42], and weight gain following initiation of HEMT appears to reflect primarily fat mass rather than improvements in muscle quality [31]. These observations highlight the need for further mechanistic and longitudinal research into how CFTR function, glycemic regulation, and muscle biology intersect, particularly in the post-HEMT era.

This study represents novel comprehensive body composition research within an adult CF population in association with well-defined clinical outcomes. Strengths included standardized procedures for data collection across the study cohorts performed in a clinical research center setting. Similarly, inclusion and exclusion criteria were well-defined and similar across cohorts. Although differences between cohorts existed, the consistency of results after adjusting for cohort membership enhances the internal validity of our analyses.

Limitations of this study include the cross-sectional design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships, and a convenience sample derived from 3 cohorts, precluding formal sample size and power calculations. The study was also limited by sampling from a single CF center, which limits the generalizability of the findings and subgroup analyses. Some variables were unavailable or measured differently between cohorts, including physical activity, duration of and adherence to CFTR modulator treatment, and sweat chloride levels. These factors, along with missing values, may have introduced selection bias and reduced statistical power. To minimize further bias, no imputation methods were applied, and the number of observations for each variable is reported for transparency. Although we examined the relationship between glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and skeletal muscle indices, glycated hemoglobin values from the previous 3 mo and total insulin dose were not available for all participants. Finally, although we used the average of the best quarterly FEV1% values over the previous 12 mo to reduce bias from transient fluctuations (i.e., infections, pulmonary exacerbations, and respiratory illness) [43], other changes in clinical status during this timeframe may still have influenced the results. Given these limitations and the lack of disease-specific thresholds for key measures, further research is needed to validate our findings and clarify the clinical implications. Longitudinal analyses and larger, well-powered studies will be necessary to establish causation between body composition and clinical outcomes in PwCF.

In conclusion, adults with CF have a >30% prevalence of low muscle mass compared with 9.3% in the healthy group. Muscle quality, assessed via PhA, correlates with muscle mass and HG strength. In PwCF, both skeletal muscle quality and strength were related to glucose tolerance. CFTR modulator usage modified the relationship between lung function and muscle mass, with a positive association observed only among those not taking CFTR modulators. Furthermore, the relationships between physical functionality and skeletal muscle mass and strength were influenced by adiposity. Together, these data highlight the importance of comprehensive and personalized nutrition assessment and management approaches in PwCF, particularly in the era of CFTR modulators.
