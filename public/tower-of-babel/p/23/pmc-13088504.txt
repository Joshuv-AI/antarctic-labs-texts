Long-term recurrence after inguinal hernia repair in a single-surgeon cohort using predefined mesh per approach
Authors: Tufan Gümüş, Erkan Güler, Tolga Girgin, Recep Temel, Volkan Sayur, Sinan Ersin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to evaluate, within a single-surgeon, predefined, approach-specific mesh protocol, whether the surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open) is independently associated with long-term recurrence after adjusting for patient-related factors.Patients and MethodsA total of 314 patients who underwent surgery using both laparoscopic and open techniques between 2013 and 2022 by a single expert general surgeon were analyzed. The patients’ age, gender, hernia side, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, preoperative American Society of Anesthesiologists scores, type of operation, postoperative complications, and recurrence rates during follow-up were retrospectively reviewed from the Electronic Patient File (EPF) system. Repairs followed a predefined, approach-specific mesh protocol (self-gripping mesh for open repairs; pre-formed polypropylene mesh for laparoscopic repairs).ResultsThe median follow-up duration was 72 months (IQR 56.5–102; range 26–140 months). Overall recurrence was observed in 24 of 314 patients (7.6%) during follow-up. Crude rates were similar between approaches (7.3% after open [14/192] vs. after laparoscopic 8.2% [10/122], p = 0.769). In multivariable analysis, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and prior repair emerged as independent predictors of recurrence.ConclusionIn a standardized setting minimizing surgeon and implant heterogeneity, recurrence was approximately 8% at a median follow-up of about 6 years and was primarily associated with patient-related factors, such as comorbidity and prior repair, rather than surgical approach. Within the context of this cohort, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and may reflect recurrence patterns observed under standardized conditions, rather than a definitive lower-bound benchmark. They also highlight the potential relevance of pre- and perioperative risk optimization beyond surgical approach selection alone.Keywords: Inguinal hernia surgery, Laparoscopic repair, Open repair, Primary hernia, Recurrent hernia

Introduction

Inguinal hernia repair is one of the most frequently performed procedures in general surgery [1]. The primary postoperative complication is hernia recurrence, which may be influenced by several factors, including surgical technique, mesh type, postoperative bleeding, infection, and patient comorbidities [2]. While the Lichtenstein procedure remains the gold standard for open repair, the increasing use of minimally invasive approaches has demonstrated comparable recurrence rates with improved postoperative outcomes [3]. Moreover, consistent use of a specific mesh type and surgical technique appears to be crucial in minimizing recurrence rates in inguinal hernia repair.

Despite the use of standardized definitions, studies employing either open or laparoscopic techniques have reported variable recurrence rates. The intricate nature of inguinal anatomy, the diversity of available meshes, surgeon preferences, and technical variations necessitate further investigation [3]. For primary hernia repair, recurrence rates appear comparable between open and laparoscopic approaches when mesh reinforcement is utilized. Furthermore, meta-analyses of randomized trials indicate no significant difference in recurrence rates for recurrent hernias treated with tension-free mesh repair, irrespective of the surgical approach [4].

Surgeon expertise is a critical determinant of outcomes in inguinal hernia repair, particularly in recurrent cases, as recurrence rates are generally lower among experienced surgeons compared to their less experienced counterparts [5]. Mesh selection also varies across institutions, and the choice of mesh can substantially impact surgical success [6]. Distinct from most previous studies, we evaluated recurrence rates in the practice of a single experienced general surgeon, utilizing one standardized mesh type, and compared outcomes between laparoscopic and open approaches to identify recurrence risk factors. To minimize heterogeneity, a single-surgeon model was employed with a predefined, approach-specific mesh protocol (self-gripping mesh for open repair; pre-formed polypropylene mesh for laparoscopic repair), thereby reducing within-approach implant variability. We hypothesized that, within this standardized framework, patient-related factors such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and history of prior repair would be the primary predictors of recurrence, whereas the surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open) would not independently influence recurrence after adjustment. Additionally, we aimed to define the long-term “floor” recurrence rate achievable under these controlled conditions.

Patients and Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a retrospective observational cohort study. Approval was obtained from the Ege University Ethics Committee on 29/05/2023, approval number 23-8T/35.

Study population and inclusion criteria

This study included 314 patients who underwent either laparoscopic or open inguinal hernia repair performed by a single expert general surgeon in the Department of General Surgery at Ege University between 2013 and 2022. Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, hernia laterality, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, preoperative ASA scores, type of operation, postoperative complications, and recurrence during follow-up, were retrospectively retrieved from the Electronic Patient File (EPF) system. Only patients with complete datasets and documented consent for participation were included in the analysis. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) was defined as a documented history of coronary artery disease, heart failure, or clinically relevant cardiac arrhythmia requiring medical treatment. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was recorded as a binary variable (present/absent) based on documented diagnosis in the medical records; detailed information on glycemic control (e.g., HbA1c levels) or insulin use was not consistently available and therefore not included in the analysis.

Patients were categorized into two groups: recurrence and non-recurrence. Open inguinal hernia repairs were performed using an anatomically shaped self-fixating polyester mesh (ProGrip™, Medtronic, MN, USA), composed of a monofilament polyester base with resorbable polylactic acid microgrips. The standard mesh size routinely used for open repairs was 12 × 8 cm, in right- or left-sided configuration according to hernia laterality. In contrast, laparoscopic procedures were performed using a pre-formed knitted polypropylene mesh of predefined size placed in the preperitoneal space. All operations were performed by a single experienced general surgeon. Study parameters were analyzed both within and between groups to evaluate factors associated with recurrence during follow-up. Recurrence was defined as a palpable or visible bulge at the operated site identified on clinical examination; in equivocal cases, the diagnosis was confirmed by ultrasound or computed tomography. Recurrence assessment was primarily based on clinical examination during routine outpatient visits, with imaging reserved exclusively for cases with equivocal clinical findings. Follow-up duration is reported as median with interquartile range owing to the skewed distribution of observation times. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 29.0, IBM, Chicago). Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. The Student’s t-test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Variables demonstrating significant differences between groups were subsequently analyzed by logistic regression to determine their independent association with recurrence during follow-up. To address heterogeneity in follow-up duration and potential time-at-risk bias, a prespecified landmark sensitivity analysis was performed. Because all patients in the final cohort had a minimum follow-up duration of at least 24 months, no patients were excluded for the prespecified landmark sensitivity analysis.

Selection of surgical approach

The choice between open and laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair was made at the discretion of the operating surgeon based on routine clinical assessment, as this was a retrospective observational study without randomization. Factors influencing the selection of surgical approach included patient age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, comorbidity burden, hernia characteristics (such as unilateral versus bilateral involvement), and overall anesthetic risk. In general, laparoscopic repair was preferentially considered in younger patients with lower perioperative risk and in cases of bilateral hernia, whereas open repair was more commonly selected for older patients and those with higher ASA scores or significant comorbidities. These selection patterns reflect real-world clinical practice and are consistent with the baseline differences observed between the two groups.

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed by a single experienced general surgeon using standardized operative techniques. All laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs were performed using the transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) approach. Patients requiring conversion to open surgery were excluded from the study to maintain technical homogeneity of the cohort. In laparoscopic TAPP repairs, a pre-formed polypropylene mesh of predefined size was placed in the preperitoneal space to ensure adequate coverage of the entire myopectineal orifice. Mesh fixation was routinely performed using tacker fixation, in accordance with commonly accepted surgical practice, particularly to reduce the risk of mesh migration and recurrence. Following mesh placement, the peritoneum was closed in all TAPP procedures using standard techniques. Open inguinal hernia repairs were performed using a standardized anterior approach with a self-gripping mesh, without routine additional fixation, in line with the predefined institutional protocol. Both direct and indirect hernia defects were managed according to standard anatomical principles within each approach. Bilateral and recurrent hernias were repaired during the same operative session using the same standardized technique corresponding to the selected surgical approach.

Analytic note

Because mesh type was perfectly collinear with surgical approach (each approach used a single predefined mesh without cross-over), mesh could not be evaluated as an independent predictor in the regression model. Therefore, our analysis compares two standardized technique packages (approach plus corresponding mesh), and no conclusions can be drawn regarding the isolated effect of mesh material on recurrence. We performed multivariable logistic regression including a-priori covariates (age, sex, BMI, ASA, DM, CVD, prior repair, surgical approach). Model results are reported as adjusted ORs (95% CI). As a sensitivity analysis, patients presenting with a recurrent hernia at baseline (prior repair) were excluded and the model was repeated. Given the baseline imbalances between groups (age, ASA, comorbidities), we performed multivariable logistic regression to adjust for these potential confounders.

Results

A total of 314 patients who underwent inguinal hernia repair between 2013 and 2022 in the Department of General Surgery at Ege University were included in the study. The mean age was 60.05 ± 13.15 years. Of these patients, 278 (88.5%) were male and 36 (11.4%) were female. At presentation, 131 (41.7%) had right-sided inguinal hernia, 92 (29.3%) had left-sided hernia, and 91 (29%) had bilateral hernia. The median BMI was 26.42 kg/m² [25.74–27.09]. A history of prior repair (baseline recurrent hernia) was documented in 23 patients (7.3%). Following the index operation, recurrence developed in 24 patients (7.6%) during follow-up. According to the ASA classification, 131 (41.7%) patients were classified as ASA I, 135 (43.0%) as ASA II, and 48 (15.3%) as ASA III (Table 1). Open repair was performed in 192 patients (61.15%), while 122 patients (38.85%) underwent laparoscopic repair. The median follow-up duration was 72 months (IQR 56.5–102; range 26–140 months), during which recurrence occurred in 24 patients (7.6%).

Patients were categorized into two groups according to recurrence status during follow-up: recurrence and non-recurrence. Within the recurrence group, most patients were classified as ASA I, reflecting the overall predominance of lower ASA classes in the study cohort rather than an association between ASA class and recurrence. The mean BMI was 26.34 kg/m² in the non-recurrence group and 27.20 kg/m² in the recurrence group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the groups (p = 0.450).

In the recurrence group, ecchymosis/edema developed in 2 patients (8.3%) and superficial surgical site infection in 1 patient (4.1%). In the non-recurrence group, ecchymosis/edema occurred in 10 patients (3.4%) and superficial surgical site infection in 3 patients (1.0%) (p = 0.190). Diabetes mellitus was identified in 8 patients (33.3%) in the recurrence group compared with 14 patients (4.8%) in the non-recurrence group, demonstrating a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Similarly, cardiovascular disease (CVD) was present in 16 patients (66.7%) with recurrence and 99 patients (34.1%) without recurrence (p = 0.001). Statistically significant differences were also observed between groups for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), DM, CVD, and prior inguinal hernia repair (Table 3). When stratified by hernia status at presentation, recurrence occurred in 16 of 291 patients (5.5%) presenting with primary hernia and in 8 of 23 patients (34.8%) presenting with recurrent hernia at baseline (prior repair), highlighting prior repair as a major determinant of subsequent recurrence. To specifically address the confounding effect of prior repair, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients with prior repair at baseline; recurrence was observed in 16 of 291 patients (5.5%) within the primary hernia cohort. Logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to assess the independent associations of these variables with recurrence during the follow-up period.

Discussion

In this single-surgeon cohort with standardized, approach-specific mesh protocols, the overall long-term recurrence rate after inguinal hernia repair was 7.6%. Recurrence rates were similar between surgical approaches, occurring in 8.2% of patients after laparoscopic repair and 7.3% after open repair. Recurrence was more frequent among patients presenting with recurrent hernia at baseline compared with those undergoing primary repair. Patients in the recurrence group more commonly had diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and a history of prior repair. Open repair was more frequently performed in older patients and those with higher ASA scores, whereas laparoscopic repair was more commonly selected for patients with bilateral hernias. No relevant differences were observed between recurrence and non-recurrence groups with respect to sex, hernia laterality, body mass index, or postoperative complications.Despite variability in individual follow-up durations, a landmark sensitivity analysis yielded findings consistent with the primary results, supporting the robustness of the observed associations.

In multivariable analyses, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and a history of prior inguinal hernia repair were identified as independent risk factors for recurrence, whereas surgical approach did not retain an independent association. Although benign prostatic hyperplasia was more prevalent among patients with recurrence, it did not remain a significant predictor after adjustment. While higher body mass index has been associated with recurrence in previous studies, no significant difference was observed in the present cohort, in which both recurrence and non-recurrence groups were classified as overweight. These findings indicate that patient-related factors, rather than the choice of surgical approach, predominantly drive long-term recurrence risk after adjustment for baseline differences. Differences in age, ASA score, and comorbidity burden between laparoscopic and open repair groups reflect real-world selection patterns, with open repair being more frequently performed in older and higher-risk patients, while laparoscopic repair is often selected for bilateral hernias [7–9]. Prior studies have reported an association between higher body mass index and increased recurrence risk [10]; however, this relationship was not observed in the present cohort. Cardiovascular disease has been identified as a risk factor for perioperative morbidity in general surgery and is more commonly associated with open repair [11], findings that are consistent with our observation of higher recurrence rates among patients with cardiovascular disease. Similarly, diabetes mellitus may impair wound healing and mesh integration through chronic inflammatory and microvascular mechanisms [12], and large population-based studies have demonstrated higher recurrence rates among patients with metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus [13]. Although benign prostatic hyperplasia has been associated with an increased risk of inguinal hernia formation [14], it did not emerge as an independent predictor of recurrence in our adjusted analyses.

An important strength of our study is the minimization of surgeon- and implant-related variability by employing a single-surgeon model with protocolized, approach-specific mesh and fixation strategies, effectively treating each surgical approach as a standardized “package” (technique plus mesh). International guidelines and prior studies emphasize that mesh characteristics and fixation methods are closely linked to the selected surgical approach and are typically optimized as an integrated technical strategy rather than applied interchangeably across techniques [15, 16]. Under these controlled conditions, recurrence appeared to be more strongly associated with patient-related factors than with the overall standardized technique package. However, because mesh type was inherently linked to surgical approach, we cannot exclude a potential contribution of mesh material to recurrence risk. Because mesh type and fixation were fully collinear with surgical approach, however, we were unable to isolate an independent effect of mesh. Future studies designed to apply the same mesh type across different approaches—or to compare multiple mesh and fixation strategies within a single approach—are necessary to disentangle the respective contributions of mesh, fixation, and surgical technique.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted retrospectively in a single center by a single surgeon, which reduced variability but may limit the generalizability of the results to broader populations and different levels of surgical expertise. Second, although recurrence was assessed in a long follow-up period, the relatively small number of recurrence events (n = 24) limited the statistical power of multivariable analyses. Third, baseline differences between laparoscopic and open groups (age, ASA score, and comorbidities) may have introduced residual confounding despite adjustment with multivariable regression. In addition, a formal hernia classification system, such as the European Hernia Society (EHS) classification, was not applied. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the long inclusion period, detailed anatomical descriptors required for reliable EHS classification were not consistently available in the electronic patient records and therefore could not be accurately reconstructed. Fourth, recurrence was primarily determined by clinical examination, with ultrasound or CT performed only in equivocal cases, and assessments were not blinded; therefore, some degree of subjectivity, ascertainment bias, and underestimation of subclinical recurrence cannot be excluded. Finally, although the median follow-up exceeded six years, some patients were lost to follow-up, and standardized imaging-based surveillance was not applied, which may have led to underestimation of true recurrence rates.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that reducing long-term recurrence after inguinal hernia repair may require greater emphasis on patient-related risk factors rather than surgical approach alone. Optimization of comorbid conditions, particularly cardiometabolic disease, and careful perioperative planning for patients with a history of prior repair may be important components of recurrence prevention. While technical standardization remains essential for procedural quality, individualized risk assessment and targeted medical optimization should be considered integral to comprehensive hernia management strategies.

Conclusion

In this cohort, recurrence appeared to be more closely associated with patient-related factors than with the standardized approach-specific technique applied; however, independent effects of mesh material could not be assessed. Longer follow-up is essential for accurately assessing recurrence, and the observed recurrence patterns within a predefined approach-specific mesh protocol should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as a generalized benchmark, underscoring the need for further studies with standardized surveillance and broader patient populations.
