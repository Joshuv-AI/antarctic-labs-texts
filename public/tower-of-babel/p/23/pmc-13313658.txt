Emerging treatments in fungal keratitis
Authors: Aafreen Bari, Tushar Agarwal, Rajesh Sinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungal keratitis is a major cause of corneal blindness, particularly in tropical and low-resource settings, and remains challenging to manage due to delayed diagnosis, limited drug penetration, emerging resistance, and host-mediated tissue damage. Conventional antifungal therapies, including natamycin and voriconazole, are often inadequate in deep, severe, or refractory infections, underscoring the need for alternative strategies. This narrative review summarizes and critically appraises emerging treatments in fungal keratitis, with a focus on recent pharmacological advances, innovative drug delivery systems, adjunctive therapies, and immunomodulatory approaches. Newer-generation azoles, echinocandins, and repurposed agents are discussed alongside advanced formulations such as liposomal amphotericin B, solubilized natamycin, nanoparticle-based delivery, and intrastromal drug administration. Adjunctive modalities, including photo-activated corneal cross-linking, photodynamic therapy, therapeutic contact lenses, and low-temperature plasma, are reviewed for their antimicrobial and corneal-stabilizing effects. The role of immunomodulators in mitigating excessive inflammation while preserving antifungal defense is also highlighted. In addition, evolving strategies for managing infections caused by emerging and fungus-like pathogens, including Pythium sp., are addressed. While many of these therapies show promising experimental and early clinical outcomes, robust randomized controlled trials and standardized treatment protocols remain limited. This review emphasizes the need for translational research, ocular-specific antifungal development, and integrated therapeutic approaches to improve visual outcomes and reduce the burden of fungal keratitis.

INTRODUCTION

Fungal keratitis is a severe and vision-threatening corneal infection that disproportionately affects populations in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. A comprehensive systematic review estimated a minimum global annual incidence of 1,051,787 cases (range: 736,251–1,367,323), with the burden potentially rising to 1,480,916 cases if culture-negative infections are presumed fungal. The highest incidence is reported from Asia and Africa, reflecting strong geographic, climatic, and occupational associations.[1]

In tropical and developing regions, fungal pathogens account for approximately 20%–60% of all culture-positive microbial keratitis cases, depending on location.[2] A large meta-analysis of 169 eligible studies reported a pooled prevalence of fungal keratitis of 23.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 20.4%–27.1%) among clinically suspected microbial keratitis, and 17.9% (95% CI: 6.96–32.42%) among culture-confirmed cases.[3] These figures highlight fungal keratitis as a major contributor to corneal blindness worldwide.

The visual morbidity associated with fungal keratitis is substantial. Available literature suggests that 8%–11% of affected eyes ultimately require evisceration or enucleation.[2] Many cases that are untreated or inadequately managed progress to corneal perforation, irreversible structural damage, or blindness. Because a large proportion of patients never reach tertiary eye care centers, particularly in rural settings, the true burden of disease is likely underestimated.

Several interrelated challenges underpin these poor outcomes. Delayed presentation and diagnosis are common due to limited access to ophthalmic services and diagnostic facilities. The conventional antifungal therapies frequently fail to achieve sterilization in deep stromal disease, and a significant proportion of patients deteriorate despite treatment, requiring surgical intervention. In resource-constrained settings, limited access to microscopy, culture, and molecular diagnostics necessitates empiric therapy, which may be delayed, inappropriate, or ineffective.

The pharmacologic limitations further compound these challenges. Many topical antifungal agents demonstrate poor corneal penetration, particularly into the deep stroma and interface zones, and often have a limited spectrum of activity. Intrinsic or acquired resistance among fungal pathogens is increasingly recognized. Manikandan et al. demonstrated higher minimum inhibitory concentrations for Fusarium species compared with Aspergillus, suggesting greater resistance trends.[4] Prolonged treatment duration, poor patient adherence, and drug-related toxicity – especially with agents such as amphotericin B – further limit therapeutic success. Delayed or inaccurate diagnosis may result in empiric therapy mismatched to the causative organism. Collectively, rising antifungal resistance, the lack of new ocular-specific antifungal classes, and high rates of surgical intervention – often associated with graft rejection, recurrence, and poor visual outcomes – underscore the need for alternative approaches.

In response to these unmet needs, several novel strategies have emerged, including nano-formulations and sustained-release drug delivery systems, photoactivated collagen cross-linking (PACK-CXL), antifungal peptides and biological agents, adjunctive immunomodulation, and gene- or RNA-based antifungal therapies. This review aims to consolidate and critically appraise the evolving landscape of emerging treatments for fungal keratitis.

ESTABLISHED MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES AND THEIR LIMITATIONS

Conventional management of fungal keratitis relies primarily on topical natamycin and voriconazole, yet both are constrained by significant pharmacologic and clinical limitations. Natamycin, the first-line agent for filamentous fungal keratitis, is fungistatic and demonstrates poor stromal and intraocular penetration, reducing its efficacy in deep or advanced disease.[5] Voriconazole offers better corneal penetration but shows inconsistent efficacy, particularly against Fusarium species.

The National Eye Institute-funded Mycotic Ulcer Treatment Trials (MUTT I and II) demonstrated that topical natamycin was superior to topical voriconazole, and that adjunctive oral voriconazole provided no additional benefit. Despite this, outcomes remain suboptimal: in MUTT II, nearly 50% of patients experienced full-thickness corneal perforation or required therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty.[67] These findings emphasize the inadequacy of existing therapies for many patients.

Additional drawbacks include the suspension formulation of natamycin, which can leave ocular surface deposits, reducing comfort, adherence, and consistency of drug delivery.[8] Even when corneal drug concentrations exceed minimum inhibitory concentrations, clinical response may remain poor.[9] The emergence of new fungal pathogens, coupled with slow antifungal drug development, has further widened the treatment gap.[10] Systemic antifungals such as voriconazole or itraconazole may be used as adjuncts, but their long-term use is limited by systemic toxicity, particularly hepatotoxicity.[11] Concerns regarding limited antifungal spectrum, resistance, ocular surface toxicity, and cost – especially in resource-limited settings – persist.[1213] Together, these limitations highlight the urgent need for innovative topical formulations, safer systemic options, and effective adjunctive therapies to improve outcomes in fungal keratitis.

EMERGING PHARMACOLOGICAL THERAPIES

Emerging antifungal therapies for fungal keratitis include newer triazoles, echinocandins, triterpenoid glucan synthase inhibitors, and advanced polyene formulations. These agents act primarily by inhibiting ergosterol synthesis or disrupting fungal cell wall integrity, with improved pharmacokinetics and enhanced corneal penetration through novel delivery systems such as nano-formulations, liposomes, and intrastromal injections. REPURPOSED AND EXPERIMENTAL DRUGS

Drug repurposing in fungal keratitis involves the use of nonantifungal or nonocular agents with demonstrated antifungal or adjunctive effects. This strategy is particularly relevant given the limitations of conventional antifungals, including poor corneal penetration and limited spectrum. Agents such as chlorhexidine, nanoparticles, hypochlorous acid (HOCl), and low-temperature plasma (LTP) have been explored as adjuncts or alternatives.

Chlorhexidine is a broad-spectrum antiseptic that disrupts fungal cell membranes.[4849] It has shown activity against both yeasts and filamentous fungi, with pilot clinical data supporting its role as an adjunct in recalcitrant cases.[50] Synergistic effects with natamycin and voriconazole have also been demonstrated, particularly against Fusarium.[51]

Nanoparticle-based systems enhance drug solubility, stability, and corneal penetration. Silver nanoparticles exhibit potent antifungal and antibiofilm activity, although toxicity limits direct use. Modified formulations and drug-loaded systems show improved efficacy and sustained release, but current evidence remains largely preclinical.[5253545556575859]

HOCl provides rapid antimicrobial activity with minimal toxicity and additional anti-inflammatory effects.[6061] A randomized trial data suggest improved epithelial healing and faster resolution when used as an adjunct.[62]

LTP is a non-pharmacologic modality that disrupts fungal structures and enhances drug penetration. Early clinical studies indicate improved healing, particularly in superficial to mid-stromal disease.[6364]

SYNERGISTIC DRUG COMBINATIONS

Combination therapy in fungal keratitis enhances antifungal efficacy, broadens the spectrum, reduces resistance, and limits toxicity by targeting complementary fungal pathways. Natamycin combined with voriconazole disrupts ergosterol through dual mechanisms, showing synergism against Fusarium and Aspergillus with improved outcomes in recalcitrant cases.[6566] Amphotericin B combined with azoles demonstrates additive or partial synergy when administered in a time-separated or localized manner, particularly against Candida and Aspergillus.[6768] Echinocandins combined with azoles or polyenes provide dual cell wall–membrane disruption, enhancing activity against Candida and Aspergillus, though Fusarium often remains resistant.[697071] Voriconazole with terbinafine produces sequential ergosterol pathway blockade and synergism against filamentous and dematiaceous fungi.[727374] Natamycin with chlorhexidine enhances membrane permeability and antifungal efficacy, particularly against Fusarium species.[75]

ROUTES OF DRUG ADMINISTRATION

Management of fungal keratitis requires a multimodal approach, as the infection often involves the deep corneal stroma and, in advanced cases, the endothelium and anterior chamber. Various routes of drug administration have therefore been explored to enhance drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy.

Topical therapy remains the mainstay of treatment and includes over-the-counter natamycin 5% and reconstituted formulations such as voriconazole 1%, amphotericin 0.1%, caspofungin 0.5%, and other newer antifungal preparations. However, the penetration of topical agents into the deeper corneal layers is often limited, particularly in severe or recalcitrant disease.

Systemic antifungal therapy serves as an important adjunct in severe fungal keratitis. Oral antifungals are known to achieve therapeutically relevant concentrations in the aqueous humour and tear film. Among these, oral voriconazole attains significantly higher tear film and intraocular concentrations compared with ketoconazole and has been associated with better visual outcomes and smaller residual corneal scars, while maintaining similar healing rates.[76]

To overcome the limitations of topical and systemic therapy, targeted drug delivery techniques have been developed. The rationale for these approaches lies in delivering high concentrations of antifungal agents directly to the site of infection, thereby improving bioavailability while minimizing systemic adverse effects.

Intracameral administration of antifungal agents is particularly useful in cases with thick, cheesy hypopyon or deep stromal and endothelial plaques, where anterior chamber involvement is evident. Intrastromal injection of antifungal agents provides localized, high-concentration drug delivery to the area of active fungal proliferation. The procedure is performed under strict aseptic conditions using a 30G needle with the bevel facing down. The needle is introduced obliquely from an uninvolved area of the cornea to reach the mid-stromal level adjacent to the abscess, which is the intended site of drug deposition. The extent of stromal hydration serves as a guide to assess the area of drug diffusion. In deep stromal fungal ulcers, circumferential barrage intrastromal injections allow uniform coverage around the lesion, ensuring targeted therapy at deeper levels.[77]

Intrastromal therapy is particularly beneficial in fungal ulcers unresponsive to conventional topical and systemic antifungal treatment. However, this modality should be avoided in eyes with significant corneal thinning or impending perforation. Intracameral and intrastromal injections may be repeated based on clinical response and, in refractory cases, can be administered up to three to five times. The commonly used antifungal agents for intrastromal injection include voriconazole (50 μg/0.1 mL), amphotericin B (5–10 μg/0.1 mL), and natamycin (Natasol – 10 μg/0.1 mL). The advantages of this route include direct drug delivery, improved penetration, sustained local drug levels, reduced epithelial toxicity, and its role as an effective adjunct to topical and systemic therapy. Intrastromal injections have been shown to be a safe and effective adjunct in the management of recalcitrant fungal keratitis. Evidence from prospective randomized controlled trials suggests that intrastromal natamycin provides comparable visual outcomes with faster resolution when compared with intrastromal amphotericin B and voriconazole, while intrastromal amphotericin B has been associated with a higher incidence of deep corneal vascularization following healing.[78]

IMMUNOMODULATORS IN THE MANAGEMENT OF FUNGAL KERATITIS

Immunomodulatory therapy in fungal keratitis focuses on controlling the excessive host inflammatory response that contributes to corneal damage and vision loss. Unlike antifungal agents that directly target pathogens, immunomodulators aim to balance immune activity, enhancing fungal clearance while limiting collateral stromal injury. These agents may be administered topically, systemically, intrastromally, or act at the genetic level. Topical agents studied include tacrolimus, cyclosporine, chondroitin sulfate, rapamycin, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), and maresin 1, while interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) functions endogenously.

Tacrolimus inhibits calcineurin-mediated T-cell activation, reducing cytokine-driven inflammation and stromal damage without inducing corneal thinning.[79808182] Cyclosporine similarly suppresses T-cell activation and has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and synergistic antifungal effects, including enhanced activity when co-encapsulated with voriconazole in nanoparticle formulations.[838485868788] Chondroitin sulfate exhibits anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair properties; modified formulations combined with natamycin reduce fungal burden and inflammatory mediators while promoting epithelial and stromal healing.[899091] VIP and maresin 1 modulate cytokine profiles by suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators and enhancing interleukin-10, reducing disease severity in experimental keratitis.[9293] Rapamycin inhibits immune overactivation and fungal proliferation while reducing corneal scarring.[9495] At the genetic level, ISG15 regulates innate immunity by suppressing excessive inflammation and enhancing antifungal defences; its upregulation attenuates disease severity, whereas silencing worsens infection.[969798]

NEWER TREATMENT MODALITIES

Photo-activated chromophore for keratitis–corneal cross-linking

PACK-CXL uses riboflavin activated by ultraviolet-A light to generate reactive oxygen species that induce collagen cross-linking, increasing corneal stiffness and resistance to enzymatic degradation while exerting antimicrobial effects through nucleic acid damage.[99100] As an adjunct to antifungal therapy, PACK-CXL has been shown to accelerate epithelial healing, reduce infiltrate size, and lower surgical intervention rates.[101102] Its advantages include broad-spectrum antimicrobial action, corneal stabilization, reduced reliance on prolonged topical therapy, and feasibility as an in-office procedure.[103] However, limited UVA penetration reduces efficacy in deep stromal infections, and endothelial toxicity may occur in thinned corneas, making careful case selection essential.[104]

Photodynamic therapy

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) employs photosensitizers such as riboflavin, rose bengal, or methylene blue, activated by specific light wavelengths to generate reactive oxygen species that damage fungal membranes, mitochondria, and nucleic acids.[105] In fungal keratitis, PDT provides direct antifungal action, inhibits biofilm formation, and modulates inflammation. Rose Bengal–mediated PDT has demonstrated a significant reduction in fungal load against Fusarium, Aspergillus, and Candida species.[106107] While PDT offers broad antimicrobial activity and minimal systemic toxicity, its effectiveness is limited in deep stromal disease due to restricted light penetration, and concerns remain regarding phototoxicity and lack of standardized protocols.

Therapeutic contact lens

Drug-eluting therapeutic contact lenses provide sustained, localized antifungal delivery, overcoming poor bioavailability and rapid clearance seen with topical drops. Econazole-loaded lenses achieved prolonged fungicidal activity, while chlorhexidine-and voriconazole-eluting designs demonstrated extended release and improved outcomes in experimental keratitis models.[108109110] Advanced nanocoatings incorporating chitosan, graphene oxide, silver nanoparticles, or phytomolecule-ZnO complexes further enhance antimicrobial, antifouling, and corneal permeability properties.[111] These lenses offer controlled drug release, improved compliance, and reduced need for systemic therapy or surgery, representing a promising adjunctive strategy in fungal keratitis management.

NEWER FUNGUS AND FUNGUS-LIKE PATHOGENS

Uncommon filamentous fungi such as Scedosporium/Lomentospora, Purpureocillium lilacinum, Talaromyces spp., and the fungus-like oomycete Pythium insidiosum have emerged as important causes of keratitis and are frequently misdiagnosed as conventional fungal ulcers. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion supported by microscopy, culture, confocal imaging, and advanced techniques such as PCR, ITS sequencing, and MALDI-TOF. Management is challenging due to variable drug susceptibility. While some organisms respond to newer azoles or combination therapy, Pythium insidiosum lacks ergosterol and requires antibacterial agents with early surgical intervention.[112]

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Emerging treatments in fungal keratitis have expanded beyond conventional antifungals to include novel drug formulations, combination therapies, immunomodulation, photo-based therapies, and advanced drug delivery systems. These approaches aim to overcome limitations of poor corneal penetration, drug resistance, and host-mediated tissue damage, thereby improving clinical outcomes. Despite encouraging experimental and early clinical results, most therapies lack large, randomized controlled trials. Future directions should focus on standardized treatment protocols, development of ocular-specific antifungal agents, precision diagnostics, and translational studies integrating nanotechnology, gene-based therapies, and personalized medicine to reduce morbidity and prevent vision loss.
