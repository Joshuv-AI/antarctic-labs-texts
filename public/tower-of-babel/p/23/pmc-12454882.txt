An injectable phosphocreatine-grafted hydrogel incorporating hierarchically structured teriparatide/SrZnP-functionalized Zn–Cu particles for osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling and osteoporotic bone regeneration
Authors: Haotian Qin, Zhiping Guan, Yuanhao Wang, Jin Zhao, Zhenhai Xie, Huaiyu Li, Chen Zhang, Weibei Sheng, Fei Yu, Jian Weng, Yingqi Chen, Deli Wang, Hui Zeng, Junyu Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoporotic bone defects remain a major clinical challenge due to impaired osteogenesis, insufficient angiogenesis, excessive osteoclast activity, and increased susceptibility to infection. To address these issues, we developed an injectable phosphocreatine-grafted gelatin hydrogel (GGP) incorporating hierarchically structured Zn-Cu particles functionalized with a teriparatide (PTH)/strontium–zinc phosphate (SrZnP) hybrid coating. This multifunctional hydrogel was fabricated via enzymatic and ionic coordination crosslinking, yielding improved mechanical properties and sustained release of Zn2+, Sr2+, and PTH. In vitro evaluations demonstrated that the hydrogel enhanced BMSC proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and mineralization, promoted HUVEC migration, tube formation, and angiogenic marker expression, and simultaneously inhibited osteoclastogenesis and bacterial growth. Transcriptomic analysis and inhibitor experiments revealed a dual paracrine mechanism mediating bone–vascular coupling: BMSC-derived HIF-1α–VEGF signaling facilitated angiogenesis, while HUVEC-derived PI3K–Akt–BMP-2 signaling enhanced osteogenesis. In vivo, the PTH/SrZnP@ZnCu-GGP hydrogel significantly accelerated bone regeneration and neovascularization in an ovariectomized rat calvarial defect model, accompanied by upregulated expression of BMP-2, RUNX2, p-Akt, and CD31. Collectively, this injectable hydrogel system offers a robust and translationally feasible strategy for coordinated osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling, osteoclast suppression, and antibacterial defense, thus holding strong potential for the regeneration of osteoporotic bone defects.

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is one of the most common metabolic bone diseases among the elderly population and has become a major global public health concern due to the accelerating aging of society [1,2]. According to multicenter epidemiological surveys, the number of individuals with osteoporosis is projected to exceed 120 million worldwide by 2050, placing a substantial burden on both individuals and healthcare systems [3]. Osteoporosis is characterized by reduced bone mass, disrupted trabecular microarchitecture, and decreased mechanical strength, all of which led to increased bone fragility and a significantly elevated risk of fracture [4,5]. Particularly under osteoporotic conditions, bone regeneration after fracture is highly compromised due to impaired osteogenic activity, insufficient angiogenesis, and hyperactivation of osteoclasts [6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new biomaterials with excellent osteogenic capacity and mechanical integrity to accelerate the healing of osteoporotic bone defects and meet growing clinical demands.

It is well recognized that bone homeostasis relies on the coordinated regulation among osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and the vascular network [7,8]. However, this balance is severely disrupted in the osteoporotic microenvironment. Excessive osteoclastic activity enhances bone resorption, while osteoblastic function and bone formation capacity are markedly suppressed. Simultaneously, angiogenic capacity is significantly reduced, impairing local nutrient supply and signaling communication [9]. Studies have shown that patients with osteoporosis have markedly fewer blood vessels and lower expression of vascular markers such as CD31 and VEGF compared with healthy individuals [6]. Furthermore, osteoclasts secrete large amounts of hydrogen ions (H+), leading to acidification of the local bone microenvironment (pH ~ 4.0), which further activates matrix metalloproteinases (e.g., MMP-9) and cathepsin K (CTSK), thereby exacerbating bone degradation [10,11]. Therefore, effective repair of osteoporotic bone defects requires the simultaneous promotion of osteogenesis and angiogenesis, as well as regulation of osteoclast activity to restore microenvironmental balance and support constructive bone remodeling.

Hydrogels, owing to their biomimetic three-dimensional network structure, favorable rheological properties, and excellent biocompatibility, have emerged as promising scaffolds for bone repair [12,13]. In particular, injectable hydrogels can undergo in situ gelation and be delivered minimally invasively, allowing them to conform to irregular defect geometries and serve as local delivery vehicles for cells, drugs, or bioactive agents [14,15]. Hydrogels based on natural polymers such as gelatin and hyaluronic acid have been widely applied in tissue engineering due to their inherent bioactivity and cell-adhesive properties [16]. However, they typically suffer from poor mechanical strength, with Young's modulus generally in the low megapascal range, making them unsuitable for load-bearing applications [17]. Although various strategies such as double-network construction and ionic crosslinking have been employed to enhance their mechanical properties, these often result in prolonged gelation time or excessive osmotic pressure, limiting their further clinical application [18,19]. Therefore, enhancing the structural integrity and functionality of hydrogels while preserving their biocompatibility remains a critical challenge in the field.

To address this issue, recent efforts have focused on incorporating functional components into hydrogel matrices to synergistically improve both mechanical performance and bioactivity. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and free metal ions have been commonly used to prepare composite hydrogels [20,21]; however, MOFs tend to have poor structural stability and complex degradation pathways, and they may cause toxicity [22]. Similarly, free metal ions can be released rapidly and accumulate at high local concentrations, potentially triggering cytotoxicity [23]. In contrast, pure metal nanoparticles offer structural stability, defined composition, and controlled degradation behavior, making them more suitable as functional fillers for hydrogel enhancement.

In this study, we present, to our knowledge, the first integration of phosphocreatine-grafted gelatin (GGP), strontium–zinc phosphate (SrZnP)-coated Zinc–copper (ZnCu) particles, and teriparatide (PTH) into a single injectable hydrogel platform. This design was rationally conceived to address the multifactorial challenge of osteoporotic bone defects by simultaneously promoting osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling, inhibiting osteoclast activity, and providing antibacterial protection. ZnCu alloy nanoparticles with uniform particle size and good dispersion were fabricated via ball milling and incorporated into gelatin/hyaluronic acid-based hydrogels to improve their mechanical strength and gelation stability. Although these nanoparticles exhibit excellent functional potential, unmodified metal particles often suffer from poor integration within the hydrogel network and tend to undergo rapid degradation in aqueous environments, leading to sudden surges in local ion concentrations and potential cytotoxic effects. To overcome these limitations, we engineered a hybrid coating of SrZnP on the surface of the ZnCu nanoparticles and further loaded them with PTH, a clinically approved osteoanabolic agent, thereby enabling dual control over particle degradation and therapeutic function [24]. The ZnSrP coating enhances the interfacial compatibility between the nanoparticles and the hydrogel matrix, contributing to improved gelation stability [24]. In addition, it serves as an inorganic platform for sustained release of Zn2+, Sr2+, and PTH, reducing the risk of burst release. Notably, both Zn2+ and Sr2+ have been reported to promote osteogenesis, inhibit osteoclast activity, and support angiogenesis [[25], [26], [27]]. PTH also exerts dual regulatory effects by promoting bone formation and suppressing bone resorption [28]. The ZnSrP matrix helps to stabilize PTH molecules and prolong their local release, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

This composite design not only resolves the issues of poor particle–matrix integration and uncontrollable ion release but also lays the foundation for achieving coordinated “osteogenesis–angiogenesis–anti-osteoclastogenesis” functionality. Additionally, to improve the biocompatibility and structural stability of the hydrogel system, microbial transglutaminase (TGase) was employed as an alternative crosslinking strategy for gelatin/hyaluronic acid-based networks. Unlike conventional chemical crosslinkers, TGase enables a mild and enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking process under physiological conditions, avoiding the generation of toxic by-products and preserving the activity of incorporated bioactive agents. This enzymatic crosslinking approach not only enhances the mechanical integrity and injectability of the hydrogel, but also provides a biocompatible environment suitable for therapeutic delivery and in vivo applications.

Taken together, the injectable PTH/SrZnP@ZnCu-functionalized GGP hydrogel developed in this study offers enhanced mechanical resilience, controlled biodegradability, and potent biological functionality. Materials and methods

Materials

The Zn–4Cu alloy was provided by Branden Medical Devices Co., Ltd. (China), as described in previous studies [24]. The alloy was cut into 1 × 1 × 1 mm cubic blocks and subsequently subjected to ball milling using a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP2) at a rotation speed of 1600 rpm.

1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). phosphocreatine (Pcr; ≥92 %, CAS No. 67-07-2, Cat# P333235) was obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gelatin from porcine skin (Type A, CAS No. 9000-70-8, Product No. V900863) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich VETEC (USA). N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Teriparatide (Cat# HY-P0059, MCE, USA). Microbial transglutaminase derived from Streptoverticillium mobaraense (TG, 200 U/g) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotech, Inc. (Shanghai, China).

Phosphocreatine-grafted injectable hydrogel synthesis

Gelatin (5 g) was dissolved in 150 mL of deionized (DI) water under constant stirring at 40 °C. Simultaneously, 10 mmol of Pcr (2.1 g) was dissolved in a mixture of 125 mL DI water and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) at a volume ratio of 3:2. To activate the carboxyl groups of creatine phosphate, 1.91 g of EDC (10 mmol) and 1.6 g of NHS (13.9 mmol) were added to the solution, which was magnetically stirred for 1 h at 25 °C, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5. The activated Pcr solution was then added to the gelatin solution and stirred overnight in a water bath at 40 °C. Following the reaction, 0.5 wt% TG was added to the mixture to initiate enzymatic crosslinking, and the solution was incubated at 37 °C for approximately 5 min to form a hydrogel, referred to as GGP (gelatin-grafted phosphocreatine hydrogel).

To prepare particle-reinforced composite hydrogels, unmodified ZnCu particles (250 mg/mL) or surface-modified SrZnP@ZnCu particles (at final concentrations of 50, 250, or 500 mg/mL, corresponding to 0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 wt%) were added to the GGP precursor solution. The suspension was gently stirred to ensure uniform dispersion of the particles within the hydrogel matrix. Subsequently, 0.5 wt% TG was added to initiate enzymatic crosslinking, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for approximately 5 min to form the hydrogel. The resulting nanocomposite hydrogels were then dialyzed against DI water for 7 days to remove unreacted components, followed by lyophilization to obtain white flocculent products. The final hydrogels were designated as ZnCu@GGP and PTH/SrZnP(0.5, 2.5, 5.0)@GGP, depending on the particle type and concentration.

Materials characterization

The morphological, structural, and compositional characteristics of the particles and hydrogels were comprehensively analyzed. The morphology and internal structure of ZnCu and PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL-2100, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The particles were dispersed in ethanol and dropped onto a copper grid prior to imaging. Both high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were performed to visualize the lattice fringes and internal morphology, respectively. Elemental distribution of the modified particles was further investigated via energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping under STEM mode to confirm the presence and spatial localization of Zn, C, O, Sr, and P elements. The phase structure of the particles and composite hydrogels was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD, X'Pert PRO, PANalytical, Almelo, Netherlands) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation, with data collected at a scanning rate of 10°/min. The chemical composition of the particles and hydrogels was characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet IS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The XPS system was operated with a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source (1486.6 eV) at 12 kV and 15 mA under a vacuum of 2 × 10−7 Pa. Prior to FTIR and XPS analysis, the hydrogel samples were lyophilized. For XPS measurements, high-resolution spectra of Zn 2p, Sr 3d, N 1s, C 1s, and O 1s were acquired and subsequently fitted using XPSPeak 4.1 software. The surface morphology of the freeze-dried hydrogels was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7401F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), and their elemental distribution was analyzed via EDS coupled to the SEM system. Before observation, hydrogel samples were lyophilized and sputter-coated with gold.

The macroscopic appearance of the hydrogels was recorded using a digital camera. Rheological measurements were conducted on a Rheometric Scientific HAAKE rheometer (Malvern, UK) at 37 °C. To initiate gelation, 270 μL of the GGP precursor solution was mixed with 0.5 wt% microbial transglutaminase (TG) and either unmodified ZnCu particles (250 mg/mL) or PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles (50, 250, or 500 mg/mL). The mixture was gently stirred and immediately transferred onto the quartz plate (20 mm diameter, 1 mm gap) of the rheometer. Time sweep analysis was performed under 1 % strain, while frequency sweep measurements were conducted on preformed hydrogels over a range of 0.1–10 Hz.

To evaluate swelling capacity, cylindrical hydrogel samples (diameter 8 mm, height 10 mm) were immersed in deionized water or PBS at 37 °C, and their morphology was recorded before and after swelling. For degradation studies, hydrogels were immersed in PBS under gentle shaking at 37 °C for up to 21 days, with the buffer refreshed every 3 days. The initial dry weight (W0) and the dry weight at each time point (W1) were used to calculate the remaining mass ratio (W1/W0 × 100 %). In addition, the concentrations of released Zn2+ and Sr2+ ions in the degradation medium at each time point were quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent 730, Agilent Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). To assess in vivo degradation, sterilized hydrogel discs of GGP, ZnCu@GGP, and PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP were implanted subcutaneously in the dorsal region of female Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 6 per group). After 36 days, the explanted specimens were carefully washed, freeze-dried, and weighed to calculate the remaining mass relative to their initial dry weight.

Mechanical properties were assessed using rectangular and cylindrical samples. Torsional flexibility was evaluated by manually twisting rectangular hydrogels (100 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm) through 360° at room temperature. Tensile testing was conducted using a universal mechanical testing machine (MX-0580, Moxin, Jiangsu, China) at a rate of 20 mm/min. Cylindrical samples (diameter 8 mm, height 10 mm) were subjected to compression tests using the same machine at a rate of 5 mm/min.

Cell culture

Mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs, Procell, Cat# CP-M194), human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Procell, Cat# CP-H096), and RAW264.7 murine macrophages (CBCAS, Cat# SCSP-5036) were used in this study. BMSCs and RAW264.7 cells were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, Cat# 12561-056) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Cat# 10099-141) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122). HUVECs were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Cat# 12391-021) containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 and the medium was changed every 2 days.

Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining

BMSCs and HUVECs were seeded in the lower chambers of 24-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well), with hydrogel discs (diameter 8 mm, thickness ∼2 mm) placed in 0.4 μm pore size Transwell inserts (Corning, Cat# 3413) for indirect co-culture. After 48 h, inserts were removed and cells were stained using the Calcein-AM/PI viability kit (Beyotime, Cat# C2015S). Live cells were stained green and dead cells red. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and the percentage of dead cells was quantified with ImageJ.

Cytoskeletal staining

After 24 h of co-culture, BMSCs and HUVECs in the lower chamber were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with rhodamine–phalloidin (Beyotime, Cat# C1033) to label F-actin and DAPI (Beyotime, Cat# C1005) for nuclear staining. Fluorescent images were taken under a fluorescence microscope and the F-actin–positive area was quantified using ImageJ to assess cell spreading and adhesion.

CCK-8 proliferation assay

Cell proliferation of BMSCs and HUVECs was evaluated on days 1, 3, and 5 using a CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Cat# CK04). After removing hydrogel inserts, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well containing 100 μL medium and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in triplicate.

LDH release assay

To evaluate membrane integrity and potential cytotoxicity of hydrogel samples, the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China, Cat# C0016). HUVECs and BMSCs were seeded into the lower chamber of 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well, and hydrogel discs were placed in the upper Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, Cat# 3413) for indirect co-culture. At days 1, 3, and 5, 200 μL of culture medium was collected from each well and incubated with the LDH reaction mixture according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 30 min at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. LDH levels were normalized to the control group and expressed as percentage values (% of control), serving as an indicator of membrane damage and hydrogel-associated cytotoxicity.

Antibacterial assay

Hydrogel samples were placed in 24-well plates and inoculated with 100 μL of bacterial suspension (106 CFU/mL) of Staphylococcus aureus or Escherichia coli (BeNa Culture Collection, China). After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and plated on LB agar for colony counting. Inhibition efficiency was calculated relative to a control group. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

In vitro osteoclast formation assay

RAW264.7 murine macrophages (CBCAS, Cat# SCSP-5036) were seeded into the lower chamber of 24-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well in α-MEM (Gibco, Cat# 12561-056) containing 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, and 50 ng/mL RANKL (Peprotech, Cat# 315-11). Hydrogel samples were placed in the upper chamber using Transwell inserts and co-cultured for 5 days. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was performed using a commercial kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 387A), and TRAP-positive areas were quantified using ImageJ. TRAP enzymatic activity was assessed using the pNPP substrate method (Beyotime, Cat# P0326) by measuring absorbance at 405 nm.

Osteogenic differentiation assays

To evaluate osteogenic differentiation, BMSCs were seeded in the lower chamber of a Transwell system at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and co-cultured with different hydrogel samples placed in the upper inserts for 7 or 14 days in osteogenic induction medium (α-MEM containing 10 % FBS, 1 % P/S, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, and 10 nM dexamethasone; all from Gibco). On day 7, cells were fixed and stained for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity using an ALP staining kit (Beyotime, Cat# C3206), and ALP activity was quantified using a colorimetric assay at 405 nm. On day 14, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was performed to detect calcium deposition, followed by 10 % cetylpyridinium chloride extraction and quantification at 562 nm.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026), and reverse transcription was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, Cat# R223-01). Quantitative PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q111-02) on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences of these genes are listed in Tables S1 and S2.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

After co-culture with hydrogels for 3 days, total RNA was extracted from BMSCs or HUVECs using TRIzol reagent. RNA integrity was assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer. Library preparation was performed using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit, followed by sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed using fastp and mapped to the mouse or human genome using STAR. DEGs were identified with DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05). GO, KEGG, and GSEA were used for enrichment analysis. Heatmaps and volcano plots were generated using R packages such as pheatmap and ggplot2.

Evaluation of sustained-release eluates

To determine whether the sustained-release eluates from PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogels retained biological activity, extracts were collected on day 14 and applied to BMSCs under five experimental conditions: (i) Vehicle (α-MEM with 10 % FBS, baseline control), (ii) Fresh PTH (2 nM, positive control, MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-P0059), (iii) Eluate-2.5 (50 % v/v), (iv) Eluate-2.5 supplemented with 100 nM PTH(7–34) (a selective PTH1R antagonist, ProSpec, Cat# HOR-266), and (v) Heat-inactivated Eluate-2.5 (65 °C, 30 min). BMSCs were cultured under these conditions for 7 days, after which osteogenic differentiation was assessed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and enzymatic activity, as well as RT-qPCR analysis of osteogenic genes RUNX2 and OCN, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SD.

Scratch wound healing assay

HUVECs was seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 90 % confluence. A linear wound was created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, and cells were washed and incubated in hydrogel-conditioned or co-culture medium. Images were acquired at 0 h and 24 h post-wounding using an inverted microscope. The wound closure percentage was quantified with ImageJ.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning, Cat# 3422) were used to assess cell migration. Cells were seeded in the upper chamber with serum-free medium, and the lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10 % FBS or hydrogel-conditioned medium. After 12 h, migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1 % crystal violet, and counted under a microscope in five random fields per well.

Tube formation assay

To assess angiogenesis, 48-well plates were coated with 150 μL/well of Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 356234) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. HUVECs were resuspended in hydrogel-conditioned medium and seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well. After 6 h, capillary-like structures were imaged under an inverted microscope. Total tube length and branch points were quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Supernatants were collected from the Transwell co-culture systems after 72 h to quantify the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). In addition, cumulative release of parathyroid hormone (PTH 1–34) from the hydrogel eluates was measured at predetermined time points. ELISA kits for VEGF (Lianke Biosciences, Cat# EK283), BMP-2 (Lianke Biosciences, Cat# EK1108), TNF-α (Lianke Biosciences, Cat# EK282HS-96), IL-6 (Lianke Biosciences, Cat# EK206HS-96), and human PTH (1–34) (Arigo, Cat# ARG82253) were used following the manufacturer's protocols. Briefly, standards and samples were added to wells pre-coated with specific capture antibodies and incubated with HRP-conjugated detection antibodies. After addition of substrate solution and termination, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). All measurements were performed in triplicate and expressed as mean ± SD.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Cat# P1050). Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# P0012). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5 % non-fat milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-VEGF (Proteintech, Cat# 19003-1-AP), anti-HIF-1α (Proteintech, Cat# 20960-1-AP), anti-PI3K (CST, Cat# 4257), anti-phospho-PI3K (CST, Cat# 4228), anti-Akt (CST, Cat# 4691), anti-phospho-Akt (CST, Cat# 4060), and anti-GAPDH (CST, Cat# 2118). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, Cat# 7074 or 7076) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo, Cat# 32106) and quantified by Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, HUVECs were seeded on glass coverslips and treated with different hydrogel samples for 3 days. Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 3 % BSA for 1 h. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 (Abcam, Cat# ab76533) or VEGF (Abcam, Cat# ab46154). After washing, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Cat# A11008) for 1 h at room temperature. F-actin was stained using TRITC–phalloidin (Beyotime, Cat# C1038), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, Cat# C1005). Coverslips were mounted with antifade reagent and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Dual-chamber transwell co-culture setup and pathway inhibition strategy

To investigate whether PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogels regulate bone–vascular coupling through paracrine interactions between BMSCs and HUVECs, a dual-chamber Transwell co-culture system (0.8 μm pore size, Corning, Cat#3422) was established, comprising six experimental groups for each observation model. For the HUVEC observation model (HUVECs seeded in the upper chamber), the lower chamber was assigned to the following conditions: (1) blank control (no hydrogel or cells), (2) BMSCs only, (3) H-PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogel alone, (4) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + 2-MeOE2 (1 μM, Selleck Chemicals, Cat#S1404, added to the upper chamber to inhibit HIF-1α signaling in HUVECs), (5) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + BMSCs, and (6) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + BMSCs + 2-MeOE2 (1 μM, added to the lower chamber to block HIF-1α in BMSCs). For the BMSC observation model (BMSCs in the upper chamber), the lower chamber was treated with: (1) blank control, (2) HUVECs only, (3) B-PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogel + HUVECs, (4) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + LY294002 (10 μM, Selleck Chemicals, Cat#S1105, added to the upper chamber to inhibit PI3K in BMSCs), (5) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + HUVECs, and (6) PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP + HUVECs + LY294002 (10 μM, added to the lower chamber to inhibit PI3K in HUVECs). All co-cultures were maintained for 72 h. After incubation, cells were subjected to wound healing assays, Transwell migration assays, tube formation assays, ALP staining, quantitative PCR (qPCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to evaluate osteogenic and angiogenic responses as well as paracrine factor secretion.

Ovariectomy-induced osteoporotic rat model

Twelve-week-old female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (230–250g) were subjected to bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) under isoflurane anesthesia (2 %) to induce postmenopausal osteoporosis (Fig. S1). After surgery, animals were housed in a controlled environment with free access to food and water for 12 weeks. Osteoporotic status was confirmed by decreased femoral bone mineral density (BMD), assessed using preoperative micro-CT scanning (Scanco Medical, μCT80).

Calvarial bone defect creation and hydrogel implantation

Twelve weeks post-OVX, a 5 mm diameter full-thickness calvarial defect was created on each rat using a dental trephine drill under anesthesia (Fig. S2). Hemostasis was achieved using sterile gauze. Rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6): Control (blank), GGP, ZnCu@GGP, and PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP. Sterile hydrogel discs (5 mm diameter) were gently placed into the defects. After implantation, the periosteum and skin were sutured in layers, and rats were allowed to recover with analgesic administration (meloxicam, 1 mg/kg/day for 3 days).

Micro-CT analysis

At 4 and 12 weeks post-implantation, rats were euthanized and the calvarial bones were harvested and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Samples were scanned using a micro-CT system (Scanco μCT80) with a resolution of 10 μm, at 70 kVp and 114 μA. Three-dimensional reconstructions were performed, and the following parameters were quantitatively analyzed using Scanco software: bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Histological staining

At 12 weeks post-implantation, calvarial specimens were harvested and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by decalcification in 10 % EDTA (pH 7.4) for 4 weeks. Samples were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate general tissue morphology. Masson's trichrome and Goldner's trichrome staining were used to assess collagen fiber deposition and mineralized bone formation, respectively. Stained sections were imaged using a digital slide scanner (Leica SCN400), and regions of newly formed bone (NB) and remaining material (RM) were identified.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

For immunohistochemical analysis, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3 % hydrogen peroxide. Sections were blocked with 5 % goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-BMP-2 (Proteintech, Cat# 18933-1-AP), anti-RUNX2 (Abcam, Cat# ab192256), anti-p-Akt (CST, Cat# 4060), anti-CD31 (Abcam, Cat# ab76533), anti-CD68 (Abcam, Cat# ab213363), and anti-TNF-α (Abcam, Cat# ab6671). After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using DAB substrate. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Images were captured using a Leica DM6 light microscope. Integrated optical density (IOD) of positively stained areas was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software in at least three random fields per section.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons were performed using either Student's t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for non-parametric data). Multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA) and R software (version 4.2.1). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism and the ggplot2 package in R. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of synthesized PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles

Fig. 1 shows the comprehensive physicochemical characterization of ZnCu and PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles, as well as the structural validation of their successful integration into the GGP hydrogel matrix. As illustrated in the schematic (Fig. 1A), Zn–Cu alloy particles were surface-modified with a SrZnP hybrid coating incorporating PTH, forming PTH/SrZnP-coated ZnCu particles. As shown in the TEM images (Fig. 1B), the ZnCu particles exhibited an average diameter of approximately 2 μm, and the PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles retained a similar size after surface modification. The corresponding STEM-EDS mapping further confirmed the presence and homogeneous surface distribution of Zn, Sr, P, and O elements in the PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles, demonstrating the successful coating of the ZnCu surface with the SrZnP layer and PTH. These observations were further supported by the XRD analysis (Fig. 1D), which displayed characteristic diffraction peaks matching those of Zn, ZnSr(HPO3)2·2H2O, Zn3(PO4)2·4H2O, and SrHPO4. These spectral features verify the successful chemical integration of PTH and SrZnP within the coating. Characterization of hydrogel

The gelation behavior and structural integration of GGP, ZnCu@GGP, PTH/SrZnP(0.5, 2.5, 5.0)@GGP samples were systematically evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1G, the precursor solution underwent a rapid sol–gel transition upon the addition of TG, forming a stable hydrogel network within minutes at physiological temperature. Notably, the incorporation of ZnCu or PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles further accelerated the gelation process, indicating enhanced crosslinking efficiency. Rheological time-sweep analysis (Fig. 1H) revealed a rapid increase in storage modulus (G′), signifying the rapid formation of a crosslinked hydrogel network. Frequency-sweep measurements further confirmed the viscoelastic properties of the hydrogels, with G′ consistently higher than the loss modulus (G″) across the tested frequency range.

The microstructure of the lyophilized hydrogels was examined via SEM in Fig. 1I. All hydrogels exhibited a porous architecture, while those loaded with particles showed denser and more interconnected pore structures, indicating enhanced mechanical integrity and network stability. The corresponding 10.13039/100004679EDS spectra (insert in demonstrated the homogeneous distribution of Zn, Sr, and P elements in the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group, supporting the successful incorporation of particles into the hydrogel network (Fig. S3). XRD pattern in Fig. 1J further revealed partially retained diffraction peaks corresponding to ZnCu and SrZnP crystalline phases, indicating the stable physical embedding of particles within the hydrogel matrix.

FTIR in Fig. 2A confirmed the chemical integration of the gelatin, Pcr, and PTH components, as evidenced by the presence of characteristic functional group peaks from each. The XPS survey and high-resolution spectra (Fig. 2B–G) provided further insight into the elemental composition and chemical bonding states of the composite hydrogels. Specifically, Zn, Sr, P, O, N, and C were clearly detected in PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP, in line with the EDS results. As shown in Fig. 2D, Sr 3d peaks were only observed in PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP and not in ZnCu@GGP, confirming the successful introduction of strontium into the hydrogel. Moreover, the high-resolution N 1s (Fig. 2E) and O 1s (Fig. 2F) spectra revealed signals corresponding to Zn–N and Zn–O coordination bonds, suggesting possible interactions between the metal particles and functional groups on the hydrogel backbone. Mechanical and rheological properties

The mechanical performance and swelling behavior of the composite hydrogels were further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2H, the stress–strain curves demonstrated that both ZnCu@GGP and PTH/SrZnP@GGP hydrogels exhibited significantly higher compressive strength than the pristine GGP hydrogel. Moreover, the compressive strength increased with the concentration of PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles (0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 mg/mL), indicating that the dual crosslinking mechanism: comprising enzymatic crosslinking by TG and additional coordination bonding between metal ions and hydrogel functional groups—produced a more robust and reinforced hydrogel network compared to enzymatic crosslinking alone. Fig. 2I and J illustrate the excellent structural resilience of the hydrogels under torsion. After being twisted 360°, both GGP and composite hydrogels recovered to their original shape without visible edge cracking, suggesting good elasticity and network integrity. Representative macroscopic images of the hydrogel samples during compression (Fig. 2I and J) further confirmed their deformation resistance and recoverability under external load.

The swelling behavior of the hydrogels was assessed in deionized water, as shown in Fig. 2K and Fig. S4. All hydrogels exhibited similar initial volumes before swelling. After immersion, the size of PTH/SrZnP@GGP hydrogels (at 0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 mg/mL) remained nearly unchanged, suggesting improved resistance to excessive expansion. In contrast, a slight volume increase was observed in the GGP and ZnCu@GGP groups. These results suggest that the incorporation of PTH/SrZnP-ZnCu particles not only enhances mechanical strength but also contributes to dimensional stability in aqueous environments.

In vitro and in vivo degradation of hydrogel

The cumulative release profiles of Sr2+ and Zn2+ ions are shown in Fig. 2L and M, while the corresponding PTH release curves are presented in Fig. S5. As the concentration of PTH/SrZnP–ZnCu particles (0.5, 2.5, and 5.0 mg/mL) incorporated into the GGP hydrogel increased, the release rate and total amount of Sr2+, Zn2+, and PTH also increased accordingly. All three agents exhibited a sustained release pattern over 21 days, with Sr2+ and Zn2+ approaching a plateau around day 14, whereas PTH showed a slightly more prolonged release. The 2.5 mg/mL group maintained a stable PTH level for over two weeks, aligning with the effective concentration range indicated by our in vitro osteogenesis data, while the 5.0 mg/mL group reached higher levels that corresponded with increased cytotoxicity in LDH assays. The corresponding in vitro and in vivo degradation behaviors of the hydrogels are shown in Fig. 2N and O, respectively. Both profiles revealed progressive mass loss over time, with faster degradation in vivo. Notably, PTH/SrZnP@GGP hydrogels retained a significantly higher residual mass than GGP and ZnCu@GGP in both conditions, indicating that the phosphate-based coating enhanced structural stability and delayed degradation in physiological environments.

Antibacterial performance was assessed using E. coli and S. aureus. Eluate bioactivity confirms functional contribution of PTH

To confirm that the bioactivity of hydrogel eluates originated from sustained PTH release, BMSCs were treated with different eluate formulations. RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. S7A) demonstrated that both Fresh PTH and Eluate-2.5 significantly upregulated RUNX2 and OCN expression compared with Vehicle (∗∗∗p < 0.001). Importantly, the osteogenic effect of Eluate-2.5 was markedly diminished upon addition of the PTH1R antagonist PTH(7–34) or after heat inactivation, indicating that the active component was PTH rather than inorganic ions. Consistent with these findings, ALP staining and activity assays (Fig. S7B and C) revealed that Eluate-2.5 induced strong ALP activity, comparable to fresh PTH, whereas the effect was largely abrogated in the antagonist and heat-inactivated groups. Together, these results confirm that PTH released from the hydrogel retains biological activity and plays a critical role in mediating the osteogenic effects of the composite system.

In the BMSC observation model (Fig. 8F–I), PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP combined with HUVECs enhanced BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation, as demonstrated by increased ALP activity and upregulated osteogenic marker expression. Importantly, application of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 to the lower chamber (HUVEC side) significantly diminished these osteogenic effects, whereas inhibition in the upper chamber (BMSC side) showed minimal impact. This suggests that PI3K-dependent paracrine signaling from hydrogel-activated HUVECs—such as secretion of BMP-2—contributes to enhanced osteogenesis in BMSCs. Supporting this mechanism, BMP-2 mRNA levels in BMSCs and BMP-2 protein concentrations in the medium were significantly reduced following PI3K inhibition (Fig. 8I–K).

Collectively, these results highlight a dual paracrine feedback mechanism mediated by PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP: HIF-1α activation in BMSCs induces VEGF secretion that promotes endothelial angiogenesis, while PI3K pathway activation in HUVECs stimulates BMP-2 release that supports BMSC osteogenesis. This reciprocal interaction establishes a coordinated bone–vascular coupling loop, which is critical for functional regeneration of osteoporotic bone defects.

In vivo evaluation of osteoporotic bone regeneration using PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogels

To assess the bone regenerative capacity of the hydrogels under osteoporotic conditions, a bilateral OVX model was established in Sprague-Dawley rats, and osteoporosis was confirmed 12 weeks later by micro-CT analysis of the proximal tibia, showing significant bone loss and reduced BMD (Fig. S8). Following OVX confirmation, 5 mm critical-sized calvarial defects were surgically created, and different hydrogel formulations were injected into the defects as illustrated in the experimental timeline (Fig. 9A). Micro-CT analysis at 4 and 12 weeks post-implantation revealed minimal bone regeneration in the blank and GGP groups. The ZnCu@GGP group exhibited moderate improvement, whereas PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP achieved the most substantial bone formation, characterized by nearly complete defect bridging and dense trabecular architecture (Fig. 9B). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) in the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group compared to all other groups at both time points (Fig. 9C and D). In addition, this group showed increased trabecular number (Tb.N) and thickness (Tb.Th) (Fig. 9E–G), and decreased trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 9F), indicating superior bone quality and structural integrity. Histological and IHC evidence of enhanced bone regeneration

To further verify the bone regeneration potential and elucidate the associated signaling mechanisms, histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were performed on the calvarial sections harvested at 12 weeks post-implantation (Fig. 10). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed limited new bone (NB) tissue in the Control and GGP groups, while the ZnCu@GGP group showed moderate NB formation. In contrast, the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group exhibited substantial NB development, with good integration into surrounding host tissue. This finding was corroborated by Masson's trichrome and Goldner's staining, which revealed enhanced collagen deposition and mineralized bone matrix in the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group. IHC staining results further supported these findings. Elevated expression of osteogenic markers BMP-2 and RUNX2 was observed in the ZnCu@GGP and PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP groups, with the latter showing the highest intensity (Fig. 10B–D). In vivo biosafety evaluation of PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogel

To assess early immune responses, immunohistochemical analysis was performed at 4 weeks post-implantation. As shown in Fig. S9, CD68 staining (Fig. S9A) revealed macrophage infiltration levels comparable across groups, while TNF-α staining (Fig. S9B) showed no excessive pro-inflammatory response in the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group relative to controls. In addition, serum cytokine profiling (Fig. S9C) confirmed that systemic TNF-α and IL-6 levels remained within physiological ranges, suggesting that the hydrogel did not induce overt local or systemic inflammation in the subacute phase. To further evaluate long-term systemic biosafety, multiple assessments were performed at 12 weeks post-implantation. ICP–MS analysis indicated no abnormal accumulation of Zn2+ or Sr2+ in major organs including the brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Figs. S10 and S11). Hematological and serum biochemical parameters (WBC, RBC, HGB, PLT, ALT, AST, BUN, Cr) showed no significant differences among groups, remaining within normal physiological ranges (Figs. S12 and S13). Moreover, histological analysis via H&E staining confirmed intact architecture of all major organs, with no evidence of inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or structural abnormalities in the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP group compared with Control, GGP, or ZnCu@GGP groups (Fig. S14). Together, these data demonstrate that the PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogel is well tolerated in vivo, without eliciting significant local or systemic adverse effects.

Discussion

Under osteoporotic conditions, the repair of critical-sized bone defects is particularly challenging due to impaired osteogenesis, insufficient angiogenesis, overactivation of osteoclasts, and an elevated risk of infection [7]. Injectable hydrogels, known for their excellent biocompatibility, injectability, and capacity to carry bioactive molecules, have been extensively applied in bone tissue engineering [15]. However, traditional hydrogel systems often suffer from poor mechanical strength, unstable release of active agents, and weak interfacial integration with inorganic components [12]. These shortcomings are especially pronounced under the highly stressed and inflammatory osteoporotic microenvironment, which can significantly compromise performance. Moreover, conventional single-crosslinking systems often fail to simultaneously regulate multiple critical aspects of bone repair, including osteogenesis, angiogenesis, anti-osteoclastogenesis, and antibacterial activity, thereby limiting their translational potential in clinical applications. Although phosphate-functionalized gelatin derivatives, creatine-based motifs, Sr/Zn-containing particles, and PTH have each been reported individually, the integration of these components into a single injectable, dual-crosslinked hydrogel—capable of spatiotemporally coordinated delivery, dual-pathway activation (PI3K/Akt and HIF-1α), and dual-mode antibacterial activity—represents a previously unreported strategy specifically tailored for osteoporotic bone repair. This distinctive combination underpins the multifunctional performance of the system and forms the basis for the subsequent mechanistic and in vivo evaluations described below.

To address these limitations, we developed a multifunctional composite hydrogel system by incorporating PTH/SrZnP-functionalized Zn–Cu alloy particles into a GGP matrix and employing a dual crosslinking strategy. Specifically, TGase catalyzed covalent crosslinking among gelatin chains, while the released Sr2+ and Zn2+ ions formed dynamic coordination bonds with carboxyl, hydroxyl, and phosphate groups to further reinforce the network. Notably, Pcr contributed both biological functionality and phosphonate groups with high metal ion affinity, enabling chelation with Zn2+ and Sr2+ [29]. This molecular-level interaction enhanced ion–matrix binding, increased crosslinking density and hydrogel stability, and improved the controlled release of therapeutic agents. The resulting hydrogel exhibited significantly improved storage modulus, compressive strength, and injectability compared with traditional physical or chemical hydrogels. Furthermore, the SrZnP coating not only enhanced particle–matrix compatibility but also enabled the sustained release of Zn2+, Sr2+, and PTH to spatiotemporally modulate key cellular behaviors and construct a regenerative microenvironment.

The sustained and controlled release of bioactive substances from the composite hydrogel played a pivotal role in remodeling the osteoporotic niche by simultaneously promoting osteogenesis, angiogenesis, anti-osteoclastogenesis, and antibacterial effects. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the hydrogel significantly enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, as evidenced by increased ALP activity, enhanced mineralized nodule formation, and upregulation of osteogenesis-related genes (RUNX2, OCN, BMP-2, ALP, OPN). Transcriptomic analysis further revealed the activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway and its downstream targets, a key regulatory axis for modulating the osteoimmune environment and osteogenic transcriptional programs [30]. This activation was closely associated with the synergistic effects of Zn2+, Sr2+, PTH, and Pcr. Previous studies have confirmed that Zn2+ promotes BMSC osteogenesis via PI3K–Akt signaling [31,32], while Sr2+ indirectly activates the pathway through the CaSR [33,34]. PTH, a clinically approved anabolic agent, enhances PI3K–Akt signaling through cAMP/PKA-mediated feedback loops [35]. Furthermore, the phosphonate groups in Pcr may contribute to ionic homeostasis and Akt phosphorylation, enhancing signaling synergy. Notably, the PI3K–Akt axis may also interact with downstream mTOR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, amplifying the commitment of osteoprogenitor cells.

Angiogenesis, a prerequisite for effective bone regeneration, becomes especially critical under osteoporotic conditions due to sparse vasculature and limited perfusion. In this study, the hydrogel markedly promoted HUVEC migration and tube formation and upregulated CD31 and VEGF expression. Transcriptome data indicated that the proangiogenic effect was mainly mediated through activation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway. HIF-1α is a master transcriptional regulator of angiogenesis that modulates VEGF-A, PDGF-BB, and other vascular-related genes, thereby facilitating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and neovascularization [36]. Zn2+ enhanced HIF-1α stabilization and VEGF expression under hypoxic conditions [32], while Sr2+ not only upregulated VEGF but also induced the formation of type-H vessels—specialized capillaries essential for osteogenic–angiogenic coupling [37,38]. Although Cu2+ release was below the detection threshold in this study, previous literature suggests its potential role in HIF-1α regulation and NO-mediated angiogenesis [39], warranting further exploration. In addition to HIF-1 signaling, the hydrogel may also activate eNOS–NO–VEGF and Notch pathways, contributing to more efficient and functionally mature vascular network formation.

To further elucidate osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling, a Transwell co-culture system was established to model BMSC–HUVEC interactions. Inhibition of PI3K-Akt signaling in BMSCs (using LY294002) or HIF-1α in HUVECs (using 2-MeOE2) led to significant downregulation of BMP-2 and VEGF, indicating a paracrine regulatory loop that amplifies intercellular signaling. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that endothelial cells secrete BMP-2 to promote osteogenesis, while BMSCs release VEGF to enhance angiogenesis [40,41]. As illustrated in Fig. 11, Zn2+, Sr2+, PTH, and Pcr were released in a spatially and temporally controlled manner, targeting both osteogenic and angiogenic cells. Zn2+ activated PI3K–Akt-VEGF in BMSCs and HIF–BMP-2 in HUVECs, enabling bidirectional crosstalk. Sr2+ supported both BMSC osteogenic differentiation and type-H vessel formation, while PTH enhanced osteogenesis via cAMP/PKA and potentially upregulated vascular factors such as VEGF and Ang-1. Beyond their individual functions, the simultaneous release of Zn2+, Sr2+, and PTH may give rise to interactive effects. Our data suggest that these factors act in a complementary manner, converging on signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and HIF-1α, which in turn promote both osteogenesis and angiogenesis. For instance, Zn2+ enhances PI3K/Akt signaling and HIF-1α stabilization, Sr2+ activates CaSR to promote osteogenesis and type-H vessel formation, while PTH strengthens osteogenic and angiogenic responses via cAMP/PKA-mediated feedback loops [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. The convergence of these pathways implies a potential synergistic effect, whereby bone–vessel regeneration is amplified beyond the sum of the individual contributions. Nevertheless, excessive ion release or disproportionate loading could potentially induce cellular stress or diminish efficacy, raising the possibility of antagonistic interactions under certain conditions. Although our current findings indirectly support synergism, systematic single- and multi-factor comparisons will be required in future studies to fully delineate synergistic versus antagonistic relationships among these bioactive components.

Importantly, this system demonstrated robust anti-resorptive activity, addressing a central pathological feature of osteoporotic defects. TRAP staining confirmed that the hydrogel inhibited osteoclast formation, likely through modulation of the RANK/RANKL/OPG axis by Zn2+ and Sr2+ [25,42]. PTH may have further enhanced this dual anabolic–anti-catabolic effect, promoting a regenerative microenvironment [43]. Sr2+ may also exert anti-osteoclast effects via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, contributing to balanced bone remodeling [44]. Additionally, the hydrogel exhibited antibacterial properties, which are advantageous for managing postoperative infection risks. In vitro assays revealed significant inhibition of S. aureus and E. coli, potentially due to the disruption of bacterial membranes and redox systems by Zn2+ and trace Cu2+ [45,46]. Although Cu2+ release was not quantifiable in this study, its localized antimicrobial contribution remains a possibility worth further investigation.

As one of the limitations of this study, while the multifunctional PTH/SrZnP(2.5)@GGP hydrogel demonstrated promising osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling and safety in osteoporotic defects, certain challenges remain for clinical translation. The system integrates multiple bioactive agents and employs enzymatic crosslinking, which, while scientifically advantageous, may raise concerns regarding scalability and regulatory approval. Each component was deliberately selected from materials with established biocompatibility profiles (and clinical approval in the case of PTH), and the enzymatic crosslinking (TGase) is already used in the food and biomedical sectors [47,48]. Nevertheless, future work will focus on simplifying the formulation by retaining only the most essential functional components, adopting modular manufacturing of the hydrogel base and bioactive-loaded particles, and utilizing GRAS/GMP-compliant materials to facilitate scale-up and approval pathways.

Another limitation is the absence of direct head-to-head comparisons with clinically used bone graft materials under identical osteoporotic defect conditions. Most existing grafts are designed for normal or traumatic bone repair and lack targeted functionalities—such as simultaneous osteogenesis—angiogenesis promotion, osteoclast inhibition, and antibacterial activity—necessary for osteoporotic bone healing. Although our in vivo data compare favorably with literature-reported outcomes for β-TCP or PTH-loaded collagen scaffolds, future studies will conduct standardized benchmarking in both small- and large-animal osteoporotic models, including load-bearing environments, to further define the translational competitiveness of our system [49,50]. Finally, although our mechanistic studies using pathway inhibitors support the involvement of PI3K/Akt and HIF-1α in both paracrine coupling and cell-intrinsic responses, the complexity of the multi-component hydrogel makes it difficult to fully resolve the contribution of each individual component. Future investigations employing genetic knockdown strategies or simplified formulations will be valuable for dissecting component-specific roles and refining the mechanistic framework. In addition, although ICP–MS analysis at 12 weeks did not show abnormal accumulation of Zn2+ or Sr2+ in major organs, more detailed pharmacokinetic investigations are needed to fully characterize systemic distribution, metabolic fate, and excretion pathways of the metal-based components. Future studies will therefore focus on longitudinal clearance profiling to further strengthen translational relevance.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a multifunctional hydrogel platform by incorporating SrZnP@ZnCu loaded with PTH into a GGP. This rational design not only significantly enhanced the mechanical integrity and gelation stability of the hydrogel, but also enabled controlled and sustained release of bioactive components (Zn2+, Sr2+ and PTH), thereby coordinating key regenerative processes. In vitro studies demonstrated excellent cytocompatibility, robust osteogenic and angiogenic activities, potent antibacterial effects against S. aureus and E. coli, and effective inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. Mechanistically, the hydrogel activated PI3K–Akt and HIF-1α signaling pathways, facilitating bone–vascular coupling and enhancing the expression of osteogenic and angiogenic factors. In vivo, the hydrogel significantly improved bone regeneration in an osteoporotic rat calvarial defect model, as evidenced by increased bone volume, mineral density, and neovascularization. Taken together, this study presents a promising biomaterial strategy that integrates structural reinforcement with multiple therapeutic functions, offering substantial translational potential for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects.
