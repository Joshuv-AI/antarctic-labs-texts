A pilot study to identify pharmacogenomic and clinical risk factors associated with opioid related falls and adverse effects in older adults
Authors: Sophia Sheikh, Rosemarie Fernandez, Carmen Smotherman, Jennifer Brailsford, Taimour Langaee, Esteban Velasquez, Morgan Henson, Taylor Munson, Andrew Bertrand, Phyllis Hendry, Stephen Anton, Roger B Fillingim, Larisa H Cavallari
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the high prevalence of pain in older adults and current trends in opioid prescribing, inclusion of genetic information in risk prediction tools may improve opioid risk assessment. Our objectives were to (1) determine the feasibility of recruiting socioeconomically disadvantaged and racially diverse middle aged and older adult populations for a study seeking to identify risk factors for opioid‐related falls and other serious adverse effects and (2) explore potential associations between the Risk Index for Overdose or Serious Opioid‐induced Respiratory Depression (CIP‐RIOSORD) risk class and other patient factors with falls and serious opioid adverse effects. This was an observational study of 44 participants discharged home from the emergency department with an opioid prescription for acute pain and followed for 30 days. We found pain interference may predict opioid‐related falls or serious adverse effects within older, opioid‐treated patients. If validated, pain interference may prove to be a beneficial marker for risk stratification of older adults initiated on opioids for acute pain.

INTRODUCTION

Opioid prescription rates in the United States have recently declined due to increased clinician awareness and education on the causes leading to the opioid epidemic. However, prescription fill rates among older adults have declined the least and are higher than any other age group. 1 This trend is troublesome given the high prevalence of pain in older adults and their susceptibility to opioid‐related adverse events. 1 Adults initiated on psychotropic drugs, like opioids, are at increased risk for falling and fall‐related fractures, particularly within the first week of initiation. 2 , 3 They are also at greater risk for complications after an opioid overdose (OD; traumatic injury, respiratory depression, death, and hospitalization). 4 , 5 OD deaths have exponentially increased within this population over the past decades, with the largest increase in older African American men. 6

Multiple factors increase older adults' risk for falls and opioid adverse events, including opioid effects on the central nervous system resulting in respiratory depression, sedation, and impaired reaction time. 7 , 8 , 9 Fall risk is highest shortly after opioid initiation and is strongest in the first week. 2 , 3 , 10 Medical history of falls, unsteady gait, neuropathy, polypharmacy, and consumption of central nervous system altering medications are reported to increase the fall risk. 9 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

There are limited tools available for identifying short‐term risk for a serious opioid adverse event in acute pain populations. The Risk Index for Overdose or Serious Opioid‐induced Respiratory Depression (RIOSORD) is one such tool. RIOSORD was originally derived using Veterans Health Administration data (VHA‐based RIOSORD) but was found to have excellent predictive accuracy when tested using prescription data from a commercially insured health plan (CIP). However, the CIP‐based RIOSORD was derived using retrospective data and prescription opioid fill rates rather than confirming opioid consumption. 20 Medications included in CIP‐RIOSORD are also associated with fall risk (opioids, benzodiazepines, and antidepressants). 15 This suggests the CIP‐RIOSORD tool may be able to detect patients at risk for falls in addition to opioid overdose.

Given the relatively high prevalence of pain in older adults and current trends in opioid prescribing, inclusion of genetic information in risk prediction tools may improve risk assessment in this vulnerable population. There has been a paucity of research investigating the contribution of genetic factors to fall risk or factors that may be unique to older adults initiated on prescription opioids for acute pain. Tools integrating both clinical and genetic risk factors would be clinically useful for risk stratification and mitigation, particularly in postoperative older adult populations.

Patients with genetic polymorphisms in genes related to opioid metabolism and clinical effects are at increased risk for opioid‐related falls and OD. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 OD risk associated with polymorphisms in the gene that encodes the liver enzyme cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) is well‐established, however, association with fall risk remains tentative. Polymorphisms in genes related to opioid transport and mediating pain response are also associated with adverse opioid‐related effects. 21 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Phenotypic variability, whether secondary to genotype or medication‐enzyme interaction (phenoconversion), may affect proteins mediating opioid metabolism (CYP2D6), opioid transport (OCT1), p‐glycoprotein (ABCB1), opioid pharmacologic effects (OPRM1 and ARRB2), and pain physiology (COMT), thus influencing susceptibility for falls or OD. Further characterization of the relationship between genetic factors and opioid‐related adverse effects is needed; in particular, studies conducted in racially diverse patient populations are needed as their involvement in genomics research thus far has been inadequate, greatly limiting our understanding of the role race may play in opioid‐related risk as well as generalizability of available genomic research findings. 29

Our objectives were to determine the feasibility of recruiting and retaining socioeconomically disadvantaged middle aged and older adult populations for a prospective study seeking to identify risk factors, including genetic, for opioid‐related falls and other serious adverse effects. Secondary objectives included exploring associations among CIP‐RIOSORD risk class, genetic variability, and other participant factors with incidence of falls and serious opioid adverse effects within 30 days of opioid medication initiation.

METHODS

Study setting

Enrollment for this study occurred in the emergency department (ED) of an urban safety‐net hospital with ~62,000 visits per year from 2018 to 2020. In 2018, the average ED patient was 54 years old, with ~50% male, 67% African American, 75% on Medicare/Medicaid, and 31% self‐pay/charity pay.

Study design

This was a pilot prospective observational study of ED patients discharged home with an opioid prescription for acute pain. Participants were eligible if aged 50 years or older, not on chronic opioid therapy (opioids used on most days for >3 months), not prescribed an opioid medication in the previous 30 days, did not have access to an opioid at home, and were able to provide written informed consent. Participants were excluded if hospitalized, non‐ambulatory, non‐community dwelling, lacked reliable telephone access to complete telephone follow‐ups, non‐English speaking, incarcerated, or had a current cancer diagnosis. The Institutional Review Board at the University of Florida approved this study.

Study measures

Figure 1 depicts the timeline of events. Feasibility was measured by participant enrollment rate and retention to day 30. Sociodemographic, clinical, and medication data were collected at enrollment. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini Cognitive Assessment (Mini‐Cog). 30 Participants with a score suggesting cognitive impairment were withdrawn from the study. As the Mini‐Cog test is not part of standard medical care, the approving institutional review board required that this assessment be performed after study enrollment. Fall risk was assessed using the Timed Up and Go Test (TUG). 31 For participants with difficulty walking due to acute pain, the 30‐Second Sit to Stand Test was used. 32 Health literacy was measured using Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine‐Short Form (REALM‐SF). 33 The Charlson Comorbidity Index was used to measure overall chronic disease severity. 34 Socioeconomic disadvantage was assessed using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) (scale of 1%–100%, increasing rank indicates increased disadvantage). 35 Falls were defined using the Hopkins Falls Grading Scale. 36 Participants were provided the Hopkins Falls Grading Scale pictorial to assist with defining falls during follow‐up. Opioid adverse effects were defined as participant‐reported low blood pressure, increased sleepiness or sedation, slow or decreased responsiveness, or decreased breathing. Risk class and probability of overdose was determined using the CIP‐RIOSORD tool. 20 CIP‐RIOSORD risk class groups were created as follows: low (risk classes 1 and 2), medium (classes 3 and 4), and high (class 5 or higher). The Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) surveys were completed to assess pain intensity, physical function, and pain interference with daily activities. 37 Mouthwash samples were collected to test for the presence of single‐nucleotide polymorphisms in CYP2D6, ARRB2, ABCB1, OPRM1, OCT1, and COMT. These polymorphisms were selected based on associations with opioid adverse effects and alterations in drug response that have been reported in the literature. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

Study timeline. ED, emergency department.

Follow‐ups

Follow‐ups were conducted via telephone on days 3 (±1), 7 (±1), 14 (±2), and 30 (±2) to assess medication use, PROMIS survey pain outcomes (pain intensity, pain interference, and physical function), incidence of falls, and opioid‐related adverse events. Every third participant completed their day 3 follow‐up in person rather than by telephone to re‐assess fall risk via the TUG or 30‐Second Sit to Stand Test. The remaining follow‐ups were completed by telephone.

Genetic testing

Genotyping for COMT (rs740603, rs4680, rs4818, rs6269, and rs4633), OPRM1 (rs1799971), ABCB1 (rs2032582 and rs1045642), ARRB2 (rs1045280), and OCT1 (rs72552763) were performed following procedures previously published. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 38 CYP2D6 genotyping and phenotype assignment using allele activity scoring was performed following procedures that have previously been published. 38 , 39

Data analysis

Descriptive summaries were frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and, medians, and quartiles for numeric variables. The main outcome was the composite outcome of having at least one self‐reported fall or self‐reported opioid adverse effect during the 30‐day follow‐up period. Univariate associations between independent variables and the composite outcome were assessed using Fisher's Exact tests for categorical data and Wilcoxon's Rank Sum tests for continuous data. Age, gender, race, fall risk at time of enrollment, highest education level completed, history of depression, type of medical insurance coverage, lives alone, visual acuity, patient feels it is easy to move around their house, patient often takes medications in a manner not prescribed, patients sometimes feel uncertain on how to take their medications, patient takes more than four prescribed medications, ADI score, pain score on day 3, pain interference on day 3, and physical function on day 3 were included in the univariate analyses. Variables significant at p < 0.1 in univariate testing (history of depression, living alone, higher pain intensity, and higher pain interference) were included in the multivariable logistic regression model using backward variable selection. The level of significance for the final model was set at 5%. The residual Chi‐square test indicated that the final model was a good fit for the data (p value = 0.217).

A sensitivity analysis was performed to ensure that one outcome (e.g., falls) was not driving the results of the logistic regression model for the composite outcome. Univariate associations between incidence of falls and the same variables used in univariate testing for the composite outcome were measured. Variables significant at p < 0.1 in univariate testing (living alone, a visual acuity of less than 20/70 with corrective lenses, higher pain intensity, and higher pain interference) were included in the multivariable logistic regression model using backward variable selection. Those significant at 5% were retained in the final model. The residual Chi‐square test indicated that the final model was a good fit for the data (p value = 0.240). All analyses were done in SAS for Windows version 9.4 and STATA version 17.

RESULTS

Feasibility of enrollment, participant retention to 30‐day follow‐up

A total of 44 participants were enrolled, and two were withdrawn (Figure 2). One was withdrawn after no longer meeting inclusion criteria when the medical team changed their treatment plan, deciding to admit the participant after initially discharging him. The second participant failed to achieve the required Mini‐Cog score to continue participation in the study. Of the remaining 42 participants, 74% (31/42) completed all scheduled follow‐up visits during the 30‐day period following study enrollment (including 9 who were selected to complete day 3 follow‐up as an in‐person visit) and 88% (37/42) completed the last scheduled follow‐up (day 30). Due to an error by the research team, one participant was enrolled and performed day 0 study measures but was never scheduled for follow‐up visits.

Demographics

The mean participant age was 60 years (SD: 5.73, range: 52–79), 52.4% (22) were men, 60% (25) African American, 57% (24) had a health literacy level of less than or equal to eighthh grade; and 40% (17) reported a fall history (Table 1). The Charlson Comorbidity mean score was 3.6 (range: 0–42). The mean ADI national percentile was 79% (median 92%). Participants were discharged with a prescription for hydrocodone (31, 74%), oxycodone (6, 14%), or tramadol (5, 12%).

30‐day incidence of falls and opioid adverse effects

Twenty‐two (52.4%) participants reported at least one fall or an opioid adverse effect within the 30 days following their ED visit (group 1). Twenty reported no falls or adverse events (group 2). Of the 22 participants with the composite outcome, 10 reported a fall only, eight an opioid adverse effect only, and four both a fall and opioid adverse effects.

Of those reporting a fall while consuming an opioid medication, most fell after day 7 (68%). Seven participants received and consumed another prescription opioid for ongoing pain—three from their primary physician, two during another ED visit, one bought prescription opioids “off the street,” and another from a “friend of a friend.” Three participants sought medical attention due to effects attributed to the newly prescribed opioid medication (7%). Two experienced a fall necessitating ED evaluation and, in one case, the fall was a consequence of over‐sedation leading to loss of consciousness. The third participant sought primary care evaluation after experiencing over‐sedation and loss of consciousness but did not report a concomitant fall. Three participants had more than one fall during the follow‐up period and two of these had a history of falls prior to enrollment.

Relationship between CIP‐RIOSORD risk class and composite outcome

Neither CIP‐RIOSORD risk class nor score were associated with the composite outcome (p = 0.158 and p = 0.179, respectively). The distribution of CIP‐RIOSORD risk class differed by African American race (p = 0.015) and by use of sedative medications (p = 0.002). Most participants identifying as African American belonged to risk class 1 (16, 64%). Increasing CIP‐RIOSORD risk class and score were both associated with medications that inhibit CYP2D6 (p = 0.005) but not CYP2D6 phenotype. CIP‐RIOSORD risk class was distributed differently across ARRB2 rs1045280 (p = 0.045) and ABCB1 rs1045642 (p = 0.03) genotype. Participants with the ARRB2 rs1045280 TT genotype had a higher proportion of participants in the medium and high‐risk class groups (54.5%) compared to those with the CT (13%) or CC (37.5%) genotype. Participants with the ABCB1 rs1045642 TT genotype had a higher proportion of participants in the low‐risk class group (78%), and none in the high‐risk class group, compared to those with the TC (61%) or CC (50%) genotype.

Clinical factors associated with the composite outcome

In univariate testing, factors associated with falls and opioid adverse effects as a composite outcome at p < 0.1 included history of depression (p = 0.048), living alone (p = 0.051), higher pain intensity on day 3 (p = 0.098), and higher pain interference on day 3 (p = 0.008; Table 2). Therefore, these variables were included in the multivariable analyses for the composite outcome.

Factors predictive of the composite outcome

In multivariable testing, only pain interference on day 3 was predictive of the composite outcome (odds ratio [OR]: 1.128, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.025–1.240, p = 0.013).

Factors predictive of a self‐reported fall within 30 days of prescription opioid initiation (sensitivity analysis)

In univariate testing, factors associated with a fall at p < 0.1 and included in the multivariable analyses were living alone (p = 0.004), a visual acuity of less than 20/70 with corrective lenses (p = 0.015) at study enrollment, higher pain intensity on day 3 (p = 0.011), and higher pain interference T scores on day 3 (p = 0.004; Table 3). In multivariable testing, living alone (OR: 16.017, 95% CI: 1.634–157.038), low visual acuity (OR: 16.772, 95% CI: 1.489–188.956), and pain interference on day 3 (OR: 1.213, 95% CI: 1.046–1.405) were predictive of a self‐reported fall.

DISCUSSION

The primary objective of this study was to determine feasibility of recruitment and retention in a diverse disadvantaged middle aged and older adult population. Study recruitment rate was 21%, retention was 88%, and the proportion of participants completing all follow‐ups was 74%. Older adult recruitment rates for health research studies have reportedly ranged from 3.3 to 68.2%. 40 Future qualitative work could be conducted to explore patient motivations or disinterest in participating in studies involving genetics and opioid risk. The additional complexity posed by the emergence of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic toward the end of study recruitment was also an important factor potentially limiting eligible patients' willingness to participate.

More participants reported falling after their day 7 follow‐up, a time period later than previously reported in the literature (increased risk in the first 7 days after opioid dispensation). 2 In the prior study, in contrast to the current study, opioid consumption was not confirmed but rather an opioid medication prescription was filled. Thus, the increased risk of falls in the first week of opioid dispensation reported previously may not be related to opioid consumption but other unmeasured factors, such as pain intensity or physical function. Another important distinction is that we included all reported falls regardless if they resulted in a significant injury or hospitalization.

We found several factors (depression, living alone, higher pain intensity, and higher pain interference) associated with the composite outcome in our older adult population initiated on opioids for acute pain. However, only pain interference was associated with the composite outcome. There are limited studies examining risk factors associated with our composite outcome for comparison. However, depression or depressive symptoms was previously linked with increased fall and opioid overdose risk. 20 , 41 Increased pain interference and pain intensity, along with depressive symptoms, were each shown to increase opioid overdose risk behavior. 42 Furthermore, pain interference was also predictive of injurious falls in a large prospective study of community dwelling older adults. 43 Pain interference has been shown to influence a person's falls efficacy (an individual's perception of their self‐efficacy to prevent a fall) and psychological concerns over falling. 44 Our results, along with previous literature, suggest pain interference may be useful as a common predictive factor to assess both risk for fall and serious opioid adverse effects over a short follow‐up period (30 days).

Depressive symptoms, living alone, and social isolation are linked with increased falls in older adults. 41 We found living alone and visual acuity were associated with falls when examined as a single outcome, each increasing the probability of falling by 16‐fold, respectively. However, the CIs for these associations were quite large and needs validation in a larger study population. We did not find depression to be associated with falling. This discrepancy is likely explained by our small participant size and that we only included patients with a depression diagnosis in our analysis, which potentially would have excluded patients exhibiting depressive symptoms but without a formal diagnosis.

Although we found pain intensity to be associated with both the composite outcome and falls only, it was not significant in multivariable testing for either outcome. Literature on the relationship between falls and pain intensity is conflicting. Some studies report an association, others no association, and still others suggest pain intensity may limit mobility thereby predisposing to falls. 43 , 45 , 46 Methodological differences in which the pain intensity tool was selected, the timing and frequency of when pain severity measurements were taken, and the outcome selected (falls, reaction time, or other mobility markers) may be responsible for these apparent contradictory findings.

β‐Arrestin 2 (ARRB2) plays a critical role in the regulation of signal transduction at G‐protein coupled receptors, including the mu‐opioid receptor, and possibly in the development of opioid addiction and tolerance. 47 We found participants with the ARRB2 rs1045280 TT genotype had a higher proportion of participants in the CIP‐RIOSORD medium and high‐risk class groups (54.5%) compared to those with the CT (13%) and CC (37.5%) genotypes. As the racial composition of our study population was largely African American, our findings echo results from a previous study demonstrating African American chronic heroin users who were T‐allele carriers had higher health‐related consequences, including overdose, from their heroin use compared to African Americans C‐allele carriers. These genotypic differences were not found in White chronic heroin users. 48

P‐glycoprotein is an important efflux pump in the gastrointestinal tract and blood–brain barrier, influencing transport of opioids to the brain. We found genotypic differences in ABCB1 rs1045642, like ARRB2 rs1045280, to also correlate with increased opioid overdose risk. Participants with the ABCB1 rs1045642 TT genotype had a higher proportion of participants in the low‐risk class group (78%), and none in the high‐risk class group, compared to those with the TC (61%) or CC (50%) genotype. In a previous study, postmortem blood samples collected from methadone‐related deaths found the TT genotype had higher methadone concentrations in the brain than the blood compared to either TC or CC genotypes. 49 Taken together, these results suggest polymorphisms in ABCB1 rs1045642 and ARRB2 rs1045280 may influence opioid toxicity and susceptible individuals may be better managed using other analgesic options or lower opioid doses.

Comparing incidence of falls to a participants' CYP2D6 phenotype (i.e., directly measuring CYP2D6 enzyme activity) rather than whether they are consuming a medication with CYP2D6 inhibitory effects, allows a direct measurement of the true degree of CYP2D6 inhibition. This information can better determine the relationship between medications and falls. Whereas others have reported an association between falls and consumption of CYP2D6 inhibitors, the majority of our study participants had a normal phenotype, therefore, we were unable to determine an association between falls and CYP2D6 phenotype. However, this comparison should be tested in the future to better characterize this potential relationship and confirm findings reported in the literature.

A strength of our study, despite its small sample size, is inclusion of a racially diverse population. There are several limitations that should be considered, including a small study population, self‐reporting of falls and opioid adverse effects, and modest recruitment rate, which may indicate a bias in those participants who were willing to consent to study participation. We examined a large number of potential associations, and some of the significant findings could be due to type I error in multiple testing. Our limited findings are exploratory and require confirmation in a larger study population. Despite these limitations, we identified that pain interference may be a potential predictive factor for opioid‐related falls or other serious opioid‐related adverse effects in older adults initiated on prescription opioids for acute pain. If validated, these factors might prove beneficial in risk stratification of older adults initiated on prescription opioids for acute pain.
