The effectiveness of Spencer muscle energy technique on pain, function and range of motion in patients with frozen shoulder: Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses
Authors: Mihret Dejen Takele, Alemu Kassaw Kibret, Gashaw Jember Belay, Moges Baye, Kefale Getie, Kassaw Belay Shiferaw, Getachew Azeze Eriku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrozen shoulder, also clinically known as adhesive capsulitis, is characterized by pain, stiffness, and gradual loss of passive or active glenohumeral range of motion. It leads to marked reduction in shoulder mobility, impacting daily activities and quality of life. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the Spencer muscle energy technique (SMET) for frozen shoulder patients.MethodsFor this systematic review and meta-analysis, we used PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) for searching from inception to January 20, 2025. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and independent authors selected clinical control trials that compare the effect of the Spencer muscle energy technique and comparative treatment in individuals with frozen shoulder. The outcomes were pain, function, and shoulder range of motion (ROM). Two authors independently did data extraction. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool, and the overall quality of the evidence was evaluated using the PEDro scale.ResultsNine studies were included in this review, and eight studies were included in the meta-analysis. Results indicated that SMET and other comparative treatments showed no statistical difference in the pain, flexion ROM, and abduction ROM standard mean difference (SMD). -0.19; 95% CI (-0.83, 0.45); I²=84%, (MD), 7.65; 95% CI (-11.7, 26.9); I²=95%, MD, 11.32; 95% CI (-9.10, 31.74); I²=96%, respectively. SMET was a statistically significant effect for shoulder function, SMD − 0.5; 95% CI (-0.78, -0.22); I² = 41%. Introduction

Frozen shoulder, also clinically known as adhesive capsulitis, is characterized by pain, stiffness, and gradual loss of passive or active glenohumeral range of motion [1]. The shoulder joint capsule is inflamed and thickened, with rigid connective tissue encasing the shoulder joint [2]. The upper extremity committee of the International Society of Arthroscopy, Knee Surgery and Orthopedic Sports Medicine (ISAKOS) defined the term “frozen shoulder” as idiopathic stiff shoulder, that is, without any trauma or specific shoulder disease. Examples include predisposing factors such as diabetes, thyroid conditions, Dupuytren contracture, and smoking. Secondary stiff shoulder is a term that should be used to describe shoulder stiffness with a known cause, such as a stiff shoulder after trauma or surgery [3]. The shoulder joint capsule’s lining and related ligaments are thought to be inflamed and swollen, which causes contracture of the shoulder joint capsule and limits shoulder movement [4].

There are three phases for that patient with frozen shoulder: Painful Phase (Duration: 2–9 months): This phase is marked by severe pain, often worsening at night, especially when lying down. The pain can be debilitating and may limit the patient’s ability to move the shoulder. Freezing Phase (Duration: 4–12 months): During this phase, the patient experiences increasing stiffness in the shoulder, making it difficult to perform simple activities of daily living. The pain may gradually decrease, but the range of motion continues to decline, leading to disuse and potential atrophy of the shoulder muscles. Thawing Phase (Duration: 5–12 months): In this final phase, there is a gradual improvement in both range of motion and pain levels. Although some stiffness may persist, patients typically experience a return to normal function. Pain may occasionally reappear as the shoulder begins to thaw and regain movement [2, 5].

The prevalence of frozen shoulder in the general population is indeed estimated to be 2–5%; when looking at gender differences, studies have shown that 8% of males and 10% of women have it, and in diabetic patients the risk of developing frozen shoulder is 10–29% [6, 7]. Most commonly affected individuals are between 40 and 60 years of age, and rarely affected people are under 40 years. Patients with hyperthyroidism and hypertriglyceridaemia have been found to have a higher incidence of frozen shoulder [8]. This condition may experience bilateral frozen shoulder up to 16%, and recurrent frozen shoulder is also relatively uncommon [9].

The conventional approach to treating adhesive capsulitis involves reducing pain, preserving range of motion, and eventually regaining function. Exercise programs that include active and passive range of motion exercises, stretching exercises supervised by a physiotherapist, self-stretching, manipulation and mobilization techniques, strengthening exercises, patient education, and at-home exercises are among the recommended treatments for frozen shoulder. Other recommended treatments include electro-modalities and physiotherapy interventions like heat therapy.

Based on the patients’ clinical condition, various forms of muscle energy technique are available for different joints and muscles. The latest osteopathic manual therapy method widely used is the Spencer Muscle Energy Technique (SMET), adopted in Western clinical practices for treating various shoulder ailments. It is a set of standardized shoulder treatments that can be used widely for prognosis, diagnosis, and treatment of frozen shoulder. It was developed by Spencer, D.O., in 1916. The evolution of this technique is traced from 1916 to date to try to identify factors in the development of manipulative methods [10].

SMET has gained popularity in recent years as an effective intervention for frozen shoulder. In this technique, the glenohumeral and scapula-thoracic joints are mobilized through osteopathic manipulative techniques. In addition to improving the functions of restricted joints, it provides positive effects on other emotional, social and cognitive areas [11].

Clinical outcomes suggest that the SMET improves shoulder range of motion more effectively than conventional physical therapy. A unique aspect of SMET is client involvement in the process. The technique involves two steps repeated in seven stages. The first step is rhythmic oscillation at the end of the available range, and the second step is isometric contractions of antagonist muscle at different ranges to achieve a new barrier. This technique works on the principle of reciprocal inhibition. The patient should be in a side-lying position with the affected shoulder facing upwards. The therapist should stand on the lateral side of the patient and stabilize the upper side of the shoulder girdle, which provides support so that the technique can be applied specifically at the joint level. It is necessary that during the application of this technique the wrist and forearm are well supported. It has seven stages to perform SMET:

Step 1 to improve the extension, step 2 to improve the shoulder flexion, Step 3: Circumduction with compression, Step 4– Circumduction with distraction, Step 5–To improve the abduction, to improve the adduction with external rotation, Step 6– Internal rotation, Step 7– Distraction in abduction [12]. In this technique, passive, smooth, rhythmic motion is designed to stretch contracted muscles, ligaments, and capsules, enhancing lymphatic flow and stimulating increased joint circulation [13]. SMET improves the pain-free range of shoulder joint motion in frozen shoulder patients by increasing blood circulation in the shoulder joint, promoting lymphatic flow and muscle stretching, and affecting the capsule and ligaments of the shoulder joint. Many medical healthcare professionals in professional sports have recently adopted this method during training sessions to improve the performance of the athletes in various sports.

As far as our search, we found two published systematic reviews on this topic [14, 15]. They indicated the combined effect of SMET with other exercise groups and manual therapy. However, the aim of this systematic review was to specifically evaluate the effectiveness of SMET alone in comparison to other treatments across a diverse patient population for reducing pain and improving function and range of motion in individuals with frozen shoulder. Therefore, this review aims to fill the gap in existing literature by isolating the effects of SMET, providing clearer insights into its effectiveness as a standalone treatment. By doing so, we hope to offer valuable evidence that can inform clinical practice and guide future research in managing frozen shoulder.

Methods

This systematic review was done in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and presented according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement [16, 17]. In addition, the study was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024605780).

Data source and search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, PEDro, Cochrane Library, and Embase were searched from inception to January 20, 2025. The search terms used included muscle energy technique, frozen shoulder, and adhesive capsulitis. The complete search strategy is provided in the supplementary file 1). To identify the relevant articles, titles and abstracts were exported to EndNote 20, and duplicates were identified and removed by one reviewer (MDT). Three authors (MB, AKK and KBS) independently screened the titles and abstracts and evaluated the articles in full for eligibility based on pre-specified criteria. Discrepancies were resolved in a panel discussion with a fourth author (GJB). Moreover, we also reviewed reference lists of included studies to identify additional eligible trials.

Eligibility criteria

We considered studies eligible for inclusion if they were clinical control trials, had a PEDro score greater than or equal to four, and were in the intervention group with the Spencer muscle energy technique, frozen shoulder patients, and studies in the English language. Duplicate data, study protocols, unpublished papers, non-full-text articles in peer-reviewed journals, non-RCTs, quasi-RCTs, unclear methodology, and conference papers were excluded.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently done by three reviewers (MDT, KG, and KBS). A systematic extraction form was used for each article to collect the following data: sample size, intervention features (e.g., type of intervention, duration, frequency, and intensity of intervention), methodological factors (outcome and its measurement tool used to assess pain, functional level, and range of motion), evaluation time, and overall results. For further information see Table 1.

Assesment of studies quality

To assess the quality of included studies, we used the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) and Grading of Recommendation Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to determine the quality of each body of evidence. Three independent reviewers (MDT, GJB, and GAE) evaluated the quality of studies. Ten of the 11 items on the PEDro scale, which were evaluated using explicit decision rules, were extracted from the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (www.PEDro.org.au) and used to evaluate the included trials critically. The PEDro scale evaluates a study’s methodological quality using key factors such as concealed allocation, intention-to-treat analysis, and follow-up sufficiency. The PEDro scale is a valuable instrument for evaluating the methodological quality of physiotherapy and rehabilitation trials. The PEDro scale has eleven components. Items 2–9 deal with a paper’s internal validity, whereas items 10 and 11 deal with the statistical analysis, making sure there is enough information to allow for a suitable interpretation of the findings [18] (see Table 2). The quality of evidence for each outcome was consequently graded as high (PEDro score 6–10), Fair (PEDro score 4–5), Poor (PEDro score ≤ 3) (see Table 2).

Risk of bias

The other method to assess the risk of bias of included studies with each respective outcome was to use the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (ROB2), an updated version of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Review of Interventions [19], with three independent authors (MDT, GAE and GJB). The main types of risk assessed at the outcome level using the following domain are bias from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results. In case of disagreement, the 3 authors would discuss the study to reach a consensus, and for full details (see Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was done using the following outcome measurement tool: Visual analogue scale (VAS) and numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) for pain, the shoulder pain and disability index (SPADI) to assess shoulder-related pain and disabilities in terms of functional outcomes and active shoulder range of motion (ROM): flexion, abduction, external rotation, and extension. The included studies were only comparing Spencer muscle energy technique to other comparative interventions. This meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager Version 5.4.1 (The Cochrane Collaboration). Post-intervention continuous data were shown as mean difference (MD) ± standard deviation (SD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Heterogeneity tests were done using the chi-squared test and the I² test. The I2 statistic was used to test for statistical heterogeneity and was assessed as follows: 0% < I2 < 25%, low heterogeneity; 25% < I2 < 50%, moderate heterogeneity; and I2 > 50%, high heterogeneity [20]. An I2 > 50% in those cases applied a random effect model because there were varied follow-up times, and the stage of frozen shoulder between studies increases the risk of heterogeneity. On the other hand, at I2 < 50%, we applied a fixed -effect model. In case of high heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was performed to assess the Spencer muscle energy technique with different comparative treatments groups in the control group. When there was high heterogeneity in a meta-analysis, the sensitivity analysis was performed, excluding studies with extreme results to explain potential heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the reliability of the effects. One study was eliminated at that time, and studies with very heterogeneous findings were also eliminated. Small-study effects (publication bias) were examined in the funnel plot, and no publication bias was observed in this study.

Results

Study selection

A total of 3780 studies was initially identified. After removing duplicates, 2490 remained for the screening process. A total of 2180 studies were excluded after screening the title and abstract. Three hundred and ten results were sought for retrieval. A full text of 157 was analysed, of which 8 studies were included in quantitative analysis (meta-analysis) (see Fig. 1).

Studies characteristics

The included studies were published between 2016 and 2025. A total of 324 frozen shoulder patients from 9 publications were included for qualitative synthesis. 8 studies were used for quantitative analysis. The number of participants selected for each study varied from 20 to 60. All of the 9 studies were clinical control trials. Five of the studies were randomized control trials [12, 21–24], and four studies were comparative clinical controlled trials [9, 25–27]. The ages of the participants range from 30 to 70 years. Six studies (66.6%) included patients who were stiff and painful for the last 3 months [9, 12, 22–24, 27]. One study (11.1%) included patients who were stiff and painful for the last 2 months’ duration [21]. The other study (11.1%) included patients who were stiff and painful for the last month [25]. A study done by Chavan et al., 2017, found no information regarding the time duration on stiff and painful time [26]. The characteristics of all included studies are summarized in the Table 1.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment of all included studies is shown in Fig. 2. For all-over risk of bias, 1 study (11.1%) was low risk of bias [27], 5 studies (55.5%) were rated as some concern [9, 21, 22, 24, 25], and 3 studies (33.3%) were rated as high risk of bias [12, 23, 26]. In the randomization process five studies (55.5%) were rated as “some concern” [9, 12, 21–23], three studies (33%) were rated as “low risk of bias” [24, 25, 27], and one study (11.1%) was rated as “high risk of bias” [26].

For the deviation from intended interventions, five studies (55.5%) were rated as “some concern” [9, 12, 23, 24, 26], and four studies (44.5%) were rated as “low risk of bias” [21, 22, 25, 27]. In addition, there is no bias in the missing outcome data across all studies. While in the measurement of outcome data, one study (11.1%) was rated as “some concern” [25] and another study (11.1%) was rated as “high risk of bias” [12]. Finally, for the selection of reported results, four studies (44.5%) were rated as “some concern” [12, 21, 24, 25], and five studies (55.5%) were rated as “low risk of bias” [9, 22, 23, 26, 27].

Outcomes

Pain

Eight studies [9, 12, 21–23, 25–27] investigated the effectiveness of the SMET compared to the control group for reducing pain in patients with frozen shoulders. One study [24] was not included in the meta-analysis due to insufficient data. The pooled effect showed no statistically significant difference between SMET and the control group (N = 234; SMD, −0.19; 95% CI (−0.83, 0.45); I²=84%; Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis with each control group was performed and showed the SMET was statistically significant compared to Maitland mobilization in reducing pain (N = 98; SMD,−0.99; 95% CI (−1.41,−0.56); Fig. 3). However, SMET is less statistically significant compared to Mulligan mobilization in reducing pain for treatment of frozen shoulder patients (Fig. 3).

Functional disability

Six studies [21–23, 25–27] were included in the meta-analysis because two studies [9, 12] didn’t report enough data for this meta-analysis. The pooled effect showed SMET is statistically significant effect for improved shoulder functional capacity compared to the control group in frozen shoulder patients (N = 212; SMD, −0.5; 95% CI (−0.78, −0.22); I2 = 41% (Fig. 4).

Range of motion

For shoulder flexion range of motion, six studies [12, 21, 23, 25–27] were included in the meta-analysis, and the other two studies [9, 22] didn’t report enough data. The overall effect showed there is no statistically significant difference between SMET and the control group for increasing shoulder flexion range of motion (N = 210; MD, 7.65; 95% CI (−11.7, 26.9); Fig. 5). Subgroup analysis for SMET compared to each control group were performed; the result showed Maitland mobilization and myofascial arm pull were statistically significant for increasing shoulder flexion range of motion compared to the SMET (Fig. 5).

For shoulder abduction range of motion, six studies [12, 21, 23, 25–27] were included in this meta-analysis. The pooled effect showed there is no statistically significant difference between SMET and the control group for increasing shoulder abduction range of motion (N = 210; MD,11.32; 95% CI (−9.10, 31.74); Fig. 6). Subgroup analysis were performed on SMET compared to each control group. The result revealed that Maitland mobilization and myofascial arm pull were statistically significant effects for improved shoulder abduction range of motion compared to the SMET (Fig. 6).

For shoulder external rotation range of motion, five studies [12, 21, 23, 26, 27] were included in this meta-analysis. The overall effect of five studies showed the control group was statistically significant for increased shoulder external rotation range of motion compared to SMET (N = 180; MD, 9.74; 95% CI (2.38, 17.10); Fig. 7. Subgroup analysis were performed on SMET compared to each control group. The result showed Maitland mobilization and myofascial arm pull was significantly improved shoulder external rotation compared to the SMET (Fig. 7). For shoulder extension range of motion, two studies [21, 27] were included in the meta-analysis; the other studies were not included due to insufficient data. The pooled effect of the two studies showed the control group showed statistically significant effects for improved shoulder extension range of motion compared to the SMET (N = 98; MD = 2.55; 95% CI (1.14, 3.96); Fig. 8).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis study to specifically compare the effectiveness of the SMET versus other comparative treatment groups on pain, function, and ROM in patients with frozen shoulder. Nine trials were included in this study, with an early 1-month to late 6-month follow-up period. Five primary intervention categories were compared with SMET, including Maitland mobilization, Mulligan mobilization, conservative treatment, Gong mobilization, and myofascial arm pull. The pooled effect showed that there was no statistical difference between SMET and the control group for pain reduction (SMD, −0.19; 95% CI (−0.83, 0.45); I²=84%). However, it is important to consider the high heterogeneity of the trails. The possible reason for this heterogeneity might be the differences in control interventions. In subgroup analysis, SMET is statistically superior in reducing shoulder pain compared to Maitland mobilization. This finding is supported by a previous narrative review [28] and a case report study [29] indicating that SMET has a superior effect compared to Maitland mobilization for reducing pain in frozen shoulder patients. On the other hand, SMET is less effective compared to the Mulligan mobilization technique for reducing pain in frozen shoulder patients. This finding, supported by an RCT study, indicated that Mulligan mobilization was more effective for reducing pain compared to SMET in frozen shoulder patients [30]. In addition, the finding of this study, supported by a previous systematic review and meta-analysis study, showed that Mulligan mobilization demonstrated statistical superiority compared to the control group for shoulder pathologies [31].

Implications for clinicians and researchers

SMET may be particularly useful for functional disability. Other techniques like Maitland or Myofascial arm pull might be more effective in improving ROM. SMET involves a series of mobilization and stretching maneuvers. So clinicians should ensure they are trained and confident in applying SMET safely. Further studies should explore the effect of SMET across different stages of frozen shoulder to determine optimal timing for intervention and investigate whether age or gender influences the effectiveness of SMET, which could provide insight to personalize the treatment approach. Pain scales (VAS, NPRS) can help quantify changes in pain level. ROM measuring before and after treatment sessions is crucial to assess immediate effects, and the functional assessment tool (SPADI) can provide insight into functional improvement.

Limitation of the study

Some potential limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the search strategy in this study was limited to English-language publications. Therefore, relevant studies published in other languages may have been missed. Second, some comparisons in this study were made based on only 1 trial. Therefore, general conclusions cannot be drawn from these comparisons. Finally, the conclusions of some studies were based on fair quality trails, and therefore the current effect estimates may be changed by future research.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that SMET shows promise in improving shoulder function in patients with frozen shoulder; however, evidence is inconsistent for pain and ROM outcomes compared to other manual therapies. Further studies should explore the effect of SMET across different phases of frozen shoulder to determine the optimal timing for intervention through large, high-quality, and well-designed randomized control trials.
