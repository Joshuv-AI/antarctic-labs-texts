The Richards Trauma Process: a before-after single-arm feasibility trial of a novel psychotherapy
Authors: Petrina Barson, Peter MacIsaac, Lena Sanci, Meredith Temple-Smith, Judy Trevena, Adrian Laughlin, Patty Chondros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Richards Trauma Process (TRTP) is a novel, very brief transdiagnostic psychotherapy that uses innovative methods to work with parts of the self to reprocess traumatic memories without using exposure techniques. Our objectives were to explore the feasibility of studying this therapy in a community setting, to document the safety and acceptability of TRTP, and to gather preliminary indications of outcomes in adults presenting with elevated levels of psychological distress.MethodsThis was a before-after single-arm trial of TRTP in consecutive new adult clients presenting to a psychotherapist in Australia. Inclusion criteria were at least mildly elevated levels of depression, anxiety, stress or trauma symptoms; no previous diagnosis of psychosis, Bipolar Disorder or Personality Disorder; and ability to maintain sobriety during the therapy. Feasibility was assessed against 15 criteria relating to 5 broad objectives, evaluating: (1) recruitment and retention capability, (2) data collection procedures, (3) resourcing, (4) acceptability and safety of TRTP, and (5) change in scores. Regarding objective 5, we estimated the mean change in scores on the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS21) and PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) between follow-up and baseline; and to check for harms (objective 4) we used descriptive statistics to look for clinically meaningful deterioration in individual scores or participants.ResultsOf 40 new clients, 23 (58%; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 41% to 73%) were eligible, consented to the study and commenced TRTP. Of these, 20 (87%; 95% CI 65% to 97%) completed TRTP (averaging six hours of therapy) and follow-up 1-month post-therapy. Eleven of 15 feasibility criteria were met. Unmet criteria could be addressed in a future efficacy trial by modifications to study procedures. All mean scores changed in the desired direction, with no individual scores or participants demonstrating clinically meaningful deterioration.ConclusionsThis is the first study that supports the feasibility of a larger trial of TRTP in a community setting. We demonstrated that recruitment and retention of participants, and data collection, were feasible, and that the intervention was acceptable, safe, and worthy of further study. Background

Mental disorders are one of the leading causes of ill-health and disability globally, with anxiety disorders and depression the most prevalent [1, 2]. In 2020–2022, the 12-month prevalence of anxiety disorders in people aged 16–85 in Australia was around 17%, and depression around 8% [3]. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is also highly prevalent, with a large attendant burden of disability and suffering [4]. Globally the burden of mental ill-health is thought to be increasing [2]. In Australia, levels of mental distress on national surveys rose in the 15 years to 2022 despite increased levels of expenditure on mental health care [5–7].

Psychological therapy is one of the main tools employed, alongside medication, to address this global health challenge. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is the most widely researched and empirically supported of all psychotherapies, with multiple trials demonstrating effect sizes ranging from ‘medium’ (0.5) to ‘large’ (0.8) for depression [8, 9] and anxiety disorders [10, 11]. However, remission rates for anxiety disorders treated with CBT may be around 50% [12], and for depression around 33% [13], leaving many in distress. This would suggest that there may be room in the therapeutic landscape for novel frameworks and treatments, either as first-line therapy or for non-responders.

The commonest and most extensively evaluated psychological treatments for anxiety disorders and depression, such as CBT and mindfulness-based therapies, are not typically trauma-focused [10, 14, 15]. In fact, within cognitive and behavioural therapies, maladaptive cognitions and experiential or behavioural avoidance, rather than trauma, are regarded as the main causes and perpetuating factors of psychopathology and are the targets of treatment [16–18]. PTSD, on the other hand, is regarded as the paradigmatic trauma-based mental disorder [4], hence psychological therapy for this disorder is usually trauma-based, meaning that specific traumatic memories and/or cognitions about these memories, are the targets of treatment [19]. Recommended evidence-based therapies for PTSD include Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapies (TF-CBT), Prolonged Exposure (PE), and Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (EMDR). While these have been shown to be efficacious, with large effect sizes, they also can have high drop-out rates (greater than 1 in 4 in some studies), possibly higher than non-trauma-focused therapies [20]. It has been credibly suggested that difficulties tolerating trauma-focused treatment may be one of the reasons for high drop-out rates [21]. Remission rates, even with these recommended therapies, vary between about 44% and 86% [22], meaning that many individuals continue to suffer. Again, it would therefore be reasonable to postulate that there may be room in the therapeutic landscape for novel psychological treatments for PTSD, for treatment non-responders or for those unable to tolerate existing trauma-focused therapies.

Overview of TRTP

The Richards Trauma Process (TRTP) [23] is a novel transdiagnostic psychotherapy. It was created by Judith Richards from her lived experience of recovery from severe Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD). Richards has protocolised the therapy and trained several hundred early adopters in Australia and elsewhere to deliver it within normal clinic workflow. TRTP can be, and is currently, delivered by a diverse range of practitioners, from a variety of professional backgrounds including mental health and medical professionals, allied health professionals, counsellors, coaches and others whose professional roles involve accompanying distressed people one-on-one. All are screened for suitability via an interview process, and if accepted complete 5 months of TRTP training and supervision, and must demonstrate competency before being certified to deliver TRTP. While the therapy is manualised, this manual is not publicly available as Richards regards it as highly important that the therapy is delivered only after appropriate training.

TRTP is delivered according to a protocol as a three-step process, generally over three or four two-hour sessions at weekly intervals. Prior to session 1, participants complete a questionnaire inviting descriptive answers about their personal history; a list of significant distressing events or periods of time throughout their life; a list of symptoms; and a description of how they would like to feel and function after therapy. Based on this information and discussion with their client, the therapist comes to a formulation regarding the likely patterns of unconscious core beliefs emanating from trauma that are maintaining psychopathology. TRTP step 1 (performed in session 1) involves psychoeducation and identifying and changing unconscious core beliefs through ‘dynamic reimagining’—a term coined by Richards to describe a participatory form of guided imagery [24] that allows clients to have a rich inner imaginative experience. Step 2 commences in session 2 and may be completed in session 2 or 3 depending on progress. It involves identification of several key traumatic memories followed by reprocessing these memories through dynamic reimagining to allow the client to vividly experience ‘re-empowerment’ and ‘re-parenting’1 [25] of younger parts of the self in relation to those memories. In the process a robust ‘adult’ self is invoked and experientially inhabited to effect this healing for younger parts, whether those wounded parts be from childhood or from earlier adult life. Step 2 therapy continues (into session 3 if needed) until the Subjective Units of Distress Score (SUDS) [26] on all memories cease to be clinically distressing (deemed to be 0–2/10). Step 3 is undertaken in session 3 (or 4 if needed). It consists largely of future-focused content and addresses the ongoing client re-application of TRTP tools. A briefer follow-up session is usually conducted at least 1 month following completion of step 3.

There is similarity in methods between CBT and some aspects of TRTP in terms of identifying and changing underlying automatic cognitions [27]. However, there are also substantial differences between the methods. In TRTP, methods for changing these cognitions rely primarily on dynamic reimagining rather than cognitive disputation or reframing, so that in TRTP the client enters into an imaginative experience of their ‘undamaged’ self in session 1. Rather than address maladaptive cognitions as a cause of unhelpful patterns [16], in TRTP these are regarded as symptoms of underlying traumatic experiences, and therefore the method addresses the trauma directly. In the past, most discussions of trauma-related mental distress limit the definition of a ‘traumatic event’ to a physical or sexual assault, a life-threatening incident, or witnessing death [4]. However, in line with evolving definitions of trauma [28], in the TRTP model a much broader definition is adopted, to include any distressing life event, either at a single time-point or over an extended period. Unlike PTSD, the diagnosis of depression or anxiety disorders does not necessitate a history of trauma [29, 30]. This does not, however, preclude the possibility of distressing events being contributory to the genesis of these conditions, and indeed this issue is controversial [31, 32]. While acknowledging that causation of depression and anxiety disorders is multifactorial [33] involving biological, psychological and social factors, in the TRTP model conditions such as depression and anxiety disorders are assumed to originate at least in part from trauma (broadly defined as above), albeit mediated through individual vulnerability. Hence TRTP treatment methods for anxiety and depression are identical to those employed for PTSD. Indeed, inverting the logic, if TRTP can be shown in future studies to be effective in conditions such as Major Depressive Disorder and Anxiety Disorders, such findings may shed light on the causation controversy.

TRTP could thus be described as a ‘trauma-focused therapy’, but its methods of addressing the trauma are very different from other therapies in this category. There are similarities with imagery rescripting as practised in CBT [34, 35] but TRTP does not involve imaginal reliving, and the dynamic reimagining is therapist-directed towards re-empowerment rather than being client-led through Socratic questioning [35]. An important proposed mechanism of change in TRTP resembles the Adaptive Information Processing theory underpinning EMDR, whereby specific traumatic memories are believed to be reprocessed into long-term memory [36]. As in EMDR, it is hypothesized that the memory reprocessing performed at step 2 of TRTP allows dysfunctionally stored memories to be stored away within larger adaptive neural networks. However the reprocessing is conducted without the client having to hold the traumatic memories in mind as in EMDR [37], and certainly without repeated or extensive recounting of memories as in Prolonged Exposure [38]. This makes it reasonable to hypothesize that TRTP may be more acceptable to traumatised individuals than other trauma-focused therapies.

TRTP exists in a similar domain to other emerging therapies such as Internal Family Systems (IFS) [39] that posit the existence of parts of the self or ‘subselves’ [40]. However TRTP conceptualises these parts differently (primarily as younger victim and adult protector) and works with them in a unique way that is more therapist-led, aiming for re-empowerment and re-parenting. The robust adult protector part that is called forth during session 2 is posited to represent the new adaptive memory network into which previously traumatic memories can be integrated.

Finally, TRTP is delivered over 6–8 h of therapy, over 3–4 weeks. An analysis of the “Better Access” program of government-subsidised mental health care in Australia showed that with an average of only 5–7 sessions, 43–55% of clients demonstrated at least a Minimal Clinically Important Difference, here defined as an improvement with small to medium Cohen’s effect size of 0.3; while 22–32% of clients deteriorated to the same degree [41]. This ‘real-world’ analysis should be compared with trial data, where it is generally observed that in order to assist 50% of participants to achieve clinically meaningful improvement via commonly delivered psychotherapies, somewhere between 8 and 18 h are required [42–44]. If TRTP is shown in future studies to achieve significant improvement or resolution of symptoms in a substantial proportion of clients, this may represent a significantly reduced investment of time compared to established methods. TRTP can be delivered by a diverse range of practitioners, and can be delivered face-to-face or over video conference. Given that, globally, health systems struggle to meet demand [45], a therapy that may deliver outcomes with low use of resources may be highly important.

An unpublished audit conducted by Trauma Therapy Training Inc. in 2017–18 of 715 clients treated with TRTP by 28 therapists appeared to show positive results, with mean reductions in the self-reported 21-item Depression Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS21) [46] post-therapy compared to pre-therapy greater than 1 standard deviation2. However, TRTP has no formal evidence base, so this study was conceived as a first step in establishing whether TRTP can and should be evaluated in a larger randomised trial. Given that TRTP has already been protocolised and delivered in the community, our main objectives were to explore the feasibility of studying the therapy in a community setting (including recruitment, retention, and data collection processes), to document the safety and acceptability of TRTP, and to gather preliminary indications of its outcomes.

Methods

Trial design

This was a before-after single-arm trial.

Participants

The study population was drawn from consecutive new adult clients (excluding couples) who presented to one experienced psychotherapist3 in Brisbane, Australia, requesting either individual psychotherapy or TRTP, between 22 December 2022 and 15 November 2023.

Inclusion criteria

At least mild levels of depression demonstrated by a score of above 9 on the DASS21 Depression scale, OR at least mild levels of anxiety demonstrated by a score of above 7 on the DASS21 Anxiety scale, OR at least mild stress levels demonstrated by a score of above 15 on the DASS21 Stress scale, OR possible PTSD diagnosis demonstrated by PCL-5 score above 31–33.

At least mild levels of depression demonstrated by a score of above 9 on the DASS21 Depression scale, OR at least mild levels of anxiety demonstrated by a score of above 7 on the DASS21 Anxiety scale, OR at least mild stress levels demonstrated by a score of above 15 on the DASS21 Stress scale, OR possible PTSD diagnosis demonstrated by PCL-5 score above 31–33.

Exclusion criteria

Prior diagnosis of a psychotic illness, Schizophrenia, Bipolar Affective Disorder, or Personality Disorder.

Prior diagnosis of a psychotic illness, Schizophrenia, Bipolar Affective Disorder, or Personality Disorder.

Drug and alcohol abuse such that sobriety could not be guaranteed for the duration of the therapy.

Therapy was provided on a fee-for-service basis as per the therapist’s usual practice. The therapist gauged suitability for TRTP, and initial willingness to accept a course of TRTP, according to his usual processes. The therapist then discussed study participation, briefly referring to the trial and requesting the client’s permission to pass their details to the Principal Investigator, but indicating that their agreement (or otherwise) to this request would have no impact on the therapeutic relationship. Following this, consent processes were handled by the Principal Investigator by phone, followed by online written consent, according to the processes and scripts approved by the ethics committee. This procedure was designed to reduce the risks of perceived coercion, either by the therapist or the Principal Investigator, to be involved in the trial. Recruitment continued until 20 participants had completed TRTP and follow-up assessment.

Procedures

This feasibility study used a before-after single-arm trial design, collecting standardised measures of depression, anxiety, stress and trauma-related symptoms in consecutive new adult clients presenting to one community psychotherapist in Brisbane, Australia. No randomisation or control group was involved. Due to the nature of the intervention blinding was not possible.

TRTP was delivered, in three ‘steps’ consistent with the established protocol, over a minimum of three, weekly, two-hour sessions, with additional sessions scheduled, if needed, until completion of TRTP step 3 (Fig. 1).

Study workflow

During or after each therapy session, the therapist recorded the following for the trial: the date and duration of the session, delivery method (face-to-face, video or phone), which step(s) of TRTP was/were covered (to ensure compliance with the three-step therapy protocol), Subjective Units of Distress Scores (SUDS) where relevant, any adverse events, additional information. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools [47, 48].

Measures

A feasibility framework, involving 11 study-related and 4 intervention-related criteria, was adapted from published frameworks [49, 50], and designed around 5 broad objectives, evaluating: (1) recruitment and retention capability, (2) data collection procedures, (3) resourcing, (4) acceptability and safety of TRTP, and (5) change in scores (see Tables 3 and 4). Our findings were reported according to the CONSORT Extension to Pilot and Feasibility Trials [55, 56]. These 15 criteria were a mixture of qualitative and quantitative. Key quantitative criteria were pre-specified (recruitment rate of 50%, retention rate of 75%) for sample size determination and were in line with comparable published rates [53, 57–60]. We did not include a criterion for adherence, because it was considered that—given the therapist was a highly experienced TRTP practitioner who is very familiar with the treatment protocol—completion of the three steps of TRTP and follow-up (retention) would be an adequate marker of adherence.

Qualitative measures

The therapist was able to note any adverse events occurring in session by a simple tick box, with room for qualitative comments about the adverse event or any other aspect of the session. The qualitative data collected from the participants prior to commencement of session 1 was therapy-related, and not collected for the trial. However, once the key traumatic memories were chosen for reprocessing in session 2, the therapist distilled the core aspects of each memory into a phrase that was recorded in the qualitative data. This data enabled him to track SUDS on each distinct memory at different time points during therapy, but was not formally analysed for the trial. Qualitative data was elicited from the participants by electronic survey at or after the follow-up consultation, via the following question: “Would you like to make any comments about whether or not TRTP was helpful to you?” The meaning of ‘helpful’ was not defined, and wording of this question was deliberately neutral to allow participants to record any benefits or adverse effects according to their own interpretation.

Quantitative measures

To explore objective 5 (change in scores), mean changes at each step of therapy (Step 2, Step 3 and follow-up 1 month after completion of therapy) compared to pre-therapy (Step 1) for depression, anxiety and stress symptoms were measured using the self-reported 21-item Depression Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS21) [46]. The three DASS21 sub-scales independently measure negative emotional states of depression, anxiety and stress with items scored on a four-point Likert scale (0 = “never over the past week” to 3 = “Applied to me very much, or most of the time over the past week”). Scores are summed for each sub-scale (Depression, Anxiety, and Stress each consisting of 7 items) and then doubled [61]. Each DASS21 sub-scale score ranges from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating greater distress. The DASS21 has been demonstrated to be highly reliable, with Cronbach’s alpha values in one study of 0.81, 0.89 and 0.78 for the sub-scales of depression, anxiety and stress respectively. It has also been found to have excellent internal consistency, discriminative, concurrent and convergent validities [62]. DASS21 reports were collected at or before each session, and at follow-up. Each sub-scale of the DASS is scored based on deviation from population norms with interpretive categories ranging from ‘normal’ through to ‘mild’, ‘moderate’, ‘severe’ and ‘extremely severe’ [63]. Participants were classified as having elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and stress based on cut-off scores of above 9, 7 and 15 for each sub-scale, respectively.

Change in mean PTSD symptoms post and pre-therapy was assessed using the 20-item PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (the PCL-5) [64]. This self-reported measure has been shown to have acceptable internal consistency, test–retest reliability and construct validity [65]. Items on this scale include, for example, “in the past month, how much were you bothered by feeling jumpy or easily startled?”. These are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 “not at all” to 4 “extremely” and then summed giving a potential score range from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater distress. A score of above 31–33 is usually taken as suggestive of a diagnosis of PTSD [66]. The PCL-5 scores were collected prior to session 1 and at follow-up 1-month post-therapy.

To further explore the data from a safety perspective (objective 4), binary variables were derived to look for clinically meaningful change in the Depression, Anxiety and Stress scores at 1-month post-therapy from baseline. Following Ronk et al., we defined clinically meaningful change on the DASS sub-scales as a change of 6.2 points or more for Depression, 7.0 or more for Anxiety and 6.2 or more for Stress [67]. A binary variable was created to look for clinically meaningful change in individual PCL-5 scores post-therapy. Following Blanchard et al., we defined clinically meaningful change on the PCL-5 at least 12 points [68].

Subjective Units of Distress Scales [26] (SUDS) are self-reported measures of distress on a 0–10 scale, with higher scores indicating greater distress. The presumed impact of step 2 on reducing the emotional charge on each distressing memory was assessed by administering the SUDS prior to, then immediately following, the memory reprocessing.

Sample size

Prior to study commencement, assuming a modest correlation between participants’ pre- and post-treatment DASS sub-scores of 0.3 to 0.5, we determined that 20 participants who complete the therapy and provide responses to the surveys pre and 1-month post-therapy, would provide 80 to 90% power (significance level = 5%) to detect a mean change in the DASS21 sub-scores at 1 month post-therapy compared to baseline of 0.8 of one standard deviation (feasibility objective 5). We anticipated that 50% of new clients would consent to the study, and 75% of those would complete the study [53, 57–60], hence to achieve a sample size of 20 participants who complete the study we would need to approach 54 individuals, to recruit 27 who agree to the study. Given that the therapist predicted that he would be approached by about eight new clients per month, we determined that it would take approximately 7 months to approach and recruit 27 participants.

Sample size justification

We aimed to recruit enough participants so that 20 would complete the therapy, which was within the range most commonly adopted in practice for pilot and feasibility studies using continuous outcomes [69] and consistent with that recommended for pilot studies when a large effect size is anticipated [70]. Furthermore, there were budget and time constraints on recruiting more participants into the study. The proposed sample size would allow us to evaluate the trial procedures and processes in the community setting and to start building the evidence-base to support the future implementation of a larger controlled trial of TRTP. The percentage of individuals recruited and retained at the end of the study was reported with 95% confidence intervals to provide information on the degree of uncertainty of the estimates.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted according to a predefined analysis plan, and independently of the Principal Investigator. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the participants’ demographics, outcome measures at each assessment time, and process measures. Percentage of participants recruited and retained in the study was presented with exact 95% confidence intervals (CI). To assess feasibility objective 5 (change in scores) a linear mixed effects model with restricted maximum likelihood and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom was used to estimate the mean change for each DASS21 sub-scale outcome at each assessment time point (step 2, step 3, and follow-up) compared to baseline (step 1). Time was fitted as a fixed effect (with step 1 as the reference group) and individuals as random effect to allow for repeated measures on the same individual. A similar mixed effects model was used to estimate the mean change in PCL-5 scores at follow-up compared to baseline. A paired t-test was used to estimate the mean change in SUDS scores (final scores compared to initial). Estimates were reported with 95% confidence interval (CI). Analyses were conducted using Stata Statistical Software version 17.0 [71].

We used Hedges g* method to estimate the standardised effect size for paired observations (with associated confidence intervals) for the DASS21 and PCL-5 measures, due to the small sample size (n = 20) with unequal variance in pre and post scores. Hedges g* was calculated using an online tool [72]. Five thousand bootstrap samples were taken, and 5000 reshuffles of the control and test labels were performed; the confidence interval is bias-corrected and accelerated.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the number of scores that met the cut-points for clinically meaningful change at follow-up compared to pre-therapy for each of the distress scores (depression, anxiety, stress, PCL-5). At the participant level we deemed that an individual had demonstrated overall deterioration if at least one of their distress scores increased by the nominated thresholds; and clinically meaningful improvement if at least one of their scores decreased by at least the threshold amount and no score increased by that amount.

Regarding the qualitative data: responses to the question to the participants in the follow-up survey about whether or not TRTP was helpful to them were analysed according to whether they responded in the direct affirmative using the word ‘helpful’ or ‘helped’ or similar words such as ‘healing’, or used other descriptors clearly indicating improvement. Qualitative comments by the therapist were separated into those that were process-related and those that reflected on outcomes, and only the latter were captured.

Results

Recruitment

Over about 11 months, between 22 December 2022 and 15 November 2023, the therapist was approached by 41 new clients. Figure 2 shows that 5 (12%) were assessed as unsuitable for TRTP according to the exclusion criteria. There were 6 people (15%) who were deemed suitable but declined TRTP. Of the remaining 30, 5 (12%) agreed to have TRTP but declined to be in the study, leaving a total of 25 (61%) who consented to participate. Two were later excluded prior to commencement, having been belatedly deemed ineligible: one being a minor, and another whose DASS21 and PCL-5 scores normalised prior to therapy hence the client was not assessed as requiring TRTP. Of the 40 new adult clients (excluding the 16-year-old), 23 (58%) were eligible and consented to the study. The recruitment percentage was higher than the predicted 50%, with 95% confidence intervals ranging from 41 to 73%.

Retention

Twenty-three commenced TRTP, of whom three (13%) withdrew and were subsequently lost to follow-up prior to step 3. Hence, 20 (87%) of the 23 who consented to the study completed TRTP and all follow-up measures and questionnaires. The 95% confidence interval ranged between 65 and 97%, which included our predicted rate of 75%. The intervention was delivered and all follow-up measures were collected over a period of 10 months between May 2023 and March 2024.

Descriptive statistics

Individual trajectories and scores at each assessment time point for each participant are provided in the Supplementary information.

No individual DASS21 scores met criteria for clinically meaningful deterioration, as defined above. Eleven out of 12 (92%) initially abnormal Depression scores, 11/14 (79%) initially abnormal Anxiety scores, and 13/17 (76%) initially abnormal Stress scores demonstrated improvement that would be clinically meaningful if found to be therapy-related in the context of a subsequent effectiveness trial.

Before treatment commencement, more than half of the participants (12/23, 52%) scored above the threshold for a likely diagnosis of PTSD (score 31–33). By the follow-up session, none (0/20, 0%) of the PCL-5 scores were at or above this threshold, although one of those original twelve was among the three non-completers on whom we do not have paired data. The remaining eleven all improved to a degree that would be clinically meaningful if found to be therapy-related in the context of a subsequent effectiveness trial.

Overall, at a participant level, no individuals showed clinically meaningful deterioration, as defined above. Instead, 90% (18/20) of individuals demonstrated overall improvement between step one and follow-up.

SUDS scores

SUDS scores were recorded before and at completion of memory reprocessing (on all 22 participants who undertook step 2) for an average of 3.4 memories per participant (standard deviation of 0.5, range 3 to 4). Pre- and post-therapy SUDS scores were averaged across all memories within an individual and then compared across all participants, with average final SUDS 6.2 points lower (on a 10-point scale) than average initial SUDS (95% CI 5.4 to 7.0).

Qualitative outcomes

The therapist’s brief descriptions of the traumatic events chosen for memory reprocessing have not been formally analysed for this study because they were primarily collected to facilitate the therapeutic process and therefore were not always interpretable to others. However, it may be of use for a general understanding of TRTP to include here that insofar as these records were interpretable, the events included parental abuse, violence, neglect, abandonment and conflict; sexual abuse and assault; bullying at school and in the workplace; betrayal, abuse, rejection and violence in relationships; death of loved ones; and early parentification.

Fifteen out of twenty treatment completers responded to the follow-up question as to whether or not the therapy had been helpful to them. All fifteen responses indicated that it had been helpful, either by directly using the words ‘helpful’ or ‘helped’ in their response (11 responses) with or without elaboration, by using the words ‘heal’ or ‘healing’ (2 responses) with or without elaboration, or by other responses clearly indicating improvement (2 responses) via phrases such as “I feel more prepared than I could imagine (to face challenging circumstances)”, and “more grounded” “more calm”, “not angry or frustrated when stressed”. One out of those 15 reported some persistence of symptoms (insomnia), and the therapist’s comments indicated persistence of symptoms at the follow-up session for a further two participants. The therapist reported an in-session adverse event in one participant (dissociation in session), and two participants reported non-serious adverse events after completion of therapy (new joint and muscle pain; low/angry mood 1–2 weeks after final session leading to change of medication). The therapist reported on which step of TRTP was undertaken in each session, and in all treatment completers this data indicated that he followed the standard three steps of TRTP.

Discussion

This study constitutes the first formal research into TRTP—a novel therapy that is already being delivered in Australia and elsewhere. We have set out many learnings from this study that can inform the design and implementation of a future trial (see Tables 3 and 4). Our findings support the feasibility of studying TRTP in a community setting, and the acceptability and safety of the method. Unmet criteria related to the study design can be addressed in future larger studies by streamlining of procedures for eligibility determination, using a larger number of therapists to deliver the intervention, modifying the REDCap platform design, and improving researcher education and oversight. The observed recruitment and retention of participants (58% and 87%) were higher than we had anticipated, but the confidence intervals were wide, where the lower confidence interval bounds were 41% and 65%, respectively. Given the uncertainty around the estimates for the percentage of participants recruited and retained, determination of sample size parameters for a future larger trial may be informed by similar published studies or a larger pilot study. Further, as part of a definitive trial, an internal pilot study may be used to inform the process of planning and to monitor the trial feasibility, including the number of participants recruited, randomised and retained, in real time.

In relation to the safety and acceptability of TRTP, all 20 treatment completers were given the opportunity to comment on the therapy, and all who took this opportunity (15/15) reported that it was helpful, with three of those reporting some persistence of milder symptoms. There were no negative comments about the therapy. However, feedback was not captured on the three individuals who failed to complete the therapy. No individual scores or participants demonstrated clinically meaningful deterioration. Instead, a high proportion of scores and individuals improved to a degree that would be clinically meaningful if found to be therapy-related in the context of a subsequent effectiveness trial.

In relation to change in scores, mean DASS21 and PCL-5 scores decreased by one standard deviation pre- to post-therapy. These changes are consistent with previous research on other therapy-based interventions in people affected by trauma [73] showing changes to the mean DASS21 of equivalent to 0.8 standard deviations or more.

Without a control group, this study does not constitute evidence of a causal relationship between TRTP and reduced measures of distress. We cannot preclude the possibility that the improvement in the mean scores may be attributable to regression towards the mean, or to external factors. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that TRTP may be a promising therapy warranting further study. The fact that Richards only makes the manual available through her formal training may make independent replication challenging.

Three out of 23 participants did not complete the therapy or follow-up measures, hence they did not contribute paired data to the quantitative analysis. A future trial should attempt to collect primary outcome measures even on participants who do not complete the therapy; and this data should be included as part of an intention-to-treat analysis if they do not withdraw all their data.

It is highly likely that there would be therapist bias in the current sample, given that only one therapist was involved. Self-selection bias may have been introduced by the fact that some of the participants specifically requested TRTP hence may have been positively disposed towards the therapy or the therapist. The fact that this therapy is promoted as an alternative to existing therapies may have created positive or negative expectancy effects.

While information about age and gender of participants was captured, the collection in a future effectiveness trial of other demographic data such as ethnic background and socio-economic status would assist with understanding the generalisability of any findings. It would also be desirable to improve the collection and analysis of qualitative data regarding the nature of any clinical change, clients’ experience of the process, and types of traumatic events reprocessed. The reduction in SUDS at step 2 may provide evidence for the hypothesised mechanism of change, should future trials demonstrate effectiveness. This could be supplemented by a dismantling study that gathers rich descriptions of clients’ reactivity to traumatic memories in order to further delineate the mechanism of action.

The fact that the therapist was highly experienced in delivering TRTP may distort the results, so further research will be needed to ascertain the generalisability of this method when delivered by a range of practitioners, and in other contexts. If effectiveness could be demonstrated in a controlled trial involving a greater range of practitioners, this may point to a potential for TRTP to be widely delivered in low health resource settings. This, along with the fact that TRTP is routinely delivered remotely via video conferencing, may reduce several of the major barriers to accessing effective psychotherapy.

None of the participants in this study received a formal DSM-5 diagnosis prior to treatment, hence it is not possible to clearly delineate the population studied beyond ‘new clients presenting to a psychotherapist scoring above cut-off on standardised measures of distress’. Future studies should explore the effectiveness of TRTP for specific mental health conditions. If benefit is demonstrated, it would be helpful for future studies to include economic analysis of cost-effectiveness and acceptability compared to existing therapies.

Conclusions

This study suggests that it would be feasible to study TRTP in a community setting, with initial indications that it is acceptable and safe. A future larger randomised controlled trial incorporating waitlist or active controls, or a non-inferiority trial comparing TRTP to more established therapies, would be warranted based on these findings, incorporating the learnings gained from this study to inform the design and conduct of the trial.
