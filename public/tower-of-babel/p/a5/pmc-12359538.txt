Admissions and outcomes after perinatal asphyxia and hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy before and after therapeutic hypothermia: a retrospective population-based study
Authors: Karoline Aker, Janicke M Syltern, Miriam Martinez-Biarge, Ragnhild Støen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) is the leading cause of brain injury in term infants, and therapeutic hypothermia (TH) has been shown to improve outcomes for infants with moderate/severe HIE. We aimed to describe admission rates and outcomes after perinatal asphyxia and HIE before and after the implementation of TH in June 2007.MethodsThis single-centre, retrospective, population-based study included term/near-term infants born between January 2003 and December 2011 and admitted to a level III neonatal unit with International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision diagnoses P20, P21, P90 and/or P91, and a clinical picture compatible with asphyxia/HIE. Outcome measures were clinical characteristics during neonatal admission and survival without cerebral palsy (CP) at 9–10 years. Data were collected from medical records and the Norwegian Quality and Surveillance Registry for CP.ResultsThe admission rate related to perinatal asphyxia was 7.63 per 1000 term/near-term live births and did not change over time. The incidence of HIE increased significantly after the implementation of TH (from 2.69 to 4.21 per 1000 term/near-term live births, incidence rate ratio 1.56 (95% CI 1.06 to 2.34)). Among 255 included infants (62% boys, mean birth weight 3691 g), significantly more infants were diagnosed with HIE after the implementation of TH compared with before (54% vs 36%, respectively, p=0.005). A total of 227 (92%) of 247 infants survived without CP, with no difference between the time periods. Among infants with moderate/severe HIE born after the implementation of TH, 20 (67%) of 30 infants were cooled and 80% of cooled and 33% of non-cooled infants survived without CP.ConclusionsAdmission rates and outcomes were stable during these 9 years, but more infants were reported with HIE after the implementation of TH. Non-cooled infants with moderate/severe HIE born after the implementation of TH had poor outcomes, and early clinical HIE evaluation remains a major challenge.

Introduction

Perinatal asphyxia and hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) continue to be among the primary causes of neonatal morbidity and mortality globally. Therapeutic hypothermia (TH) has been found to improve outcomes for infants with moderate and severe HIE and was introduced in clinical practice around the world between 2007 and 2010. The latest Cochrane review reported death or major disability at 18–24 months in 46% of cooled and 61% of non-cooled infants, hence a 25% relative risk reduction with cooling.1 A more recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) reported an even lower rate (29%) of adverse outcomes among cooled infants.2

Therapeutic hypothermia has a short therapeutic window and has, therefore, made it essential to classify the severity of HIE as soon as possible and, at the latest, within 6 hours of age.1 3 However, the HIE staging used in clinical practice was not designed to identify moderate/severe HIE during the first few postnatal hours.46 A correct diagnosis of perinatal asphyxia and HIE is also difficult due to the lack of uniform and objective definitions.79 Population-based studies on the incidence and outcomes after assumed perinatal asphyxia and HIE often rely on one or only a few proxy variables, such as Apgar scores, cord blood pH or provision of TH.1012 Both Apgar and cord pH have poor sensitivity and specificity for adverse childhood outcomes.13 14 Incidence rates of moderate/severe HIE based on the provision of TH alone will be inaccurate. Infants with mild HIE are increasingly being cooled, and a significant number of infants with moderate/severe HIE are, for various reasons, not cooled.1518 To accurately monitor the clinical significance of perinatal hypoxia/ischaemia severe enough to necessitate admission for neonatal care, its treatment and its consequences, studies with access to detailed individual data are needed.

We studied admissions related to perinatal asphyxia/HIE to a Norwegian tertiary-level neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) from 2003 to 2011 and collected data on survival without cerebral palsy (CP) at 9–10 years of age. The aim was to provide population-based temporal trends in admissions and outcomes before and after the implementation of TH in June 2007.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective, population-based observational study of term and near-term infants admitted to a level III NICU with a diagnosis and clinical history consistent with perinatal asphyxia between 2003 and 2011. The unit is the only tertiary care referral centre in the county and the only unit offering TH. Throughout the study period, there were an additional one to three birth units from which all infants in need of medical care were referred.

All infants born at or near term (gestational age (GA) ≥36 weeks) in the county between January 2003 and December 2011 who were admitted to the NICU for more than 24 hours with an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision diagnosis related to perinatal asphyxia were eligible (figure 1A). The diagnoses were identified through the patient administrative system and local admission protocols, and all relevant medical records were reviewed to ascertain that the inclusion criteria were met. Eligible infants were included if they had clinical or biochemical signs of perinatal asphyxia, defined as metabolic acidosis, low Apgar, need for resuscitation at birth and/or renal, liver or neurological dysfunction. Infants with major congenital malformations, syndromes expected to seriously affect the life span (such as trisomy 13 and 18), and infants with a medical course consistent with other diagnoses (eg, metabolic disorder, infection, severe lung disease and congenital anomalies) were excluded. Three infants with sudden unexpected collapse during the first postnatal hours were excluded. Included infants were defined as having had perinatal asphyxia. Patient and public involvement

Patients, parents/caregivers and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Data collection

Background data on the number of live births at or above GA 36 weeks in the county was collected from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway. Data on birth weight, GA, 5 min Apgar score and cord blood pH in the background population were collected from local databases. Medical data from the initial admission were collected from hospital records. All medical records were reviewed for information about HIE status and neurological symptoms. If data on HIE status were missing, a retrospective HIE classification was done based on the description of neurological symptoms during the first 72 hours according to a modified Sarnat score by an experienced neonatologist (RS) blinded to long-term outcomes.19 All infants admitted with mild HIE or missing data on initial HIE status, but who presented with seizures within 24 hours of life, were reclassified as moderate HIE. Infants with moderate or severe HIE were grouped together due to difficulties distinguishing them based on a retrospective chart review.

Amplitude-integrated electroencephalography (aEEG) was routinely used for evaluating infants for TH but was not consistently reported in the medical records and, therefore, not included in this study. Cerebral MRI done as part of regular clinical practice was, for research purposes, reviewed and scored according to Rutherford by a neonatologist (MM-B) with experience in neuroimaging.20 The MRIs were classified as normal/mildly abnormal (normal or mild basal ganglia and thalami (BGT) score and/or normal, mild or moderate white matter (WM) score) or moderately/severely abnormal (moderate or severe BGT score and/or absent posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC), and/or severe WM score). Images were also categorised in seven patterns of brain injury: normal (no signs of abnormalities), only mild/moderate WM score, only severe WM score, mild BGT score with normal, mild or moderate WM score, mild BGT score with severe WM score, moderate BGT score with any WM score and global brain injury (severe BGT score, WM and cortex with absent PLIC and brainstem abnormalities). The assessor was blinded to HIE status, study period and treatment.

Long-term outcome

An adverse outcome was defined as death or any diagnosis of CP at age 9–10 years. CP was diagnosed in accordance with the Surveillance of CP in Europe.21 Data on CP, CP-subtype and Gross Motor Function Classification System22 were collected from the Norwegian Quality and Surveillance Registry for CP (NorCP) and the patient administrative system. The NorCP is a consent-based national medical quality registry established in 2001, and medical information on children with CP is collected at the time of diagnosis and at ages 5 and 15–17 years.23

Statistical analysis

The study cohort was divided into two groups for comparison: infants born before (January 2003 to May 2007) and after (June 2007 to December 2011) the implementation of TH. An incidence rate ratio (IRR) calculator was used to compare the incidences of perinatal asphyxia, HIE, moderately/severely abnormal MRI and adverse outcomes between the two periods, reported as IRR with 95% confidence interals (CIs). Clinical data are presented as total count and percentage for categorical variables and as mean and SD or median and IQR for continuous variables as appropriate. The χ2, Fisher’s exact test or linear-by-linear Association was used to assess the differences in proportions between the groups for categorical variables, and the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess the differences between groups for continuous variables. Any values of p<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Clinical variables in infants with moderate/severe HIE born after the implementation of TH were compared according to the provision of TH. Statistical analyses were done using the IBM SPSS Statistics (V.27, IBM), and the IRR was calculated in Stata statistical software (V.17, StataCorp).

Results

From 2003 to 2011, there were a total of 33 403 live births at or near term in the county (table 1). The overall rate of caesarean sections and operative vaginal deliveries was 15.9% and 8.6%, respectively, with no difference between the two periods. The incidence of NICU admissions related to perinatal asphyxia was 7.63 per 1000 term/near-term live births with no difference between the periods (table 1). There was a significant increase in the incidence of any HIE after the implementation of TH (2.69 before vs 4.21 per 1000 after, IRR 1.56 (95% CI 1.06 to 2.34)).

138 of 255 (54%) infants had no HIE, 70 (27%) had mild HIE and 47 (18%) had moderate/severe HIE. A larger proportion of included infants was diagnosed with HIE after the implementation of TH (54% vs 36%, respectively, p=0.005). The retrospective evaluation of HIE diagnoses resulted in a reclassification of HIE in 18 of 116 (16%) and 19 of 139 (14%) infants before and after the implementation of TH, respectively.

26 infants received TH after June 2007, of which 20 had moderate/severe HIE, 5 had mild HIE and 1 had no HIE. The infant with no HIE was started on TH but discontinued after 3 hours due to a normal EEG and no HIE. Three of the five cooled infants classified with mild HIE had abnormal aEEG on admission.

10 of 30 (33%) infants with moderate/severe HIE after the implementation of TH were not cooled. Of these, one had redirection of care due to a poor prognosis and died at 3 hours of life. One was not considered for TH due to an assumed prenatal insult. The remaining eight had a delayed diagnosis of HIE due to other concurrent diseases (n=1) or late presentation of seizures (beyond 6 hours of life, n=7). Two infants with moderate/severe HIE had TH discontinued due to normal EEG (n=1) and respiratory and circulatory instability (n=1).

Eight (3%) infants did not have available outcomes at 9–10 years of age due to emigration. 227 of 247 (92%) infants survived without CP (table 2). Nine infants died, all with moderate/severe HIE. Two of 133 (2%) infants with no HIE and 2 of 69 (3%) infants with mild HIE and available outcomes were diagnosed with CP (figure 2). Among infants with moderate/severe HIE, 6 of 16 (38%) and 10 of 29 (34%) infants had an adverse outcome before and after the implementation of TH, respectively. Discussion

In this population-based observational study, admission rates for neonatal care related to perinatal asphyxia/HIE did not change after the implementation of TH. There was, however, a significant increase in the incidence of HIE among admitted infants. Outcomes were especially poor among infants with moderate/severe HIE who did not receive TH despite being born after June 2007, when TH was made available. Outcomes among cooled infants were better than reported in previous RCTs, but population-based incidences of death or CP did not change after TH.

The unclear definitions of perinatal asphyxia make it challenging to compare rates between studies.92426 Two studies defining asphyxia by a low 5 min Apgar score alone reported incidences of 5.38 and 8.5 per 1000 live births, which is similar to our finding.25 26 The incidence of moderate/severe HIE of 1.4 per 1000 reported in this study is consistent with other contemporary studies.16 27 28 An incidence of 0.77 per 1000 was found in a population-based Spanish study; however, this was based on a survey with well-recognised limitations.29 Low incidences of moderate/severe HIE have also been reported from Sweden (0.67 and 0.8 per 1000) using TH as a proxy for the diagnosis.30 31 This is problematic because TH for mild HIE has increased in many settings,12 16 whereas some studies, including ours, have shown that a substantial proportion of infants with moderate/severe HIE do not receive TH.15 16

The increased incidence of HIE after the implementation of TH in combination with lower Apgar scores and more need for chest compressions may reflect a real increase in clinically significant perinatal hypoxic-ischaemic events. However, that should also have led to increased admission rates related to perinatal asphyxia, which we did not observe. The proportion of outborn infants was also similar between the two periods, making changes in referral practices unlikely. Recent guidelines encourage close monitoring of infants with suspected HIE to evaluate the need for TH.32 It is likely that the availability of a neuroprotective treatment has raised clinicians’ awareness of neurological signs and symptoms. The rapid evolution of encephalopathy after birth, the subjective nature of HIE scoring, and the time pressure to select infants for TH may have made clinicians more prone to diagnose encephalopathy compared with the period before cooling.

In the present study, one-third of infants with moderate/severe HIE born after TH became available were not cooled. These infants had significantly lower birth weight, higher cord pH and less resuscitation at birth compared with cooled infants, and it may be speculated that infants in this group had more prenatal insults. They also had a high incidence of severe brain injuries on MRI and poor outcomes, which has also been reported by others.33 34 In contrast, cooled infants had a low incidence of abnormal MRI and better outcomes than reported in the original TH trials.1 20 35 This is consistent with several more recent reports after the implementation of TH in clinical practice23639 and has been explained by a drift towards cooling of less severely affected infants,17 18 40 earlier initiation of TH, overall advancements in obstetric and neonatal care or a combination of these factors. This, together with poor outcomes among non-cooled infants, highlights the importance of improving the entry criteria for TH.12 16 17 The majority of non-cooled infants had a delayed HIE diagnosis, and it is possible that an early aEEG would have improved early identification.33

In this study, there was no increase in survival without CP after the implementation of cooling, neither on a population level nor among infants classified with moderate/severe HIE. Others have reported that the incidence and severity of CP decreased in Norway over the years 1999–2010 and suggested that this may be related to the implementation of TH in clinical practice.41 Whether timely selection for cooling of all infants with moderate/severe HIE would have improved outcomes with time is uncertain. Future research should assess the need for a more structured clinical pathway with early aEEG monitoring of infants admitted to an NICU after a suspected hypoxic-ischaemic event.

The main strengths of this study are the population-based design and the use of individual detailed clinical data, which ascertained that all eligible infants were screened for inclusion. We argue that the combination of broad inclusion criteria and detailed exclusion criteria based on individual patient data ascertained a more accurate estimate of the clinical significance of perinatal asphyxia and HIE compared with studies using proxy variables like Apgar scores or provision of TH.

The retrospective collection of data is a limitation. Poor documentation of HIE status made it necessary to reclassify the HIE diagnoses in some of the infants. However, the proportion of infants that were reclassified was similar in the two periods. Without prospective data collection, this may still be the best available method. Another limitation is the lack of data on impairments other than CP (cognitive, behavioural and sensorineural), which may affect a substantial proportion of infants with HIE.42 43 These outcomes may greatly impact the lives of these infants but were, unfortunately, not available in this study.

Conclusions

The increased incidence of HIE after the implementation of TH may be due to a greater focus on neurological signs and symptoms after a neuroprotective treatment became available. Survival without CP did not change over time, but this was mainly driven by poor outcomes among infants with moderate/severe HIE who were not cooled. A majority of them had seizures presenting after 6 hours of life. This highlights the need for further research to improve the early selection of infants who may benefit from TH.
