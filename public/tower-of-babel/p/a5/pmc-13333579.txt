Size dependent antibacterial mechanism of magnesium oxide nanoparticles against Ralstonia solanacearum
Authors: Kun Nie, Qiulan Xia, Kai Yuan, Zongze Geng, Dong Zhou, Lin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnesium oxide nanoparticles (MgONPs) demonstrate size-influenced antibacterial activity against Ralstonia solanacearum, with physical disruption mechanisms appearing to play a more prominent role than reactive oxygen species (ROS) effects under the tested conditions. While 30 nm particles generated maximum ROS levels, 20 nm MgONPs exhibited superior bactericidal efficacy, reducing survival to 36% at 300 mg/L versus 46% for 30 nm particles—a superiority consistently supported by motility, membrane, and transcriptomic assessments even at the complete-killing concentration (500 mg/L). This size-dependent efficacy was further validated in vivo. ROS scavenging tests and transcriptomics suggested that oxidative stress may not be the primary driver of antimicrobial action, though a potential synergistic role cannot be entirely excluded. The primary mechanisms involved size-specific physical membrane damage, DNA integrity changes, and metabolic interference. Smaller particles (20 nm) caused significant membrane destabilization and uniquely disrupted acetyl-CoA metabolism and oxidoreductase activity through downregulation of ribosome biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and amino acid degradation pathways. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that particle size modulates catabolic gene expression and redox-related processes, with 20 nm particles inducing distinct metabolic suppression patterns. This study provides insights into the ongoing debate regarding ROS versus physical mechanisms, suggesting that nanoscale precision combined with surface properties governs antibacterial efficiency. The findings position MgONPs as a tunable antimicrobial platform where optimized particle dimensions and formulation strategies enhance pathogen control through targeted physical disruption and metabolic interference. These mechanistic insights offer a basis for engineering nanomaterials with improved bactericidal performance via optimization of multiple physicochemical properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04771-8.

Introduction

Metal Oxide Nanoparticles (MONPs), with characteristics like high specific surface area, adjustable surface activity, and environmental stability, exhibit revolutionary potential in the realm of green prevention and control of plant diseases [1, 15]. On one hand, MONPs display potent broad-spectrum antibacterial or insecticidal capabilities and are capable of effectively suppressing the growth of diverse plant pathogens and pests [20, 26, 38]. On the other hand, MONPs also have the functions of promoting plant growth and enhancing plants’ resistance to stress [3, 10, 12, 19, 21]. Among numerous MONPs, magnesium oxide nanoparticles (MgONPs) present wide-ranging application prospects in the domain of agricultural disease prevention and control, thanks to their merits including high thermal stability, excellent biocompatibility, low cost, and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity [16, 29, 45]. MgONPs have shown potent antibacterial potential against a variety of plant pathogens [2, 8, 29, 39, 58, 64], revealing their immense potential as high-efficiency antibacterial agents. However, there is still a great deal of controversy about the exact mechanism of the antibacterial action of MgONPs. Numerous studies have indicated that the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) might play a crucial role in its antibacterial effectiveness [7, 37, 48]. On the surface of MgONPs, oxidation reactions may occur, generating copious ROS such as superoxide radicals (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{O}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radicals (OH−) [29, 37]. The excessive ROS will cause an upsurge in cellular oxidative stress. As a direct consequence, DNA, protein, and cell damage occur, eventually resulting in cell necrosis [7, 33]. In contrast, some studies have shown that there is a size-mediated non-ROS antibacterial mechanism for MgONPs, that is, the material exerts its antibacterial effect by causing mechanical damage, pH reactions, etc. through its own size. Anicˇić et al. [4] discovered that when the production of ROS decreased as a result of reduced surface defects in MgONPs, its antibacterial activity actually increased, which was primarily attributed to the acid–base reaction between the surface of MgONPs and the bacterial cell wall. Leung et al.'s study demonstrated that three different types of MgONPs still exhibited strong toxicity towards Escherichia coli without generating ROS, and their antibacterial mechanism was associated with cell membrane damage [35]. Other research has shown that small sized MgONPs can penetrate cells and directly disrupt cell membranes, resulting in the leakage of cytoplasmic contents and cell death [27, 47]. Meanwhile, there is a distinct size effect in this mechanical damage [57], that is, nanomaterials with smaller particle sizes possess higher antibacterial activity as they can more readily penetrate and damage cells [17, 18]. Moreover, some studies have also indicated that MgONPs possess both ROS-mediated and size effect mediated antibacterial mechanisms [23, 51, 62]. Thus, further clarifying the dominant mechanism (ROS generation or size effect) of the antibacterial effect of MgONPs is not only vital for deepening the antibacterial theory of nanomaterials but also offers a scientific foundation for the precise design and safety evaluation in its agricultural applications.

Based on the above analysis, we synthesized MgONPs of three different sizes (20 nm, 30 nm and 40 nm) to systematically investigate the size‑dependent antibacterial mechanisms against R. solanacearum. We aimed to clarify the controversial relationship between particle size effect and ROS‑mediated damage in MgONP‑induced bacterial inhibition. To achieve this, we performed a series of experiments including in vitro antibacterial activity assays, bacterial motility and biofilm formation tests, cell membrane integrity and DNA damage analyses, as well as intracellular ROS quantification. In parallel, transcriptomic profiling was conducted to capture global gene expression changes in response to different‑sized MgONPs. Furthermore, ROS scavenging assays using ascorbic acid and in vivo tobacco bacterial wilt control experiments were carried out to dissect the relative contributions of ROS stress and physical size effects. Through this integrative approach, we aim to determine whether particle size plays a more dominant role than ROS generation in the antibacterial action of MgONPs against R. solanacearum, and to provide a mechanistic basis for the rational design of size‑optimized MgONP‑based bactericides.

Materials and methods

R. solanacearum strains and growth conditions

R. solanacearum strain CQPS-1 (phylotype I, race 1, biovar 3), originally isolated from an infected tobacco plant, was provided by Ding Wei from Southwest University, China [41]. Strains were routinely grown at 28 ± 2°C in B medium, which consisted of (per liter): 10 g of peptone, 1 g of yeast extract, and 1 g of Casamino Acids, for liquid culture [8]. For plating, the medium was solidified with 15 g of agar and supplemented with 5 g of glucose per liter (BG medium) [41].

Synthesis of MgO nanoparticles

MgCl2·6H2O solution (1.0 mol/L), NaOH solution (0.28 mol/L) and Na2CO3 solution (1.0 mol/L) were mixed in equal volumes into distilled water and fully stirred at 60 °C for 2 h, then washed and filtered to obtain white precipitate. The white precipitate was dried in a drying oven at 80 °C for 1 h. MgONPs with different particle sizes were obtained by calcination at 550 ℃, 650 ℃ and 750 ℃ for 2 h [59].

Characterization of MgO nanoparticles

Transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100; Japanese electron, Tokyo, Japan) and scanning electron microscope (SEM, S3400N; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was used to monitor the morphology of nanoparticles [61]. The surface chemical composition of MgONPs was evaluated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA). X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) analysis was performed on a drop-coated film on a glass substrate from a MgONPs sample. The particle size distribution of MgONPs was evaluated and measured using Nano Brook 90 Plus PALS highly sensitive zeta potential and particle size analyzer [8]. Infrared spectra were obtained by using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR, Nicolet iS20, Thermo Scientific, USA), Brunauer emmett teller (BET, Canta AUTOSORB IQ, Quantachrome Instruments, USA) equation based on the N2 adsorption–desorption used to test the specific surface area.

Antibacterial activity assessment

The antibacterial activity of MgONPs with different particle sizes (20 nm, 30 nm, and 40 nm) against R. solanacearum was evaluated using the plate colony counting method, with reference to the methods described by Cai et al. [8] with slight modifications. R. solanacearum strain CQPS-1 was cultured in B medium at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h to reach the logarithmic growth phase, after which the bacterial culture was adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 1.0, corresponding to approximately 108 CFU/mL. MgONPs of each particle size (20 nm, 30 nm, and 40 nm) were separately prepared as stock solutions at 10,000 mg/L in sterile deionized water and dispersed by ultrasonication to ensure uniform suspension before use. To prepare the treatment mixtures, 1 µL of the bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) and appropriate volumes of the MgONP stock solution were added to 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and diluted with B liquid medium to a final volume of 100 mL. At this point (time zero), the bacterial concentration in each flask was 103 CFU/mL, and the final MgONP concentrations were 500, 400, 300, 200, 100, and 0 mg/L (control), achieved by adding 5.0, 4.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1.0, and 0.0 mL of the stock solution, respectively. Each particle size was tested separately across the full concentration gradient. The mixtures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 8 h. After incubation, the bacterial concentration reached approximately 109–1010 CFU/mL. Then, the resulting culture was diluted 10⁻fivefold, and 10 µL of this dilution was spread onto BG solid medium and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Colony-forming units were counted, and the optimal bactericidal concentration was defined as the lowest concentration of MgONPs at which no bacterial growth was observed on the plates after 48 h of incubation.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\text{Cell Viability}} = \left( {{\mathrm{A}}/{\mathrm{B}}} \right) \, *{ 1}00\% $$\end{document}where A represents the number of Colony forming units after treatment with different concentrations of the drug and B represents the number of Colony forming units on the control plate.

Growth curve assay

The effect of MgONPs on the growth kinetics of R. solanacearum was evaluated by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) over a 24 h period using a UV spectrophotometer. To prepare the treatment mixtures, 10 µL of the bacterial suspension (10⁸ CFU/mL) and appropriate volumes of the fully sonicated MgONP stock (10,000 mg/L) solution were added to 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and diluted with B medium to a final volume of 100 mL. The final bacterial concentration in each flask was 103 CFU/mL, and the final MgONP concentrations were 500, 400, 300, 200, and 0 mg/L (control). The flasks were incubated at 28 °C with continuous shaking at 150 rpm. At 2 h intervals over a 24 h period, each flask was removed from the shaker and allowed to stand for 20 min to allow MgONP particles to settle completely. Then, 2 mL of the supernatant (upper layer of the culture) was aseptically withdrawn, and the OD600 was measured using a UV spectrophotometer. The spectrophotometer was zeroed using B medium without bacteria or MgONPs. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and growth curves were plotted as mean OD600 values with standard deviations. Growth parameters such as lag phase duration, exponential growth rate, and maximum optical density were analyzed using appropriate statistical methods.

Swimming and twitching motility assay

To compare the effects of MgONPs with different particle sizes on the swimming and twitching motility of R. solanacearum, motility assays were performed on BG agar medium with different agar concentrations. For swimming motility, the medium contained 0.3% (w/v) agar; for twitching motility, the medium contained 1.6% (w/v) agar. MgONPs were added to the media to a final concentration of 500 mg/L. An aliquot of 2 µL of R. solanacearum suspension (108 CFU/mL) was spotted onto the center of each plate. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 48 h, after which bacterial migration zones were observed and measured. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

SEM observations of bacterial morphology

The morphological changes of R. solanacearum after treatment with MgONPs were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), referring to our previous study [11]. For each particle size, the 1 mL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) was mixed with the corresponding MgONP stock solution in B liquid medium to achieve a final MgONP concentration of 500 mg/L and a final bacterial concentration of 107 CFU/mL in a total volume of 10 mL. The mixtures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h. After incubation, the bacterial cultures were collected by centrifugation at 8,000×g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the bacterial pellets were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for at least 12 h. The fixed samples were then dehydrated through a graded series of tert-butanol/ethanol mixtures (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), twice for each concentration, with each step lasting 5 min. After dehydration, the tert-butanol was drained, and the samples were placed in a 4 °C refrigerator for 5–10 min until the tert-butanol solidified. The samples were then freeze-dried in a freeze dryer for 30–60 min. The dried samples were coated with gold and observed under SEM. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to detect the elemental composition at the interaction sites. The experiment was repeated at least three times.

Biofilm suppression assay

The effect of MgONPs of different particle sizes on biofilm formation by R. solanacearum was evaluated using the crystal violet staining method in a 96-well plate [44]. Briefly, 1 µL of bacterial suspension (106 CFU/mL), 10 µL of the fully sonicated MgONP stock solution (10,000 mg/L), and 189 µL of B medium were added per well, yielding a final volume of 200 µL with a bacterial concentration of 5 × 103 CFU/mL and MgONP concentration of 500 mg/L. Controls included a MgONP-free control (sterile deionized water replacing nanoparticle stock solution), a nanoparticle-only control (MgONPs with B medium, no bacteria) for each particle size, and a blank control (B medium only). Plates were incubated statically at 28 °C for 24 h and 48 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and wells were gently washed with distilled water. Adherent biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water. Bound crystal violet was solubilized with 200 µL of 95% ethanol for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Absorbance values from nanoparticle-only controls were used to subtract background staining. Each treatment was replicated at least three times.

LIVE/DEAD assays to infer cell membrane integrity

To evaluate cell membrane integrity, bacterial viability was assessed using the Calcein-AM/PI Cell Activity and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Yuanye Bio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial samples were pretreated as described in Sect. 2.7. After treatment, bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000×g for 5 min, washed twice with 1 × Assay Buffer (diluted from 10 × Assay Buffer with sterile deionized water), and resuspended in 1 × Assay Buffer to a final concentration of approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL. For staining, 1 µM Calcein-AM (final concentration) was added to the cell suspension and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. Subsequently, 5 µM propidium iodide (PI, final concentration) was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for an additional 5 min. After staining, cells were centrifuged at 450×g for 5 min, washed once with 1 × PBS, and resuspended in 1 × PBS. A 5 µL aliquot of the stained suspension was placed on a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan). Live cells (with intact membranes) were visualized as green fluorescence (excitation/emission: 490/515 nm), while dead cells (with compromised membranes) exhibited red fluorescence (excitation/emission: 535/617 nm). For quantitative analysis, at least five random fields per sample were captured, and the percentages of live and dead cells were calculated using image analysis software (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, USA). Each treatment was performed in triplicate.

Genomic DNA content analysis

To evaluate the effect of MgONPs with different particle sizes on the genomic DNA content of R. solanacearum, bacterial samples were pretreated as described in Sect. 2.7. After treatment, 10 mL of each bacterial culture was collected by centrifugation at 8000×g for 5 min. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A 40 µL aliquot of the total DNA extract from each sample was mixed with loading buffer and subjected to electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel containing 0.5 µg/mL ethidium bromide. Electrophoresis was performed at 100 V for 40 min in 1 × TAE buffer [31]. DNA bands were visualized under a UV gel documentation system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The intensity of the genomic DNA bands was compared among treatment groups to assess variations in DNA content. To exclude potential interference from residual nanoparticles during DNA extraction, nanoparticle-only controls (nanoparticles processed through the extraction procedure without bacteria) were included to verify that nanoparticles did not introduce artifactual bands or affect electrophoretic mobility. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Accumulation of intracellular oxidative stress induced by nanoparticles

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in R. solanacearum after MgONP treatment were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer's instructions with modifications. DCFH-DA is cell-permeable and, upon intracellular oxidation by ROS, is converted to the highly fluorescent compound dichlorofluorescein (DCF), which can be detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/525 nm [5].

R. solanacearum was cultured and treated with MgONPs (20 nm, 30 nm, and 40 nm at 500 mg/L) as described in Sect. 2.7. After treatment, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000×g for 5 min, washed twice with sterile PBS (pH 7.4), and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of approximately 10⁷ CFU/mL. DCFH-DA was added to a final concentration of 10 μM, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess probe and resuspended in PBS. For qualitative observation, 5 μL of the stained suspension was placed on a glass slide and immediately observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000, Tokyo, Japan) with excitation at 488 nm and emission collected at 500–530 nm. For quantitative analysis, the fluorescence intensity of the stained bacterial suspensions was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/525 nm. The following controls were included in each experiment to exclude non-specific oxidation: (i) nanoparticle-only control (MgONPs + DCFH-DA without bacteria, processed identically) to subtract background fluorescence caused by direct dye oxidation by MgONPs; (ii) blank control (PBS only) for instrument baseline. The fluorescence value of the nanoparticle-only control was subtracted from each treatment group. Each experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates, and three technical replicates were measured per biological replicate. For additional quantification, fluorescence intensity from confocal images was analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). At least five random fields per sample were analyzed to calculate the mean fluorescence intensity per treatment. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test (P < 0.05) to compare ROS levels among different treatment groups.

Transcriptome data analysis for the response of R. solanacearum to MgONPs stress

Bacterial samples were prepared and treated with MgONPs as outlined in Sect. 2.7. Following incubation, cells were harvested, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min, and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. All transcriptomic sequencing services were provided by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed as previously described [9]. Library quantification was performed using TBS380, and paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw image data underwent base-calling and quality scoring via the Illumina GA Pipeline to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Initial data cleaning involved a custom Perl script to discard low-quality reads, reads containing over 5% ambiguous bases (N), and reads with adapter contamination.

The data generated from the Illumina platform were used for bioinformatics analysis. For bioinformatics analysis, data from the Illumina platform were processed on the I-Sanger Cloud Platform (www.i-sanger.com), supported by Shanghai Majorbio, following established procedures [9]. Briefly, raw paired-end reads were quality-trimmed and filtered using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) with default parameters. The resulting high-quality reads from each sample were then aligned to the R. solanacearum reference genome (assembly accession: GCF-021117135.1) using Bowtie2 [11]. To estimate ribosomal RNA contamination, a random subset of 10,000 raw reads per sample was aligned against the Rfam database (http://rfam.xfam.org/) using BLAST. Transcript abundance was estimated using RSEM [14], which applies an expectation–maximization algorithm within a statistical model that accounts for paired-end reads, variable read lengths, and fragment length distributions to quantify isoforms. Expression levels are reported in Transcripts Per Million (TPM). Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 [14]. Genes meeting the criteria of a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1 (corresponding to a > twofold or < 0.5-fold change) were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The FDR was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Functional interpretation of the DEGs was achieved through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. GOATOOLS was utilized to identify significantly enriched GO terms, while KOBAS 2.0 was employed for KEGG pathway enrichment [23]. Statistical significance for both GO and KEGG analyses was determined using Fisher's exact test, with an FDR-adjusted P-value (corrected P-value) < 0.05 set as the threshold for significance.

Scavenging assay (ascorbic acid method)

To confirm that the reactive oxygen species (ROS) production induced by MgONPs was specifically derived from nanoparticle treatment, a scavenging assay was performed using ascorbic acid (AA), a potent free radical scavenger. A concentration of 0.05 mM was selected based on previous studies demonstrating that this concentration effectively inhibits oxidative damage in microbial systems without exhibiting pro-oxidant effects observed at higher concentrations [46]. Additionally, 0.05 mM ascorbic acid has been validated as an effective positive control in radical scavenging assays [50]. R. solanacearum strain CQPS-1 was cultured in B medium at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 6 h to reach the logarithmic growth phase, and the bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 1.0 (approximately 108 CFU/mL). For the assay, 10 µL of the bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) was added to a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask, followed by 500 µL of MgONP stock solution (10,000 mg/L, for each particle size separately) and an appropriate volume of ascorbic acid solution (prepared in sterile deionized water) to achieve a final concentration of 0.05 mM. The mixture was then diluted with B medium to a final volume of 10 mL, resulting in a final bacterial concentration of 105 CFU/mL and a final MgONP concentration of 500 mg/L. A control group without ascorbic acid (replaced with an equal volume of sterile deionized water) was included in parallel. Additionally, a nanoparticle-only control (MgONPs with ascorbic acid and DCFH-DA, without bacteria) and a blank control (B medium only) were prepared to exclude background fluorescence. All samples were incubated at 28 °C for 4 h with shaking at 150 rpm. After incubation, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA method as described in Sect. 2.11. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (excitation/emission: 488/525 nm). The fluorescence value of the nanoparticle-only control was subtracted from each treatment group to correct for any direct dye oxidation by MgONPs. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Effects of ROS stress and MgONPs size effect on the disease resistance of tobacco

For pathogenicity assays, tobacco seedlings of the variety Nicotiana benthamiana cv. Yunyan 87 (seeds purchased from Yuxi Zhong Yan Tobacco Seed Co., Ltd., Yuxi City, Yunnan Province, China) were cultivated in seedling trays until the four-leaf stage and then used for experimental determination. The R. solanacearum suspension (10⁸ CFU/mL) was prepared as described in Sect. 2.4. An aliquot of the suspension was collected and mixed with MgONP solution (500 mg/L final concentration) with or without 0.05 mM ascorbic acid, and incubated at 28 °C for 4 h to obtain the processed bacterial cell suspension. The bacterial suspension processed without MgONPs served as a control. For inoculation, 10 mL of the processed bacterial suspension was injected into the base of tobacco stems. Each treatment consisted of 15 tobacco plants. Disease incidence was recorded daily, and disease severity was graded according to the national standard GB/T 23222–2008. Briefly, the grading was performed on a per-plant basis as follows: 0 grade, no symptoms; 1 grade, occasional chlorotic spots on the stem, or wilting of less than half of the leaves on the affected side; 3 grade, black streaks on the stem, or wilting of more than half of the leaves on the affected side; 5 grade, more than two-thirds of the leaves wilted; 7 grade, all leaves wilted; 9 grade, whole plant dead. The experiment was repeated at least three times.

Effects of different particle sizes of MgONPs on tobacco physiology and biochemistry

Effects on tobacco seed germination rates

Tobacco seeds (N. benthamiana and N. tabacum cv. Yunyan 87) were surface-sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 30–60 s, followed by five rinses with sterile deionized water. Seeds with an average germination rate greater than 95% were prescreened. Filter paper was placed in Petri dishes (diameter: 90 mm) and fitted to the bottom. Then, 8 mL of MgONP suspension (500 mg/L) was added to each Petri dish, and 40 seeds were evenly placed in each dish. A control group was prepared using sterile deionized water instead of MgONP suspension. The dishes were sealed with polyethylene film to prevent water evaporation and incubated in a controlled environment chamber at 28 °C with a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark (light intensity: 200 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1) and relative humidity of 60–70%. Seed germination was observed daily for 7 days, and the germination rate was recorded. Each treatment was replicated at least three times.

Effects on tobacco seedlings

MgONP suspension (500 mg/L) was prepared using sterile deionized water, and a clean water control (sterile deionized water) was included. Tobacco seedlings (N. benthamiana and N. tabacum cv. Yunyan 87) at the three-leaf stage were selected. For each treatment, 10 seedlings were randomly assigned, and each seedling was irrigated with 20 mL of MgONP suspension. All seedlings were grown in Pindstrup substrate (peat-based growing medium) under controlled conditions: 28 °C, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod (light intensity: 200 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1), and 60–70% relative humidity. Sterile deionized water was applied regularly, and Hoagland nutrient solution was supplied once per week. To minimize error, all treatments were randomly distributed, and each treatment was replicated at least four times. Plant height and root length were measured 30 days after treatment.

Thirty days after treatment, the upper leaves of tobacco were collected from different plants (200 mg fresh weight per sample) and rinsed with tap water. The fresh leaf sample (FW) was floated in distilled water for 4 h, and the turgid weight (TW) was measured. The leaf was then dried in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h to obtain the dry weight (DW). Results and discussion

Characterization of MgONPs

The synthesis process of MgONPs is illustrated in Fig. 1E. TEM and SEM revealed that all three materials possess similar porous nanosheet morphologies (Fig. 1A–C, F; Supplemental Fig. 1). EDS mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of Mg and O elements (Fig. 1D). XRD analysis (Fig. 2A) showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 42.9°, 62.3°, and 78.6°, corresponding to the (200), (220), and (222) planes of cubic MgO (JCPDS card No. 45-0946), confirming the successful synthesis of MgONPs. The average primary particle sizes, calculated using the Scherrer equation, were 20.4 nm, 30.7 nm, and 38.9 nm for the three samples, respectively. These values, which we denote as MgONP-20, MgONP-30, and MgONP-40, represent the crystallite sizes and are within a relatively narrow range (approximately 20–40 nm). XPS spectra (Fig. 2B) showed similar peaks (C1s at 285 eV, Mg2p at 50.9 eV, and Mg2s at 88.9 eV) across all samples, indicating comparable surface chemical compositions. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2C) confirmed the presence of Mg-O bonds (400–800 cm⁻1) and surface hydroxyl groups (O–H, ~ 3600 cm⁻1). BET analysis (Fig. 2D) showed that the specific surface area decreased with increasing particle size, measuring 18.89, 15.46, and 8.29 m2/g for MgONP-20, MgONP-30, and MgONP-40, respectively. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed hydrodynamic diameters in the ranges of 20–25 nm, 80–85 nm, and 115–120 nm (Supplemental Fig. 2). The larger hydrodynamic sizes compared to the primary crystallite sizes suggest the formation of nanoparticle aggregates or clusters in suspension, a common phenomenon for metal oxide nanoparticles [28]. Antibacterial activity of the MgONPs against R. solanacearum in vitro

To evaluate the antibacterial effect of MgONPs with different primary particle sizes against R. solanacearum, we performed plate colony counting and growth curve analyses. As shown in Fig. 3A, the antibacterial activity of MgONPs against R. solanacearum exhibited a clear concentration-dependent and size-dependent pattern. At each tested concentration (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mg/L), the bacterial survival rate was lowest for the 20 nm MgONPs, followed by the 30 nm particles, and highest for the 40 nm particles, indicating a size-dependent antibacterial activity (P < 0.05). The bactericidal efficacy increased with increasing concentration for all particle sizes, which is consistent with previous reports [30]. The optimal bactericidal concentration was 500 mg/L for all three particle sizes, at which all MgONP preparations completely inhibited bacterial growth (Fig. 3A; Supplemental Fig. 3A). The growth curves of R. solanacearum in B medium further supported these observations (Fig. 3B–D). As the MgONP concentration increased, bacterial growth was increasingly suppressed, particularly within the first 12 h. At 500 mg/L, MgONP-20 caused the most pronounced inhibition (Fig. 3B). Previous studies have suggested that smaller nanoparticles may diffuse more rapidly and interact more efficiently with bacterial cells, while larger particles or aggregates may primarily cover the cell surface without penetrating [25]. Mechanism of the antibacterial activity of MgONPs

Swimming and twitching motility assay

Inhibition of bacterial motility is a more sensitive early indicator of sublethal toxicity than cell death, reflecting the impairment of bacterial physiological functions by nanomaterials under sublethal conditions [6]. Cytomembrane integrity measurement and observation

Biofilm formation is a critical factor enabling R. solanacearum to successfully invade the host xylem and cause wilting symptoms [36]. Evaluating the effect of materials on biofilm formation is therefore an important basis for determining whether they can effectively block pathogen colonization and spread [8]. In this study, MgONP treatment significantly reduced the total amount of biofilm after 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 4D). Among the three sizes, MgONP-20 showed the strongest inhibitory effect on biofilm formation.

The LIVE/DEAD assay was further employed to assess the impact of MgONPs on cell membrane integrity [56]. As shown in Fig. 4C, the control group exhibited predominantly green fluorescence, indicating intact cell membranes. In contrast, after incubation with MgONPs (500 mg/L), prominent red fluorescence emerged, indicating membrane compromise. The red fluorescence intensity increased as the particle size decreased. Quantitative analysis of red fluorescence area using ImageJ software (from at least five random fields per sample, three independent replicates) revealed that the mean red fluorescence area was 35.3 ± 5.9 for the control, 81.8 ± 13.5 for MgONP-20, 107.1 ± 12.8 for MgONP-30, and 45.8 ± 7.7 for MgONP-40 (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Fig. 3D). Statistical analysis revealed that the red fluorescence area in the 20 nm group was significantly higher than that in all other groups, and the 30 nm group was significantly higher than the 40 nm and control groups (P < 0.05, Supplemental Fig. 3D). These results indicate that MgONPs can disrupt bacterial cell membranes, with smaller particles exhibiting a greater capacity to cause membrane damage. This verified the destructive effect of MgONPs on cell membranes and indicated that MgONPs with smaller particle sizes possess higher antibacterial activity, which is consistent with the result that MgONPs can cause mechanical damage to the cell membrane, ultimately leading to the death of pathogenic bacteria [27, 47]. Other studies have shown that particle size is related to the ease by which small individual particles penetrate into bacterial membranes [43]. Moreover, smaller nanoparticles possess greater mobility and potentially enhanced absorption across biofilms [13]. There may be some mechanism, such as physical laceration of the bacterial cell membrane by MgONPs, more small particles entering the cell, and bacterial cells eventually die. Based on the above experimental results, we speculated that the smaller the particle size, the higher the bactericidal efficacy.

SEM observation further revealed morphological changes (Fig. 4E). Untreated R. solanacearum cells had smooth and intact surfaces, whereas MgONP-treated cells appeared crumpled, with large areas of damage and twisted fractures. The most severe damage was observed in the MgONP-20 treatment group.

MgONPs can disrupt or disintegrate the cell wall to penetrate bacterial cells and even cause cell death [27]. Notably, smaller particles possess a higher specific surface area, which facilitates more extensive contact with bacterial cell surfaces. Consistent with this, the degree of surface rupture increased with decreasing particle size, suggesting that enhanced contact promotes greater antibacterial efficacy. Previous studies have also emphasized that direct contact between bacteria and the MgO surface is critical for antimicrobial activity [53]. In addition, EDS analysis showed that Mg could be observed on the surface of treated bacteria (Supplemental Fig. 3C), and there was no trace of particle internalization inside the bacteria, confirming that damage was inflicted when bacteria encountered MgONPs.

Effect of MgONPs on genomic DNA content

To further investigate the effects of MgONPs on bacterial cells, genomic DNA was extracted from R. solanacearum after treatment with different sizes of MgONPs (500 mg/L) and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 4F, the genomic DNA band intensity was markedly lower in the MgONP-treated groups compared to the control, with the MgONP-20 treatment showing the weakest band. This suggests a reduction in extractable genomic DNA content, which may be attributed to DNA degradation. These results are consistent with previous reports that metal oxide nanoparticles can affect bacterial DNA integrity [8, 52].

ROS Strength antibacterial activity of the MgONPs toward R. solanacearum

When pathogens are exposed to MgONPs, oxidation reactions may occur (Fig. 5A). The ROS generated include at least superoxide radicals (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{O}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radicals (OH−) [29]. Therefore, we used a DCFH-DA kit to detect ROS production by bacterial cells under the action of MgONPs [40, 63]. As depicted in Fig. 5D, the treated bacteria produced higher amount of ROS comparing with CK, which confirmed that R. solanacearum generated a large amount of ROS after being exposed to MgONPs [8]. To quantify ROS levels, we performed fluorescence intensity analysis using ImageJ software on confocal images (at least five random fields per treatment, three independent replicates). The mean fluorescence intensities were: control, 14.50 ± 6.97; MgONP-20 nm, 145.50 ± 23.57; MgONP-30 nm, 133.77 ± 24.03; and MgONP-40 nm, 65.25 ± 15.22. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test revealed that MgONP-20 and MgONP-30 induced the highest ROS levels, while MgONP-40 nm induced significantly lower ROS levels than both MgONP-20 nm and MgONP-30 nm (P < 0.05). All three MgONP treatments induced significantly higher ROS levels than the control (P < 0.05). (Supplemental Fig. 3E). The oxygen vacancy catalysis of MgO crystals can dissolve oxygen in water on the surface through the single-electron reduction activity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{O}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}, which is strongly oxidizing and can penetrate into cells, thus ultimately killing bacteria [29]. We further used a fluorescence spectrophotometer to determine the ROS levels in bacteria exposed to MgONPs. The results revealed a significant increase in ROS content in R. solanacearum exposed to MgONPs, with MgONP-30 nm generating the highest amount of ROS, followed by MgONP-20 nm, and MgONP-40 nm producing the least (Fig. 5B). However, this ROS burst pattern appears paradoxical when juxtaposed with the antibacterial efficacy of different sizes (Figs. 3A, 4), where MgONP-20 nm exhibited optimal bactericidal performance despite generating intermediate ROS levels. These observations suggest a decoupling phenomenon between ROS generation and bactericidal efficacy [4, 35]. A possible explanation is that ROS production depends not only on particle size but also on surface defects, which can vary with synthesis temperature [4]. In our system, MgONP-20 nm and MgONP-30 nm, synthesized at 550 °C and 650 °C respectively, may possess different densities of oxygen vacancies that favor ROS generation, while the larger hydrodynamic size and lower specific surface area of MgONP-40 nm may reduce both ROS generation and physical interaction with bacterial cells. To validate this decoupling hypothesis, we conducted ROS scavenging assays focusing on the antioxidant defense system. Cellular redox homeostasis is dynamically regulated by both non-enzymatic antioxidants (e.g., ascorbate, glutathione) and enzymatic systems (SOD, CAT), which collectively modulate intracellular ROS levels to maintain pro-oxidant/antioxidant balance [49]. Specifically, ascorbic acid (AA)—the most abundant water-soluble antioxidant in plant systems—plays dual roles in photosynthetic electron transport and stress-responsive ROS scavenging [42]. AA is widely involved in scavenging ROS in response to various environmental stresses in plants through direct or indirect pathways [22, 34]. As evidenced in Fig. 5C, exogenous AA supplementation significantly attenuated MgONP-induced ROS burst. Meanwhile, the addition of AA did not significantly alter the in-vivo antibacterial activity of MgONPs (Supplemental Fig. 4). This finding suggests that, under our experimental conditions, ROS generation is not the primary driver of the antibacterial effect. However, we acknowledge that AA may not scavenge all types of ROS equally, and that residual ROS or localized oxidative stress at the nanoparticle-bacteria interface could still contribute to the overall toxicity [49].

In vivo efficacy of MgONPs against tobacco bacterial wilt

We designed an experiment to evaluate the effects of ROS stress and size effects on disease resistance in tobacco. The results demonstrated that MgONPs exhibited significant efficacy in controlling tobacco bacterial wilt, and MgONPs-20 nm are better than MgONPs-30 nm and MgONPs-40 nm (Fig. 6A). These findings align with the direct antibacterial activity of MgONPs against R. solanacearum. There are many studies that show ROS play an important role in the plant response to pathogenic stresses, with ROS acting as signaling molecules and activating different pathways to inhibit pathogens [55, 60]. However, our findings revealed that the addition of ascorbic acid to scavenge ROS did not significantly alter the antibacterial efficacy of MgONPs against tobacco bacterial wilt compared to the control group without ascorbic acid (Fig. 6B). The corresponding disease indices showed no significant reduction after ROS elimination, suggesting that the observed effects primarily stem from the size-dependent properties of MgONPs. Comparative analysis indicates that particle size effects play a more dominant role than ROS stress in the prevention and control of tobacco bacterial wilt. These nanoparticles act by disrupting cell membrane integrity and inducing oxidative stress, ultimately leading to bacterial death [23], and their antibacterial activity is influenced by the particle size, pH, concentration, and form of active MgO species [43]. Logarithmic-phase R. solanacearum was treated with DI water (control) or 500 mg/L MgONPs of each size, with three biological replicates per group. These results indicate that MgONP-20, the smallest particles, elicited more pronounced transcriptional changes related to catabolism and redox metabolism compared to the larger particles, which aligns with its higher antibacterial activity observed in phenotypic assays.

To explore whether the transcriptomic data reflect ROS-mediated stress, we examined the expression patterns of redox-related DEGs among the three treatments (Supplemental Fig. 8). Similar expression patterns were observed across different particle sizes, and no consistent enrichment of oxidative stress-related pathways was detected in the common downregulated genes. However, given the early time point and the potential for secondary effects, these data do not definitively exclude a role for ROS. Instead, they suggest that metabolic inhibition, as reflected by downregulation of catabolic processes and oxidative phosphorylation, likely contributes to the antibacterial effects of MgONPs.

Effects of MgONPs on plant physiology and biochemistry

The engineered nanomaterials have certain effects on plants, which damage to plant physiological functions, resulting in the efficiency of seed germination growth, increasing the ROS production, and the damage of cell walls [54]. To evaluate whether the MgONP concentration effective against R. solanacearum (500 mg/L, as determined in Sect. 3.2) exerts any phytotoxic effects on tobacco, we assessed seed germination, seedling growth, and leaf relative water content after exposure to MgONPs of different particle sizes. As shown in Fig. 9A, seed germination rates were highest in the control group, followed by MgONP-40 and MgONP-30, with MgONP-20 showing the lowest germination rate (P < 0.05). After 30 days of growth (Fig. 9B-D), similar trends were observed for plant height, root length, and leaf relative water content. The control group consistently exhibited the highest values, followed by MgONP-40, MgONP-30, and MgONP-20, with statistically significant differences among treatment groups (P < 0.05). These results indicate that exposure to 500 mg/L MgONPs caused modest reductions in certain growth parameters, with the smallest particles (20 nm) showing the greatest impact. However, the plants remained healthy with no visible signs of toxicity. Notably, previous studies have shown that MgONPs at lower concentrations (0–250 μg/mL) can promote tobacco growth [10], suggesting a concentration-dependent biphasic effect. Therefore, while the optimal antibacterial concentration (500 mg/L) effectively controls R. solanacearum, it may not be optimal for plant growth. For practical applications, careful concentration optimization is needed to balance antibacterial efficacy with plant safety. Conclusions

Our study investigated the antibacterial mechanisms of MgONPs with different particle sizes against R. solanacearum. Our results show that MgONP-20 exhibited the highest antibacterial activity, despite generating lower ROS levels than MgONP-30. In vivo tobacco bacterial wilt assays validated this size‑dependent activity. ROS scavenging experiments revealed that eliminating ROS with ascorbic acid did not significantly alter the antibacterial efficacy of MgONPs in vitro or in vivo. Transcriptomic analysis further indicated that MgONPs downregulated pathways related to ribosome biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and amino acid degradation, with MgONP-20 showing more pronounced effects on catabolic and redox-related processes. These findings suggest that the antibacterial activity of MgONPs against R. solanacearum may be primarily governed by particle size-related physical properties rather than ROS generation. Although ROS may contribute to the antibacterial effects, it is likely not the primary determinant. The superior efficacy of smaller particles can be attributed to their stronger membrane disruption and metabolic interference. These findings provide new insights for the design of nanomaterials aimed at precise plant pathogen control.
