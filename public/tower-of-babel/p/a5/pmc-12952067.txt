Plasma extracellular vesicle levels in acute carbon monoxide poisoning: the Eastern Piedmont experience
Authors: Angelo Puggioni, Sara Nembrini, Teresa Esposito, Andrea Giovanniello, Giuseppe Cappellano, Davide Raineri, Alessia Burgener, Lorenza Scotti, Elena Grossini, Roberta Rolla, Gianmaria Cammarota, Marcello Manfredi, Annalisa Chiocchetti, Rosanna Vaschetto, HBO Eastern Piedmont Network Collaborators
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning is a major cause of preventable morbidity worldwide and a frequent reason for emergency department (ED) visits and death [1]. While hypoxic injury is central to its acute presentation, CO exposure also activates oxidative and inflammatory pathways that contribute to both acute toxicity and delayed neurological sequelae (DNS). Despite prior efforts to identify circulating biomarkers of these processes [2], no reliable indicator for early risk stratification is currently available, as carboxyhemoglobin levels correlate poorly with clinical severity and neurological outcome.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been recently proposed as mediators and measurable markers of inflammatory injury, raising the possibility that EV profiles may reflect the biological response to CO exposure. To test this hypothesis in a real-world clinical setting, we conducted a multicenter observational analysis across seven hospitals in Piedmont, Italy, all referring patients to a single hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) facility. Adults with confirmed acute CO exposure were enrolled on ED, with blood samples collected before (T0) and after HBOT (T1). Samples were processed immediately or stored at − 80 °C, with gentle handling and no chemical fixation. A parallel comparison group of healthy controls (HCs), consisting of physicians and nurses entering the hyperbaric chamber with patients, was included. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or legal representatives.

EV identification and phenotyping followed our previously validated flow-cytometry protocol using a custom kit [3]. EVs were labeled with an APC-conjugated lipophilic cationic dye to identify vesicular membranes, and with FITC-conjugated phalloidin to exclude non-vesicular particles (Becton and Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA – catalogue number 626267); intact EVs were gated as LCD-positive and phalloidin-negative events. Monoclonal antibodies were used to identify leukocyte-derived (CD45⁺), endothelial-derived (CD31⁺), and platelet-derived (CD31⁺CD41a⁺) EVs (Becton and Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA – catalogue number 626266). Neutrophil-derived EVs were further identified in plasma using CD45/CD66b staining with CD41-based exclusion of platelet-derived vesicles. Samples were acquired on a high-sensitivity flow cytometer, and nanoparticle tracking analysis assessed vesicle concentration and size.

Clinical follow-up at 45 days assessed DNS. Data are reported as median (IQR) or frequencies. Group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test, paired analyses employed the sign test, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 72 individuals with CO poisoning and 36 HCs were included (Table 1). Patients had a median age of 55 years; 43% were female, and 30% were active smokers. At least one pre-existing comorbidity was present in 40% of patients, most commonly arterial hypertension (25%), chronic respiratory disease (19%), ischemic heart disease (10%), and diabetes mellitus (9%). Severe intoxication criteria were met by 53% of the cohort, with a median admission carboxyhemoglobin level of 21% and impaired consciousness documented in 23% of cases. HBOT was delivered at 2.5 or 2.8 atmospheres absolute (ATA), with a mean exposure of 70 min to 100% oxygen per session. Treatment-related complications were uncommon (9%), with otalgia being the most frequently reported event.

At baseline, total circulating EV levels were significantly higher in CO-exposed individuals than in controls (median 3.2 × 109/mL [IQR 2.4 – 4.5 × 109] vs. 2.6 × 109/mL [IQR 2.3 – 3.4 × 109], p = 0.032), while particle size distributions were comparable between groups. Among whole-blood EV subtypes, leukocyte-, endothelial-, and platelet-derived vesicles did not differ significantly. In contrast, plasma neutrophil-derived EVs (CD66b⁺) were markedly elevated in CO-poisoned patients (11/µL [IQR 5.8 – 27.3] vs. 3.5/µL [IQR 0.6 – 11], p < 0.0001). EV levels measured immediately after HBOT showed no consistent modulation, apart from a modest reduction in EV size, likely related to the early timing of post-treatment sampling.

At 45-day follow-up, 45 of the 72 enrolled patients were successfully contacted. Most reported good overall health (89%), while four individuals (9%) described mild symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, or asthenia. Cognitive screening with the Pfeiffer test revealed no appreciable decline among patients evaluated at both baseline and follow-up. Only one patient, a 55-year-old woman not treated with HBOT due to a contraindication, developed DNS 28 days after exposure, with MRI evidence of bilateral supratentorial white matter lesions and subsequent progression. The low number of neurological events precluded meaningful analysis of associations between EV profiles and neurological outcomes.

These findings are consistent with experimental and clinical evidence linking CO exposure to inflammatory and vascular activation. Arya et al. reported marked alterations in circulating microparticles following acute CO poisoning, particularly of neutrophil origin [4], while experimental models demonstrate that CO exposure promotes EV release from multiple cellular sources, including leukocytes, platelets, and endothelial and glial cells, leading to disruption of vascular and central nervous system homeostasis [5]. Together with evidence that CO induces neutrophil-driven inflammatory responses [2], this literature supports the pronounced increase in CD66b⁺ EVs observed in our cohort. The absence of significant differences in endothelial-derived EVs contrasts with earlier reports [4] and likely arises from methodological differences, as our approach selectively identifies intact EVs through phalloidin exclusion [3].

In conclusion, this multicenter clinical experience indicates that acute CO poisoning is associated with increased circulating EVs, predominantly of neutrophil origin. These findings support EV signatures as potential markers of inflammatory injury in CO toxicity and underscore the need for larger studies with extended follow-up to define their prognostic relevance.
