Efficacy and Safety of Dexmedetomidine‐Esketamine Versus Dexmedetomidine Alone as Premedication for Pediatric Anesthesia Induction: A Systematic Review, Meta‐Analysis, and Trial Sequential Analysis
Authors: Munder Lateiresh, Alhasan Altayf, Ayham Qatza, Abubaker Alghazal, Hasan Al‐sakkaf, Muhammed Elhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEmergence delirium (ED) is a common complication in pediatric anesthesia. Although intranasal dexmedetomidine (DEX) is widely used, its application is constrained by a slow onset, residual risk of ED in some patients, and risks such as bradycardia and hypotension. Esketamine (ESK), an NMDA receptor antagonist, may provide a faster onset and reduce these side effects.ObjectiveThis study compared the efficacy and safety of intranasal DEX‐ESK combination versus DEX alone as premedication for anesthesia induction in pediatric patients undergoing surgery.MethodsElectronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and Embase) were systematically searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The primary outcomes included the ED incidence and the onset of sedation. Secondary outcomes included mask acceptance score, FLACC pain score, post‐anesthesia care unit (PACU) length of stay, and adverse events. A random‐effects model generated pooled effect estimates—risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes. Prediction intervals were also reported to reflect the expected range of effects in future similar studies. Trial Sequential Analysis was performed. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.ResultsFive RCTs encompassing 466 pediatric patients were included in the quantitative synthesis. The DEX‐ESK combination was associated with a reduction in ED incidence (RR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.97; p = 0.04) and a shorter time to sedation onset (MD = −3.95 min; 95% CI: −4.77 to −3.14; p < 0.01). Secondary analyses demonstrated improved mask acceptance (MD = −0.77; 95% CI: −1.27 to −0.27; p < 0.01), reduced FLACC pain scores (MD = −0.36; 95% CI: −0.70 to −0.02; p = 0.04), and shorter PACU length of stay (MD = −1.83 min; 95% CI: −2.75 to −0.91; p < 0.01). Adverse event incidence did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionThe intranasal DEX‐ESK combination was associated with improved outcomes compared with DEX monotherapy for pediatric premedication including reductions in ED incidence, a modest acceleration in sedation onset, improved mask acceptance, and slightly shorter PACU length of stay, without an increased risk of adverse events. This combination may represent a feasible and safe premedication option for pediatric patients.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO: CRD420251236740

Introduction

Approximately 5 million children annually undergo surgical procedures, and 50%–70% experience significant preoperative anxiety. This anxiety correlates with increased distress during recovery, heightened separation anxiety, and elevated emergence delirium (ED) risk [1, 2]. ED is a postoperative complication occurring during recovery from general anesthesia, with an incidence in children reported to range from 10% to 80% [3, 4]. ED encompasses hallucinations, delusions, and disorientation [5]. Brief episodes of pediatric ED can precipitate self‐injury, prolonged hospitalization, increased nursing burden, and heightened healthcare costs [6].

Dexmedetomidine (DEX) is widely used in pediatric anesthesia to provide calm, cooperative sedation and analgesia without respiratory depression [7, 8]. By acting on α2‐adrenergic receptors in the locus coeruleus [9], DEX produces calm sedation with smooth onset and excellent tolerability [7, 10]. However, DEX exhibits a slow onset of sedation [11], prolongs post‐anesthesia care unit (PACU) recovery time, increases the risk of bradycardia and hypotension, elevates healthcare costs, and has a residual risk of ED in some patients [12]. Esketamine (ESK), an N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, is utilized for pediatric premedication through non‐competitive receptor inhibition [13]. ESK reduces separation anxiety and restlessness without respiratory compromise, while providing rapid sedation onset [14, 15]. In contrast to DEX, ESK stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and may mitigate DEX‐induced circulatory depression [16]. Preliminary evidence indicates that the DEX‐ESK combination offers complementary benefits, including faster onset of sedation and reduced ED incidence compared with DEX monotherapy [17, 18, 19, 20].

Currently, no comprehensive meta‐analysis has directly compared intranasal DEX–ESK combination versus intranasal DEX monotherapy for pediatric surgical sedation. This systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) addresses this evidence gap by evaluating both interventions in the pediatric population.

Methods

Protocol Registration and Reporting Format

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. Methodological quality was assessed using the AMSTAR 2 tool, which evaluates the quality of systematic reviews that incorporate both randomized and non‐randomized studies [22] (Tables S1–S3). The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251236740), minimizing selective reporting.

Literature Search

A systematic search was conducted across five major electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and Embase for studies published until August 4, 2025. The search strategy included Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free‐text keywords to identify studies of pediatric patients requiring sedation before general anesthesia. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Table S4.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) Study design: Only RCTs were included to ensure high‐level evidence. (2) Population: Participants were children or adolescents aged 18 years or younger. (3) Intervention and comparator: Trials comparing intranasal DEX combined with ESK against intranasal DEX alone. (4) Language: No language restrictions were imposed to reduce potential language bias.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) Non‐eligible study designs, such as case reports, case series, cohort studies, reviews, and animal studies. (2) Adult populations, defined as participants older than 18 years. (3) Studies with insufficient or invalid data, where relevant outcome measures could not be reliably extracted or analyzed.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

All identified records were imported into EndNote (Version 20.0.1, Clarivate Analytics) for reference management. Duplicates were automatically removed and manually verified. The cleaned records were transferred to the Covidence platform for screening. Four independent reviewers (A.A., H.A., AL.A., A.Q.) screened titles and abstracts in pairs, then assessed full texts for eligibility. Disagreements were resolved by a fifth reviewer (M.L.). Data extraction included study characteristics, participant information, and outcomes. ED and onset of sedation were considered primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included the mask acceptance score, face, legs, activity, cry, consolability (FLACC) pain score, length of PACU stay, and adverse events.

The operational definitions and measurement criteria for all outcomes are detailed in Table S5.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias in all included RCTs was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool [23], a validated framework for assessing methodological quality. This tool examines five domains: the adequacy of randomization and allocation concealment; deviations from intended interventions; completeness and handling of outcome data; accuracy and reliability of outcome measurement; and the risk of selective outcome reporting. Each domain was classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. Overall assessments were visually summarized using the Robvis tool to enhance clarity and transparency [24].

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of evidence for our outcomes was rated using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) framework [25], which assesses five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Certainty levels were categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. A Summary of Findings table was generated using the GRADEpro GDT software (McMaster University and Evidence Prime, 2025; gradepro.org), facilitating transparent presentation and interpretation of evidence strength.

Data Analysis

The characteristics of the included studies were tabulated and summarized using descriptive statistics, providing an overview of study design, participant demographics, interventions, and outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted with the “Meta” package in R (version 4.4.2; RStudio, Vienna, Austria). Pooled effect sizes were calculated using a random‐effects model with maximum likelihood estimation to address heterogeneity across studies. Continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MDs), and binary outcomes as risk ratios (RRs), both with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic and classified as mild (< 50%), moderate (50%–75%), or substantial (75%–100%). Prediction Intervals (PIs) were also generated to estimate the expected range of true effects across different clinical settings [26]. Statistical significance was set at a p‐value < 0.05. Sensitivity analyses, including leave‐one‐out methods, assessed result robustness by sequentially excluding individual trials and recalculating pooled effects and heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using contour‐enhanced funnel plots; asymmetry was interpreted as suggestive of publication bias [27]. Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) was conducted using TSA software version 0.9.5.10 Beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit, Copenhagen, Denmark) to evaluate the robustness of the meta‐analytic results and control for the risk of random errors due to sparse data or repeated significance testing. A two‐sided significance level (α) of 5% and a type II error (β) of 20% (power = 80%) were prespecified. For dichotomous outcomes, the required information size (RIS) was calculated based on a relative risk reduction (RRR) derived from studies assessed as having low risk of bias. For continuous outcomes, the RIS was estimated using empirical assumptions, with anticipated MDs and standard deviations (SDs) obtained from the included trials. O'Brien–Fleming α‐spending boundaries were applied to construct the trial sequential monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, and futility. Evidence was considered conclusive when the cumulative Z‐curve crossed both the conventional boundary and the trial sequential monitoring boundary after reaching the RIS [28].

For the mask acceptance score outcome, sensitivity analyses tested three plausible SDs: a near‐zero value (0.0001), a moderate value (0.5), and the SD from one included study (1.54) [19], addressing the issue of two studies [20, 29] reporting SD = 0 and evaluating the impact on statistical significance.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

Study selection identified 3463 records through database searches. After removing 859 duplicates using the Covidence platform, 2604 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 2577 were excluded as irrelevant. Twenty‐seven full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility; twenty‐two were excluded due to ineligible study design (n = 4), duplicate trials (n = 2), adult populations (n = 3), absence of comparator of interest (n = 7), absence of intervention of interest (n = 2), or non‐target route of administration (n = 4). Five RCTs [17, 18, 19, 20, 29] met the inclusion criteria for the meta‐analysis. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

The five included trials enrolled 633 patients, of whom 466 were included in the quantitative synthesis. All participants were aged less than 18 years and underwent various surgical procedures. Baseline characteristics of the enrolled participants are presented in Table 1.

Quality Assessment of Included Studies

Most included studies demonstrated low risk of bias across all assessed domains. Four studies [17, 18, 19, 29] showed low risk of bias across all domains. One study Liao et al. [20] presented some concerns, primarily related to potential bias in the selection of the reported results (Domain 5). A detailed risk of bias summary is presented in (Figures S1 and S2).

Primary Outcome

Emergence Delirium

Five RCTs [17, 18, 19, 20, 29] (n = 466; DEX‐ESK n = 231, DEX alone n = 235) compared ED incidence. Combined intranasal DEX–ESK was associated with a lower incidence of ED compared with DEX alone (RR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.97; p = 0.04; PI = 0.16–2.16). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I 2 = 58%, df = 4, p = 0.05) (Figure 2).

Forest plot of emergence delirium (ED).

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis identified Zhang et al. [29] as the primary source of heterogeneity. Excluding this study eliminated heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%) and strengthened the pooled effect (RR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.27–0.68; p < 0.01; PI = 0.21–0.90). This study uniquely favored DEX monotherapy (Figure S9).

Onset of Sedation

Three RCTs [17, 18, 19] (n = 248; DEX‐ESK n = 122, DEX alone n = 126) assessed sedation onset. Combined intranasal DEX–ESK produced significantly faster sedation onset compared with DEX alone (MD = −3.95 min; 95% CI: −4.77 to −3.14; p < 0.01; PI = −5.74 to −2.16). Heterogeneity was low (I 2 = 32.9%, df = 2, p = 0.23) (Figure 3).

Forest plot of the onset of sedation.

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of this result, with significant pooled MD across all iterations (p < 0.01) (Figure S10).

Secondary Outcomes

Mask Acceptance Scores

Three RCTs [19, 20, 29] (n = 281) assessed mask acceptance. Sensitivity analyses using three plausible SD values (0.0001, 0.5, and 1.54) consistently demonstrated superior mask acceptance with combined intranasal DEX‐ESK versus DEX alone (Figures S3–S5). The pooled MD were −0.78 (95% CI: −1.29 to −0.26; I 2 = 81%; PI = −2.74 to 1.19), −0.77 (95% CI: −1.29 to −0.26; I 2 = 79%; PI = −2.72 to 1.17), and −0.77 (95% CI: −1.27 to −0.27; I 2 = 66%; PI = −2.49 to 0.95) for SDs of 0.0001, 0.5, and 1.54, respectively. All comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.01).

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed stable direction and significance across all SD assumptions. Excluding Liao et al. [20] produced a larger effect size and eliminated heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%) (Figures S11–S13).

FLACC Pain Score

Three RCTs [18, 19, 20] (n = 317; DEX‐ESK n = 159, DEX alone n = 158) compared FLACC pain scores. Combined intranasal DEX–ESK reduced FLACC pain scores compared with DEX alone (MD = −0.36; 95% CI: −0.70 to −0.02; p = 0.04; PI = −1.31 to 0.59). Moderate heterogeneity was present (I 2 = 55%, df = 2, p = 0.11) (Figure S6).

Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis revealed non‐robust statistical significance. Excluding either Xing et al. [19] or Qi et al. [18] rendered results non‐significant (p > 0.05). Excluding Xing et al. [19] further reduced heterogeneity, indicating a substantial influence on the pooled estimates (Figure S14).

PACU Length of Stay

Three RCTs [18, 19, 20] (n = 317; DEX‐ESK n = 157, DEX alone n = 160) assessed PACU length of stay. Combined intranasal DEX–ESK significantly shortened PACU duration compared with DEX alone (MD = −1.83 min; 95% CI: −2.75 to −0.91; p < 0.01; PI = −3.85 to 0.20). Low heterogeneity was observed (I 2 = 0%, df = 2, p = 0.59) (Figure S7).

Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed robust findings, with significant reductions maintained across all iterations (p < 0.01) (Figure S15).

Adverse Events

Two RCTs [18, 19] (n = 189; DEX‐ESK n = 93, DEX alone n = 96) reported adverse events. No significant difference in adverse events occurred between combined intranasal DEX–ESK and DEX alone (RR = 1.61; 95% CI: 0.89–2.89; p = 0.11; PI = 0.04–72.04). Heterogeneity was negligible (I 2 = 0%, df = 1, p = 0.99) (Figure S8). Sensitivity analysis corroborated findings across all study exclusions (Figure S16).

Trial Sequential Analysis

For ED, the cumulative Z‐curve crossed the conventional significance boundary but not the trial sequential monitoring boundary, and the required information size (RIS) was not reached. This indicates inconclusive evidence, and more trials are needed (Figure 4a). For the onset of sedation, the cumulative Z‐curve crossed both the conventional and trial sequential monitoring boundaries and exceeded the required information size (RIS), indicating conclusive evidence of a significant effect (Figure 4b).

Trial sequential analysis (TSA). (a) TSA for the emergence of delirium. (b) TSA for the onset of sedation.

Publication Bias and Certainty of Evidence

Funnel plots (Figure S17) visually assessed publication bias, revealing no apparent asymmetry, though the small sample size limited interpretation. Egger's regression test was not performed due to insufficient study numbers, which compromises reliability. High certainty of evidence was assigned to ED, onset of sedation, mask acceptance scores, and FLACC pain scores. Moderate certainty was assigned to PACU duration and adverse events. Certainty of evidence assessments are summarized in the Summary of Findings table (Table S6).

Discussion

This meta‐analysis of 5 RCTs involving 466 patients synthesizes the available evidence on intranasal DEX‐ESK versus DEX monotherapy as premedication for anesthesia induction. The combination significantly improved several perioperative outcomes, including ED incidence and onset of sedation, compared with DEX monotherapy. These improvements enhance perioperative outcomes in pediatric patients undergoing surgery.

Although single‐agent ESK or DEX reduces ED incidence in children [17, 30], and both agents decrease postoperative delirium (POD) risk in adults when used as anesthesia adjuvants [31, 32], limited evidence supports their combined use for pediatric ED prevention. The current meta‐analysis found that combined intranasal DEX‐ESK was associated with a reduction in ED incidence compared to DEX monotherapy (RR = 0.58, p = 0.04), representing a 42% relative risk reduction. Moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 58%) arises from study‐specific differences in dosing regimens and procedural factors, while TSA reveals insufficient evidence accrual for conclusive findings, thereby necessitating additional RCTs across diverse populations. Wen et al. demonstrated the clinical value of combination therapy in pediatric neurosurgical patients, showing that combined DEX‐ESK significantly reduced POD within the first five postoperative days and intraoperative ED in the PACU [33]. The observed synergy in the DEX‐ESK combination likely mitigates residual excitatory states inadequately controlled by DEX monotherapy.

In our meta‐analysis, intranasal DEX‐ESK significantly reduced the onset of sedation by a mean of 3.95 min compared with DEX alone (95% CI −4.77 to −3.14), with low heterogeneity. Although this difference achieved statistical significance, its clinical relevance remains context‐dependent: modest for individual patients but potentially valuable in high‐volume pediatric centers, where cumulative time savings enhance workflow efficiency. The accelerated onset of sedation reflects the complementary pharmacokinetics of DEX and ESK. ESK achieves peak concentrations rapidly via intranasal administration, bypassing first‐pass hepatic metabolism [34, 35]. Conversely, DEX exhibits a slower onset but develops anxiolytic properties simultaneously [36, 37]. This combination creates a biphasic sedation profile: rapid ESK dissociation initiates sedation while DEX anxiolytic effects develop concurrently, achieving target sedation depths more efficiently than monotherapy.

The present meta‐analysis demonstrated that combined intranasal DEX‐ESK demonstrated superior mask acceptance compared to DEX monotherapy across three sensitivity analyses using varying SD assumptions. This consistency across multiple statistical assumptions strengthens confidence in the findings, particularly given that two included studies [20, 29] reported zero SD, which precludes standard meta‐analytic pooling. Improved mask acceptance during induction reflects enhanced preoperative anxiolysis and cooperation, which may contribute to smoother perioperative experiences. Zhang et al. reported mask acceptance rates of 86.7% with DEX‐ESK versus 62.2% with DEX monotherapy, with differential benefits evident in subgroup analysis: children with elevated preoperative anxiety (Parental Separation Anxiety Scale [PSAS] ≥ 3) achieved 80% mask acceptance with combination therapy versus 27.8% with DEX monotherapy. In comparison, children with lower baseline anxiety (PSAS < 3) demonstrated comparable acceptance between groups (92.0% combination versus 85.2% DEX) [29]. These differential benefits suggest that combination therapy addresses a distinct physiological mechanism, where ESK's rapid dissociative properties overcome residual anxiety persisting despite adequate DEX‐induced sedation. This complementary action may facilitate management of preoperatively anxious pediatric patients by enhancing mask acceptance, thereby increasing psychological confidence among caregivers and surgical teams while attenuating autonomic activation.

In our meta‐analysis, intranasal DEX‐ESK reduced mean FLACC scores by 0.36 points (0–10 scale) compared with DEX alone, reaching statistical significance. This modest difference indicates a trend toward smoother emergence rather than meaningful analgesia. These findings are hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in larger trials targeting pain‐specific outcomes. The present meta‐analysis demonstrated a significantly shorter PACU length of stay with DEX‐ESK versus DEX alone, a reduction of 1.83 min. Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of this finding. Clinical significance must be interpreted within the context of established PACU discharge criteria and high‐volume surgical settings [38]. This difference shows limited clinical importance for individual patients, because discharge criteria emphasize hemodynamic stability, adequate oxygenation and ventilation, pain control, and emotional readiness for family reunification [39]. Potential operational benefits in high‐volume centers remain theoretical and cannot be quantified from the current data.

The safety profile is a critical consideration in pediatric anesthesia. The present meta‐analysis identified no significant differences in adverse events between combination therapy and DEX monotherapy. This safety profile holds particular significance given the distinct hemodynamic properties of each agent [40]. DEX initially increases blood pressure and heart rate, but subsequently produces gradual reductions in both [41]. Conversely, ESK sympathomimetic activity increases blood pressure and heart rate, offsetting DEX's bradycardic and hypotensive effects [42, 43]. This pharmacodynamic complementarity represents a mechanistic advantage of combination therapy. Combination therapy reduced volatile anesthetic requirements, which independently increase ED risk [44]. In the Wen study, fewer patients in the DEX‐ESK group required sevoflurane (87.0% [80/92] vs. 96.7% [89/92]; p = 0.028) and exhibited lower sevoflurane area under the curve (2.0 [0.9–3.0] vs. 2.4 [1.1–3.6]; p = 0.041) compared with the control group [33]. This anesthetic‐sparing effect contributes to superior ED prevention. Both ESK and DEX demonstrate intrinsic neuroprotective properties via anti‐inflammatory pathways and modulation of perioperative stress [45, 46]. These mechanisms complement each other, providing additional protection beyond ED prevention.

This study has several strengths and provides robust evidence from 5 RCTs showing a 42% relative risk reduction in ED incidence with combination therapy, a clinically significant benefit for pediatric surgical patients. Secondary outcomes further support this benefit, revealing a reduction in sedation onset time and improved mask acceptance, effects extending from induction through emergence. By combining agents with complementary pharmacodynamic profiles, combination therapy optimizes outcomes while minimizing adverse effects. These findings contribute to evidence supporting multimodal premedication strategies over single‐agent protocols in pediatric anesthesia.

This study has several important limitations. The small number of trials (n = 5) restricted statistical power and precluded detailed subgroup analyses. Although included studies demonstrated high methodological quality, their modest sample sizes limited the precision of pooled estimates. Four of five trials received low‐risk‐of‐bias ratings; however, one trial raised concerns about selective reporting, potentially biasing the pooled estimates. The limited number of trials restricted funnel plot assessment for publication bias; adequate detection of asymmetry requires larger sample sizes. Given that all included trials were published in peer‐reviewed journals, selective non‐publication cannot be excluded. The included trials focused primarily on otolaryngologic procedures (tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy). Generalization to other surgical specialties warrants caution. All included RCTs originated exclusively in China, potentially limiting generalizability due to ethnic and pharmacogenomic differences and variations in standardized protocols.

Future studies should prioritize pragmatic, multicenter RCTs directly comparing intranasal DEX‐ESK with established premedication regimens, such as midazolam or clonidine, across diverse pediatric surgical populations to confirm potential superiority. Dose‐finding and timing studies, ideally supported by pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic modeling, are needed to refine dosing strategies while minimizing adverse events. Long‐term outcomes necessitate preliminary pilot feasibility studies before proceeding to adequately powered longitudinal evaluations of neurodevelopment spanning 6–12 months. These studies must address inherent challenges, such as participant attrition, confounding variables (including socioeconomic status and perioperative insults), and the demands of sufficiently large sample sizes. Mechanistic studies employing electroencephalography or biomarkers could elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ED, while pharmacogenomic analyses may identify predictors of treatment response to enable more precise, individualized applications.

Conclusion

Intranasal DEX–ESK premedication was associated with a lower incidence of emergence delirium, a shorter time to sedation onset, improved mask acceptance, and lower FLACC pain scores compared with DEX monotherapy. Its safety profile remains comparable, with no meaningful increase in adverse events despite the theoretical risk of pharmacologic interaction. Effective use of this approach requires well‐defined dosing pathways and standardized perioperative protocols.
