Numerical Analysis on Flow and Heat Transfer Performance of High-Pressure Hydrogen/Methane Mixtures in Horizontal Pipelines
Authors: Yunlong Zhou, Guangyao Miao, Xutong Ye, Dandan Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blending hydrogen with natural gas is necessary before fully transitioning to pure hydrogen energy. This approach also offers a practical way to reduce carbon emissions. High-pressure hydrogen/methane mixtures are the study object, and the flow and heat transfer performance inside horizontal pipelines are investigated through numerical analysis. General flow and heat transfer trends for the mixture components are identified across varying mole fractions, mass flow rates, ambient temperatures, and operating pressures. The results indicate that the addition of hydrogen reduces frictional resistance. When the molar fraction of hydrogen varies within the range of 5% to 30%, the maximum reduction in the friction factor is 1.03%. At a hydrogen molar fraction of 15%, the operating pressures of 10 and 12 MPa significantly influence convective heat transfer. Specific heat slightly decreases with increasing pressure. A new empirical correlation for thermal properties is proposed, predicting friction factor data with an error of less than ±10% and Nusselt number data with an error of less than ±20%.

Introduction

The global energy shortage and issues related to environmental contamination require urgent solutions. As an ideal clean energy source, natural gas has been widely applied in industrial, transportation, and aerospace fields. Natural gas is stored and transported under low temperatures and high pressure and then evaporated through a vaporizer to supply downstream customers. Natural gas primarily comprises methane, with a content of over 90%. Therefore, current studies on natural gas primarily focus on methane as a substitute.

The industrial community regards hydrogen as the fuel of the future and considers it an environmentally friendly energy source. Its widespread availability, cleanliness, low carbon emissions, and high efficiency and flexibility advantages are crucial in driving the global energy transition. It is a significant energy selection for accomplishing deep decarbonization goals. − However, hydrogen energy accounts for less than 3% of the global final energy consumption. , This is mainly due to the long distances between hydrogen production and consumption areas. Currently, long-distance hydrogen transportation is not only a lack of infrastructure for hydrogen transmission and storage but also challenges such as high costs and limited transport volumes. − Integrating hydrogen into the natural gas pipeline can facilitate transregional, cost-effective, and wide-scale hydrogen transport. Relevant studies indicate that mixing hydrogen with natural gas for transport is feasible. ,

Projects blending natural gas with hydrogen have origins dating back to earlier times, including the European Union’s NaturalHy project, the UK’s Hydeploy and H21 Leeds City Gate projects, and Germany’s DVG project. China’s hydrogen blending projects started relatively late. Currently, the HBNG demonstration project is only being carried out in two cities, Chaoyang in Liaoning Province and Jincheng in Shanxi Province, with an additional 12 projects under construction or in planning stages. Research and projects involving hydrogen blending with natural gas typically use blend ratios of under 20%. For instance, Australia’s HyP Gladstone and HyP Murray Valley projects inject a blend of 10% hydrogen into the natural gas pipeline. The HyBlend project in the United States aims to blend hydrogen at ratios ranging from 1 to 30%, with gradually increased plans in the hydrogen mixture. The SoCalGas project in the United States aims to achieve a 20% hydrogen blend. The Chaoyang Gas Hydrogen Blending Demonstration Project in Liaoning, China, has been safely operating for over two years with a hydrogen concentration of 10%.

Various related studies have investigated the flow heat transfer of high-pressure fluids, most of which are working media focused on H2O, CO2, refrigerants, hydrocarbon fuels as well as pure methane. , Ruan et al. extensively investigated vertical pipelines’ supercritical methane flow heat transfer mechanisms. The study indicated that heat transfer deterioration primarily occurs in the upper regions of the pipeline, especially when the fluid approaches its critical point. The heat transfer coefficient initially increases with fluid temperature and then decreases, reaching its peak near the pseudocritical temperature. Zhu et al. comprehensively analyzed the flow and heat transfer trends of CO2 mixtures in smooth horizontal pipes with a diameter of 2 mm. The result indicates that the mixed heat transfer coefficient increases with the proportion of carbon dioxide and initial temperature under consistent operating conditions. Their proposed predictive model for the flow boiling heat transfer coefficient of CO2, propane, and their mixtures in small-diameter pipes aligns closely with experimental results from previous studies. Liu et al. focused on the convective heat transfer properties of supercritical n-decane inside pipelines. To delve into the mechanism of forced convection and natural convection interaction under mixed convective conditions, they built a predictive model for Nu, which helps elucidate the heat transfer properties of combined convection.

Other researchers have also studied various pipelines’ flow and heat transfer characteristics. Zhang et al. studied the heat transfer behavior of CO2, propane, and their nonazeotropic mixtures in helical coils, and observed general trends in heat transfer under the same pressure ratio for different mass fractions. They found that the peak heat transfer coefficient increased accordingly with the propane mass fraction from 22.24% to 100%. With increasing pressure, the specific heat of the mixture slightly decreased, while the heat transfer coefficient of the mixture showed minimal variation with pressure changes. Zhou et al. investigated a detailed study on the thermal-hydraulic performance of S-CO2/propane mixtures in a printed circuit heat exchanger. They found that the CO2/propane as a heat transfer medium showed lower pressure drops and higher heat transfer efficiency. The heat transfer capability of the mixture showed minimal variation with increasing pressure, while there was a slight decrease in specific heat. Compared to the mixture, the pressure drop of pure CO2 was more sensitive to changes in mass flow rate.

Due to the differing thermodynamic properties, it is crucial to thoroughly examine the previously established correlations between the friction factor and the Nusselt number for single-phase fluids to ascertain their suitability in hydrogen/natural gas mixtures. Therefore, it is particularly imperative to conduct numerical simulations to explore the mixture flow and heat transfer performance. This study addresses the limitations of previous research, the insufficient investigation of flow and heat transfer characteristics, and the lack of correlations for such working fluids by employing numerical simulation to examine the effects of mole fraction, mass flow rate, ambient temperature, and pressure on the flow and heat transfer performance of mixtures in horizontal circular tubes, and proposes a new correlation for the flow and heat transfer characteristics of hydrogen/methane mixtures in horizontal pipelines.

Model and Numerical Method

Physical Model and Boundary Conditions

This paper simulated the flow and heat transfer process of a high-pressure hydrogen/methane mixture in a horizontal pipeline. Adopting SolidWorks software builds the model, with the pipe diameter shown in Figure being 24 mm, thickness 2 mm, and length 3000 mm. Fluid flows along the positive x-axis, with the mixture flowing along the pipeline under gravity. To ensure the simulated accuracy of the flow and heat transfer performance of hydrogen/methane mixtures under varying operating conditions in the pipeline, the solid domain of the pipe adopts steel material with a λ = 16 W/m·K. Constant wall temperature is set for the wall surfaces, and the entire outer surface of the domain is subjected to no-slip boundary conditions. The inlet is defined as a mass flow inlet, while the outlet is a pressure outlet. The inlet temperature is fixed at 280 K, with mass flow rates of 50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 kg/m2·s and working pressures of 6, 8, 10, and 12 MPa, respectively. The environmental temperatures considered are 248 K, 273 K, and 308 K. The study investigates the mixture flow and heat transfer characteristics in a horizontal pipe during different conditions of mole fraction (ranging from 5% to 30%), mass flow rates, operating pressures, and environmental temperatures.

Pipeline schematic.

Physical Properties of Mixtures

Under high pressure, mixing methane with hydrogen can reduce pipeline frictional resistance, increase volumetric flow rate, and consequently lower transmission costs. The data for the hydrogen/methane mixtures utilized in this paper are computed using Fluent’s NIST real gas database. NIST, the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s thermodynamic and transport properties materials database (REFPROP v 9.1) accurately evaluate the properties of fluids over a wide temperature range, thus making the simulation results more precise. −

According to ref , this paper primarily focuses on analyzing the trends of Fanning friction factor and Nusselt number variations. However, the thermal physical properties of hydrogen are extremely complex. Even in a compressed state, hydrogen can only provide approximately one-third of the energy per unit volume compared to methane. Therefore, mixing hydrogen into methane leads to significant alterations in the thermophysical properties. Figure shows the variation of T cr and P cr for hydrogen/methane mixtures as a function of hydrogen mole fraction.

Variation of T cr and P cr of the mixture with hydrogen mole fraction.

Figure depicts the variation trends of the hydrogen/methane mixture’s thermophysical properties with a temperature ranging from 190 to 500 K, with hydrogen mole fractions ranging from 5% to 30%, under an operating pressure of 8 MPa. As the hydrogen mole fraction increases, the thermal conductivity rises, while the specific heat capacity decreases between 190 and 255 K and increases above 255 K. Additionally, the density and dynamic viscosity lower, and the critical temperature decreases. The critical pressure for hydrogen is P = 1.2964 MPa, and for methane, it is 4.5992 MPa. Minor fluctuations in temperature can lead to swift variations in thermal performances close to the pseudocritical temperature. As the increase in hydrogen mole fraction, the density of the mixtures undergoes significant changes. Within the temperature range of 280–310 K, the mixture density decreased by 5.46%, compared to a decrease of 9.26% when the mole fraction is 5%. In the vicinity of the critical point, thermophysical properties exhibit significant fluctuations. Therefore, the variations in the thermophysical parameters of the mixture before 240 K are more pronounced compared to those after 240 K. Specifically, the sharp change in specific heat before 210 K in Figure (c) is due to missing data in the NIST REFPROP database. Since the temperature range investigated in this study is above 330 K, this data gap does not affect the findings presented here.

Control Equations and Numerical Methods

In this study, the numerical simulation assumes constant turbulence and heat transfer. The three-dimensional model is numerically analyzed using ANSYS FLUENT 22.0. Assuming that the fluid distributes on all sides uniformly, and ignores axial heat transfer and fouling resistance on both sides. Since the fluid flow is in the turbulent regime, this study employs the SST k–ω turbulence model to predict the turbulent flow. This model provides accurate predictions in the near wall region and offers reliable results for the far-field in free-streamflow, making it suitable for accurately simulating near-wall flow structures. It is noteworthy that the effectiveness of the SST k–ω model (as described by the following eqs and ) has been validated in supercritical fluid flow and heat transfer by previous studies such as those by Yang et al. and Cui et al.

Palko and Anglart conducted a thorough investigation into the sensitivity of the SST k–ω model’s predictions for fluids to variations in y+, discovering that the simulation results exhibited lower sensitivity to changes in the wall proximity parameter y+ when y+ values are less than 1. Mesh independence tests using grids of different sizes. All these grids had y+ values less than 1. The SIMPLE algorithm is used to solve the pressure and velocity coupling problem. The convection and diffusion terms in the momentum and energy equations are discretized utilizing a second-order upwind scheme. Convergence criteria are considered met when the variation in outlet temperature is less than 1% and all residuals fall below 10–6. Grid Independence Verification and Turbulence Model Analysis

Figure illustrates the structured grid generated based on the dimensionless wall distance (y+). This study employed ICEM software to construct the computational domain and generate the grid. To more accurately capture the sharp temperature gradients near walls, the local boundary layer has been refined. All grid cells are close to hexahedrons, with their accuracy exceeding 0.85.

Mesh division on the flow cross-section.

Grid independence testing dominants in simulations, considering the influence of grids on calculations. Figure compares six sets of grids to investigate the impact of grid quantity on the integrity of numerical results. By comparing the changes in pressure drop and the Nusselt number inside the channel, we analyzed the differences among the six different grid sizes, considering both accuracy and computational efficiency. The results indicate that the variables stabilize when the grid count reaches 1.3 million. Therefore, 1.3 million grids are selected for the numerical simulation. A boundary layer is established on this grid, with a thickness of 0.001 mm for the first boundary layer and a growth factor of 1.

Grid independence verification.

Calculations are performed using two turbulence models to validate the mathematical model’s precision further. Experimental and simulation studies concerning hydrogen/methane as the pipe working fluid are scarce compared to extensive studies in horizontal pipes with supercritical CO2. Therefore, this study adopts the same circular pipe model as Dang, employing a pipe structure with a length of 500 mm. Supercritical CO2 flows inside the pipe while the cooling water is circulated through the annular space. The wall heat flux is q = 12 kW/m2. At the inlet, the mass flow rate is G = 200 kg/m2, the temperature is T in = 330 K and the pressure is P in = 8 MPa. The physical properties of the flowing medium, S-CO2, are obtained from NIST. The numerical boundary conditions are based on experimental conditions, the heat transfer coefficient h is regarded as the evaluation criterion as shown in Figure . The maximum deviation in the validated heat transfer coefficient h using the SST k–ω model compared to literature values remains below 7.1%. Therefore, the numerical model and approaches utilized in this study are confirmed to be trustworthy.

Results and Discussion

Influence of Molar Fraction

Considering the complexity of practical engineering applications, this section focuses on discussing the influence of molar fraction on the flow and heat transfer performance of hydrogen/methane mixtures. The parameter settings are as follows: inlet mass flow rate G = 100 kg/m2·s, ambient temperature T at = 308 K, and operating pressure P = 8 MPa. Figures – displays the significant results under different conditions. Figure illustrates the effect of changes in hydrogen molar fraction on the mixture’s flow and heat transfer behavior. As the hydrogen molar fraction increases, the pressure drop of the mixture increases more rapidly and uniformly. This indicates that the presence of hydrogen enhances the critical pressure.

(a) Pressure drop and (b) temperature along the flow path variation with varying molar fractions of hydrogen.

When the mixture ratio improves from 5% to 30%, the pressure drop increases by 34.1 Pa and the temperature rises by 1.86 K. The pressure drop has a linear relationship with the fluid temperature. With the mass flow rate remaining constant, along the flow direction as the temperature inside the pipe increases, the pressure drop also increases significantly. This can be attributed to the fact that hydrogen has a lower density. As the hydrogen molar fraction increases, the density of the fluid gradually decreases during the flow process. Since the pressure drop is primarily influenced by fluid density, the change in pressure drop is significant. When the mass flow ambient temperature and pressure and pipeline structure remain unchanged, the fluid flow rate increases, so the cost is pressure loss. Figure illustrates the variation in fluid velocity along the flow direction.

Velocity along the flow path variation with varying molar fractions of hydrogen.

As the mixing ratio increases, the velocity of the mixture exhibits an upward trend. This is attributed to hydrogen’s lower density and viscosity, which result in negligible sedimentation. Lower viscosity accelerates the fluid velocity from the near-wall region to the main flow region, causing as the molar fraction increases, accelerating the flow speed along the flow direction At α = 30%, the average density of the mixture is 38.91 kg/m3, representing a decrease of 31.78% compared to when α = 5%. Compared to the reference at α = 5%, the average velocity increases by 30.26% at α = 30%. Figures and respectively illustrate the temperature and density variations at different positions within the pipe for different mixture ratios. The asymmetric distribution of temperature, density, and turbulent kinetic energy in the pipe cross-section is attributed to gravitational effects. Due to significant density variations near the pseudocritical region, buoyancy effects become pronounced, leading to nonuniform distributions of velocity and temperature in the radial direction.

Influence of Mass Flow Rate

Figures and illustrate the temperature and turbulent kinetic energy distribution within the pipe under varying mass flow rates for hydrogen molar fractions of 5% and 30%, respectively. As the mole fraction of hydrogen enlarges, a corresponding escalation in the trend of turbulent kinetic energy is observed. Figure compares the pressure loss of the mixture under various mole fractions and mass flow rates. From Figure (a), it can be observed that the pressure loss increases with the rise in mass flow rate. When the mass flow rates change, when the hydrogen mole fraction increases from 5% to 30%, the pressure loss increases by 29.06%, 29.65%, 30.04%, 30.23%, and 30.33%, respectively. When the hydrogen mole fraction is 15% and the mass flow rate G = 150 kg/m2·s, a fluctuation point appears, with a pressure drop of 475.39 Pa.

Distribution of temperature inside the pipeline with varying mass flow rates.

Distribution of turbulent kinetic energy inside the pipeline with varying mass flow rates.

(a) Pressure drop and (b) temperature variation with varying mass flow rate.

For the same mole fraction, the influence of mass flow rate on pressure drop amplifies as the hydrogen content increases. Specifically, the minimum pressure loss is observed at α = 5% and a mass flow rate G = 50 kg/m2·s. Furthermore, mass flow rate increases also affect the temperature of the fluid, demonstrating a trend where higher mass flow rates correlate with lower average temperatures within the pipe. This is because hydrogen has a faster flow velocity and carries away more heat during the flow process. Figure (b) illustrates fluid temperature decreases when the mass flow rate increases, displaying a fluctuation point at α = 15%. As shown in Figure , this behavior is principally due to the changes in specific heat capacity at the critical temperature: before the critical temperature, the specific heat capacity declines sharply with increasing α; conversely, beyond the critical temperature, it increases with higher α values. Figure compares the effects of different mixture ratios and mass flow rates on the Fanning friction factor and the Nusselt number. The Fanning friction factor exhibits fluctuations and shows an overall decreasing trend with different mass flow rates. The maximum decrease occurs at G = 50 kg/m2·s with a decrease of 1.03%. Due to increased turbulent tendencies, the Nusselt number increases with a growth in the mass flow rate of the mixture, resulting in improved heat transfer performance. Compared to α = 5%, when the mass flow rate is G = 150 kg/m2·s, the mixture Nusselt number decreases by 33.11%. When the mass flow rate is G = 50 kg/m2·s, the Nusselt number decreases by 32.64%.

(a) Fanning friction factor and (b) Nusselt number variation with varying mass flow rates.

Influence of Ambient Temperature

Figure illustrates the changes in pressure drop and temperature with variations in ambient temperature. When an ambient temperature T at = 308 K, the pressure drop increases most noticeably, with the growth ratio gradually increasing with the increase in hydrogen mole fraction, reaching up to 14.2%. When α = 30%, compared to the ambient temperature of T at = 248 K, the pressure drop increases by 10.66% (T at = 273 K) and 26.42% (T at = 308 K) respectively. The maximum temperature loss occurs at a temperature of 248 K and a mixture ratio of α = 5%.

(a) Pressure drop and (b) temperature variation with varying ambient temperature.

With the inlet temperature fixed at 280 K, when the ambient temperature is 248 K, the pipe wall absorbs heat from the fluid inside the pipe, resulting in a significant temperature difference, which enhances the heat transfer performance. Additionally, as the mixing ratio increases, a negative growth is observed when the ambient temperature is at or below zero degrees. Figure depicts the impact of ambient temperature variation on the Fanning friction factor and the Nusselt number. Variation of the Fanning friction factor follows a linear trend with fluid temperature. At an ambient temperature of T at = 308 K, an increase in the mole fraction of hydrogen leads to a temperature rise of 1.86 K, accompanied by a decrease in the Fanning friction factor by 0.031%. At an ambient temperature of T at = 248 K, the temperature decreases by 1.96 K, and a decrease in Fanning friction factor of 2.67% occurs. The Nusselt number varies inversely with ambient temperature. At T at = 273 K, an increase in the mole fraction of hydrogen leads to a reduction in the Nusselt number by 33.73%. When α = 5%, compared to the ambient temperature of T at = 248 K, the Nusselt number decreases by 6.05% and 11.56% respectively. When α = 30%, the Nusselt number decreases by 4.02% and 8.50% respectively. The observed phenomenon may be attributed to the reduced temperature difference between the pipe inner wall and the fluid, which leads to a lower heat transfer rate and thus results in higher fluid temperatures inside the pipe at elevated ambient conditions. As shown in Figure , the increase in thermal conductivity with rising temperature further contributes to the decrease in Nusselt number.

(a) Fanning friction factor and (b) Nusselt number variation with varying ambient temperature.

Influence of Operating Pressure

Figure illustrates the variation of mixture properties at various pressures, with operating pressures ranging from left to right as 6 MPa, 8 MPa, 10 MPa, and 12 MPa. Figure depicts the temperature cloud chart and curve chart, indicating that temperature changes are not important when there are pressure variations However, at a mixture ratio of α = 15%, temperature changes reach a peak at pressures of P = 10 MPa and P = 12 MPa. The Fanning friction factor and Nusselt number exhibit similar trends. Figure (a) illustrates the variation of pressure drop under different operating pressures, with the constant mole fraction. As the operating pressure increases, the pressure drop decreases. However, as the mole fraction increases, the rate of increase in pressure drop diminishes with increasing operating pressure. When operating pressure P = 12 MPa, the pressure drop increased by 28.71%. For every 2 MPa increase in operating pressure, the pressure drop decreased on average by 24.26%, 19.42%, and 15.38%, respectively. This is because lower pressure results in smaller dynamic viscosity and density. As the temperature increases, the mixture viscosity decreases, leading to increased velocity, making the changes in pressure drop more pronounced.

Thermophysical properties variation of the mixture with varying operating pressures.

Distribution of temperature inside the pipeline with varying operating pressures (α = 30%, G = 100 kg/m2·s).

(a) Pressure drop and (b) temperature variation with varying operating pressure.

In Figure (b), at α = 15%, upon increasing the operating pressure from 10 to 12 MPa, the temperature rises by 0.191 and 0.132 K. Increasing the operating pressure reduces the critical temperature of the mixture due to changes in its physical properties at different operating pressures. Specifically, at 12 MPa, the specific heat capacity decreased by 10.84% compared to 6 MPa. When α increases from 5% to 30%, the fluid temperature is 0.28 K lower than P = 6 MPa.

From Figure , it can be seen that the Nusselt number distribution of the mixture displays little variation under different operating pressures. This difference is primarily evident at α = 15%, with 10 and 12 MPa operating pressures. This is particularly noticeable when the operating pressures are 6 and 8 MPa, where the trends in Nusselt number variation nearly coincide. Meanwhile, a fluctuation point occurs at α = 15%. When the operating pressure is at 10 and 12 MPa, the Nusselt number rises by 11.48% and 10.75%, respectively, compared to the same friction factor. The variation in the Fanning friction factor is more pronounced. Similarly, peaks occur at α = 15% when the operating pressure is 10 and 12 MPa. The peak points increased by 0.13% and 0.09%, compared to the preceding operating point. These two points exhibit growth rates of 3.81% and 5.11%, respectively, relative to this mixture ratio’s lowest operating pressure point (P = 6 MPa). These characteristics are attributable to the fluid’s proximity to its pseudocritical point, where the specific heat capacity varies significantly with changes in operating pressure. The specific heat capacity peaks at 9.01 kJ/kg when P = 8 MPa. Beyond the pseudocritical point, the specific heat capacity decreases rapidly, demonstrating an inverse relationship with pressure variations. The velocity changes are shown in Figure .

This paper proposes new correlations for predicting the flow and heat transfer performance of hydrogen and methane mixtures. Conclusions

The paper establishes a numerical model to analyze the flow and heat transfer in a horizontal pipeline for hydrogen/methane mixtures. Built upon these foundations, the thermal characteristics of hydrogen/methane mixtures are meticulously analyzed. The impacts of different mole fractions, mass flow rates, ambient temperatures, and operating pressures on pressure drop, temperature, the Fanning friction factor, and the Nusselt number are studied through numerical simulations. Comparisons of typical convective heat transfer correlations, three dimensionless numbers, Reynolds number, Prandtl number, and Density ratioare selected to fit the new correlation. The following conclusions are drawn.(1)Within the α range from 5% to 30%, at the same operating pressure, owing to the lower density and small viscosity of hydrogen, the pressure drop along the flow path in the horizontal pipeline increases with the hydrogen mole fraction increase. When α changes from 5% to 30%, the temperature increases by 1.87 K, and the flow follows the law that the hydrogen molar fraction increases and the Fanning friction factor decreases.(2)The increase in mass flow rate and hydrogen molar fraction, the pressure drop and temperature across the hydrogen/methane mixture show a corresponding elevation. Particularly, when α = 15%, the impact of the mass flow rate on convective heat transfer is notably more significant compared to scenarios with other mixing ratios. As the mass flow rate becomes more sizable and the hydrogen mole fraction increases, the rate of growth in the Nusselt number diminishes.(3)With the inlet temperature held constant, as the ambient temperature varies within the range of 248 to 308 K, the Fanning friction factor for the mixture (α = 15%) increases by 6.59%, whereas the Nusselt number decreases by 9.87% As the hydrogen mole fraction increases, Fanning friction factor demonstrates a decreasing trend, with the maximum reduction reaching 2.61% at a hydrogen mole fraction of (α = 30%). This observation suggests that the hydrogen/methane mixture exhibits superior flow and heat transfer performance under relatively lower ambient temperatures. When α = 15%, the operating pressures of 10 and 12 MPa result in peak values, significantly impacting the Fanning friction factor. At the same mole fraction, compared to an operating pressure of 6 MPa, the temperature increased by 0.191 and 0.132 K, and the Nusselt number increased by 11.48% and 10.75%, respectively.(4)By comparing existing correlations and analyzing their deviations, we have established a new correlation for a more precise to predict the hydrogen/methane mixture flow and heat transfer characteristics. Established the Fanning friction factor and the Nusselt number novel established correlations successfully confine the predictive error within ±4% and ±5% margins, specifically.(5)By employing numerical simulation methods combined with practical application requirements to study the flow and heat transfer characteristics of hydrogen-blended mixtures in high-pressure pipeline systems, and by obtaining the flow and heat transfer patterns of mixed working fluids in pipes, this work provides parameter support for the design and retrofitting of hydrogen pipelines, thereby improving hydrogenation process technology.
