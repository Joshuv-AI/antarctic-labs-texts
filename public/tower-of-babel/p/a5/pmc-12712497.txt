Endoscopic Layered Adhesiolysis of Achilles Tendon–Flexor Hallucis Longus Complex via Medial Portals After Gastrocnemius Fascial Turn-Down Flap and Flexor Hallucis Longus Transfer for Chronic Achilles Tear
Authors: Tun Hing Lui, Amanda Mun Yee Slocum, Charles Churk Hang Li, Jerome Chi Wang Poon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGood outcomes have been reported after gastrocnemius fascial turn-down flap with flexor hallucis longus (FHL) augmentation for chronic Achilles tendon (AT) rupture. However, postoperative peritendinous fibrous adhesions with tightness and ankle stiffness can occur because of extensive soft tissue dissection and postoperative immobilization. Adhesions can occur at the deep surface of the FHL muscle, between the AT and FHL muscle, and between the Achilles tendon and the deep fascia of the leg. The purpose of this Technical Note is to describe the details of endoscopic layered adhesiolysis of the AT-FHL complex. This minimally invasive approach allows adequate release of every plane of the complex and immediate vigorous mobilization of the foot and ankle. This technique, combined with endoscopic adhesiolysis of the muscles in the superior posterior compartment of the leg and endoscopic adhesiolysis of the AT after tendon repair, forms a comprehensive system for managing various situations after AT repair or tendon transfer. It is indicated in symptomatic fibrous adhesions of the AT-FHL complex after FHL transfer when the patient experiences pain and tightness over the operated region of the AT with corresponding tenderness and is recalcitrant to conservative treatment.

Superficial posterior compartment of the leg contains the gastrocnemius, soleus and plantaris muscles, and the Achilles tendon (AT), which is the conjoined tendon of plantaris, gastrocnemius, and soleus muscles.1 Post-traumatic and postoperative fibrous adhesions can affect the muscles, the Achilles tendon, or both. Degeneration or trauma can lead to AT rupture. Achilles tendon tear usually occurs at its hypovascular zone, which is 4 to 6 cm proximal to its calcaneal insertion.1,2 A rupture that has been untreated for 4 to 6 weeks or more is called a chronic AT rupture.1 Chronic AT rupture can lead to gait dysfunction and persistent debilitating pain, which often hampers daily routine activities.1,3 Triceps surae contraction, decreased blood supply to the ruptured location, and gaps created by tendon degradation make treating chronic Achilles tendon rupture more challenging than treating acute rupture.2 It frequently requires surgical treatment by an end-to-end repair, a peroneus brevis or flexor digitorum longus or flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon transfer, free tendon autografts (hamstring), the use of synthetic materials (Marlex mesh, polymers, carbon fiber, polyester, and a cellular dermal tissue matrix), a turn-down flap, a VY tendinous flap, or the use of dual techniques.1,2 Good outcomes have been reported after gastrocnemius fascial turn-down flap with FHL augmentation.1,3,4 However, postoperative heel cord peritendinous fibrous adhesions with tightness and ankle stiffness can occur because of extensive soft tissue dissection and postoperative immobilization.2,5

Adhesions can occur at the deep surface of the FHL muscle, between the AT and FHL muscle, and between the AT and the deep fascia of the leg.6,7 Conservative treatment is first-line management and includes physical therapy, dynamic splinting, and sliding exercise. Surgical release is needed if symptoms persist. Whenever possible, endoscopic adhesiolysis is preferable to open surgery because it allows for less extensive dissection and smaller surgical incisions, which in turn enable immediate and vigorous mobilization of the foot and ankle.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 The purpose of this Technical Note is to describe the details of endoscopic layered adhesiolysis of the AT-FHL complex via medial portals after gastrocnemius fascial turn-down flap and FHL transfer for chronic Achilles tear. This technique, combined with endoscopic adhesiolysis of the muscles in the superior posterior compartment of the leg and endoscopic adhesiolysis of the AT after tendon repair, forms a comprehensive system for managing various situations after Achilles tendon repair or tendon transfer.6,9,11, 12, 13, 14 It is indicated in symptomatic fibrous adhesions of the AT-FHL complex after FHL transfer when the patient experiences pain and tightness over the operated region of the Achilles tendon with corresponding tenderness and is recalcitrant to conservative treatment. It is contraindicated if the symptoms are due to other pathologies (e.g., fibrosis of the gastrocsoleus muscles or fibrosis of the muscles or tendons of the tibialis posterior or flexor digitorum longus muscle) (Table 1).

Surgical Technique

Preoperative Assessment and Patient Positioning

Details of the previous operation(s) should be studied carefully. The site and extent of tightness should be determined by detailed history taking (self-report of painful sites) and physical examination (tender spots).11 Action and excursion of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles should be assessed. The active and passive ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion ranges with the knee extended and flexed are recorded. The distal neurological status should be examined. Magnetic resonance imaging may give important information about the previous FHL transfer (Fig 1) and the site and extent of the fibrous adhesions (Fig 2).

Portal Placement

This procedure is performed via the proximal medial and distal medial portals, which are at the medial edge of the AT-FHL complex and at the proximal and distal ends of the previous surgical scar, respectively (Fig 3).

Endoscopic Adhesiolysis of the Deep Surface of the Flexor Hallucis Longus Muscle

The endoscopic working space is over the deep surface of the FHL muscle. The distal medial portal is the viewing portal, and the proximal medial portal is the working portal. The fibrous tissue at the deep surface of the muscle is resected from medial to lateral with an arthroscopic shaver (Dyonics; Smith & Nephew) until its fibular origin is exposed (Fig 4). The proximal medial portal is the viewing portal, and the distal medial portal is the working portal. The FHL muscle is released from Kager’s fat pad and the superior surface of the calcaneal tuberosity (Fig 5).

Endoscopic Adhesiolysis of the Interface Between the Achilles Tendon and the Flexor Hallucis Longus

The endoscopic working space is between the FHL muscle and the AT. The distal medial portal is the viewing portal, and the proximal medial portal is the working portal. The FHL muscle is released from the AT with the arthroscopic shaver and SuperCut scissors (Stille) (Fig 6). The proximal medial portal is the viewing portal, and the distal medial portal is the working portal. The fibrous adhesions between the FHL muscle and AT are released down to the AT insertion (Fig 7).

Endoscopic Adhesiolysis of the Superficial Surface of the Achilles Tendon

The endoscopic working space is between the AT and the deep fascia of the leg. The distal medial portal is the viewing portal, and the proximal medial portal is the working portal. The fibrous adhesions between the AT and the deep fascia of the leg are released with the arthroscopic shaver. Any suture knot from previous operation(s) is removed with the SuperCut scissors, hemostat, and arthroscopic shaver (Fig 8).

Discussion

Fibrous adhesion of the AT-FHL complex after FHL transfer is mainly a clinical diagnosis depending on accurate history taking and physical examination.11 The treatment aim is to improve the patient’s subjective feelings.11 The key to the success of treatment relies on mutual communication between the surgeon and the patient, letting the patient understand the operation and have a realistic expectation.11

Intraoperatively, it is important to achieve complete release of every plane of the AT-FHL complex and avoid injury to the tibial neurovascular bundle. The medial portals at the medial edges of the AT-FHL complex are located posterior to the tibial neurovascular bundle. Instrumentation via these medial portals and working around the AT-FHL complex can make sure that the neurovascular bundle is away from the operative site. The release is from medial to lateral and from proximal to distal until normal tissue is seen. This can ensure complete release of the complex.

The advantages of this technique include small incisions and better cosmetic outcome, minimal soft tissue trauma, complete release of the AT-FHL complex, and immediate vigorous mobilization. The potential risks of this technique include injury to the sural nerve, saphenous nerve, and tibial neurovascular bundle and persistent or recurrent stiffness of the AT-FHL complex (Table 3).
