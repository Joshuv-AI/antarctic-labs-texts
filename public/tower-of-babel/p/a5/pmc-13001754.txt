Practice advisory on the management of the perioperative period of scoliosis correction surgery
Authors: Tiangang Lou, Lu Chen, Guihua Xiang, Wei Yan, Xin Pi, Kun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractScoliosis correction surgery is a complex procedure aimed at rectifying the spinal lateral curvature and rotation. Perioperative anesthetic management is a critical determinant of both surgical success and patient safety. As surgical and anesthetic techniques continue to advance, scoliosis correction has become increasingly established in clinical practice. However, there remains a paucity of standardized guidelines specifically addressing anesthetic practices in this procedure. This article aims to review several frequently encountered challenges during the perioperative phase of scoliosis correction surgery, with a particular emphasis on anesthetic considerations.Keywords: Scoliosis, Anesthesia, Pain management, Patient safety

Introduction

Scoliosis is characterized by a lateral curvature of the spine with a rotational component exceeding 10°, along with vertebral rotation.1 Congenital scoliosis arises from early embryonic abnormalities in spinal development, with approximately half of the patients exhibiting anomalies in other organ systems.2 Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is more prevalent, with an incidence rate of 2–4 % among adolescents aged 10–16.3 Only 10 % of these patients with scoliosis require treatment, particularly when the curve severity, as measured by the Cobb method, exceeds 40° and there is a high likelihood of progression.4 Most idiopathic thoracic curves are predominantly right-sided; left-sided thoracic curves are more prone to be associated with other thoracic cage abnormalities.5, 6 Scoliosis deformity correction is a multidisciplinary process that involves both non-surgical and surgical correction methods.7 Conservative treatment methods mainly include observation, bracing, and physical therapy, while surgical correction is usually considered in case of severe scoliosis deformities.8

Surgical intervention can correct deformities to a certain extent, halt their progression, and play an active role in preventing the deterioration of cardiopulmonary function as well as aiding in the recovery of long-term pulmonary function.9 The prognosis for patients with severe spinal deformities who do not receive treatment is significantly worse than that for those who undergo surgery.10 Patients with complex spinal deformities are generally younger and exhibit a wide range of severity.11 The correction surgery for complex spinal deformities is intricate, characterized by an extensive surgical field, significant blood loss, and prolonged operation duration.11, 12 Perioperative management is challenging and requires close collaboration between anesthesiologists and surgeons, with a multidisciplinary preoperative assessment and meticulous perioperative management to achieve precise anesthesia.

Beyond surgical correction, perioperative anesthesia management directly influences patient outcomes in scoliosis surgery. Multimodal strategies—real-time intraoperative neuromonitoring (somatosensory and motor evoked potentials) to detect and avert neurologic injury, lung-protective ventilation to reduce postoperative pulmonary morbidity, antifibrinolytic therapy to limit blood loss and transfusion, proactive temperature regulation to counteract coagulopathy, and multimodal analgesia to enable early mobilization—collectively shorten extubation time, decrease respiratory complications, and improve functional recovery.13, 14

The review introduces several key issues in the anesthetic management of scoliosis correction surgery. These include but are not limited to patient positioning, airway management, hemodynamic stability, and postoperative pain control. The heterogeneity of the patient population and the complexity of the surgical procedure present unique challenges for anesthesiologists. However, each patient's situation is unique, necessitating an individualized anesthetic plan.15

Preoperative assessment

Preoperative general condition assessment

A thorough evaluation of cardiopulmonary function is essential,16 particularly for patients with thoracic scoliosis, as aggressive correction may lead to postoperative cardiopulmonary insufficiency.17 The larger the Cobb angle, the greater the impact on cardiopulmonary function.18, 19 Many patients with spinal scoliosis present to the hospital with symptoms of neurological compression.20 Preoperative assessment should include a detailed examination of sensory changes and muscle weakness in the lower limbs.21 Furthermore, it is essential for the anesthesiology team to comprehend the surgical procedure and its magnitude to anticipate the operation's duration and potential blood loss.22, 23 Patients undergoing osteotomy and decompression are at a higher risk of significant hemorrhage.24 Therefore, preparing an adequate blood supply in anticipation of possible blood loss is imperative.

Airway assessment

A thorough preoperative airway assessment is essential for patients undergoing scoliosis correction surgery due to potential anatomical abnormalities associated with spinal deformities. Severe scoliosis, defined by a Cobb angle ≥ 40°, can result in tracheal or bronchial deformities, particularly in kyphoscoliosis, which may complicate intubation.25, 26 Standard airway evaluation techniques, including the Mallampati score, thyromental distance, and assessment of mouth opening, should be employed to identify predictors of a difficult airway. In most cases, patients can be managed with standard oral or nasotracheal intubation under general anesthesia. Nasotracheal intubation with laryngoscopy is often recommended for patients with tracheal curvature to ensure proper airway management. In cases where a difficult airway is anticipated, alternative techniques such as fiberoptic bronchoscope intubation may be planned; however, conscious fiberoptic bronchoscope intubation is not a standard practice and is reserved for specific scenarios with significant airway challenges.27

For patients with severe scoliosis or substantial cardiopulmonary comorbidities, neuraxial anesthesia—either alone or combined with light general anesthesia—may be considered to minimize risks associated with general anesthesia. Preoperative planning should include preparation for difficult airway scenarios, such as preoxygenation，and ensuring ready availablility of supplemental oxygen.28

Preoperative communication

Effective preoperative communication is a cornerstone of successful scoliosis correction surgery, ensuring that patients and their families are well-informed and prepared.29 This process involves discussing the surgical procedure, including its expected duration, patient positioning (typically prone), and potential risks, such as neurological complications or blood loss. A key component of this communication is educating patients about the intraoperative monitoring techniques used to safeguard spinal cord integrity. Historically, the intraoperative wake-up test (also known as the Stagnara test) was employed to assess spinal cord function by briefly awakening the patient during surgery to evaluate motor function. However, this technique has largely been superseded by continuous electrophysiological monitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) and motor evoked potentials (MEPs), which provide real-time feedback on spinal cord integrity with high sensitivity and specificity. Despite this advancement, the wake-up test remains relevant in specific scenarios, such as when advanced monitoring is unavailable or when persistent signal reductions in SSEPs or MEPs necessitate confirmation of motor function. Patients should be informed about the possibility of the wake-up test, including its purpose and what to expect, to ensure informed consent and reduce intraoperative anxiety. Additionally, preoperative education can improve postoperative outcomes by setting realistic expectations for pain management and recovery, as evidenced by studies showing reduced narcotic use and lower pain scores with effective patient education.30 Collaboration among surgeons, anesthesiologists, and the child life team further enhances patient preparation, particularly in addressing anxiety and ensuring compliance with postoperative care instructions.

Anesthesia Selection

Tracheal intubation

The selection of sedative agents is crucial for ensuring patient comfort and the success of intubation. Research indicates that a low dose of dexmedetomidine (DEX), when combined with local anesthesia and airway blockers, can effectively assist awake or fiberoptic bronchoscope intubation without significantly impacting hemodynamic stability.31, 32 The advantage of DEX lies in its ability to provide sedation without significantly suppressing respiratory drive.32 This allows patients to maintain adequate arousability after intubation, facilitating the necessary assessment of neurologic function.31, 33 This method allows for the maintenance of patient comfort and airway patency while minimizing the risk of neurological injury.

Surgical spinal corrections involving high anterior thoracic levels or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) necessitate the isolation of one lung for optimal surgical access.34 Traditionally, one-lung ventilation (OLV) is achieved using a double-lumen endotracheal tube (DLT). However, in single-staged anterior followed by posterior spinal fusions and for postoperative mechanical ventilation, the DLT must be exchanged for a single-lumen endotracheal tube (SLT).35 An alternative approach involves the use of a SLT with an integrated bronchial blocker, which can provide OLV and be conveniently converted to a standard SLT by deflating the blocker post-procedure.36 In patients with restrictive lung disease, maintaining adequate oxygenation during OLV can be challenging and may necessitate the application of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) to the non-ventilated lung and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to the ventilated lung.37, 38

Anaesthetic selection

While the overall prevalence of malignant hyperthermia (MH) susceptibility is low (estimated at 1:10,000 to 1:250,000 anesthetics), it is higher in patients with certain myopathies (e.g., RYR1related disorders such as central core disease) that may cooccur with scoliosis phenotypes.39, 40 Accordingly, risk stratification should prioritize personal/family MH history, known myopathy, and prior anesthetic reactions. For patients with known or suspected MH susceptibility, the use of triggering volatile agents and succinylcholine should be avoided and TIVA should be considered as the primary technique; additionally, dantrolene availability and MH preparedness should be ensured.41, 42 When MH risk is low, technique should be selected based on surgical/monitoring needs: TIVA generally preserves MEP/SSEP signal quality better than volatile anesthesia, facilitating reliable intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) and potentially faster wake-up testing.43 In contrast, lowdose volatile regimens may be acceptable when MEP reliance is limited and MH risk is negligible.44

Monitoring

Circulating system monitoring

Proper management of respiratory and hemodynamic functions is crucial for enhancing patient outcomes throughout surgical procedures. This involves closely monitoring lung compliance and airway pressures, and considering the effects of positive pressure ventilation on venous return and surgical bleeding.13 Management of hemodynamics requires the use of invasive arterial pressure monitoring and volume management guided by indicators such as Central Venous Pressure (CVP), Stroke Volume Variation (SVV), and Pulse Pressure Variation (PPV).45

Neurological monitoring

The IONM utilizing somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) and transcranial motor evoked potentials (MEPs) has been established as an effective method for predicting an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including paraparesis, paraplegia, and quadriplegia, during spinal surgery.46, 47, 48 The IONM modalities are highly specific. Patient positioning

Ensuring proper positioning during surgery to minimize pressure on nerves and blood vessels.52 Before general anesthesia is induced, it is essential to assess a patient's upper and lower limb mobility to confirm neurological integrity.53, 54 Proper patient positioning during spinal surgery is a critical task shared by anesthesiologists and surgeons.55 Patients susceptible to spinal cord compression should be positioned under light sedation, with their limb movements monitored before the induction of general anesthesia.56

For surgeries involving complex spinal deformities that necessitate the patient to be in a sitting position, preparations must be made to manage the risk of venous air embolism.57, 58 These complex procedures often require a combined anterior and posterior surgical approach to address the spinal deformity effectively.59, 60 In cases of lower lumbar surgeries, patients are typically placed in the prone position to facilitate surgical access. Due to the potential decrease in lower limb blood flow that can occur during pelvic retractions, it is advisable to place a pulse oximetry probe at the toes to monitor peripheral oxygenation.61

Anterior thoracolumbar surgeries are generally performed with the patient in a lateral position. This choice of positioning is primarily aimed at achieving the best possible surgical exposure while ensuring the safety and comfort of the patient throughout the operation. The lateral decubitus position facilitates effective access to the thoracolumbar region and reduces the risk of compression on peripheral nerves.62 Additionally, the lateral decubitus position can help maintain the patient's hemodynamic stability, which is crucial during surgery. This positioning can reduce the risk of sudden blood pressure fluctuations and ensure adequate blood flow to vital organs, contributing to a smoother surgical experience. Special attention must be paid to the positioning of the dependent arm, leg, and neck to prevent nerve compression and ensure patient comfort and safety throughout the procedure.63

Intraoperative management

Perioperative fluid management

Preoperative fasting and the physiological responses to anesthesia can lead to significant fluid shifts and losses during surgery.64 The management of fluid therapy in major surgical cases, especially in adolescent spinal correction surgery, requires a delicate balance to avoid both volume overload and insufficient resuscitation.65, 66 Anesthesiologists may administer high volumes of crystalloid fluids to patients undergoing major surgery to prevent complications arising from hypovolemia, such as reduced cardiac output (CO) and tissue oxygen supply, which can lead to organ dysfunction.67

Meanwhile, on the other hand, in adolescent spinal deformity correction surgery, aggressive crystalloid administration can increase the cardiopulmonary burden and is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality.68 A retrospective study has identified that restrictive fluid administration during posterior correction surgery for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis can lead to improved postoperative ventilation and oxygenation, as well as a reduced incidence of pulmonary edema.69

Observational analyses in complex spine surgery suggest that higher net crystalloid administration correlates with pulmonary complications, with an inflection (“change-point”) around 1.8 L and a cohort median near 2.1 L.70, 71 “Net fluid” typically reflects total intraoperative intake minus outputs (e.g., urine) and does not fully account for blood loss or third-space shifts. Because such thresholds are model-dependent and hypothesis-generating, they should inform—but not replace—goal-directed strategies using dynamic indices (e.g., SVV/PPV), tissue perfusion markers, and individualized risk (lung disease, renal vulnerability).70, 71

Caution: Avoid rigid volume caps; prioritize goal-directed therapy, timely vasopressors for vasoplegia, and balanced blood product strategy over indiscriminate crystalloid loading.

Fluid management in adolescent spinal correction surgery is a complex clinical challenge. Anesthesiologists must carefully consider the risks and benefits of fluid administration strategies to optimize patient outcomes. Transfusion management

Patients with neuromuscular diseases are at a higher risk of significant blood loss due to prolonged and extensive surgical procedures or lower density of the bones.12 The effect of antifibrinolytics in reducing blood loss and transfusion administration has been described in scoliosis surgery, particularly in patients with neuromuscular disorders. Tranexamic acid (TXA) is one of the most widely used antifibrinolytics.14 The risk of adverse events, including thromboembolism, has not been increased in prophylactic administration during scoliosis surgery.76

The other practice involves hemodilution to target a hematocrit level of 25–30 % and the transfusion of red blood cells to enhance oxygen carriage, with current guidelines suggesting a hemoglobin concentration target of 70–90 g/L.77 It also encompasses autologous blood reinfusion, induced hypotension with a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 50–55 mmHg—adjusted from the preoperative baseline by a reduction of 30 %, yet with caution in individuals with a history of stroke, myocardial infarction, or hypertension—and the surveillance of urine output to guarantee sufficient tissue perfusion.78

Temperature management

Patients undergoing spinal deformity correction surgery are at risk for perioperative hypothermia due to long surgical incisions (10–40 cm) and prolonged exposure of large soft tissue areas to the cooler temperatures of the operating room environment, leading to significant heat loss.79 Hypothermia during general anesthesia can impair platelet function and disrupt the coagulation cascade, leading to increased blood loss.80 It is essential to avoid hypothermia to reduce central vasoconstriction and blood loss.81 Therefore, strategies such as real-time temperature monitoring, ambient temperature control, and the use of active warming devices are indispensable for blood conservation.82 Pre-warming pediatric patients before entering the operating room for spinal deformity correction surgery can reduce the incidence of intraoperative hypothermia and allogenic blood transfusion requirements.83

Pain management

Multimodal analgesia is the cornerstone of postoperative recovery after scoliosis correction.84 A pragmatic bundle includes acetaminophen, judicious NSAIDs, peri-incisional/local infiltration, neuraxial or catheter techniques where feasible, and opioid-sparing adjuncts are tailored to phenotype.85

NSAIDs and fusion: Evidence remains mixed. Fusion risk appears related to dose, duration, and specific agent86; brief, low-to-moderate dosing in the immediate postoperative period may balance analgesia with bone-healing concerns, whereas prolonged/high-dose regimens warrant caution in high-risk fusions.87 Notably, recent data suggest no increase in bleeding or epidural hematoma in minimally invasive fusion with NSAIDs.88, 89 Practical approach: use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration; avoid prolonged high-dose ketorolac in long, multi-level constructs or in patients with additional nonunion risk factors.90, 91

Ketamine: Benefit is context-dependent. In opioid-tolerant adults, subanesthetic ketamine reduces pain and opioid consumption after complex spine fusion.92 In opioid-naïve pediatric posterior fusion under modern multimodal regimens, prolonged low-dose ketamine has not reduced opioid use.93 Mechanistic and clinical differences (tolerance/central sensitization, dosing, and background analgesia) likely explain the divergence.

Regional/neuraxial techniques: Epidural analgesia or intrathecal morphine can substantially reduce early pain and opioid exposure but require careful timing (coagulation status) and team expertise. Erector spinae plane catheters are an emerging option where epidurals are unsuitable.94

Personalized opioid strategy: Patient factors (age, tolerance, pharmacogenomics) can guide opioid choice and dosing. Pair PCIA with non-opioid components to keep VAS < 4 in the first 48 h when feasible.95, 96

Postoperative neurologic deficit

Postoperative neurologic deficit is a significant concern following complex spinal reconstructive surgery, impacting both patient outcomes and quality of life. A literature review of spinal surgeries reveals an incidence of neurologic deficits in 0.15–8.3 % of cases.97 To reduce this complication, Multimodal intraoperative monitoring has become the standard of care for complex reconstructive spinal surgery.98

Discussion

The anesthetic management of scoliosis correction surgery necessitates a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach. Proper patient positioning is essential to maintain optimal spinal alignment while minimizing the risk of peripheral nerve injury. Effective airway management is of critical importance due to the potential challenges associated with intubation and the requirement for prolonged mechanical ventilation in the intra- and postoperative periods. Maintaining hemodynamic stability relies on precise titration of anesthetic agents in conjunction with the judicious use of vasoactive medications. Adequate postoperative pain control is crucial not only for ensuring patient comfort but also for promoting early mobilization and facilitating recovery.

Despite significant advancements in the perioperative management of scoliosis correction surgery, several limitations and areas for future research warrant attention. A primary challenge is the absence of universally accepted, clear guidelines for anesthesia in this complex procedure, necessitating highly individualized anesthetic plans due to the inherent heterogeneity of the patient population and the intricate nature of the surgical interventions. This variability underscores the difficulty in establishing a singular "one-size-fits-all" approach.

Current literature also presents areas of conflicting evidence, such as the precise impact of various anesthetic agents on intraoperative neuromonitoring signals, which is critical for spinal cord integrity. Similarly, the optimal role and efficacy of certain analgesic adjuncts, such as low-dose ketamine in pediatric populations, require further clarification. The delicate balance in fluid management, while now informed by specific thresholds, still presents a complex clinical challenge that could benefit from more refined, individualized protocols. Furthermore, the long-term effects of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs on spinal fusion success, despite their analgesic benefits, remain an area requiring ongoing investigation.

Future research should prioritize the development of robust, evidence-based guidelines that integrate patient-specific factors and surgical nuances to standardize anesthetic practices while maintaining flexibility for individualization. Comparative studies of effectiveness are needed to resolve current controversies regarding anesthetic choices and their impact on neuromonitoring. Refining fluid management strategies through goal-directed approaches and exploring the broader application of awake spinal surgery to mitigate positioning-related complications are also critical.99 Finally, continued investigation into advanced multimodal pain management strategies, including the long-term efficacy and safety of novel regional anesthesia techniques, biologics, and technology-driven innovations such as artificial intelligence and machine learning in optimizing perioperative care, will be essential to further improve patient outcomes and quality of life.70 This structured identification of specific research priorities transforms the general acknowledgment of gaps into an actionable agenda, guiding future scientific inquiry in the field.

Conclusions

While no single anesthetic approach is universally applicable to scoliosis correction surgery, a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s clinical status and the procedural requirements is essential. Future research should aim to establish concise evidence-based guidelines that standardize anesthetic protocols while allowing for individualized adaptation, which will be pivotal for optimizing outcomes.
