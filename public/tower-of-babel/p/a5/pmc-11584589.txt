Chicken GLUT4 function via enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and inhibiting ribosome pathway in skeletal muscle satellite cells
Authors: Lin Zhang, Pengna Luo, Huihong Li, Yuxian Pan, Huaiyong Zhang, Xuemeng Si, Wen Chen, Yanqun Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glucose Transporter 4 (GLUT4) is a crucial protein facilitating glucose uptake and metabolism across cell membranes in mammals. However, information on GLUT4 in birds has historically been limited. In this study, we investigated the dynamic expression profile of chicken GLUT4 using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and examined its potential effects and mechanisms via GLUT4 overexpression and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in chicken primary skeletal muscle satellite cells (CP-SMSCs). Our results demonstrated that chicken GLUT4 is differentially expressed across tissues, with predominant expression in skeletal muscles, and across developmental stages of CP-SMSCs, with notable upregulation during the phases of cell proliferation and early differentiation. Notably, 0.1 μM insulin for 60 min significantly elevated the expression of GLUT4 in CP-SMSCs (P < 0.05). GLUT4 overexpression in CP-SMSCs promoted cell proliferation, as evidenced by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (P < 0.05) and 5-Ethynyl-2′-Deoxyuridine (EDU) assays (P < 0.05), and enhanced glucose consumption following 0.1 μM insulin treatment (P < 0.05). However, it inhibited glucose consumption 12 h after the addition of 5 g/L glucose (P < 0.05). After overexpressing GLUT4, we identified 302 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CP-SMSCs, with 134 upregulated and 168 downregulated. These DEGs are primarily enriched in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome, cardiac muscle contraction, ATP metabolic processes, and mitochondrial protein complexes. Specifically, in the enriched oxidative phosphorylation pathway, the upregulated DEGs (12) encode mitochondrial proteins, while the downregulated DEGs (6) are nuclear genome-derived. The ribosomal pathway is predominantly inhibited, accompanying with the downregulation of the translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 7 (TOMM7)/translocase of inner mitochondrial membrane 8 (TIMM8A) complex responsible for mitochondrial protein transport, and a reduction in 28S (LOC121106978) and 18S (LOC112533601) ribosomal rRNAs. In conclusion, chicken GLUT4 is dynamically modulated during development and acts as an insulin responder that significantly regulates cellular glucose uptake and cell proliferation. This regulation occurs mainly through enhancing the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and inhibiting ribosomal pathway.

INTRODUCTION

Glucose Transporter 4 (GLUT4, SLC2A4) is an essential glucose transporter pivotal for regulating glucose uptake and metabolism in mammalian cells, particularly in muscle and adipose tissues (Huang and Czech, 2007; Martins et al., 2021). GLUT4's dynamic translocation to the cell membrane in response to insulin is well-documented and critical for maintaining glucose homeostasis (Jaldin-Fincati et al., 2017). This tightly regulated process underscores the importance of GLUT4 in metabolic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, where impaired GLUT4 function results in defective glucose uptake and elevated blood glucose levels (Chadt and Al-Hasani, 2020).

GLUT4 plays a multifaceted role in cellular metabolism (Bryant and Gould, 2020), primarily responsible for insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. This process involves the translocation of GLUT4-containing vesicles from intracellular storage sites to the plasma membrane (Sadler et al., 2013). This translocation is mediated by a complex signaling cascade initiated by insulin binding to its receptor, which activates the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway and subsequently the protein kinase B (AKT) pathway (Krishnasamy, 2018). AKT phosphorylates several downstream targets, including acetyltransferases E1A binding protein p300 (P300) and cAMP response element binding protein (CBP), which are crucial for GLUT4 exocytic translocation (Krishnasamy, 2018; Martins et al., 2021). Once at the plasma membrane, GLUT4 facilitates the entry of glucose into the cell, where it can be utilized for energy production through glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), or stored as glycogen (Chang et al., 2023; Nakrani et al., 2023). In mammalian muscle cells, this process is vital for maintaining energy homeostasis during periods of increased demand, such as exercise (Richter, 2021).

Beyond its role in glucose transport, GLUT4 is implicated in various other cellular functions. For instance, GLUT4 activity influences cellular redox state and oxidative stress responses (Walton, 2017). By modulating glucose flux, GLUT4 affects the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms (Ramachandran and Saravanan, 2015; Walton, 2017). Additionally, GLUT4 interacts with other metabolic pathways, including those involving amino acids (Bernard et al., 2012) and lipids (Atkinson et al., 2013), thereby integrating multiple aspects of cellular metabolism.

The regulation of GLUT4 is crucial for metabolic health. Insulin resistance, a hallmark of type 2 diabetes and obesity, is characterized by impaired GLUT4 translocation and function, leading to reduced glucose uptake and hyperglycemia (Czech, 2017; McNay and Pearson-Leary, 2020). Understanding the mechanisms underlying GLUT4 dysfunction in these conditions has been a major focus of metabolic research. Therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing GLUT4 expression or function show promise in improving glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity (Correa-Giannella and Machado, 2013; Vlavcheski et al., 2020; Perreault et al., 2021).

Despite extensive research on GLUT4 in mammalian systems, our understanding of GLUT4 function in avian species remains nascent. Birds, being endothermic vertebrates with unique metabolic features such as high blood glucose and insulin resistance (Satoh, 2021; Du et al., 2023), offer a compelling opportunity to explore GLUT4's role in regulating glucose metabolism from a different physiological angle. The recent study on the cloning and characterization of chicken GLUT4 (Luo et al., 2023) has opened new avenues for investigating its role in avian physiology, particularly its function in skeletal muscle tissues. Skeletal muscle satellite cells are essential for muscle growth, repair, and regeneration (Chang and Rudnicki, 2014), making them a critical focus for understanding the metabolic pathways influenced by GLUT4 in chickens. Given the importance of skeletal muscle in poultry production, elucidating the function and regulation of GLUT4 in skeletal muscle satellite cells can provide valuable insights into improving muscle growth and metabolic efficiency in poultry.

In this study, we explore the dynamic expression profile of chicken GLUT4 through real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). By employing GLUT4 overexpression and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) techniques in skeletal muscle satellite cells from chickens, we aim to elucidate the potential effects and mechanisms modulated by chicken GLUT4. Our study will contribute to the burgeoning field of avian metabolism, offering new insights into the functional dynamics of chicken GLUT4 and its potential applications in poultry science.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample Collection

Arbor Acres broiler chick embryos were incubated under conditions set at a temperature of 37 ± 0.5°C and a humidity level of 55 to 60%. After hatching, the chicks were cage raised and provided with free access to water and a conventional balanced diet formulated according to the Chicken Feeding Standard (NY/T33-2004) until d 7 posthatch (D7). Following an overnight fast (12 h), the birds were euthanatized by cervical dislocation. The tissue samples including heart, liver, lung, kidney, pectoralis, leg muscle, proventriculus and brain were collected at embryonic d 14 (E14), embryonic d 19 (E19), and D7 from male chickens (n = 4). All collected tissue samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C for further use. The experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with animal welfare guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Henan Agricultural University (approval No. HNND20191201).

Isolation and Culture of Chicken Primary Skeletal Muscle Satellite Cells

CP-SMSCs were isolated from the pectoralis muscles of 5-day-old Arbor Acres broilers following the established protocols (Han et al., 2019). Minced muscle was treated with 0.1% type I collagenase (C8140, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and 0.25% trypsin (T1360, Solarbio, Beijing, China) to release cells. The cell suspension was filtered and subjected to Percoll density centrifugation to isolate CP-SMSCs. After purification by differential attachment, the cells were cultured in growth medium composed of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) (C3130-0500, VivaCell, Shanghai, China), 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 26140-079, Grand Island, NY, USA), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator (MCO-5AC, Sanyo, Osaka, Japan). Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, cells were induced to differentiate with a differentiation medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2% horse serum). The medium was replaced every day.

Construction of GLUT4 Overexpression Vector and Cell Transfection

The GLUT4 overexpression vector (pcDNA3.1-GLUT4-EGFP) was constructed using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (C113-02, Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China). The GLUT4 coding sequence (GenBank: OP491293.1) was amplified from chicken skeletal muscle cDNA via conventional PCR with the following primers: 5′-gggagacccaagctggctagcATGCCGATTGGATTCCAGC-3′ and 5′-gtaccgtcgactgcagaattctGCCATCTTCCCCCCCCCT-3′ (the underline indicates the homologous arm sequence). After linearization of the plasmid with NheI and EcoRI restriction endonucleases, the purified PCR product was subcloned into the pcDNA3.1-EGFP vector (HonorGene, Changsha, China) and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All transient transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (MF138-04, Mei5 Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were divided into 2 groups: the negative control group (NC, transfected with pcDNA3.1-EGFP vector), and the GLUT4 overexpression group (OE, transfected with pcDNA3.1-GLUT4-EGFP vector).

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using the TransZol RNA isolation kit (TransGen Biotech Co. Ltd, Beijing, China). 500 ng total RNA were reverse-transcribed to synthesize the first-strand cDNA in a 20 μL reaction system using HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (plus gDNA wiper) (R312-02, Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR primers were designed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Primer-BLAST tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) and their amplification efficiency were checked. The optimized primers used for RT-qPCR were listed in Table S1. RT-qPCR was performed with a LightCycler 96 instrument (BioRad, Hercules, CA) and in a 10 μL reaction system containing 5 μL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China), 0.2 μL (0.1 μM each) of forward and reverse primers (Table S1), 1 μL of cDNA template, and 3.6 μL of ddH2O. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Each experiment consisted of at least 3 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates. The amplification experiment included a negative control where template cDNA was omitted. The 2−ΔΔCt method (Trick et al., 2021) was employed for relative quantification of gene expression, with β-actin as the internal reference gene.

Insulin Treatment on GLUT4 Expression in CP-SMSCs

CP-SMSCs were seeded into 12-well plates in complete DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20% FBS. After reaching 80 to 90% confluency, cells were washed with phosphate buffered solution (PBS; Solarbio, Beijing, China) and starved in serum-free medium for 12 h. Following starvation, cells were treated with rapid-acting insulin (Insulin Aspart Injection, NovoRapid, China) in a series of concentrations (diluted in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20% FBS), and harvested at 15, 60, and 120 minutes post-treatment, respectively (n = 4).

Glucose Consumption under Insulin/Glucose Stimulation

Cells were transfected at approximately 50 to 60% confluence. After transfection for 48 h, cells were co-treated with 0.1 μM insulin (diluted in complete DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20% FBS), and the culture medium (250 µL) were collected at multiple time points (0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min) after treatment (n = 6). Additionally, cells were co-treated with 5 g/L glucose (diluted in complete DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20% FBS), and the culture medium (250 µL) were collected at 0 h and 12 h postinoculation (n = 6). Glucose contents in the collected culture medium were determined using a Glucose (GLU) Assay Kit (A154-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 505 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Cell Counting Kit-8 Assay

To assess cell proliferation and viability, CP-SMSCs were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Cells were transfected at approximately 50 to 60% confluence. Each treatment group contained 6 biological replicates, and each sample underwent 3 technical replicates. After transfection for 22, 46, and 70 h, 10 μL of enhanced CCK-8 reagent (A311-01, Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each sample at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT).

5-Ethynyl-2′-Deoxyuridine Incorporation Assay

CP-SMSCs were evenly seeded into a 96-well plate. Cell transfection was performed after reaching approximately 50 to 60% confluence. After 48 h of transfection, 100 μL of 50 μM EDU from the Cell-Light EDU Apollo 567 In Vitro Kit (C10310-1, Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 h according to the manufacturer's instructions. Images of 3 random fields per well were captured using a fluorescence inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The analysis of cell proliferation was performed using ImageJ software (version 6.0) (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Each treatment group included 10 independent replicates.

RNA Sequencing

After reaching about 50 to 60% confluence, CP-SMSCs were transfected with GLUT4 overexpressing vector (pcDNA3.1-EGFP vector as the negative control). After 48 h of transfection, cells were collected for RNA-seq (n = 3/group). Total RNA was extracted from both the GLUT4 overexpressing group and the negative control group using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio, Tokyo, Japan). RNA integrity was assessed with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA). PolyA-tailed mRNA was enriched from total RNA using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and then fragmented randomly. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized with random hexamers primers in the presence of M-MuLV reverse transcriptase, followed by synthesis of the second cDNA strand using dNTPs as substrates with the DNA polymerase I system. The cDNA fragments approximately 370∼420 bp in length were selected using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA). PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system. The quality of the library was evaluated on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Following the generation of clusters, sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, which produced 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw data underwent processing to generate clean data through a filtering process using fastp software (version 0.19.7). This process removed reads containing adapters, poly-N sequences, and those of low quality. Furthermore, parameters such as Q20, Q30, and GC content were computed. All subsequent analyses utilized the high-quality clean data obtained from these processes.

The clean reads were aligned to the NCBI database GRCg7b reference genome (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/016/699/485/GCF_016699485.2_bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b/GCF_016699485.2_bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b_genomic.fna.gz) using HISAT2 v2.0.5 software. The mapped reads from each sample were assembled with StringTie (v1.3.3b) in a reference-based approach (Pertea et al., 2015). Feature Counts (1.5.0-p3) was employed to quantify reads mapped to each gene, and the Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKM) value for each gene was calculated based on gene length and mapped reads. Differential expression analysis between 2 comparison groups was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Genes with |log2fold change (FC)| ≥ 0 and P-value ≤ 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were further analyzed for gene ontology (GO) enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, and visualization of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway was performed using online bioinformatics tools (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/basic_local_go_pathway_enrichment_analysis_122). The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on transcriptomics data using online bioinformatics tools (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/plot_basic_PCA_plot_034). Twelve genes were randomly selected for RT-qPCR confirmation. Details of all primers used in RT-qPCR were listed in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis were performed in SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistically significant differences between 2 groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test, and differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's post-hoc test. Statistical significance was considered at P ≤ 0.05. All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Graphical plots were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

RESULTS

Tissue Expression Pattern of Chicken GLUT4

First, we assessed GLUT4 expression levels in various tissues of broilers across different developmental stages using RT-qPCR. Chicken GLUT4 exhibited distinct expression patterns during different stages. At E14, no significant differences were observed in GLUT4 expression among the analyzed tissues (Figure 1A). However, by E19, the expression levels of GLUT4 were notably higher in breast and leg muscles (P < 0.001), with breast muscle exhibiting significantly higher expression compared to heart, liver, lung, kidney, proventriculus, and brain tissues (Figure 1B). By D7, the expression levels of GLUT4 in striate muscles, including heart, breast muscle and leg muscle, were significantly higher compared to other tissues (P < 0.05) (Figure 1C). Dynamic Change of GLUT4 in CP-SMSCs

Subsequently, we further examined the expression pattern of GLUT4 in CP-SMSCs during the phases of cell proliferation and differentiation using RT-qPCR (Figure 2A). After 9 d’ differentiation, the satellite cells coalesced to form multinucleated myotubes (Figure 2B). The study revealed a strikingly dynamic modulation of chicken GLUT4 level synchronous with cell development in CP-SMSCs. The expression of GLUT4 was notably enhanced at the 24th and 36th h of the cell proliferation phase in contrast to the initial stage (about 7-fold). Furthermore, the gene expression of GLUT4 steeply plunged by the third day of differentiation and consistently remained at a lower stratum throughout the observation duration (Figure 2A). Response of Chicken GLUT4 to Insulin Treatment in CP-SMSCs

We further investigated the impact of insulin treatment on chicken GLUT4 expression of CP-SMSCs. It showed that after 15 and 60 minutes of treatment, insulin regulated GLUT4 expression in a dose-dependent manner. Low-dose insulin promoted GLUT4 expression, whereas high-dose insulin inhibited its expression (Figure 3A and 3B). At 60 min, a pronounced variation in GLUT4 expression was noted across treatments, with the highest levels observed in cells treated with 0.1 μM insulin, which were significantly greater than those in other groups (P < 0.05) (Figure 3B). To further investigate the role of GLUT4 in glucose metabolism, we examined the effects of an overexpressed GLUT4 vector combined with exogenous insulin/glucose on cellular glucose uptake. It was observed that glucose contents in the culture medium dynamically changed over time. However, the overexpression of GLUT4 notably promoted glucose consumption in CP-SMSCs at 15, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after providing 0.1 μM insulin (P < 0.05) (Figure 5C). On the other hand, the overexpression of GLUT4 inhibited glucose consumption of CP-SMSCs at 12 h after adding 5 g/L glucose (P < 0.05) (Figure 5D). In conclusion, chicken GLUT4 was involved in cellular glucose uptake.

Gene ontology and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted to identify involved pathways induced by GLUT4 overexpression. It indicated that DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome, cardiac muscle contraction, ATP metabolic process and mitochondrial protein complexes (Figure 6B and Figure S1C, Table S3). Therein, the upregulated DEGs were predominantly involved in oxidative phosphorylation, cardiac muscle contraction, lysine degradation, cellular respiration, and ATP metabolic processes (Figure 6C and Table S3). However, the downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, tyrosine metabolism, arginine, and proline metabolism, as well as cysteine and methionine metabolism (Figure 6D and Table S3). Notably, oxidative phosphorylation emerged as a pathway where both up-regulated and down-regulated genes were enriched (Figure 6, Figure 6, Figure 7). Interestingly, all 12 up-regulated DEGs related to oxidative phosphorylation encoded mitochondrial proteins (Figure 6C, Figure 7 and Table S2), while 6 down-regulated DEGs related to oxidative phosphorylation were nuclear genome-derived (Figure 6D, Figure 7, and Table S2). Ribosomal proteins encompass mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs) and cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins (CRPs) (Pecoraro et al., 2021). It was found that several ribosomal protein DEGs from both MRPs (4) and CRPs (7) were significantly downregulated, impacting overall ribosomal protein related genes' level (Figure 6E, Table S2 and Table S4). The 28S rRNA functions as a ribozyme, facilitating the formation of peptide bonds (Akhoundi et al., 2017). The 18S rRNA serves as the active center for protein synthesis within the 40S ribosomal subunit. The study indicates that the expression of 18S rRNA tends to decrease in correlation with the increased oxidative damage markers observed in atherosclerotic plaques (Hayashi, 2014). Here, the levels of 28S (LOC121106978) and 18S (LOC112533601) ribosomal rRNAs were significantly downregulated by overexpressing GLUT4 (Table S2). The translocase of the inner mitochondrial membrane/translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane (TIM/TOM) complex is responsible for transporting proteins synthesized from nuclear DNA across the mitochondrial membrane for utilization in oxidative phosphorylation (Chacinska et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2022; Haastrup et al., 2023). DISCUSSION

GLUT4 is the main protein responsible for facilitating glucose transport, playing a pivotal role in maintaining glucose homeostasis in mammals (Leto and Saltiel, 2012; Chang et al., 2023; Knudsen et al., 2023). However, research on avian GLUT4 has long lagged behind. It has been believed for a long time that birds lacked GLUT4, which might contribute to their hyperglycemia (Seki et al., 2003; Xiong and Lei, 2021). Based on chicken GLUT4 sequence information (Luo et al., 2023), we further conducted the functional studies of chicken GLUT4.

Chicken GLUT4 Mediates the Regulation of Glucose Homeostasis

Our research demonstrated that, similar to GLUT4 in mammals (Chang et al., 2023; Knudsen et al., 2023), chicken GLUT4 is responsive to insulin. Under insulin stimulation, GLUT4 effectively promotes glucose uptake in cells. However, higher concentrations of insulin lead to a suppression of GLUT4 levels in CP-SMSCs. Similarly, excessive glucose can inhibit GLUT4 expression, and thereby reduce glucose intake. This dynamic modulation indicates a negative feedback loop involving GLUT4's crucial role as a glucose transporter in maintaining biological system homeostasis (Chang et al., 2023). The study reported that insulin increased cell surface levels of GLUT4 by directing it into a recycling pathway in a dose-dependent manner (Govers et al., 2004). Additionally, high glucose levels inhibit myogenesis in C2C12 cells by reducing both basal and insulin-stimulated GLUT4 expression and glucose uptake through the inhibition of AKT signaling (Luo et al., 2019).

Chicken GLUT4 Being Dynamically Regulated With Development

Consistent with the finding of Luo et al.(Luo et al., 2023), chicken GLUT4 is dynamically regulated across tissues and is predominantly expressed in striated muscles, similar to GLUT4 in mammals (Bryant and Gould, 2020), which suggests a conserved function across birds and mammals. Both skeletal muscles and the heart have a high demand for glucose. The elevated expression of GLUT4 in these tissues reflects their high metabolic rate and the substantial requirement for glucose to generate ATP, which fuels cellular processes including muscle contraction (Shadrin et al., 2016).

Chicken GLUT4 also exhibited dramatically dynamic modulation during the development of skeletal muscle satellite cells. It appears that chicken GLUT4 primarily functions during the proliferation phase and the initial stages of differentiation in CP-SMSCs. This expression pattern correlates with the evolving metabolic demands of cells throughout these distinct phases of the cell cycle and differentiation (Ryall et al., 2015; Evano and Tajbakhsh, 2018; Shamim et al., 2018). During proliferation and early differentiation, cells actively divide and prepare for differentiation. Elevated GLUT4 expression during these stages likely supports increased glucose uptake, aiding in energy production and biosynthesis (Moreno et al., 2003; Dumont et al., 2015). Conversely, the sharp decline in GLUT4 gene expression during late differentiation suggests a reduced need for glucose transport as cells mature and fuse into myotubes. As differentiation progresses beyond the initial days, cells became more specialized as mature muscle cells, primarily relying on oxidative pathways for energy, in contrast to the predominantly glycolytic metabolism of proliferating cells (Ryall et al., 2015; Shamim et al., 2018). Therefore, the decreased GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake reflects the evolving metabolic profile and functional specialization of differentiating muscle cells. These changes underscore the dynamic metabolic adaptations occurring during cell differentiation.

GLUT4 Promotes Cell Proliferation Independent of Related Markers

The results indicate that overexpressing chicken GLUT4 enhances satellite cells' overall proliferation. The lack of significant changes in proliferation-associated marker levels suggested that GLUT4 may promote proliferation through a different mechanism that does not directly involve these markers. GLUT4, a glucose transporter, could enhance cell proliferation by improving glucose uptake and thus supporting the energy and biosynthetic needs of rapidly dividing cells (Moreno et al., 2003; Dumont et al., 2015).

GLUT4 may Primarily Function Through Activating Mitochondrial Oxidative Phosphorylation

RNA-seq further revealed that GLUT4 promotes cell proliferation mainly by activating oxidative phosphorylation. This activation primarily involves mitochondrion-originated oxidative phosphorylation genes (12/18), while inhibiting several nuclear-originated genes associated with complex I, complex IV, as well as ATP synthase (complex V) (Figure 7) (Yang et al., 2020; Alldred et al., 2021). The oxidative phosphorylation process serves as the primary energy source for most cells, with tight regulation of nuclear and mitochondrial gene expression governing the macromolecular complexes involved in the electron transport chain (English et al., 2020; Soto et al., 2022). These mitochondrially-originated proteins play essential roles in electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation, which are crucial for cellular energy production (Chagas et al., 2022; Soto et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023). The up-regulation of mitochondrial genes involving in oxidative phosphorylation suggests a direct activation or enhancement of mitochondrial function. Conversely, the down-regulated genes in oxidative phosphorylation that are derived from the nuclear genome indicate a complex regulatory adjustment, possibly through feedback mechanisms (Mottis et al., 2019; English et al., 2020). This is logical, as the increased glucose uptake facilitated by GLUT4 can lead to a higher flux through glycolysis, enhancing ATP production capacity and subsequently feeding into mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to meet the energy demands of proliferating cells.

It also suggests that chicken GLUT4 may impact cellular metabolism by affecting mitonuclear and mitocellular communication (Mottis et al., 2019; English et al., 2020) and enhance the oxidative phosphorylation pathway by altering the mitochondria-nuclear balance (Houtkooper et al., 2013). The inhibition of the ribosomal pathway may indicate a shift in resource allocation, where the cell prioritizes energy production (oxidative phosphorylation) over protein synthesis (ribosome function). This also implies a feedback mechanism to prevent excessive cell proliferation (Wakim and Grewal, 2020). Furthermore, it is important to consider the interplay between ribosomes and mitochondria (Dai et al., 2017; Milenkovic et al., 2023).

Cellular homeostasis is crucial for optimal cell function, metabolism, growth and survival (Rué and Martinez Arias, 2015). Cells must adapt to the changes brought about by the highly active glucose metabolism resulting from GLUT4 overexpression. The downregulation of certain nuclear-derived oxidative phosphorylation genes and ribosomal genes can be interpreted as a biochemical adaption of cells to altered metabolic requirements (Shi et al., 2017). These genes encode proteins crucial for oxidative phosphorylation and protein synthesis, both of which are highly energy-demanding processes. As cellular energy shifts towards increased glucose metabolism, a reduction in other energy-consuming activities becomes necessary. The upregulation of glycolysis due to GLUT4 overexpression may lead to feedback inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation, preventing mitochondrial overload and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (Wakim and Grewal, 2020). Our research showed that the cross-talk between mitochondria and ribosomes is crucial under fluctuating glucose conditions induced by chicken GLUT4, enabling cells to fine-tune metabolic activities and respond adaptively during nutritional stresses.

Overall, by elucidating the molecular mechanisms of chicken GLUT4, this study bridges the knowledge gap in avian glucose metabolism, underscoring the intricate balance of metabolic pathways influenced by this pivotal transporter. Further research in this domain will not only enhance our understanding of avian physiology but also offer broader insights into the conservation and divergence of metabolic regulation mechanisms across vertebrates.

CONCLUSIONS

Chicken GLUT4 is dynamically expressed across birds’ tissues and the developmental phases of CP-SMSCs, with predominant levels in skeletal muscle and during the proliferation and early differentiation phases of CP-SMSCs. Insulin regulates chicken GLUT4 levels in a dose-dependent manner in CP-SMSCs, low concentrations of insulin promote GLUT4 expression, while high concentrations of insulin and glucose inhibit it. GLUT4 promotes the proliferation of CP-SMSCs, along with regulating glucose uptake. RNA-seq analysis revealed that 302 genes were modulated by GLUT4 overexpression in CP-SMSCs. GLUT4 overexpression weakened ribosomal function and enhanced the oxidative phosphorylation pathway primarily through activating mitochondrial-originated oxidative phosphorylation genes, while inhibiting several nuclear-originated oxidative phosphorylation genes.
