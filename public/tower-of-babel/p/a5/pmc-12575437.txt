A Case Report of Bronchogenic Cyst in the Posterior Wall of Gastric Fundus With Elevated CEA and CA199 in Cystic Fluid
Authors: Yeqing Chen, Zhenxiang Wang, Guanpo Zhang, Mingwei Han, Hongyu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBronchogenic cysts originate from abnormal foregut budding during embryogenesis. Two pathogenic mechanisms exist: (1) ectopic migration of tracheobronchial precursors forming cysts in extrapulmonary sites, and (2) retained bronchial buds developing pulmonary cysts through mucus accumulation. Despite anatomical variations, all cysts retain respiratory epithelium with mucinous glands, pathognomonically reflecting their embryological origin. Herein, we report an unusual case of bronchogenic cyst in the posterior wall of the gastric fundus with elevated CEA and CA199 in cystic fluid. CT showed that a kind of circular soft tissue density shadow was visible under the submucosa of the stomach floor, with a size of about 3.9 × 3.7 cm, uniform density, and clear boundary. Pathology after surgical resection of the gastric tumor: the cystic wall‐like tissue was locally lined with ciliated columnar epithelium, the mucosa showed chronic inflammation, and foreign body giant cell reaction, calcification, and ossification were observed in the submucosa. The patient was diagnosed as having a bronchogenic cyst in the posterior wall of the gastric fundus and was followed up for 12 months after the operation, with no obvious discomfort.

Introduction

It is well established that bronchogenic cysts are due to the sprouting of an abnormal bronchial branch, which occurs during embryonic development when a portion of the trachea and bronchi expands outward and the epithelium secretes mucus to gradually form a cyst [1]. Meanwhile, tissue from the primitive trachea may be shed during differentiation to develop ectopic bronchial cysts in other locations; for example, gastric bronchogenic cysts may form in the stomach wall [2]. Since these diseases are rare and underreported, we report a case of bronchogenic cyst in the posterior wall of the gastric fundus with elevated CEA and CA19‐9 in the cystic fluid as follows.

Case History and Examination

A 37‐year‐old male was admitted to the hospital on January 11, 2024, due to an electronic gastroscope that found a submucosal protrusion of the gastric fundus for 1 year. He had no abdominal pain and belching at normal times and no weight loss. Physical examination showed no positive signs. A gastroscope was performed and found that a protuberant lesion was situated in the fundus of the stomach, with a smooth surface and a size of about 35 × 40 mm, which was soft to the touch (Figure 1). Enhanced CT showed that a kind of round soft tissue density shadow was seen in the submucosa of the stomach bottom, with a size of about 3.9 × 3.7 cm, uniform density, and clear boundary. After enhancement, it was not enhanced, with annular enhancement at the edge and a nodular dense shadow growing in the cavity, which was considered neurilemmoma (Figure 2). An ultrasonic endoscope was performed and indicated that it originated from the third layer, and there was no echo shadow. A small amount of flocculent high echo was seen inside, with a cross‐sectional size of about 34.1 × 47.0 mm, and the raised part was located outside the cavity (Figure 3). Preliminary diagnosis: gastric fundus bulge (considering deep cystic gastritis or gastric schwannoma). For multidisciplinary consultation, EUS‐FNA was recommended. EUS‐FNA operation: 10 mL of turbid cystic fluid was extracted, and the drawing test was positive, with CEA 3824 U/mL and CA199 670.1 U/mL in the cystic fluid, and no tumor cells were found in pathology. Deep cystic gastritis was considered in the patient's gastric fundus bulge, and neurilemmoma and tumor markers in cystic fluid increased, but the possibility of malignant transformation was not ruled out. There were indications for surgery. On January 25th, 2024, laparoscopic partial gastrectomy was performed. The surgical findings showed that a round tumor with a size of about 4 × 3 cm was seen in the posterior wall of the gastric fundus, which grew in the cavity and partially grew out of the cavity. The capsule was intact and had no obvious adhesion to surrounding tissues. Pathology of the resected gastric tumor after the operation: the cystic wall‐like tissue was locally lined with stratified ciliated columnar epithelium, the mucosa showed chronic inflammation, and foreign body giant cell reaction, calcification, and ossification were seen in the submucosa (Figure 4). Immunohistochemistry: CK7 (++), CD1a‐2 (++), MU5AC (++), TTF‐1 (++), P63 (++).

Histopathology of the gross specimen shows that the cystic wall‐like tissue is locally lined with stratified ciliated columnar epithelium, the mucosa shows chronic inflammation, submucosal interstitial edema, and collagen fiber hyperplasia, as well as foreign body giant cell reaction, calcification, and ossification, which are consistent with a gastric wall bronchogenic cyst.

Differential Diagnosis

Gastric bronchogenic cysts exhibit nonspecific clinical and laboratory findings. Initially asymptomatic when small, these lesions demonstrate progressive enlargement due to mucus secretion from respiratory‐type epithelial lining. Symptomatic presentation typically arises from mass effect on adjacent structures.

Standard imaging modalities frequently lead to preoperative misdiagnosis, with common differentials including gastrointestinal stromal tumors, schwannomas, and deep cystic gastritis. CT reveals well‐circumscribed lesions with hyperattenuating content (50–60 Hounsfield units), while MRI demonstrates T2‐weighted hyperintensity with postcontrast wall enhancement and focal calcifications. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), though rarely documented, characteristically shows submucosal heterogeneous hyperechoic lesions with anechoic components, occasionally arising from the muscularis propria layer with posterior acoustic enhancement.

Definitive histopathological criteria require identification of bronchial elements: (1) ciliated pseudostratified epithelium with mucinous glands, (2) cartilaginous components. The presence of well‐differentiated dual‐layer smooth muscle bundles defines esophageal cysts, while isolated ciliated epithelium suggests foregut cysts. Immunohistochemical TTF‐1 positivity confirms bronchial origin.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was diagnosed as a bronchogenic cyst in the posterior wall of the gastric fundus and was followed up for 12 months after the operation and recovered well without obvious discomfort.

Discussion

Gastrobronchial cyst is a rare congenital disease in clinic [3, 4, 5], which is caused by abnormal development of the embryonic bronchial system, caused by abnormal bronchial germ breaking off from bronchial branches and remaining in different parts. Anatomy is divided into intrapulmonary type, mediastinal type, and ectopic type. It is composed of one or more tissues, including bronchial ciliated columnar epithelium, elastic fibers, smooth muscles, glands, and cartilage tissues. Bronchial cyst is defined as a cystic lesion characterized by ciliated columnar epithelium (single layer or pseudo‐stratified) lining the inner wall [6]. The stomach is a rare site of bronchogenic cyst, most common in the small gastric curvature or gastroesophageal junction. Gastrobronchogenic cysts are rare, lack specificity in clinical manifestations and routine imaging examination, and have a high misdiagnosis rate before operation. They were diagnosed as schwannoma, stromal tumor, and deep cystic gastritis. CT, MRI, and EUS are helpful for the diagnosis of gastrobronchial cyst, and the diagnosis depends on pathological examination. In the differentiation of pathology and esophageal cyst, gastrobronchial cyst must be accompanied by cartilage or respiratory gland. Immunohistochemical expression of TTF‐1 confirmed the origin of bronchial epithelium. This case was misdiagnosed as schwannoma and deep cystic gastritis before operation. Endoscopic ultrasonography can help diagnose gastrobronchial cyst, and the gross specimen and immunohistochemistry can make a definite diagnosis after operation. This case of bronchogenic cyst in the posterior wall of gastric fundus with significantly increased CEA and CA199 in puncture fluid under ultrasound guidance is rarely reported. This disease is a benign lesion, but there is a trend of further growth and it can become malignant. In 1999, foreign scholars reported cases of adenocarcinoma in retroperitoneal bronchogenic cysts [7], and then in 2017, foreign scholars reported literature of intestinal adenocarcinoma caused by bronchial cysts [8]. Treatment principles: follow‐up observation is recommended for patients with small lesions and no symptoms, and submucosal resection is also feasible. For patients with severe symptoms, surgery is recommended, especially for those with infection and malignant symptoms; early surgery results in good prognosis for most cases. If conditions permit, fine needle aspiration under the guidance of ultrasound endoscope can be performed before operation, such as turbid cystic fluid and the increase of tumor markers (CEA, were observed) in cystic fluid, which indicates the possibility of malignant transformation, and ESD treatment should be avoided as far as possible. At present, there is no description of abnormal increases in tumor markers in puncture sac fluid at home and abroad, and whether it can indicate that the disease has a tendency for malignant transformation needs further clinical confirmation.
