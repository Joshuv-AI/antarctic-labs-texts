Enhancing COVID-19 forecasts with a lightweight multi-head depthwise separable convolution network
Authors: Haoyuan Lan, Shunjiang Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the challenge of forecasting COVID-19 with limited data, this paper presents CDSCnet (Chunking Depthwise Separable Convolution network). It is a lightweight model designed for small datasets, employing three fixed convolution heads to capture long-term dependencies without relying on recurrent units. Through a comprehensive comparative analysis with existing models, including CNN-LSTM variants, using COVID-19 datasets from 7 countries, such as India, Brazil, and the United States, we demonstrate the superior performance of CDSCnet. Across both the 8 smooth datasets and the 3 high-noise datasets, CDSCnet achieved optimal prediction accuracy, attaining a maximum MAE reduction exceeding 50% against the reproduced results in Spain task. These experimental results confirm that CDSCnet effectively captures the dynamics of epidemic spread, proving its utility as a reliable decision-support tool for COVID-19.Keywords: COVID-19, Deep learning, ForecastingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The emergence of the novel coronavirus (COVID-19) in late 2019 has brought a profound global impact, causing significant casualties and economic disruption1,2. To support policymakers in formulating effective control strategies3, constructing reliable epidemic prediction models has emerged as a hot topic in academia. Early studies primarily relied on compartmental models (e.g., SEIR, SQEIR, SEQAIJR4–6) and traditional time series models (e.g., ARIMA7–9). In recent years, deep learning has demonstrated considerable potential in epidemic prediction due to its ability to model complex nonlinear relationships. Fundamental neural network architectures, such as CNN and RNN, have shown remarkable efficacy: Liu et al.10 proved RNN yielded significantly lower errors on U.S. datasets than traditional regression models; Kunjir et al.11 demonstrated that CNN surpassed decision trees in R2 scores on Chinese and Canadian datasets; Kırbaş et al.12 provided evidence that LSTM consistently outperforms both ARIMA and NARNN across eight datasets.

With deepening research, advances in neural network applications for epidemic modeling have primarily followed three directions: hybrid mechanism integration, deep architecture ensembles, and Transformer-based architectures. In the first direction, Swaraj et al.13 merged ARIMA and NAR models, achieving superior prediction accuracy in India compared to individual sub-models. Zheng et al.14 coupled an ISI compartmental model with BERT-LSTM, precisely capturing infection trends in Wuhan. Zivkovic et al.15 enhanced ANFIS with swarm intelligence algorithms (PSO/GA/FPA/CESBAS), significantly improving accuracy on Chinese datasets. Regarding deep ensembles, Xu et al.16 demonstrated that a CNN-LSTM combination outperformed single models in predicting daily new cases in Brazil and other countries. Jin et al.17 proposed a sophisticated TCN-GRU-DBN-Q-SVM framework, demonstrating the effectiveness of multi-network collaboration on the USA, India, and the U.K. As to Transformer-based models, Li et al.18 introduced a hybrid model combining Transformers with GCN, which outperforms LSTM and GRU in New York task, and Khairul et al.19 designed a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) model that performed well in U.S. county-level predictions by integrating multi-source features. However, the efficacy of Transformer-based models in time series forecasting remains debated. Zeng et al.20 noted that attention mechanisms exhibited inferior performance to linear models on certain datasets (e.g., ETTm1), alongside excessive computational costs. Furthermore, Bharadi et al.21 reported that Transformer-based models lagged behind hybrid architectures such as Multi-head CNN-LSTM across several metrics (cumulative cases, recoveries, deaths) on the U.S. and India datasets.

Reviewing existing studies, we find that hybrid models combining CNN and LSTM are significantly competitive. Specifically, References21–25 demonstrate that, compared to LR, MLP, SVM, ARIMA, GAN-GRU, even Transformer-based models, these hybrid models achieve superior accuracy in fitting datasets from dozens of countries across Europe, Asia, and the U.S.

Although hybrid models such as CNN-LSTM have demonstrated potential, there is room for improvement when applied to certain complex epidemic sequences. Notably, they frequently struggle to adequately capture mutation trends and are prone to overfitting on limited datasets16,22,25. This indicates that such models still face challenges in modeling complex temporal dependencies.

Meanwhile, recent advancements in deep learning-based time series forecasting offer promising avenues. For instance, Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) leverage dilated convolutions to expand the receptive field, demonstrating that convolutional architectures possess superior capability in processing long sequences compared to RNNs26. To mitigate the restricted receptive fields in TCN intermediate layers, Liu et al.27 proposed SCINet, a multi-convolutional head interaction architecture that has surpassed numerous competitors, including TCN, across various datasets such as traffic and electricity.

Nevertheless, its substantial parameter size, which exceeds 107 in ELC and Traffic tasks27, makes it incompatible with COVID-19 prediction tasks involving limited datasets (fewer than 103 samples)28–31.

Therefore, by incorporating the design philosophies of TCN and SCINet, this paper presents CDSCnet (Chunking Depthwise Separable Convolution network), a convolution-centric architecture specifically tailored for small datasets that retains the capacity to capture complex temporal dynamics. The key designs are as follows:

Lightweight structure The convolution head number is fixed at three, replacing the strategy of doubling heads at each layer in SCINet, which significantly reduces parameters.

Methodology

This section outlines our core framework, comprising three key modules: data processing, models, and evaluation metrics. First, the original dataset is normalized by a Min–Max scaler and segmented via a sliding window technique to extract temporal features suitable for the neural network. Subsequently, we detail our proposed model, baseline models and the validation strategy. Finally, diverse evaluation metrics, including MAPE, MAE, RMSE, and R2, are employed to systematically validate the model’s performance on datasets from multiple countries.

Sliding window

To construct a tensor compatible with neural networks, a sliding window strategy is employed. Let the normalized sequence be X = {x1,x2,…,xn}. By defining the window size (T) and prediction step (t), the sequence X is restructured into the input tensor =[X1,X2,…,Xn-t-T+1] and the label Y = [Y1,Y2,…,Yn-t-T+1]. Here, Xi = {xi:xi+T-1} represents a tensor containing window size T, and its corresponding label Yi = {xi+T:xi+t+T-1} covers prediction step t. Neural networks learn a mapping function f to transform the input tensor to its label: f[X1,X2,…,Xn-t-T+1] → [Y1,Y2,…,Yn-t-T+1].

Models

CDSCnet

Depthwise separable convolution (DSC), a lightweight convolutional network introduced by the Google research team in 2017, operates in two steps: a depthwise convolution that independently extracts features from each input channel, followed by a pointwise convolution that fuses these features to produce the final output33. Under identical conditions, DSC exhibits higher computational efficiency compared to standard CNN34.

CDSCnet is based on DSC and primarily consists of three core components: the Convolution Splitter, the CDSC_block, and the Residual Module, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The Convolution Splitter performs tensor splitting while ensuring the consistency of the sub-tensor shapes. The CDSC_block serves as the fundamental computational mechanism of CDSCnet, responsible for feature extraction. Finally, the Residual Module executes AveragePooling downsampling and integrates its results via element-wise addition with the sub-tensors.

Proposed model.

Furthermore, CDSCnet incorporates two auxiliary components. The first is a DSC layer, designed to align the processed tensor timesteps (T) with the labels. The second, FFN (Feed-Forward Network) consists of two Dense layers, which are responsible for feature fusion and generating the final output.

The Convolutional Splitter first divides the input tensor (N, T, D) along the second dimension into three consecutive sub-tensors (N, T₁, D), (N, T₂, D), and (N, T₃, D). Subsequently, A padding mechanism, similar to back padding, ensures uniform timesteps (T) by padding the shorter sub-tensor with replicated feature vectors from its last timestep, thereby satisfying T1 = T2 = T3, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Splitter.

The CDSC_block includes an Interaction module and an Intra-learning module. Figures 3 and 4 demonstrate the detailed workflow.

CDSC_block.

Interaction and Intra-learning.

In the interaction module, a dilated convolution fuses information across the three sub-tensors to expand the receptive field, which makes them more powerful for long-range dependencies learning, while simultaneously reducing the sequence size. Subsequently, we implement a padding mechanism, called odd_to_even. For a subsequence with an odd timesteps (T), its final time step is replicated and appended, while an even subsequence (T is even) is left unchanged. This process is critical for two reasons: it guarantees dimensional compatibility for subsequent summation operations and mitigates information loss before a MaxPooling layer of window size 2, which is prone to discarding the terminal element of an odd sequence. Next, a MaxPooling layer is applied for downsampling, which filters less salient information and reduces sequence dimensionality. The processed tensor is then fed into three DSC layers, each designed to learn distinct features for the corresponding convolution head.

Within the intra-learning module, each sub-tensor is processed by a convolution head respectively, facilitating independent feature extraction. The initial DSC facilitates communication across adjacent timesteps, thereby capturing local temporal dependencies. Then, the processing mirrors the Interaction module, incorporating the odd_to_even padding and a MaxPooling layer. The final DSC layer performs deeper feature extraction on the filtered tensor, yielding richer representations while simultaneously expanding the receptive field.

DSC used above, all employ back padding and hard_swish activation. Back padding, it ensures dimensional consistency between the output and input tensors, while simultaneously preserving local continuity32. But why not causal padding? The reason is that in time series, useful information, close to the prediction target, is usually concentrated at the end of tensors35–37. This observation is further corroborated by Cai et al., who integrate Temporal Attention into LSTM, shifting the model’s attention toward the mid-to-late phases of the tensor. By prioritizing regions where crucial medical information resides, it boosts accuracy by 1–2% in COVID-19 clinical prediction tasks38. Furthermore, given that values covered more frequently by the convolutional kernel exert a greater influence on feature extraction39–41, it is critical to align the kernel’s focus with these information-rich regions. Consequently, back padding allows a kernel to access bottom values as frequently as possible, thereby fully leveraging the information located at the tensor’s end. And there is no need for worrying about information leakage. Because this padding does not utilize any labels or future values. To elaborate: For a mapping f:[X1,X2,…,Xn] → [Y1,Y2,…,Yn], where f(X1) = Y1, the input X1 = (× 1, × 2,…,xt) is padded at its end by replicating its own last value xt to compensate for convolution-induced length reduction. The X1 is transformed into X1’ = (× 1, × 2,…,xt,xt,…,xt). All values in X1’are derived solely from the X1, ensuring no information leakage from its label Y1 or any future values (e.g., X2). Therefore, no look-ahead bias occurs. In addition, hard_swish draws inspiration from Google’s MobileNet series. Following the introduction of Depthwise Separable Convolution (DSC) in MobileNetV133, a significant limitation of the ReLU emerges: it causes information loss by zeroing out negative values. This issue is particularly detrimental when the channel numbers are small, leading to a decline in feature representation capability in MobileNetV242. To address this, hard_swish was proposed in MobileNetV3 as an effective alternative, experimentally demonstrating a superior balance between accuracy and computational efficiency43. Given that the maximum channel count for DSC in our proposed method is only 32 (far fewer than the 576 in MobileNetV3-Small43), we adopt hard_swish to preserve feature representation, consistent with the original recommendations for low-channel configurations.

Notably, after the CDSC_block_2, the outputs, three sub-tensors, are then reshaped into a larger tensor. This rearrangement adheres to the original timesteps order (T), maintaining the consistency between the reconstructed tensor and the input tensor.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the residual module is composed of three operations. The first, Odd_to_even, is functionally identical to its counterpart in the CDSC_block. The second is AveragePooling, selected instead of MaxPooling for its inherent noise-smoothing capabilities, which makes it more suitable for the residual module. The third, split operation, consistent with the previously mentioned convolution split-head strategy, partitions the processed tensor into three sub-tensors. Each sub-tensor is then element-wise added with the features from its corresponding convolution head in the CDSC_block. Furthermore, these residual modules are sequentially stacked, where the output of the AveragePooling in each module serves as the input for the subsequent one.

Residual module.

Drawing inspiration from SCINet, this layer operates without pooling or activation functions26. Specifically, it adapts an input of shape (N, T, D) to an output of shape (N, T*, D). Finally, a Feed-Forward Network (FFN) performs feature fusion to produce predicted tensors. It comprises two Dense layers: the first employs the activation Hard_swish, while the final one is a linear layer without any activation.

Baseline model

To systematically evaluate the performance of the proposed CDSCnet, this study designed a series of comparative experiments. The baseline models and validation strategies are as follows:

We select two types of baselines. The first, literature-reproduced models (e.g., the CNN-LSTM by Xu et al.16). We optimize hyperparameters of the original model to obtain a better version. Then, we conduct comprehensive comparisons to systematically evaluate our proposed CDSCnet against both its reproduced version and the original results reported in the literature.

The second, custom-designed baseline models. We construct a 7-layer CNN-GRU architecture with the following order: CNN → MaxPooling → CNN → MaxPooling → GRU → CNN → Dense, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Then, we respectively substitute the CNN with a DSC, GRU with a LSTM, resulting in 4 variants: DSC-GRU, DSC-LSTM, CNN-GRU, and CNN-LSTM. These models are employed to analyze generation disparities with CDSCnet on cross-regional datasets.

Related variants.

Our validation strategy prioritizes cross-regional generalization. Accordingly, we selected three representative papers in COVID-19 prediction16,22,25 that cover datasets from Europe, Asia, and the Americas. It ensures the comparisons are not limited to a specific region, allowing for a valid assessment of CDSCnet’s generalization. Beyond the baselines from the studies, comparisons with custom-designed baselines provide a comprehensive evaluation.

Complexity analysis

For a given window size T, SCINet reports a worst-case time complexity of O(Tlog T), where the O(logT) term stems from its recursive tree-like convolutional head structure. This structure is simplified to fixed convolutional heads in CDSCnet, which significantly reduces model complexity. Furthermore, CDSCnet maintains a constant number of dilated convolutional layers, unlike TCN, which requires (log2T) layers. Consequently, the complexity of CDSCnet is simple, amounting to only O(T).

Brief summary

Synthesizing experiments on seven cross-regional datasets, CDSCnet exhibits superior prediction. It performs best in 11 tasks covering infections, deaths, and recoveries, as measured by MAE, MAPE, R2 and RMSE. Furthermore, after smoothing high-noise datasets (e.g., Switzerland and Croatia), its performance is particularly pronounced. This validates the strength of CDSCnet in cross-regional forecasting and its adaptability to datasets quality, offering a reliable, high-precision tool for analyzing complex infectious dynamics.

Furthermore, an analysis of parameters, FLOPs, and computational complexity shows that CDSCnet attains this leading performance while maintaining high computational efficiency. This lightweight design not only underscores the practical utility of the model but also validates the soundness of our architectural choices.

Conclusion

In response to the challenges faced by existing epidemic prediction models in handling complex temporal dependency modeling and generalization under limited dataset scenarios, this paper proposes a novel lightweight convolution neural network, called CDSCnet. The core contributions and conclusions of this study are summarized as follows:

First, a lightweight and efficient architecture based on DSC is proposed. This study systematically introduces Depthwise Separable Convolutions (DSC) into epidemic temporal prediction tasks, innovatively combining it with a fixed three-convolution head design. Additionally, the architecture incorporates back padding and a downsampling residual mechanism based on AveragePooling. This design effectively controls the parameters while maintaining robust feature extraction capabilities, thereby mitigating the overfitting typically associated with complex models on small datasets.

Second, the comprehensive performance superiority of the model is validated across cross-regional datasets. Systematic tests, conducted on datasets from seven countries spanning different continents, encompass both smooth and high-noise characteristics. The results demonstrate that CDSCnet outperforms mainstream hybrid models (e.g., CNN-LSTM) in accuracy across 11 prediction tasks, with a maximum reduction in MAE exceeding 50%. This confirms the CDSCnet’s exceptional cross-regional generalization capabilities and its effectiveness in capturing complex epidemic fluctuation patterns.

Third, the critical role of dataset quality in unleashing CDSCnet performance is revealed. Experimental findings indicate that appropriate smoothing preprocessing for high-noise datasets significantly enhance the prediction accuracy of CDSCnet (e.g., reducing RMSE by over 30%). This suggests that CDSCnet is capable of learning more effective features from high-quality datasets, and that its performance ceiling is positively correlated with dataset quality. This provides valuable insights into the synergy between “data preprocessing” and “model optimization” in practical applications.

In a short, the proposed framework, CDSCnet, in this study offers a new technical pathway for epidemic prediction. It can be also extended to other time-series forecasting tasks characterized by similar dataset features, such as mobile network and climate datasets, thereby reinforcing its applicability across diverse scenarios.

Discussion

Although CDSCnet demonstrates impressive performance across the three cases, this study has several limitations:

First, CDSCnet relies solely on a single feature as input, such as daily cases or deaths, without incorporating critical factors that influence pandemic dynamics—e.g., policy interventions, vaccination, and mobility. Moreover, integrating spatial information presents a promising enhancement. Fofanah et al. develop DeepEVD45 and ST-FLAM46, both of which integrate spatial information and outperform baseline methods in Ebola virus disease forecasting. We plan to adopt this approach in future work. Additionally, for single-feature tensors, we leverage the broadcasting mechanism within the residual module to align with the output shape of the CDSC_block, thereby avoiding the parameter overhead introduced by additional structures. However, this broadcasting mechanism fails to accommodate multi-feature inputs, necessitating alternative strategies.

Second, this study employs fixed hyperparameters, such as a kernel size of three and three convolution heads. The kernel size follows standard practice in CNN-based epidemic forecasting. Regarding the number of heads, SCINet employs a recursive two-head structure that splits tensors into even and odd subsequences. However, this tree-like architecture introduces significant parameters as heads double per layer. We simplify the structure and compensate potential accuracy loss by expanding convolution heads to three, striking a balance between lightweight design and performance. Why not four or five heads? More heads mean more parameters. For a lightweight design, we choose three. As to the remaining hyperparameters, we strived to maintain a unified hyperparameter setting across all datasets to highlight the robustness and generalization of CDSCnet. While minor adjustments are unavoidable, the settings remain consistent overall, demonstrating that the model can achieve high performance without extensive dataset-specific tuning. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a more systematic exploration of the number of heads and other hyperparameters remains an important direction for future work.

Third, this study is limited to single-step forecasting and does not consider multi-step prediction. Given that contemporary deep learning research for time series increasingly emphasizes long-term predictive capability, future work will involve designing multi-step experiments to systematically evaluate the long-term forecasting performance.

Beyond the future work outlined above, several promising directions are worth noting, such as replacing MaxPooling with Singular Pooling47 to enhance feature extraction capability and integrating CDSCnet components with other architectures, like the Residual Joint Antenna Network48. For instance, embed the CDSC_block as a feature extractor within the framework, or consider replacing standard CNN with more efficient DSC. A lightweight but efficient structure align well with the goal of the Residual Joint Antenna Network.
