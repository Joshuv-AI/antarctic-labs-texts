Functional Independence Measure assessed early after admission predicts home discharge in older adults admitted to acute general medical wards: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Eiichi Kakehi, Yukako Matsumoto, Kyoichiro Namura, Kae Sugiyama, Yukinobu Akamatsu, Shigehisa Sakurai, Takafumi Nozaki, Keisuke Shoji, Seiji Adachi, Kazuhiko Kotani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine whether Functional Independence Measure (FIM) scores assessed during the early phase of hospitalisation, before the initiation of rehabilitation, independently predict home discharge among older adults admitted to an acute general medical ward.DesignRetrospective observational cohort study.SettingThe General Medicine Department of a municipal general hospital in Japan.ParticipantsOf 1168 community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years admitted from home, the study included 744 who received rehabilitation and underwent early-phase FIM assessment before rehabilitation.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the discharge destination (home vs facility). The predictive value of the total FIM score (FIM-T), motor score (FIM-M) and cognitive score (FIM-C) was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses.ResultsOverall, 494 patients (66.4%) were discharged home, 175 were discharged to facilities, and 75 died in hospital. The admission FIM was measured during the early phase of hospitalisation, with a mean assessment time of 4 days after admission. Early-phase FIM scores were significantly higher in the home discharge group than the facility discharge group (FIM-T: 65.8 vs 44.8; FIM-M: 38.1 vs 23.1; FIM-C: 27.7 vs 21.8; all p<0.001). After adjustment for age, clinical variables and social factors, higher FIM-T, FIM-M and FIM-C scores remained independently associated with home discharge (adjusted OR per 1-point increase for FIM-T: 1.06). ROC analysis showed moderate discriminative ability (areas under the curve of 0.75 for FIM-T and FIM-M, and 0.71 for FIM-C).ConclusionsEarly-phase FIM scores, including both motor and cognitive domains, independently predict home discharge among older adults in acute general medical wards and may support individualised discharge planning in routine practice. These findings may be most generalisable to acute care hospitals where early-phase functional assessments are performed at the initial rehabilitation evaluation, which, in our setting, occurred a mean of 4 days after admission.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The Functional Independence Measure (FIM) is widely used in rehabilitation settings. It is recognised as a useful predictor of home discharge among patients recovering from stroke, heart failure and orthopaedic conditions. Previous studies in acute general medical wards reported associations between home discharge and AlphaFIM (a simplified FIM version) scores at admission, FIM scores assessed during nutrition support team interventions and FIM scores at discharge. However, the predictive value of full FIM scores assessed early during hospitalisation has not been fully established.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrated that full FIM scores, assessed on average 4 days after admission and before the initiation of rehabilitation, can independently predict home discharge in older adults admitted to acute general medical wards. All FIM domains (total, motor and cognitive) remained significant predictors after adjustment for age, clinical factors and social factors. Early-phase FIM scores showed better discrimination than the Barthel Index and demonstrated good calibration. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that predictive performance was consistent across different assessment timings, suggesting minimal immortal time bias. Interaction analyses indicated that the predictive effect of higher FIM scores was weaker among women, among patients with higher care levels, and among those living with family, suggesting that social support may offset the association with lower functional status.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study suggests that a comprehensive FIM assessment in the early phase of hospitalisation and before the initiation of rehabilitation is useful for discharge planning in older adults admitted to acute general medical wards. FIM scores also reflect broader social support needs by capturing both motor and cognitive functions. Incorporating the FIM into routine early assessment may facilitate timely discharge planning. Further prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate its generalisability.

Introduction

Like many high-income countries, Japan faces the pressing challenge of a rapidly ageing population.1 In this context, enabling older adults who have been hospitalised for acute medical conditions to return home safely has become a key priority for both health policy and clinical care. Achieving this goal requires preventing physical and cognitive decline and maintaining the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL). In acute care wards, early rehabilitation alongside medical treatment is expected to improve mobility, prevent functional decline and facilitate discharge to home.2

Several functional assessment tools have been investigated to predict discharge destinations among older adults admitted to acute general medical wards. However, recent systematic reviews have indicated that none can be universally recommended as an optimal indicator.3 4 Commonly used instruments, such as the Katz Index,5 the De Morton Mobility Index6 and the Barthel Index (BI),7 primarily focus on physical function or ADL and include little assessment of cognitive function. However, both physical and cognitive function are significant determinants of home discharge, and their interaction plays a key role in determining whether older adults can safely return home.8 Medical and social factors, including multimorbidity and insufficient social support,8 9 have also been reported as important barriers to achieving home discharge. These considerations highlight the need for a more comprehensive functional assessment tool that integrates physical and cognitive domains and accounts for relevant social factors.

The Functional Independence Measure (FIM) is a standardised tool that is widely used in rehabilitation settings to assess both physical and cognitive function.10 However, reports on the use of the FIM in patients admitted to acute general medical wards remain limited. Previous studies reported that FIM scores assessed at hospital discharge were strong predictors of discharge home.11 However, FIM assessments conducted at discharge may reflect the effects of medical treatment and rehabilitation, thereby limiting their utility for early discharge planning. In contrast, FIM scores assessed at admission reflect patients’ baseline functional status before any intervention, making them potentially useful for early prediction of discharge destination. For example, during the early phase of hospitalisation, the FIM motor domain assessed at the time of nutrition support team intervention has been reported to predict home discharge among malnourished patients.12 The AlphaFIM, a simplified version of the full FIM, has demonstrated predictive value when assessed at admission.13 However, no studies have evaluated the predictive validity of full FIM scores assessed during the early phase of hospitalisation, before the initiation of rehabilitation, in patients on an acute general medical ward. Previous studies have also not adequately considered social support factors; thus, the independent predictive value of early FIM scores remains unclear.

This study aimed to determine whether early-phase FIM scores obtained before the initiation of rehabilitation independently predicted home discharge among older adults admitted to an acute general medical ward, and to evaluate the discriminative ability of these scores. In addition, the FIM is not routinely assessed in extremely frail or highly independent patients; therefore, we examined baseline differences between patients with and without FIM assessments to address potential non-random missingness. We also explored potential interactions between FIM domains and key clinical and social factors that might modify the association between functional status and discharge outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective observational cohort study based on a review of medical records of patients admitted to the General Medicine Department of a municipal general hospital in Japan.

Selection of the study population

This study included community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years or older who were living at home before admission and were hospitalised in the General Medicine Department of a municipal general hospital in Japan between 1 April 2018 and 31 March 2020. Acute medical conditions were diagnosed on admission by one of five board-certified internists, each assigned to a specific weekday. All physicians had more than 10 years of clinical experience and adhered to standard clinical practice guidelines. Eligible patients were those who received rehabilitation alongside acute medical treatment during the early phase of hospitalisation and underwent FIM assessment before the initiation of rehabilitation. Eligibility decisions were made by the attending physician.

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years or older who had been living at home before admission; (2) patients admitted directly from home; (3) patients who required rehabilitation because of functional decline caused by an acute illness and (4) patients who underwent FIM assessment at the time of the initial rehabilitation evaluation. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients transferred to other departments shortly after admission; (2) patients admitted solely for diagnostic work and (3) patients admitted for respite care.

For older patients admitted from home, we performed an a priori sample size calculation. Assuming a small-to-moderate standardised mean difference in admission FIM scores between the home-discharge and facility-discharge groups (effect size Cohen’s d=0.25), a two-sided α level of 0.05 and a β level of 0.20 (power 80%) indicated that 53 patients would be required per group. We conservatively estimated the assumed effect based on the magnitude of between-group differences in FIM scores reported in previous studies.11 12 The power analysis was performed using G*Power V.3.1.7.9 for Windows (Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany).

Data collection and processing

We collected baseline data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), serum albumin levels, reason for admission, comorbidities, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), number of prescribed medications and polypharmacy (n≥5 medications), care level, presence of family members, and BI and FIM scores (total (FIM-T), motor (FIM-M) and cognitive (FIM-C) scores). Patient information was extracted from the electronic medical records of a municipal general hospital in Japan. We defined the reason for admission as the primary diagnosis recorded at admission. Comorbidities primarily included dementia, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic heart failure, chronic respiratory disease and malignant neoplasm. Care levels were assessed using the Japanese Long-Term Care Insurance system, which classifies support and care needs on a scale from 1 (least dependent) to 5 (most dependent).14 This study classified care level as a social factor, reflecting physical and cognitive function, and the availability of long-term care services and community support systems. The BI was assessed at admission for all patients aged 65 years or older. These variables were considered potential confounders affecting home discharge, as suggested in previous reports.712 1517

Instrument

The FIM was used to assess the patients’ functional status at admission. It contains 18 items divided into motor (self-care, sphincter control, mobility and locomotion) and cognitive (communication and social cognition) domains. Each item is scored on a 7-point scale that reflects the level of assistance required.10 Assessments were performed by trained rehabilitation professionals (physical, occupational or speech-language therapists) who had received standardised instruction in FIM evaluation.

Outcome

We classified discharge destination into three categories: home discharge, facility discharge and in-hospital death. Discharge to a facility included transfers to chronic care hospitals or nursing facilities. The primary outcome was discharge destination, defined as home discharge vs facility discharge, with in-hospital death analysed separately. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, time to initiation of rehabilitation and duration of rehabilitation.

Statistical analysis

We presented categorical variables as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as mean±SD for parametric data or medians with IQRs for non-parametric data. We compared categorical variables using the χ2 test. For continuous variables, we used the Student’s t-test for parametric comparisons between two groups, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric comparisons. For comparisons across three groups, we used a one-way analysis of variance for parametric data and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-parametric data.

Patients with extremely low ability to perform ADLs, severe clinical conditions or who were sufficiently healthy and did not require rehabilitation did not undergo FIM assessment, raising the possibility of non-random missingness. We therefore divided patients into groups based on whether the FIM was assessed and compared their baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes to evaluate the potential impact of this missingness.

To assess the predictive ability of the FIM for home discharge, we performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses and calculated ORs with 95% CIs. To construct the multivariable models, we prespecified an adjustment set based on existing evidence and clinical relevance. To evaluate the incremental value of the FIM in predicting home discharge, we constructed and compared four hierarchical logistic regression models: (1) a minimal model including only the FIM; (2) an age-adjusted model including the minimal model plus age; (3) a clinical model including the minimal model plus age, sex, BMI, serum albumin, reason for admission, comorbidities, the CCI and the number of prescribed medications and (4) an extended model including the clinical model plus key social factors such as the care level and living with family members before admission.

We evaluated the discriminative ability of the FIM to predict home discharge using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% CIs.18 We also evaluated the BI using the same approach for comparison. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, along with corresponding sensitivity and specificity.19 We also calculated positive predictive values (PPVs) and negative predictive values (NPVs) to assess the predictive accuracy of the FIM.20

We assessed model calibration using the Brier score, calibration slope and calibration plots. We calculated the Brier score from the mean squared error between predicted and observed home discharge. The calibration slope was estimated by logistic regression, with the logit of the predicted probability as the sole predictor. We also visually assessed calibration by plotting observed vs predicted home discharge across deciles of predicted risk.

To examine whether the timing of FIM assessment influenced its predictive accuracy, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratified by the timing of FIM measurement (≤ 1 day, 2–3 days and ≥4 days after admission). This enabled us to evaluate potential sources of bias arising from differences in assessment timing, including immortal time bias and delayed evaluation.

To assess potential interactions between FIM-T and clinical and social factors, as well as between the FIM-M and FIM-C domains, we included interaction terms in the multivariable logistic regression model, along with the corresponding main effects and covariates. We interpreted statistically significant interaction terms as evidence of effect modification.

A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses used SPSS V.28 (IBM). We excluded participants with missing baseline data from the analyses.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Results

Participants

In total, 2330 patients were admitted to the General Medicine Department during the study period, of whom 1999 were aged ≥65 years. Among these older adults, 1326 had been living at home before admission and were considered eligible for inclusion, whereas those residing in nursing homes (n=473), long-term care facilities (n=123) and those hospitalised in other hospitals (n=77) were excluded from the analysis. We excluded anyone transferred to other departments (n=140), hospitalised for respite purposes (n=10) and admitted for diagnostic work (n=3). A total of 1168 older adults admitted from home were included in the expanded dataset. We also excluded 424 patients who did not undergo FIM assessment. This left 744 patients for analysis (see online supplemental figure 1). All participants underwent baseline assessments of BMI and serum albumin levels, and complete data were available for both parameters. Complete follow-up was achieved until discharge in all cases, as all patients were hospitalised.

Baseline characteristics of participants with and without FIM assessment

Baseline characteristics are summarised in online supplemental table 1. Patients who underwent FIM assessment were older and had lower serum albumin levels than those without FIM data. Ischaemic stroke and acute heart failure were more common reasons for admission in the assessment group. Comorbidities, including dementia, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease and chronic heart failure, were more prevalent in this group, and the CCI was higher. Polypharmacy was also more common. A greater proportion of these patients were assigned to higher care levels under the Long-Term Care Insurance system. BI scores were lower, indicating poorer baseline functional status. These patients had longer hospital stays; however, in-hospital mortality was lower than in those without FIM data (10.1% vs 14.6%, p<0.001).

Among patients without FIM data, those not discharged home were older and had lower BMI and serum albumin levels than those discharged home. They also had a higher prevalence of dementia, chronic heart failure and malignant neoplasm, as well as higher CCI scores. Higher care levels and lower BI scores were more common in this subgroup, and hospital stays were longer. Within the non-home discharge subgroup, patients who died were older and had lower BMI and albumin levels than those discharged to facilities. They also exhibited a greater comorbidity burden, including higher CCI scores and more frequent malignant neoplasms, along with poorer functional status.

Characteristics of the cohort and outcomes

Demographic, clinical and functional characteristics were compared in the FIM assessment group among those discharged home, to a facility or who died in the hospital (table 1). Overall, 494 of the 744 (66.4%) were discharged home, 175 (23.5%) to facilities and 75 (10.1%) died in the hospital. Compared with those discharged home, those discharged to a facility and those who died in hospital were significantly older (p=0.008). Pneumonia was the most frequent reason for admission among those who died in hospital (p<0.001). In contrast, both ischaemic stroke and dehydration were more common in those discharged to facilities (p=0.002 and p=0.044), and urinary tract infections (UTIs) were most common in those discharged home (p<0.001). The ‘other’ category comprised various conditions with fewer than 30 cases each, which were grouped to maintain adequate statistical power. Those who died in hospital had higher rates of dementia, chronic kidney disease and malignant neoplasm (p<0.001, p=0.035 and p=0.021), and higher CCI scores (p=0.002). Long-term care levels were significantly higher among those who died in hospital (p=0.019), and patients discharged to facilities were significantly less likely to have been living with family members (p=0.028).

Predictive value of admission FIM and BI scores for home discharge

Table 2 shows the associations between FIM and BI scores at admission and at discharge, by destination (home vs a facility). In the crude analysis, higher FIM-T scores were significantly associated with increased odds of home discharge (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.05, p<0.001). This association remained consistent after adjustment for age (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.06, p<0.001) (online supplemental table 2), the clinical model (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.06, p<0.001), and the extended model including social factors (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.07, p<0.001). Similar independent associations were observed for the FIM-M and FIM-C scores, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.06 to 1.10 (all p<0.001). The BI score also maintained a stable predictive association across all models, with adjusted ORs of 1.02 to 1.04 (all p<0.001).

Predictive ability of FIM and BI scores for home discharge: ROC curve analysis

Figure 1 shows the ROC curves for the predictive performance of early-phase FIM scores (FIM-T, FIM-M and FIM-C) and the BI score. Table 3 summarises the cut-off values and diagnostic indices for each measure. The early-phase FIM-T score showed an AUC of 0.75 and a cut-off value of 54, with a sensitivity of 69.0%, specificity of 73.7%, PPV of 69.0%, NPV of 73.7%, and accuracy of 70.3%. The early-phase FIM-M and FIM-C scores also demonstrated predictive ability, with AUC values of 0.75 and 0.71, respectively. The BI score had an AUC of 0.69 and a cut-off value of 15, with a sensitivity of 67.0%, specificity of 64.0%, PPV of 67.0%, NPV of 64.0%, and accuracy of 66.2%. Overall, the early-phase FIM-T and FIM-M scores demonstrated greater discriminative ability than the BI score, as reflected by higher AUCs and accuracy.

Calibration performance

For FIM-T, the Brier score improved across model tiers (0.165 for the minimal and age-adjusted models, 0.150 for the clinical model, and 0.144 for the extended model). The calibration slope was 1.038 for all models, indicating excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Calibration plots showed that observed event rates closely followed the 45-degree reference line across deciles of predicted risk, demonstrating good calibration throughout the risk spectrum.

Sensitivity analysis of the home discharge discriminative ability by timing of FIM assessment

To evaluate the potential influence of immortal time bias and delayed functional assessment, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratifying patients by the timing of their FIM assessment at admission (≤1 day, 2–3 days, or ≥4 days after admission). Table 4 shows that the discriminative performance of the FIM varied across measurement times: AUC values were relatively low when assessment was within the first day of admission, highest on days 2 to 3, and moderately acceptable on or after day 4. For FIM-T, the AUC values were 0.67, 0.78 and 0.74. A similar pattern was observed for FIM-M, with AUC values of 0.67, 0.78 and 0.73, and for FIM-C, with AUC values of 0.67, 0.71 and 0.72.

Interaction analyses

The associations between FIM-T and home discharge differed across several subgroups. Significant interactions were observed for sex (OR 0.972, p=0.010), long-term care level (OR 0.989, p=0.003) and living with family members (OR 0.963, p=0.023). No significant interactions were identified between FIM-T and FIM-M, FIM-T and FIM-C, or FIM-M and FIM-C, indicating that the motor and cognitive domains did not exert synergistic or antagonistic effects on home discharge.

Discussion

This study showed that the FIM-T score assessed during the early phase of hospitalisation, at an average of 4 days after admission and before the initiation of rehabilitation, independently predicted home discharge among older adults admitted to an acute general medical ward. This association remained significant even after comprehensive adjustment for clinical and social factors. Similar independent associations were also observed for the FIM-M and FIM-C domains. ROC curve analysis showed that all FIM domains had greater discriminative ability for predicting home discharge than the BI. Predictive performance varied slightly by assessment timing, but overall discrimination remained acceptable, with FIM assessments on days 2–3 offering particularly strong prognostic value. These findings suggest that early functional assessment with the FIM may be valuable for supporting discharge planning in acute care settings for older adults.

Various assessment tools have been proposed to predict discharge destinations among older adults admitted to acute general medical wards. However, no single indicator has been established as universally optimal.3 4 In this population, physical decline and cognitive impairment often coexist, reducing independence in performing ADL and influencing the ability to return home.8 Discharge decisions are also shaped by social factors, including social isolation, access to long-term care resources and family support.8 9 Comprehensive community care planning has been shown to facilitate home discharge,21 underscoring the need for multidimensional assessment. In this study, FIM-T remained an independent predictor of home discharge even after adjustment for clinical and social factors, with similar findings for FIM-M and FIM-C. ROC analysis showed that FIM-T and FIM-M had superior discriminative ability compared with the BI. The cut-off values identified in the ROC analysis may be clinically useful thresholds: patients with early-phase FIM-T scores below 54, FIM-M scores below 23 or FIM-C scores below 27 may be at higher risk of non-home discharge and could benefit from earlier and more intensive discharge planning. The FIM evaluates both physical and cognitive domains and therefore provides a more comprehensive assessment of the functional capacity required for independent living after acute hospitalisation. Although the FIM demonstrated greater discriminative ability than the BI in this study, the difference in AUC was modest. In clinical practice, the FIM requires assessment of 18 items and standardised training for evaluators, which may increase the assessment burden in busy acute care settings. Therefore, the choice of assessment tool should consider not only predictive performance but also feasibility in routine clinical workflows.

In the sensitivity analysis stratified by FIM assessment timing, the predictive ability of the FIM remained generally stable throughout the early phase of hospitalisation. If there was substantial immortal time bias, we would have expected a much higher AUC in the group assessed on the first day. However, it was slightly higher in the group assessed on or after day 4 than in those assessed within 1 day, probably reflecting clinical instability during the acute phase and suggesting that immortal time bias had only a minimal impact. The modest increase in AUC observed among patients assessed on days 2 to 3 may reflect improving clinical stability during this period. Overall, the FIM demonstrated moderate-to-good discriminative ability across all timing groups, indicating that early-phase FIM assessments are not sensitive to timing differences and are useful predictors of discharge planning.

To clarify how functional status influenced discharge outcomes across patient subgroups, we examined interactions between FIM-T and key clinical, social and functional variables. All significant interactions had ORs below 1, indicating that the effect of higher FIM-T scores on home discharge was weaker among women, patients with higher long-term care levels and those living with family members. In these groups, home discharge was achievable even at relatively lower FIM-T levels. FIM-T more strongly determined outcomes among men, those with lower care-need levels and individuals living alone. This finding does not contradict the main effect that living with family favoured home discharge, but instead suggests that family support can compensate for lower functional status. The interaction, therefore, reflects differences in the strength of association rather than a reversal of direction across subgroups. No significant interactions were found among the FIM-T, FIM-M and FIM-C domains, indicating that motor and cognitive function contributed independently. FIM-T can therefore be considered a sufficiently integrated and informative measure for predicting home discharge.

Overall, 10.1% (n=75) of patients who underwent FIM assessment died in hospital. These patients had markedly lower FIM scores at admission, probably reflecting advanced frailty, reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to acute illness. These factors may have directly contributed to mortality. Previous studies have also reported an association between lower FIM scores and an increased risk of death.22 Our findings were consistent with these reports, suggesting that lower early-phase FIM scores may indicate a higher risk of dying in hospital. The FIM was not originally developed to predict mortality, but our findings also suggest that independent functional status assessed in the early stages of hospitalisation may be a useful indirect indicator of poor prognosis.

Several studies have demonstrated the usefulness of the FIM in predicting discharge destination among older adults in acute general medical wards. D’Souza et al reported an association between home discharge and AlphaFIM scores, a simplified version containing six motor and communication items, which were assessed at a median of 2 days after admission. This was particularly true when combined with walking independence.13 However, the AlphaFIM focuses mainly on motor function, provides a limited assessment of cognitive and social domains and has restricted validation and clinical use.23 Other studies using the full FIM evaluated functional status at later stages of hospitalisation. Tachi et al found that higher discharge FIM subscores for self-care, sphincter control, transfers and locomotion predicted home discharge.11 Anada et al reported that FIM-M scores at the time of intervention from nutrition support teams predicted home discharge, whereas FIM-C scores did not.12 In contrast, our study assessed the full FIM at a much earlier time point, with evaluations conducted on average 4 days after admission and before meaningful recovery, across a broad population with diverse acute illnesses. All FIM domains (FIM-T, FIM-M and FIM-C) were independent predictors of home discharge even after adjusting for clinical and social factors. The identification of FIM-C as an independent predictor distinguishes our findings from previous reports and highlights the importance of cognitive function in determining discharge readiness.

The primary diagnosis at admission plays a crucial role in determining the likelihood of home discharge among older adults hospitalised for acute medical conditions. In this study, UTIs were significantly associated with home discharge. The generally favourable prognosis for UTIs may explain this finding, together with their rapid response to antibiotic therapy and relatively limited impact on both physical and cognitive function.24 In contrast, the presence of multiple comorbidities was a significant barrier to home discharge, which is consistent with previous reports linking multimorbidity to functional decline and reduced independence.25 26 These results highlight the utility of FIM scores in capturing the functional consequences of both favourable conditions (eg, UTI as primary diagnosis) and complex clinical presentations involving multimorbidity.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-centre study, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Second, the exclusion of patients without FIM data may have introduced selection bias. Patients who did not undergo FIM assessment likely included both those with relatively preserved functional status and those who were too severely ill to undergo early evaluation. Because the decision to perform FIM assessment was based on the attending physicians’ clinical judgement, the missingness of FIM scores was likely non-random. Consequently, excluding patients with extremely poor or very good functional status may have influenced the observed predictive accuracy of the model, potentially resulting in either overestimation or underestimation of its discriminative performance. Third, although the FIM was intended to reflect functional status at admission, assessments were conducted a mean of 4 days after admission and may therefore have been influenced by early clinical changes. In acute medical settings, functional status can change during the first 48–72 hours after admission as the acute illness stabilises. Therefore, early-phase FIM assessments may reflect functional status after initial treatment rather than a strict baseline at admission. Fourth, evaluations were conducted by trained rehabilitation professionals; however, interrater variation could not be fully eliminated. Fifth, disease severity and the activity of chronic conditions were not stratified beyond the primary diagnosis, which may limit the applicability of the results. Finally, discharge destination in older adults is influenced by healthcare systems, long-term care insurance structures and cultural expectations regarding family caregiving. In Japan, the availability of the Long-Term Care Insurance system and family support may shape the role of social factors included in our model, such as care level and living with family members, in discharge planning. Therefore, the findings may not be fully generalisable to healthcare systems with different institutional and social contexts.

Conclusions

This study showed that FIM-T scores assessed during the early phase of hospitalisation, before the initiation of rehabilitation, are independent predictors of home discharge in older adults admitted from home to an acute general medical ward. In addition to the FIM-T score, both the FIM-M and FIM-C domains had predictive value, underscoring the importance of early assessment of multiple functional domains during hospitalisation. As a comprehensive assessment of physical and cognitive function early in hospitalisation, the FIM may provide important information for predicting postdischarge living arrangements and facilitating timely discharge planning and care transition decisions. Further prospective multicentre studies are warranted to validate these findings and to establish whether the FIM is a practical decision-support tool in acute care settings.
