Study on pistachio shell filled PLA composites for FDM-based processing
Authors: S Rashia Begum, B Aditya Devan, S Kavya, H Pranav, R Rakul, M Vasumathi, P Senthamaraikannan, Adeolu A Adediran, M Saravana Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this research, Polylactic Acid (PLA)/Pistachio shell particles (PSP) composite filaments were developed using a single screw extruder for Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) process. Alkali and silane treatments were applied to PSP to enhance bonding between the filler particles and the PLA matrix. FTIR analysis indicated the removal of lignin and hemicellulose from the PSP surface and formation of siloxane bonds due to the chemical treatments. XRD analysis indicated reduced crystalline organization due to treated PSP incorporation. Despite slight decreases in tensile, compressive, and flexural strengths, PSP addition improved tensile modulus and matrix stiffness. When comparing 2%, 4%, and 6% PSP additions, the 4-wt% composite demonstrated the highest tensile strength (49.81 MPa), which can be attributed to the improved dispersion of PSP particles as confirmed by SEM images. In contrast, the 6-wt% composite exhibited the maximum compressive strength (43.06 MPa) and flexural strength (58.06 MPa), likely due to the higher filler loading providing greater resistance to deformation. Hardness and impact resistance also showed steady improvement with increasing PSP content. However, the inclusion of treated PSP reduced the wettability of the PLA matrix. Interestingly, the 4-wt% composite recorded the highest Melt Flow Index (12.80 g/10 min), suggesting enhanced processability. Taken together, the 4-wt% composite with uniformly dispersed PSP offered the best balance of mechanical and thermal properties.Keywords: PLA, Pistachio, Filament, Contact angle, 3D printingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) has revolutionized manufacturing over the past decade by expanding material capabilities, enabling customization at scale, integrating with Industry 4.0 principles, and enhancing sustainability through reduced waste and innovative design possibilities. It has outshined many traditional subtractive manufacturing methods by virtue of its capacity to fabricate intricate shapes and objects without the need for specialized tools or moulds, rendering it ideal for prototyping and custom production1. Moreover, AM’s distinguishing feature is its prudent use of raw materials, which promotes a paradigm change towards low or zero-waste manufacturing2, an important factor in today’s industrial environments.

In the present scenario, FDM is the most popularly known and widely used AM technology. The FDM filaments are commonly made of thermoplastic materials such as Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene (ABS), Polylactic Acid (PLA), Polycarbonate (PC) and nylon3. Among these materials, PLA is popular for its environment friendly nature. Unlike other petroleum-based polymers, it is non-toxic, safe and biodegradable. PLA finds wide usage in packaging, disposable products, biomedical, pharmaceutical, and textile industries4. The investigation of biodegradable materials is a developing field in AM that is driving the utilization and assessment of eco-friendly materials like wood5, starch6, etc., as additives in 3D printing filaments. Previous research has utilized bamboo fibre7, peanut shell8, banana fibre9, palm and coir10, jute fibre11, kenaf fiber12, areca nut13, coffee husk14, etc., as fillers in polymer matrix composites for reducing cost and improving properties15–17. However, they are largely overlooked in the polymer composite industries. The potential economic and environmental benefits of their inclusion are undermined by this neglect. Several reports describe mechanical and morphological characterization of plant-based fiber polypropylene composite. Maurya et al. developed polypropylene-based hybrid composites reinforced with sisal fiber and cenosphere particles, showing improved dynamic mechanical properties, enhanced storage modulus (up to 13.35 GPa), and better crystallinity, with CTAB treatment enhancing interfacial bonding and rheological processability18. Similarly, Kumar et al. fabricated Himalayan agave fiber-reinforced PP composites hybridized with GO-coated fly ash, achieving a peak tensile strength of 40.47 MPa and flexural strength of 53.52 MPa, with GO treatment improving dispersion, interfacial adhesion, and thermal performance19, while in another study, they utilized pineapple crown fiber in LDPE/EPR composites and observed a significant increase in mechanical performance, reaching a tensile strength of 29.25 MPa and flexural modulus of 1814 MPa, highlighting strong matrix–fiber compatibility and promising potential for upcycling agricultural waste20.

Pistachio shells, which are frequently thrown away as waste after the nuts are collected, contain a wealth of unrealized potential. They are produced in large quantities, especially in United States of America, Iran and Turkey21. These shells are abundant in lignocellulosic components like cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. They find application in personal care products, composite materials, paints, fillers, building construction materials, and waste water treatment22. In this work, PLA/Pistachio shell composites are fabricated for additive manufacturing application. However, the hydrophilic nature of PSP makes them incompatible with the hydrophobic PLA polymer. Generally, alkali (mercerization) treatment, silane treatment, acetylation, peroxide treatment, permanganate treatment, benzoylation treatment, iIsocyanate treatment, malleated coupling agent treatment and enzymatic treatment have identified as good chemical treatments for increasing the interfacial compatibility between the lignocellulosic particles and the polymer matrix23,24. In this work, alkali and silane treatments were carried out on the PSP to enhance the compatibility with the PLA matrix.

Various research studies have explored the use of biofillers as reinforcing agents in polymer matrices. For instance, Laura Aliotta et al. developed sustainable 3D printed poly(lactic acid) (PLA)/Hazelnut shell powder bio-composites for design applications. Three different Hazelnut Shell Powder content levels (ranging from 10% to 30% by weight) and the effects of three different raster angles (0°, ± 45°, and 90°) on the mechanical properties were investigated. Design of experiments was carried out, and the best configuration had a nozzle temperature of 220 °C, a printing speed of 25 mm/s, and a raster angle of ± 45°. With this configuration, prototypes of ornamental pots, jewelry, and home furniture were produced as demonstrators25. M.U. Obidiegwu et al. studied the Effect of Walnut Shell Powder on the Properties of Polypropylene Filled Composites. It was observed that the density, water absorption, and flame propagation rate showed a gradual increment, while tensile properties did not show remarkable improvement. This composite is useful for lightweight applications since the density is slightly improved26. Pravin P. Patil et al. experimentally investigated the Pistachio shell concentrations on the Mechanical performance of hemp fiber Reinforced Polyester Composites. The inclusion of pistachio shells changed the overall surface strength of polyamide nanocomposites, as observed from tension, bending, and impact tests27. Daramola et al. studied the Mechanical and wear behavior of polylactic acid matrix composites reinforced with crab-shell synthesized chitosan microparticles. In this study, chitosan particles were extracted from crab shells by enzymatic hydrolysis extraction technique, and composites of polylactic acid with 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% chitosan particles were fabricated using compression molding. The optimum values were found at 6% chitosan particle reinforcement for mechanical properties and 10% for wear resistance28. Essabir et al. developed bio-composites based on polypropylene reinforced with almond shell particles. The results showed a clear improvement in mechanical and rheological properties from the use of almond shell particles in the matrix, both with and without maleic anhydride compatibilizer, corresponding to a gain in Young’s modulus of 56.2% and 35%, respectively, at 30% particle loading. Thermal analysis revealed that incorporation of particle in the composites resulted in an increase in the initial thermal decomposition temperature29.

Although numerous studies have focused on developing polymer matrix composites (PMC) with bio-fillers using conventional methods like injection molding and compression molding, research on bio-composites developed by additive manufacturing (AM) technology remains limited. Additionally, there is limited research on understanding the effect of filler particle size and dispersion in the matrix on the processability of filaments using FDM technology. In this study, PLA/Pistachio shell composites were fabricated using a single-screw filament extruder. The Pistachio shell particles (PSP) were subjected to alkali and silane treatment to enhance their interfacial adhesion with the PLA matrix30. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis was conducted to understand the effect of these chemical treatments on the PSP. The crystal structure of the PLA/PSP composite was explored using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Melt Flow Index (MFI) of the composites was tested to understand their rheological behavior. Contact angle measurements of the 3D printed samples were taken to study the effect of PSP addition on the wettability of the composites with water. Mechanical properties of the composites were evaluated using tensile, compression, flexural, impact, and hardness tests. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the tensile fracture surface were used to study the morphology of the composites and the dispersion of the PSP in the PLA matrix. Thermal behavior of the composites was studied using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

3D printing grade REVODE 110 PLA granules procured from Banka Bioloo Limited, Hyderabad has been used in this study. It has a density of 1.20–1.30 g/cm3, melt flow rate 6–12 g/10 min (at 190 °C/2.16 Kg), melting point 155–170 °C and a glass transition temperature of 60 °C. The pistachio shells were procured from a local nutshell dealing industry located in Maharashtra. The 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane extra pure 98% also called KH550 coupling agent (C9H23NO3Si, M. W = 221.37 g/mol), sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH, M. W = 40.00 g/mol), and acetic acid glacial extra pure AR 99.9% (C2H4O2, M. W = 60.05 g/mol) required of carrying out the alkali and silane treatments were procured from Scientific Chemicals, a supplier in Broadway, Chennai, India.

Preparation of pistachio shell particles

From the raw pistachio shells procured, the qualitatively inferior shells were discarded after thorough visual inspection. The shells were then washed with distilled water multiple times to remove the dust and other impurities. The washing was continued till the water became clear and the shells were then dried in sunlight for a day. This was followed by drying in the hot air oven at 85 °C for 24 h to reduce the moisture. The dried shells were first crushed by hammering on a steel plate and then powdered using a jaw crusher. The powder from the jaw crusher was initially sieved using BSS 200 mesh. Finally, the powder was again sieved using a BSS 300 mesh to obtain particles less than 53 μm in size. Surface modification of pistachio shell particles using chemical treatment

Pistachio shell is mainly composed of cellulose (30–55 wt%), hemicellulose (20–32-wt%), and lignin (12–38 wt%), apart from ash, wax, and other impurities31. Being a lignocellulosic material, it has a natural hydrophilic nature as it contains strongly polarized hydroxyl groups32,33. The addition of hydrophilic PSP to the hydrophobic PLA polymer is naturally incompatible. This reduces the particle-matrix stress transfer and lowers the mechanical strength of the composite. It also increases the water absorption rate and dimensional instability of the composite34. Hence, suitable surface modifications and coupling agents should be used to enhance the interfacial adhesion between the PLA matrix and the lignocellulosic particles. Figure 2 shows the chemical treatment for Pistachio Shell Particles to enhanced the interfacial adhesion. The major chemical treatments are alkali, silane, acetylation, benzoylation, acrylation, maleated coupling agents, and isocyanates35,36. In the present work, alkali and silane treatments have been used as shown in Figure. For alkali treatment, the prepared PSP are soaked in a 5-wt% aqueous NaOH solution for 5 h at room temperature. PSP is added to the solution in such a way that the ratio of the PSP to the alkali solution is 1:20 (wt/wt). After treatment, the shell particles are thoroughly washed with deionized water until the pH becomes 7 (neutral). They are then dried in hot air oven at 85 °C for 8 h. Due to the alkali treatment, hemicellulose, lignin, wax, and other impurities are removed to a large extent. The removal of these components make the surface of the particles rougher, increasing the interfacial adhesion between the particles and the matrix and thereby improving the mechanical properties37. The diminishment of O–H groups due to alkali treatment weakens the hydrophilicity of the lignocellulosic PSP38. This was followed by the silane treatment. 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (KH550) coupling agent is used. Prior to silane treatment, the pH of deionized water is adjusted to 3–4 by adding acetic acid. 4-wt% KH550 coupling agent is dissolved in this solution and stirred continuously for 30 min to hydrolyze the silane completely. The alkali-treated PSP are then soaked in the prepared silane solution and continuously stirred for 24 h at room temperature. The particles settled at the bottom are collected by filtering them out using Whatman Grade 41 filter paper. After treatment, the shell particles are washed several times to remove the silane residue with distilled water until the pH becomes 7 (neutral). The chemically treated particles are then dried in the hot air oven for 8 h at 85 °C to remove the moisture and then powdered and sieved again to obtain the chemically treated PSP that are used to reinforce the PLA matrix and form the composite. The reactions happening during the chemical treatments are as shown in Fig. 3. The silane treatment increases the interfacial adhesion due to the formation of covalent bonds between the amine groups of KH550 and the terminal carboxylic groups of PLA39,40, which act as a bridge between the reinforcement and the matrix as shown in Fig. 3(iv).

Extrusion of the composite filament

The PLA granules were initially dried at 50 °C for 4 h to remove moisture. Chemically treated PSP were then mixed with PLA granules in 2%, 4%, and 6% weight ratios of PSP/PLA and ball milling of the mixture was done in a planetary ball milling machine at 200 rpm for 30 min for proper coating. These coated PLA granules were fed into the single-screw filament extruder that is used to extrude out the composite filaments. Neat PLA filaments were extruded first, followed by 2-wt%, 4-wt%, and 6-wt% composite filaments. Extrusion temperature was maintained at 165 °C, screw speed at 20–30 rpm, producing 1.75 mm diameter filaments. Figure 4(a) displays the various composite filaments fabricated, which were flexible and exhibited almost uniform diameter along their length.

(a) Fabricated PLA/PSP filaments using single screw extruder, (b) Fabricated ASTM standard specimens using FDM, Tensile Test specimens ASTM D638, Flexural Test specimens ASTM D790, Compression Test specimens ASTM D695, Charpy Impact Test specimens ASTM D6110.

Fabrication of ASTM standard specimens using FDM technology

The ASTM standard specimens used for various mechanical testing were fabricated using FDM method. The composite filaments were printed using EMRAK technologies’ ULTRON FDM machine. Initially, 0.6 mm nozzles were employed; however, nozzle clogging occurred frequently, making continuous printing challenging. Eventually, a 1.0 mm nozzle was utilized which ensured uninterrupted high-quality printing. The printing parameters were 50 mm/s printing speed, 0.05 mm layer thickness, 100% infill density, and a 200 °C extrusion temperature. The filling mode employed a rectilinear pattern in the outskirts of the design and a 45°-grid pattern in the center. Figure 4(b) shows the standard ASTM specimens that were printed using the FDM technology to carry out different mechanical testing.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis was done to study the impact of chemical treatments on PSP and to identify the functional groups present. The FTIR analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 FT-IR Spectrometer, in the spectral range 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Morphological analysis of the fracture surface of the composites were done using scanning electron microscopy. SEM images of the tensile test fracture surface of the composites were analysed to study the mode of failure and the particle dispersion in various composites. The SEM images were taken in the TESCAN VEGA3 XMU Scanning Electron Microscope. Prior to SEM analysis, samples were gold-coated using a sputter coater.

Water contact angle

Water contact angle, indicative of surface wettability, was measured using a Kyowa contact angle meter via the sessile drop method. A drop of distilled water was placed on the 3D printed composite surface, and Kyowa Interface Science’s FAMAS software facilitated accurate contact angle measurement through advanced image processing.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

TGA was done to analyse the thermal stability of the composites at elevated temperatures and DSC was used to determine the various thermal transition temperatures. The analysis was conducted using a NETZSCH STA 499 F3 Jupiter thermal analyzer42. Samples (Neat PLA, 2-wt%, 4-wt%, and 6-wt% composites) weighing about 30 mg were heated at a rate of 10 °C/min from 30 °C to 600 °C in an N2 environment (60 mL/min). The same heating cycle was applied for both TGA and DSC analyses.

The various transition temperatures like Glass transition temperature (Tg), Cold crystallization temperature (Tc) and the melting temperature (Tm) of the neat PLA and the composites were found from the DSC thermograms. Melt flow index (MFI)

MFI indicates the rheological behavior or the ease of extruding the molten polymer through the nozzle. The testing was carried out according to ASTM D1238 standard using a load of 2.16 kg and extrusion temperature of 170 °C in the Melt Flow Index tester.

Results and discussion

FTIR analysis of untreated and chemical treated pistachio shell particles

FTIR plots of untreated and silane treated PSP are as shown in Figure 5. It shows the effect of the alkali and silane treatment in the form of valley shifting and absence of valleys corresponding to certain functional groups. The valley at 2883.82 cm−1 and 2881.74 cm−1 presents in untreated and silane treated PSP spectra respectively are due to the C-H stretching of the cellulose and hemicellulose44. However, the slight shift in the valley and its broadening in the silane treated PSP plots indicates that there has been modification in the chemical structure due to the chemical treatment. The valley at 1729.90 cm−1 in the untreated PSP plot is due to the C=O stretching vibration of the carboxylic group of hemicellulose and lignin32. The valleys at 1371.34 cm−1and 1239.87 cm−1 are due to stretching of the C-O bonds of acetyl group of hemicellulose and lignin43. The absence of these valleys in the silane treated PSP plots indicate that the alkali treatment has removed the unwanted hemicellulose and lignin largely from the PSP leading to rougher surface of the filler particles. The broad valley between 3500 cm1 and 3200 cm−1 observed in both the untreated and chemical treated plots are due to the stretching of hydrogen bonded O-H groups45. However, it is seen that in the silane treated PSP plot this valley becomes wider and its intensity is decreased. This indicates that the number of O-H groups decreased due to alkali treatment and reduced the hydrogen bonding38. The appearance of the valley at 713.47 cm−1 in the silane treated PSP spectra represents the -Si-O-Si- symmetric stretching45. This indicates the auto polymerization among the silanol groups. The condensation reaction as shown in Figure. 3(iii) along with this auto polymerization between the silanol groups leads to better interfacial adhesion between the filler particle and the PLA matrix.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The X-ray diffraction results of the chemical treated PSP, neat PLA and 6-wt% composite is as Fig. 6. The peaks observed at 2θ = 16.56° and 19° in XRD pattern of neat PLA indicates the (110) and (203) lattice planes respectively and the broad peaks at 2θ = 37.91°, 64.25° and 77.31° along with the peak at 2θ = 44.07°indicates the semi crystalline structure of PLA. The calculated crystallinity index of neat PLA is 56.94%. The peak observed in treated PSP pattern at 2θ = 15.64, 17.71° and 22.39° with the corresponding lattice planes (1–10), (110) and (200) indicates cellulose I crystallites45. The broader peak in the 6-wt% composite XRD pattern at 2θ values from 10° to 25° is associated with the semi crystalline nature of the PLA/Pistachio shell composite46. However, the appearance of the broad peak suggests more amorphous phase or a less organized crystalline structure. This can be attributed to the strong interfacial interaction between the silane treated PSP and PLA matrix that hinders the mobility of polymer chains near the interface, leading to reduced crystallinity in that region.

Water contact angle

The water contact angle measurement was done to understand the wettability of the 3D printed surface made out of neat PLA and the PLA/PSP composite. The images from the contact angle meter illustrating the liquid-composite surface interface and the contact angle are as shown in Fig. 7 (a-d). From these images, it is found that the PLA/PSP composite surface is wettable and hydrophilic in nature. The variation in the wettability of the surface due to the addition of the silane treated PSP in the PLA matrix is evident from the increasing trend of water contact angle as shown in Fig. 7(e). The water contact angle increased from 52.8° in neat PLA to 53.5° and 57.8° in the 2-wt% and 4-wt% composites respectively. This indicates that the surface becomes less wettable due to the addition of the filler particles. This is because the siloxane bonds formed due to silane treatment being hydrophobic in nature increases the hydrophobic tendency. The silane-grafting act as barrier for interaction of water molecules with the surface of the composites44. The work of Nawadon Petchwattana et al.34, indicates that the water absorption of the composites made out of wood flour particles as filler in PLA matrix is reduced significantly because of silane treatment. This is because of the improved interfacial adhesion. In the present work, the surface becomes less wettable due to addition of the filler particles and this composite can be proposed as a suitable candidate for food packaging application where the material is constantly exposed to moisture and water. The absorption of water decreases the mechanical strength of the composites47 and hence the wettability of the surface should be decreased for good durability of the composite material during its application. From Fig. 7(e), it is observed that, regardless of the silane treatment the contact angle of the 6-wt% composite decreases to 50.1°. This is because of particle agglomeration, which diminishes the advantages of silane treatment.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The effect of addition of the PSP on the thermal stability of the PLA matrix is studied using thermogravimetric analysis. Figure 8(a) shows the TGA curves of neat PLA and the various composites developed. As evident from Fig. 8(a), neat PLA and the composites shows a single stage thermal degradation. The temperature at onset of thermal degradation (Tdo) slightly reduced in the composite compared to that of neat PLA. In neat PLA, the degradation started at 301.98 °C. However, the Tdo values of 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% are 292.13 °C, 296.11 °C and 292.28 °C respectively. Hence, there is decrease in the thermal stability of the composites, due to the addition of the PSP. It is also noted that the temperature at the end of degradation (Tde) generally increased slightly in the composites. In neat PLA, the thermal degradation ended at 365.87 °C. However, the Tde values of 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% are 364.05 °C, 374.76 °C and 369.19 °C respectively. Also, after heating to 600 °C the maximum weight loss is observed in neat PLA (99.99%) and the weight loss is minimum in the 6-wt% composite (98.06%). The percentage residue left in neat PLA is the least (0.00138%). This indicates that the interfacial interactions between the PSP and PLA matrix slows the thermal degradation to some extent37.

(a) TGA curves and (b) DTG plots of Neat PLA, 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites.

To understand the thermal degradation in more detail the DTG curves were plotted as shown in Fig. 8(b). From these curves, it is observed that the depth of the valleys is reduced for the composites and they became broader. Hence, due to the addition of the silane treated PSP, there is a prolongation of the thermal degradation37. The temperature at maximum decomposition rate (Tdmax) also shows a slight increasing trend. The maximum thermal decomposition rate in neat PLA is observed at 352.85 °C. But the Tdmax values for 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites are 346.85 °C, 356.66 °C and 360.46 °C respectively. Unlike previous works, regardless of the alkali and silane treatment the thermal stability of the composites did not improve38. However, the alkali and silane treatment were effective in the removal of the hemicellulose and lignin. The thermal degradation of hemicellulose, cellulose and lignin happens in the temperature ranges of 220–315 °C, 315–400 °C, and 160–900 °C respectively38. Had the hemicellulose and lignin were not removed effectively by the chemical treatments, additional peaks would have been observed in the DTG curves due to the thermal degradation of hemicellulose and lignin at lower temperatures45. In addition, the absence of thermal decomposition of cellulose in the DTG curve indicates good bonding between the filler particles and the PLA matrix.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The DSC thermograms of neat PLA and the various composites are as shown in Fig. 9 DSC analysis done to understand the fusion and crystallization of the composites. The presence of separate broad glass transition, cold crystallization, melting and thermal degradation regions indicate the semi-crystalline nature of the composites48. The first endothermic peak represents the glass transition. The glass transition temperature (Tg) was located as the point of inflection in this region. The Tg values decrease slightly from 66.21 °C in neat PLA to 66.19 °C, 65.89 and 65.77 °C in 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. Although this small decrease in Tg value does not have much significance, it indicates that the PSP embedded themselves within the PLA matrix resulting in micro gaps due to poor bonding with the matrix38. The agglomerated particles have a plasticizing effect and reduces the glass transition temperature.

DSC thermograms of Neat PLA, 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites.

The first exothermic peak in DSC thermogram indicates the cold crystallization process due to the polymer chain rearrangement. A drop in the cold crystallization temperature (Tc) values from 130.33 °C for neat PLA to 124.54 °C, 121.96 °C and 126.50 °C is observed in 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. This decrease in Tc values indicates that the treated PSP acted as effective nucleating agents and made the PLA crystallization quicker49. The second endotherm in the DSC thermogram indicates the melting phenomenon. The melting temperature (Tm) varied from 164.75 °C in neat PLA to 167.21 °C, 165.06 °C and 164.74 °C in 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. The slight increase in melting temperature in the composites is due to the presence of a greater number of crystalline zones due to the nucleating effect of the PSP32. The degree of crystallinity (Xc) values of neat PLA and the 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites are 3.75%, 1.28%, 11.84% and 7.26% respectively. The increase in Xc values in the 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites can be attributed the nucleating effect of the alkali and silane treated PSP. The chemical treatments improve the surface compatibility of the filler particles leading to good interfacial adhesion and increase in crystallinity. This variation is crystallinity is due to the effect of some competing processes. On the one hand, the PSP act as nucleating sites and improve the crystallinity. On the other hand, the particles act as obstacles for the polymer chains to form stable crystals49. In addition, the long siloxane bonds formed due to the auto polymerization reaction of the silanol during the silane treatment of the particles have a lubricating effect on the PLA chains. It improves the chain mobility and decreases the degree of crystallinity50. Hence, the variation in Xc is due to the combined effect of these competing factors.

Melt flow index (MFI)

The MFI values of neat PLA and the different composites are shown in Fig. 10. Neat PLA showed the least MFI value of 9.95 g/10 min. Due to the addition of the treated PSP the MFI values increased to 10.69 g/10 min, 12.80 g/10 min and 10.22 g/10 min in 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. The melting temperature of the filler particles are higher than that of the PLA matrix, which is around 165 °C. At the testing temperature of 170 °C, the PLA matrix melts while the PSP fillers remain solid, as PSP is a lignocellulosic material that does not melt but rather degrades at higher temperatures. The solid PSP particles exert localized pressure on the surrounding molten PLA due to their rigidity and higher density, which likely contributes to increased material flow through the nozzle during extrusion48. Hence, there is an increase in the MFI values in the composite. Many previous works indicate that due to the addition of untreated lignocellulosic particles the MFI reduces44. This is due to the poor interfacial compatibility and the presence of hemicellulose, lignin and other impurities the negatively affects the continuity of the matrix and hamper the heat transfer from the matrix to the particles. The increase in the MFI values in the present study indicates that the alkali treat was effective in removal of the hemicellulose, lignin and other impurities. Also, the siloxane bonds formed due to silane treatment acted as bridge between the filler particles and the PLA matrix leading to better heat transfer and easy rearrangement of the PLA chains making the extrusion of the molten polymer easy45. The MFI value is the highest in the 4-wt% composite (12.80 g/10 min). This is because of good dispersion of the filler particles in the PLA matrix. The slight decrease in the MFI of the 6-wt% composite is due to the agglomeration of the filler particles.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the composites depend on many factors like the concentration of the filler particles, filler particle size, uniform dispersion of the particles in the PLA matrix, particle agglomeration, presence of voids and particle pull out and the interfacial compatibility and adhesion between the particles and the polymer matrix34. The mechanical properties of the composites were evaluated by carrying out tensile test, compression test, flexural test, impact test and the Hardness test. It is very much essential to understand the mechanical properties of the composite in order to design components out of it that will not fail under the applied load.

Tensile test

The tensile test stress vs. strain plots of neat PLA and the various composites are as shown in Fig. 11(a-d). From the stress vs. strain plot it is clear that all the samples failed at the yield point indicating brittle fracture. This is substantiated from Fig. 11(b) where the broken specimens of the tensile test show no necking phenomenon due to plastic deformation. Hence, the FDM printed sample failed in a brittle mode due to the applied tensile load. Based on the stress–strain curves shown in Fig. 11(b), the failure strain values were extracted to better characterize the brittle nature of fracture. The failure strain of neat PLA was approximately 2.84%, while the composites showed reduced elongation, with failure strains of 2.36% (2 wt%), 2.52% (4 wt%), and 2.41% (6 wt%) respectively. These relatively low strain values confirm that all samples failed at or near the yield point without undergoing plastic deformation. The tensile strength and the tensile modulus of the composites calculated from the stress vs. strain plot are summarized in Fig. 11(c, d). The tensile strength decreased from 52.74 MPa for neat PLA to 46.66 MPa, 49.81 MPa and 47.54 MPa for 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. The reduction in tensile strength is mainly attributed to the agglomeration of filler particles within the PLA matrix, which hinders effective interfacial bonding despite alkali and silane surface treatments. These particle clusters disrupt uniform load transfer between the matrix and filler, acting as stress concentration sites that promote crack initiation and accelerate crack propagation under tensile loading, ultimately lowering the composite’s strength47. Moreover, the voids due to particle pull out and the micro-void due to moisture absorption weaken the composites. The SEM analysis was done to support the tensile test results. The SEM images of the tensile fracture surface shown in Fig. 12(a-d) also shows the particle agglomeration and the presence of voids in the matrix. It also substantiates that alkali and silane treatment have improved the interfacial compatibility and bonding. Many previous works also indicates that the alkali and silane treatment of the lignocellulosic fillers improve the mechanical strength in comparison to the untreated filler37. The 4-wt% composite exhibits the highest tensile strength of 49.81 MPa, primarily due to the uniform dispersion of pistachio shell particles in the PLA matrix, which enhances stress transfer efficiency and minimizes stress concentration points. From Fig. 11(d), it is observed that there is an increasing trend of tensile modulus. The tensile modulus of neat PLA (2316.05 MPa) increased to 2496.38 MPa, 2600.98 MPa and 2878.07 MPa in the 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. This increase in the tensile modulus indicates that the composite becomes stiffer and PLA’s anti-deformability property increases with the addition of the treated PSP. Due to the good interfacial adhesion between the filler particles and the PLA matrix, these particles restrict the polymer chain movement and results in a stiffening action and increased modulus37. In addition, these filler particles acted as nucleation sites resulting in more crystalline zones in the matrix. This also led to the rise in the tensile modulus50. Although the addition of treated PSP may slightly reduce tensile strength, it significantly enhances stiffness, impact resistance, thermal stability, and processability—attributes that are highly beneficial for practical applications such as FDM printing. Notably, the 4-wt% composite showed the best balance, with a higher Melt Flow Index and good mechanical behavior. Plus, using PSP, a renewable and low-cost agricultural waste, adds environmental and economic benefits. Altogether, these factors make the composites a strong candidate for sustainable packaging and similar applications, where a combination of performance, printability, and sustainability matters more than tensile strength alone.

Morphology of tensile fracture surface using SEM

Figure 12(a-d) are the SEM images showing the morphology of the fracture surface of the tensile test sample. The tensile fracture surface of the neat PLA is very smooth and flat as shown in Fig. 12(a). This indicates the brittle nature of the fracture49. On the other hand, with the addition of the treated PSP voids are observed in the tensile fracture surface of the composites as shown in Fig. 12(b). These big voids in the 6-wt% PLA/PSP composite fracture surface are due to the pull out of the agglomerated pistachio shell particles2. The voids (indicated by yellow circles in the Fig. 11(b-d)) act as weak zones and reduces the strength of the composites when compared to that of neat PLA. Many previous works have reported a clearance between the particles and the PLA matrix51 suggesting poor interfacial compatibility between the particles and the matrix. However, such gaps and clearances are not observed in the PLA/PSP composites of present study as shown in Fig. 12(c-d) SEM images. This indicates good interfacial bonding between the filler particle and the PLA matrix. Also as shown in Fig. 12(b) and Fig. 12(d) the matrix around the voids was extended when the pistachio shell particles were pulled out during the tensile test. This also indicates that the alkali and silane treatments have improved the interfacial adhesion between the PSP and the PLA matrix37. However, the addition of the PSP negatively affects the continuity of the polymer matrix. As proportion of PSP in the matrix increases, the particles are aggregated together (indicated by green circles) as shown in Fig. 12(b-d). These particles act as stress concentration points and helps in the crack propagation leading to reduced strength37. Many small micro-voids (indicated by blue rectangles) are also observed in the composite as seen in Fig. 12(d). The moisture present in the particle and PLA results in micro-voids during filament extrusion and 3D printing processing using FDM technology34. It is observed from Fig. 12(c) that there is a better dispersion of the PSP in the 4-wt% composite matrix than other composites. This resulted in better load transfer between the filler particle and the polymer matrix during the tensile test2 and it showed improved strength than the other composites.

Compression test

During the compression test, the samples were not crushed even under a load of 120 MPa. This is because the direction in which the layers were printed was in accordance with the direction of compressive load and the material got distributed radially outwards. Hence, the maximum compression was set to 67%in order to avoid the jaws from colliding with each other and damaging the load cell. The compressive stress vs. strain plot is as shown in Fig. 13(a). Three regions can be distinctly seen in the compressive stress vs. strain plot. They are the elastic deformation, yielding and the densification regions2. Initially the deformation due to the compression happens elastically followed by the long yielding region. After this, the densification happens where all the voids and defects get compacted together and there is a steep increase in the compressive load. Since the samples were not fractured, the strength evaluated was the 0.2% offset compressive yield strength. The compressive yield strength and the compressive modulus of the various composites are as shown in Fig. 13(c, b).

(a) Stress-Strain curve, (b) Compressive Strength comparison and (c) Compressive Modulus comparison of Neat PLA, 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% PLA/PSP composites.

The compressive strength is maximum in neat PLA with value of 62.71 MPa. It decreases to 38.41 MPa, 34.82 MPa and 43.06 MPa respectively in the 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites. This uneven trend is likely due to the complex interaction between the filler particles and the PLA matrix. At lower filler loadings (2 wt% and 4 wt%), the PSP particles may not have dispersed well, leading to more agglomeration and weak points where stress concentrates, reducing compressive strength. Interestingly, at 6 wt%, we see a slight improvement, which could be due to better particle packing and a stiffening effect that starts to balance out the earlier negative effects. Also, the irregular shape and stiffness of the PSP particles, along with their natural variability, can impact how stress is distributed within the material. So, the non-linear trend reflects the reality of working with bio-based fillers, where particle behavior isn’t always predictable or consistent. The pistachio shell particles are stiffer and have sharp corners. Hence despite the chemical treatments, the particles may cut the polymer matrix and the adhesion between the matrix and the filler particles may be broken under the compressive load due to stress concentration at the sharp corners of the particle. Hence, the polymer chains are detached from the particles and flows away from the center radially outwards and results in decreased compressive yield strength. The compression modulus also decreases from 1242.90 MPa in neat PLA to 1196.35 MPa and 881.16 MPA in 2-wt% and 4-wt% composites respectively. However, with addition of more than 6-wt% of filler particles, the compressive modulus increases to 1223.45 MPa due to the stiffening action of the filler particles45.

Flexural test

The flexural properties were evaluated using three point bending test. During the flexural loading, the top layers are subjected to compressive stress whereas the bottom layers are subjected to tensile stress. The failed specimen after the flexural test is as shown in Fig. 14(a). The flexural stress vs. strain plot is as shown in Fig. 14(b). The flexural strength and flexural modulus of the composites are shown in Fig. 14(c, d). The cracks initiated from the tensile side of the flat specimen and the fracture was located in the middle of the specimen as shown in Fig. 14(a). As shown in Fig. 14(c), the flexural strength is maximum for neat PLA having the value 63.77 MPa. The strength decreased to 57.66 MPa, 52.20 MPa and 58.06 MPa in the 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% composites respectively. The PSP despite the chemical treatment interferes with the continuity of the PLA matrix and leads to easy fracture due to stress concentration at the site of particle aggregation49. The maximum flexural modulus of 1221.33 MPA is observed in the 2-wt% composites when compared to that of neat PLA which has flexural modulus of 972.23 MPa. This is due to stiffening action of the chemical treated PSP52. However, the flexural modulus decreases with addition of more that 2-wt%PSP. This is because of the particle agglomeration and the particle pull out associated with the increase in the filler content.

Charpy impact test and shore D hardness

During the Charpy impact test the kinetic energy of the pendulum is transferred to the composite and is consumed in generation of the damage. The failed specimens after impact testing are as shown in Fig. 15(a). The impact strength measures the impact energy absorbed by the composite material53. Figure 15(b) shows the impact strength of the various composites. An increasing trend is observed in the impact strength due to the addition of the chemical treated PSP. The neat PLA showed least impact strength of 1.86 kJ/m2. The value increased to 3.72 kJ/m2 in 2-wt% composite and the 4-wt% composite showed the maximum impact strength of 6.21 kJ/m2 because of the uniform distribution of the filler particles in the PLA matrix. The small decrease in the impact strength value to 5.58 kJ/m2 in the 6-wt% composite can be attributed to the agglomeration of the filler particles37. At higher loading levels, PSP particles tend to cluster instead of dispersing uniformly. These agglomerates act as stress concentrators, creating weak points in the matrix that reduce its ability to absorb and dissipate impact energy effectively. So, although the chemical treatments improve matrix–filler compatibility, excessive filler content can offset those benefits, leading to a modest decline in impact resistance at 6 wt%.

(a) Fractured impact samples with different weight% of PSP, (b) Impact Strength comparison and (c) Shore D hardness comparison of Neat PLA, 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% PLA/PSP composites.

The shore D hardness values of different composites are as shown in Fig. 15(c). The baseline durometer hardness value of 78 HSD observed in neat PLA aligns with literature values, confirming the inherent rigidity of the material54. From Fig. 15(c), it is observed that there is an increase in the hardness value due to the addition of treated PSP. The filler particles being rigid and stiffer compared to the PLA matrix helps the composites to bear more load against indentation or penetration44. As the concentration of the hard pistachio shell particles on the surface increases, the resistance to indentation or the hardness of the composites also increase. The hardness increases to 79 HSD, 81 HSD and 82 HSD in 2-wt%, 4-wt% and 6-wt% respectively. The higher stiffness of the chemical treated pistachio shell particles along with the increase in the degree of crystallinity due to the nucleating phenomenon makes the surface composites harder49.

Conclusions

The PLA/pistachio shell composites were thoroughly analyzed using structural, mechanical, thermal, and morphological characterization techniques. FTIR and XRD confirmed the removal of lignin and hemicellulose and improved interfacial bonding due to silane treatment, though crystallinity decreased slightly due to disrupted matrix continuity. The 4-wt% composite exhibited the best overall balance, showing a tensile strength of 49.81 MPa, impact strength of 6.21 kJ/m², and the highest crystallinity at 11.84%. The 6-wt% composite had the highest compressive (43.06 MPa) and flexural (58.06 MPa) strengths, as well as increased hardness (82 HSD). Water contact angle peaked at 57.8° for the 4-wt% composite, indicating reduced wettability. The maximum Melt Flow Index (12.80 g/10 min) also occurred at 4-wt%, supporting improved processability. While thermal stability slightly decreased, the rate of degradation slowed, and the treated PSP acted as an effective nucleating agent. These enhancements, combined with PLA’s non-toxic and biodegradable nature, make the composites promising candidates for food packaging applications.
