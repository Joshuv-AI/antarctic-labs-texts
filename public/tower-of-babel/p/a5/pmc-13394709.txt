The Quebec Parental Insurance Plan: a policy example for reducing inequalities, supporting family functioning and healthy child development
Authors: Gabrielle Garon-Carrier, Catherine Julien, Rachel Margolis, Sophie Mathieu, Amélie Petitclerc, Caroline Fitzpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study traces the historical evolution of the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP), the paid parental leave program introduced in 2006 in Quebec, Canada, and its contribution in promoting economic, health, and gender equity; family well-being; and healthy child development. Drawing on both historical insights and reviewed evidence, it offers evidence-based policy considerations to inform the design of inclusive, equitable, and adaptable parental leave programs. Key considerations for policymakers include broadening eligibility criteria, ensuring financial adequacy of benefits, and fostering gender equity through targeted incentives for paternity and shared parental leave. Flexibility in leave arrangements and continuous program evaluation are also identified as essential considerations to addressing families’ dynamic and evolving needs. Considerations for practitioners include the importance of facilitating informed discussions with families about their parental leave entitlements, emphasizing the role of leave in supporting child development, maternal recovery, and mental health. Researchers are encouraged to adopt an equity-focused approach to studying parental leave by examining its intersections with related policy domains and its differential outcomes across diverse family types. Future studies should also employ longitudinal and mixed-method designs to investigate the mechanisms linking parental leave to family and child outcomes.Keywords: Quebec parental leave program, gender, health and economic equity, policy considerations, family functioning, child health

Introduction

Paid parental leave promotes economic, social, and gender equity, and benefits family well-being by providing financial support when parents take leave from work to care for a newborn child. Paid parental leave has generated interest in child development research because of its capacity to improve newborn physical health at birth and during infancy (e.g. decreases in infant mortality, premature birth, and rehospitalizations) (Rossin 2011, Hajizadeh et al. 2015, Roy Choudhury and Polachek 2021, Jou et al. 2020), to facilitate initiation and continuation of breastfeeding (Mirkovic et al. 2016, Van Niel et al. 2020), and improve infant brain functioning (Brito et al. 2022). Paid parental leave programs also support parent and family well-being by decreasing the risk of postpartum depression (Andres et al. 2016, Kornfeind and Sipsma 2018, Van Niel et al. 2020, Heshmati et al. 2023), union dissolution (Margolis et al. 2021), intimate partner violence (Gartland et al. 2011), and by increasing fathers’ engagement in child rearing (Wray 2020).

Several mechanisms can explain the positive contribution of paid parental leave to both family functioning and child development (Brough et al. 2009, Jou et al. 2018, Bullinger 2019, Whitney et al. 2023, Morrissey et al. 2024). First, paid parental leave may mitigate financial hardship following childbirth. In turn, greater family resources for childrearing could translate into better nutrition and other goods that enhance child health and development. Second, paid parental leave allows for “increased positive parental engagement and time dedicated in health-promoting behaviors,” such as medical appointment and follow-up for their child, and sensitive parent-child interactions. Third, the financial security provided by paid parental leave is likely to “enhance physical and mental health” and greater well-being among parents, by reducing financial stress, increasing time allocated to exercising and stress management, employment opportunities, as well as improving work-life balance and family relations.

In January 2006, the Canadian province of Quebec implemented its own paid parental leave policy, the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP), over and above the existing Canadian federal policy. The QPIP is the most generous paid parental leave policy in Canada (Mathieu et al. 2020) and is credited with contributing to Quebec’s high level of female labor force participation (Moyser and Milan 2018). The QPIP has now been in place for almost 20 years and has undergone significant changes since its implementation. As such, it is timely to provide a historical overview and updated policy design of the QPIP within the Canadian landscape to inform policy considerations and future research.

Parental leave in Canada prior to the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan

In Canada, paid parental leave is administered through the Employment Insurance program. Under this program, eligible workers are entitled to 15 weeks of nontransferable maternity leave, compensated at 55% of their average insurable earnings. The program also provides shared parental leave, i.e. weeks of parental leave benefits that can be shared between both parents. Specifically, families may choose between 35 weeks of shareable parental leave benefits at 55% of income replacement rate or an extended option of 61 weeks at a 33% replacement rate. Significant reforms to the Employment Insurance program were introduced in 2000 through amendments to the “Employment Insurance Act.” These changes expanded the duration of shareable parental leave from 10 to 35 weeks, reduced the eligibility threshold from 700 to 600 hours of insurable employment, and eliminated the requirement for both parents to serve a waiting period. Instead, only one parent—most often the mother—is now required to complete the one-week waiting period before receiving benefit payments. For a comprehensive historical analysis of parental leave benefits in Canada, see Mathieu (2023).

Implementation of the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan

Motivations leading to the creation of the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan

In 2006, the QPIP replaced the federal Employment Insurance program for parental leave benefits within the province of Quebec. Prior to this reform, parental leave in Quebec was administered under the Employment Insurance program, which remains the law of the land in all other Canadian provinces and territories. The introduction of the QPIP pursued two primary objectives: (i) to provide more assistance to low-income families, and (ii) to break down gender roles by encouraging increased maternal participation in the labor force and promoting paternal involvement in childcare and domestic responsibilities (Marshall 2008, Havet et al. 2022). To achieve these aims, the QPIP implemented several key policy changes: it lowered the eligibility requirements, introduced two benefit options with varying durations and income replacement rates, included nontransferable weeks of benefits for fathers, increased the wage coverage, and offered additional supplements for low-income families. Lowering the eligibility requirements under the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan

Prior to the implementation of the QPIP, eligibility for parental benefits was determined by a minimum of 600 hours of insurable employment in the previous year (see Table 1). As a result, nonstandard workers, such as part-time employees, contract workers, students, and self-employed workers—a definition that encompasses a sizeable portion of workers that are disproportionately represented among low-income earners—were ineligible for parental benefits (Mathieu et al. 2020). The QPIP broadened access to parental leave benefits by replacing the hours-based criterion with an earnings-based threshold of $2000 CAD in the tax year preceding childbirth. As of 2025, this $2000 CAD earnings threshold is equivalent to approximately 121 hours of work at Quebec’s minimum wage, which is significantly lower than the 600-hour requirement that remains in place under the Employment Insurance (see Table 2). In addition, the QPIP also eliminated the mandatory waiting period before benefit payments begin. These changes in eligibility have led to an increase in parental leave uptake in Quebec. Maternity leave uptake rose from 64% in 2005 (under the Employment Insurance program) to 74% in 2006 (under the QPIP) to 82% in 2023. Similarly, paternity leave uptake almost tripled, increasing from 28% to 73% over the same period. By 2023, 90% of births in Quebec were covered by the QPIP, compared to 78.3% of births in the rest of Canada covered under the federal EI program (Statistics Canada 2024).

Offering flexibility in policy design

The QPIP provides a flexible two-tier benefits structure: the “Basic Plan and the Special Plan.” (see Table 1). The Basic Plan offers 18 weeks of maternity benefits (for the birthing parent) and 5 weeks of paternity benefits (for the non-birthing parent), both at a wage replacement rate of 70%. It also includes 32 weeks of shareable parental leave, with the first 7 weeks compensated at 70% and the remaining 25 weeks at 55%. The Special Plan provides a shorter duration of paid leave, but at a higher replacement rate. It offers 15 weeks of maternity benefits and 3 weeks of paternity benefits, both at 75%, along with 25 weeks of shareable parental leave benefits at 75% of wage replacement rate. This flexible design allows families to select either a longer leave period with moderate compensation (Basic Plan) or a shorter leave duration with higher income replacement (Special Plan). Furthermore, since 2021, the benefit utilization period has been extended from 12 to 18 months, thereby accommodating diverse financial needs, and personal preferences or circumstances. In 2023, 79% of QPIP recipients selected the Basic Plan, while 21% opted for the Special Plan (Conseil de gestion de l’assurance parentale 2026). Adapting the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan to promote parental leave uptake among fathers

The QPIP is also structured to promote greater use of parental leave benefits among fathers. One of its key features was the allocation of nontransferable weeks of leave specifically for fathers (or the nonbirthing parent). Prior to the implementation of the QPIP, fathers in Quebec were technically eligible for up to 35 weeks of leave through the shareable parental benefits scheme offered by the Employment Insurance program. However, empirical evidence indicates that mothers predominantly made use of this entitlement (Margolis et al. 2021). The QPIP introduced a distinct provision of 3 to 5 weeks of leave (depending on the selected plan) that was exclusively reserved for fathers. This policy innovation positioned Quebec among the most generous jurisdictions within OECD countries with respect to the duration of paternity leave (Patnaik 2019, Jou et al. 2020). As of 2023, the proportion of fathers (or partners) who claimed parental leave benefits in Quebec reached 73.0%, which is markedly higher than the 49.3% observed in the rest of Canada (see Table 1; Statistics Canada 2023).

Furthermore, a significant change was implemented in 2022 as an incentive for fathers to use more weeks of the shareable parental benefits. With the Basic Plan, an additional 4 weeks of shareable benefits at 55% of insurable earnings were granted if each parent received at least 8 weeks of shareable parental benefit. With the Special Plan, 3 additional weeks of benefits at 75% of earnings were provided once each parent had utilized at least 6 weeks of parental shareable benefit. In 2023, 44% of Quebec families reported sharing parental leave benefits between parents, in comparison to only 20% in the rest of Canada (Conseil de gestion de l’assurance parentale 2026).

Increasing the income replacement rate

An additional feature of the QPIP that has contributed to longer durations of parental leave is the enhanced income replacement rate. Compared to the 55% of average earnings provided under the federal Employment Insurance program, the QPIP offers a more generous replacement rate of 70%–75% for both maternity and paternity leave, as well as for some weeks of shareable parental leave, depending on the selected basic vs special plan. This higher rate in income replacement has improved the financial feasibility of taking longer parental leave. Furthermore, since October 2020, QPIP recipients have been permitted to earn income while on leave, provided certain conditions are met (see Table 1). They can work and receive QPIP benefits simultaneously, as long as their total earnings do not exceed 100% of the insurable earnings ceiling.

The QPIP also raised the ceiling for insurable earnings. In 2025, the annually adjusted maximum insurable income in Quebec was $98 000 CAD, while the annual ceiling was set to $65 700 CAD in other Canadian provinces and territories. This means that eligible parents in Quebec could receive on average up to $1178 CAD per week for a 50-week parental leave, compared to a maximum of $695 CAD per week in the rest of Canada. For example, in 2025, a mother with an annual income of $88 000 CAD would have received a total of $52 885 CAD in benefits under the QPIP for a 50-week parental leave. If she opted to work part-time during her leave, she could also earn up to an additional $31 750 CAD in paid work without reduction in QPIP benefits. Under the Employment Insurance program, this mother would receive $34 745 CAD in benefit payments, and any employment earnings during her leave would reduce benefits by 50 cents for every dollar earned under the Working While on Claim provisions. As a result, if a mother earned $31 750 in paid work during her parental leave period, her benefits would be reduced by approximately half that amount (subject to program rules and thresholds), making it impossible to retain both the full Employment Insurance benefit amount and the full $31 750 in employment earnings. In both the QPIP and Employment Insurance programs, however, parents may receive employer-paid supplementary benefits that top up pre-leave earnings for a specified period, without reducing statutory benefits.

In Quebec, the maximum insurable income used to calculate parental leave benefits is adjusted yearly on January 1, based on the rate established by the “Commission des normes, de l’équité, de la santé et de la sécurité du travail” (CNESST). The CNESST is the body to which the Government of Quebec has entrusted the administration of the Act respecting occupational health and safety, and compliance with labor rights and obligations. The CNESST typically adjusts this amount each year to reflect fluctuations in average wage levels in Quebec. As the QPIP operates as an insurance-based scheme designed for workers, both employees and employers are required to contribute. In 2025, the premium rate was set at 0.494% for employees (with a yearly maximum premium of $484.12 CAD) and 0.692% for employers (with a yearly maximum premium of $678.16 CAD). Self-employed workers are also required to contribute at a rate of 0.878% (with a yearly maximum annual premium of $860.44 CAD). These rates have remained fixed since 2020.

Promoting inclusivity of low-income and diverse family structures

The QPIP also introduced measures to address the unique needs of low-income families. Between 2006 and 2023, families with an annual income below $25 921 CAD were eligible for a supplement benefit covering up to 80% of the net household income, depending on the number and age of children. This measure remained unchanged for over 15 years, with no adjustments made for inflation. Low-income mothers in Quebec returned to work earlier than do high-income mothers (Haeck et al. 2019), which suggested that the financial compensation offered through the QPIP was not sufficient to support family expenditures at the lower end of the income distribution (Haeck et al. 2019). Indeed, the proportion of QPIP recipients eligible for the low-income supplement declined from nearly 7% in 2006 to less than 5% in 2019.

In response to this impediment, three major changes have been made to the QPIP. First, the eligibility threshold for the low-income supplement has been adjusted to $32 760 CAD in 2024. The new threshold is the equivalent of the annual income of an individual working 40 hours per week at the minimum wage ($15.75/h * 40 h * 52 weeks = $32 760 CAD). Unlike the previous fixed threshold, this new threshold is now indexed to inflation and will increase in accordance with future adjustments to the minimum wage. Meanwhile, the eligibility threshold for low-income supplement under the Employment Insurance program remains unchanged at $25 921 CAD, regardless of variations in the cost of living. Second, benefit calculations are now based on individual income rather than household income. When the eligibility criteria were tied to the household income, many women were excluded from receiving the low-income supplement benefits due to their partner’s higher earnings. In Quebec, the majority of those receiving low-income supplements are women, highlighting the gendered implications of this measure. Third, under the new parameters, both parents can now receive low-income supplement benefits simultaneously if they each meet the criteria for eligibility. Prior to September 2021, only one parent could receive the supplement benefits at a time, which disproportionately affected families where both parents had low incomes or preferred to receive benefits concurrently rather than alternately.

Furthermore, as part of Bill 51, the QPIP undertook measures in 2021 to address disparities in benefit entitlements between single-parent and dual-parent households, and between biological and adoptive parents, by standardizing access to the same number of weeks of benefits, regardless of the family structure (Mathieu 2021). With the adoption of Bill 51, the amendments made to the law put an end to the prior differential treatment between adoptive and biological parents and, by extension, between adopted and non-adopted children (Lavallée et al. 2016). That is why in 2021, the QPIP introduced adoption-specific benefits equivalent in duration and wage replacement rate to those granted to biological parents. Under the Basic Plan, adoptive parents are entitled to a total of 50 weeks of leave and an additional 5 weeks of leave exclusive to single adoptive parents. The Special Plan provides 40 weeks of leave and 3 weeks exclusive to single adoptive parents. These modifications underscore the QPIP’s commitment to inclusivity by accommodating diverse family configurations, even those representing a small proportion of recipients.

Objective statement

Beyond the motivations for implementing the QPIP and its subsequent policy reforms, it is important to assess the extent to which the program has generated measurable benefits for families and children. This study provides a descriptive synthesis of existing research on associations between QPIP and outcomes related to economic and gender equity, family functioning and well-being, and child development. It also identifies implications for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers.

Materials and methods

We conducted a narrative policy-focused review of the literature using PubMed, ScienceDirect, and MEDLINE databases for articles published in English or French between 1 January 2006 and 1 April 2024. Search terms included a combination of keywords such as “parental leave,” “maternity leave,” “paternity leave,” “Quebec or Canada.” To be included, studies had to focused on the QPIP, used data from Quebec or Canadian database with relevance to Quebec. Quantitative or qualitative studies focusing on highly specific populations (e.g. physicians, surgical residents) were excluded. We identified a small body of literature (n = 9 quantitative studies) published over the past 25 years examining associations between paid maternity, paternity, or parental leave under the QPIP and outcomes related to economic, gender, and health equity, family functioning and well-being, and child development, since its implementation in 2006. This narrative review synthesizes findings from these studies alongside publicly available data to inform policy considerations. Because the study did not involve human participants, animals, or the collection of identifiable personal data, ethics approval was not required. Furthermore, no new data were generated or analyzed in this review. All studies reviewed were conducted prior to the many reforms applied to the QPIP since 2021.

Results

Quebec Parental Insurance Plan promotes economic and gender-based equity among families with young children

Some research has evaluated whether the QPIP has led to more equal sharing of parental leave, paid work, and earnings between mothers and fathers. This research has used econometric methods such as difference-in-differences modeling and local average treatment effect, taking advantage of the introduction of the QPIP in the province of Quebec (vs the rest of Canada). The implementation of the QPIP has been associated with more equal sharing of leave benefits between mothers and fathers (Margolis et al. 2019). Drawing on administrative data from 1998 to 2012, the authors estimated the likelihood of either parent using maternity or parental benefits, and patterns of benefits sharing before and after specific policy changes in Canada in 2001 (lowering the eligibility criteria from 700 to 600 hours of work) and in Quebec in 2006 (implementation of QPIP), stratified by yearly household income (< 30 K, between 30 and 89 K, >90 K). This approach captured how the policy changes in Canada and Quebec affected families differently across the income spectrum. The implementation of the QPIP was associated with a significant change in how parental benefits were used across income groups (Margolis et al. 2019). The QPIP led to a 22.7%-point increase in sharing of benefits between mothers and fathers in Quebec. This increase was greater among middle- and high-income families than among low-income families (Margolis et al. 2019).

Another study revealed that the QPIP was associated with increased maternal time spent at work (Patnaik 2019), leading to more equal time dedicated to parenting and paid work for mothers and fathers. These findings were based on time diary data from Canadian General Social Survey data collected in 2005 (prior to the implementation of the QPIP) and in 2010, where 1/5 participants were from Quebec. In comparison to 2005, fathers exposed to the QPIP (2010 data) spent 15 minutes longer per day doing housework (an increase of 21%). Mothers exposed to the QPIP experienced significant gains in time spent in paid work, with one hour more in paid work per day and 80 more minutes physically at the workplace. Mothers exposed to the QPIP were also 5.4 percentage points more likely to be employed full-time (Patnaik 2019).

In addition to promoting more equal sharing of leave duration and paid work, paternity leave uptake in the QPIP has been associated with reduced gender wage inequity, without lowering household income (Choi et al. 2025). In this study, the authors examined the fathers’ use of parental benefits on their earnings, their partners’ earnings, the total family income, and wage inequity within couples. This was investigated in the short-run (1–2 years after a childbirth), and the long-run (up to ten years after childbirth) among first-time fathers using an intention-to-treat, difference-in-differences analysis exploiting the introduction of the QPIP in 2006. This was achieved using longitudinal administrative tax file data compiled by Statistics Canada from 2003 to 2016.

While a small short-term negative contribution of leave uptake has been found on mothers’ and fathers’ earnings, fathers’ use of paternity leave after a first birth led to significantly higher paternal and maternal earnings 8–10 years after a first birth. Mothers gained significant wage increases—7.5% higher earnings 2 to 4 years after a first birth, 7.1% higher 5 to 7 years after a first birth, and 3.7% higher 8 to 10 years after a first birth. Together, all these studies provide strong research evidence supporting the QPIP’s effectiveness in reducing income and gender inequity related to leave duration, paid work, and earnings.

Research evidence supports the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan in enhancing family functioning and maternal health

Several studies revealed that the QPIP has improved family well-being by reducing the risk of maternal depression (Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023), decreasing the risk of parents’ union dissolution (Margolis et al. 2021), and improving parental engagement in child rearing (Patnaik 2019, Wray 2020, Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023). There is no question that pregnancy and childbirth are challenging experiences both for mothers and the family. Time off work, job protection, and income supports may reduce stress and enhance well-being during the postpartum period. Prior studies reported consistent evidence of paid parental leave in reducing maternal depression in European countries (Avendano et al. 2015), Australia (Bilgrami et al. 2020), and the United States (Kornfeind and Sipsma 2018, for at least 12 weeks of paid or unpaid leave) (see Aitken et al. 2015, and Van Niel et al. 2020, for a review).

Two studies have evaluated the contribution of the QPIP on maternal depression. One study used administrative health data from 2001 to 2006 reporting all types of medical services provided by physicians to mothers covered by the Quebec universal public health care program, including public prescription drug plan associated with depressive symptoms (Haeck et al. 2016). Using a strict regression discontinuity design, they found no evidence that the 2006 implementation of the QPIP had sizeable contribution to maternal physical or mental health care costs, as measured by physicians’ fee-for-service billing costs, prescription drug costs, or the number of hospitalizations. However, it should be noted that the outcome variables of interest in this study set an exceedingly high bar for the measurement of depression, as many new mothers may experience depressive symptoms but never seek physical or psychiatric care (e.g. Heneghan et al. 2000). Furthermore, the contribution of the QPIP on maternal depression was estimated very shortly after the implementation of the program, decreasing the chance to capture significant long-term benefits.

Another study used Canadian survey data, the National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth, covering the 2002 to 2009 period to estimate the intent-to-treat effect of the QPIP, once again, comparing parents in Quebec to similar parents in the rest of Canada (Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023). Maternal depression, measured with the well-validated Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, showed a 12.9 percentage point decline in maternal depression scores. This study also revealed some benefits of the QPIP on maternal behavior when the child was aged 2–3 years, with a significant increase of more than 20% of a standard deviation on the positive interaction scale (e.g. how often do you laugh together) (Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023).

The QPIP also appears to improve relationship among partners (Margolis et al. 2021). The authors compared the probability of union dissolution between parents exposed to the QPIP (those living in Quebec and who had a child in 2006) and the probability for those who were not exposed to this policy (parents living outside of Quebec or parents who had children in Quebec prior to 2006). Using a difference-in-differences approach and local average treatment effects with Canadian administrative data, they estimate a decrease in the separation/divorce rate by 0.5 percentage point five years after childbirth, from 8.6% to 8.1%, representing a 6% reduction in separation as a result of the QPIP. The greatest reduction in union dissolution was found among couples of working partners earning similar incomes before childbirth (about 33% of families), and among couples with a native-born father (78% of families; Margolis et al. 2021).

The QPIP may also improve family functioning by improving paternal engagement in child rearing. Findings from time diary data collected as a part of the Canadian General Social Survey in 2005 (prior to implementation of the QPIP) and in 2010 (after the implementation of the QPIP) suggest that children receive considerably more care time from their fathers after the onset of the program (Patnaik 2019, Wray 2020). In comparison to 2005, fathers exposed to the QPIP spend 36 minutes more physically at home on a daily basis, and 21 minutes more allocated to child care (Patnaik 2019). In this study, child care was the sum of time spent in routine care (e.g. feeding children), interaction (e.g. reading to children), and travel and communication related to child care. Using the same data source, another study used a difference-in-differences method to compare fathers’ involvement in child rearing in Quebec (vs rest of Canada) prior to and after the implementation of the QPIP (Wray 2020). The reserved paternity leave led to a direct increase in fathers’ time in charge of their children, measured as time spent caring for children when the mother was not present. This resulted in fathers taking on 2.2 additional hours of solo parenting time per week (Wray 2020). However, it remains unknown how fathers’ increased time in caring for their children translates into children’s developmental gains or into mental health benefits for fathers or mothers.

Quebec Parental Insurance Plan could improve child physical and mental health during early childhood

Paid parental leave can indirectly promote child growth and physical health through breastfeeding and attentive health care. Paid parental leave facilitates both the initiation and duration of breastfeeding (Mirkovic et al. 2016, Kornfeind and Sipsma 2018, Van Niel et al. 2020), which foster infants’ immunity (Jackson and Nazar 2006) and neurobehavioral outcomes, especially among premature and very low birth weight children (Quigley et al. 2012, Härtel et al. 2020). One study revealed that the QPIP did not necessarily increase the incidence of breastfeeding but significantly increased breastfeeding duration. It increased the probability of breastfeeding by 7.4 percentage points beyond the critical 6-month period recommended by the World Health Organization (Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023). This increased probability was greater among highly educated mothers (Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023). This finding is similar to that from Haeck et al. (2019), who found that the QPIP increased the likelihood of breastfeeding for more than six months by six percentage points.

Paid parental leave can also promote newborns’ nurturance and establishment of secure attachment (Plotka and Busch-Rossnagel 2018) and consequently improve children’s social and emotional adjustment during early childhood. This hypothesis is supported by studies showing lower maternal sensitivity among full-time working mothers in comparison to part-time working mothers during the first year of child life (Brooks-Gunn et al. 2010). One study conducted in Quebec showed that, in comparison to children of mothers on leave, children of working mothers in the first 5 months of life were at higher risk of developing separation anxiety symptoms during early childhood (Garon-Carrier et al. 2023). Using data from Quebec Longitudinal Study of Child Development (1998–2021), the authors investigated whether maternal leave and families’ economic situation when the child was 5-months old predicted anxiety symptoms from 17 months to 6 years. They showed that maternity leave for more than 5 months was associated with lower risk of developing child separation anxiety symptoms, in contrast to maternal employment prior to 5 months after childbirth.

Another study estimated the impact of the QPIP on children’s emotional disorder (e.g. depressive symptoms, expression of sadness), anxiety, separation anxiety, hyperactivity and inattention, and physical aggression and opposition at the age of 2 to 3 years, using the Canadian National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth (Haeck et al. 2019). Controlling for various child and family characteristics (e.g. child gender, number of siblings, single parent status, maternal and paternal education, breastfeeding), the QPIP program decreased children’s emotional disorder and anxiety (−.10 SD) and also slightly decreased separation anxiety (−.05 SD). There was no significant impact of the QPIP on child hyperactivity and inattention, or physical aggression and opposition (Haeck et al. 2019).

Discussion

Drawing on the evidence synthesized in our comprehensive narrative review, as well as our historical analysis of the QPIP and its policy evolution within the Canadian landscape, multiple evidence-based policy considerations for QPIP and for paid parental leave schemes more broadly can be derived using the QPIP as a case study for informing the design and refinement of such policies. These considerations are not intended to suggest that other jurisdictions should replicate the QPIP. Rather, they are grounded in lessons learned from the Quebec experience and are meant to inform policy across diverse contexts.

Evidence-based policy considerations

Based on the QPIP, we proposed several considerations in Section For paid parental leave schemes aimed at informing policymakers interested in designing, developing, implementing, or optimizing paid parental leave programs worldwide. It is important to note that the current evidence base on the QPIP relies primarily on quasi-experimental studies. Although these methodological designs can provide valuable insights into program effects, they do not allow to establish causal inferences, nor to specifically identify which parameters of the program should be prioritized over others. Therefore, the order in which the considerations are presented should not be interpreted as implying a hierarchy of importance among them.

For Quebec Parental Insurance Plan

The QPIP has proven successful in achieving its original objectives, by increasing parental leave uptake, enhancing support for low-income families, promoting gender equity, and contributing to family well-being. The following policy considerations do not call for major structural reforms but instead focus on strengthening the program’s equity, inclusivity, and effectiveness among groups that appear to benefit less from it (Julien et al. 2025). Indeed, the QPIP should be viewed primarily as a successful program that would benefit from targeted improvements rather than substantial redesign.

Considerations for research on paid parental leave schemes

Several limitations can hinder our comprehensive understanding of mechanisms by which paid parental leave may support family and child healthy development. For example, most studies in our literature review estimated the contribution of the QPIP using federal tax files data (Margolis et al. 2019, Margolis et al. 2021, Choi et al. 2025), cross-sectional Canadian General Social Survey (Patnaik 2019, Wray 2020), and the Canadian National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth (Haeck et al. 2016, Haeck et al. 2019, Lebihan and Mao Takongmo 2023). These data sources were sometimes combined with other survey data (e.g. Survey of Young Canadians, Canadian Community Health Survey) or administrative data (e.g. Haeck et al. 2016, 2019). While these data sources allow observing incremental changes and long-term trends before and after the QPIP implementation in 2006 at the population-level, they do not capture individual differences in how parental leave and its various conditions (e.g. access to leave, type of leave, leave duration) are associated with family functioning and child development. The inability to account for individual differences and the limitations inherent in the research design are common challenges in studies examining the effects of paid parental leave. Second, most research on paid parental leave is done in research and policy silos, with little attention paid to how related policies—especially early childhood education and care and income support policies (e.g. unemployment insurance, child benefits welfare)—can also affect family and child well-being. Third, there is limited research attention given to issues of family diversity and questions about which families and children are excluded from parental benefits. Given gaps in research evidence, we recommend that researchers:

Investigate the intersections between parental leave and related policy domainsFuture research should move beyond siloed approaches and explore how parental leave policies are inter-connected with other care/work and social policies, including early childhood education and care, income support programs (e.g. unemployment insurance, child benefits), and employment standards (e.g. remote work and flexible work) (see de Laat et al. 2023). Such work could adopt a policy ecosystem perspective, examining cumulative effects on family well-being and child health. These interactions across policies could be modeled using both qualitative comparative analysis and integrated microsimulation models, to estimate joint and interacting contributions.One crucial next step to advance comprehensive research in this field and to facilitate policy change worldwide would be to disentangle which combinations of policy factors (e.g. wage replacement rates, benefit ceilings) best contribute to family well-being and healthy child development.

Conclusions

The QPIP has contributed significantly to reducing income, health, and gender inequities, and to foster family functioning and healthy child development. It serves as a valuable policy framework for the development, execution, and improvement of paid parental leave programs worldwide. Through a historical analysis of the QPIP and a comprehensive narrative, policy-focused review of the literature, this study offered several considerations for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers.
