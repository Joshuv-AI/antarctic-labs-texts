Background

The pancreas is an organ located in the upper part of your belly, or abdomen, as shown in figure 1. Pancreatitis means inflammation, which is swelling and irritation. When the pancreas becomes irritated, it can cause pain and make it harder for your body to break down food properly. The irritation can happen quickly (which is called acute pancreatitis) or it can occur over a longer time (which is called chronic pancreatitis).

Causes

The pancreas plays a role in making “juices” to help with breaking down food (which is called digestion) and controlling blood sugar. The most frequent causes for acute pancreatitis are stones that come from the gallbladder, which is known as cholelithiasis. This occurs in 6 out of 10 cases. The gallbladder is a small pouch found by your liver that acts as a storage tank for bile, which is a fluid used to help break down fatty food.

The second most frequent cause for acute pancreatitis is alcohol use, which makes up 3 out of 10 cases of acute pancreatitis.

Medical options

The treatment for pancreatitis focuses on helping you feel better although your body heals itself. This means medications to treat pain, fluid in a small tube in your arm if you are dehydrated, and sometimes it can even mean not letting you eat. Eating makes the pancreas work harder, and that can sometimes make it more difficult for it to recover. As you start to feel better, you can slowly drink more and begin to eat. We refer to this treatment of symptoms as “supportive care.”

If you are having a lot of nausea and vomiting, a small tube may be needed to help empty your stomach. This tube goes through your nose and into your stomach to help remove fluids from your stomach, which is referred to as a nasogastric tube. Even though you may not have eaten recently, your stomach still makes fluid that will be pulled out through this tube. The tube can also be used to give you nutrition if you are unable to eat. In rare cases, you may need nourishment to be given through the small tube in your arm vein if you cannot be given food within a reasonable time.

In more serious cases of pancreatitis, you may need care in the intensive care unit, or ICU. The ICU is a special part of the hospital where patients can be closely watched and receive a higher level of care. This may be necessary if pancreatitis affects other parts of your body, such as your breathing, blood pressure, or kidneys. In the ICU, you may receive extra support, including fluids, medications, oxygen, or machines that help your body rest and recover.

Surgical options

The need for operation in pancreatitis depends on what caused it. The most common reason for operation is when pancreatitis is caused by gallstones. There are two main types of procedures that may need to be performed during your hospital stay:

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography: more commonly referred to as an ‘ERCP’, is a procedure where a small camera is inserted through your mouth and advanced to the location where the tubes from the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas drain. A stone causing blockage can usually be removed this way.

Cholecystectomy: or the removal of your gallbladder. If gallstones are suspected to be the cause of your pancreatitis, your doctor will likely recommend that your gallbladder be surgically removed during your hospital stay. This is recommended to prevent the symptoms from reoccurring.

In rare cases, pancreatitis can lead to pockets of fluid or infected areas around the pancreas. If this happens, a procedure may be needed to remove that fluid and help your body heal. This is often done by placing a small drain through the skin to allow the fluid to come out. In some situations, video-assisted retroperitoneal drainage, referred to as a VARD, may be performed. This procedure uses small tools and a camera to safely remove infected or dead tissue around the pancreas.

Care after treatment

After you leave the hospital for pancreatitis, there are simple steps you can take to help your body heal.

Try to avoid alcohol and tobacco, as they can make your pancreatitis symptoms worse. Eating foods that are low in fat can also help, because it will give your pancreas a chance to rest and recover.

If you had operation, taking care of your incisions is important. Keep the areas clean and dry. You can shower and let soapy water run over the incisions, but avoid soaking them in baths, pools, hot tubs, etc. Follow the instructions your surgeon gives you.

Possible complications

The symptoms you experience from pancreatitis can greatly vary. However, you should feel better when you are sent home from the hospital. After you leave the hospital, it is important to watch for problems that can sometimes happen during your recovery. The most common issue is your symptoms coming back again. If you notice similar problems again, then you should call your doctor or seek medical care immediately.

What happens next

Discharge location

If your hospital stay for pancreatitis was straightforward, you will likely go home without needing much additional treatment.

Wound care

If you had operation, follow your surgeon’s instructions for caring for your incisions. Keep the areas clean and dry. Smaller wounds usually do not need much extra care.

Call your doctor if you notice any of the following:

Thick, yellow, green, or foul-smelling fluid.

A small amount of light, clear fluid can be normal, but anything that looks like pus, smells bad, or is getting worse should be looked at by a doctor.

Diet

Your pancreas helps your body break down fatty foods. After an episode of pancreatitis, it’s best to avoid high-fat foods for a few weeks. This gives your pancreas time to rest and lowers the chance that your symptoms will come back.

Activity

Staying active helps your body recover, whereas sitting or lying around too much can increase the risk of other problems. Be sure to follow your surgeon’s recommendations about activity level and any restrictions if you did have operation.
