Nucleophosmin 1 induction is an early event in a Phenobarbital induced proliferation response in rat but not human liver 3D microtissues
Authors: Matthew Elcombe, Stephanie Wallace, Simon Plummer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A key event in the process of rat but not human liver carcinogenesis caused by constitutive androstane receptor activators such as phenobarbital (PB) is hepatocyte proliferation, but the mechanism(s) underpinning this response is not fully understood. Previously we showed that rat liver microtissues (LiMTs) can recapitulate a PB-induced hepatocyte proliferation response (1). In this follow up study we used our microTMA technology coupled with transcriptomics and immunofluorescence (IF) staining to elucidate mechanisms of rat liver carcinogenesis in this model. We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on transcriptomics data generated from laser microdissected liver microtissue microTMA FFPE sections from control and PB-treated LiMTs against custom liver cell proliferation and constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) activation signatures (2) and found that the former signature was significantly (q<0.25) enriched in rat but not human LiMT differentially expressed gene lists. This process also identified the cell proliferation gene nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) as being significantly induced (p < 0.05) in rat but not human LiMTs. IF staining of parallel microTMA FFPE sections coupled with quantitative image analysis confirmed that the NPM1 protein was induced by PB treatment in rat but not human liver microtissues after 24 and 48 hrs PB treatment. In conclusion we have identified induction of nuclear NPM1 expression as an early event in PB-induced rat hepatocyte cell proliferation.

Introduction

The relevance of the 2 year bioassay for human risk assessment is questionable due to its limited predictivity for human cancer risk, especially with regard to non-genotoxic carcinogens (NGC) [2], [3]. Initiatives to improve the efficiency of NGC risk assessment require the identification of mechanistic biomarkers and human relevant models [4], [5] that can be used to develop waiver rationales of rodent cancer bioassays [6]. These key event biomarkers have utility in Bradford Hill schemes for risk assessment and in understanding the human relevance of a carcinogenic response observed in rodent studies [7].

An early key event in rat liver carcinogenesis caused by nuclear hormone receptor activation is a transient hepatocyte proliferation response which occurs in a window 8–28 days after treatment with certain NHR activator compounds [8], [9]. Hepatocyte proliferation is a key event in the process of phenobarbital (PB) induced liver carcinogenesis, and PB-induced liver carcinogenesis in rodents has been shown to involve the activation of the constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) [10], a mechanism that is not considered relevant to humans [10]. Following PB-mediated CAR activation, gene expression is altered leading to increased hepatocellular proliferation, clonal expansion and formation of altered foci, and the development of liver adenomas/carcinomas [11], [12].

We have previously shown that hepatocyte proliferation induced by PB occurs in rat but not human 3D liver microtissues [1], indicating that these models recapitulate known in vivo species differences [11], [12]. The induction of phase 1 and 2 enzymes observed in the rat and human liver microtissues was also consistent with published results [13].

We wanted to investigate the mechanism(s) by which CAR activation by PB treatment caused this key event in particular the target gene(s) or protein(s) initiating CAR-activation cell proliferation mediating species differences at this level. Previous studies have sought to identify the early molecular events in this proliferation key event [14], however the underlying mechanism(s) has yet to be fully elucidated. We postulated that certain genes controlling cell division may be altered by PB in rat but not human liver microtissues, and to identify these genes, we assessed bias in differentially expressed gene data derived from these microtissues by performing a GSEA-based assessment of over-representation (enrichment) in a set of gene alterations associated with CAR-mediated hepatocyte proliferation in rats [15].

We found that certain genes in this CAR-mediated liver proliferation ‘signature’ were significantly altered in the rat but not the human liver microtissues. NPM1 along with PSDM4 were the most significantly altered genes in the ‘rat cell proliferation’ signature that were associated with its enrichment in rat but not human S1500 surrogate panel (Mav et al., 2018) transcriptomics data. Our whole genome transcriptomics analysis also identified other rat cell proliferation signature genes that were induced by PB in rat but not human LiMTs consistent with our observations of species differences in hepatocyte proliferation in these liver microtissues. We focused our phenotypic anchoring analysis on NPM1 because of its key role in regulating cell division [16]. Through this process we confirmed NPM1 as being significantly induced by PB treatment at the RNA and protein levels in rat but not human liver microtissues suggesting that the differential activation of this gene could contribute to species differences in this liver carcinogenesis/hepatocyte proliferation response.

Methods

Liver microtissue treatments

InSphero rat liver 3D microtissues (LiMTs) made from primary rat hepatocytes and rat non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) (InSphero #MT-02–00104) and human LiMTs made from multi-donor primary human hepatocytes and human NPCs (InSphero #MT-02–302–04) were cultured in fresh 3D Insight rat liver maintenance medium (InSphero #CS-07–002–01) or 3D Insight human liver maintenance medium-AF (InSPhero #CS-07001a-01), respectively, and then treated with PB at a final concentrations of 500, 750, 1000 and 2000 μM. A HGF (Sigma #H9661–5UG) positive control treatment for hepatocyte proliferation was also included in the experiment at a concentration of 50 ng/ml for rat and 100 ng/ml for human LiMTs. The rat and human LiMTs were treated (one spheroid per well) with PB dissolved in rat or human liver maintenance medium, respectively, for four different time periods: 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h, in 96 well gravity trap™ plates. Experiments were performed at least twice on different batches of LiMTs. 24 hr prior to fixing, a media change was performed using media containing BrDU (Sigma #B5002–100mg). After incubation of the LiMTs for the appropriate periods of time, they were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde [Pierce 16 % paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific #28908)], diluted in dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) (ThermoFisher #14190–094) for 30 min at room temperature (RT), after which they were rinsed in DPBS.

Cell viability analysis

Following treatments the rat and human LiMTs were lysed in ATP lysis buffer (Perkin Elmer#ORT0531) for ATP measurement using a PerkinElmer ATPlite Luminescence ATP Detection Assay System (PE#6016943) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Microtissue microarray (microTMA) construction, H&E and histopathological analysis

Histopathological analysis of H&E sections was performed from a microTMA constructed using a previously published method [1]. Briefly, fixed LiMTs were loaded into the wells of a 2 % agarose mould containing 96 wells, maintaining the same orientation as used in the 96 well plate, and sealed using molten 0.7 % agarose. We embedded 12 LiMTs per treatment in the microTMA mould. The agarose mould containing LiMTs was dehydrated for a minimum of 12 hr in 70 % ethanol, and the microTMA mould was processed to paraffin wax in a tissue processor (Leica TP1020). Following wax embedding, the microTMA block was sectioned (6 µM) using a microtome (Reichert-Jung 2030) onto glass microscope slides and Zeiss PEN 1 membrane slides.

TempO-seq transcriptomic analysis

Control and PB treated (48 hr) fixed rat and human LiMTs were processed into a 96 well microTMA block and 6 μM sections through the spheroids were mounted on Zeiss PEN1 membrane slides (Zeiss catalogue number: 415190–9042–000). The sections were then dewaxed in histoclear and rehydrated in graded ethanols. For each treatment group and species 4 LiMT sections were collected by laser catapult microdissection (LCM) using a Zeiss PALM MicroBeam microscope and gene expression measured using the rat and human TempO-Seq S1500+ panels [17] following a protocol developed by BioSpyder for Tempo-Seq analysis of FFPE samples. Briefly, lystates were produced from the FFPE LCM samples using lysis buffer and FFPE protease and Detector Oligos added for TempO-Seq analysis as described in Trejo et al. [18] and sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq2500 to generate raw counts per gene per sample. Using R, a DeSeq2 analysis was performed on the raw counts per gene per sample data and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified relative to controls.

Using Hotgenes, an open source R package developed by Pfizer (https://github.com/pfizer-opensource/Open-Hotgenes), a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on the rat and human DEG lists against custom rat liver cell proliferation, AhR, CAR, and PPAR alpha activation signatures [15], [19], [20], [21]. We also used Illumina’s BaseSpace Correlation Engine (BSCE) to enable the comparison and correlation of DEG datasets through ranked-based directional enrichment statistics [22]. The rat and human DEG lists were uploaded to BSCE and a meta-analysis performed against the custom CAR activation signature [20].

Microarray whole genome transcriptomic analysis

Four whole fixed spheroids from each of the 24, 48, 72 and 96 h dose-response treatments (500, 750, 1000 μM PB) were lifted from the 96 well LiMT plates. RNA from these samples was extracted using the Qiagen miRNeasy FFPE Kit according to the Qiagen RNeasy FFPE Handbook 09/2011. The RNA yield was quantified using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and extracted RNA was treated with DNase to remove any trace of genomic DNA. 50 ng of RNA was used to generate amplified cDNA with the Sigma TransPlex Whole Transcriptome Amplification system.The cDNA was purified then quantified via Nanodrop spectroscopy. 1800 ng of cDNA generated in the transplex reaction was labelled using the Agilent SureTag DNA labelling Kit and the specific activity (pmol Cy3/ug cDNA) and concentration (ug/ml) of labelled cDNA were quantified by NanoDrop spectroscopy. A specific activity of 15–50 pmol Cy3/ug DNA was considered sufficient for hybridisation on Agilent arrays.

1800 ng of purified Cy3-labelled cDNA samples were combined with Agilent-CGH block and Agilent 2x Hi-RPM Hybridisation Buffer (components of the Agilent Oligo aCGH/ChiP-on-chip Hybridisation kit). Hybridisation to Agilent Whole Genome Expression microarrays was performed at 20 RPM at a temperature of 65 °C for 17 h. Washing steps were performed according to the Agilent Oligonucleotide Array-Based CGH for Genomic DNA Analysis Protocol. The Agilent Scanner was equipped with extended dynamic range (XDR) software and Agilent Feature Extraction Software was used for the data extraction from raw microarray image files to generate raw microarray data. Using R, the raw microarray data was normalised with a quantile algorithm using the BiocLite.R and limma packages and DEGs identified relative to controls.

Immunostaining

MicroTMA slides were dewaxed in histoclear and then heat induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was performed using IHC world epitope retrieval (IHC world). Parallel slides were either hematoxylin and eosin stained or immunostained. BrDU/NPM1 single immunofluorescence (IF), NPM1/HepPar1 or NPM1/Ki67 dual IF were performed as follows: microTMA slides were dewaxed in histoclear and rehydrated in graded ethanol (100–70 %) and subjected to HIER in IHC world epitope retrieval solution for 40 min at 99 °C in an epitope retrieval steamer (IHC world). After HIER the slides were washed in distilled water and mounted in PBS on a Shandon disposable immunostaining chamber according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blocking buffer (1 % BSA (Sigma A7907 100 G), 0.2 % triton X 100 (VWR 3063324) and 3 % normal goat serum (Sigma G9023–10 ml) in PBS) was added to the chamber and the slides incubated for 2 h at room temperature (RT). BrDU (AbCam ab152095)/NPM1 (AbCam ab183340) primary antibody at a 1:200 and 1:100 dilution, respectively (PBS/0.1 % BSA/0.5 % normal goat serum/0.2 % triton) and NPM1 (ab183340)/HepPar1 (BIO SB PI5629) 1:100 or NPM1/Ki67 (AbCam #ab15580), which included a primary antibody negative control (wash buffer alone), was then added to the chambers and the slides incubated overnight at 4 °C. Slides were washed in wash buffer (0.1 % BSA, 0.2 % Triton, 0.5 % normal goat serum in PBS). The secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit Alexa fluor 568/ 488 (Life technologies #A11011-A/A-11034) diluted at 1:500 in wash buffer was added to the single IF and goat anti-rabbit Alexa fluor 488 (Life technologies # A-11034)/Mouse Alexa fluor Cy5 (Life Technologies #A-10524) secondaries were added to the dual IF and the chambers and the slides incubated for 2 h at RT.The slides were then washed in wash buffer and TrueView autofluorescence quencher (Vector # SP-8400–15) was added to the slides and incubated for 5 min at RT. The slides were washed in wash buffer. Sytox green counterstain was added to the BrDU stained slides. Spectral DAPI was added to the NPM1, NPM1/HepPar 1 and NPM1/Ki67 stained slides and the slides incubated for 5 min. The slides were washed in PBS and mounted using antifade mountant (Vectashield, Vector laboratories # H-1000).

The IF-stained slides were imaged using a Zeiss Axiovert 200 M.

Image analysis

An ImageJ algorithm was used to analyse single IF stained rat and human LiMTs to calculate the total number of cells in each LiMT (DAPI positive) and the total number of cells that expressed NPM1 (Alexa fluor 488 positive). The algorithms convert the IF stained LiMT images to grey scale and then identifies cells pixel intensities above set thresholds. To remove any false positive results due to background staining, the threshold values were determined from primary negative stained LiMT images. The cells are first identified in the DAPI channel using a Gaussian blur filter that identifies the nuclei. Following this, the algorithms either classify the nucleus/cell as NPM1 positive (above threshold in the Alexa fluor 488 channel) or NPM1 negative (below threshold in the Alexa fluor 488 channel).

The hepatocyte labelling index was determined using an ImageJ algorithm which calculates the percentage of BrDU positively stained hepatocyte nuclei relative to total hepatocyte nuclei as follows: Cell counts were based on detection of nuclear staining by the nuclear fluorescent dye Sytox green, each image was thresholded in the Sytox green channel, and quantitation of selected nuclear objects (Sytox green stained) was performed based on size, excluding objects too small or large for hepatocyte nuclei, using filters designed to satisfy the following criteria:

a. Hepatocytes were counted excluding non-hepatocyte cells.e.g., Kupffer cells;

b. Binucleate cells stained positively for BrdU were counted as one cell (not two cells);

c. Small non-hepatocyte nuclei BrdU-labelling were not counted.

Thresholded and selected nuclei were counted to deliver BrdU labelling index (LI%) for rat or human hepatocytes using the following equation:

LI (%) = 100 x (Number of BrDU Labelled Hepatocyte Nuclei)/(Total Hepatocyte Nuclei in spheroid section) %.

Results

Cell viability analysis

ATP levels in the rat and human LiMTs were similar to control in all of the PB treatments indicating that the PB treatments did not cause any acute cytotoxicity to the liver microtissues, (Supplementary Table 1).

Hepatocyte proliferation analysis

PB treatment for 48 h caused a dose-dependent increase in bromodeoxyuridine (BrDU) labelling (LI%) in rat but not human LiMTs, and there were also significant increases in LI% following PB treatment of rat but not human LiMTs at the 72 and 96 h time points (Supplementary Table 2). Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) treatment caused significant increases in LI% in both rat and human LiMTs, indicating that hepatocytes from both species can proliferate in response to this growth factor.

Whole genome transcriptomic analysis

We performed global microarray transcriptional analysis at 24, 48, 72 and 96 hr across a dose range (500, 750 and 1000 μM) and identified several other cell proliferation genes that were significantly induced in the rat LiMTs in a dose-dependent manner along with NPM1 at one or more of these time points, (Supplementary Table 3). PB treatment caused dose-dependent increases in expression of Npm1, Nup205, Krt18, Ube2i, Arpp19, BanF1, Psmd2, Creb3, Plcb1, Psmd14, Rbbp8, and Wdr6 in rat LiMTs and either dose-dependent decreases or no dose dependent change to these genes in human LiMTs. Apex1, App and Cebpa were dose-dependently induced by PB treatment in both rat and human LiMTs at one or other of the time points.

Dual IF staining for NPM1 and HepPar1, a rat hepatocyte cytoplasmic marker, showed that nuclear NPM1 staining was located in hepatocytes (Fig. 2 C middle panel). Discussion

In this study we have identified induction of nuclear NPM1 expression as an early event in PB-induced rat hepatocyte cell proliferation. We have also identified several other cell proliferation genes induced by PB in rat liver microtissues and found that these genes are for the most part repressed in human liver microtissues that do not proliferate in response to PB. This data provides new insights into potential mechanisms underlying species differences between rats and humans at this level.

The induction of rat hepatocyte cell proliferation by PB is known to be a transient response, disappearing after 8–14 days post treatment. The data here shows that the largest NPM1 response to PB was seen at the earliest time points (24/48 hrs) and then tapered off at 96 hr (data not shown) which suggests that this response is also transient in nature. This result is consistent with in vivo data showing that the proliferation response to PB is also transient in nature [8].

Our immunostaining analysis with the hepatocyte marker HepPar1 showed that nuclear NPM1 expression induced by PB treatment in rat but not human LiMTs occurred in hepatocytes and was also colocalized with nuclear Ki67 expression, consistent with our BrDU labelling data showing a significant increase in labelling index (LI%) in rat but not human hepatocytes.

NPM1 induces tumour cell proliferation by promoting the expression of PRDX6, an antioxidant protein, inhibiting intracellular reactive oxygen species levels [23], while knocking out PRDX6 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells has been shown to prevent cell proliferation [24]. These findings align with our own data that also include significantly increased PRDX6 protein expression [25] (data not shown: FC=1.9, p = 0.02) in the rat but not in the human liver microtissues. It is notable that NPM1 also signals in an axis with Yes-associated protein (YAP), a gene previously shown to be pivotal for CAR-mediated induction of proliferation in primary mouse hepatocytes [26], by regulating YAP nuclear translocation [27].

In conclusion we have identified the cell proliferation regulating genes NPM1 and PRDX6, together with several other cell proliferation ‘signature’ genes, as being induced by PB treatment in rat but not human hepatocytes in a 3D liver microtissue model. Consequently these genes could be biomarkers of this CAR-mediated response, used for evidence-based risk assessment in mode of action (MOA) studies to understand the human relevance of this procarcinogenic response observed in rodent studies.
