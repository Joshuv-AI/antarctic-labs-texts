Effect of ultrasonic-assisted hot-air drying on the quality and drying kinetics of Taraxacum mongolicum
Authors: Ming-Hui Wei, Li Yang, Xue-Qing Fan, Xue-Meng Zhou, Yang Yang, Yu-Cheng Zhou, Jing-Jing Chen, Chun-Juan Pu, Wen-Zheng Shi, Sheng-Wei Xiao, Pei-Dong Chen, Gui-Sheng Zhou, Chao Li, Hui Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Taraxacum mongolicum Hand.-Mazz (TMHM), a member of the Asteraceae family, is characterized by its considerable species diversity and widespread distribution across China, Europe, and North America [1]. Studies have shown that TMHM contains significant concentrations of phenolic acids and polysaccharides, which exhibit antioxidant capacity and anti-inflammatory properties [1], [2]. Furthermore, TMHM is rich in diverse nutrients, including vitamin C, carotene, amino acids, and essential trace elements [3]. Regular consumption of TMHM can supplement various compounds often deficient in modern diets [3], [4]. With the growth of the health product industry, TMHM has emerged as an important raw material for functional foods such as wines, candies, beverages, and coffee [4]. There has been growing domestic and international demand for TMHM. However, fresh TMHM has a high moisture content (MC), typically ranging from 85% to 90%. This makes it highly susceptible to microbial spoilage, which results in a short shelf life, undesirable odor, and significant economic losses. Therefore, reducing MC through drying is essential for extending the storage time and improving product quality of TMHM.

Drying technology was proved to significantly reduce MC of TMHM, thereby inhibiting microbial proliferation, preventing spoilage, and enhancing product value [2]. Additionally, drying could mitigate the undesirable enzymatic browning chemical reactions. Various methods have been developed for drying TMHM, including shade drying (S-D), air drying (A-D), open sun drying (OS-D), vacuum drying (V-D), freeze drying (F-D), and hot air drying (HA-D). Among these, S-D, A-D, and OS-D were common in farms and small-scale operations due to their operational simplicity and low cost. However, OS-D was often heavily affected by weather, leading to low production efficiency and uneven quality. F-D preserved the original properties of the material by maintaining low temperature and vacuum conditions, thereby inhibiting microbial activity and enzyme action [5]. Nevertheless, its high energy consumption and equipment costs limited large-scale application. Similarly, V-D could enhance moisture removal under low oxygen conditions, preventing oxidation and accelerating drying [5], [6]. As a traditional method, HA-D offered better process control and was an economical and convenient approach in production. However, continuous hot air circulation could cause issues such as product shrinkage, oxidation, and significant loss of active components [6]. To address these limitations, pretreatment represented an important strategy to accelerate the HA-D process and produce high-quality dried products [7]. From the previous reports [8], [9], [10], various pretreatment techniques were integrated with HA-D, including boiling water blanching (Bw-HA-D), steam kill-green (Sk-HA-D), microwave (Mw-HA-D), and ultrasound (Us-HA-D). Although Bw-HA-D and Sk-HA-D could increase the drying rate and reduce browning, they often led to adhesion of leaves and stems, hindering heat and mass transfer [8], [11]. Mw-HA-D used electromagnetic waves to generate internal heat, offering high energy efficiency but potentially causing uneven heating [9]. Us-HA-D employed the mechanical waves to enhance moisture transfer, reducing drying time and energy consumption compared to other traditional methods [10]. These methods have been utilized for drying TMHM in both farm and industrial settings. However, the selection of a drying method often relied on empirical experience rather than a comprehensive scientific evaluation. More critically, there was currently no systematic study that compared these methods and assessed their impact on the overall quality profile of the dried TMHM. Previous studies on drying medicinal plants, including those on TMHM, have often focused on a single method or a limited set of quality indicators. This fragmented approach made it difficult to identify the optimal drying technique. The ideal method must simultaneously maximized the retention of bioactive compounds, preserved desirable color, and maintained acceptable drying efficiency. Consequently, a significant research gap existed in the identification of a scientifically-grounded, suitable drying method for TMHM based on a holistic assessment of multiple quality attributes. Drying could significantly affect the antioxidant content and activity, morphology, texture, and color of samples, which were the crucial factors determining consumer acceptance. Therefore, it was particularly important to compare the quality of the dried TMHM samples obtained through different drying methods and quickly screen out a suitable drying method.

Drying involved the coupled heat and mass transfer mechanisms, accompanied by physical and chemical changes, making process efficiency and product quality highly dependent on parameter control [12]. These complexities posed a challenge to the multivariate optimization of TMHM dehydration. Traditional “one-factor-at-a-time” and ‘‘full-factors’’ approaches were inefficient in detecting interactive effects among variables [13], [14]. Response surface methodology (RSM) was an effective statistical tool for modeling and optimizing multi-variable processes [15], and it was widely used to improve drying efficiency and product quality [13]. Therefore, RSM was employed to further optimize the drying process of the suitable drying method for TMHM in the present study.

The main objectives of this study were to: (1) systematically compare the effects of different drying methods (S-D, A-D, OS-D, V-D, F-D, HA-D, Bw-HA-D, Sk-HA-D, Mw-HA-D, and Us-HA-D) on the quality attributes of the dried TMHM and identify the most suitable drying method; (2) comprehensively evaluate the drying characteristics, rehydration capacity, microstructure, color, active component retention, and antioxidant activity of the dried TMHM using the selected suitable drying method; and (3) optimize the drying parameters of the suitable drying method using RSM to maximize product quality and drying efficiency. The findings would be expected to facilitate industrial-scale production of high-quality dried TMHM with the improved drying efficiency.

Materials and methods

Materials and methods

The chemical standards of monocaffeoyltartaric acid, chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, chicoric acid, ferulic acid, luteolin-7-O-glucoside and isoquercitrin were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The purity of these standards was over 98%. The chromatographic grade of methanol and formic acid were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay kit, hydroxyl free radical assay kit and 2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhy-drazyl (DPPH•) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The ultrapure water was prepared by a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, MA, USA). All other reagents were analytical pure.

Fresh TMHM samples were collected from Nanniwan Town (located at latitude 36°20′ N and longitude 109°44′ E) in Shaanxi province, China, and identified and authenticated as Taraxacum mongolicum Hand.-Mazz by the corresponding author, Prof. Hui Yan. TMHM was harvested at the end of October 2024. To ensure the authenticity and reliability of the collected samples, the basic quality testing was performed according to the microscopic and thin layer items for TMHM in Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition). Before use, all samples were picked manually to remove dust, sand, and other unwanted materials. Then, the samples were washed and cut into 2–3 cm segments, The average moisture content of the prepared samples at the beginning of the drying experiments was 82% (w.b.).

The half-thickness (H), a key parameter for the drying kinetics model, was directly measured. Specifically, the total thickness of thirty randomly selected fresh TMHM leaves (including both the blade and petiole segments) was measured at multiple locations using a SATA digital caliper (Model 91511, 0–150 mm range, Accuracy: ±0.01 mm). The average total thickness was calculated to be 2.0 mm. Hence, a half-thickness value of H = 1.0 mm was used for the calculation of the effective moisture diffusivity.

The processes of different drying methods and the selection of suitable drying technology

To identify a more suitable method for the efficient drying of TMHM, ten different drying methods were initially evaluated and screened, with each method undergoing three parallel experiments. In each parallel experiment, TMHM samples weighing 80 ± 0.5 g were randomly selected and subjected for drying. All sample groups were uniformly spread in a single layer on straight-edged mesh carrier trays constructed from 304 stainless steel with consistent dimensional specifications. All drying equipment utilized in this study was housed in a humidity- and temperature-controlled laboratory. The operational parameters for each drying method were established based on the results derived from the preliminary experimental investigations. In accordance with the standards of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition), the drying endpoint was determined through a defined monitoring scheme. During the rapid water-loss phase, samples were inspected at 2-hour intervals. The monitoring frequency was increased to hourly once the samples became rigid and fragile and fractured upon gentle bending, indicating the transition to the slow water-loss phase. At this phase, the moisture content was immediately and quantitatively measured using a LC-DHS-10A rapid moisture analyzer (Licheng Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with a readability of ± 0.05% and an accuracy of 0.001 g. The drying process was terminated only when the instrument confirmed that the moisture content had reached or fallen below the safe level of 13% (w.b.). The post-drying specimens were vacuum-sealed and stored in food-grade preservation bags, which exhibited the superior barrier performance against gases and moisture. For some drying methods involving thermal treatment, the dried samples were immediately transferred to the sealed glass desiccators containing color-changing silica gel at the bottom, and were cooled to room temperature prior to vacuum packaging and storage. In the current study, a comparative analysis were carried out to evaluate the effects on dried TMHM achieved through the application of ten distinct drying methods. Ten distinct drying methods were applied and compared in this study, which are concisely listed as follows:

(1) Shade-drying (S-D): Drying in a shaded, ventilated environment at an average ambient temperature of 20 ± 2℃.

(2) Air-drying (A-D): Drying in a climate-controlled chamber at 18 − 20°C with forced-air circulation at 1.2 ± 0.1 m/s.

(3) Open-sun-drying (OS-D): Drying under direct sunlight at an average temperature of 30 ± 3°C with systematic sample rotation.

(4) Vacuum drying (V-D): Drying in a vacuum oven at 55 ± 0.5°C under an absolute pressure of −0.085 MPa.

(5) Freeze drying (F-D): Drying by pre-freezing at −80°C and subsequent sublimation under a vacuum pressure of 1.0 Pa.

(6) Hot air drying (HA-D): Convective drying in an oven at 55 ± 0.5°C with an air velocity of 1.5 m/s.

(7) Blanching pretreated HA-D (Bw-HA-D): Pretreatment with boiling water blanching (60 s) followed by HA-D.

(8) Microwave pretreated HA-D (Mw-HA-D): Pretreatment with microwave irradiation (800 W, 60 s) followed by HA-D.

(9) Steam kill-green pretreated HA-D (Sk-HA-D): Pretreatment with steam (60 s) followed by HA-D.

(10) Ultrasound pretreated HA-D (Us-HA-D): Pretreatment in an ultrasonic bath (40 kHz, 30 min) followed by HA-D.

The specific operational procedures for each method were provided in Sections 1.1–––1.10 of the Supporting Information

The evaluation indicators for the comprehensive scoring were selected to provide a balanced assessment of both process efficiency and key product quality attributes. Drying time was chosen as a direct measure of processing efficiency and energy consumption. Regarding product quality, the color parameters were critical visual attributes that influenced consumer acceptance and indicated the extent of thermal degradation. Most importantly, the contents of individual and total phenolic acids were selected as the primary bioactive compounds responsible for the medicinal value of TMHM. A comprehensive evaluation methodology was employed to screen the optimal drying method for TMHM, standardizing the diverse metrics and eliminating discrepancies among the tested ten methods. The procedure followed established literature [16], wherein the comprehensive scores (CS) were calculated using Eq. (1):(1)CS=C1W1+C2W2+······+C12W12+C13W13

where C1 − C13 normalized values for chicoric acid content, monocaffeoyltartaric acid content, chlorogenic acid content, caffeic acid content, ferulic acid content, luteolin-7-O-glucoside content, isoquercitrin content, total phenolic acids content, L* value, a* value, b* value, total color difference (ΔE), and drying time. To enable a fair comparison across these diverse indicators, the original data were normalized to a [0,1] scale using the Min-Max normalization method which were described in Section 3.1 of Supporting Information. The corresponding weights W1 − W13 were determined through the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) [17], as detailed in Section 3.2 of Supporting Information. In brief, a panel of experts performed pairwise comparisons of the criteria, and the resulting weights were derived from a judgment matrix that passed the consistency check (CR < 0.10). The final weights were defined as 1.918, 1.492, 1.492, 0.639, 0.213, 0.213, 0.213, 1.492, 0.639, 1.066, 0.213, 1.705, and 1.705, respectively.

Drying characteristics

Determination of moisture content

The dry basis moisture content (M) at each time point was calculated according to Eq. (2) [7]:(2)M=mt-m0m0

where M was the moisture content (g water / g dry matter) at any time, mt was the mass of the sample (g) at the moment of t (min), and m0 was the mass of absolutely dried sample (g).

Determination of moisture ratio

The drying process was investigated by examining how the moisture ratio (MR) of TMHM changed over time. The MR was determined by Eq. (3) [18]:(3)MR=mt-mem0-me

where mt, me, and m0 was the moisture content at any time (g water / g dry matter), the equilibrium moisture content (g water / g dry matter), and the initial moisture content (g water / g dry matter), respectively. The equilibrium moisture content was determined by drying TMHM samples to constant weight under specific treatment conditions, with the absolute dry mass verified by oven-drying at 105°C.

Determination of drying rate

The drying rate (DR) was defined as the moisture loss rate per unit time during the drying process, expressed in units of g / (g·min). This parameter could be calculated using the relationship established in Eq. (4) [19].(4)DR=mt1-mt2t1-t2

where mt1 and mt2 were the moisture contents at t1 and t2, respectively.

Determination of effective moisture diffusivity

The effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) of the sample was modeled using Fick's second law [20], [21], as represented by Eq. (5).(5)lnMR=ln(8π2)-π2tDeffH2

The formula (5) revealed that the natural logarithm of the moisture ratio (lnMR) for TMHM exhibited a linear relationship with drying time (t). Consequently, by determining the slope (k) of the linear plot obtained from Eq. (5), the quantitative relationship between Deff and k could be established as shown in Eq. (6).(6)Deff=-kH2π2

In the above Eq. (5), (6), MR, t, H, and Deff were the moisture ratio, the drying time, the half-thickness of the sample within the infinite slab model, and the effective moisture diffusivity, respectively. The value of H (1.0 mm) was determined experimentally as described in Section 2.1. The parameter k represented the absolute value of the slope derived from the linear regression of lnMR against drying time t.

Color profiles

The observed color changes were attributed to the biochemical and chemical reactions during drying process. The reaction rates were significantly influenced by processing parameters and drying methodologies [30]. The surface color parameters of TMHM were measured at room temperature using a calibrated chroma extractor (CM-5, KONICA MINOLTA, Tokyo, Japan), with the fresh TMHM sample serving as reference specimens. To minimize systematic errors, six replicate measurements were taken for each sample. The total color difference (ΔE), chroma (C), and browning index (BI) were calculated to assess sample appearance by the following equations [[27], [31]]:(14)ΔE=L∗-L0∗2+a∗-a0∗2+b∗-b0∗2(15)C=a∗2+b∗2(16)BI=100x-0.310.172(17)x=a+1.75L∗5.645L∗+a∗-3.012a∗

Where the subscribed L*, a*, and b* with 0 represented the values of the fresh sample, and the non-subscribed were those of the drying treated sample.

Rehydration rate characteristics

The rehydration rate (RR) was a key quality indicator for the dried products [31]. In this study, the RR was determined according to the previous method [32] with minor modifications. Briefly, 5.0 ± 0.1 g of TMHM sample was immersed in 100 mL of distilled water at 25°C. At 10-min intervals, the sample was removed from the water, and the surface moisture was gently blotted with the filter paper. The sample was then weighed and immediately returned to the same container for continued rehydration. This process was repeated until two consecutive mass measurements remained constant, indicating that equilibrium was reached. All measurements were conducted in triplicate. The RR was calculated using the following equation:(18)RR=WtWd

where Wt represented the mass of the sample (g) at any rehydration time t (min), and Wd represented the absolute dry mass (g) of the sample, which was determined by drying the sample to a constant weight at 105°C after the rehydration test.

In the present study, the Peleg model was employed to fit the rehydration dynamics of the dried TMHM samples, which was assessed using the following equation:(19)Mt=M0+tk1+k2t

where M0 and Mt represented the moisture content (d.b.) at the beginning of rehydration and at any moment (t) during rehydration process, respectively. Furthermore, k1 denoted the rate constant, and k2 was the constant asymptotic moisture level.

Microstructure analysis

The surface and cross-sectional microstructural features of the dried TMHM samples were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi SU8020, Tokyo, Japan) at x500, x1000, and x2000 magnification, respectively. The dried TMHM leaves were carefully sectioned into 1-mm thick transverse slices using a precision blade under sterile conditions. The prepared samples were then affixed to the aluminum stubs with double-sided conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a 10-nm thick gold layer using a Hitachi MC1000 ion sputter coater (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to enhance surface conductivity. All the samples were stored in a desiccator prior to imaging to prevent moisture absorption, and all the SEM images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV under high vacuum conditions.

Magnetic resonance imaging analysis

The internal water distribution within TMHM sample was visualized using a benchtop magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system (MesoMR23-060H-I, Niumag Electric Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) equipped with a 0.5 T permanent magnet and a 60 mm diameter radiofrequency coil. All measurements were conducted at a controlled temperature of 32°C. A multi-echo sequence was employed for image acquisition with the following optimized parameters: the spectral bandwidth (SW) of 100 kHz, the offset frequency (O1) set to 232,246 Hz, 90° radiofrequency pulse duration (RFA90) of 2.6 μ s, and 180° radiofrequency pulse duration (RFA180) of 3.9 μ s. The imaging protocol comprised a repetition time of 200 ms, echo time of 16 ms, and a single-slice acquisition mode with 5 mm slice thickness. Data were collected in a 256 × 256 matrix format with four scan averages to enhance signal-to-noise ratio.

Determination of the characteristic phenolic acid analogues of TMHM extracts

The analytical method was developed for the quantitative analysis of seven classical phenolic acid analogues in TMHM using high-performance liquid chromatography equipped with a diode array detector (HPLC-DAD). Due to the space limit, the technical details including standard solution preparation, sample preparation, HPLC-DAD conditions, and methodological validation results were provided in 2.1, 2.2, 2.3 of Supporting Information.

Determination of the antioxidant capacity of TMHM extracts

For the preparation of TMHM extracts, the dried TMHM samples (0.5 g) were precisely weighed and homogenized with 10 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol solution. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 20 min. Following centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was carefully collected and diluted to a final volume of 10 mL with 80% methanol in a volumetric flask. The resulting extract solution was stored at −20°C until further analysis.

The antioxidant capacity of TMHM extracts was systematically evaluated through three validated spectrophotometric methods: the FRAP assay [33], the DPPH• radical scavenging assay [[6], [34]], and the hydroxyl radical scavenging assay [34]. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) under standardized conditions.

For the FRAP assay, 0.1 mL of TMHM extract was combined with 3.0 mL of the freshly prepared FRAP working solution. The reaction mixture was incubated at 25°C for 10 min in the dark, and the absorbance was immediately measured at 590 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. In the DPPH• assay, 0.2 mL of TMHM extract was mixed with 3.8 mL of DPPH• solution. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated at 25°C for 30 min. The absorbance values were recorded at 517 nm. For hydroxyl radical scavenging evaluation, a Fenton reaction system was employed. The reaction mixture contained 0.5 mL of TMHM extract, 1.0 mL of FeSO4 (6 mM), 0.5 mL of H2O2 (6 mM), and 1.0 mL of salicylic acid (6 mM). After incubation at 37°C for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The control groups containing 80% methanol were simultaneously analyzed for all assays.

Experimental design for optimization of the screened Us-HA-D for TMHM

In comparison with other drying methods, Us-HA-D could simultaneously improve the product color, shorten the drying time, enhance the moisture diffusivity, increase the content of phenolic acid analogues, and strengthen the antioxidant activity of the dried TMHM. Therefore, based on the results of Section 2.2, Us-HA-D was selected as the suitable drying method for TMHM in the further study.

To further refine the product quality during the dehydration, RSM was employed to optimize the drying process of Us-HA-D. Based on previous studies, the process of Us-HA-D was influenced by several factors, including hot-air temperature, hot-air velocity, ultrasound power, and ultrasound time. The preliminary single-factor experiments were systematically conducted to evaluate the individual effects of each parameter on both the final moisture content and total phenolic acid content of TMHM. Specifically, hot-air temperature (35-75°C), hot-air velocity (1–5 m/s), ultrasound power (300–800 W), and ultrasound time (10–60 min) were investigated across their respective operational ranges. Each parameter was evaluated through independent trials to determine its primary impact on the drying efficiency and phenolic acids retention. Based on these preliminary findings, operational boundaries were established for each factor to construct RSM experimental design. The defined ranges of the independent variables were selected to ensure coverage of the parameter space while maintaining the practical relevance to the industrial drying conditions. This experimental matrix enabled the systematic investigation of factor interactions and their combined influence on the quality attributes of Us-HA-D treated TMHM.

RSM using a central composite design (CCD) was subsequently used to model and manage the relationships between the independent factors, including hot-air temperature, hot-air velocity, ultrasound power, and ultrasound time. The multiple response variables included cichoric acid content, chlorogenic acid content, monocaffeoyltartaric acid content, caffeic acid content, the total antioxidant capacity, the hydroxyl radical scavenging ability, DPPH activity, the drying time, and the color parameters L*, a*, and b*. The data from CCD were fitted to a polynomial model as expressed in Eq. (20):(20)Y=φ0+∑i=1Nφixi+∑i=1Nφiixi2+∑i=1N-1∑j=i+1Nφijxixj

where Y, [GRKSLP]0, [GRKSLP]i, [GRKSLP]ii and [GRKSLP]ij represented the dependent variable, intercept term, linear coefficient, squared coefficient and interaction coefficient, respectively. The adequacy of the model was assessed using F ratio, adjusted determination (Radj2), coefficient of determination (R2), and lack-of-fit test, accordingly.

The multi-response optimization was executed using the desirability function approach in Design-Expert 13.0 (Stat-Ease Inc., USA). This method converted individual response to the desirability values (0–1 scale) for the simultaneous optimization [13].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The data processing and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) for statistical analysis and Origin 2024 (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) for graphical representation. The significant differences among groups were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison test at a 5% significance level.

Results and discussion

Effects of different drying techniques on the quality indicators of TMHM

Color was recognized as a pivotal quality attribute for the dried TMHM due to its significant influence on the acceptance and marketability of consumers. As depicted in the photographic insets (Fig. S1), TMHM samples subjected to F-D, Us-HA-D, and HA-D exhibited a brighter green color compared to those processed using the other seven drying methods. In this study, the color parameters of dried TMHM samples were quantified using L* (lightness/darkness), a* (red/green), and b* (yellow/blue) values, alongside ΔE and BI. The elevated L* values and the reduced ΔE were established as the desirable criteria for assessing the color quality of edible dried products [35]. During drying, the enzymatic and non-enzymatic browning reactions typically resulted in the decreased L* levels and the increased a* and b* values.

The color values of fresh TMHM sample were recorded as L0* = 40.53 ± 7.74, a0* = −15.07 ± 3.26, and b0* = 29.20 ± 4.13, which served as the reference color values. In Fig. 1A, a visual comparison of these reference values with the data clearly demonstrated that notable differences in L*, a*, and b* values were observed among the dried TMHM samples using different methods, reflecting the variations in color characteristics. TMHM samples treated with Mw-HA-D and Sk-HA-D displayed the similar color properties, characterized by lower L* and higher a* and b* values, indicating darker appearances. The reduced lightness in these samples was likely due to higher temperatures and ample oxygen availability, which promoted the browning reactions, in agreement with the previous studies [36]. Additionally, the microwave drying generated the intense frictional heat from water molecules [6]. The elevated a* values in Mw-HA-D-treated samples were possibly a result of the enhanced enzymatic and non-enzymatic browning reactions, such as Maillard reaction, which produced reddish-brown hues [37]. Among all drying methods, F-D resulted in the highest L* and the lowest a* values, indicating superior color preservation, followed by Us-HA-D. These two methods likely minimized thermal exposure, thereby better maintaining the original color of fresh TMHM. The parameter ΔE, representing the total color difference relative to the fresh sample, was used to evaluate the magnitude of color change. As a general rule, lower ΔE values indicated less deviation from the fresh standard [18]. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the values of ΔE for the ten drying methods, in ascending order, were as follows: Us-HA-D < B-HA-D < Sk-HA-D < Mw-HA-D < A-D < HA-D < OS-D < S-D < F-D < V-D. The smallest ΔE was observed in Us-HA-D group, suggesting minimal overall color alteration, which was consistent with higher quality dried products presented the lower ΔE values in previous studies [[18], [32]]. However, the interpretation of ΔE required caution, as it did not convey the direction of change in individual color coordinates. A notable example was the F-D sample. Despite its high ΔE value, this resulted primarily from a significant increase in L* and a decrease in a* (Fig. 1A). For this specific material, such shifts were qualitatively associated with favorable color retention. Additionally, the significant variations in BI were detected among the drying methods (Fig. 1B). OS-D, Sk-HA-D, and Mw-HA-D exhibited notably higher BI values, which might be attributed to the heat-intensive processes that accelerated the enzymatic and/or non-enzymatic browning reactions [8].

As a functional food ingredient, the dried TMHM samples were utilized in wines, candies, energy drinks, and other health-promoting products [38]. TMHM possessed a wide variety of biological activities including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, and gastric-protective effects. These properties were largely ascribed to its phenolic constituents, such as chicoric acid, caffeic acid, monocaffeoyltartaric acid, isoquercitrin, ferulic acid, chlorogenic acid, and luteolin-7-O-glucoside [[38], [39], [40]]. Consequently, the contents of these phenolic compounds were selected as key evaluation indicators for screening optimal drying methods. A radar chart (Fig. 1C) illustrated the variations in the characteristic phenolic acid analogs and total phenolic acid contents across the dried TMHM samples. The significant disparities were observed among the drying methods. The total phenolic acids, chicoric acid, and monocaffeoyltartaric acid contents were relatively higher, while other phenolic acids showed lower levels. Among the different drying methods treated TMHM samples, F-D exhibited the highest contents of target phenolic acids, followed by Us-HA-D, both significantly outperforming the other drying methods. This was attributed to F-D maintaining samples in low-temperature and vacuum conditions, which inhibited aerobic microbial proliferation and enzymatic activity [6], thereby preserving phenolic compounds and preventing their degradation. Us-HA-D also demonstrated the superior retention of phenolic acids, primarily due to ultrasonic pretreatment inactivating enzymes and reducing enzyme-induced degradation [41]. Conversely, Mw-HA-D showed the lowest retention, likely due to microwave-induced high energy causing the decomposition of phenolic acid analogs [2]. It was concluded that F-D and Us-HA-D effectively inhibited degradation of total phenolic acids, resulting in the optimal retention in the dried TMHM samples.

The correlation coefficients (ranging from + 1 to −1) were used to quantify the relationships between variables. The positive coefficients indicated direct associations, while negative coefficients indicated inverse associations. The correlations among the phenolic acid analogues, and the color parameters of TMHM after drying (Fig. 1D) were analyzed to determine their relationship. The strong positive correlations were observed between L* and chicoric acid (r = 0.73, p ≤ 0.001), monocaffeoyltartaric acid (r = 0.71, p ≤ 0.001), chlorogenic acid (r = 0.73, p ≤ 0.001), ferulic acid (r = 0.74, p ≤ 0.001), luteolin-7-O-glucoside (r = 0.71, p ≤ 0.001), isoquercitrin (r = 0.56, p ≤ 0.01), and total phenolic acids (r = 0.73, p ≤ 0.001). Similarly, b* showed positive correlations with chicoric acid (r = 0.61, p ≤ 0.001), monocaffeoyltartaric acid (r = 0.71, p ≤ 0.001), chlorogenic acid (r = 0.70, p ≤ 0.001), ferulic acid (r = 0.65, p ≤ 0.001), luteolin-7-O-glucoside (r = 0.49, p ≤ 0.01), isoquercitrin (r = 0.44, p ≤ 0.05), and total phenolic acids (r = 0.70, p ≤ 0.001). Conversely, a* exhibited strong negative correlations with chicoric acid (r = -0.85, p ≤ 0.001), monocaffeoyltartaric acid (r = -0.90, p ≤ 0.001), chlorogenic acid (r = -0.83, p ≤ 0.001), ferulic acid (r = -0.89, p ≤ 0.001), luteolin-7-O-glucoside (r = -0.76, p ≤ 0.001), isoquercitrin (r = -0.43, p ≤ 0.05), and total phenolic acids (r = -0.90, p ≤ 0.001). The robust correlations were observed between a* and total phenolic acids (r = -0.90), which presented a very strong predictive link between color and phytochemical composition. This relationship was mechanistically justified by the role of phenolic compounds as primary substrates for enzymatic browning. During drying, higher retention of phenolic acids limited their oxidation by enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase, thereby inhibiting the formation of dark-colored pigments. This process resulted in a lighter color (higher L*), a more vibrant yellow hue (higher b*), and reduced the development of reddish-brown compounds (lower a*). Therefore, the color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were served as rapid and non-destructive proxies for assessing phenolic acid retention, and providing a valuable tool for the preliminary screening of optimal drying methods.

The suitable drying technology selection of TMHM based on comprehensive evaluation

The drying times of TMHM samples subjected to various drying methods were illustrated in Fig. 1E. The considerable variation in drying time was observed among the different drying methods, and the ten methods were ranked by drying times from shortest to longest as follows: Mw-HA-D < HA-D < Us-HA-D < Sk-HA-D < B-HA-D < V-D < OS-D < F-D < A-D < S-D. Notably, Mw-HA-D exhibited the shortest drying duration. This superior efficiency was attributed to the intense microwave pretreatment, which rapidly generated internal heat and vapor pressure, profoundly disrupting cellular structures and thereby drastically accelerating the subsequent drying [9]. In comparison, the ultrasonic pretreatment in Us-HA-D mainly created microchannels for water migration through cavitation to enhance drying. Although this mechanism was effective, it did not produce strong microwave internal heating and pressure. Consequently, the total drying time of Us-HA-D was 3 h longer than that of Mw-HA-D. As expected, the conventional HA-D process lacked any pretreatment to overcome the intrinsic limitations of slow heat and mass transfer, and was proved to be the slowest among the above three drying methods. The remaining other drying methods required substantially prolonged durations, largely due to the less efficient heat-medium exchange.

The analysis of the results indicated that the drying techniques had complex and interrelated effects on multiple quality attributes of the dried TMHM. To holistically assess these impacts, a comprehensive evaluation methodology was implemented to identify the optimal drying technique that could yield the high-quality dried TMHM products. This approach prioritized higher retention of phenolic acid analogues, lower browning degree in color appearance, and acceptable drying efficiency. The results of this comprehensive assessment, integrating both drying behavior and product quality metrics, were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 1F). The composite scores were calculated according to Eq. (1), as detailed in Section 2.2. A color gradient from purple (low score) to red (high score) was used to represent composite scores. Among all the evaluated drying technologies, Us-HA-D achieved the highest composite score (0.8536), followed by F-D (0.8106), B-HA-D (0.6921), HA-D (0.5921), S-D (0.5751), OS-D (0.5720), Sk-HA-D (0.3778), A-D (0.3692), Mw-HA-D (0.2139), and V-D (0.1686). The superior performance of Us-HA-D was attributed to its enhanced the color preservation, higher retention of phenolic acid analogues, and reduced drying duration. Consequently, Us-HA-D was selected as the suitable drying technology for the subsequent studies focusing on the comprehensive drying characteristic evaluation, process optimization, and multidimensional quality assessment.

A stacked bar chart (Fig. 1G) was utilized to further compare the phenolic acid profiles between the ultrasound-pretreated and conventional hot-air drying methods. The results clearly demonstrated that Us-HA-D significantly enhanced the retention of major phenolic acids in dried TMHM. Notably, Us-HA-D led to a predominant increase in the contents of chicoric acid, monocaffeoyltartaric acid, and chlorogenic acid, which were elevated by 1.408-, 1.417-, and 1.556- fold, respectively. Consequently, the total phenolic acid content was also significantly higher in Us-HA-D group (1.403-fold increase). These findings suggested that the ultrasonic pretreatment presented a distinctive effect on the preservation of phenolic acids. This result aligned with previous reports [[32], [42]], in which ultrasonic-assisted drying was shown to enhance the retention of phenolic metabolites.

A scatter plot (Fig. 1H) was used to illustrate the cumulative changes in the multidimensional quality indicators between Us-HA-D and HA-D. The two-way ANOVA with post-hoc multiple comparisons tests were employed to the statistical analysis for all quality indicators between the two groups. Statistical analysis revealed that Us-HA-D significantly up-regulated the levels of several key indicators including chicoric acid, chlorogenic acid, and BI (p < 0.0001), L (p < 0.001), as well as total phenolic acids, a*, and b* (p < 0.01). A moderate significant up-regulation was also observed for ferulic acid and monocaffeoyltartaric acid (*p < 0.05). In contrast, ΔE was significantly down-regulated (**p < 0.0001). No statistically significant changes were found for caffeic acid, luteolin-7-O-glucoside, and isoquercitrin (p > 0.05). These results demonstrate that Us-HA-D could robustly improve the overall quality of the dried TMHM by selectively enhancing the retention of major phenolic acids and favorably modifying color attributes.

The drying characteristics of the dried TMHM samples using the screened suitable Us-HA-D method

Drying kinetics curves

Drying kinetics was employed to investigate the relationship between dehumidification extent and process variables during drying, which were influenced by processing conditions including drying temperature, drying duration, and pre-treatment properties. Modelling the drying process was a critical component in drying method development [43]. The evaluation of drying kinetics facilitated the estimation of drying rates and optimization of process parameters. Based on the prior findings, Us-HA-D was determined as the suitable drying method, and was applied to reduce TMHM moisture content at a safe level of 13 ± 0.5% (d.b.), utilizing a factorial design with varying combinations of air temperature, air velocity, ultrasound power, and ultrasound duration. In the control experiment, TMHM samples were solely subjected to HA-D without ultrasonic pretreatment. By monitoring the dehydration process, the drying curves and drying rates were determined, thereby establishing drying kinetics useful for the equipment design and process optimization.

The drying characteristics of the pretreated TMHM are shown in Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B. The drying temperature, air velocity, ultrasonic duration, and ultrasonic power were all found to significantly affect the drying behavior. Initially, the effects of drying temperature (DT, 45-65°C) and air velocity (DS, 2–4 m/s) were investigated under the fixed ultrasound power (UP, 500 W) and duration (UT, 30 min). The drying curves (Fig. 2A) demonstrated that the moisture content reduction during Us-HA-D varied markedly with drying temperature and air velocity. The rapid moisture removal occurred early in the process, with over 70% of initial moisture eliminated within the first 60 min across all conditions, followed by a significant rate decline (p < 0.05). The drying times for HA-D at 45°C, 55°C, and 65°C were 347 min, 133 min, and 140 min, respectively, while for Us-HA-D, the corresponding times were 377 min, 160 min, and 113 min (Fig. 2A). A noteworthy finding was that the ultrasonic pretreatment prolonged the drying process at 45 and 55°C compared to HA-D alone. This phenomenon could be attributed to the water-absorbing nature of the ultrasonic pretreatment. During the ultrasonic water-bath treatment, TMHM samples likely absorbed a significant amount of water, thereby increasing their initial moisture load at the start of the hot-air drying stage. At the lower and medium temperatures, the driving force for moisture evaporation might be insufficient to efficiently overcome this additional water load, leading to a net increase in total drying time. In contrast, at the high temperature of 65°C, the enhanced thermal energy provided a sufficiently strong driving force to leverage the microchannels created by ultrasound, drastically improving internal moisture diffusion and resulting in a net reduction in drying time. Drying at 65°C required only 40.34% of the time needed at 45°C under HA-D, and a comparable duration was observed at 55°C. Under Us-HA-D, the drying at 65°C took only 29.97% of the time required at 45°C and 70.62% of that at 55°C. These results indicated that increasing the drying temperature in both methods shortened the drying time, which was attributed to the enhanced molecular energy and facilitated moisture evaporation [44]. The elevated temperatures raise the internal temperature of TMHM, accelerating the moisture migration from the interior to surface. Air velocity also influenced drying time (Fig. 2A). Generally, the higher air velocities shortened drying time at a given ultrasound power and duration. However, an unexpected increase in average drying time (from 147 min to 152 min) was observed when air velocity rose from 3 m/s to 4 m/s at the air temperature of 55°C and the ultrasound power of 500 W. The effects of ultrasound power and duration on drying characteristics were evaluated under fixed air temperature (55°C) and air velocity (3 m/s). Crucially, two independent experimental sets were conducted: one to compare ultrasound powers (400, 500, and 600 W) at a fixed duration of 30 min, and another to compare ultrasound durations (20, 30, and 40 min) at a fixed power of 500 W. The drying kinetics curves for these treatments are presented in Fig. 2B. The moisture content of fresh TMHM decreased rapidly in the first 30 min for all treatments, likely due to the rapid evaporation of the surface moisture [10]. Statistical analysis revealed that ultrasound power exerted a significant (p < 0.05) effect on the drying endpoint, whereas the main effect of ultrasound duration was not statistically significant. For the duration comparison the post-hoc groupings indicated that while the 30 min treatment showed a numerically superior result, the differences between the three durations were not statistically conclusive. This implied that the positive impact of ultrasound on moisture transfer, primarily through cavitation-induced microchannels [47], reached a plateau at this power setting. When the ultrasonic time was extended to 20 ∼ 30 min, the drying time was not significantly shortened, which was consistent with the research results that excessive ultrasound could lead to structural damage and increase the resistance of water migration. [10]. Post-hoc analysis for the power comparison indicated that applying 600 W led to a significantly lower final moisture content compared to both 400 W and 500 W. This result underscored that only the highest power level (600 W) provided a distinct advantage, reducing drying time by more than 14% and 23% compared to 400 W and 500 W, respectively. This enhancement was likely due to more intense cavitation and mechanical effects at higher power, which more effectively created microstructural channels for moisture migration [[44], [48]].

Drying rate curves

The relationship between DR and MR under varying drying conditions for Us-HA-D-processed TMHM was illustrated in Fig. 2C-E. According to the classical drying kinetics categorization, the drying process typically comprised three stages including the accelerating, constant-rate, and falling-rate phases [49]. In this study, the entire drying process was characterized by accelerated and decelerated phases, with no distinct constant-rate stage. This pattern aligned with findings from Yu et al. [50], who similarly reported an absence of a clear constant-rate phase in the ultrasonic-pretreated HA-D systems. The behavior of the drying curves in Fig. 2C-E could be interpreted as follows: during the initial drying stage, the drying medium (hot air) heated the TMHM, creating a significant humidity gradient between the material and drying medium. In the initial stage of drying, TMHM contained abundant free water, establishing a pronounced humidity gradient between the sample and the drying air. Driven by both temperature and humidity gradients, the moisture in TMHM moved from the interior to the surface and evaporated into the air. The moisture diffusing from within effectively replenished the surface losses of TMHM, resulting in a rapid increase in DR [51]. As the drying progressed, the internal moisture content decreased, reducing the humidity gradient and thus the driving force for moisture migration. Once the internal moisture transfer rate equaled the surface evaporation rate, DR reached its peak, marking the transition from the accelerating to the decelerating stage. The subsequent drying stages exhibited gradual DR reduction. In the final drying stages, the vacuolar moisture depletion and free water removal increased the evaporation difficulty, while the structural collapse of internal channels further impeded the moisture migration. Consequently, the internal diffusion rates fell below the surface evaporation rates, causing DR decline.

Notably, the ultrasound pretreatment increased the porosity of TMHM, creating more microchannels that improved the moisture migration, particularly during mid-to-late drying stages where migration resistance was highest, thereby boosting DR in HA-D-processed TMHM (Fig. 2D). Moreover, at the same temperature, DR of Us-HA-D was significantly higher than that of conventional HA-D (Fig. 2E). In summary, increasing DR was essential for achieving efficient and low-energy drying. The improved DR achieved with Us-HA-D for TMHM drying enhanced production efficiency, highlighting the broad application potential of Us-HA-D in the TMHM processing industry, particularly for high-efficiency production lines focused on high-quality products.

Effective moisture diffusion coefficient

Deff, a pivotal parameter quantifying internal moisture diffusion capacity within a sample, was intricately linked to DR and moisture migration dynamics during the drying process [49]. Deff was utilized to measure the dehydration capability of TMHM, with higher Deff values indicating that the material was more easily dried [52]. Fig. 3 depicted the relationship between ln(MR) and drying time for TMHM samples under varying the operational conditions of Us-HA-D and HA-D. The ln(MR) values exhibited a consistent decreasing trend with a negative slope across all plots. The R2 for the linear fitting of ln(MR) versus drying time ranged from 0.92256 to 0.97733, confirming a robust linear correlation for the two drying methods under diverse conditions. Deff values (×10-10 m2/s) for HA-D at air velocities of 2 m/s (Fig. 3A) and 4 m/s (Fig. 3B) were 1.73 and 2.72, respectively, while those at the temperatures of 45°C (Fig. 3C), 55°C (Fig. 3D), and 65°C (Fig. 3E) were 1.15, 2.72, and 2.59, respectively. For Us-HA-D under the identical conditions, the corresponding Deff values were 1.86 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3F), 2.36 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3G), 1.06 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3H), 2.16 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3I), and 3.05 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3J). When the ultrasound duration was varied, Deff values for Us-HA-D reached 1.79 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3K) at 20 min and 1.97 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3L) at 30 min. A systematic increase in Deff was observed with the rising ultrasound power, yielding the values of 2.48 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3M) at 400 W and 3.03 × 10−10 m2/s (Fig. 3N) at 600 W. These results were in agreement with the recommended range of 10−10 to 10−7 m2/s as reported by Ramos et al. [53]. Notably, Us-HA-D led to a significant enhancement in Deff at the elevated air temperatures and ultrasound powers compared to HA-D alone. For instance, at 65°C, Deff for Us-HA-D (3.05 × 10−10 m2/s) was approximately 18% higher than that for HA-D (2.59 × 10−10 m2/s), demonstrating the synergistic effect of ultrasound and heat on promoting moisture mobility. This observation was consistent with literature indicating that Deff was positively influenced by both thermal and ultrasonic effects [[29], [54]]. Analysis of microstructure and rehydration characteristics of the dried TMHM samples using the screened suitable Us-HA-D method

Microstructure analysis

Significant microstructural alterations occurred in the dried TMHM samples, both untreated and subjected to ultrasonic treatment, as depicted in Fig. 5A-I. To better understand the factors influencing drying behavior, the microstructural modifications were examined under varying the operational conditions of Us-HA-D and HA-D. We hypothesized that water diffusion and evaporation in TMHM associate with the microstructural changes induced by different drying methods. The surface morphology appeared relatively smooth and compact with only limited shallow pores for HA-D treated samples under the drying temperatures at 45°C (Fig. 5A), 55°C (Fig. 5B), and 65°C (Fig. 5C). Notably, the samples dried at 65°C displayed the increased surface cracking and cell collapse compared to the lower drying temperatures. This phenomenon was attributed to the rapid water evaporation at the elevated temperatures, generating the internal water and temperature gradients, which disrupted the void structure of TMHM [58]. These microstructural findings provided a compelling explanation for the anomalous drying kinetics observed in Section 3.3.3, where Deff at 55°C exceeded that at 65°C. The fused and collapsed surface morphology at 65°C (Fig. 5C) likely acted as a formidable barrier to moisture transport, effectively negating the benefit of the higher temperature. Conversely, the open structure at 55°C facilitated uninterrupted moisture egress. Therefore, it was concluded that beyond a critical temperature, the trade-off between increased molecular kinetic energy and destructive microstructural changes became negative effect, leading to an overall reduction in drying efficiency.Fig. 5Microstructure of TMHM in different drying processes. In contrast, the samples subjected to ultrasonic pretreatment (UP = 500 W, and UT = 30 min) at 45, 55, and 65°C exhibited the pronounced structural disruptions, including gaps, deformations, and cracks, particularly along the cell walls of adjacent cells (Fig. 5D–F). These changes were likely caused by ultrasound-induced cell damage, solute loss, and membrane rupture. The ultrasonic waves could disrupt the non-covalent bonds within cell wall polymers, while the free radicals generated during sonication weaken cell wall strength, leading to the increased intracellular space and irregular surface porosity [47]. This study observed ultrasound-induced micro-channels and cell wall disruptions were in strong agreement with the previous findings [59]. In their study on ultrasound-assisted drying of cumin seeds, they reported similar microstructural changes, such as the formation of micro-pores and the breakage of surface bristles, which facilitated moisture migration [59].

The cross-sectional analyses (Fig. 5G-I) further revealed that the samples without ultrasonic pretreatment maintained relatively intact structures with few pores across all temperatures. However, the ultrasonic pretreatment induced the visible cross-sectional holes and even the whole structural damage of TMHM, including cracks and peeling, which intensified with the rising drying temperatures (Fig. 5J-L). The enhanced mechanical vibrations and pressure fluctuations from ultrasound and heat at the higher temperatures could exacerbate cell membrane rupture and structural damage [[44], [49]]. The microstructure provided the complementary validation for DR differences between Us-HA-D and HA-D. The increased porosity and structural disruption in ultrasonic-treated samples facilitated the moisture diffusion and evaporation of TMHM, thereby explaining the accelerated DR in Us-HA-D compared to HA-D. This microstructural perspective elucidated the mechanistic basis for drying efficiency variations across different treatment methods.

Rehydration rate analysis

The RR was defined as the proportion of moisture lost during processing or storage that could be reabsorbed through replenishment or humidification. It served as a key indicator of the product’s ability to retain or regain moisture, which was critical in food processing for maintaining texture, shelf life, appearance, and nutritional quality that directly influence consumer acceptance [44]. The rehydration curves of TMHM are shown in Fig. 6. During the soaking process, water migrated from the exterior to the interior of TMHM, with the absorption rate being rapid initially and gradually slowing over time. As shown in Fig. 6A and Fig. 6D, the rehydration curves for the two drying methods exhibited upward trends with increasing drying temperature. At 65°C, both Us-HA-D and HA-D demonstrated the slower attainment of maximum equilibrium moisture content, indicating that the elevated temperatures enhanced the material’s capacity to absorb water. However, Us-HA-D treated samples consistently exhibited lower RR values compared to HA-D under the identical drying temperatures. This phenomenon was linked to irreversible cellular structure damage observed in the above SEM images, attributed to the combined effects of ultrasound power (500 W) and duration (30 min) that caused the severe shrinkage and collapse. The severe shrinkage and structural collapse not only reduced the porosity and micro-channels necessary for capillary force to drive water penetration, but also potentially promoted the leaching and surface deposition of hydrophilic components, thereby reducing the rehydrating ability of the material. These components, such as pectins and polysaccharides, could form a denser, less hydrophilic surface layer upon drying, which further impeded water ingress. The similar phenomenon was reported in the previous studies [[51], [60]].Fig. 6The rehydration ratio curves of TMHM from HA-D and Us-HA-D with different drying temperatures (A); different air velocities (B); and different ultrasound conditions (D). In HA-D treated samples, the highest RR was achieved at an air velocity of 2 m/s, while the lowest occurred at 4 m/s (Fig. 6B and Fig. 6E). This was likely due to the surface hardening caused by rapid moisture evaporation at the higher air velocities, which impeded water penetration during rehydration. Interestingly, ultrasound pretreatment could markedly improve RR of Us-HA-D treated samples dried at 4 m/s, possibly because ultrasonic disruption mitigated the surface hardening and promoted a more porous structure of TMHM. Beyond the drying temperature and air velocity, the ultrasound power and duration were also identified as key factors influencing RR. The highest RR was observed at UT = 20 min, followed by 30 min and 40 min (Fig. 6C and Fig. 6F). This result suggested that the prolonged ultrasonic exposure initially increased surface voids and structural changes, enhancing rehydration. However, the excessive ultrasound duration caused irreversible cellular damage that reduced RR [44]. The ultrasound power showed relatively minor effects due to the stable cross-linked structure of cellulose, hemicellulose, and polysaccharides in TMHM, which limited water absorption despite partial cell wall disruption. The previous studies confirmed that the ultrasound pretreatment positively influenced RR in dried foods processed via Us-HA-D [[51], [61]].

The rehydration kinetics were modeled using the Peleg equation, with the results presented in Table S2 and Fig. 6D-F. The model fitness was assessed based on R2 values close to 1, along with low χ2, MSE, and RMSE values. The constant K1, related to initial mass transfer rate (t = 0), showed that smaller K1 values corresponded to higher initial rehydration efficiency. The constant K2 related to the equilibrium moisture content, with smaller values corresponding to higher moisture contents at equilibrium during rehydration. Regarding the effects of temperature, both excessively high and low drying temperatures reduced K1. Among the samples dried at 55°C, the highest K1 values of HA-D and Us-HA-D were 5.35059 and 5.55941, respectively. At the identical temperatures, the ultrasound-pretreated groups consistently showed higher K1 values than non-treated groups. This phenomenon indicated a slower initial water absorption rate, which aligned with the aforementioned structural damage (severe shrinkage and collapse) induced by the relatively high ultrasound intensity (500 W, 30 min). Although ultrasound could create microporosity under these specific conditions, the overall deterioration of the cellular infrastructure appeared to be the dominant factor, hindering water penetration and leading to the observed lower rehydration efficiency. Compared with the other drying air velocities, the drying air velocity was set at 2 m/s which obtained the lowest K1 and K2. Among ultrasound parameters, UP = 600 W and UT = 20 min groups displayed the minimal K1. The capacity constant K2 peaked in the drying temperature of 55°C for HA-D (K2 = 0.26159) and Us-HA-D (K2 = 0.26159), while other conditions resulted in K2 within a narrow range (0.19148–––0.23568), consistent with studies linking K2 to the composition and rehydration conditions of material.

The fresh TMHM was characterized by high and uniform water distribution throughout the tissue (Fig. 7A, and Fig. 7B). Notably, ultrasonic pretreatment led to a broader dispersion of moisture signals (evident as more extensive green/yellow areas in Fig. 7B compared to Fig. 7A), indicating the facilitated moisture redistribution prior to drying. During drying, the surface layer of TMHM, being most exposed to the hot drying medium, firstly experienced the rapid evaporative water loss. This created a concentration gradient between the surface and interior of TMHM, driving moisture migration from inward to outward. Consequently, the internal moisture content decreased, accompanied by the reduced redness values. The progressive dehydration induced the structural contraction and gradual reduction in area of TMHM. The proton density plots revealed the heightened resistance to moisture transfer within TMHM, complicating the further water removal.

Under the equivalent drying durations, the proton density images of Us-HA-D processed TMHM exhibited significantly smaller area and lower redness values compared to HA-D (Fig. 7C and Fig. 7D). This disparity was attributed to ultrasound pretreatment enhancing the internal vibrational energy, which improved moisture migration rates and accelerated the drying process. The application of Us-HA-D was demonstrated to markedly enhance dehydration efficiency and augment moisture diffusion kinetics. These findings underscored the efficacy of ultrasonic-assisted drying in optimizing moisture removal during TMHM processing.

Experimental design for optimization of the screened Us-HA-D for TMHM

Single factor test

Based on the aforementioned results, Us-HA-D was selected as the appropriate drying method for TMHM. According to previous studies, the performance and product quality of Us-HA-D were highly dependent on the control of critical parameters, including air temperature (A), air velocity (B), ultrasound power (C), and ultrasound duration (D), to maximize drying efficiency and preserve product integrity [[10], [50]]. Given the complex nature of coupled heat and mass transfer during drying, optimization of these parameters was considered essential to achieve efficient and energy-conscious dehydration. Consequently RSM was subsequently employed to further optimize Us-HA-D process for TMHM. Chicoric acid was recognized as both the major active ingredient and a key quality marker of TMHM in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition). Chlorogenic acid, monocaffeoyltartaric acid, and caffeic acid were reported to act as essential biosynthetic precursors to chicoric acid [63]. The retention of these phenolic compounds during drying was regarded as critical, given their widespread presence in TMHM and their importance in functional food applications, particularly due to their well-documented antioxidant properties [1], [2]. These compounds showed inactivate pro-oxidants and scavenge free radicals, owing to their distinctive chemical structures [38]. Therefore, this study selected the contents of these phenolic acid analogues, along with the antioxidant activity of the dried TMHM, as key response variables for process optimization. Additionally, since drying time served as a critical indicator for evaluating the efficiency of drying methods, it was also selected as a key response variable. As previously mentioned, the color of a dried product represented a crucial and appealing attribute that significantly influenced the consumer acceptance. Therefore, the colorimeter values (L*, a*, and b*) of the dried TMHM were regarded as another set of important response variables for optimizing Us-HA-D process.

The experimental ranges for all independent variables were established according to the preliminary experiments and the previous reports [50]. The independent variables included A (400–––600 W), B (20–––40 min), C (45–––65°C), and D (2–––4 m/s). The dependent responses were designated as follows: cichoric acid content (Y1), chlorogenic acid content (Y2), monocaffeoyltartaric acid content (Y3), caffeic acid content (Y4), the total antioxidant capacity (Y5), the hydroxyl radical scavenging ability (Y6), DPPH activity (Y7), the drying time (Y8), and the color parameters L* (Y9), a* (Y10), and b* (Y11). Effect on the drying time

The experimental values of Y8 were observed to span a range from 150 to 700 min across different variable combinations (Table 3). The acquired results underwent evaluation against various mathematical models, encompassing linear, two-factor interaction, quadratic, and cubic models. The model performance assessments revealed that the quadratic model emerged as the most suitable representation of the experimental data for Y8. Specifically, R2 and R2adj values of the quadratic model were calculated as 0.9794 and 0.9589, respectively. Furthermore, the lack-of-fit test for the quadratic model of Y8 yielded an insignificant p-value of 0.5669, indicating that the selected quadratic model represented the optimal mathematical framework for predicting the impact of Us-HA-D conditions on Y8. Additionally, the ANOVA analysis revealed an F-value of 47.64 and a p-value < 0.0001, further confirming the significance of the response surface quadratic model in predicting Y8 (Table 4).

According to the developed model, the shorter drying times were generally associated with higher air velocity, irrespective of the specific hot-air drying conditions (Fig. S2C). In contrast, a gradual increase in Y8 was observed when air velocity was reduced at constant air temperature. This behavior could be explained by cooling effect induced by airflow, which became more pronounced at higher velocities. As reported in the literature, the increased air velocity could enhance surface cooling, thereby lowering the temperature at the product surface and reducing moisture diffusivity [45]. Additionally, the drying time was notably reduced at the elevated air temperatures, which could be attributed to the enhanced driving force for heat and mass transfer under such conditions [44]. It was also evident from the model that the decreasing trend of Y8 with rising temperature was more pronounced than the decrease associated with air velocity.

Validation of the optimized process

To systematically optimize Us-HA-D process, the desirability function approach was employed for the simultaneous optimization of multiple responses [64]. This method transformed each response (Y1-Y11) into an individual desirability value (di) that ranged from 0 (completely undesirable) to 1 (fully desirable). The geometric mean of these individual desirability values was then calculated to obtain a global desirability (GD), which served as a comprehensive objective function for optimization. The goal was to maximize the value of GD, thereby identifying the process parameters that best balance all responses. The optimization targets included maximizing Y1, Y2, Y3, Y4, Y5, Y6, Y7, Y9, and Y11 while minimizing Y8 and Y10.

The credibility of this optimization process relied on the predictive capability and reliability of the underlying regression models. As summarized in Table 4, the quadratic models for most key responses (including Y1, Y2, Y5, Y8, Y9, Y10 and Y11) exhibited high statistical significance, with p < 0.0001 for model terms, and a non-significant lack-of-fit with p > 0.05. This result indicated that these models effectively fit the experimental data without significant unexplained variance. Furthermore, the high adjusted coefficients of determination (R2adj > 0.83 for these models except Y4 and Y6) provided strong evidence that the models captured most of the variation in the responses, ensuring high predictive accuracy. It was on this foundation of statistically robust models that the optimal solution derived from the desirability function was deemed highly reliable.

The optimal values for the key parameters were identified as follows: A was set at 535.356 W, B at 30.388 min, C at 60.212°C, and D at 3.775 m/s. Under these optimized conditions, the predicted values for Y1 − Y11 were 1.696%, 0.036%, 0.232%, 0.001%, 47.911 μmol Trolox/g, 34.993%, 57.156%, 150.44 min, 44.536, −7.245, and 29.120, respectively. To evaluate the validity and accuracy of the regression equation, three parallel experiments were conducted under the practical constraints. For the operational convenience, the experimental conditions were selected: A of 535 W, B of 30 min, C of 60°C, and D of 3.8 m/s. The observed actual values for Y1 − Y11 were recorded as 1.723%, 0.033%, 0.300%, 0.001%, 46.265 μmol Trolox/g, 35.317%, 55.369%, 160 min, 43.133, −7.043, and 27.373, respectively. The overall desirability value under the optimum condition was 0.820. These experimental results demonstrated a close alignment with the predicted model, thereby confirming the efficacy of the optimized process parameters determined by the quadratic regression model. This close match between predicted and experimental values confirmed the robustness and reliability of the optimization approach.

Underlying mechanism analysis

Based on the experimental findings, a mechanistic diagram was formulated to elucidate the drying-promotion effects of Us-HA-D on TMHM (Fig. 9). The results demonstrated that Us-HA-D contributed to three primary improvements: (i) the microchannels were created within the tissue structure, facilitating moisture transfer and consequently accelerating drying rates; (ii) the enzymatic and non-enzymatic browning reactions were effectively suppressed, thereby better preserving the original color characteristics of fresh TMHM; (iii) the cell wall disruption was promoted, leading to the enhanced cellular permeability. This structural damage facilitated the release and extraction of intracellular phenolic compounds during the analytical process, resulting in higher measured contents of phenolic acids and improved antioxidant activity in the dried product. It was noteworthy that the improved extractability, while beneficial for analysis, could theoretically render the released compounds more vulnerable to thermal and oxidative degradation during drying. However, under the optimized Us-HA-D conditions, the dominant factor governing phenolic acid retention was the significant reduction in total drying time. The ultrasonic acceleration of moisture removal drastically curtailed the duration of heat exposure, and the benefit of this shortened processing window outweighed the potential risk associated with increased compound mobility. According to the literature, the biosynthesis of chicoric acid primarily relied on the phenylalanine pathway, which encompassed a series of multi-step enzymatic reactions [24]. Among these enzymatic processes, both chlorogenic acid and monocaffeoyltartaric acid were served as essential precursors for the formation of chicoric acid [63]. Notably, when Us-HA-D was applied at the moderate temperatures (45°C and 55°C), significantly higher recovery of phenolic acids was achieved compared to the conventional drying methods. Specifically, increases of 28–––41% for chicoric acid, 34–––56% for chlorogenic acid, and 23–––42% for monocaffeoyltartaric acid were observed compared to samples not exposed to ultrasonic. This enhanced recovery was attributed to the combined effects of the improved extractability due to structural disruption and reduced oxidative degradation resulting from the shorter drying time [47]. Additionally, the substantial increase in these phenolic acids might be explained by cell matrix breakdown releasing bound phenolic compounds, along with the potential modulation of enzymatic activities during Us-HA-D treatment. However, a notable decrease in the total phenolic content was detected at higher temperatures (65°C) in conjunction with ultrasound treatment. This decline could be attributed to the heightened cell damage, which caused the increased oxidation of phenolic compounds at the elevated drying temperatures. Conclusion

In this study, Us-HA-D was systematically identified as the optimal drying method for TMHM through comprehensive evaluation of ten drying techniques. The drying process of Us-HA-D was accurately described by the Weibull model, demonstrating superior fitting performance among eight mathematical models. Us-HA-D could significantly enhance the drying efficiency, as evidenced by the increased Deff and accelerated DR, which were attributed to the formation of microchannels and porous structures observed in microstructural analysis. The results of MRI revealed more uniform moisture distribution and faster water removal in Us-HA-D treated samples compared to the conventional HA-D. Although some structural alterations led to reduced rehydration capacity, ultrasonic pretreatment effectively mitigated surface hardening and improved water absorption at higher air velocities. Us-HA-D could excellently preserved the natural color parameters (higher L* and lower ΔE values) while minimizing browning reactions. Furthermore, Us-HA-D significantly enhanced the retention of key phenolic acids of TMHM, including chicoric acid, chlorogenic acid, and monocaffeoyltartaric acid, and maintained the superior antioxidant capacity. The process optimization of Us-HA-D established that the moderate ultrasound power (535 W) and duration (30 min), combined with the specific air temperature (60°C) and velocity (3.8 m/s), achieved optimal quality attributes. The integration of multiple analytical approaches demonstrated that Us-HA-D was a promising industrial-scale technology for producing the high-quality dried TMHM with the enhanced functional properties, offering significant potential for applications in food industries.
