Inorganic thermoelectric thin films: From synthesis to application
Authors: Teng Wang, Fan Sun, Jae Sung Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThermoelectric technologies constitute a sustainable paradigm for waste heat recovery. Inorganic thermoelectric thin films, leveraging low-dimensional transport phenomena and tunable physical properties, have emerged as premier candidates for flexible, miniaturized electronics. This review introduces various fabrication methodologies—including atomic layer deposition, molecular beam epitaxy, chemical vapor deposition, magnetron sputtering, and solution-based processing—elucidating their operational principles, comparative advantages, and inherent limitations. Subsequently, the deployment of these films in power generation and active cooling is discussed, emphasizing innovations in architectural design and mechanical properties. Finally, we outline promising research directions, including the convergence of hybrid fabrication strategies, artificial intelligence-driven optimization, and multifunctional device engineering for advancing thermoelectric thin films. Collectively, these developments signal a pathway toward next-generation thin-film flexible electronic systems that are both self-powered and thermally regulated.Subject areas: Chemistry, Physics, Materials science

Introduction

Thermoelectric (TE) technologies represent a transformative paradigm situated at the critical interface of energy sustainability and materials innovation. As global energy demands escalate and the environmental consequences of fossil fuel consumption become increasingly acute, the development of efficient energy harvesting systems has become a priority for the scientific community. In this context, solid-state TE devices, which possess the unique capability to directly convert thermal energy into electrical energy via the Seebeck effect, offer a compelling solution for waste heat recovery.1 Considering that approximately 60% of globally consumed energy is dissipated as waste heat, the potential to recapture a fraction of this energy presents a significant opportunity to enhance overall system efficiency.2 TE generators (TEGs) are particularly engineered to exploit this unutilized thermal potential. They are capable of harvesting ambient or high-grade waste heat from a myriad of sources, ranging from industrial furnaces and automotive exhaust systems to household appliances and the metabolic heat generated by the human body. This versatile capability facilitates the autonomous operation of electronic devices, effectively mitigating the reliance on frequent battery replacements or tethered external power supplies. Such autonomy is indispensable in operational environments where conventional power access is spatially limited. Prominent examples include distributed sensor networks, remote environmental monitoring systems, and the field of wearable electronics.1 Consequently, the advancement of TE technologies aligns intrinsically with the principles of the circular economy, contributing directly to the fabrication of resilient, self-sustaining, and carbon-neutral energy infrastructures.

The conversion efficiency of TE materials is quantitatively evaluated using the dimensionless figure of merit, ZT = S2σT/κ. Here, S denotes the Seebeck coefficient (representing the thermopower), σ is the electrical conductivity, T is the absolute temperature, and κ is the thermal conductivity, comprising both lattice (κL) and electronic (κe) contributions.1 A higher ZT value signifies superior TE performance, thereby dictating the thermodynamic efficiency of the device. Consequently, the optimization of TE materials necessitates a complex balancing act: maximizing the power factor (S2σ) while simultaneously minimizing thermal conductivity. For instance, the Seebeck coefficient is inversely proportional to the carrier concentration, whereas the electrical conductivity is directly proportional to the carrier concentration. Meanwhile, the Wiedemann-Franz law dictates that electrical conductivity is proportional to the electronic thermal conductivity, implying that improvements in charge transport typically result in increased thermal losses.3 To overcome these trade-off relations, thus far, a variety of semiconductor materials have emerged because their tunable electronic band structures and phonon transport characteristics offer pathways to decouple these conflicting parameters. Through strategies such as carrier concentration optimization and band engineering, as well as phonon transport engineering, significant enhancements in ZT values have been achieved.3 Moreover, the recent rapid progress in computational materials discovery, high-throughput synthesis strategies, and innovative device architectures is steadily positioning TE technologies as viable power solutions.4,5

While bulk TE materials have been the subject of research for decades, recent attention has been attracted toward inorganic TE thin films. This transition is driven by the unique physical properties, versatile fabrication routes, and broad application potential inherent to low-dimensional systems. Thin-film architectures are not merely miniaturized versions of their bulk counterparts. They provide a unique platform to manipulate transport physics at the quantum level, serving as the foundation for next-generation power generators and active cooling systems in distributed sensor networks and wearable electronics, as shown in Figure 1.

The physical imperative for low-dimensional TE systems stems from the ability to decouple interdependent TE transport parameters. Dresselhaus et al. proved that reducing the dimensions of a material below the carrier mean free path alters the density of states (DOS), enhances the Seebeck coefficient without a commensurate reduction in electrical conductivity.6 Furthermore, the thin-film geometry inherently introduces a high density of interfaces and grain boundaries. These structural features act as effective scattering centers for phonons, particularly those with mean free paths exceeding the film thickness or grain size, thereby suppressing the lattice thermal conductivity toward the “phonon-glass” limit while preserving the “electron-crystal” electronic transport.7,8

Beyond fundamental physics, the development of thin-film processing enables the exploration of diverse TE materials. While classic bismuth telluride (Bi2Te3)-based alloys remain the benchmark for near-room-temperature applications, thin-film techniques have been implemented in emerging candidates.9 For instance, GeTe-based films exhibit high performance through the manipulation of phase transitions and vacancy engineering.10 Similarly, materials such as Cu2Se and Ag2Se, which possess liquid-like sublattices, have been successfully fabricated as flexible thin films, offering exceptional mechanical compliance alongside high TE efficiency.11,12 Furthermore, the extreme anisotropy of hole-doped SnSe, which historically complicated bulk processing, can be effectively managed via texturing control in solution-processed or sputtered films.11,12 Due to the sensitivity of materials to precursor and thermal dynamics, diverse synthesis methods are developed, ranging from physical vapor deposition to wet-chemical approaches.11,12,13,14,15

The evolution of TE devices from rigid configurations to flexible and conformal substrates imposes the ability to deposit TE materials conformally over flexible and complex surfaces, which becomes paramount.5,9 This creates a trade-off defined by quality, scalability, and conformality. Delicate layer-by-layer growth methods, such as atomic layer deposition (ALD) and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), excel in pushing ZT limit, but often struggle with throughput.13,15 Conversely, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), magnetron sputtering, and solution-based processing offer superior scalability and cost-effectiveness for large-area deployment but require rigorous optimization to approach high TE performance.11,12,14 This review navigates this technological landscape, comparing the advantages and disadvantages between different synthesis techniques and the resulting material and device performance to guide the design of inorganic TE thin films.

Synthesis methods for inorganic TE thin films

Pushing the TE limit: ALD and MBE

ALD

ALD is a thin-film growth technique distinguished by its ability to provide unparalleled atomic-scale control over film thickness. Besides, its low-temperature processing compatibility with CMOS techniques and ability to deposit conformal passivating protective layers mitigate device degradation, thereby enhancing the environmental stability and operational lifespan of microscale TE devices.13 The core mechanism of ALD relies on sequential, self-limiting surface reactions. This self-terminating nature ensures that film growth proceeds in a layer-by-layer fashion, enabling the deposition of highly uniform and conformal coatings even on complex, high-aspect-ratio three-dimensional (3D) structures. During ALD, the substrate is alternately exposed to complementary precursors, each reacting only with specific surface sites to build the film one atomic layer at a time. A typical cycle includes exposure to precursor A, chemisorption onto reactive surface groups, a purge step to remove unreacted species and byproducts, exposure to precursor B to complete the surface reaction, and a second purge to clear the chamber before the next cycle. Due to this chemisorption-driven growth mechanism, the deposition rate is inherently strictly defined (typically approaching around 1 Å per cycle). This characteristic affords researchers exceptional reproducibility and precise control over thickness, uniformity, and conformality, and facilitates the synergistic optimization of otherwise coupled TE parameters.

Metal oxides have represented the most extensively investigated class of ALD-grown TE materials, primarily due to their straightforward precursor chemistry, reliable nucleation behavior on diverse substrates, and mature processing protocols. Representative binary and ternary systems—including ZnO, TiO2, layered cobalt oxides, and complex spinel oxides—can be readily synthesized and doped.16,17,18,19 By meticulously adjusting precursor types, pulse ratios, and deposition temperatures, researchers can achieve controlled tuning of the carrier concentration, thereby optimizing electrical conductivity and the Seebeck coefficient.20,21,22 However, despite the facile synthesis and chemical stability of these oxides, their TE utility is often constrained by intrinsic material properties. As illustrated in Table 1, oxide TE thin films frequently exhibit intrinsically high electrical resistivity. Furthermore, the strong ionic bonding in oxides typically results in high thermal conductivity.28 These factors collectively limit the overall TE performance of metal oxide thin films, necessitating the exploration of alternative material systems. Precursor developments have expanded the capabilities of ALD into the realm of chalcogenide-based materials, which typically possess superior intrinsic TE properties, such as high carrier mobility and low thermal conductivity. A notable advancement in this field is the introduction of less toxic alkylsilyl compounds of Te and Se as precursors.29 These novel precursors offer a more straightforward reaction route for the elimination of metal precursors compared to alkyls and alkylamides of Se and Te, therefore facilitating the ALD synthesis of metal selenide and telluride films.23,24,25,26,30,31 Furthermore, the versatility of ALD has been demonstrated in the synthesis of two-dimensional (2D) materials (e.g., MoS2) and superlattices.27,32 For instance, Yang et al. leverage a highly conformal, low-temperature ALD process to architect SbOx/Sb2Te3 superlattice heterostructures as shown in Figures 2A and 2B.27 By precisely interspersing amorphous SbOx sub-nanometer layers within the crystalline Sb2Te3 matrix, the authors effectively construct a robust interfacial potential barrier that filters out low-energy charge carriers. This energy filtering effect, dynamically coupled with SbOx-induced quantum confinement and localized lattice strain, successfully decouples the interdependent TE parameters. Consequently, the twin grain boundaries and nanostructured interfaces intensify mid-to-long-wavelength phonon scattering, aggressively suppressing the thermal conductivity to an ultralow 0.40 Wm−1K−1 for the 10:300 configuration, and a maximum power factor of 530.7 μWm−1K−2 is achieved in the 10:1000 configuration, as shown in Figures 2C and 2D. Despite these interfacial engineering advancements, the overall ZT remains relatively modest, probably due to the poor TE properties of amorphous SbOx layers. The advantage of ALD in conformality is further manifested in the conformal ultrathin coatings on TE powder particles as depicted in Figures 2E and 2F.33,34,35 The excellent conformality of ALD ensures that ultrathin films uniformly encapsulate individual grains, allowing for the fine-tuning of interfacial energy filtering and grain boundary scattering even after the sintering process.

Despite the exceptional nanoscale advantage, the ALD faces several limitations. Primarily, ALD has an extremely low deposition rate. The cyclic, self-limiting nature of precursor pulses and intermediate purging steps severely restricts growth efficiency (e.g., maximal growth rates of just 0.16 Å/cycle for Sb2Te3).24 This makes the fabrication of the thick films (typically micron-scale) highly impractical. Furthermore, low growth temperature and the following incomplete surface reactions often lead to non-crystalline films, which hinder high TE performance. Coupled with the high cost of the vacuum processing equipment and specialized precursors, these limitations currently impede the cost-effective, large-scale manufacturing of ALD-based TE devices.

MBE

MBE is a highly precise thin-film deposition process that takes place within an ultra-high vacuum environment.36,37 Within this pristine vacuum chamber, solid elemental source materials are heated inside effusion cells until they evaporate, forming focused, collision-free “molecular beams.” These beams travel directly toward a carefully selected, heated crystalline substrate. Upon reaching the substrate, the evaporated atoms or molecules condense and assemble in a meticulous, layer-by-layer fashion. By precisely regulating the temperature and flux of each individual beam, researchers can control the exact structural arrangement, literally building complex materials atom by atom. This extraordinary level of atomic precision makes MBE a uniquely powerful tool for TE research by decoupling electrical and thermal transport through advanced interface engineering, paving the way for pushing the ZT limit.

The landscape of TE thin films synthesized via MBE encompasses a diverse array of materials as summarized in Table 2, which can be grouped based on their structural characteristics and intrinsic transport mechanisms. The first primary category comprises metal chalcogenides—notably Bi2Te3, Sb2Te3, and SnTe—which leverage van der Waals epitaxy to form highly oriented, strain-free layers.15,38,39,40 A second distinct family involves wide-bandgap transition metal oxides and nitrides, such as La-doped SrTiO3 and epitaxial ScN, where MBE is utilized to precisely manage structural vacancies to achieve high carrier mobilities.41,42 A third category involves complex nanoscale heterojunctions and artificial superlattices (e.g., InAs/AlSb and PbTe/Te), designed specifically to manipulate band offsets and quantum confinement.43,44 Recently, MBE has also been extended to multi-elemental intermetallic alloys, successfully stabilizing highly complex phases such as α-MgAgSb.45 To demonstrate the profound impact of MBE’s atomic precision, several micro-level case studies are highlighted herein. First, advanced heterostructure engineering was demonstrated in InAs/AlSb systems to directly address bipolar conduction at high temperatures, as shown in Figure 3A.43 By inserting wide-bandgap p-type AlSb layers into a p-type InAs host, Peter G. Burke et al. created targeted minority carrier blocking barriers. This precise band-offset engineering physically prevented the thermal diffusion of minority electrons, while allowing majority holes to maintain high mobility, as shown in Figure 3B. Consequently, the Seebeck coefficient was artificially sustained at elevated temperatures, leading to a 200% increase in the power factor compared to bulk equivalents without such engineered interfaces. A second breakthrough involves the application of MBE to synthesize flexible α-MgAgSb films. Because this alloy is highly sensitive to stoichiometry and prone to forming deleterious secondary phases, conventional sputtering fails to yield high performance.45 By utilizing a quasi-equilibrium ultra-high vacuum environment, MBE allowed precise stabilization of the pure α-phase (Figure 3C). This structural perfection preserved the material’s intrinsic high band degeneracy and low lattice thermal conductivity, achieving a peak power factor of 19.3 μWcm−1K−2 at 473 K and a room temperature ZT of 0.8, ranking among the highest for flexible inorganic TE as presented in Figures 3D and E.

Despite these profound physical advantages, the transition of MBE from a laboratory research tool to widespread industrial application is hindered by severe limitations. Most critically, MBE suffers from low deposition rates. Even in modernized suboxide or plasma-assisted processes, growth rates hover around a mere 1 μm/h.37 This lack of throughput renders mass production highly impractical when compared to high-yield techniques such as CVD and solution-based processes. Additionally, MBE requires expensive investments for initial equipment and continuous, costly maintenance to address mechanical failures.

Overall, both ALD and MBE offer atomic-level precision and sub-nanometer thickness control during thin-film synthesis. This shared capability for layer-by-layer growth establishes both methods as exceptionally powerful tools for pushing the TE limit. By engineering these atomic boundaries, both ALD and MBE enable researchers to decouple and manipulate interdependent TE parameters. However, a universal bottleneck for both techniques lies in their low deposition rates and slow growth kinetics. Whether constrained by the cyclic, self-limiting precursor chemisorption of ALD or the slow physical condensation of molecular beam fluxes in MBE, this inherently restricted throughput renders the routine fabrication of the thicker films typically required for macro-scale devices highly impractical. Additionally, both advanced methodologies demand expensive investments for a high vacuum state and incur high maintenance costs.

Balancing scale and performance: CVD, magnetron sputtering, and solution-based processing

CVD

CVD distinguishes itself by delivering high and highly tunable deposition rates, exceptional step coverage, and the industrial scalability required for manufacturing large+-area TE films. CVD is a highly versatile TE thin film processing technology in which inorganic TE thin films are synthesized on a heated substrate through the chemical reaction of gas-phase precursors.14 In detail, CVD produces thin films by delivering volatile precursors into a heated reaction chamber, where they undergo gas-phase reactions, surface adsorption, and subsequent decomposition to form solid deposits. Film growth is governed by precursor chemistry, mass transport, and surface reaction kinetics. In a typical process, volatile precursor gases are introduced into a reaction chamber at high temperatures and low pressures to enhance reactivity and control film quality. Within the gas phase, these precursors may thermally decompose or react with co-fed species to form reactive intermediates, which are then transported by diffusion and convection to the substrate surface. The efficiency of this mass transport directly affects film thickness and uniformity. Upon reaching the substrate, the reactive species adsorb and participate in surface reactions that drive nucleation and material growth. Competing surface processes—including adsorption, desorption, nucleation, and crystal growth—collectively determine the composition and microstructure of the resulting film. These processes depend sensitively on substrate type, temperature, pressure, precursor concentration, and growth duration, which together act as key parameters governing film quality and TE performance. Furthermore, by carefully engineering the reactor volume, temperature gradients, and fluid dynamics of the precursor flow, this continuous gas-phase chemical reaction effortlessly provides inherent industrial scalability for large-area uniform TE thin films.

With continued progress in precursor design and process control, CVD and its variants have emerged as versatile techniques for synthesizing compositionally tunable inorganic TE thin films. A wide range of inorganic TE materials can be deposited by CVD. The dominant category comprises metal chalcogenides such as Bi2Se3, Bi2Te3, GeTe, PbTe, Sb2Te3, SnS, SnSe, and SnTe due to high Seebeck coefficients and low electrical resistivity arising from an optimal carrier concentration and appropriate band gap, which can be further tuned via alloying, chemical doping, and microstructural engineering, as shown in Table 3.46,47,48,49,50,51,52,53 A second class is oxides, such as La-doped SrTiO3 and Al-doped ZnO, which have been developed for high-temperature stable, low-cost, and environmentally friendly TE thin films.54,55 They are wide band gap n-type semiconductors yielding an intrinsically large Seebeck coefficient. Additionally, their carrier concentration, band structure, and thermal conductivity can be tuned via chemical doping and nanostructuring. Finally, emerging halide perovskites, quaternary chalcogenides (e.g., Cu2ZnSnS4), halide perovskites (e.g., CsPbBr3 and Cs2AgBiBr6), 2D materials, such as large-area continuous graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides (e.g., MoS2 and PtSe2), and superlattices have also been reported.56,57,58,59,60,61 These systems take advantage of low lattice thermal conductivity through intense phonon scattering to enhance high ZT. A recent study utilizes metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) to fabricate controlled hierarchically engineered superlattice structures (Figure 4A). These epitaxial films—specifically p-type Bi2Te3/Sb2Te3 and n-type Bi2Te3/Bi2Te2.7Se0.3—leverage periodic nanoscale interfaces to dramatically suppress cross-plane lattice thermal conductivity to approximately 8 mWm−1K−1.62 Concurrently, they preserve in-plane carrier mobility through strong covalent networks, culminating in a remarkable ZT of nearly 2.0 near 300 K. For demonstrating scalability, CVD has been used to grow centimeter-scale polycrystalline graphene films as shown in Figure 4C.58 Through electric double-layer gating, this rapid, large-area synthesis achieved giant maximum power factors of 6.93 and 3.29 mWm−1K−2 for p- and n-type configurations, respectively.

Despite these remarkable successes, the practical implementation of CVD for TE thin films is constrained by several limitations. First, the complex chemistry of CVD introduces the potential for unintentional impurity doping from precursor residues. Second, the relatively high growth temperatures are incompatible with flexible polymer substrates. Third, while CVD can readily deposit macroscopic superlattices, the continuous, fluid nature of gas-phase transport makes it exceptionally difficult to achieve the extreme, atomic-level abrupt interfaces that ALD and MBE can easily produce.

Magnetron sputtering

The paramount advantage of magnetron sputtering lies in its ability to sputter large-area uniform inorganic TE thin films with thickness control and excellent adhesion. More importantly, it allows for deposition at room temperature, which enables the use of various flexible substrates that cannot withstand high temperatures. The process of magnetron sputtering is fundamentally driven by the bombardment of a solid source material (the target) by energetic ions—typically positively charged Ar+ generated within a plasma discharge. Upon impact, a cascade of momentum transfer events occurs within the target lattice, resulting in the ejection of surface atoms, which subsequently traverse the vacuum chamber and condense onto a substrate. The key innovation is the use of a magnetic field parallel to the target surface, which traps electrons, increases plasma density, and enhances ionization efficiency. Furthermore, because the substrate is spatially separated from the main plasma generation zone, the thermal load on the substrate is significantly reduced, enabling deposition at relatively low temperatures. This characteristic renders magnetron sputtering highly compatible with thermally sensitive and flexible substrates, which are essential for wearable TE applications. Unlike chemical methods that rely on precursor reactivity, sputtering allows for the “forced” mixing of immiscible elements or the creation of metastable phases, expanding the material design space available for TE optimization. A critical advantage of this technique is its ability to preserve complex stoichiometries, enabling the precise replication of target compositions in the deposited film. This capability is particularly vital for multicomponent systems. Furthermore, the TE properties of sputtered films can be systematically tuned through adjustments in sputtering parameters. In detail, sputtering pressure strongly influences film composition, grain orientation, and grain size through the dictation of the mean free path of the ejected atoms and, consequently, their kinetic energy upon arrival at the substrate, while substrate temperature directly governs surface bonding dynamics and crystallization.63 In addition, substrate type and post-annealing procedures further modulate microstructure, relieving internal stresses and healing point defects.64,65 Thereby, the TE performance of sputtered thin films is governed by a complex interplay of synthesis parameters.

The magnetron sputtering technique has been successfully adapted to synthesize a broad spectrum of inorganic TE materials, ranging from high-performance metal chalcogenides to high-performance inorganic alloys including Ag2Se, Ag2Te, and Mg3Bi2 as shown in Table 4.66,67,68,69,70,71,72,73,74,75 For example, Zou et al. uses radio frequency magnetron sputtering to deposit highly (001)-oriented n-type Bi2Te3 and p-type Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 films onto polyimide substratesas shown in Figure 5A.76 By systematically tuning the deposition temperature up to 723 K, the researchers achieved precise electronic state modulation; specifically, the highly active atoms excited by the radio frequency power supply relaxed preferentially along the c-axis, which fundamentally enhanced in-plane carrier mobility. This structurally driven mobility optimization, coupled with finely tuned carrier concentrations, leads to room temperature power factors of 3.2 and 6.1 μWcm−1K−2 for the n-type and p-type films, respectively. The synthesized thin films configured into TE legs for device assembly exhibited exceptional structural integrity, maintaining a nearly unchanged electrical resistance after 900 bending cycles at a 5 mm radius, as shown in Figures 5B and 5C. As for another example, the flexible Mg3Bi2 thin films were successfully synthesized via magnetron sputtering.73 By precisely tuning the Bi content and annealing time, Hu et al. triggered the formation of multi-dimensional defects, including lattice distortions, edge dislocations, and the encasement of Mg3Bi2 crystals within amorphous Mg, which fundamentally altered the transport and mechanical physics. Specifically, the optimization of carrier concentration through defect-induced p-n transitions alongside multi-scale phonon scattering was utilized to decouple the interdependent TE parameters while simultaneously enhancing the interfacial adhesion and flexibility on polyimide substrates, as presented in Figure 6A. This dual-functional approach led to a significant suppression of lattice thermal conductivity to below 0.25 Wm−1K−1 and a peak ZT value of 0.19 at 333 K. Furthermore, the resulting flexible device demonstrated remarkable mechanical endurance, maintaining over 90% of its stable performance (exhibiting <10% resistance degradation) after 500 bending cycles at a radius of 5 mm, as shown in Figures 6B and 6C.

Despite these advantages, key challenges remain in magnetron sputtering in TE research. Most notably, the further optimization of TE properties and long-term stability issues. Since the conventional doping method is not suitable, and the inherent complexity of the interplay of sputtering parameters, precisely tuning key TE parameters is difficult. Meanwhile, sputtered films suffer from progressive performance degradation, limiting the operational lifespan of the devices in ambient environments.

Solution-based processing

Solution-based processing has become an attractive strategy for synthesizing inorganic TE thin films because of its ability to produce scalable TE films with low cost and printability. The process typically involves precursor dissolution, film deposition, solvent removal, nucleation and crystal growth, followed by necessary post-treatments such as annealing. Major solution-based processing routes include spin coating, printing techniques, colloidal nanocrystal assembly, and electrodeposition. These methods enable rapid, large-area deposition and are highly compatible with industrial manufacturing.

Metal chalcogenides are the most widely explored class for solution-processed TE films, with their TE performance summarized in Table 5.77,78,79,80,81,82,83,85 Early breakthroughs demonstrated that chalcogenide SnS2 can be dissolved in hydrazine-based solvents via “dimensional reduction.”86,87 Further advances employ safer thiol-amine solvent systems (“alkahest solvents”) capable of dissolving bulk chalcogenides such as Cu2Se, Bi2Te3, Sb2Te3, and SnSe into molecular inks that convert into phase-pure crystalline films after mild annealing, as presented in Figure 7A.88,89,90,91 Film properties depend critically on precursor chemistry, solvent selection, and annealing conditions. For example, Heo et al. achieve exceptionally low-temperature TE performance in Ag-doped SnSe thin films through spin-coating and precisely controlled heat treatments. The introduction of Ag triggers the formation of intermediate SnSe2 and AgSnSe2 secondary phases, which drive extreme lateral grain growth and crystallographic texturing along the highly conductive b-c plane, as shown in Figures 7B–7D.90 This structural evolution modulates transport mechanisms: Interfacial charge transfer across the semiconductor heterojunctions boosts hole concentration, while the in-plane orientation elevates carrier mobility up to 125.0 cm2V−1s−1. Concurrently, grain boundary scattering suppresses lattice thermal conductivity, yielding a remarkable room temperature power factor of 11.97 μWcm−1K−2 and a peak ZT of 0.93 at 550 K. Furthermore, the study ingeniously addresses mechanical ductility by encapsulating the 200 nm-thick films in polyimide and transferring them onto a pre-stretched elastomer. The resulting strain-releasing wrinkled topology enabled the device to endure 200 stretching cycles at 20% strain with negligible property degradation. Despite these advancements, an open question regarding the long-term interfacial adhesion of the wrinkled assembly under thermal and mechanical cycling. In addition, solution-processed hybrid composite films that combine inorganic nanostructures with polymers (e.g., PEDOT: PSS and PVDF) offer enhanced flexibility and mechanical durability.92,93,94 For instance, Park et al. introduce a novel organic-inorganic hybrid film synthesized via a solution-mixing method that coats exfoliated p-type Bi2Si2Te6 nanosheets with camphorsulfonic acid-doped polyaniline (PANI).94 By systematically optimizing the PANI coating cycles, the researchers achieved precise modulation of charge carrier transport; specifically, the inherently high charge carrier concentration of the doped PANI increased the overall carrier concentration of the composite, while interfacial interactions induced band convergence effects. This synergistic optimization resulted in an outstanding power factor of 4.11 μWcm−1K−2 at 500 K for the composite with two coating cycles. The structural origin of the film’s exceptional mechanical ductility lies in the strong interfacial adhesion between the inorganic nanosheets and the PANI matrix, which facilitates effective stress transfer. Consequently, the films exhibited a remarkably high tensile strength of up to 43.3 MPa and maintained their structural integrity with no significant change in resistance or Seebeck coefficient after 1,000 bending cycles. However, the long-term environmental stability should have test, as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy revealed surface oxidation of the Bi2Si2Te6 nanosheets. Another advantage of solution-based processing in the inorganic TE area that must be mentioned is that the ink design has enabled a variety of printing methodologies—including screen printing, inkjet printing, and 3D printing—to be used for low-cost fabrication of flexible TE devices.77,84,95,96,97 Ink formulation (viscosity, particle size, homogeneity), printing parameters (droplet size, substrate temperature, printing speed, and film thickness), and post-treatment processes are critical for producing dense, uniform, and high-performance TE films. As a typical representative, Taneli et al. implemented drop-on-demand inkjet printing to deposit few-layer graphene flakes suspended in an isopropyl alcohol and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) ink.84 By subjecting the printed films to ex situ thermal annealing at 673 K, the insulating PVP binder is effectively removed, creating a disordered, nanoporous network of interconnected graphene flakes. This structural architecture uniquely embodies a phonon-glass electron-crystal paradigm; while the electrical transport retains the high-mobility characteristics of few-layer graphene, the randomly oriented nanoporous interfaces severely quench cross-plane and in-plane phonon propagation. Consequently, the lattice thermal conductivity is suppressed to around 1.00 Wm−1K−1, and a room temperature power factor of 0.18 μWcm−1K−2 is achieved. Meanwhile, the films exhibited well structural integrity, maintaining a stable resistance after 10,000 bending cycles at a 4.5 mm radius. Though a 3-fold improvement of power factor compared to previous solution-processed all-graphene structures is presented, further enhancement of TE properties is still expected since the room temperature ZT is only at approximately 10−3.

Although solution processing offers substantial advantages, challenges remain in achieving precise microstructural control, ensuring long-term stability, and implementing robust doping strategies. Continued advances in ink chemistry, printing physics, and post-processing methods will be essential for realizing the full potential of solution-processed TE materials and devices.

All in all, CVD, magnetron sputtering, and solution-based processing converge on macroscopic engineering advantages for the synthesis of inorganic TE thin films. A universal strength among these methodologies is their relatively higher deposition rates compared to MBE and ALD, which establish the foundational scalability required for the large-area industrial manufacturing of TE devices. A universal trade-off among these three methods, however, lies in their inherent bottlenecks at the micro- and nanoscale. In direct contrast to ALD and MBE, these processes exhibit a shared susceptibility to defect generation and unintentional impurity incorporation during film growth. This problem manifests through distinct pathways—arising from precursor residues in CVD, energetic plasma bombardment dynamics in magnetron sputtering, and trapped solvents, insulating binders, or resulting nanoporosity in solution-processed methods—but universally acts to compromise TE parameters such as charge carrier mobility.

Applications of inorganic TE thin films

Fundamentals of TE devices

The fundamental building block of a TE device (TED) is the thermocouple, a structural unit composed of alternating p-type and n-type semiconductor legs. These legs are electrically connected in series to sum the electromotive force, while being thermally connected in parallel through highly conductive metallic electrodes to facilitate heat transfer.98 Functionally, TEDs operate in dual modalities depending on the driving force. In the power generation mode, the device is subjected to a temperature gradient, generating a thermovoltage via the Seebeck effect. Conversely, in the active cooling mode, an applied electric current drives heat from one junction to the other via the Peltier effect, creating a controllable temperature difference. The former is evaluated by the energy conversion efficiency (η), whereas the latter is measured using the coefficient of performance (COP), which describes the achievable temperature difference. Both η and COP scale with the ZT; thus, high-ZT materials form the foundation of high-performance TEDs.99 Although ZT defines the theoretical upper limit of device performance, practical operation is strongly influenced by device-level and system-level considerations. In practical operation, the realized performance is often significantly lower, attenuated by device-level constraints and system-level parasitic losses. Consequently, architectural configuration and interface engineering are as critical as material synthesis.100,101

Device architecture dictates the thermal and electrical pathways within the system. TEDs are generally implemented with either vertical or lateral configurations, depending on the orientation of their legs and heat-flow direction. In vertical configurations, heat flows through the device from top to bottom, enabling efficient thermal contact with heat sources and sinks and allowing relatively high packing densities. However, short leg lengths constrain the achievable temperature gradient. Lateral configurations, in which heat flows parallel to the device plane, facilitate simpler fabrication and allow longer TE legs, but suffer from parasitic heat losses through the substrate, which reduce output power and cooling performance. Building on these two basic configurations, numerous geometric structures have been developed—including single-leg, π-type, cylindrical, and radial designs—to meet the needs of diverse application scenarios.99

Thin-film-based TEDs (TFTEDs) represent a distinct evolution in energy harvesting technology, inheriting unique advantages from their low-dimensional geometry. Their exceptionally high-power density is derived from the precise nanoscale control achievable during synthesis, while their intrinsic mechanical flexibility far surpasses that of thinned bulk counterparts. Furthermore, thin-film synthesis methods—such as sputtering and ALD—are directly compatible with microelectronics manufacturing processes. This compatibility enables the seamless integration of energy harvesters directly onto processor chips for hotspot cooling or into autonomous sensor nodes. Against this backdrop, the following sections summarize recent progress in TFTEDs, highlighting their indispensable role in enabling the self-powered operation of modern microelectronic systems.

Power generators

The growing demand for microscale, wearable, and flexible electronics—such as health monitors, smart textiles, and distributed sensor networks—has created an urgent need for reliable, maintenance-free power sources. TFTEGs provide a compelling solution by converting body heat or ambient temperature gradients into electrical power, enabling continuous and sustainable energy harvesting without batteries or external charging. Unlike solar or mechanical harvesters, TFTEGs deliver stable DC output and operate under a wide range of environmental conditions, including periods of inactivity or low illumination. Their small size, flexibility, and ability to conform to curved surfaces make them highly suitable for next-generation self-powered systems.

Recent research has focused on high-performance inorganic TE thin films such as Bi2Te3, Ag2Se, and quaternary chalcogenides. Bi2Te3-based thin films remain the benchmark near room temperature, with strategies including defect engineering, carrier concentration tuning, and band structure modulation used to enhance their performance.76,102,103,104,105,106 Ag2Se thin films have emerged as leading candidates for flexible devices due to their high-power factors and intrinsic ductility. Yang et al. reported a ZT of 1.27 in Te-doped Ag2Se thin films, with only 2.5% performance degradation after 1000 bending cycles at a 6.3 mm radius.107 A flexible TFTEG was assembled from p-type Sb2Te3 and n-type Ag2Se films, which were integrated using copper electrodes explicitly chosen for their minimal contact resistance. To guarantee mechanical stability and environmental durability, the thin films were encapsulated with a protective organic-polymer composite layer comprising 90% poly(vinyl laurate) and 10% N-methylpyrrolidone applied via a spin-coating process. Ultimately, the fully assembled device delivered an exceptional maximum output power density of 1.5 mWcm−2 alongside an open-circuit voltage of 6 mV under a 20 K temperature difference, while retaining robust structural integrity exhibiting only a 2.5% performance loss following 1000 bending cycles as shown in Figure 8A. In parallel, sustainable alternatives such as quaternary chalcogenides (e.g., Cu2ZnSnS4), and Mg-based systems have attracted increasing interest due to their low cost and favorable power factors.109,110 For example, planar TFTEGs were developed using cost-effective p-type Cu2ZnSnS4 and Cu2ZnSnSe4 paired with n-type AlxZn1-xO, synthesized on soda-lime glass substrates via direct current and radio frequency magnetron sputtering, followed by a highly controlled thermal chalcogenization process.109 The planar devices were assembled through a sequential fabrication strategy utilizing mechanical scratching and Kapton-tape masking to define the alternating p-n leg geometries, which were subsequently connected in series using thermally evaporated silver contacts reinforced with silver paste for structural and electrical durability. Thorough device evaluation revealed that the optimized Cu2ZnSnSe4-based generators exhibited a minimal internal resistance of 3.79 Ω and delivered a peak planar power density of 279 nWcm−2 at a 150 K temperature difference, while reducing material cost by over 80%. Furthermore, these devices demonstrated robust operational stability by retaining over 75% of their initial power output after seven thermal cycles, highlighting their viability as sustainable and non-toxic alternatives for micro-energy harvesting applications (Figure 8B). Device-level innovations have also played an essential role in high-power output.108,111 High-performance p-type Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 and n-type Bi2Te3 inorganic thin films were synthesized onto 25 μm flexible polyimide substrates utilizing a precisely controlled magnetron co-sputtering process. For device integration, these 2D planar thin-film arrays were engineered into an interconnected three-dimensional wavy architecture, enabling the alternating p/n legs to efficiently harness vertical, out-of-plane temperature gradients without necessitating an additional bulky heat sink. Under an applied vertical temperature difference of 11 K, the fully integrated wavy-structure generator achieved a maximum open-circuit voltage of 54.6 mV and a peak output power of 697.4 nW. Consequently, this configuration yielded an exceptional normalized output power density of 106.6 mWm−2K−2 while maintaining excellent compliance to extreme mechanical deformation for curved heat sources, as shown in Figure 8C.108 Substrate-free inorganic TE films, which adhere closely to curved surfaces and offer compact form factors, represent another promising direction.112 These developments collectively indicate that adaptability, environmental sustainability, and cost-effectiveness are becoming central objectives in TFTEG research.

TE coolers

Accelerating advances in modern electronics—driven by increasing computational power, miniaturization, and high integration density—have resulted in substantial heat generation at both device and system levels. Effective thermal management has therefore become essential for maintaining performance and preventing degradation or failure. Thin-film TE coolers (TFTECs) offer a promising means of addressing these challenges. As solid-state devices with no moving parts, they provide silent operation, high reliability, and precise localized cooling. Moreover, their compatibility with semiconductor fabrication enables direct on-chip integration, allowing efficient hotspot cooling in next-generation electronics.

Mirroring the trends observed in TFTEGs, TFTECs primarily rely on Bi2Te3-based films due to their superior TE properties.113,114 Gong et al. deposited nanocrystalline p-type Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 and n-type Bi2Te3 thin films onto flexible polyimide substrates via a high-temperature magnetron sputtering process, followed by a controlled post-annealing treatment to induce recrystallization and optimize carrier mobility. The TFTEC was engineered into a novel radial planar architecture utilizing standard photolithography and wet etching, with the alternating p-n legs connected in series through highly conductive Ti/Au/Ti sputtered electrodes specifically selected to minimize electrical contact resistance. To ensure environmental durability against moisture and oxidation while bolstering mechanical integrity, a final protective polyimide passivation layer was spin-coated over the fully assembled array. Under operational testing at a 360 K background temperature, this micro-integrated radial device achieved a maximum net cooling temperature difference of 4 °C and an overall hot-to-cold end temperature gradient of 7.26 °C, delivering rapid on-chip hotspot mitigation with a remarkably low power consumption of only 4.76 mW at an optimal 15 mA driving current, as presented in Figure 9A.113 Even greater performance has been achieved through superlattice engineering. Ihtesham et al. were heteroepitaxially synthesized p-type Bi2Te3/Sb2Te3 and n-type Bi2Te3/Bi2Te2.83Se0.17 utilizing a MOCVD method.115 For device assembly, the top epitaxial surfaces of these active materials were metalized and bonded to an aluminum nitride die header, followed by chemical substrate removal and subsequent bonding to a second trace-containing aluminum nitride header to form the complete circuit. To critically minimize electrical contact resistance, the authors strategically employed plated gold for the n-type elements and evaporated Cr/Ni/Au for the p-type elements. Ultimately, the fully fabricated TFTEC delivered a maximum cooling flux of 258 Wcm−2 alongside a maximum temperature difference of 43.54 K, as shown in Figure 7B, highlighting the capability of thin-film architectures for extreme thermal management.

Conclusions and perspectives

This review has summarized the main deposition methodologies for inorganic TE thin films, focusing on the distinct capabilities of ALD, MBE, CVD, magnetron sputtering, and solution-based processing. As evidenced by the comparative analysis presented herein, no single technique emerges as a universally superior solution; rather, the optimal selection is strictly limited and depends upon the requirements of quality, scalability, and conformability. Looking forward, the advancement of inorganic TE thin films and the corresponding devices will increasingly reflect an all-in-one pursuit: the maximization of the ZT, the industry-satisfied scalability, and the simultaneous enhancement of mechanical flexibility. In this complex multidimensional optimization space, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are poised to play a transformative role. Traditional trial-and-error experimentation is often too slow to navigate the vast compositional and processing parameter spaces inherent to quaternary and quinary TE systems. Large language models and generative AI can accelerate this process by mining vast literature databases to suggest novel precursor chemistries and synthesis routes. Furthermore, ML algorithms—trained on high-throughput experimental data—can predict phase stability and transport properties, enabling the inverse design of synthesizing various TE materials with targeted performance metrics. Recently, data-driven methodologies—ranging from active learning loops, transfer learning, and Bayesian optimization to sophisticated frameworks such as graph neural networks, specialized architectures such as DopNet, and deep generative models—have already been utilized to accurately capture complex structure-property relationships in TE research and accelerate high-throughput screening. These interventions bypass the prohibitive computational costs of traditional first-principles calculations, enabling the rapid prediction of lattice thermal conductivity and the precise modeling of point defects, dislocations, and high-entropy phases. Integrating these computational tools with autonomous synthesis platforms represents a paradigm shift that could significantly shorten the development cycle for TE thin-film materials and devices.116,117 Beyond single-method optimization, the field is moving toward hybrid fabrication strategies that synergistically combine multiple techniques. For instance, the high deposition rates of sputtering or solution processing with the interfacial precision of ALD post-coating could overcome individual process limitations, creating composite structures with enhanced stability and carrier filtering properties.118 Furthermore, from a device perspective, both optimizing material ZT values and expanding new application scenarios should be given due attention. Recent advancements in flexible TE films have increasingly emphasized multi-functionality, transitioning from isolated power generation toward integrated, self-sustained sensory platforms. For example, Li et al. utilize interface-engineered Ag/Ag2Se/PVP composite films deployed within a dual-module architecture.119 In this system, one flexible TE generator (f-TEG) acts as a highly sensitive biometric interface, translating the thermal and mechanical dynamics of a user’s physical contact into distinct time-series voltage signals. When processed through a convolutional neural network, these signals enable highly accurate user identification. Concurrently, a second f-TEG module operates as a dedicated energy harvester, exploiting the temperature gradient between a localized heat source and the ambient environment. Coupled with a voltage booster, the harvested low-grade heat provides sufficient, stable power to run a photoplethysmography sensor for the real-time, wireless monitoring of physiological metrics, including heart rate and blood oxygen saturation. This synergistic integration underscores the critical role of versatile inorganic TE thin films in driving the autonomous, intelligent healthcare electronics. Moreover, progress has been made in hybrid photovoltaic/TE systems for energy conversion.120 The up-to-date solar-powered integrated TE generator (SPI-TEG) shows even greater multifunctionality. Relying only on sunlight, the SPI-TEG could co-produce electricity, freshwater, concentrated brine, and solid salt. These new breakthroughs highlight the interdisciplinary trend of future development for inorganic TE thin films, and broader engagement from other research communities—such as computer science, catalysis, solar cells, and batteries—is anticipated.

In summary, inorganic TE thin films have transitioned from academic curiosities to critical enablers of the IoT and wearable technology ecosystems. As deposition technologies mature and are increasingly augmented by AI-driven design, these materials are positioned to solve fundamental energy challenges. The successful translation of these laboratory advances into commercial reality will ultimately depend on a coordinated effort to harmonize material efficiency with manufacturing scalability and conformability, ensuring that TE technology plays a central role in the sustainable energy landscape of the future.
