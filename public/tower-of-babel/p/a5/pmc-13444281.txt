Dietary potassium diformate improves slaughter performance, systemic metabolic homeostasis, and intestinal health without compromising growth performance in Qingyuan Partridge chickens
Authors: Bin Wang, Baobei Ge, Yunzhi Peng, Feng Chen, Yizhe Zhang, Huize Tan, Xiaona Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 63-day feeding trial was conducted with 1,800 one-day-old male Qingyuan Partridge chickens, covering three feeding phases: starter (days 1–22), grower (days 23–42) and finisher (days 43–63). Birds were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments with six replicate pens of 75 chickens each: an antibiotic-containing control (ACC), an antibiotic-free control (AFC), and two groups supplemented with 0.45% and 0.60% potassium diformate (KDF), respectively. The evaluated endpoints included phase-specific growth performance, slaughter traits at day 63, routine hematology, serum biochemistry, arterial blood gas profiles, and cecal microbiota characterized via 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing. Growth and blood indices were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, while microbiota data were assessed via PERMANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 was defined as a statistical trend. Over the entire experimental period, no significant intergroup differences were detected in average daily gain (ADG) or feed conversion ratio (FCR), though 0.60% KDF significantly increased FCR exclusively during the starter phase. The 0.60% KDF group achieved slaughter rate (SR), semi-eviscerated weight ratio (SEWR) and eviscerated weight ratio (EWR) comparable to the antibiotic control, and significantly outperformed the 0.45% KDF group. The AFC group exhibited elevated serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase (CK), all of which were attenuated by KDF supplementation. The 0.45% KDF group had significantly higher serum carbon dioxide combining power (CO₂CP) and lower chloride (Cl⁻) than both the AFC and ACC groups, while the anion gap (AG) remained unchanged. KDF did not alter cecal microbial alpha diversity but selectively regulated the relative abundance of specific taxa, enriching butyrate-producing genera and suppressing opportunistic pathogens including Gammaproteobacteria. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional prediction revealed that 0.60% KDF downregulated microbiota-mediated infectious disease pathways. Collectively, dietary supplementation with 0.60% KDF was identified as the optimal inclusion level for Qingyuan Partridge Chickens in the present trial. Dietary KDF exerts negligible improvement on overall growth performance but markedly improves core slaughter traits, regulates host metabolism and enriches beneficial cecal microbes. This work provides dosage-specific recommendations and a theoretical basis for KDF application in premium native yellow-feathered broilers in China.

Introduction

With the large-scale intensification of the poultry industry, the overuse of in-feed antibiotics has led to critical issues such as pathogenic bacterial resistance and drug residues in animal products (Hamid et al., 2019; Ragaa et al., 2016). Following the widespread ban on antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) across major livestock-producing regions worldwide, new challenges including retarded growth and reduced feed conversion efficiency have emerged (Castanon, 2007), making the development of safe and effective antibiotic alternatives a central priority in animal production. Among numerous candidate alternatives, potassium diformate (KDF) stands out due to its unique mode of action. Upon entering the animal digestive tract in its intact form, KDF gradually dissociates to release formate ions (HCOO⁻), hydrogen ions (H⁺), and potassium ions. The released HCOO⁻ disrupts the protein structure of bacterial cell walls, and H⁺ lowers intestinal pH, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria (Ostling et al., 1993; Windisch et al., 2001). Additionally, undissociated formic acid can penetrate bacterial cell walls and interfere with their metabolic processes, thereby comprehensively modulating the intestinal microbial community (Luise et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2009). KDF also possesses favorable characteristics, including low irritation, convenient transportation, and complete environmental biodegradability, exhibiting broad application prospects in animal production (Luise et al., 2020).

KDF was approved by the European Union as the first non-antibiotic alternative to AGPs in 2001 and was officially authorized for use in pig and broiler chicken feed in China in 2005 (Castanon, 2007; Zhou et al., 2009). Extensive studies have confirmed that KDF can inhibit pathogenic bacteria, promote animal growth, and improve nutrient digestibility. For instance, KDF effectively alleviates intestinal epithelial inflammation induced by enterohaemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) infection and significantly reduces the risk of Salmonella infection in broilers (Chen et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). In a broiler model of necrotic enteritis challenge, KDF enhances disease resistance by modulating the intestinal microbial community, while also improving apparent ileal and fecal nutrient digestibility to promote growth (Mikkelsen et al., 2009). These findings suggest that beyond routine dietary supplementation for gut health and growth promotion, KDF can serve as a short-term intervention for acute necrotic enteritis and intestinal inflammation of other etiologies. Furthermore, KDF has been proven safe and effective in weaned piglets and finishing pigs, demonstrating its application potential across different livestock species (Htoo et al., 2012; Mroz et al., 2002).

The Qingyuan partridge chicken, a high-quality native broiler breed in China, is renowned for its tender meat and unique flavor, conferring high economic value. However, its production still faces challenges, including susceptibility to intestinal pathogenic invasion and suboptimal growth performance. Although the application of KDF has been widely documented in commercial broilers (e.g., white-feathered broilers) and pigs, its comprehensive effects on indigenous chicken breeds, which differ markedly from commercial broilers in rearing environments and growth cycles, remain unclear. Therefore, this 63-d trial compared two KDF inclusion levels against medicated and unmedicated control diets, with primary emphasis on carcass slaughter traits, systemic blood homeostasis, and cecal microbial remodeling. This study aimed to evaluate the application potential and identify the optimal supplemental dosage of KDF in Qingyuan Partridge chicken production, and to provide theoretical and practical technical references for antibiotic-free feeding of indigenous broilers.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

A total of 1,800 one-day-old male Qingyuan Partridge Chicken No. 2 chicks were obtained from Wens Foodstuff Group Co., Ltd. The experiment was conducted at the Broiler Experimental Farm of the Wens Foodstuff Group Research Institute using a litter-based floor rearing system. This study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of South China Agricultural University (Approval ID: SYXK-2024-0136).

Chicks were randomly divided into four groups, each consisting of six replicates of 75 birds. Group 1 (ACC) received a basal diet supplemented with 2.4 g enramycin per 1,000 kg feed (Enramycin Premix, Hisun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China). Group 2 (AFC) received the same basal diet without antibiotics. Groups 3 and 4 were fed the antibiotic-free basal diet directly supplemented with 0.45% and 0.60% KDF, respectively. The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements for yellow-feathered broilers specified in the Chinese National Standard for Compound Feeds for Layers and Broilers (Standardization Administration of the People’s Republic of China, 2020). The experimental period lasted 63 days, divided into three phases: starter (days 1–22), grower (days 23–42) and finisher (days 43–63). All groups were managed under identical conditions, with feeding, watering, cleaning, disinfection and disease prevention protocols strictly following the facility’s standard operating procedures. During the starter phase, the initial brooding temperature was set at 35°C and reduced by 1–2°C daily until stabilizing at 20°C. House relative humidity was maintained at 60%–70% for the first two weeks, then lowered to 55%–60%. Moderate ventilation was maintained in the poultry house to keep ammonia concentration below 10 ppm. The lighting program was implemented as follows: 24 hours of continuous illumination on days 1–3, 23 hours on days 4–7, followed by a daily reduction of 1 hour until a constant 16-hour photoperiod was established. Clean drinking water, ad libitum feed access and dry, clean litter were provided throughout the experiment.

Growth performance determination

At the end of each phase, pen-level data including initial / final body weight, average feed intake (AFI), average daily gain (ADG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), mortality count and dead bird body weight were recorded. Birds were fasted for 12–14 hours before weighing, with ad libitum access to water maintained during fasting.

Blood index determination

On day 63, one bird with body weight close to the pen average was selected from each replicate pen for collection of arterial and venous blood to measure multiple blood indices (n = 6 replicate pens per treatment). After a 12–14 hours fasting period, brachial vein blood was collected into EDTA-anticoagulant tubes for hematology analysis and plain tubes for serum biochemistry analysis (Xinle Medical, Shijiazhuang, China). Serum was separated from non-anticoagulant samples by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min and analyzed using the ADVIA XPT system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Blood samples in anticoagulant tubes were subjected to routine hematology testing using the ADVIA 2120i fully automatic hematology analysis system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). In addition, blood from the common carotid artery was collected into sodium heparin-coated vacuum tubes (Xinle Medical, Shijiazhuang, China) and analyzed for blood gas parameters via the RAPIDLab® 348EX blood gas analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany).

Slaughter performance and organ index determination

On day 63, two birds with body weight close to the pen average were selected from each replicate pen for slaughter performance and organ index determination. The mean value of the two birds within each pen was calculated for subsequent statistical analysis, with n = 6 replicate pens per treatment. According to the Terminology and Statistical Methods for Poultry Performance Testing, the following parameters were determined: slaughter rate (SR), semi-eviscerated weight ratio (SEWR), eviscerated weight ratio (EWR), pectoral muscle ratio (PMR), abdominal fat ratio (AFR) and organ indices. Dissections were conducted under professional supervision in accordance with animal ethics and welfare standards.

Cecal microbiota 16S rRNA sequencing analysis

After slaughter, cecal contents were collected from one representative chicken per replicate pen for 16S rRNA sequencing (n = 6 replicate pens per treatment). Total DNA was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit (QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). After verification of DNA integrity and purity via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplification was performed using barcoded primers targeting the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. PCR products were recovered and purified, and libraries were constructed according to the protocol of the ALFA-SEQ DNA Library Prep Kit (FINDROP, Guangzhou, China). Amplicon libraries were subjected to PE250 paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp software (v0.14.1) (Chen et al., 2018) for sliding window quality trimming of paired-end sequences (parameters: -W 4 -M 20; Q20 ≥ 99%, minimum read length 50 bp). Primer sequences were removed using cutadapt software (DOI:10.14806/ej.17.1.200), yielding paired-end clean reads. Paired-end reads were assembled using the fastq_mergepairs module of usearch software (v10, http://www.drive5.com/usearch/) with a minimum overlap of 16 bp and a maximum of 5 bp mismatches in the overlap region. Unqualified tags were filtered to obtain raw tags, which were further trimmed using fastp (parameters: -W 4 -M 20) to generate clean tags. Following sequencing and quality control, the average length of clean tags across all samples was 225 bp, with ≥ 20 000 clean tags per sample as the minimum effective sequencing depth threshold.

Clustering analysis of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was performed using the UPARSE algorithm with a 97% similarity threshold (Edgar, 2013), yielding a total of 1,106 OTUs. Representative sequences of each OTU were annotated against the SILVA 138 database using the -sintax function of usearch to obtain taxonomic information with 0.7 confidence parameter (Quast et al., 2013). R software (v4.3.0) was used for shared / unique species statistics, community composition analysis and species abundance clustering. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon) were calculated using the -alpha_div module of usearch (v10) and the vegan package in R. Beta diversity was analyzed via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix using the vegan package in R. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify significantly differential species among groups, with the significance level α of the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test set to 0.05 and the log-transformed LDA score threshold set to 2.0. OTU abundances were normalized via PICRUSt (V1.1.4) and submitted for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional prediction based on GreenGenes gg_13_5 annotations (DeSantis et al., 2006; Ismaeil et al., 2026).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for growth performance, slaughter performance, routine blood parameters, serum biochemical indices and blood gas parameters were conducted using SAS 8.1 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Residual normality and variance homogeneity were verified via the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively, prior to parametric testing. One-way ANOVA was used to detect overall treatment effects, and Tukey’s honest significant difference post-hoc test was adopted for multiple pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 was defined as a trend.

Statistical analysis of cecal microbiota data was performed as follows. Alpha diversity indices were compared by one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric tests as appropriate. Intergroup differences in beta diversity were assessed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix, with PERMDISP confirming homogeneous community dispersion (P = 0.521). Genus-level relative abundances were analyzed via Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum tests (α = 0.05).

Results

The effects of dietary KDF supplementation on growth performance across different feeding phases are summarized in Table 3. The ACC group exhibited the highest FBW and ADG, which were significantly higher than those of the 0.60% KDF group. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, dietary KDF supplementation had no significant effect on the Chao1 or Shannon index of the cecal microbiota (n = 6, P > 0.05). PCoA revealed no significant differences in cecal microbial community among groups (PERMANOVA: pseudo-F = 1.05, R² = 0.124, P = 0.387; Fig. 1C). At the phylum level, the dominant phyla across all groups were Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes and Tenericutes (Fig. 1D). Compared with the ACC group, both the AFC and KDF-supplemented groups exhibited a numerical reduction in Bacteroidetes relative abundance (ACC, 0.674; AFC, 0.632; 0.45% KDF, 0.631; 0.60% KDF, 0.618) and a numerical increase in Firmicutes (ACC, 0.300; AFC, 0.345; 0.45% KDF, 0.342; 0.60% KDF, 0.360). At the genus level, the dominant genera in the ACC and AFC groups were Alistipes, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus and Barnesiella. Discussion

As a non-antibiotic feed additive approved by the European Union (Pearlin et al., 2020), KDF has been widely documented to exhibit antimicrobial, growth-promoting and nutrient digestibility-enhancing effects in livestock, poultry and aquatic species. However, its comprehensive effects, dose-response relationship and underlying regulatory mechanisms in native broiler breeds remain largely unexplored. In this study, using Qingyuan Partridge chickens as a model, we systematically evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with 0.45% and 0.60% KDF on growth performance, slaughter performance, blood metabolic parameters and cecal microbiota, with comparisons to the ACC and AFC groups. We demonstrate that dietary supplementation with 0.60% KDF maintains growth performance comparable to that of antibiotic-supplemented diets throughout the rearing period, while significantly enhancing carcass slaughter traits and remodeling the cecal microbial community, thereby showing strong potential as a feed additive for native broilers. The observed regulatory effects are consistent with previous findings while also exhibiting breed- and dose-specific patterns.

Regarding growth performance, we found that 0.60% KDF exerted a transient inhibitory effect on ADG and FCR during the starter phase, whereas no significant differences in core growth indicators (ADG, AFI, FCR) were observed among groups over the entire 63-day rearing period, indicating that KDF cannot serve as a robust growth promoter for Qingyuan Partridge chickens. This result aligns closely with the findings of Mikkelsen et al. in a necrotic enteritis challenge model in Cobb broilers, who reported that dietary KDF supplementation had no significant effect on weight gain or FCR but effectively reduced necrotic enteritis-related mortality (Mikkelsen et al., 2009). In the present study, the 0.60% KDF group had numerically the lowest starter-phase mortality (16.5% of the AFC group), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.247). This numerical trend suggests a potential health benefit, which requires further validation in larger-scale trials. Notably, Chen et al. found that dietary supplementation with 0.4% KDF significantly increased ADG and reduced FCR during the starter phase in Arbor Acres (AA) white-feathered broilers (Chen et al., 2024), which differs from the effects observed in our study. This discrepancy may be attributed to breed-specific characteristics: AA broilers are fast-growing with rapid intestinal development and higher tolerance to the acidifying effect of KDF, whereas Qingyuan Partridge chickens, as a slow-growing native breed, have immature intestinal function during the starter phase. We speculate that the rapid acidification induced by high-dose KDF may slightly reduce feed palatability and increase the gastrointestinal metabolic burden of young chicks, leading to transient growth inhibition, and this inhibitory effect disappeared during the grower and finisher phases as intestinal development matured. Additionally, the 0.45% KDF group showed significantly higher mortality during the finisher phase, a phenomenon not observed in the 0.60% KDF group. This is consistent with the finding of Li et al. in Cherry Valley ducks, where 0.8% KDF significantly improved growth performance, while the effect of 1.2% KDF was diminished, confirming that KDF has a relatively narrow effective dose window (Li et al., 2024). The elevated mortality in the 0.45% KDF group during the finisher phase may be attributed to an insufficient dosage that fails to sustain inhibition of intestinal pathogenic bacteria. No clinical symptoms associated with infectious diseases were observed in any experimental group throughout the entire trial period. However, as postmortem examinations were not performed on dead birds, the specific etiology underlying the increased mortality in this group remains unclear, which constitutes one of the main limitations of the present study.

With respect to slaughter performance, the 0.60% KDF group exhibited the highest SR, SEWR and EWR among all groups, which were significantly higher than those in the 0.45% KDF group and comparable to those in the ACC group. This result is consistent with the findings of Chen et al. in AA broilers, who confirmed that dietary KDF significantly improved core slaughter performance including SEWR, EWR and PMR, indicating that KDF effectively improves carcass quality with important economic implications (Chen et al., 2024). However, unlike the findings in fast-growing broilers, the PMR of the 0.60% KDF group in this study was significantly lower than that of the 0.45% KDF group. This difference may be related to the breed-specific muscle deposition pattern of Qingyuan Partridge chickens: as a high-quality native breed, muscle deposition in these birds is characterized by uniform carcass development rather than the pectoralis-prioritized deposition observed in fast-growing broilers (Ding et al., 2021). The improved nutrient utilization efficiency induced by KDF appears to be allocated more toward overall slaughter performance such as eviscerated weight ratio rather than specifically toward pectoral muscle deposition, aligning with the breeding objectives for native broilers that prioritize meat quality and flavor. However, post-slaughter meat quality traits were not evaluated in the present study. We will refine the experimental protocol to incorporate this assessment in follow-up research.

White blood cell count showed a near-significant trend among groups (P = 0.080), with numerically higher values in the ACC group than in the AFC and KDF groups. This trend may arise from two potential effects of long-term enramycin exposure. First, dietary antibiotics may modulate gut microbiota composition and trigger mild innate immune activation in the intestinal mucosa, increasing leukocyte mobilization into the peripheral circulation (Redmond et al., 2011; Sawerus et al., 2022). Second, sustained antibiotic administration may alter the trafficking and distribution dynamics of immune cells between lymphoid tissues and the bloodstream, rather than simply enhancing systemic immune function. As this difference only reached a statistical trend rather than formal significance, the underlying mechanism remains speculative. Further investigations targeting inflammatory cytokine profiles and tissue immune cell localization are required to verify these hypotheses. The potential confounding effect of subtle environmental stress variations across groups should also be considered when interpreting this result (Bedanova et al., 2010; Fathi et al., 2025).

The AFC group had significantly higher UA and BUN levels than the KDF-supplemented groups. UA and BUN are terminal metabolites of protein catabolism in poultry, and their elevated levels directly reflect enhanced protein breakdown and reduced nitrogen utilization efficiency (Harr, 2002; Scope et al., 2020). Compared with the AFC group, the 0.60% KDF group showed 47.4% lower BUN and 26.0% lower UA levels, this can be interpreted that KDF effectively reduces protein catabolism, improves nitrogen utilization efficiency and promotes protein deposition in carcass tissues. This is fully consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. in weaned piglets, who reported that KDF significantly improved the apparent total tract digestibility of dry matter and crude protein, with digestibility improvements negatively correlated with FCR reduction, directly validating the nutrient utilization-enhancing effect of KDF (Zhang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the AFC group showed significantly elevated LDH and CK levels, which were markedly reversed by KDF supplementation. These parameters are sensitive markers of systemic tissue damage and inflammatory response (Rezende et al., 2021), and their reduction indicates that KDF effectively alleviates subclinical inflammation and tissue injury in poultry under antibiotic-free conditions. This conclusion is directly supported by the in vitro study of Lin et al., who demonstrated that KDF alleviates EHEC-induced intestinal inflammatory damage by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and upregulating intestinal tight junction protein expression to maintain barrier integrity (Lin et al., 2024). Notably, elevated LDH and CK levels may also stem from catching stress, muscle trauma during blood sampling or other physiological fluctuations. Given that all birds were reared under identical housing conditions and subjected to consistent experimental procedures, the reduced LDH and CK levels in KDF-treated groups may suggest that dietary KDF contributes to attenuating physiological stress responses in chickens. Additionally, the 0.60% KDF group had significantly higher serum glucose levels, consistent with the findings of Li et al. in Cherry Valley ducks supplemented with 0.8% KDF, which may provide more available energy for tissue synthesis and vital activities (Li et al., 2024). In this study, birds in the 0.45% KDF group had significantly higher serum CO₂CP than the AFC group and lower chloride concentrations than both the AFC and ACC groups, with no significant difference in the AG. This pattern may be attributed to two potential mechanisms. First, KDF may dissociate into formate and K⁺ in the intestinal tract. Absorbed formate could be fully oxidized via hepatic one-carbon metabolism, consuming intracellular H⁺ and potentially elevating plasma bicarbonate levels, reflected by increased CO₂CP (Pietzke et al., 2020). To maintain plasma electroneutrality, the kidneys may reduce chloride reabsorption and increase its urinary excretion to compensate for the rise in bicarbonate, thus preserving a stable AG (Cowieson et al., 2026; Flaherty, 2013). Second, these changes may also relate to improved intestinal health. In the AFC group, enriched pathogenic taxa and subclinical inflammation may activate epithelial chloride channels, causing excessive loss of chloride and bicarbonate into the gut lumen (Timbermont et al., 2011). By inhibiting pathogens including Gammaproteobacteria and alleviating intestinal inflammation, 0.45% KDF may reduce such pathological ion loss. Meanwhile, absorbed formate may partially replace chloride in systemic anion transport (Ragaa et al., 2016). Collectively, these effects may lower serum chloride and increase bicarbonate retention, with reciprocal changes that maintain AG stability. The alterations in the 0.45% KDF group therefore likely represent a chloride-responsive, normal-AG elevation of metabolic alkali reserve, reflecting coordinated physiological acid-base and electrolyte regulation. The absence of further changes in the 0.60% KDF group may stem from two factors. First, systemic acid-base homeostasis may exhibit a plateau effect, where renal compensatory capacity offsets additional alkali load beyond a certain dose threshold. Second, higher KDF doses may exert stronger local intestinal acidification, with more formate acting locally as an antimicrobial in the gut lumen rather than being absorbed to exert systemic alkalinizing effects.

The regulatory effects of KDF on host metabolism and phenotypic traits observed in this study are primarily mediated through its selective modulation of the cecal microbial community. We found that KDF supplementation did not alter the alpha diversity of cecal microbiota, consistent with previous reports in broilers and pigs (Luise et al., 2020), indicating that KDF does not cause non-specific, broad-spectrum disruption of the intestinal microecosystem but rather exerts its effects by selectively regulating the relative abundance of specific microbial taxa. At the phylum level, KDF supplementation numerically increased the relative abundance of Firmicutes and decreased that of Bacteroidetes, a pattern fully consistent with the results of Li et al. in Cherry Valley ducks (Li et al., 2024). Firmicutes is the dominant phylum in the chicken cecum and is primarily responsible for fermenting dietary fiber and complex carbohydrates to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), the main energy source for intestinal epithelial cells. In this study, dietary KDF supplementation elevated the cecal Firmicutes / Bacteroidetes ratio. This microbial shift is widely recognized as a classic biomarker of intestinal metabolic benefits, directly linked to enhanced host energy harvest and nutrient utilization efficiency (Obianwuna et al., 2024). Consistent evidence from native and commercial broiler models confirms that an increased F / B ratio is positively associated with superior growth performance and feed conversion efficiency (Deryabin et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2022), providing a microbial mechanistic basis for KDF-induced improvements in nitrogen utilization and slaughter performance. At the genus level, we found that the 0.60% KDF group significantly enriched Faecalibacterium, while the 0.45% KDF group enriched Phascolarctobacterium, both of which are well-recognized butyrate-producing bacteria (Ikeyama et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2021). Similarly, Sun et al. found in a Salmonella Pullorum infection model in broilers that dietary supplementation with 0.5% KDF significantly promoted the colonization of probiotics including Faecalibacterium (Sun et al., 2022). Butyrate produced by these bacteria not only serves as the preferred energy source for intestinal epithelial cells to maintain barrier integrity but also exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, suggesting a potential mechanistic link with the reduced levels of blood inflammatory markers observed in this study. Gammaproteobacteria is a well-documented reservoir of opportunistic pathogens in the gut, encompassing numerous pathogenic taxa such as Enterobacteriaceae, Salmonella, and Escherichia-Shigella (Moreira de Gouveia et al., 2024). Dietary KDF supplementation significantly reduced the abundance of this bacterial class in this study. This result aligns with the findings of Mikkelsen et al., who reported that 4.5 g/kg KDF significantly reduced the abundance of Clostridium perfringens and Enterobacteriaceae in the broilers jejunum (Mikkelsen et al., 2009). Sun et al. also confirmed that KDF significantly reduced Salmonella load in the chicken cecum and alleviated intestinal pathological damage, indicating that the specific inhibitory effect of KDF on intestinal pathogenic bacteria is a core mechanism underlying its ability to improve disease resistance and reduce mortality in poultry (Sun et al., 2022). However, the abundance of Gammaproteobacteria was significantly elevated in the cecum of the ACC group. This observation indicates that enramycin supplementation may impose selective pressure on antibiotic-resistant taxa within the gut microbiota, further underscoring the necessity of strict regulation of feed antibiotic use in poultry production. In addition, functional prediction revealed that the 0.45% KDF group was mainly enriched in amino acid metabolism and energy metabolism pathways, while the 0.60% KDF group downregulated infectious disease-related pathways. This dose-dependent functional difference may partially explain why the 0.45% KDF group exhibited moderate metabolic regulatory effects but increased mortality during the finisher phase due to insufficient antibacterial activity, whereas the 0.60% KDF group achieved the optimal balance between metabolic regulation and antimicrobial efficacy, resulting in the best overall performance. This further confirms the narrow effective dose window of KDF and provides a direct basis for its precise application in native broilers. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the KEGG pathway data herein represent only predicted functional potential. Furthermore, the annotation accuracy of the PICRUSt pipeline declines when applied to poultry-specific microbial taxa and lineage-specific functional pathways. Accordingly, follow-up studies employing metagenomics, metatranscriptomics or untargeted metabolomics are warranted to elucidate the precise metabolic mechanisms underlying the regulatory effects of KDF.

Taken together, this study systematically elucidated the application effects and regulatory mechanisms of KDF in Qingyuan Partridge chickens, filling the research gap regarding KDF application in high-quality native Chinese broiler breeds, and identified 0.60% as the optimal supplementation dose. KDF exerts its beneficial effects by selectively regulating the cecal microbial community—enriching butyrate-producing beneficial bacteria, inhibiting opportunistic pathogens, and optimizing microbial metabolic functions—which in turn maintains intestinal barrier integrity, alleviates systemic subclinical inflammation, improves nutrient utilization efficiency and ultimately enhances carcass quality while preserving growth performance comparable to that achieved with antibiotic-supplemented diets.

This study has several limitations. First, mechanistic indicators including intestinal morphological parameters, cecal short-chain fatty acid concentrations, intestinal barrier-related protein expression and inflammatory cytokine levels were not quantified. Future studies could focus on these parameters to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying KDF-mediated regulation of intestinal health in native broilers. Second, our findings are constrained by the single-facility, single-flock trial design, and thus may not be fully generalizable to commercial production scenarios. Third, Farsani et al. demonstrated in rainbow trout that the combination of KDF with probiotics exerted synergistic effects, significantly improving growth performance, digestive enzyme activity, and health status (Farsani et al., 2020). Future research may also explore the synergistic application of KDF with probiotics, exogenous enzymes or other antibiotic alternatives in native broilers to further enhance its efficacy in antibiotic-free production systems.

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with 0.60% KDF was identified as the optimal nutritional regimen for Qingyuan Partridge chickens, as it preserved growth performance comparable to that of birds fed antibiotic-supplemented diets while concurrently improving key carcass slaughter traits (SR, SEWR and EWR), regulating systemic metabolic homeostasis and enhancing intestinal health. The improvement in intestinal health was achieved through reshaping the cecal microbial community structure—specifically by enriching beneficial probiotic taxa (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Phascolarctobacterium) and inhibiting pathogenic microbial groups (e.g., Gammaproteobacteria)—and optimizing microbial functional pathways to upregulate nutrient metabolism and downregulate disease-associated pathways. This study provides the systematic evidence for KDF application in Chinese native yellow-feathered broilers, validates its potential as a safe and effective feed additive for native broiler production and provides a robust theoretical and practical foundation for the sustainable development of the poultry industry.
