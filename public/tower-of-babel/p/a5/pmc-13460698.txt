Device-free, Cost-effective Sacrospinous Fixation Using Absorbable versus Nonabsorbable Sutures: A 5-Year Prospective, Cohort Study
Authors: Ruchika Garg, Neelansha Varshney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:To evaluate the efficacy and safety of device-free concurrent sacrospinous fixation (SSF) performed at the time of vaginal hysterectomy using absorbable (polyglactin no. 1) versus nonabsorbable (polypropylene no. 1) sutures for preventing vaginal vault prolapse.Materials and Methods:A prospective, comparative, cohort study included 185 women undergoing vaginal hysterectomy for pelvic organ prolapse (POP) stages II–IV from January 2016 to December 2018. Prophylactic SSF was performed in 90 patients using Polyglactin No. 1 sutures while in 95 patients using nonabsorbable sutures (Polypropylene No. 1). In both groups, Garg’s technique was performed using Allis tissue forceps rather than a Capio device. Absorbable sutures (n = 90) were passed through the full thickness from sacrospinous ligament and tied to the vault while nonabsorbable sutures (n = 95) were placed submucosally in a double helix pattern and tied beneath the mucosa. The outcomes included anatomical success (POP-Q stage < II), recurrence rates, complications, and quality of life. Follow-up occurred at 1 month, 6 months, and annually for 5 years.Results:Anatomical success was achieved in 89/90 (98.9%) patients till 5 years with absorbable sutures, with a vault prolapse recurrence rate of 1.1% (1/90) versus 4.2% (4/95) with nonabsorbable sutures (P = 0.37). The complications included fever (1.1%) and urinary tract infection (UTI) (3.3%). No suture exposure or granuloma formation occurred with absorbable sutures, unlike 2.1% with nonabsorbable sutures (P = 0.50). Garg’s technique reduced the cost by avoiding Capio device.Conclusion:Concurrent sacrospinous fixation using Polyglactin no. 1 sutures with a simplified device-free technique demonstrated high long-term anatomical success rate and lower complication rates comparable to nonabsorbable sutures. This approach may be particularly useful in the resource-constrained settings.

INTRODUCTION

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) affects up to 50% of parous women at some stage of life.[1] Vaginal vault prolapse occurs in approximately 1% cases after 3 years and rises to 5%–15% within 15 years following hysterectomy when prophylactic measures of preventing vaginal vault prolapse was not performed during hysterectomy.[2] The vault prolapse occurs due to preexisting weak pelvic floor muscles before the hysterectomy, multiple vaginal deliveries, menopause (drop in estrogen levels leads to a loss of collagen and reduced tissue strength), chronic constipation, chronic bronchitis, repeated heavy weight lifting and connective tissue disorders. There are various nonsurgical treatments for mild vaginal vault prolapse such as lifestyle changes (treating constipation and avoiding heavy lifting), vaginal Pessary, and Kegel exercises.

There are various surgical treatments for moderate and severe cases of vaginal vault prolapse such as abdominal sacrocolpopexy (a surgical mesh is used to attach the vagina to the sacrum), sacrospinous ligament fixation, uterosacral ligament fixation, and colpocleisis. Sacrospinous fixation (SSF) is a standard vaginal approach for apical support, traditionally using nonabsorbable sutures.[3] Biodegradable (absorbable sutures like Polyglactin) sutures have an important role in sacrospinous fixation (SSF) for vaginal vault prolapse since it may reduce the complications such as suture exposure and granuloma formation which can occur while using nonabsorbable sutures.[4] In low-resource settings, expensive suture-capturing devices like Capio may not always be available. Our study evaluates prophylactic SSF using Polyglactin No. 1 sutures and our simple technique, comparing the outcomes with nonabsorbable sutures over 5 years. A simplified technique utilizing Allis tissue forceps and routine surgical instruments avoids the need for expensive suture-capturing devices, thereby improving feasibility in the low-resource settings. This comparative cohort study aimed to evaluate the 5-year anatomical outcomes and complication rates of concurrent SSF performed with an absorbable suture (Polyglactin no. 1) versus a nonabsorbable suture (Polypropylene no. 1) using a simplified, device-free technique.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

A prospective comparative study was conducted at S. N Medical College, Agra from January 2016 to December 2018. Concurrent sacrospinous fixation was done during vaginal hysterectomy in women considered at high risk for future vault prolapse – such as those with obesity, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive airway disease, chronic constipation, or poor connective tissue support were included. The exclusion criteria included malignancy or surgical contraindications. We enrolled 185 women aged 40–75 years undergoing vaginal hysterectomy for POP stages II–IV (POP-Q system). In 95 women, sacrospinous fixation was done with nonabsorbable sutures (polypropylene no. 1) from 2016 to 2018 were used for the comparison with 90 patients where sacrospinous fixation was done using absorbable suture (Polyglactin no. 1) using the same surgical technique. Ethical approval was obtained (IRB no. 2016-OBG-032) and informed consent was obtained.

Surgical technique

After vaginal hysterectomy and cystocele repair (if required), we proceeded to sacrospinous fixation (SSF).

A posterior vaginal wall incision was made to expose the rectovaginal space.

A laparoscopic light source was used for the enhanced visualization of the pararectal space.[5]

Pararectal dissection

Blunt dissection was performed laterally to expose the pararectal space on the right side, with the help of index finger by gentle sweeping and rolling movement until ischial spines are reached. SSF was routinely performed on the right side to reduce the risk of rectosigmoid injury. Sacrospinous ligament (SSL) was identified. Three long bladed retractors were used to retract the peritoneum and rectum to clearly visualize the sacrospinous ligament [Figures 1-4]. Tip of suction cannula and right angle forceps were used to remove the fascia over the sacrospinous ligament.

Ligament grasping

Allis tissue forceps was used to securely grasp the SSL 1.5–2 cm medial to the ischial spine (avoiding pudendal neurovascular structures), eliminating the need for expensive Capio suture-capturing device.

Suture placement

For absorbable sutures (Polyglactin No. 1 on 40 mm half-circle, a heavy round body needle), two sutures were passed through the SSL and coccygeus muscle complex (2 cm medial to ischial spine to avoid injury to neurovascular bundle), using a 12-inch long needle holder from upside down, suture is then passed through the full thickness of the vaginal vault, and tied to secure the apex [Figure 5].

Final step is suture is passed through the full thickness of the vault, note the elevation of vault as the suture is tightened

For nonabsorbable sutures (Polypropylene No. 1), sutures were placed submucosally in a double helix pattern and tied beneath the mucosa to minimize exposure risk.[5]

Closure: The vaginal epithelium was closed with absorbable sutures using continuous locking stitches, ensuring no dead space. This technique will be referred as Garg’s technique.

Concomitant repairs (e.g., anterior/posterior colporrhaphy) were performed as needed.[6] Prophylactic antibiotics were administered.[7]

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: Anatomical success (POP-Q stage <II at the vaginal apex).

Secondary outcomes: Vault prolapse recurrence (defined as vaginal vault at POP-Q stage ≥2 during the follow-up of the sacrospinous fixation) and complications (e.g., fever, urinary tract infection (UTI), suture exposure, and granuloma formation). Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) Scale and the Pelvic Floor Disability Index-20 (PFDI-20).

The English questionnaire was translated and culturally adapted into Hindi following the WHO translation guidelines (forward translation, expert panel review, back-translation, and pretesting) similar to previously published Indian research, ensuring comprehensibility for Hindi-speaking participants. The scores were collected at the 1-month, 6-month, and annual follow-up visits

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (software developed by IBM Corp., New York, USA). The continuous variables (e.g., PISQ-12 scores) were compared using Independent samples t-test and categorical variables (e.g., recurrence rates) using Chi-square tests. Kaplan–Meier analysis estimated recurrence-free survival, with log-rank tests comparing absorbable and nonabsorbable suture groups. P <0.05 was statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The absorbable suture cohort (n = 90) had a mean age of 58.4 ± 7.2 years, mean parity of 3.1 ± 1.2, and mean body mass index (BMI) of 23.3 ± 4.1 kg/m2. POP stages were: Stage II (48.9%), Stage III (38.9%), and Stage IV (12.2%). Anatomical outcomes

None of the patients developed vault prolapse (POP-Q stage ≥2) till 4 years of follow-up of SSF surgery. Vault prolapse recurrence occurred in 1/90 (1.1%) patients with absorbable sutures (at 5 years) versus 4/95 (4.2%) with nonabsorbable sutures (at 5 years) (P = 0.37). In other words at 5 years, 89/90 (98.9%) patients in the absorbable suture group achieved POP-Q stage <II, compared to 91/95 (95.7%) in the nonabsorbable group (P = 0.37). Statistical analysis showed higher success rate for absorbable sutures, however, the difference was not statistically significant (log-rank P = 0.37).

Complications

The complications in the absorbable suture group included fever (1/90,1.1%), transient buttock pain (5/90, 5.56%), and UTI (3/90, 3.3%). The nonabsorbable group had higher complication rates: Fever (3/95, 3.16%, P = 0.62), transient buttock pain (8/95, 8.4%, P = 0.57), UTI (5/95, 5.3%, P = 0.72), and suture exposure/granuloma formation (2/95, 2.1%, P = 0.50) shown in Table 1. No cuff infection, chronic pain, ischiorectal abscess, nerve injury, hematoma, or urinary dysfunction occurred in our study. These complications were detected more in nonabsorbable suture group but were not statistically significant. Suture exposure/granuloma occurred only in the nonabsorbable group (2.1%, P = 0.50).

Cost efficiency

Garg’s technique, using Allis tissue forceps and routine instruments, eliminated Capio device cost, reducing procedural costs significantly.

Patient satisfaction and quality of life

High satisfaction rates reported for both sutures, with the Patient Global Impression of Improvement scores ranging from “much improved” to “very much improved.” PFDI-20 and the Prolapse Quality of Life questionnaire (P-QOL) also demonstrated significant improvements in scores postsurgery for both sutures and Garg’s technique, indicating an enhanced quality of life and reduced prolapse symptoms. Patient-reported outcomes demonstrated significant improvement following surgery. The median PGI-I score at the 5-year follow-up was 2 (“Much better”) in both the absorbable and nonabsorbable suture groups (P = 0.85). Preoperative and 5-year postoperative PFDI-20 scores showed a significant reduction in symptom burden, from a mean (standard deviation) of 125.4 (28.7) to 32.1 (12.3) in the absorbable suture group and from 118.9 (30.1) to 35.6 (14.8) in the nonabsorbable suture group (P < 0.001 for both within-group comparisons; P = 0.42 for between-group difference at 5 years).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that concurrent SSF with Polyglactin (absorbable) No. 1 sutures using Garg’s Technique achieved a numerically lower vault prolapse recurrence rate of 1.1% compared to nonabsorbable sutures. Absorbable suture was superior to nonabsorbable suture and had a potentially favorable safety profile. Full-thickness suturing of absorbable sutures avoided suture exposure and granuloma formation, unlike nonabsorbable sutures (2.1%, P = 0.50).

The study by murillo et al. showed that Vaginal SSLF with delayed absorbable suture (polydiaxanone) provided durability similar to permanent suture in terms of prolapse recurrence.[8] While the study by Pratiksha Gupta et al. showed no recurrence of vaginal vault prolapse in any of the 32 patients who undergone sacrospinous fixation using Polydiaxone suture.[9]

Garg’s Technique, using Allis tissue forceps and endoscopic lighting, enhanced visualization and cost-efficiency, aligning with simplified SSF approaches. The operating time was 7–15 min for pursing SSL fixation alone. The recurrence rates are influenced more by the preoperative stage of prolapse rather than the choice of the suture material. The lower recurrence rate observed with absorbable sutures supports the concept that early degradation allows fibrosis and scar formation to provide durable apical support.[10] Nonabsorbable sutures, despite submucosal placement, carried exposure risks. Complication rates were lower with absorbable sutures as compared to nonabsorbable suture.

The study by Ramesh Kumar et al. showed that the rrecurrence of prolapse after left sided sacrospinous fixation using nonabsorbable polypropylene suture occurred in 25% patients while the need for repeat surgery was 17% in that study.[11]

In the study by Dangal et al., right-sided sacrospinous fixation was done using Polydiaxanone suture and on 6 months’ follow-up, 5.2% women complained of occasional right buttock pain, 5.2% patients had cystocele, which did not require surgery, 2.1% patients had vault prolapse and 1.05% patient had urinary incontinence.[12]

The study by Anna Padoa et al. compared the efficacy and safety of sacrospinous ligament fixation (SSLF) using permanent versus absorbable suture materials 12 months following surgery which showed anatomical success rate (POP ≤ stage 2 at 12 months) similar between both the groups (69% in Group 1 vs. 67% in Group 2 [P = 0.77]).[13]

There were statistically significant improvements in sexual function, dyspareunia, and body image in both groups, suggesting that the apical support type does not adversely affect the sexual outcomes.

Limitations and areas for further research

The limitations include the single-centered design. Larger multicentered randomized trials are needed to validate these findings and further evaluate the long-term outcomes across the diverse populations.

CONCLUSION

In this 5-year comparative cohort study, concurrent SSF using Polyglactin No. 1 absorbable suture via a simplified, device-free Garg’s technique resulted in high anatomical success rates, a lower incidence of complications, and no suture exposure. Recurrence rates were numerically lower compared to nonabsorbable sutures, although this difference was not statistically significant. Garg’s technique presents a safe, effective, and cost-efficient option for apical support during vaginal hysterectomy, particularly in the resource-conscious settings. Future randomized trials are warranted to confirm these findings.
