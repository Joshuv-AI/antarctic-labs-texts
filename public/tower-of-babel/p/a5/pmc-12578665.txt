Effectiveness of the presence of diabetologists for perioperative complications in patients with diabetes undergoing colorectal cancer surgery: A nationwide inpatient database in Japan
Authors: Masataka Shikata, Atsushi Goto, Sayuri Shimizu, Nozomu Kamei, Daisuke Chujo, Itaru Endo, Akira Shimada, Kiyohide Fushimi, Kohjiro Ueki, Kazuyuki Tobe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionLittle is known about the effect of diabetologists on perioperative complication risk. Given that colorectal cancer surgery is known to carry a high complication risk among patients with diabetes, this study aimed to examine the association between the presence of diabetologists at the facility level and the risk of perioperative complications during colorectal cancer surgery.Materials and MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we sourced the number of board‐certified diabetologists from the Japan Diabetes Society and combined it with Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) data, encompassing receipt data from acute care facilities across Japan. We used a modified Poisson model to estimate the risk of perioperative complications associated with the presence of diabetologists by adjusting for potential confounding patient and facility factors.ResultsAnalysis included 887 facilities, of which 299 (34%) had no diabetologists. A total of 24,714 patients with diabetes underwent colorectal cancer surgery between April 2018 and March 2019, with a median age of 73 years (interquartile range: 67–79 years) and 16,274 (66%) men. There were 3,165 (13%) perioperative complications. After adjustment, the risk of perioperative complications was 0.86 times (95% confidence interval: 0.77–0.96) lower in centers with at least one diabetologist than in those without a diabetologist.ConclusionsOur study suggests that the presence of a diabetologist may be important in reducing the risk of perioperative complications during colorectal cancer surgery in patients with diabetes. Future studies factoring in other facility factors and surgical types may be required to further validate our findings.Keywords: Board‐certified diabetologists, Diabetes, Perioperative complications

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is a common disease 1 . Populations with poorly controlled glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels (HbA1c ≥7% [53 mmol/mol)] have longer hospital stays and an increased risk of postoperative complications 2 . In 2008, the prevalence of diabetes in patients who underwent surgery was 14.9%, which increased to 15.3% by 2020, suggesting a further increase in this percentage by 2030 3 . Hence, the importance of perioperative management in patients with diabetes is expected to increase. In Japan, hospitalists who are dedicated exclusively to inpatient care are not commonly employed; instead, surgical attending physicians are typically responsible for managing hospitalized patients 4 . Consequently, diabetologists become involved in perioperative glycemic management upon request from the attending physician. Their involvement often includes providing specific instructions regarding insulin dosage adjustments and modifications to administration schedules. Thus, diabetologists play a critical role in maintaining glycemic control during hospitalization, particularly in the perioperative period.

Interventions by diabetologists decrease perioperative complications and shorten the length of hospital stay in patients with diabetes 5 , 6 , 7 . These findings indicate that the risk of perioperative complications is lower and the length of hospital stay is shorter in facilities with a diabetologist than in those without one. However, no studies have reported on the allocation of diabetologists at the facility level. To fill this knowledge gap, colorectal cancer was selected as the target disease because according to a report from the Japan Nosocomial Infection Surveillance, 49% (5,650/11,475) of surgical site infections in gastrointestinal surgeries (170,170 cases) are caused by colorectal surgery 8 , which is an important cause of perioperative complications. Furthermore, people with diabetes mellitus have an increased risk of perioperative complications in colorectal cancer surgery 9 , 10 , 11 .

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate perioperative complications and length of hospital stay in patients with diabetes undergoing colorectal cancer surgery by analyzing claims data from acute care facilities across Japan and data on the number of diabetologists from the Japan Diabetes Society, and comparing facilities with diabetologists to those without. Through this, we aimed to understand the distribution of diabetologists across Japan and gain knowledge of the overall presence of diabetologists per facility and patient outcomes. We believe that our study may help assess the role of diabetologists in the management of perioperative diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from two databases: the Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) database and the Diabetes Specialist database. The DPC database is an insurance claims database collected from acute care facilities across Japan 12 . Annually, approximately 7 million patients are registered in the database, with over 1,000 participating facilities. The database captures 50% of the total number of patients acutely hospitalized in Japan, indicating comprehensive coverage. In the DPC data, Form 1 is a brief medical record of the individual patient, and the EF file consists of medical procedures. From the DPC database, we extracted cases that underwent surgery between April 2018 and March 2019, identified by K‐codes in Form 1, which denote surgical procedures. The Japan Diabetes Society approves board‐certified diabetologists. A database of board‐certified diabetologists was obtained from the Japan Diabetes Society. At the time of this study 8 , the Japan Diabetes Society had board‐certified diabetologists. Each specialist reported his or her place of employment to the society, and we obtained the data. The DPC and diabetologist databases were merged using the health organization number as a unifying key. Patient‐level information was obtained from the DPC database. Personal information was considered when extracting the data for analysis. Moreover, regarding the diabetologist database, we were able to access information at the level of each specialist. Information on the number of diabetologists was aggregated at the facility level and linked to the DPC database. After combination, information that could identify medical institutions was excluded.

All study procedures were performed in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments, and the ‘Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects’ published by the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare of Japan. The Ethics Committee of Yokohama City University approved the study protocol (approval number: F230300005). All data were anonymized and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patient selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were patients diagnosed with diabetes mellitus who underwent colorectal cancer surgery under general anesthesia with a hospital stay not exceeding 365 days. We defined diabetes mellitus as a case in which any of the following International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD10) codes beginning with ‘E1’ were included 13 : comorbidity at admission, primary diagnosis at admission, the disease, which led to admission, the disease with the greatest medical resource use, and the disease with the second greatest medical resource use. We defined patients who underwent colorectal cancer surgery as those who had a DPC code (060035, 060040) defining malignancy of the colon and rectum and for whom colorectal cancer surgery was documented from the EF file (Table S1). General anesthesia was defined using Form 1.

Outcomes

Outcome variables included the incidence of perioperative complications, length of hospital stay, and process indicators. Perioperative complications were identified based on the ICD10 codes listed in Form 1 and procedures documented within 30 days postoperatively in the EF file 14 . The ICD10 codes indicative of complications included: surgical site infection (SSI) (T793, T813, T814, and T941), anastomotic leakage (K65 and T813), ileus, bowel obstruction (K560, K562, K565–567, and K913), respiratory failure (J12–18, J690, J691, J958, J959, J96, and J80), heart failure (I50), acute coronary syndrome (I21–25), pulmonary embolism (I26), stroke (I60–66), acute renal failure (N17), urinary tract infection (N10, N30, and N390), and sepsis (A021, A227, A241, A267, A282, A327, A394, A40, A41, A548, B007, B349, B377, and P36). Perioperative complications were defined as procedures performed within 30 days of the following: abscess (surgery or puncture), ischemic heart disease (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft), dialysis (patients with N185 on admission excluded), and ventilator therapy. Information on the length of hospital stay was extracted using Form 1.

We defined three process indicators from the EF file: HbA1c levels, glycoalbumin (GA) measurements during hospitalization, and frequency of blood glucose measurements per week. HbA1c, GA, and blood glucose levels were measured using the following codes: HbA1c (160010010), GA (160151050), and blood glucose (160000750, 160000850, 160149650, and 160019410).

Measurements

A database of board‐certified diabetologists was obtained from the Japan Diabetes Society. The number of diabetologists was provided as a categorical variable (0, 1, 2, 3–5, and 6 or more) because obtaining the number of diabetologists as a continuous variable made it easier to identify facilities.

We obtained data on age, sex, BMI, smoking history, diagnosis as defined by ICD10 codes, DPC codes, cancer stage from DPC Form 1, and the names of the surgeries and procedures performed from the DPC EF file. BMIs below 14 or above 40 were deemed significant outliers, classified as missing values, and subjected to missing‐value imputation. We calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) using Quan weighting 15 , 16 Cancer stages were categorized from 0 to 4, according to the UICC disease classification system. Laparoscopic surgery was defined from the EF file. The average number of inpatient admissions and colorectal cancer surgeries was determined from the information aggregated by the facility. The average number of inpatient admissions was categorized as 0–300, 300–600, or more than 600. The number of colorectal cancer surgeries was divided into less than 50, more than 50 and less than 100, and more than 100 surgeries per a 2‐year period.

Statistical analysis

Continuous values are presented as quartile ranges and categorical variables as numbers and percentages (%). Multiple regression analysis with robust standard errors was used to examine differences in the length of hospital stay and risk of perioperative complications, number of blood glucose measurements, HbA1c measurements, and GA measurements. A modified Poisson regression analysis with robust standard errors was used to determine the perioperative complication risk, HbA1c measurement, and GA measurement ratios 17 . We selected potential confounding factors based on their clinical importance from among the factors available in the dataset. For the regression analyses, we adjusted for the following potential confounding factors: BMI, age, sex, CCI, cancer stage, fiscal year, type of surgery (laparoscopic), average annual hospitalization, and number of colorectal cancer surgeries per year per facility. We considered that the effect of diabetologists was not uniform; therefore, we conducted a stratified analysis to assess the variation in this effect. Stratified analyses were applied to the following variables, assuming variability in effects: exclusion of university hospitals and differentiation by sex, age groups (<65 vs ≥65 years), and cancer stages (stages 0–3 vs stage 4). We imputed missing data by using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) 18 . The clusters were assigned dummy codes to identify facilities and years. The following factors were used as predictors of MICE: facility code, fiscal year, presence or absence of complications, sex, cancer stage, average number of patients at the facility where the operation was performed, number of operations at the facility where the operation was performed, number of diabetologists, length of hospital stay, age, BMI, CCI, laparoscopic surgery, study entry date (day of an operation), HbA1c measurement, GA measurement, and number of blood glucose measurements. The missing percentages for each variable are shown in Table S2. Each result was combined according to the Rubin rule 19 . The number of models (M) utilized was set to 100. Standard errors were robustly estimated through the clustering of facility IDs, with confidence intervals (CIs) set at 95% (2.5%–97.5%). Data were analyzed using R software version 4.2.3 20 and the CCI was calculated using the Comorbidity Package 21 .

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted three sensitivity analyses. First, we used negative control populations to assess unmeasured confounding factors 22 . Facilities with diabetologists may be superior not only because they have diabetologists available, but also due to the number of surgeons and intensivists, staff proficiency, and use of intensive care units. Moreover, we expected the impact of diabetologists on people without diabetes to be scant; hence, we believed that the comparability among facilities could be investigated by examining this issue among people without diabetes. Therefore, we examined the association between the presence of diabetologists and the risk of perioperative complications and length of hospital stay in individuals without diabetes to ensure comparability between facilities with and without diabetologists. Second, we compared the number of diabetologists in Categories 0, 1, 2, 3–5, and 6 to assess the dependent relationship, predicting that outcomes for people with diabetes would improve as the number of diabetologists increased. Third, we performed a complete data analysis excluding cases with missing values.

RESULTS

We analyzed 887 healthcare facilities. Among the facilities evaluated, 299 (34%) lacked a board‐certified diabetologist, 199 (22%) employed one, 148 (17%) employed two, and 241 (27%) employed three or more.

Participants were selected according to the following inclusion criteria: diabetes, general anesthesia, colorectal cancer‐related DPC codes, colorectal cancer‐related surgery, and length of hospital stay (Figure 1). Consequently, 24,714 individuals with diabetes were included in this study.

Perioperative complications occurred in 3,165 cases (13%). After adjusting for potential confounding factors, the risk of perioperative complications was lower in facilities with at least one diabetologist than in facilities without a diabetologist (risk ratio: 0.86 [95% CI: 0.77–0.96]; risk difference: −2.5% [95% CI: −4.4 to −0.60]) (Figure 2). The stratified analysis revealed a consistently lower risk ratio and risk difference for perioperative complications across most subgroups, except in the 20–64 years age group and Stage 4 cancer.

The median length of hospital stay was 17 days (95% CI: 13–27). After adjustment for potential confounding factors, the length of hospital stay tended to be shorter (−1.19 days [95% CI: −2.45 to 0.07]) in facilities with at least one diabetologist compared with those without a diabetologist (Figure 2). Analyses stratified by age, sex, cancer stage, and non‐university hospitals also showed a trend toward a shorter length of stay.

After adjustment for potential confounding factors, HbA1c measurements during hospitalization were 0.88 (0.80–0.96) times lower, GA measurements were 3.01 (2.01–4.51) times higher, and the number of blood glucose measurements per 7 days were 1.29 (0.16–2.43) more in facilities with at least one diabetologist than in facilities without a diabetologist (Figure 3). Stratified analyses by age, sex, cancer stage, and non‐university hospital also showed a trend toward HbA1c measurements, GA measurements, and the number of blood glucose measurements per 7 days.

For the sensitivity analysis, we conducted a similar analysis for people without diabetes. In this analysis, 96,231 individuals without diabetes who underwent colorectal cancer surgery were included (Figure 1). Among this population, the risk ratio (0.97 [95% CI: 0.88–1.07]) and risk difference (−0.40% [95% CI: −1.8 to 1.0]) for perioperative complications were similar between patients with and without a diabetologist (Table S3). However, the length of hospital stay was not precise (−0.74 days [95% CI: −1.68 to 0.20]) (Table S4).

Analysis of the number of diabetologists in the 0, 1, 2, 3–5, and 6 categories showed that patients in the group with more diabetologists tended to have a lower risk of perioperative complications (Table S5) and shorter hospital stays (Table S6). Moreover, HbA1c levels were measured less frequently (Table S7), whereas GA and blood glucose were measured more regularly (Tables S8 and S9).

The results from the complete case analyses were similar to those obtained after multiple imputations (Figures S2–S6).

DISCUSSION

In this large–scale nationwide study conducted in Japan, we found that the presence of a diabetologist was associated with a reduced risk of perioperative complications in patients with diabetes and colorectal cancer. In addition, facilities with diabetologists tended to have shorter lengths of hospital stay and a higher frequency of GA measurements, a marker that reflects short‐term glycemic control. This suggests that patients who undergo surgery at a facility with a diabetologist have their glucose metabolism evaluated more appropriately and have fewer perioperative complications than those who undergo surgery at a facility without a diabetologist.

Consistent with previous studies examining interventions by teams including diabetologists 5 , 6 , 7 , 23 , our study suggests that the presence of diabetologists may reduce the risk of perioperative complications and shorten the length of hospital stay. Koproski et al. reported that an intervention by a team that included a diabetologist improved glycemic control, shortened the length of stay, and reduced readmission rates 6 . In facilities with a diabetologist, it is hoped that intervention by a team including a diabetologist will be possible and blood glucose control will improve. However, in contrast to previous studies that examined the effect on patients, this study examined the effect of the presence of diabetologists in facilities. We used comprehensive data collected from acute care facilities nationwide in Japan, considered the results to be broadly applicable to acute care facilities in Japan, and demonstrated the usefulness of diabetologists in acute care facilities.

There are several possible reasons why the presence of a diabetologist may decrease the risk of perioperative complications. First, it has been suggested that glycemic control is based on an appropriate assessment of glucose metabolism. The frequency of GA and blood glucose monitoring during hospitalization was higher when a diabetologist was available than when a diabetologist was absent. GA levels are better than HbA1c levels for assessing short‐term blood glucose variability; hence, we believe that a more appropriate blood glucose assessment indicator is selected when a diabetologist is available 24 , 25 . Second, we considered the possibility of reducing the perioperative blood glucose variability. A previous report evaluating the intervention of glucose management teams suggested that the intervention reduced blood glucose variability 7 . Although specific interventions were not mentioned 7 , we speculate that more specialized treatments, such as multiple daily injections and continuous intravenous insulin infusion, were implemented with the intervention of a diabetes specialist. Third, we hypothesized that diabetologists provided in‐hospital education, which may have improved the knowledge of diabetes among healthcare workers. We assumed that education on diabetes and the presence of diabetologists would contribute to improving the quality of care and outcomes of patients with diabetes throughout facilities where diabetologists were available.

This study has several strengths. First, we analyzed nationwide, large claims data from acute care facilities across Japan, along with data on the number of diabetologists from the Japan Diabetes Society. The DPC database covers 50% of the total number of acute care inpatients in Japan; hence, our results may be widely applicable to acute care facilities. Second, this study investigated the impact of the presence of a diabetologist on the facility, not on patients. This study aimed to provide suggestions on the need for the availability of diabetologists in acute care facilities.

Nevertheless, this study also had a few limitations. First, there may be a difference in the severity of diabetes between facilities with and without a diabetologist because the glycemic control status and diabetic complications of people with diabetes were not assessed. Patients with poor glycemic control and/or more severe complications may be more likely to choose care facilities with diabetologists than those without, resulting in unmeasured confounding factors. To address this unmeasured confounding factor, we added the CCI and cancer stage to the adjustment factors, which may have affected the perioperative complications. Second, we could not assess facility functions other than the number of diabetologists. The number of surgeons 26 , intensivists, and infectious disease specialists, along with the presence of intensive care units and the overall capacity of the hospital, could indicate the functions of the facility. However, these factors were not measured, and uncontrolled confounding may occur due to their influence. The impact of the presence or absence of a diabetologist per facility on patient outcomes may be explained by the lack of control over facility factors. However, in an analysis of patients without diabetes, in whom the impact of diabetologists was considered to be small and influenced by facility function, there was no difference in the risk of perioperative complications and length of hospital stay between facilities with and without a diabetologist. While we could not evaluate other facility characteristics beyond the availability of diabetologists, and we recognize that our findings may be influenced by unmeasured factors, the results of our sensitivity analyses indicate that having a diabetes specialist involved in this study seems to be linked to a lower risk of perioperative complications and shorter hospital stays. Third, it remains unclear whether patients actually received care directly from diabetologists, as this study was not able to assess their individual involvement in patient management. However, in facilities where diabetologists were present, we observed improvements in process indicators, such as increased measurement of glycated albumin—an appropriate short‐term glycemic control marker—and a higher frequency of blood glucose measurements. These findings suggested that the presence of diabetologists may have a positive impact on glycemic management. Moreover, diabetologists contribute not only through direct clinical care but also by educating healthcare staff and establishing institutional systems for diabetes management. Therefore, in this study, we defined the presence of diabetologists as the exposure variable. Fourth, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the characteristics of the DPC database. The voluntary participation of hospitals in the DPC database may lead to a lack of nationwide representativeness. Furthermore, regional disparities in population demographics and healthcare infrastructure may restrict the applicability of our results to all healthcare settings. Nevertheless, the DPC data used in this study cover approximately 50% of all acute inpatient admissions in Japan, and we believe our findings are broadly applicable to a wide range of healthcare institutions.

In the future, it may be important to increase the number of facilities with diabetologists to improve the quality of perioperative medical care. Our study suggests that facilities with a diabetologist have adequate glycemic control and fewer perioperative complications than those without a diabetologist. As this study was conducted using DPC data obtained from acute care facilities throughout Japan, our results may be applicable to a wide range of facilities. As most of the data included facilities that did not have a diabetologist on staff, future discussions throughout Japan are expected.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the presence of a diabetologist may reduce the risk of perioperative complications and shorten the length of hospital stay in patients with diabetes and colorectal cancer. Therefore, to improve the prognosis of patients with diabetes undergoing surgery, it may be important to include a diabetologist at facilities where colorectal cancer surgery is performed. Future studies factoring in more confounding factors may be required to further validate our findings. Moreover, investigating the effects of the presence of diabetologists on the perioperative complications for other surgeries may be necessary before our results can be applied to the surgical protocols for other types of surgery.
