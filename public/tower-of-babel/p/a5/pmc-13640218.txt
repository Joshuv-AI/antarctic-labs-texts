Impact of vitamin D supplementation on the outcomes of kidney transplant recipients: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the most effective treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. The widespread adoption of tacrolimus-based triple maintenance immunosuppressive therapy has significantly improved the short-term survival rates of graft kidneys in recent years [2, 3]. However, the enhancement of long-term survival remains less pronounced. Immunologically mediated rejection, disturbances in calcium and phosphorus metabolism, and post-transplant bone disease (PTBD) continue to be significant factors contributing to reduced quality of life and survival among kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) in the long term [4].

Post-transplant secondary hyperparathyroidism is one of the most significant complications among KTRs. Additionally, PTBD is prevalent and characterized by a unique metabolic and skeletal condition that leads to poor bone quality, an increased risk of fractures, accelerated vascular aging, and a decline in allograft function [4, 5]. The pathogenesis of PTBD is multifactorial, with one of the primary contributors being a disrupted parathyroid hormone-vitamin D axis, along with an altered calcium-phosphorus balance and the effects of immunosuppressive drugs such as prednisone and calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) [6].

Post-transplant vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent in kidney transplant recipients (KTRs), with epidemiological data indicating that insufficiency ranges from 46.9% to 75% and deficiency from 14% to 85% [7]. This deficiency often persists from the dialysis period, where supplementation traditionally focused on activated vitamin D analogs rather than nutritional forms. These two classes differ fundamentally in their mechanisms, activated analogs bypass hepatic 25-hydroxylation and renal 1α-hydroxylation to exert direct hormonal effects, primarily suppressing parathyroid hormone through action on the parathyroid gland and intestine. In contrast, nutritional vitamin D functions as a pro-hormone requiring sequential hydroxylation to become biologically active, thereby addressing the underlying 25(OH)D substrate depletion that characterizes post-transplant vitamin D insufficiency. Given these distinct pharmacokinetics, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic objectives, pooling these interventions in meta-analysis would introduce significant clinical heterogeneity and obscure the specific effects of nutritional vitamin D repletion. Therefore, this review focuses exclusively on nutritional vitamin D supplementation.

The clinical consequences of post-transplant vitamin D deficiency extend beyond mineral bone disorder. Recent cohort studies have linked vitamin D deficiency to transplanted renal interstitial fibrosis [7], decreased glomerular filtration rate, acute rejection [8], and various systemic diseases such as type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease [9]. Vitamin D-deficient KTRs also face an increased risk of all-cause mortality [10].

Accordingly, vitamin D supplementation has been proposed to offer multiple potential benefits, including improvement of hyperparathyroidism, promotion of bone health, modulation of immune responses, and possible positive effects on graft function [11–13]. However, individual studies have been limited by small sample sizes and have not provided definitive conclusions. To address this evidence gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to consolidate available data and evaluate the specific impact of nutritional vitamin D supplementation on post-transplant outcomes in KTRs.

Method

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline. Our study is registered in the PROSPERO database, under the registration number ID: CRD420251009160. The full protocol is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Search method

We conducted a comprehensive electronic database search spanning PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science, covering literature published until January 1, 2025, to locate studies assessing vitamin D supplementation impacts on post-transplant clinical parameters in renal allograft recipients. The search algorithm was architected using PICOS components (Table 1), emphasizing two dimensions: study subjects (kidney transplant recipients) and experimental treatment (vitamin D supplementation). Medical subject headings paired with associated text words—namely “vitamin D”, “25-dihydroxyvitamin D”, “kidney transplantation”, “renal transplantation”, “kidney transplant”, “renal transplant”, “kidney graft”, and “renal graft”—constituted the search string. The investigation was restricted to English-language articles. Bibliographic scanning of identified papers was additionally performed to detect any studies escaping the initial electronic retrieval. We have now prepared Supplementary Table 7, which provides the precise search strings for each database.

Screening process

Initially, articles were screened based on their titles, and duplicate records were removed by Endnote software, version 20.0. Next, the eligibility of the records was evaluated based on their titles and abstracts, leading to the exclusion of articles deemed irrelevant. Finally, full-text articles were screened for eligibility based on the availability of relevant data regarding vitamin D supplementation status and graft kidney outcomes. The remaining articles were selected for qualitative analysis. Each article was subsequently examined independently for data extraction. The extracted data included study design, the number of recipients in each study, demographics (sex and age), vitamin D status, and outcomes. For trials with multiple publications reporting distinct predefined endpoints, we included each publication only for the specific outcomes reported therein to avoid duplicate patient counting. When multiple publications from the same trial reported data for the same outcome, we retained the report with the most complete data.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective cohort studies focused on kidney transplantation; (2) studies investigating the effects of nutritional vitamin D supplementation after kidney transplantation, regardless of whether supplementation was administered universally or targeted specifically to patients with measured vitamin D deficiency. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical studies that did not involve vitamin D supplementation; (2) Studies using activated vitamin D analogs (e.g., calcitriol, alfacalcidol, paricalcitol) instead of nutritional vitamin D; (3) abstracts, reviews, meeting and letters; and (4) studies lacking quantitative data for any prespecified outcome, meaning no numerical mean with standard deviation, median with interquartile range, or event counts were reported in the text, tables, or figures. This study was prepared according to the PRISMA checklist.

Inclusion of outcomes

Vitamin D status was mostly assessed by 25(OH)D and partly by 1,25(OH)2D. To ensure consistency across included studies, we adopted the Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline definitions as the reference standard for vitamin D status: deficiency was defined as [25(OH)D] < 20 ng/mL, insufficiency as 20–30 ng/mL, and sufficiency as > 30 ng/mL. For studies reporting 25(OH)D in nmol/L, values were converted to ng/mL using the standard conversion factor. A summary table is provided in the Supplementary Table 5. Graft function was assessed using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. Parathyroid function was evaluated by measuring intact parathyroid hormone levels. For PTH, values in pmol/L or ng/L were converted to pg/mL, serum calcium, and serum phosphate, as these parameters collectively reflect parathyroid function, while the T-score of the femoral neck was utilized to comprehensively assess bone density. The femoral neck was chosen as the main skeletal site because it contains a high amount of both cortical and trabecular bone, representing the two bone types most impacted by rapid bone loss after transplantation. Additionally, it is the anatomical area most commonly reported in transplant studies, enabling data to be combined statistically, which makes femoral neck bone mineral density (BMD) a clinically meaningful outcome. Proteinuria levels were determined using specific values of the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR, mg/mmol). We extracted the following additional study-level characteristics as potential confounders: (a) time from kidney transplantation to study enrollment; (b) donor type; (c) maintenance immunosuppressive regimen. These variables were summarized qualitatively and their distributions across studies are presented in Supplementary Table 6.

Assessment of risk of bias

We utilized the Version 2 of the Cochrane tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB 2) to assess the risk of bias in the included RCTs across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results. An “overall risk of bias” for a study was judged as “low risk” if all domains were low risk of bias, and as “high risk” if any domain showed high risk of bias or if several domains raised concerns significantly reducing confidence in the results. For the non-randomised intervention studies we used the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions) tool, which evaluates bias across seven domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results. An overall risk-of-bias judgement for each study was assigned as “low”, “moderate”, “serious”, “critical”, or “no information” according to the most severe rating among the domains. Two investigators ( B.Q.Y. and J.Z.W) independently judged the risk of bias of the selected studies following the established guidelines.

Publication bias

Funnel plots were constructed to visually assess publication bias for the primary outcomes of 25(OH)D and PTH. For 25(OH)D, the funnel plot displayed a relatively symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate, with most studies falling within the pseudo 95% confidence limits, suggesting a low risk of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. 11). For PTH, the funnel plot showed noticeable asymmetry, with one study (K. Martin Wissing 2005) positioned far outside the confidence limits on the left side, and the remaining studies clustered toward the right, indicating potential publication bias or the influence of a small-study effect (Supplementary Fig. 12). Given the limited number of included studies, formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry were not performed, as these tests have low statistical power with fewer than 10 studies.

Statistical analyses

Data synthesis was performed according to the PRISMA guideline. Mean difference (MD) was used as the primary effect measure for continuous outcomes because all variables were converted to the same unit of measurement across studies. As recommended by the Cochrane Handbook, MD is preferred when outcomes are measured on a consistent scale, as it provides a direct and clinically interpretable estimate of the treatment effect in the original measurement units. Forest plots were constructed for each meta-analysis to examine study-level data. We used I2 statistics to describe the percentage of the variation across studies. Significant heterogeneity was indicated by P < 0.05. A random-effects model was employed as the primary meta-analytic approach for all outcomes. This decision was based on the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity across included studies, including variations in study design, vitamin D dosing regimens, and duration of supplementation, rather than solely on observed statistical heterogeneity metrics. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics; Sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing 1 study at a time and repeating the meta-analysis to evaluate the stability of results. All statistical analyses were performed with StataMP-17 software and R studio software .

Results

Included study

A total of 3,416 records were identified from the three databases. After removing 1,426 duplicate records using EndNote software, 1,990 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 163 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Subsequently, 155 articles were excluded, including 27 conference abstracts, 125 reviews or meta-analyses, 2 articles lacking extractable quantitative data, and 1 same study. Ultimately, 8 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. Assessment of risk of bias

Supplementary Fig. 1 presents the RoB-2 risk of bias traffic light charts for the three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) included in the analysis. At the domain level, all studies were assessed as “low risk” for D1 (randomization process), D3 (missing outcome data), D4 (measurement of the outcome), and D5 (selection of the reported result). One study (Yoshitsugu Obi et al., NCT01817699), which employed an open-label design, was judged to have a “high risk” for D2 (deviations from intended interventions) due to the lack of blinding of participants and personnel. However, after comparing with the protocol-specified analyses, we concluded that these deviations had minimal effect on the objectively measured outcomes. The overall risk of bias was “low” for two trials and “some concerns” for the open-label trial by Obi et al., indicating acceptable internal validity and supporting the trustworthiness of the combined findings. The specific assessment details for the three RCTs are provided in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 3. Supplementary Table 4 presents the ROBINS-I evaluations for the five observational studies included. In summary, two studies (Marie Courbebaisse 2023 and Yuri Battaglia 2023) were assessed as having a low risk of bias, while the other three were deemed to have a moderate risk; none were identified as high risk. Overall, the evidence was judged to have an acceptable risk of bias, supporting the trustworthiness of the subsequent analyses.

Different vitamin D supplementation strategies and their effects on vitamin D levels in KTRs

The included studies were compared for specific values of 25(OH)D levels at their respective study endpoints across different groups. Doses of vitamin D ranged from 800 to 7000 IU daily. The meta-analysis revealed a combined standardized mean difference (MD) of 11.58 ng/ml (95% CI: 2.27–20.89). There was heterogeneity (I² = 98.4%, 95% CI: 97.8%-98.9%; Q-test P < 0.0001; τ² =99.24). Compared to the group without vitamin D supplementation, all studies in the treatment group demonstrated significantly higher 25(OH)D levels at the end of the follow-up period, and these differences were statistically significant (P = 0.023, Fig. 2).

Effect of post-transplant vitamin D supplementation on renal function levels

A total of five studies compared changes in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of graft kidneys following vitamin D supplementation at the follow-up endpoint. The meta-analysis results revealed a combined MD of 0.94 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI: -7.89 to 9.77). There was substantial heterogeneity (I² = 91.4%, 95% CI: 82.9%-95.7%; Q-test P < 0.0001; τ² = 45.97). The eGFR was not significantly higher in the treated group compared to the group without vitamin D supplementation, suggesting that there may not be a correlation between vitamin D treatment and improvement in eGFR in graft kidneys (P = 0.782, Fig. 3).

Effect of vitamin D supplementation on parathyroid hormone levels

Six studies compared changes in parathyroid hormone levels following vitamin D supplementation at the follow-up endpoint. The combined MD was − 29.8 pmol/L (95% CI: -75.87 to 16.28). There was heterogeneity (I² = 98.6%, 95% CI: 98%-99%; Q-test P < 0.0001; τ² =91.24). Compared to the group without vitamin D supplementation, intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH) levels were not significantly lower in the treated group at the follow-up endpoint (P = 0.157, Fig. 4).

Effect of vitamin D supplementation on bone mineral density

We utilized the femoral neck T-score to assess bone mineral density (BMD) in each group. Out of a total of eight studies, two specifically evaluated BMD. We ultimately included the two studies in our meta-analysis, which revealed a combined MD of 0.48 (95% CI: 0.27 to 0.68). There was no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, 95% CI: 0–90%; Q-test P = 0.7718; τ² = 0). This finding suggested that the vitamin D-treated group exhibited a significant improvement in the femoral neck T-score at the end of the follow-up period compared to the untreated group, with a statistically significant difference when compared to the group without vitamin D supplementation (P = 0.022, Fig. 5).

Effect of vitamin D supplementation on urinary protein

A total of two studies evaluated the effect of vitamin D supplementation on urinary protein. Using the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio at the follow-up endpoint as the assessment method, the meta-analysis revealed a combined MD of 12.56 mg/mmol (95% CI: -57.26 to 82.37). A total of six studies evaluated changes in serum calcium and phosphorus levels in KTRs following vitamin D supplementation. Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each outcome. For 25(OH)D, the pooled mean difference was 11.58 ng/mL (95% CI: 2.27 to 20.89, P = 0.023), and individual estimates were consistently positive across all iterations (ranging from 9.21 to 14.05 ng/mL), with all 95% CIs overlapping the pooled estimate, indicating that the significant beneficial effect of vitamin D supplementation on 25(OH)D levels was robust and not driven by any single study. For eGFR, the pooled mean difference was 0.94 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI: -7.89 to 9.77), and individual estimates ranged from − 3.07 to 2.21 mL/min/1.73m2; notably, exclusion of the study by Hu et al. (2022) yielded a statistically significant negative result (-3.07, 95% CI: -4.45 to -1.69, P = 0.006), indicating that this study was a key determinant of the non-significant pooled effect. For PTH, the pooled mean difference was − 29.80 pg/mL (95% CI: -75.87 to 16.28), with individual estimates remaining negative across all iterations (ranging from − 9.76 to -35.66 pg/mL); however, omission of the study by Hu et al. (2022) substantially attenuated the effect size and shifted the 95% CI toward the null (-9.76 pg/mL, 95% CI: -22.96 to 3.44), suggesting that this study exerted a considerable influence on the overall pooled estimate. For serum calcium, all individual estimates were close to zero (ranging from − 0.12 to 0.00 mg/dL), and the pooled mean difference of -0.07 mg/dL (95% CI: -0.29 to 0.14) remained stable regardless of which study was omitted, demonstrating high robustness. Similarly, for serum phosphorus, individual estimates ranged from 0.07 to 0.29 mg/dL, with the pooled estimate of 0.24 mg/dL (95% CI: -0.18 to 0.66) showing minimal fluctuation across sensitivity analyses, confirming the stability of this null finding. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses demonstrate that the findings for 25(OH)D, calcium, and phosphorus were highly robust, whereas the results for eGFR and PTH were more sensitive to the inclusion of individual studies, particularly the study by Hu et al. (2022). (Supplementary Figs. 2–6).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. For PTH, stratification by vitamin D dosage (≤ 2000 vs. >2000 IU/day) revealed no significant subgroup interaction under the random-effects model (χ²1 = 1.05, P = 0.31), although heterogeneity was markedly higher in the lower-dose subgroup (I2 = 99%) than in the higher-dose subgroup (I2 = 72.8%). When stratified by follow-up duration (≤ 12 vs. >12 months), a significant subgroup difference was observed under the common-effect model (χ²1 = 61.02, P < 0.001) but not under the random-effects model (χ²1 = 2.77, P = 0.100), with the ≤ 12-month subgroup showing extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 99%) compared with no heterogeneity in the > 12-month subgroup (I2 = 0%). For eGFR, stratification by follow-up duration showed a significant subgroup difference under the common-effect model (χ²1 = 27.85, P < 0.001) but not under the random-effects model (χ²1 = 1.45, P = 0.230); the ≤ 12-month subgroup exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 83.9%), whereas the > 12-month subgroup was not estimable with only one study. Stratification by vitamin D dosage similarly showed a significant difference under the common-effect model (χ²1 = 31.47, P < 0.001) but not under the random-effects model (χ²1 = 1.09, P = 0.300), with high heterogeneity in the ≤ 2000 IU/day subgroup (I2 = 86.5%) and no heterogeneity in the > 2000 IU/day subgroup (I2 = 0%). In summary, subgroup analyses did not identify statistically significant effect modifiers under the random-effects model for either PTH or eGFR (Supplementary Figs. 7–10).

Discussion

We conducted a meta analysis of the existing literature on the effects of therapeutic-dose vitamin D supplementation on various outcomes in KTRs. Although fracture, graft survival and mortality data are currently unavailable, correcting vitamin D deficiency is biologically plausible to attenuate post-transplant bone disease, which is established risk factor for future fractures and cardiovascular events in KTRs.

Our analysis revealed that post-transplant vitamin D supplementation markedly increased serum 25(OH)D levels without affecting blood calcium and phosphorus levels, and it did not lead to an increase in adverse effects such as hypercalcemia. However, our meta-analysis did not find evidence that vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced parathyroid hormone levels or effectively ameliorated secondary hyperparathyroidism after transplantation when compared to placebo or untreated controls. It is worth noting that although the reduction in iPTH levels following vitamin D supplementation did not reach statistical significance in our analysis, a discernible downward trend in PTH was nonetheless observed in kidney transplant recipients. Such a clinically meaningful reduction in PTH, even in the absence of statistical significance, should not be dismissed as entirely without value. In the clinical management of secondary hyperparathyroidism, modest PTH reductions may still contribute to long-term bone and mineral metabolism stability, particularly when achieved safely without hypercalcemia or other adverse effects. The lack of statistical significance may be attributable to insufficient statistical power, heterogeneity in vitamin D dosing regimens and formulations across included studies, or relatively short follow-up durations. Future well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and longer intervention periods are warranted to further clarify the potential clinical benefit of post-transplant vitamin D supplementation on PTH reduction. Interestingly, regular post-transplant vitamin D supplementation enhanced femoral neck bone mineral density T-score in KTRs. This finding align with those of previous observational and interventional studies [8, 14, 22, 23]. But he observed change in femoral neck T-score should be interpreted with substantial caution. This analysis included only two studies, rendering the pooled estimate highly fragile. Furthermore, neither included study reported fracture incidence or other hard skeletal endpoints. While the T-score finding suggests a potential effect on bone mineral density at the femoral neck, this does not establish clinical benefit in terms of fracture prevention. KTRs have unique bone disease pathophysiology that may limit the generalizability of T-score improvements to actual skeletal outcomes. This finding should be considered exploratory, and future studies with fracture endpoints are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of vitamin D supplementation on bone health in this population. Unfortunately, the impact of vitamin D supplementation on post-transplant graft function in KTRs, when compared to placebo or control, remained inconclusive, as our meta-analysis did not find evidence that vitamin D supplementation significantly improved graft function after kidney transplantation. Furthermore, no significant effect of vitamin D supplementation on proteinuria was observed in our analysis.

Chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) is prevalent after kidney transplantation and is characterized by a distinct metabolic and skeletal profile, referred to as post-transplant bone disease (PTBD). PTBD results in poor bone quality, a high risk of fractures, accelerated vascular aging, and deterioration of allograft function. The etiology of PTBD is multifactorial, encompassing preexisting CKD-MBD, metabolic alterations in calcium-phosphorus homeostasis, and immunosuppressive therapy [15, 24]. Among these factors, hyperparathyroidism is one of the most significant contributors of metabolic bone alterations, often reflecting a maladaptive response to persistently elevated PTH levels. Typically, more than 50% of KTRs experience elevated PTH levels one year after transplantation [25]. High levels of PTH promote bone resorption by activating osteoclasts, which break down bone tissue to release minerals such as calcium into the bloodstream. Additionally, PTH inhibits bone formation by reducing both the function and number of osteoblasts. This imbalance between bone resorption and formation results in a decrease in bone density and quality, ultimately leading to PTBD. A substantial body of evidence from experimental and clinical studies indicated a correlation between reduced 25(OH)D levels and disrupted vitamin D metabolism in KTRs. Only 12% of these recipients were found to have 25(OH)D levels exceeding 30 ng/mL, while deficiencies and insufficiencies in vitamin D ranged from 30% to 81%, respectively [10, 26]. In the context of immunosuppressive therapy, the long-term use of corticosteroids is a significant contributor to osteoporosis through various pathways. The mechanism by which corticosteroids affect vitamin D metabolism is not fully understood. Corticosteroids induce enzymes involved in vitamin D catabolism and elevate PTH levels [27]. In addition to corticosteroids, other immunosuppressive agents have been implicated in the dysregulation of vitamin D metabolism observed in KTRs. There is considerable evidence that calcineurin inhibitors (CNI) is inversely associated with decreased levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) [28]. One possible explanation is that calcineurin inhibitors inhibit hepatic CYP3A4 25-hydroxylase activity, leading to lower circulating levels of 25(OH)D; however, the downregulation of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) induced by calcineurin inhibitors may also contribute to this effect [29]. As a result, KTRs experience a combination of changes in bone remodeling, including impaired bone mineralization and osteoclast function. This is evidenced by decreased osteoclastogenesis, increased osteoclast apoptosis, and enhanced bone resorption, all of which contribute to bone loss [30]. Furthermore, there is a lack of reliable and consistent data regarding the role of vitamin D therapy in post-transplant bone disease (PTBD) and the prognosis of kidney transplantation.

A large amount of research has demonstrated that vitamin D deficiency is linked to negative outcomes such as infections, organ rejection, and even mortality following kidney transplantation [8, 31]. This deficiency may arise from various factors, including suboptimal supplementation strategies and alterations in specific pathways that influence vitamin D metabolism in vivo after kidney transplantation. The PTH-FGF23-vitamin D axis is crucial for regulating calcium-phosphorus metabolism and renal function. FGF23, primarily secreted by osteoblasts and osteoclasts, plays a key role in phosphorus metabolism [32]. Recent studies have revealed that FGF23 not only inhibits the activity of 1α-hydroxylase and the synthesis of 1,25-(OH)2D in the kidney via its receptors FGFR1-4 and its co-receptor Klotho, but also impacts kidney and bone metabolism through additional signaling pathways [33, 34]. For instance, FGF23 can directly inhibit the differentiation and maturation of osteoblasts through the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby affecting bone mineralization [35]. Consequently, kidney transplant recipients may require increased vitamin D supplementation post-transplant to achieve adequate levels of 25(OH)D.

Although the significance of vitamin D, particularly in bone and mineral metabolism following kidney transplantation, is now widely acknowledged, post-transplant vitamin D supplementation protocols for KTRs remain contentious due to insufficient evidence. Vitamin D deficiency, which is commonly associated with chronic renal failure, significantly contributes to disturbances in mineral and bone metabolism, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular complications and bone disease [22, 36]. While there are relevant systematic reviews that summarize the impact of post-transplant vitamin D deficiency on outcomes in KTRs [10], there has yet to be a systematic review that consolidates the outcomes of post-transplant vitamin D supplementation in KTRs.

To the best of our knowledge, a previous Cochrane review by Palmer et al. evaluated vitamin D and analogs for preventing bone loss and other outcomes in kidney transplant recipients. That review concluded that vitamin D therapy reduced bone loss at 12 months but found insufficient evidence regarding effects on fractures, graft function, or mortality. Our meta-analysis builds upon this work by specifically isolating the effects of nutritional vitamin D, rather than pooling nutritional and activated forms. This distinction is clinically meaningful because these agents have distinct metabolic pathways and therapeutic targets. Furthermore, our review includes more recent studies published since the Cochrane review’s last search, providing updated evidence on PTH control, BMD, and emerging outcomes such as proteinuria. However, we acknowledge that our findings should be interpreted cautiously given several important limitations. The first one is the small number of included studies, which may result in low certainty of the evidence. The integrated studies concluded only 3 randomized controlled trials, and the rest were prospective cohort studies which were susceptible to be influenced by various factors such as dosage of immunosuppressants, the degree of primary bone dieases and patient compliance. More valid evidence is required otherwise the effect of post-transplant vitamin D supplementation might be overrated. We acknowledge that the initial pooled analysis exhibited substantial heterogeneity. Additional sources of variability may include different timing of initiation, baseline 25(OH)D concentrations, concurrent use of calcium or cinacalcet, and genetic polymorphisms of vitamin D metabolism. However, data were too sparse to perform meta-regression on these factors. Future individual-patient-data meta-analyses are warranted to quantify the contribution of each covariate. Third, the inclusion of randomized controlled trials and cohort studies introduced significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity. The resulting mixture of randomized and non-randomized evidence is a major source of potential bias. Non-randomized studies are susceptible to confounding by indication, as patients receiving vitamin D supplementation may differ systematically from those who do not in terms of baseline health status, mineral bone disease severity, physician practice patterns, or healthcare access. While we employed a random-effects model to account for anticipated between-study variance, this statistical approach cannot fully eliminate inherent bias from observational designs. Readers should therefore interpret our findings as associative rather than causal, particularly for outcomes where RCT evidence remains scarce. And the included studies used heterogeneous definitions of vitamin D deficiency and sufficiency, which may have introduced classification bias. While we harmonized these definitions to the greatest extent possible, some studies used thresholds that diverged substantially from the Endocrine Society criteria. This variability could have affected the distribution of baseline vitamin D status across studies and, consequently, the observed treatment effects. At the same time, transplant vintage and immunosuppressive regimens varied considerably among included studies, and donor type was not uniformly reported. These factors may introduce additional heterogeneity, as treatment response may differ between early and late post-transplant periods or by donor type. And Most included studies did not control for bisphosphonates, calcimimetics, or active vitamin D. Battaglia et al. performed subgroup analyses in untreated patients, and Wissing et al. standardised calcium supplementation; other RCTs relied on randomisation. Observational studies without adjustment (e.g., Courbebaisse 2009, Hu 2022) may have residual confounding, addressed in sensitivity analyses. The dose-stratified iPTH subgroup analysis was underpowered and may have been confounded by variable baseline 25(OH)D levels and non-standardised calcium supplementation across studies, limiting the ability to isolate a true dose-response effect. To reduce bias to the greatest extent possible, we performed “leave-one-out” sensitivity analyses to evaluate the stability of the combined estimates for all outcome measures. These analyses not only increased our confidence in the main conclusions but also properly emphasized the uncertainty related to the secondary outcomes. In general, the findings suggest that our meta-analysis results concerning biochemical markers are more reliable. At the same time, our meta-analysis did not find any improvement in proteinuria or eGFR with vitamin D supplementation. However, the lack of statistically significant results should not be taken as proof that there is no effect. Due to the small number of studies available, these analyses lacked sufficient power, so a meaningful impact of vitamin D supplementation on kidney function or proteinuria cannot be ruled out. Future adequately powered trials should prespecify stratification or standardisation of concurrent therapies to better isolate native vitamin D effects and clarify these relationships.

More importantly, none of the studies included were designed with enough power to detect significant clinical outcomes like fractures, graft failure, or overall mortality; the follow-up period seldom went beyond 12 months. Therefore, although vitamin D supplementation seems to enhance surrogate biochemical and bone density measures, its effect on outcomes that matter to patients remains uncertain. This issue should be investigated in longer, well-powered randomized clinical trials with larger participant numbers and extended follow-up periods to provide robust evidence regarding the impact of vitamin D supplementation after transplantation.

Conclusion

Post-transplant nutritional vitamin D supplementation in kidney transplant recipients was associated with increased serum 25(OH)D levels and improved femoral neck bone mineral density. Although a downward trend in parathyroid hormone levels was observed following supplementation, this reduction did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. While our results support the rationale for correcting vitamin D deficiency after kidney transplantation, they do not establish definitive clinical efficacy for improving post-transplant bone disease. Whether vitamin D supplementation can meaningfully ameliorate bone-related outcomes in kidney transplant recipients remains uncertain. More extensive and longer-duration randomized controlled trials with prespecified bone-related endpoints are necessary to guide evidence-based practice.
