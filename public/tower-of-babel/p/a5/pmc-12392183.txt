NMR-Based Serum Metabolomics and Correlation Analysis Unraveled Metabolic Alterations Underlying Pathophysiology of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Rimjhim Trivedi, Shubham Kumar, Pramod Kumar, Sanjay Yadav, Sada Shiv, Dinesh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a growing global health concern, with a high prevalence among individuals aged 45–50 years and above. Chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction play pivotal roles in its progression. This clinical metabolomics study aimed to identify distinct metabolic alterations associated with insulin resistance and other clinical features of T2DM. Fasting serum samples from 43 T2DM patients (mean age 51 ± 9 years) and 34 age- and sex-matched controls (mean age 49 ± 11 years) were analyzed using 800 MHz NMR spectroscopy. Serum metabolite concentrations were quantified using CHENOMX software and compared using multivariate and univariate statistical methods, with the diagnostic potential evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. T2DM patients exhibited elevated levels of glucose, mannose, urea, glutamate, and phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio (PTR), alongside reduced levels of alanine, valine, threonine, histidine, glutamine, and key metabolic ratios such as glutamine-to-glucose (QGR), alanine-to-glucose (AGR), glutamine-to-glutamate (QER), and glutamine-to-mannose (QMR). Metabolic alterations suggest heightened inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, which is disrupting glucose–alanine cycling, the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), and glutamine and amino acid metabolism. This study highlights the role of metabolomics in understanding T2DM pathophysiology and offers potential biomarkers for improved diagnosis and disease management.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM, a progressive metabolic disorder) is characterized by a chronic hyperglycemia condition resulting from impaired insulin action, commonly known as insulin resistance. The persistent Insulin resistance and altered glucose homeostasis lead to a cascade of metabolic disorders. The chronic hyperglycemia associated with T2DM leads to various microvascular (neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy) and macrovascular complications (atherosclerosis, peripheral artery disease, stroke, and coronary artery disease). − The global prevalence of diabetes in 2024 was estimated at 589 million individuals aged 20–79 years and is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050. T2DM accounts for over 90% of all diabetes cases worldwide.

The rising prevalence of T2DM, particularly, in aging population have been linked to the sedentary lifestyle, dietary changes, obesity, and deficiency of essential micronutrients (including Vitamin D and Vitamin B12). Accumulating data have shown that various dietary interventions supplementing nutrients/micronutrients (such as minerals, vitamins, etc.) can improve mitochondrial functioning and other metabolic dysregulations associated with T2DM such as carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism. , A growing body of studies has also shown the intriguing possibility that T2DM can be managed, and its complications can be avoided or delayed with early detection, lifestyle management, and proper medication. Studies have shown the role of metabolic alterations in disease progression and associated chronic complications and how aging can be a major factor in exaggerating the complications.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is one of the principal analytical techniques used for metabolomics studies. Particularly, the 1H NMR is a more widespread and commonly used approach due to its high sensitivity, minimal sample preparation, allows for reliable quantitation, and high reproducibility that makes it favorable for data comparison across studies. A plethora of studies carried out in the past have shown the metabolic alterations in T2DM and its associated complications. − The majority of such studies have evaluated the relationship between a wide range of metabolites, insulin resistance, and T2DM. However, relatively few studies have assessed the role of aging in these metabolic disturbances. − Therefore, in this study, we systematically investigated serum metabolic alterations in middle-aged T2DM patients. Further, we examined the association of these metabolic changes with insulin resistance and other key clinical parameters of T2DM, aiming to elucidate their pathological relevance in disease development and progression. Metabolic alterations were also compared across different age cohorts to explore age-related differences.

Results

Clinical Characteristic

The study prospectively included 43 T2DM and 34 Normal control (NC) subjects based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and HOMA-IR. The clinical and demographic characteristics of subjects are summarized in Table . T2DM patients exhibited significantly higher fasting glucose and insulin levels. Further, insulin resistance was assessed by using the HOMA-IR parameter, which was notably elevated in T2DM patients (Table ). The mean age of the T2DM group was 51 years, while the control group had a mean age of 49 years, thus highlighting that the study population belongs to the middle-aged group (Table ). The male-to-female ratio in both study groups was also comparable, with 34 and 28% women, respectively, in T2DM and NC groups (Table ). Compared to the NC (24 ± 3 kg/m2), the BMI of the T2DM group (26 ± 5 kg/m2) was significantly higher (p < 0.05), indicating a prevalence of overweight individuals in T2DM subjects. However, there was no significant difference in the waist circumference of the two groups. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure (142 ± 22/85 ± 12 mmHg) were significantly elevated (p < 0.05; p < 0.01, respectively) in the T2DM group compared to NC (130 ± 15/85 ± 7 mmHg), indicating a prevalence of high normal blood pressure and grade 1 hypertension. Lipid profiles were significantly altered in T2DM patients, with higher levels of LDL (119 ± 67 mg/dL), VLDL (25 ± 18 mg/dL), triglycerides (123 ± 91 mg/dL), and total cholesterol (189 ± 77 mg/dL) compared to the NC group (LDL = 95 ± 45 mg/dL, VLDL = 19 ± 10 mg/dL, triglycerides = 94 ± 50 mg/dL, and total cholesterol = 163 ± 38 mg/dL), while HDL levels remained unchanged. To assess age-related differences, the middle-aged T2DM group was also compared with younger and older cohorts, including young-age T2DM patients (T2DMY) and old-age T2DM patients (T2DMO), along with their respective age- and sex-matched normal controls (NCY and NCO). Often, metabolic ratios are devoid of analytical variations associated with confounding factors and may serve as better indicative markers of a pathophysiological condition. Therefore, prior to conducting multivariate analysis, we performed an initial screening to identify metabolite ratios with potential diagnostic relevance using the ≪Biomarker Analysis≫ module of the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web-based platform. This module generated ratios for all possible metabolite pairs and ranked them based on statistical significance (p-values). Metabolite ratios that demonstrated both statistical significance between groups and biological relevance were selected for further multivariate analysis. These included the glutamine-to-glucose ratio (QGR), glutamine-to-mannose ratio (QMR), glutamine-to-glutamate ratio (QER), alanine-to-glucose ratio (AGR), and phenylalanine-to-tyrosine ratio (PTR). The final data matrix, comprising 39 metabolic features (34 individual metabolites and 5 selected metabolic ratios), was then subjected to multivariate analysis (See Table S1 of SI).

Multivariate Approaches for Pattern Recognition

The data matrix containing concentration profiling information of circulating metabolites of each sample was used for multivariate statistical analysis. The 2D score plot based on unsupervised PCA showed clustering trends but no clear group separation between the T2DM and NC (see SI, Figure S2). Further, supervised analysis using partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed clear group separation in the 3D score plot, with Component 1 and Component 2 accounting for 32.1 and 24.4% of the variance, respectively (Figure A). Model performance was assessed via 10-fold cross-validation, yielding R 2 = 0.52 (good fit) and Q 2 = 0.41 (moderate predictive ability; Figure B).

To improve interpretability of the PLS-DA model and reduce noise, the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) variant was further applied by separating the predictive components from the orthogonal (nonpredictive) variability in the data (Figure S3). OPLS-DA eliminates irrelevant variability for class separation and enhances the model performance by focusing on the variation that is directly related to the class distinction. Compared to PLS-DA, the OPLS-DA score plot clearly provided discrimination between T2DM and NC groups, as shown in the SI (see Figure S3A). The cross-validation of the OPLS-DA model produced R 2 = 0.57 and Q 2 = 0.46, indicating good fit and moderate predictive power while confirming model validity against overfitting. The VIP (variable importance in projection) score plots based on both PLS-DA (Figure C) and OPLS-DA models (SI, Figure S3C) were then evaluated for screening metabolic features of discriminatory potential and considered as candidate biomarkers. The discriminatory metabolic features were further evaluated for statistical significance using two-sample t tests, as depicted in Figure A. The relative quantitative variations of the metabolic features between the study groups were visualized using box-and-whisker plots, as shown in Figure B. These significantly altered metabolic features were further examined in both the younger and older cohorts, and the detailed results are presented in Annexure-III of the SI.

Correlation Analysis

To establish the clinical relevance of the observed metabolic alterations, the statistical correlation of clinical features (HbA1c, Insulin, and HOMA-IR) (Figure A–C) and metabolic ratios (QGR, QMR, and AGR) (Figure D–F) was performed using the Spearman correlation analysis technique. The correlation coefficient (r value) reflects the strength of the relationship, whereas the positive or negative sign of the correlation coefficients indicates direct or inverse association, respectively. Insulin showed a significant positive correlation with waist circumference (WC) (r = 0.597, p < 0.0001), weight (r = 0.570, p < 0.0001), and BMI (r = 0.534, p < 0.001). In contrast, insulin exhibited a strong negative correlation with glycine (r = −0.481, p < 0.001), while moderate negative correlations were observed with PTR (r = −0.399, p < 0.001), phenylalanine (r = −0.398, p < 0.001), threonine (r = −0.383, p < 0.001), and aspartate (r = −0.379, p < 0.001) (Figure A). HOMA-IR exhibited a pattern similar to insulin, showing strong positive correlations with waist circumference (r = 0.527, p < 0.0001), weight (r = 0.494, p < 0.0001), and BMI (r = 0.494, p < 0.01). It also demonstrated a significant positive correlation with fasting glucose (r = 0.560, p < 0.0001). In contrast, HOMA-IR was negatively correlated with glycine (r = −0.470, p < 0.001) and threonine (r = −0.406, p < 0.01), as well as moderately with glutamate, aspartate, creatinine, phenylalanine, lysine, and histidine (p < 0.05 for each) (Figure B). Fasting glucose showed a strong positive correlation with glucose levels (r = 0.426, p < 0.001). It exhibited significant negative correlations with the metabolic ratios QGR (r = −0.641, p < 0.0001), QMR (r = −0.547, p < 0.0001), and AGR (r = −0.508, p < 0.001). Moderate negative correlations were observed with glutamine (r = −0.381, p < 0.01), acetate (r = −0.370, p < 0.01), and tyrosine (r = −0.330, p < 0.05) (Figure C).

Metabolite–metabolite correlations reveal how interconnected metabolic pathways are altered in T2DM. For example, strong correlations between metabolites involved in gluconeogenesis, TCA cycle, and glucose–alanine cycling highlight the systemic metabolic dysregulation characteristic of T2DM. Clearly evident that the altered metabolic features are strongly correlated in the T2DM group, suggesting affected metabolic pathways are driving T2DM.

Discussion

The present study aims to evaluate the age-related metabolic alterations associated with T2DM using NMR-based metabolomics, a powerful tool for investigating metabolic disturbances in health and disease. Despite its potential, challenges remain in ensuring consistency and reproducibility across metabolomics studies. To address this, we profiled fasting serum metabolites in middle-aged T2DM patients along with the age- and sex-matched normal control (NC) group. In addition to explicit metabolite concentrations, the present study also involved metabolic ratios to gain further insights into disease pathophysiology and pathway dynamics based on statistical correlation analysis. The multivariate statistical analysis revealed a clear separation between the two groups, underscoring a distinct metabolic profile. The altered metabolic profile in the T2DM group included significant changes in glucose, mannose, glutamine, histidine, urea, alanine, glutamate, and glycine levels. In addition, key metabolic ratios, namely, QGR, AGR, QMR, and QER, were markedly decreased, while PTR was elevated. To further explore the relevance of these findings in the context of aging, the metabolic alterations observed in middle-aged T2DM patients were compared with those in younger and older T2DM cohorts. These metabolic alterations provided valuable insights into the underlying disease pathogenesis (Figure ) and its association with aging, helping to identify potential age-related metabolic disruptions in T2DM.

Circulatory Alanine Levels and AGR Reflecting Enhanced Hepatic Gluconeogenesis

Our findings emphasize the metabolic alterations associated with insulin resistance in T2DM subjects, particularly changes in circulating glucogenic amino acids, such as alanine, glutamine, and glutamate. These alterations align with studies linking insulin resistance to disrupted gluconeogenesis and impaired alanine metabolism. , Alanine is converted to pyruvate in the liver by alanine aminotransferase, generating glutamate, which contributes to urea synthesis. Reduced serum alanine levels and AGR suggest increased alanine aminotransferase activity and enhanced hepatic gluconeogenesis, supported by elevated glutamate and urea levels, indicating heightened urea cycle activity. , Spearman correlation analysis revealed strong negative associations between AGR and clinical markers like HbA1c and fasting glucose, as well as circulating glucose and mannose, a metabolite linked to insulin resistance. ROC analysis further confirmed AGR’s high diagnostic value, underscoring its role in assessing metabolic dysregulation in T2DM. These results are further supported by animal studies showing more pronounced hepatic dysfunction in aging T2DM mice compared to controls.

Altered Glutamine Metabolism and Inflammation

Glutamine, the most abundant amino acid in the human body, is vital for energy metabolism, cellular health, and redox homeostasis. , In our study, circulating glutamine and QGR levels were significantly reduced in T2DM patients, with glutamine showing a progressive decline across age cohorts. These changes suggest enhanced glutaminolysis to meet energy demands under chronic inflammation. , QGR also showed a negative correlation with HOMA-IR, highlighting its negative association with insulin resistance. Our findings align with previous studies linking low glutamine levels to increased insulin resistance and impaired skeletal muscle metabolism, with evidence that supplementing glutamine can improve insulin sensitivity, inflammation, and thus help in maintaining glucose homeostasis. , It has been well reported that Inflammation induces oxidative stress, disrupts mitochondrial function, and exacerbates hyperglycemia by stimulating glucagon secretion. Beyond energy metabolism, glutamine supports antioxidant defense through glutathione synthesis and modulates inflammation by shifting the metabolic state from proinflammatory to anti-inflammatory. − The reduced level of QER hints to activated glutamine metabolism, likely involving mitochondrial enzymes such as glutaminase, and may therefore be indicative of altered mitochondrial metabolic phenotype in T2DM. These findings underscore the contribution of metabolic alterations to T2DM pathogenesis and implicate glutamine, QGR, and QER as promising biomarkers for tracking disease progression, insulin resistance, inflammatory activity, and mitochondrial dysfunction. ,

Phenylalanine-to-Tyrosine Ratio as an Indicator of Oxidative Stress in T2DM

Mitochondrial dysfunction, driven by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage, is a key feature of T2DM, especially in aging individuals. Oxidative stress and inflammation contribute to insulin resistance, β-cell dysfunction, and vascular complications. , Phenylalanine and tyrosine are closely linked amino acids with tyrosine synthesized from phenylalanine via phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), a process dependent on tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). Under oxidative stress, BH4 availability decreases, impairing PAH activity and reducing phenylalanine-to-tyrosine conversion. This leads to an elevated level of circulating phenylalanine, reflecting metabolic stress and systemic inflammation. Studies consistently report increased phenylalanine levels in T2DM, insulin resistance, and obesity, suggesting its role as a biomarker of metabolic dysregulation. Our findings are consistent with previous reports, demonstrating significantly elevated PTR levels in middle-aged and older T2DM patients. This suggests oxidative-stress-induced dysfunction of phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), highlighting a potential metabolic imbalance that may contribute to T2DM pathophysiology in aging individuals compared to the younger cohort (Figure ).

Elevated Mannose Levels and Reduced Glutamine-to-Mannose Ratio in T2DM

The liver plays a central role in mannose metabolism, and alterations in circulating mannose levels may indicate impaired liver function. In diabetic individuals, plasma mannose levels remain stable after glucose loading, unlike those in normoglycemic individuals, where they decline. This suggests that hepatic glycogenolysis is a key source of mannose in diabetes, reflecting disrupted glucose–mannose metabolism. Elevated mannose levels are also linked to a higher BMI, as obesity is associated with increased mannose concentrations. Mannose is crucial for glycosylation, which regulates insulin receptor function, and its dysregulation in the liver may contribute to hepatic insulin resistance, playing a role in T2DM progression. Increased mannose levels may serve as an indicator of insulin resistance, altered liver function, and enhanced glycogenolysis. − Additionally, mannose metabolism influences glutamine levels, leading to reduced QMR. Our findings confirm significantly decreased QMR levels, with ROC analysis highlighting its strong diagnostic potential and negative correlation with HbA1c and fasting glucose. When comparing across age cohorts, mannose levels were significantly elevated in middle-aged T2DM patients, suggesting a potential link among the onset of aging, altered liver function, and increased insulin resistance. In contrast, the QMR ratio remained consistently reduced across all of the age groups.

Conclusions

The multivariate statistical analysis revealed a clear separation between the two groups, underscoring distinct metabolic differences. The altered metabolic profiles included glucose, mannose, glutamine, histidine, urea, alanine, glutamate, and glycine. Furthermore, key metabolic ratios, such as QGR, AGR, QMR, and QER, were notably decreased, whereas PTR was found to be increased in the T2DM group. Further, understanding the associations between clinical parameters and metabolic profiles helped identify key pathways for therapeutic targeting. For instance, positive correlation patterns between circulatory PTR levels and clinical parameters suggest that managing chronic inflammation and oxidative stress could improve metabolic abnormalities in T2DM patients. Further, strong negative correlations between circulatory QGR or AGR levels and clinical parameters (such as fasting glucose or HbA1c) highlight their utility as noninvasive biomarkers for disease diagnosis and progression monitoring.

Studies have established an association of low-grade inflammation, aging, and oxidative stress with the development and progression of T2DM. Aging also impacts cellular metabolism and so mitochondrial energy and amino acid metabolism. Emerging evidence and studies have revealed a causal relationship between impaired mitochondrial functioning and T2DM. Aging increases the risk of T2DM through mechanisms such as insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, while T2DM accelerates aging by promoting oxidative stress, age-related mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered energy metabolism, thus further exacerbating the relationship between aging, inflammation, and type 2 diabetes. As mitochondrial dysfunction impacts several cellular and biological metabolic processes, interventions to improve mitochondrial functioning have immense potential to improve disease management strategies in T2DM. Metabolomics offers a powerful tool for exploring these relationships and gaining a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying aging and age-related diseases such as T2DM.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Participants diagnosed with T2DM were recruited from the Outpatient Department of the participating institution after obtaining informed consent. All individuals included in the T2DM study group were follow-up cases attending the clinic for routine monitoring and were evaluated based on clinical diagnostic criteria defined by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the World Health Organization (WHO), including fasting plasma glucose levels ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%. All of the methods followed are in accordance with the study protocol approved by the institutional ethical committee of All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Raebareli (IEC code F. 7/BIOETHICS/AIIMS-RBL/APPRO/EM/2021/2). All patients were on standard treatments of T2DM, predominantly metformin, though specific medication data (e.g., dosages or duration) were not systematically recorded. Exclusion criteria included terminal illness, comorbidities, pregnancy or lactation, insufficient blood samples, and lack of informed consent. Comprehensive case histories were collected using bilingual questionnaires (English and Local language), covering age, sex, physical parameters (weight, height, waist circumference, and BMI), pulse rate, and blood pressure. Age- and sex-matched control subjects from similar socioeconomic backgrounds were included. All controls had normal fasting glucose levels and no known history of diabetes, neurological disorders, cardiovascular diseases, or other acute or chronic illnesses.

Sample Collection

After 12 h of fasting, peripheral venous blood samples were collected from all enrolled subjects in the morning via venipuncture, using BD Vacutainers without additives for metabolomics studies, insulin estimation, and lipid profiling (HDL, VLDL, LDL, triglycerides, and total cholesterol) and BD Vacutainer with sodium fluoride for plasma glucose analysis. Serum (for NMR) and plasma (for clinical biochemistry) samples were subsequently prepared as described in previous studies , and were then aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Samples were processed within 40 min of collection as the presence of cells in whole blood can modulate the concentration of glucose and lactate and thus can affect the metabolic profile.

Sample Preparation and NMR Measurement

The stored serum was thawed and vortexed for homogeneity, and 300 μL was aliquoted for metabolomics analysis. It was mixed with an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (200 mM, 0.9% NaCl in 100% D2O, pH 7.4) containing 0.01% NaN3 to prevent contamination. Additionally, 0.1 mM TSP (sodium 3-trimethylsilyl-(2,2,3,3-d4)-1-propionate) was added for referencing of the chemical shift axis. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, 550 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube (Wilmad, USA). To prevent metabolite degradation, the samples were maintained at 4 °C until data acquisition. NMR experiments were conducted at 300 K by using a Bruker Avance III spectrometer, operating at a proton frequency of 800.21 MHz. Further details, including acquisition parameters, spectral preprocessing, and metabolite identification and quantification, are discussed in Annexure-I of SI. The NMR spectra were processed and used for concentration profiling of serum metabolites using the NMR suite of commercial software CHENOMX (v9.0, https://www.chenomx.com) following the details described in SI, see Annexure-I. The quantified metabolites were then exported in a Microsoft Excel sheet and used for multivariate analysis (MVA). Before MVA, we assessed the discriminatory potential of various metabolic ratios using the Biomarker Analysis module of MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca). The detailed steps of this procedure are provided in Annexure-II of the SI. Based on this analysis, five metabolic ratios were rationally selected for further investigation, that is, glutamine-to-glucose (QGR), alanine-to-glucose (AGR), glutamine-to-mannose (QMR), glutamine-to-glutamate (QER), and phenylalanine-to-tyrosine (PTR) for multivariate modeling and diagnostic testing. The circulatory concentrations of all the metabolites were reported in μM, except for glucose and lactate, and the concentrations were reported in millimolar (mM).

Multivariate and Univariate Statistical Analysis

The concentration profiling data were further subjected to multivariate statistical analysis using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca). Both unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) methods were used for reducing the data dimensionality to a series of principal components (PCs), each representing correlated metabolic changes. The first two and three PCs containing the most relevant information were used to generate the score plots for visual interpretation. The supervised PLS-DA model was used to identify metabolic disparities and identify metabolic features of discriminatory potential. The robustness and predictive performance of the PLS-DA model were evaluated using a 10-fold cross-validation algorithm, assessing classification accuracy across the top five latent variables. Key metrics included R2, indicating goodness-of-fit, and Q2, reflecting the model’s predictive capability. Discriminatory metabolic features contributing to group differentiation were identified based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) score. The statistical significance between metabolic changes was evaluated using an unpaired two-sample t test with FDR-adjusted p-values (<0.05). Diagnostic accuracy of the metabolic features was assessed using univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. We also generated multivariate ROC curves using Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) for balanced subsampling and PLS-DA as a classification and feature ranking method so that to identify a set of metabolic features of highest cumulative diagnostic performance.

Correlation Statistical Analysis

The associations between clinical parameters (HbA1c, insulin, and HOMA-IR) and metabolic features (including metabolic ratios) were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis performed using inbuilt modules of MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca). The statistical correlations between metabolic features were also evaluated using the Pearson rank correlation method in MetaboAnalyst software. The metabolite–metabolite correlation heatmaps were generated using the BIOCPR open-source tool (https://biocpr.shinyapps.io/corplot/).
