Incidental Right Atrial Appendage Thrombosis in a Patient With a Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion Device
Authors: Salomon Chamay, Kanchi Raval, Bahar Fadaee, Darya Chekhava, Seth Uretsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight atrial appendage (RAA) thrombosis is exceedingly rare, is usually found incidentally on imaging, and carries a high thromboembolic risk.Case SummaryAn 83-year-old man with chronic atrial fibrillation complicated by previous bleeding episodes on anticoagulation and managed most recently with a WATCHMAN device presented after a mechanical fall. Imaging work-up revealed a new incidental finding of RAA thrombosis.DiscussionThis case highlights an uncommon thrombotic finding and that atrial fibrillation is a biatrial disease. Occlusion of the left atrial appendage does not eliminate the risk of right-sided thrombosis.Take-Home MessagesThromboembolic prevention in patients with chronic atrial fibrillation is challenging. Left atrial appendage occlusion devices, although effective, do not eliminate the risk of RAA thrombosis, as atrial fibrillation is a biatrial disease.

History of Presentation

An 83-year-old man presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain after a mechanical fall. Initial vitals were within normal limits, and physical examination was remarkable only for abdominal distention without tenderness or peritoneal signs. Computed tomography imaging of the chest and abdomen performed as part of a trauma work-up revealed an incidental finding of a right atrial appendage (RAA) mass concerning for a new thrombus (Figure 1).Take-Home Messages•Even after left atrial appendage occlusion, patients with chronic atrial fibrillation and elevated thromboembolic risk factors may be predisposed to right atrium and right atrial appendage thrombosis.•Although management strategies mirror those of left atrial appendage thrombosis, treatment of right atrial appendage thrombosis should be guided on a case-by-case basis, with a goal of preventing further thromboembolic and bleeding complications.Figure 1Axial and Coronal Views of Cardiac Computed TomographyAxial (left) and coronal (right) views of cardiac computed tomography demonstrating right atrial appendage thrombosis (yellow arrows).

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (AF), hypertension, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction status post biventricular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement, severe aortic stenosis treated with transcatheter aortic valve replacement, severe functional mitral regurgitation status post transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), nonobstructive coronary artery disease, and chronic kidney disease stage 3B. The patient's AF course had been complicated by significant intolerance to long-term anticoagulation, including a prior gastrointestinal tract bleed, multiple mechanical falls complicated by a bilateral subarachnoid hemorrhage, and most recently, a spontaneous left psoas muscle hematoma approximately 9 months before presentation. Given his elevated bleeding risk and concomitant severe functional mitral regurgitation, a multidisciplinary decision involving electrophysiology and the structural heart team was made to pursue left atrial appendage (LAA) occlusion with simultaneous mitral TEER 10 months before his current presentation.

However, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a small, layered thrombus within the LAA (Figure 2, Video 1), prompting abortion of the procedure. A plan was subsequently made to reattempt the intervention with cerebral embolic protection using a SENTINEL Device (Boston Scientific). Differential Diagnosis

At the time of his current presentation, the differential diagnoses included RAA thrombosis, myxoma, cyst, malignant tumors, and endocarditis. A benign or malignant RAA malignancy was less likely, given past imaging studies that did not show any abnormalities within the atria or appendage.

Investigations

During the current admission for recurrent falls and newly identified RAA mass, cardiology was consulted. Cardiac computed tomography angiography was obtained for further characterization and confirmed the presence of a smooth, homogenous, hypodense RAA mass measuring approximately 2.0 × 2.0 cm and lacking contrast uptake. Review of TEE studies during WATCHMAN evaluation and before implantation demonstrated no RAA abnormalities. However, retrospective review of past imaging was remarkable for the presence of mild-to-moderate tricuspid regurgitation, severe right ventricular (RV) enlargement, and right atrial (RA) dilation with an area of 41 cm2 (20 cm2/m2 indexed to body surface area) and an indexed volume of 86 mL/m2 measured using Simpson's biplane method. Transthoracic echocardiography performed during the current admission demonstrated a dilated RV cavity, moderately reduced RV free wall strain of −14.4% (reference value −20% or more negative), presence of an iatrogenic atrial septal defect with left-to-right interatrial shunting, and RA enlargement with an estimated RA pressure of 15 mm Hg. Because of the radiologic characteristics of the mass, history of chronic AF, RA and RV dilation and dysfunction, and lack of prior RAA abnormalities on prior echocardiographic imaging, a diagnosis of new RAA thrombosis was considered.

Management/Interventions

Management options were discussed extensively with the patient and his family, with particular focus on balancing the risks of thromboembolism against the patient's substantial bleeding risk. Despite his history of recurrent falls, the decision was made to resume anticoagulation, as the perceived thromboembolic risk was felt to outweigh bleeding concerns. The patient was initiated on reduced-dose apixaban and discharged with home physical therapy. At 6-month follow-up, the patient reported no recurrent falls, bleeding events, or thromboembolic complications.

This case highlights the occurrence of RAA thrombosis in a patient with chronic AF after LAA occlusion, raising important questions regarding residual thrombotic risk and optimal anticoagulation strategies in high bleeding risk populations.

Discussion/Concluding Statement

AF is a disorder characterized by synchronous remodeling of the right and left atria, including progressive enlargement, deformation, and impaired function of the atrial appendages. The prevalence of AF in the general population across age groups is 0.4%, affecting more than 2.2 million people in the United States, and is 9% in adults older than 80 years, making it the most common sustained arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice.1 Patients with AF have an increased risk of atrial thrombosis, cerebral stroke, and peripheral embolism.

Atrial thrombosis, with a reported prevalence of 13%, is considered the most common complication of AF. Although AF is associated with decreased ejection velocity for both the LAA and the RAA, studies have not found a significant association with enlargement of the RAA area.2 Heart failure and valvular heart disease can alter flow states and increase the risk of thrombosis. Regurgitative valvulopathies are more hemodynamically disruptive than stenosis, as the latter leads to disruptive flow and the former causes drastic fluid dynamics.3 Mitral valvulopathy is more hemodynamically relevant as it directly affects the atrium.3 Structural characteristics such as a smaller RA area and larger RAA neck widths compared with the left atrium and LAA, respectively, lower the risk of blood stasis in the RA/RAA, which explains the higher incidence of LAA thrombus formation (88%).2,4 RAA thrombi can occur in the setting of marked RA dilation, reduced appendage emptying velocities, and low flow states.5,6 Despite the low incidence of 0.6% to 5%, RAA thrombosis carries a clinically increased risk of embolic complications and mortality in patients with concomitant right heart thrombi.5,7,8

Evaluation with TEE in patients with AF is often focused on the LAA, which may result in underdetection of RAA thrombi and lead to the development of pulmonary embolism or systemic emboli through shunts or a patent foramen ovale. TEE is the gold standard modality for LAA thrombus screening, and it may provide information about the RA area and RAA morphology, including the presence of spontaneous echo contrast, which is considered the precursor of thrombus formation.4 Depending on the hemodynamics in the RA/RAA area, clots can form directly inside the RA, resulting in a more rounded morphology that may be attached to the atrial walls, with little or no mobility.4 This type of clot is associated with risk factors such as AF, RA enlargement, and correction of interatrial septal defects and can lead to embolic complications.4 Right heart thrombus detection is challenging and often requires alternative imaging modalities such as multidetector computed tomography or cardiac magnetic resonance.4 The differential diagnosis for RAA thrombosis includes benign and malignant neoplasms, infective endocarditis, fat-containing lesions, and artifactual etiologies.9 Neoplastic lesions characteristically enhance with contrast, present with internal heterogeneity or calcification, and have irregular margins. Lipomatous lesions were excluded by the absence of true fat attenuation and atypical appendage location. Infective vegetation was excluded by the appendage location without valvular involvement, smooth morphology, and absence of systemic infectious features. Computed tomography artifact was excluded by the lesion's persistence across multiple imaging planes and cardiac phases as well as its well-defined margins. Atrial appendage thrombus management options include anticoagulation, thrombolysis, or surgical embolectomy. Atrial appendage occlusion device implantation is primarily reserved for left-sided thrombus prevention. LAA occlusion devices do not guarantee complete prevention of thromboembolic risk, are associated with peridevice leaks and device-related thrombus, and do not eliminate the potential for right-sided thrombus formation. Treatment with anticoagulation can be limited because of the risk of bleeding. In the context of pulmonary embolism from right-sided thrombus, retrospective studies show an elevated risk of venous thromboembolism recurrence with anticoagulation therapy plus reperfusion strategies compared with anticoagulation therapy alone.4 Although treatment and management strategies for patients with RA/RAA thrombosis should be on a case-by-case basis, factors such as hemodynamics, risk of bleeding, RA/RAA dysfunction, and cause of thrombosis should be factored into decision making. Currently, evidence in favor of one treatment strategy over the others is lacking, and management guidelines for patients with RAA thrombosis after LAA occlusion have not been established.

In our patient's case, a smooth, homogenous, and hypodense mass with lack of contrast uptake was initially noted on computed tomography imaging. Although these imaging characteristics highly favored a diagnosis of thrombosis, the addition of clinical history and past echocardiographic examinations aided in a clearer diagnosis. Retrospective imaging review identified important risk factors for right-sided thrombosis. A large RA area and volume along with altered right-sided hemodynamics from RV enlargement and dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation, and atrial septal defect with left-to-right shunting mechanistically promote blood stasis and a hypercoagulable state within the RA and RAA. The decision to resume anticoagulation was based on shared decision making, which favored the possibility of a bleeding event over the risk of a thromboembolic complication. Established management guidelines for patients with atrial thrombosis after WATCHMAN implantation are lacking. A lower dose of apixaban was selected in our patient because of his significant bleeding history and to mitigate the risk of further bleeding events. Our patient was monitored with follow-up at 1, 2, 4, and 6 months after hospital discharge, without any evidence of bleeding or thromboembolic complications.
