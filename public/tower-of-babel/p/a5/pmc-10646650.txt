Simple demographic, laboratory and chest radiograph variables can identify COVID-19 patients with pulmonary thromboembolism: a retrospective multicentre United Kingdom study
Authors: Anmol Gangi-Burton, Nathan Chan, Abhishekh H Ashok, Arjun Nair, on behalf of the RADIANT group
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To (1) identify discriminatory demographic, laboratory and initial CXR findings; (2) explore correlation between D-dimer and radiographic severity scores; and (3) assess accuracy of published D-dimer thresholds to identify pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) in COVID-19 patients.Methods:Retrospective study including all COVID-19 patients admitted from 1st to 30th April 2020 meeting inclusion criteria from 25 United Kingdom hospitals. Demographics, blood results, CXR and CTPA findings were compared between positive and negative PTE cohorts using uni- and multivariable logistic regression. Published D-dimer cut-offs were applied.Results:389 patients were included [median age 63; 237 males], of which 26.2% had a PTE. Significant univariable discriminators for PTE were peak D-dimer, sex, neutrophil count at the time of the D-dimer and at admission, abnormal CXR, and CXR zonal severity score. Only neutrophil count at peak D-dimer remained significant for predicting PTE on multivariable analysis (p = 0.008). When compared with the published literature, sensitivity for PTE were lower than those published at all cut-off values; however, specificity at different cut-offs was variable.Conclusions:In this multicentre COVID-19 cohort, univariable admission factors that could indicate pulmonary thromboembolism were male sex, high neutrophil count and abnormal CXR with a greater CXR zonal severity score. The accuracy levels of published D-dimer thresholds were not reproducible in our population.Advances in knowledge:This is a large multicentre study looking at the discriminatory value of simple variables to determine if a patient with COVID-19 has PTE or not, in addition to comparing D-dimer cut-off values against published values.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a highly infective disease which has resulted in severe respiratory illness across the globe. There have been several reported complications of COVID-19 including pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE); the reported incidence of PTE ranges from 15 to 44%. 1–3 A meta-analysis of 36 studies demonstrated that for COVID-19 patients admitted to the intensive therapy unit (ITU), the pooled incidence of PTE was 28% compared with 10% for patients not in ITU. 4 However, the pathophysiology of PTE in COVID-19 is uncertain; it has been suggested that PTE could be both direct and indirect effects of COVID-19, augmented by individual patient risk factors. 5 COVID-19 patients, by virtue of their hypercoagulable state, also have higher levels of blood markers including D-dimer, fibrinogen, prolonged prothrombin time and international normalised ratio. 6 This is likely to be due to a combination of both systematic inflammation and true thrombotic disease, making it challenging to determine which patients may have PTE based on a D-dimer level alone. 7 In addition, COVID-19 patients may have occult thrombosis or microvascular thrombosis. 8 Up to 43% of COVID-19 patients have elevated D-dimer values 9 with patients with more severe disease having higher D-dimer values. 10 In addition, a raised D-Dimer has been identified as a risk factor of poor prognosis. 6 However, alternative (i.e., raised) thresholds of D-dimer values for improving the specificity of PTE diagnosis – inevitably at the expense of sensitivity – are currently not recommended. 11

During the pandemic, thousands of patients have been admitted with COVID-19 in hospitals within the United Kingdom (UK). As both COVID-19 and PTE can result in hypoxia, symptoms of chest pain and shortness of breath in addition to a raised circulating D-Dimer level, it has been clinically challenging to identify COVID-19 patients who have a higher pre-test probability of PTE. Crucially, such pre-test stratification is essential to ensure that a highly stretched imaging workforce is performing CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) on only the highest probability patients. The studies to date on the differences between the positive and negative pulmonary embolism cohorts in COVID-19 have been relatively small and single 12–14 with a couple of multicentre studies. 15,16

Clinical assessment, blood tests and chest radiographs (CXRs) are simple tests carried out in most Emergency Departments in the UK and are central to COVID-19 decision-making. 17 Submission of such data to a national database is currently being performed, 18 but still requires a degree of manual data collection and analysis. Demographic, laboratory and chest radiograph findings are variables that can be easily collected in a multicentre setting.

To the best of our knowledge, a multicentre evaluation of the discriminatory value of simple variables that can be easily collected in the Emergency Department and inpatient hospital setting in patients with PTE compared to those without PTE is not previously well established in the literature.

As such, the aims of this study were to: (1) identify any demographic, simple laboratory and CXR findings that distinguish COVID-19 patients who go on to develop pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) in a hospital setting with particular attention to simple markers of COVID-19 inflammation; (2) explore the relationship between D-dimer and radiographic severity scores; and (3) describe the accuracy of different published thresholds of D-dimer value for identifying PTE in this population.

Methods and materials

Patient data collection

Informed consent for analysis of this de-identified data was not required for this retrospective study in line with the general notice under the Health Service Control of Patient Information Regulations 2002 supporting the response to COVID-19. 19 This multicentre service evaluation retrospective study included all patients admitted from first to 30th April 2020 that met the inclusion criteria from across hospitals in the UK. The inclusion criteria included all patients that were aged 16 years and over, had a positive real-time reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) test for COVID-19, underwent a CXR within 72 h of admission and had a CTPA during their admission. Patients were excluded if the peak D-dimer was carried out later than 6 weeks from admission, if they had a CTPA carried out later than 8 weeks from admission and if the RT-PCR test was carried out more than 1 week following the admission chest radiograph, lymphocyte count greater than 40 × 109/litre and those with underlying haematological malignancy as the neutrophil and lymphocyte results would be significant outliers. D-dimer levels in COVID patients have been shown to peak between 5 and 10 days. 20,21 To account for this, we included patients with a peak D-dimer within the first 6 weeks from admission (Figure 1).

Consort diagram demonstrating the patient selection process.

Data collection and radiographic scoring

The study was conducted across 25 hospitals in the UK (Supplementary Material 1) and data was collected by 66 radiology trainees from 15 Clinical Radiology training schemes. Patient demographic and laboratory blood data were extracted from electronic medical records. The laboratory variables collected included neutrophil count at admission, lymphocyte count at admission, peak D-dimer, neutrophil count at the time of peak D-dimer and lymphocyte count at the time of peak D-dimer. The peak D-dimer refers to the highest D-dimer value during the patient’s admission. D-dimer values were converted into ng/ml for analysis. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated, a high NLR has been previously shown to be marker of poor prognosis in COVID patients, 22,23 in addition to an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality and massive embolism in non-COVID patients. 24

During central review, all anonymised data waere rereviewed and patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded.

UK radiology trainees at each site reviewed CXR images. This was carried out by one trainee assessing a unique set of CXRs at each site, with a total of 66 trainees evaluating CXR opacities. Trainees assessed CXRs and classified parenchymal opacities as peripheral, central or diffuse. In addition, they scored parenchymal involvement by dividing CXRs into six zones: right upper zone, right mid zone, right lower zone, left upper zone, left mid zone and left lower zone, and used a previously published simplified binary scoring system, 11 stating whether opacities were present or not for each zone whereby a score of 1 was given if opacities were present and 0 if no opacities (Figure 2). Trainees were provided with a protocol document with instruction on how to do this including instructions on how to divide CXRs into six zones and to seek advice from supervising senior trainees and/or Consultant Radiologists if they were uncertain about CXR interpretation. The zonal severity scores were sent by trainees for central review where they were then added together to give a total zonal severity score for each patient ranging from 0 to 6.

The six CXR parenchymal scoring zones.

The British Society of Thoracic Imaging (BSTI) published guidance on 16th March 2020 on how to classify patterns and severity of COVID-19 to help radiologists with image interpretation and standardising reports. 25 For those with a documented BSTI classification on the CXR report, the BSTI category (classic, indeterminate and non-COVID) and severity (mild, moderate and severe) were documented. 25

For CTPA studies, trainees at each site reviewed the existing report and documented whether the CTPA was positive or negative, the largest location of pulmonary embolus (central, lobar, segmental and subsegmental), which lobe(s) the pulmonary emboli involved. One trainee reviewed a cohort of patients at their site and documented whether the CTPA was positive or negative. If there were any discrepancies, they discussed these with senior trainees and/or Consultant Radiologists.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 27, IBM corporation).

All continuous variables were not normally distributed and are thus described as medians with interquartile (IQR) and full range; whereas, categorical variables are described as a number and/or percentage. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to assess for differences between continuous variables in the positive and negative PTE cohorts, whereas χ2 test was used to determine differences between categorical variables.

Spearman’s correlation was also used to assess for co-linearity between the variables that demonstrated statistically significant differences between the positive and negative PTE cohorts. Logistic regression was then performed to examine the influence of independent variables on the diagnosis of PTE, taking care to exclude co-linear variables. The Hosmer and Lemeshow Test was used to test the overall fit of the final regression model.

The area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of D-dimer for identifying PTE in our cohort was calculated. The sensitivity and specificity of previously published D-dimer thresholds in our cohort were analysed using the ROC curve. 26–31

Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Out of the 5837 patients assessed for inclusion, 389 consecutive patients were included [median age 63, IQR 53–73, range 18–93; 237 males (60.1%)]. 26.2% (102/389) patients had a pulmonary embolus (Figure 1).

Description of CTPA findings

The location of pulmonary emboli was most commonly found to be within the segmental pulmonary arteries (n = 36, 35.3%) compared with 12.7% in central (n = 13), 29.4% in lobar (n = 30) and 21.6% (n = 22) in subsegmental pulmonary arteries. Pulmonary emboli were most likely to be found in the right lower lobe (79.4%) (Supplementary Material 2).

The peak D-dimer was statistically significantly higher in the positive pulmonary embolus cohort (p < 0.001). The PTE-positive cohort were also more likely to be male (p = 0.036), have a higher neutrophil count both at the time of the peak D-dimer (p < 0.001) and at admission (p < 0.001), and a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio at the time of the peak D-dimer (p = 0.03) but age, lymphocyte count at admission, lymphocyte count at the time of the peak D-dimer and the NLR at the time of admission were not statistically significant. In addition, we found no statistically significant difference in the percentage change in NLR from admission to the time of peak D-dimer between the positive and negative PTE cohorts (p = 0.06).

No significant correlation was found between age and peak D-dimer (Spearman correlation coefficient 0.07, p = 0.329), or between sex and mean total severity score (Spearman correlation coefficient −0.08, p = 0.17).

The median time between the PCR test to CTPA was 5 days (IQR 5–13.5), whereas the median time between the peak D-dimer to CTPA was 1 day (IQR 0–3) and median time from the PCR test to peak D-dimer was 3 days (IQR 0–11).

Description of CXR findings

Patients with a PTE were more likely to have an abnormal admission CXR compared to those without a PTE [88/102 (86.3%) versus 218/287 (76.0%), p = 0.029]. The opacities were most commonly located in a bilateral [76/88 (86.4%)] and peripheral distribution [52/88 (59.1%)]. The CXR zonal severity score at admission was statistically significantly higher in the PTE-positive group (4 (IQR 3–5) versus 3 (IQR 2–4), p = 0.017). The BSTI classification was mentioned in 228 reports (58.6%) (Table 2).

Multivariable analysis

On multivariable logistic regression, the neutrophil count at peak D-dimer was the only factor that remained a significant predictor of PTE (b = 0.097, SE 0.03, p = 0.0047). There were strong correlations between neutrophil count at the time of peak D-dimer and NLR at peak D-dimer (r = 0.756, p < 0.001), and neutrophil count at the time of peak D-dimer and neutrophil count at admission (r = 0.638, p < 0.001); and moderate correlations between peak D-dimer and the neutrophil count at the time of peak D-dimer (r = 0.446, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Material 3).

Relationship between peak D-dimer values and severity scores

There was a nonlinear relationship but a generalised upward trend between D-dimer values and total CXR severity score and BSTI severity score [Figure 3 (a) and (b)]. However, there was only a very weak statistically significant correlation between zonal severity score and peak D-dimer (r = 0.16, p = 0.01).

Discussion

Diagnostic strategies for pulmonary thromboembolism in COVID-19 are fraught with dilemma. On the one hand, the attending clinician could take a cautious approach and request CT pulmonary angiography on any patient with an elevated D-dimer and a subjectively “discordant” hypoxia, exhausting an already stretched imaging service; on the other hand, a conservative approach combining empirical anticoagulation but reserving imaging for only the sickest patients who remain refractory to treatment carries a perceived danger of missing the opportunity to treat milder pulmonary thromboembolism earlier, before it causes deterioration.

Mindful of this conundrum, our aim was to assess whether we could leverage easily collected demographic, laboratory and chest radiograph data in the Emergency Department to identify COVID-19 patients most likely to have a PTE. Another advantage is that these simple variables are commonly the only results available to radiology trainees at the time of vetting scans on the frontline. We found that COVID-19 patients with PTE were significantly more likely to be male, and at admission are more likely to have an abnormal CXR (within 72 h) with bilateral CXR opacities, as well as higher neutrophil count and higher CXR severity score. Peak D-dimer values were higher, and NLR at peak D-dimer (but not at admission) as well as neutrophil count at peak D-dimer were also greater in the PTE cohort. Only the neutrophil count at peak D-dimer remained significant for PTE on multivariable analysis. This fact notwithstanding, our findings suggest that a high total severity score on a CXR (taken within 72 h), coupled with raised neutrophil count at admission, could thus be used in a practical sense to earmark patients who may need greater surveillance for PTE. In turn, such patients may benefit from earlier immunomodulation or enhanced thromboprophylaxis strategies, especially if they fail to improve or deteriorate.

Our large multicentre study involving results from 25 hospitals across the UK demonstrated that the rate of PTE (26.2%) in patients with COVID-19 in hospital is line with the reported literature ranging between 5 and 31%. 32

Previous studies have demonstrated that clinical predictors of PTE include statin therapy prior to admission, body mass index greater than 30 kg/m2 and increase in D-dimer value of 6 µg l−1. 33 In line with several other studies, we did not find any significant difference between age and presence of PTE but did find that male patients were more likely to have a positive CTPA. 27,34

Although the D-dimer level was significantly higher in the positive PTE cohort, a diagnostically acceptable negative predictive value was not demonstrated in this study. This is probably unsurprising; the pathophysiology of PTE in COVID-19 is multifactorial and involves immunothrombosis. COVID-19 patients, by virtue of their hypercoagulable state, have higher levels of blood markers including D-dimer, fibrinogen, prolonged prothrombin time and international normalized ratio, probably from a combination of both systematic inflammation and true thrombotic disease (including occult and microvascular thrombosis), making D-dimer alone unsuitable for identifying PTE.

Interestingly, the NLR on admission in the PTE cohort was not statistically significantly higher, although this ratio has been identified as an independent risk factor for critical illness in COVID-19 patients. Liu et al found that on admission the NLR was statistically higher in the severe COVID-19 cohort and the ratio increased quicker compared to those in the mild group. 35 Instead, both the NLR and the neutrophil count at peak D-dimer were higher in our PTE cohort, with only the neutrophil count at peak D-dimer remaining significant on multivariable analysis. We can only speculate that both the raised NLR and the neutrophil count at peak D-dimer probably indicate a heightened level of active immunothrombosis that is sustained in the PTE cohort, whereas it diminishes in the non-PTE cohort, leading to the statistically significant difference.

Our finding of a higher CXR severity score within 72 h of admission in the PTE cohort contradicts the findings by Fang et al 3 where severity of lung disease did not differ between the two cohorts on CT. 3 The latter is likely to be due to the smaller number of patients in each severity group compared with our study. We expected that the greater CXR severity (as a surrogate of increased inflammation) would correlate with higher peak D-dimer. However, the weak positive and relatively non-linear correlation between peak D-dimer and CXR severity score in our cohort implies that such a postulation is oversimplistic. On the one hand, unlike the NLR- which only becomes significant at peak D-dimer- the CXR, by virtue of being acquired at an earlier timepoint, may be insensitive to the extent of inflammation and therefore lag behind D-dimer values. Further investigations in temporal trends of CXR and contemporaneous D-dimer values would help us better understand this relationship.

There have been several single-centre studies which have suggested varying D-dimer cut-off levels. For instance, 500 µg l−1 with a 98.5 sensitivity and 12% specificity, 28 2660 µg l−1 with a 100% sensitivity (95% CI 88–100) and 67% (95% CI 52–79). 30 D-dimer values can be significantly elevated in patients with COVID-19 without PTE. 36 Disappointingly, the sometimes-high sensitivity and specificity of published D-dimer cut-offs for COVID-19-related PTE were not reproducible in our cohort. Similar to our findings, a meta-analysis using data from 27 studies showed that using lower cut-off values of 500 or 1000 µg l−1 had a high sensitivity but low specificity (96% sensitivity and 10% specificity; 91% sensitivity and 24% specificity, respectively) and higher cut-off values reduced the sensitivity (3000 µg l−1,72% sensitivity and 63% specificity), therefore not allowing this to be a threshold test to rule out PTE. 26 Another meta-analysis found that cut-off levels varied between 1000 and 4800 µg l−1. 37 As such, we do not think there is adequate justification for a higher threshold to be used for PTE diagnosis in COVID-19.

Although it would be controversial to use variables such as sex and age to limit patient’s receiving a CTPA in day-to-day practice, these variables could be used to prioritise patients with an increased likelihood of PTE for an urgent CTPA in an already overstretched UK imaging service. Future studies investigating whether such thresholds could perform better when integrated with other potentially significant demographic, laboratory and radiographic variables such as those found in our cohort, and with their temporal trends, would be welcome.

Limitations

As this was a multicentre retrospective study across a range of hospital both with electronic and paper patient records, we limited clinical data collection to those that would be easily accessible across all sites. As a result, demographic and clinical data collected included sex, age and laboratory blood results. Another limitation of this study was the lack of follow-up data. We did not include outcome data at discharge and consideration of adverse outcomes such as death. Given the multicentre nature with over 60 readers, performing central review to obtain interobserver agreement was not practical, especially in the midst of the pandemic. We note that, while the evidence for trainee interpretation in COVID-19 is limited, trainees have been shown to have substantial interobserver agreement when classifying COVID-19 CXR by severity, only slightly lower than for experienced radiologists (κ = 0.66 vs κ = 0.69–0.71), 38 whereas third-year trainees had moderate interobserver agreement for a range of cardiothoracic pathology on CXR (κ = 0.585) 39 and better diagnostic performance with more years of trainee experience. 40 Another limitation was that we did not find out the type of D-dimer assay used at each site which is a limitation of multicentre studies. 41 There is also ongoing evolution of the COVID-19 virus with the emergence of several new strains. Further research is required to assess if new strains have increased risk of pulmonary thromboembolism.

Conclusion

This multicentre study demonstrated that there are demographic, blood result findings and CXR findings which are significantly different between positive and negative PTE cohorts in COVID-19 patients. At admission, male sex, raised neutrophil count and increased CXR zonal severity score may help select patients that require a CTPA. Only neutrophil count at the time of the peak D-dimer was shown to be a significant predictor of PTE on multivariable analysis. In general, higher peak D-dimer values were demonstrated with higher CXR severity. The accuracy of published D-dimer thresholds for identifying PTE in COVID-19 was not reproducible in our population, where we observed relatively lower sensitivity for all published thresholds albeit with variable levels of specificity.
