Biosynthesis, characterization, antibacterial, antibiofilm, and antioxidant activities of sodium nanoparticles from Escherichia coli metabolites
Authors: Malak Mezher, Mahmoud I Khalil, Taymour A Hamdalla, Dalia El Badan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The sodium nanoparticles (NaNPs) were synthesized from Escherichia coli metabolites.•NaNPs showed pure crystalline structure with rod shape, 125.6 nm size, and 4.125 eV energy gap.•NaNPs exerted significant antibacterial and antibiofilm potentials against clinical bacteria.•NaNPs had notable scavenging activity against DPPH.Keywords: Sodium nanoparticles Escherichia coli, Metabolites, Antibacterial, Antibiofilm, Antioxidant

Introduction

A significant rise in multidrug resistance has recently been observed worldwide, primarily due to increased drug use, prolonged treatments, insufficient antibiotic tolerance, and improper dosing methods.1 Society has now entered an age known as the “post-antibiotic era”, where antibiotic resistance is causing a significant crisis.2 This resistance has resulted in an increase in numerous bacterial infections, particularly nosocomial, gastrointestinal, and colon-associated diseases.3 Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel antimicrobial agents to successfully address the challenges posed by rising multidrug resistance.

In addition to multidrug resistance, there is a notable increase in oxidative stress worldwide, linked to the existence of free radicals like hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide, and hydroxyl radicals. Usually, this oxidative stress occurs due to an imbalance in producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in cellular harm and threatening human health.4 The management of oxidative stress has been thoroughly investigated via multiple therapies, highlighting the pressing demand for newly formulated antioxidative substances.5

Nano-materials demonstrate exceptional characteristics, especially in the areas of biology and medicine. These layers primarily operate by interacting with other molecules, which improves their usefulness in biomedical applications as they can interact with microbial cells and oxidative substances.6 Nanoparticles (NPs) possess unique characteristics, including their tiny size, diverse shapes, extensive surface area, stability, low melting point, and additional intrinsic properties.7, 8

NPs can be synthesized using physical, chemical, and biological methods, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Physical methods, such as laser ablation, evaporation–condensation, and ball milling, typically require high energy input and specialized equipment but produce NPs with controlled size and shape.9 Chemical methods, including chemical reduction, sol–gel, and co-precipitation, allow precise control over composition and morphology; however, they often involve toxic reagents and generate hazardous by-products.10 In contrast, biological or green synthesis employs microorganisms, plant extracts, or bacterial metabolites (BMs) to reduce metal ions into NPs under mild conditions, offering a cost-effective, eco-friendly, and sustainable approach.10

Historically, NPs were created through a combination of physical and chemical methods. However, these techniques necessitate complex equipment, are expensive, and produce low outputs. Although the chemical method is often regarded as less expensive than physical techniques, it depends on hazardous and unstable substances that may threaten both human safety and the ecosystem. These difficulties led to the investigation of novel green synthesis options that are affordable, simple, non-harmful, and eco-friendly.8 These processes use extracts from plants or microbes that serve as reducing and stabilizing agents in NP synthesis, owing to their rich supply of active biomolecules like proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, and different polymers.11 Many organisms, including fungi, bacteria, and plants, can aid in the production of NPs and provide notable advantages in this field. Essential elements that contribute to the benefits of these organisms in NP production are their stable growth temperatures and periods, alongside the straightforwardness of extraction techniques.11 Nonetheless, the production of NPs via bacteria is becoming popular, as NPs generated by bacteria offer distinct benefits compared to alternative techniques. This method produces pure substances without needing demanding requirements. Furthermore, BMs that engage with metals act as capping agents, aiding in the reduction of agglomeration and aggregation of the NPs.12

Lately, the synthesis of metallic NPs has garnered significant interest because of their efficacy in numerous biomedical applications, especially concerning antimicrobial and antioxidant attributes.11, 13 Metallic NPs are considered very promising due to their significant effectiveness against a wide range of pathogens. Among these metallic NPs, silver, gold, zinc, and copper oxides are the most thoroughly investigated.14

Among metallic NPs, sodium NPs (NaNPs) are relatively rare and have not been thoroughly studied historically. Compared to other metallic NPs, they are considered to have similar physical and chemical characteristics to potassium NPs (KNPs).15 Previous research shows that KNPs are roughly 20 nm in size, which greatly affects their efficacy against pathogens.16 Additionally, a prior study detailed the green synthesis of KNPs utilizing the aqueous extract of Polyalthia longifolia leaves, indicating an average particle size of 200 nm. Nonetheless, these KNPs showed an important role as nano-fertilizers.17 A different study illustrating the green synthesis of KNPs using the aqueous extract of Sideroxylon capiri showed that the particles varied in size from 200 to 360 nm and displayed agglomeration. The particles showed significant antibacterial effectiveness against Bacillus, Enterobacter, and Fusarium species.18 Sheoran et al. also emphasized in an earlier study the production of KNPs from the leaf extract of Morus alba, showing an average particle size ranging from 21 to 30 nm. The KNPs mentioned demonstrated significant potential as fertilizers.19 Considering these results, it can be expected that NaNPs might show considerable antibacterial and antioxidant properties.

Recent research has concentrated on the processes that contribute to nanotoxicity. A main process includes the generation of ROS, leading to oxidative stress in cells, and eventually causing cell lysis. Additionally, the small dimensions and extensive surface area of NPs enable them to penetrate cells, allowing interaction with organelles and potential cellular damage. An additional mechanism includes particular ion charges present on the NP surface that interact with microbial cell DNA, resulting in cell death. It is essential to acknowledge that metallic NPs can additionally release metal ions that lead to cellular harm.20 Nonetheless, the mechanisms outlined are not completely comprehended and need additional explanation.

Thus, the main hypothesis of this research is to biosynthesize NaNPs from the metabolites of Escherichia coli (E. coli), characterize them utilizing several advanced techniques to identify their morphology, evaluate their antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against clinical isolates, such as Klebsiella pneumonia (K. pneumonia), Streptococcus pneumonia (S. pneumonia), Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium), and Salmonella Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium), and evaluate their antioxidant potential against 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH). We are confident that our efforts will greatly enhance progress in medical applications.

Methodology

Cultivation of E. coli and synthesis of NaNPs

After isolating E. coli from wastewater samples and identifying it by VITEK,21 the organism was placed in nutrient broth (NB) at a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland. The bacterial solution was kept at 37 ˚C for 24 h. The bacterial solution was subsequently centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 25 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant with the metabolites was filtered through Whatman paper (1 mm), and the pellet was discarded.22 To create NaNPs, the metabolites were mixed in equal volumes with 5 mM sodium nitrate (NaNO3) and stored in the dark at 37 ˚C for 24 h while agitating continuously at 120 rpm. The resultant solutions underwent centrifugation three times at 13000 rpm for 10 min each to purify the NaNPs. The NaNPs were then dried in an oven set to 40 ˚C. For biomedical uses, NaNPs solutions were dissolved in sterile distilled water at concentrations between 0.03125 and 1 mg/mL.

Characterization of the NaNPs

The NaNPs' morphology was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), Photoluminescence (PL), and Ultraviolet–Visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis), as outlined by Darwich et al.23

Antibacterial properties of the biosynthesized NaNPs

Isolation, identification, and cultivation of bacteria

The antibacterial properties of NaNPs were assessed against four clinical bacterial strains: K. pneumonia, S. pneumonia, E. faecalis, and S. Typhimurium. These bacteria were provided by Hamoud Hospital University Medical Center (HHUMC, Lebanon) and were earlier characterized using the VITEK 2 Automated System. To assess antibiotic susceptibility, along with antibacterial and antibiofilm investigations, a bacterial culture colony was suspended in Muller Hinton broth (MHB) and modified to a 0.5 McFarland standard.

Antibiogram assay

The resistance of bacterial isolates to multiple drugs was assessed against a range of broad-spectrum antibiotics utilizing the disc diffusion technique. The antibiotics evaluated consisted of Cefotetan (30 µg/disc), Piperacillin (100 µg/disc), Tetracycline (30 µg/disc), Cephalexin (30 µg/disc), Norfloxacin (10 µg/disc), Enrofloxacin (5 µg/disc), Pipemidic acid (20 µg/disc), Amoxicillin (25 µg/disc), Cefixime (5 µg/disc), Cefepime (30 µg/disc), Pefloxacin (5 µg/disc), Amikacin (30 µg/disc), Doxycycline (30 µg/disc), and Ertapenem (10 µg/disc). The method consisted of applying 100 µL of bacterial suspensions onto Muller Hinton Agar (MHA) and positioning the antibiotic discs prior to incubating at 37 ˚C for 24 h. ImageJ software (version 1.54 g, 2015) was then employed to evaluate the zones of inhibition (ZOIs). The results were evaluated against the guidelines set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024).24 The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Agar well diffusion

The test was performed to establish the ZOIs of the biosynthesized NaNPs. The experiment was conducted on MHA plates. A total of 100 µL of bacterial suspensions was uniformly distributed over the plates, which were subsequently punctured with a cork borer (6 mm). Then, 100 µL of the prepared NaNPs, with concentrations ranging from 0.03125 to 1 mg/mL, were added to the wells. Purified water acted as the control. Following a 1 h diffusion period at 4 ˚C, the plates were then incubated for 24 h at 37 ˚C. The zones of ZOIs were measured using ImageJ software, with any diameters greater than 7 mm deemed effective.23 The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Minimum inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations (MIC and MBC) assay

The broth microdilution method for evaluating the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was employed to examine the antibacterial properties of the biosynthesized NaNPs. In the wells of 96-well microplates, 90 µL of MHB and 10 µL of a bacterial suspension (0.5 McFarland standard) were added. Afterward, 100 µL of NP solutions with concentrations between 0.03125 and 1 mg/mL were added to the wells. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 ˚C for 24 h. The control cultures comprised only bacteria and broth. Following the incubation phase, the optical density (O.D.) was assessed at 595 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader. The MIC was determined to be the minimum concentration of NaNPs that inhibited visible bacterial proliferation. The MBCs were identified by applying 10 µL from the clear wells onto MHA plates. The MBC was identified as the minimum concentration that led to the death of 99 % of the bacteria, shown by the lack of colony growth on the agar.21 The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Time kill assay

The test was performed to identify the time needed for the biosynthesized NaNPs to suppress bacterial growth. Every well in a 96-well microplate was allocated 90 µL of MHB and 10 µL of a bacterial suspension calibrated to a 0.5 McFarland standard. Afterward, 100 µL of NaNPs, with a concentration two times that of the MIC, was added. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial growth was observed over 24 h by assessing the O.D. at 595 nm through an ELISA.23 The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Antibiofilm assay

The ability of biosynthesized NaNPs to inhibit bacterial biofilm formation and eliminate existing biofilms was assessed using biofilm inhibition and destruction tests. The biofilm inhibition assay was conducted using 96-well microtiter plates. Each well included 90 µL of MHB and 10 µL of a bacterial suspension normalized to 0.5 McFarland. The plates were kept at 37 °C for 3 h to promote early bacterial adhesion. Following this duration, 100 µL of NaNPs in concentrations between 0.3125–4 mg/mL (initiating from 2 × MIC) was added, and the incubation was prolonged for another 24 h at 37 °C. Bacterial cultures that were not treated served as the negative control. After incubation, the wells were washed three times with distilled water and then dried at 40 °C for 15 min. Biofilms were subsequently treated with 1 % crystal violet (CV) for 15 min at room temperature. Any leftover stain was removed by rinsing the wells five times with distilled water. The presence of biofilm was visually validated by noting purple rings on the well walls. Subsequently, 100 µL of pure ethanol was added to dissolve the attached CV, and the O.D. was assessed at 595 nm with an ELISA reader to measure the biofilm mass.21 A decrease in biofilm mass exceeding 10 % was considered significant, whereas negative values suggested a promotion of biofilm formation.21, 25 This is based on previously established methodologies in antibiofilm research. Small variations (below 10 %) are often attributed to natural fluctuations or experimental error rather than true antibiofilm activity. Hence, a reduction beyond this threshold is regarded as biologically meaningful. Conversely, negative values indicate an increase in biofilm biomass relative to the untreated control, suggesting potential stimulation of biofilm formation. The biofilm eradication test used a comparable method, with the primary difference being that bacteria were initially permitted to incubate in MHB for 30 h to facilitate mature biofilm growth prior to NaNPs treatment. The methods for staining and quantification were similar to those used in the inhibition assay.21 The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

DPPH free radical inhibition test

The DPPH free radical scavenging assay was used to evaluate the antioxidant properties of the biosynthesized NaNPs. For this assay, 1 mL of NaNP solution at various concentrations (12.5–––200 μg/mL) was mixed with 1 mL of 0.3 M DPPH solution prepared in methanol. A combination of DPPH and methanol without any NPs acted as the negative control. The reaction mixtures were stored in darkness for 30 min to prevent degradation due to light exposure. Following incubation, the absorbance was assessed at 517 nm with a spectrophotometer.23, 26 All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the DPPH scavenging activity percentage was calculated using equation (2). The experiment was repeated in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and significance testing (t-Test) were performed using Excel software (64-bit version), while graphs were generated using Origin software (64-bit version).

Characterization of the NaNPs

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns were examined using a Bruker D8 Advance with Cu-Kα radiation of wavelength 1.54060 Å. Data was gathered within a 2θ range of 20˚ − 80˚ at a speed of 0.02˚ per second. The XRD profiles were optimized using the MAUD software (version 2.992).23, 27 The major diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values correspond well with the standard Na reference pattern (JCPDS No. 04–0588) for cubic Na. Fig. 2 illustrates the XRD patterns of the NaNPs. It showed the existence of nine diffraction peaks. The peaks detected at 32.8˚, 38.6˚, 42.9˚, 50.3˚, 55.4˚, 64.9˚, 66.1˚, and 73.5˚ align with (311), (222), (220), (222), (400), (220), (420), and (422), respectively. The ninth peak seen at 24.2˚ has not been previously detected in NPs produced by bacteria. To verify the dimensions of the biosynthesized NaNPs, the crystalline size was determined from the XRD data applying the Scherrer equation (3). Upon completing the calculations, the average crystalline size of the NaNPs measured was roughly 125.61 nm. Furthermore, the level of crystallinity was determined using equation (4). Following the calculations, the percentage of crystallinity for the biosynthesized NaNPs was around 63.45 %.

XRD patterns of the NaNPs biosynthesized from the metabolites of E. coli.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was conducted using a microscope functioning at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV with a resolution of 0.1 nm. TEM images were taken at a magnification of 100 nm and analyzed with ImageJ software to evaluate the morphology and size distribution of the NPs.23 The TEM micrographs of the NaNPs, displayed in Fig. 3b, are annealed at a 200 nm scale and reveal that most of the NPs exhibit a rod-like morphology and are not clustered. Furthermore, the sizes of the particles are not consistent. By utilizing ImageJ software to measure the particle dimensions, the average particle size was determined by fitting the particle size histogram to a log-normal distribution, resulting in an approximate size of 125.8 nm, as illustrated in Fig. 3a.

(a) Particle size distribution and (b) TEM images of the NaNPs biosynthesized from E. coli metabolites.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR is a valuable method for identifying unknown substances and assessing the quantity and quality of constituents in a sample.28 A Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer was employed to analyze the functional groups of the synthesized NaNPs within the spectral range of 4000–––400 cm-1, at ambient temperature. Fig. 4 displays the FTIR spectra of the material synthesized at the nanoscale. The findings showed the creation of different vibrational bands. It should be noted that there are no existing studies on NaNPs. Nonetheless, in comparison to other metals, sodium (Na) shares similar traits with potassium (K) since they are in the same group in the periodic table. Consequently, the peaks achieved resemble those attained for KNPs. The observed peaks were at 3852.1, 3429.7, 2963.3, 2926.4, 2855.2, 2365.4, 2111.9, 1739.1, 1649.1, 1541.1, 1337.9, 1307.8, 1236.6, 1058.1, 908.8, and within the range of 620.3 – 430.2 cm-1.

FTIR patterns of the NaNPs biosynthesized from the metabolites of E. coli.

Photoluminescence (PL)

The PL analysis is crucial for examining the inherent and external characteristics of the biosynthesized nanostructures.29 A JASCO-FP-8600 fluorescence spectrophotometer equipped with a Xenon (Xe) laser was utilized to capture PL spectra at an excitation wavelength of 420 nm and spanning a range of 400 – 700 nm. The experiment was conducted using fluorescence emission spectroscopy at ambient temperature with an excitation wavelength of 420 nm and a visible range of 400 – 700 nm, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The PL spectra were analyzed using the Voigt function. The observable luminescence of NaNPs is due to the recombination of electron-hole pairs between the d-band and the sp-conduction band situated above the Fermi level.30 The PL results exhibited two peaks. A significant peak with high intensity was seen at 458 nm, while another peak was noted at 490 nm.

PL data of the NaNPs biosynthesized from the metabolites of E. coli.

Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis)

The UV–Vis technique is crucial for analyzing the chemical properties of NPs.28 The optical features of the NaNPs were evaluated at room temperature with a UV–visible spectrophotometer over a wavelength range from 200 to 700 nm. The UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed the formation within a range of 200 – 700 nm. Fig. 6a displays the UV–Vis results. The absorption peak was observed at 280 nm. The direct bandgap energy (Eg) of the biosynthesized NaNPs was determined from absorbance data by utilizing the correlation between the absorption coefficient (α) and photon energy (hv) as represented in equation (5).23 The recorded absorbance (A) was employed to determine the absorption coefficient (α) using the equation (6). The direct bandgap was obtained from Tauc’s plot (Fig. 6b) by extrapolating the linear segment of the Tauc’s plots curves (αhν)2 against the photon energy hν. The energy acquired from the NaNPs showed an average band gap of 4.125 eV. Urbach energy (Eu) denotes the extent of the band tails of the localized states. It is determined by the slope (lnα) of the linear section in relation to photon energy as per equation (7).23 Fig. 6c shows the results of the change in lnα as it relates to hν. The inverse of the slope of the linear section in the tail area is utilized to derive the Eu values of the NaNPs. The value of Eu determined is 1.19 eV.

MIC and MBC results

The MIC and MBC findings, illustrated in Table 3 and Fig. 7, demonstrated the bacteriostatic and bactericidal capabilities of the NaNPs. The NaNPs demonstrated a bactericidal effect on E. faecium (MBC = 0.125 mg/mL) and S. Typhimurium (MBC = 0.0625 mg/mL). Nonetheless, the NaNPs did not impact the pneumonia strains. It can be presumed that the NaNPs have a comparable impact on both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The MBC/MIC ratio verifies the bactericidal effect of the NaNPs. A ratio of less than 4 indicates the bactericidal effect of a NP.31 The MBC/MIC ratio is commonly used to categorize the antibacterial nature of a compound. Specifically, a ratio ≤ 4 indicates that the NPs exhibit a bactericidal effect, meaning they are capable of killing the bacterial cells rather than merely inhibiting their growth. However, a ratio > 4 suggests a bacteriostatic effect, where bacterial growth is inhibited but the cells are not necessarily killed. Our findings demonstrated a ratio of 2. This validates the antibacterial properties of our NaNPs. The findings indicated statistical significance with p-values less than 0.05, as demonstrated in Table S1.

Antibiofilm results

Biofilms are recognized to exhibit greater resistance than bacterial isolates.32 Therefore, concentrations beginning at MIC × 2 were used to evaluate the antibiofilm effectiveness. The findings displayed in Table 5 and Fig. S3 indicated a strong inhibition of biofilm formation by the NaNPs, with inhibitory percentages reaching 61.089 ± 4.118 % for E. faecium and 61.459 ± 1.401 % for S. Typhimurium. Nonetheless, the NaNPs displayed minimal and statistically insignificant destructive impact on the examined isolates. They eliminated E. faecium and S. Typhimurium biofilms at a comparatively high concentration (2 mg/mL), achieving a destruction rate of 10.538 ± 16.262 % for E. faecium and 21.927 ± 16.875 % for S. Typhimurium. The growth of the biofilm was inhibited in a manner dependent on concentration. The majority of the outcomes were significant with p-values < 0.05, as demonstrated in Table 5.

Discussion

Characterization of the NaNPs

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The eight peaks in the XRD pattern recorded between 32.8 and 73.5˚ have been previously identified in biologically synthesized NPs. Nevertheless, the ninth peak detected at 24.2˚ has not been seen before in bacterial-created NPs. This peak is not attributable to crystalline NaNP phases but rather to the biomolecules from E. coli metabolites adsorbed on the NP surface. Such features are commonly reported in green synthesis routes and represent residual organic matter rather than a new crystalline phase.33 Other peaks (above 30˚) exhibit crystalline sodium oxide (NaO).34 The peaks located at 42.9˚, 50.3˚, 55.4˚, 64.9˚, and 66.1˚ signify the existence of Na hydroxide (NaOH).35 Furthermore, the minor peaks detected may be attributed to impurities created by capping agents throughout the synthesis.35 These peaks verify the crystalline structure of the NaNPs and suggest the cubic phase of Na. Moreover, earlier research indicates that the existence of these peaks and the crystallinity of the NPs account for their small dimensions.35 The crystalline size (125.61 mm) and the degree of crystallinity (63.45 %) align, given the presence of certain impurities in the samples as noted earlier. Our finding aligns with an earlier investigation conducted by.36 Our findings are corroborated by earlier research, where Hassan et al. identified comparable peaks while synthesizing NPs from Rhizopus oryza (R. oryza), which matched those observed in our current study.37 Furthermore, Abdel-Maksud et al. synthesized NPs from Lactobacillus gasseri (L. gasseri) and showed identical peaks, thereby confirming the crystallinity of the NPs. In general, the XRD patterns demonstrate the purity of the produced NaNPs and disclose their crystalline arrangement.35

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM results indicating a NP size of 125.8 nm align with the XRD patterns. The irregular structure observed can be linked to the reducing and capping agents found in the metabolites of bacteria, as demonstrated by Darwich et al., who created silver NPs using pine needle extracts and noted agglomeration caused by the reducing agents in the pine extract utilized.38 The dimensions of the produced NaNPs vary from existing literature that generally indicates metallic NP sizes between 30 – 100 nm, although some research, such as that from Patil et al. and Siddique et al., has reported larger dimensions reaching 600 nm.16, 23, 39, 40, 41, 42, 23, 44 This indicates that the size of our NaNPs is acceptable and falls within the nano-range while suggesting that the measured NaNP size remains in the nano-scale but surpasses the typical range outlined in most studies.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The different vibrational bands observed for the NaNPs are described as follows. The peaks noted at 3852.1 and 3429.7 cm-1 are indicative of the O–H stretching group. The O–H group is attributed to the existence of alcoholic or phenolic stretches along with various bioactive metabolites.43, 44 The peak at 3429.7 cm-1 is associated with N–H stretch. This group described the existence of amid or amine groups within the bacterial protein membrane.45 The O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, originate from hydroxyl and amide groups in the BMs rather than residual solvents. These functional groups are known to act as reducing and capping agents, where phenolic O–H moieties reduce Na+ ions and amine/amide groups bind to the NP surface, forming a stabilizing corona that prevents aggregation and enhances aqueous stability.46 The peaks noted at 2963.3, 2926.4, 2855.2, and 2365.4 cm-1 relate to C–H stretching, indicating the presence of alkanes.19 Furthermore, the peak seen at 2111.9 may relate to N=C=S. The peaks noted at 1739.1, 1649.1, and 1541.1 cm-1 correspond to C=C, C=O, and C=N stretches, respectively. These account for the existence of aromatic rings in the nucleus's nucleic acids, amides, and polysaccharides.17 The peak detected at 1337.9 cm-1 is attributed to C-N stretching, indicating primary amines and aliphatic amino acids are present.38,41 The observed peaks at 1307.8, 1236.6, 1058.1, and 908.8 cm-1 relate to C-O–H, C-O, and C=O stretching. These stretches signify the existence of carbonyl groups participating in the reduction of Na + to NaNPs.41, 44 The observed peaks at 1307.8, 1236.6, 1058.1, and 908.8 cm-1 relate to C-O–H, C-O, and C=O stretching. These stretches signify the existence of carbonyl groups participating in the reduction of Na + to NaNPs.18 Furthermore, the detection of C=O implies the existence of aldehyde, ester, and carboxylic acid groups.45 The C-O stretching further corroborates the connection between the produced NaNPs and OH/CO groups.19, 44 The peaks recorded within the range of 620.3 – 430.2 cm-1 are ascribed to the functional group of metal–oxygen bonds (NaO), thereby indicating NaO stretching.17

Photoluminescence (PL)

The PL analysis of NaNPs is performed to provide insight into their electronic structure, surface defects, and optical properties, which are closely related to their biological activities. PL emission can reflect the presence of surface states or trap sites, which influence interactions with bacterial cells and ROS generation, thereby affecting antibacterial, antibiofilm, and antioxidant activities. The PL findings revealed two peaks (458 and 490 nm) within the blue emission spectrum. Blue emissions were reported to fall between 454 – 490 nm.47, 48 The visible emissions are influenced by the synthesis conditions, NP size, and their morphology. The blue emissions arise from the recombination of electrons in the Na vacancies with the hole present in the valence band.38 To clarify the mechanism, the visible luminescence is due to the excitation of electrons from the d-bands into states that are above the Fermi level. Furthermore, an electron from the sp-band recombines with the hole photoluminescent through radioactivity after undergoing relaxation via electron–phonon scattering, resulting in energy dissipation.49

Ultraviolet–Visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis)

The intense band observed at 280 nm is attributed to transitions of aromatic and phenolic groups in the capping biomolecules. Metal NPs exhibit UV-region responses; in our system, any NaNP specific feature is convoluted with the capping layer absorption. The absence of long wavelength shoulders or secondary bands suggests minimal aggregation, consistent with TEM data.50 The absorption peak observed at 280 nm resembles the one previously identified by Dolati et al., who produced copper oxide NPs from Bacillus coagulans (B. coagulans) metabolites.51 This peak indicates the conversion of NaNO3 to NaNPs.52 The quantity of electrons released during the reduction of NO3 to NO2 influences the intensity of the color achieved. This reduction is accountable for the conversion of Na+ to Na0.53 Furthermore, the recorded result accounts for the excitation of metals during the creation of the NaNPs.53, 54 Previous reports indicated that a single peak formation signifies small-sized NPs, while the peak noted at 270 – 280 nm suggests rapid photocatalytic interaction.54 The UV results observed demonstrate ideal states of the synthesized NaNPs, given the lack of fluctuations in the peaks and absorption intensities. The bandgap energy acquired (4.125 eV) resembles values found in earlier research on the creation of plasmonic nanoparticles from plant extracts.43, 44 The energy obtained (1.19 eV) aligns with earlier studies that noted the synthesis of silver (Ag) and KNPs.18, 41

Antibacterial potential of the NaNPs

Antibiogram

Our results have shown multi-drug resistance of the tested clinical bacterial isolates. These results are confirmed by previous reports showing a high emergence and danger of the tested bacterial isolates.55, 56, 57, 58 It’s worth mentioning that the tested antibiotics are of broad-spectrum potential. They act by different mechanisms including inhibition of DNA and protein synthesis, inhibition of cell wall synthesis, and inhibition of folic acid metabolism.59 In addition, it is shown that bacteria can resist antibiotics by limiting their uptake, and modifying their target.60 For that, new antibacterial agents are needed to decrease the spread of multidrug resistant bacteria.

Agar well diffusion

The findings indicated a comparable impact on both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. This is similar to the antibacterial effect of zinc-oxide NPs reported by Aouadi et al., who showed a significant inhibition of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus).61 This may be due to various factors, such as bacterial shape, production methods, dimensions, surface area, and chemical makeup of the NPs. These factors are crucial for antibacterial effectiveness.39 Comparable findings were reported in an earlier study demonstrating a notable antibacterial impact of alginate NaNPs on K. pneumonia and S. aureus.62 The NaNPs function through various mechanisms, such as generating ROS that leads to cell injury, engaging with the cell wall, and modifying internal metabolic processes by disturbing protein structures.37

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The findings validated the agar well diffusion outcomes, demonstrating a comparable effect on both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Previous reports have indicated that NPs typically demonstrate a stronger inhibitory effect on Gram-positive bacteria compared to Gram-negative bacteria because Gram-negative bacteria possess a protective cell wall.23, 31, 63 Nevertheless, additional reports indicated a superior effect of NPs on Gram-negative bacteria compared to Gram-positive bacteria.37, 40 The disparity in the efficacy of NPs towards Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria is linked to differences in their bacterial structures, where Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick peptidoglycan layer without an outer membrane, while Gram-negative bacteria have a thinner peptidoglycan layer accompanied by lipopolysaccharides and an outer protective cell wall.37 The NaNPs described in this study show comparable effects on both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, making them a promising candidate in antibacterial applications. The bactericidal properties of NaNPs can be attributed to their purity, crystalline structure, various functional groups, and low bandgap energy.23, 47, 64 However, the resistance observed in S. pneumonia and K. pneumonia may stem from the relatively larger size of the NPs (125.8 nm), leading to agglomeration and reducing their effectiveness in infiltrating the bacterial cell membrane. The lower MBC of NaNPs for S. Typhimurium (0.0625 mg/mL) compared to E. faecium (0.125 mg/mL) can be attributed to differences in cell wall architecture. The thinner peptidoglycan layer and negatively charged outer membrane of Gram-negative S. Typhimurium facilitate NP penetration and bactericidal activity, whereas the thicker, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan of Gram-positive E. faecium provides increased resistance, requiring a higher concentration for effective killing.46 When comparing NaNPs to other metal-based NPs, silver NPs (AgNPs) and copper oxide NPs (CuO NPs) are widely reported to exhibit strong antibacterial activity at very low concentrations, often with MBC and MIC values in the range of 0.01–––0.1 mg/mL for Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, depending on synthesis method and particle size.65 In our study, the biosynthesized NaNPs showed MBC values ranging between 0.0625–––0.125 mg/mL, which are comparable to the effective concentrations reported for other metal NPs synthesized via biological routes, although slightly higher than highly potent AgNPs. This could be attributed to intrinsic chemical reactivity of the metal (Ag > CuO > Na), particle size and shape, affecting surface area and interaction with bacterial membranes, and capping and stabilizing biomolecules from the biosynthesis process, which can modulate NP-bacteria interactions.8 Overall, NaNPs exhibit significant antibacterial activity that is within the effective range for biologically synthesized metal NPs, providing a promising alternative with low cost synthesis and biocompatible capping agents. This information is added to the manuscript and highlighted.

Time kill test

The duration of inhibition (5 h) aligns with the adhesion and initial colonization of the bacteria.38 This clarifies how NaNPs can hinder the adherence of bacterial cells and their colonization by restricting nutrient absorption by the bacteria. They also inhibit bacterial cells by altering their conformation, which modifies the bacteria's metabolic processes and ultimately leads to cell lysis.66

Antibiofilm potential of the NaNPs

Biofilms are recognized to have greater resistance compared to bacterial isolates. This is attributed to their capability to produce exopolysaccharides that enhance their adhesion. This characteristic enhances their durability. To eliminate biofilms, it is believed that NaNPs impede the release of exopolysaccharides through targeted interactions, thereby hindering their adhesion to biofilms.21 The higher biofilm inhibition (62 %) compared to destruction of pre-formed biofilms (21 %) can be attributed to the NP size (125.8 nm) and the protective exopolysaccharide matrix of mature biofilms. While NaNPs effectively prevent biofilm formation by interacting with planktonic cells, penetration into the dense exopolysaccharides of established biofilms is limited, reducing destructive efficacy, consistent with literature reports.65

Radical scavenging potential of the NaNPs

The antioxidant capability of the NaNPs can be elucidated by various mechanisms. The latter encompasses the capability of NaNPs to function as reducing agents and hydrogen donors, thereby enhancing their antioxidant effectiveness.38 Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that NPs can engage in redox reactions through the donation of a hydrogen atom. This also enhances the antioxidant capacity. It is important to note that the antioxidant effect is directly related to the concentration of the NaNPs, where the scavenging effect grew as the NaNPs concentration increased.38 The measured DPPH scavenging activity of the biosynthesized NaNPs (23 %) is lower than that of ascorbic acid (49 %) primarily due to the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of the NaNPs. Unlike standard antioxidants such as ascorbic acid, which have multiple hydroxyl groups capable of donating hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals, NaNPs rely on the surface-bound BMs like proteins and phenolics for radical scavenging. The lower density of such active moieties on the NP surface limits their overall antioxidant efficacy.46 It’s worth mentioning that the assay was conducted under standard conditions, but factors such as NP dispersion, solvent interactions, and accessibility of surface-bound functional groups can affect the measured DPPH activity. Larger NP size (125.8 nm) and the capping layer may partially hinder direct interaction between radicals and reactive groups on the NP surface. Similar results have been reported for biogenic NPs, where antioxidant activity is often lower than pure chemical antioxidants due to limited surface-exposed functional groups, though still sufficient to confer biological activity.46 When compared to other metal-based NPs, a study by Arslan et al. reported that copper-oxide NPs had similar effect to our NaNPs.66 In addition, another study reported the synthesis of iron-oxide NPs and showed significant antioxidant potential similar to our reported results.67

Conclusion

The present research sought to evaluate the antibacterial, antibiofilm, and antioxidant activities of NaNPs produced from the metabolites of E. coli. Following the synthesis of NPs, XRD characterization demonstrated a pure crystalline structure of NaNPs, displaying nine peaks located between 32.8 and 73.5˚. The TEM findings indicated a size of 125.8 nm for the NaNPs which had a rod-shape. The FTIR indicated various functional groups, particularly Na stretching. The PL results revealed two peaks in the blue and visible emissions. The UV–Vis data indicated absorbance at 280 nm, revealing a bandgap of 4.125 eV. Furthermore, following the isolation of the bacteria, two Gram-positive bacteria (S. pneumonia and E. faecium) and two Gram-negative bacteria (K. pneumonia and S. Typhimurium) were obtained. The antibiogram showed that the bacterial isolates exhibited multidrug resistance. The antibacterial outcomes revealed a bactericidal action of the NaNPs on E. faecium and S. Typhimurium (MBCs falling between 0.0625 – 0.125 mg/mL). The NaNPs suppressed bacterial growth after 5 h of incubation. The antibiofilm findings demonstrated a strong inhibitory effect of the NaNPs on biofilm formation (inhibition percentage reaching 62 %) and a limited biofilm destruction effect (maximum destruction percentage of 21 %). The antioxidant findings indicated a notable scavenging effect of the NaNPs (antioxidant percentage of 23 %) on DPPH. These findings elucidate the potential of NaNPs for application in various biomedical areas, particularly in antibacterial, antibiofilm, and antioxidant domains.
