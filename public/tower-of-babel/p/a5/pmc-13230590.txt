Circadian disruption of brain temperature and heart rate in two periods after intracerebral hemorrhage surgery: a retrospective study
Authors: Lu-Ting Kuo, Ue-Cheung Ho, Hsueh-Yi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) remains a severe subtype of stroke with limited tools to predict postoperative outcomes. This retrospective study investigated postoperative brain temperature (BrT) and heart rate (HR) rhythms in 74 surgically treated ICH patients and evaluated their associations with 1-year outcomes. BrT and HR data from two postoperative periods (1–60 h [A] and 61–120 h [B]) were analyzed using the Cosinor method to determine circadian rhythmicity. BrT and HR rhythms were observed in 60.8% and 36.4% of patients, respectively, and rhythm transitions occurred in 35.1% and 43.2%. Multivariable analysis identified age, initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, BrT rhythm in period B, and HR mesor in period A as independent predictors of 1-year functional outcome, whereas only initial GCS predicted mortality. Postoperative analysis of BrT and HR rhythms reveals dynamic alterations in physiological cycles after ICH and offers additional prognostic information beyond conventional clinical parameters.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for 20–40% of all stroke cases, with only a minority of survivors achieving long-term functional independence1. Clinical and radiological variable-based predictive models have been developed to estimate mortality and functional outcomes after ICH2, which support clinical decision-making and facilitate communication among healthcare providers, patients, and their families. However, existing predictive models remain limited, particularly in surgically treated patients with ICH, for whom outcomes are shaped by a complex interplay of clinical and radiological factors that complicate accurate prognostication3.

Biological rhythms are natural cycles of behavior, physiology, and metabolism regulated by internal biological clocks, distributed throughout the body, with the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus being the main pacemaker4. These rhythms are critical for overall health maintenance and the regulation of numerous physiological processes, including the sleep/wake cycles, hormonal secretions, metabolic functions, and immune responses. Biological rhythms are synchronized with environmental cues, including light-dark cycles, temperature, and time of food intake, through various clock genes and signaling pathways5. Disruptions in the circadian rhythm can negatively impact recovery and outcomes in patients with neurological disorders, including ICH6, and affect the function of various body systems regulated by the brain, including the endocrine, immune, and cardiovascular systems.

Stroke patients may have disrupted circadian rhythms and sleep disorders, affecting rehabilitation outcomes7. Therefore, internal clock status evaluation during ICH treatment is important. Brain temperature rhythm is another predictor of functional outcome after ICH8. Nevertheless, the intracranial condition, including intracranial pressure (ICP), extent of brain swelling, and brain metabolism, may exhibit dynamic changes during the post-ICH or postoperative period. However, whether the characteristics of rhythmic variations in brain temperature (BrT) and heart rate (HR) change during treatment after ICH remains unclear.

Heart rate was therefore included not only as a marker of autonomic regulation but also as a complementary systemic circadian signal that is continuously available in neurocritical care monitoring, allowing comparison between central brain temperature rhythms and peripheral physiological rhythmicity after intracerebral hemorrhage. We investigated changes in BrT and HR rhythms and other characteristics during the postoperative course in patients with ICH instead of a single measurement. The primary objectives were to assess two-period changes in the acute stage after ICH surgery and evaluate their utility as prognostic indicators.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study, conducted between 2011 and 2018 at National Taiwan University Hospital and its Yunlin branch, analyzed BrT, HR, clinical characteristics, and 1-year patient outcomes in patients who underwent surgical treatment for ICH. The study was conducted in accordance with local regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki and included 1-year patient follow-ups after surgical intervention. The National Taiwan University Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 201211025RIB) granted ethical approval for the study, which waived the need for informed consent owing to the retrospective design.

Population

Inclusion criteria were unilateral ICH of > 30 mL at the basal ganglia, age > 18 years, and ICH surgery with ICP monitoring, with available BrT and HR data for > 120 h post-surgery. Exclusion criteria were previous brain diseases, substance addiction, combined traumatic injuries, infection within 72 h of admission or mortality within 5 days after ICH surgery, and patients who signed a “do-not-resuscitate” order. Patients underwent Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) assessment and cerebral computed tomography (CT) upon admission. Endoscopy-assisted removal of hematoma and intra-parenchymal fiber optic ICP monitor placement for measuring ICP and BrT were based on various factors, including GCS score, age, pupillary examination results, hematoma volume, CT findings, and neurological deterioration incidences9. An ICP monitoring device (Camino Laboratories, San Diego, CA, USA; model 110-4BT) was used. Standard ICU monitoring procedures were used, including electrocardiograms to record HR and invasive measurement of arterial blood pressure (BP). Hourly neurological evaluations were conducted, including GCS score, pupil reflex to light, and pupil size.

Postoperative care included mechanical ventilation, fluid resuscitation, oxygenation, and antiepileptic medications. Antibiotic prophylaxis was administered for a minimum of 3 days post-surgery. When the 2-hour ICP exceeded 20 mmHg within the first 3 postoperative days, head CT was conducted, followed by appropriate treatment; otherwise, CT was conducted on the third postoperative day. Unless ICP was elevated, the ICP catheter was utilized until approximately the seventh postoperative day.

Following transfer to general wards, the patients underwent rehabilitation programs. Outpatient evaluations included the mRS and survival status. The mRS measures the degree of disability in stroke patients using seven levels, where 6 represents death and 0 implies complete recovery and absence of symptoms. An mRS score of 3–6 indicated an unfavorable outcome and ≤ 2 was considered a favorable outcome after 1 year of surgery.

Brain temperature and HR rhythm

To ensure an unbiased assessment of cosinor parameters, this study only included cases with continuous data available for at least 5 days on both BrT and HR post-surgery. These data were divided into 24-hour segments to analyze circadian rhythms. Data characteristics, including waveform of oscillations, mesor of temperature oscillations (a rhythm-adjusted mean), rhythm amplitude, and acrophase (time of the day representing the highest temperature or HR), were collected. The circadian rhythm of BrT and HR was analyzed using the cosinor method, which comprises fitting data to a 24-hour cosine curve and estimating rhythm parameters10. Although physiological rhythms in critically ill patients may not follow a perfect sinusoidal pattern, cosinor analysis provides robust estimation of mesor and rhythmicity and has been widely applied in clinical circadian research. Physiological data were obtained through continuous automated monitoring in the neurocritical care unit, resulting in high data completeness. For rhythm analyses, patients with three or more consecutive missing hourly BrT or HR data points were excluded to preserve reliability of cosinor fitting; isolated missing observations were retained without imputation.

Statistical analyses

BrT and HR post-surgery data were divided into two time periods—period A (1–60 h post-surgery) and period B (61–120 h post-surgery)—and analyzed to determine the presence/absence of circadian rhythm. Univariable analysis was performed using logistic regression, whereas multivariable logistic regression with progressive selection was used to recognize independent predictors of mortality and functional outcomes. Incomplete data were excluded before analysis. The Chi-squared test was used to analyze the correlation between the presence/absence of BrT and HR rhythms. p-values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. SPSS 26 was used for statistical analysis (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Because this was a retrospective observational study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients during the study period. However, given the observed effect sizes of key predictors in the multivariable models, the study was considered to have adequate statistical power to detect clinically meaningful associations.

Results

Demographic data

Table 1 summarizes the clinical and demographic data. Overall, 85 patients whose ICP was monitored and BrT measured during the study period underwent surgery for basal ganglia hemorrhage and 74 who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were enrolled. The mean age was 60.4 years (range 18–89 years). At admission to the emergency department, the mean GCS score was 8.1 (range 3–12). One-year post-surgery, 24 patients (32.4%) had an mRS score of < 3. Seven patients (9.5%) had died.

Brain temperature patterns and rhythmicity

The mesor ± SD of BrT in period A was 37.95 ± 0.7 °C, with a temperature of 37.1–37.5 °C in 24.3% of patients, 37.6–38.0 °C in 48.6%, and 38.1–38.5 °C in 13.5%. The mesor ± SD for BrT was 37.6 ± 0.6 °C in period B, with 28.4% of patients showing a temperature of 37.1–37.5 °C, 32.4% showing 37.6–38.0 °C, and 24.3% showing 38.1–38.5 °C. The amplitude of BrT for the majority of patients in period A (77.0%) and period B (74.3%) was < 0.4 °C. The cosinor analysis revealed the presence of BrT rhythm, defined as p < 0.05, in 45 (60.8%) patients during both periods. Among the 45 patients with an intact BrT rhythm in period A, 13 (28.9%) exhibited an acrophase between 12:00 and 18:00, whereas nine (20.2%) had an acrophase between 18:00 and 24:00. Similarly, among the 45 patients with an intact BrT rhythm during period B, 18 (40.0%) exhibited an acrophase between 12:00 and 18:00, whereas seven (15.6%) had an acrophase between 18:00 and 24:00.

HR patterns and rhythmicity

The mesor ± SD for HR was 88.7 ± 11.0 beats per minute (bpm) in period A, with 16.2% of patients showing HR of 70.1–80 bpm, 40.5% showing 80.1–90 bpm, 23.0% showing 90.1–100 bpm, and 13.5% showing 100.1–110 bpm. The mesor ± SD for HR was 86.1 ± 11.6 bpm in period B, with 21.6% of patients showing an HR of 70.1–80 bpm, 35.1% showing 80.1–90 bpm, 37.8% showing 90.1–100 bpm, and 8.1% showing 100.1–110 bpm. In each period, 27 patients (36.4%) showed the presence of rhythm. Among patients with an intact HR rhythm, nine (33.3%) in period A and 10 (37.0%) in period B exhibited an acrophase between 12:00 and 18:00. An acrophase between 18:00 and 24:00 was observed in five (18.5%) and four (15.8%) patients during periods A and B, respectively.

Correlation between rhythms of brain temperature and HR

Using chi square analysis, a significant association was observed between the presence or absence of BrT and HR rhythms in period A (p = 0.024), whereas no significant association was found in period B (p = 0.564).

Two-period analysis of rhythm changes in brain temperature and HR

During the postoperative course, the presence or absence of BrT and HR rhythms changed in some patients (Table 2). In the BrT rhythm analysis, 32 patients (43.2%) exhibited a persistent presence of rhythm across both periods, whereas 16 (21.6%) showed an absence of rhythm in both. Additionally, 13 patients (17.6%) had a rhythm present in period A but absent in period B, whereas another 13 (17.6%) showed the opposite pattern. In the HR rhythm analysis, 11 patients (14.9%) exhibited a persistent presence of rhythm in both periods. Moreover, 31 patients (41.9%) had absent HR rhythms throughout both periods. Additionally, 16 patients (21.6%) showed a presence of rhythm in period A but an absence in period B, whereas another 16 (21.6%) showed the opposite pattern.

Predictive factors for long-term functional outcome and mortality

In the univariable analysis of 1-year functional outcomes assessed by mRS, initial GCS score, age, and the presence or absence of a BrT rhythm in period B were identified as potential predictors of postoperative functional outcomes (Table 3). In the multivariable analysis (Table 4), the functional outcome 1 year after ICH surgery was significantly associated with age, initial GCS score, the presence of BrT rhythm in period B, and mesor of HR in period A. In the univariable analysis of the mortality/survival status 1-year post-surgery, initial GCS score and WBC > 10 K were significant prognostic factors (Table 5), whereas initial GCS score was the only independent predictor in the multivariable analysis (Table 6).

Discussion

We investigated the circadian variability of BrT and HR in two periods within the first 5 days following ICH surgery, between which many patients displayed alterations in the rhythmic status of HR and BrT. A greater number of patients had intact biological rhythms of BrT (43.2%) than of HR (14.9%) in both periods. Our study presents the first two-period analysis of biological rhythms in patients with ICH to disclose the rhythmic variations at early and late periods and their predictive values. Because all patients underwent surgery based on established clinical indications, the circadian patterns observed in this study reflect physiological characteristics of the postoperative neurocritical care population rather than direct effects of surgical intervention itself (Fig. 1).

In this study, age, initial GCS score, presence of BrT rhythm in period B, and the mesor of HR in period A were significantly associated with 1-year functional outcomes. Our findings align with existing literature indicating that initial GCS score and age are significant predictors of mortality and survival following ICH11,12. Aysenne et al. reported that the ICH score at 24 h provides better prognostic accuracy than that at admission, likely reflecting dynamic brain changes in the acute phase and underscoring the need to select indicators at appropriate time points13.

We detected circadian rhythm of BrT in 60.8% of patients with ICH and HR in 36.5% in period A. Changes in circadian rhythm between the early (period A) and late postoperative periods (period B) were identified in 26 patients (35.1%) for BrT and 32 patients (43.2%) for HR. Circadian rhythms are regulated by the SCN in the hypothalamus, with gene oscillations driving rhythmicity14,15. Endogenous rhythms are synchronized to the 24-hour cycle by external cues such as light, temperature, food, and exercise14,16. Through neural and hormonal mechanisms, the SCN coordinates body clocks to produce rhythmic neuronal activity17. These rhythms regulate physiological processes, adaptation to environmental changes, and responses to acute pathology18,19. Illness, aging, comorbidities, and medication can disrupt rhythms via altered gene expression and melatonin secretion6,20. Surrogate markers, such as core body temperature, melatonin, blood pressure, heart rate, and cortisol, are commonly used to assess circadian status10.

Patients with ICH often develop circadian rhythm disruptions, manifesting as sleep disorders or irregular biological rhythms8,21,22. Such changes have prognostic significance in patients with stroke. In a study of 50 patients with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, diurnal blood pressure variation was lost in 44 patients (88%), and reverse dipping was observed in 29 patients (65.9% of non-dippers)23. In a study of 17 stroke patients, circadian variations in BP, neurohumoral, and coagulation factors were diminished and abnormal BP rhythm patterns were frequently observed during the acute phase24. Among 100 patients with ICH within 24 h post-surgery, 43%, 46%, and 52% lacked rhythmic changes in systolic BP, diastolic BP, and HR, with rhythmicity linked to better 90-day outcomes25. In subarachnoid hemorrhage from aneurysm rupture, ~ 90% preserved diurnal temperature rhythm, though cosinor analysis showed acrophase shift, reduced amplitude, and elevated mesor26. A case of intraventricular and subarachnoid hemorrhage involving the hypothalamus demonstrated paradoxical insomnia and circadian disruption27.

Our findings extend this knowledge by demonstrating such disruptions specifically in surgical patients with ICH. BrT is affected by cerebral blood flow, blood temperature, and metabolism and differs from systemic temperature in absolute values and circadian rhythmicity28. Postoperative BrT and HR changes may result from direct or indirect effects on the SCN29. ICH disrupts circadian gene expression30. Large hematomas or edema may raise ICP, compressing hypothalamic structures and impairing circadian regulation. Thus, disrupted BrT and HR rhythms likely reflect multiple factors, including ICP changes, altered blood flow, neurotransmitters, and inflammatory responses31,33. ICU confinement, sedation, mechanical ventilation, and limited light exposure may further impair or mask endogenous rhythmicity, contributing to attenuation of observable circadian patterns in this postoperative neurocritical care cohort rather than directly altering intrinsic circadian pacemaker function. Notably, acrophase timing showed considerable dispersion in our cohort, with only a subset of patients demonstrating peaks during the expected daytime window, while others exhibited shifts toward earlier or later phases. Similar phase variability has been described in acute cerebrovascular disorders, suggesting that circadian disruption in neurocritical illness may involve instability of temporal organization rather than a uniform directional phase shift.

Biological rhythm analysis may have therapeutic potential. Circadian oscillations are vital for homeostasis and their disruption contributes to chronic diseases. The circadian clock strongly affects immune cell differentiation and responses33. Desynchrony from shiftwork or jet lag increases risks of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and cancer20,34. The assessment of sleep in ICH has gained attention owing to its critical role in motor learning, memory consolidation, and overall recovery35. Sleep disturbances are common after ICH and have been associated with poorer rehabilitation outcomes36. As sleep is tightly regulated by the circadian system, disruptions in circadian rhythms can exacerbate sleep abnormalities. Circadian dysregulation also affects injury susceptibility, recovery, and therapeutic response in stroke32,37. Dim light impairs recovery and increases mortality38. Chronic sleep problems further raise risks of vascular and metabolic disorders39,40. Although chronotherapeutic strategies (light, feeding, medications, physiotherapy) have been proposed, their efficacy remains uncertain and warrants further study.

These findings should be interpreted as prognostic associations rather than causal effects, given the multifactorial determinants of mortality after intracerebral hemorrhage. This study has some limitations. First, although age was adjusted for in multivariable analyses, age related differences in circadian regulation and other patient factors, including comorbidities and hematoma characteristics, may not be fully captured by statistical modeling alone, and residual physiological heterogeneity across individuals could have influenced rhythmic patterns, though some older patients retain stable rhythms41. Second, the single-center design with limited cases may restrict generalizability, requiring larger studies. Nonetheless, our two-period analysis of BrT and HR rhythms provides clinical value for postoperative ICH decision-making and communication. As multiple factors can disrupt rhythmicity, further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, circadian rhythms of brain temperature and heart rate frequently change during the early postoperative period after intracerebral hemorrhage surgery. The reappearance or preservation of rhythmic patterns in the later acute phase is associated with more favourable long term functional outcomes. Baseline clinical severity, including the Glasgow Coma Scale, remains an important established predictor of prognosis. Continuous monitoring and rhythmic analysis of physiological signals may provide complementary information beyond traditional clinical indicators and help guide prognostic assessment and rehabilitation planning.
