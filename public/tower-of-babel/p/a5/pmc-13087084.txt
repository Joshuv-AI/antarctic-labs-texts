Introduction

Breath analysis has emerged as a non-invasive and promising tool for early cancer and tumor diagnosis. Recent technological advancements, particularly in gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), have fueled research efforts to identify volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and volatile sulfur compounds (VSCs) in exhaled breath as potential cancer biomarkers [1].

The human breath contains a complex mixture of metabolic byproducts, and the ability to detect these compounds with high sensitivity and specificity has led to a rapidly expanding body of research over the past two decades. This growth highlights the potential of breath analysis to improve diagnostic accuracy, monitor disease progression, and guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

While earlier studies have provided valuable descriptive overviews of breathomics, few have systematically integrated data-driven methods to interpret the evolving research landscape [2, 3]. Conventional literature analyses often rely on manual screening or keyword-based retrieval, which may overlook semantically relevant studies or emerging interdisciplinary links. To address this limitation, the present study applies an AI-enhanced evidence mapping approach that leverages large language models (LLMs) to semantically evaluate and synthesize global research on exhaled breathomics for cancer diagnosis from 2005 to early 2025.

This approach aims to provide a comprehensive overview of technological and translational progress, identify key research hotspots and collaboration networks, and highlight remaining challenges in the clinical implementation of VOC-based cancer diagnostics. By combining computational intelligence with systematic evidence synthesis, this study aims primarily to develop and validate an AI‑enhanced evidence‑mapping framework for the efficient and reproducible screening of breathomics literature. This framework not only offers a scalable approach to research synthesis but also provides new insights into how artificial intelligence can advance non‑invasive cancer detection and precision oncology.

Discussion

Growth trajectories and evolving technological context

The field of exhaled-breath research for cancer detection has expanded markedly over the past two decades. Our bibliometric and evidence-mapping analysis from 2005 to early 2025 indicates sustained growth in research outputs alongside diversification of methods and collaborative networks. This growth is underpinned by technological advances, including gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometry (PTR-MS), electronic-nose platforms, and increasingly, machine-learning pipelines that have collectively broadened the analytical toolkit for breathomics [14]. The COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) further accelerated interest in non‑invasive diagnostics, leading to a notable surge in publications that explored breath analysis for respiratory infections as well as cancer, thereby demonstrating the versatility of the approach [15].

The geographical and collaborative landscape reveals that the United States and China lead in publication volume, with the U.S. maintaining higher citation impact and H‑index scores, indicative of greater academic influence. European countries, despite fewer total publications, show higher centrality in international collaboration networks, underscoring their pivotal role in facilitating global research integration. Institutions such as Zhejiang University, Imperial College London, and the Technion–Israel Institute of Technology have emerged as key hubs, whose strategic interdisciplinary and multinational partnerships amplify their impact. The collaborative efforts of leading researchers (e.g., H. Haick and A. Amann) have been instrumental in identifying volatile cancer‑associated compounds and developing novel diagnostic tools [16, 17].

Concurrently, the integration of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly advanced data‑analysis capabilities, enabling more accurate pattern recognition and predictive modeling [18]. For instance, one study combining AI with GC‑MS reported a sensitivity of 70%, specificity of 86%, and overall accuracy of 81% for lung‑cancer detection [19]. This trend is expected to continue toward real‑time analysis and portable diagnostic devices. Keyword‑evolution analysis reflects a shift from early studies focusing on specific volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and single diseases, through a phase of methodological expansion (e.g., oxidative stress, exhaled‑breath condensate), toward more holistic, systems‑level approaches that seek to decipher complex disease mechanisms and identify comprehensive biomarker panels [20, 21].

Biological Interpretability and mechanistic gaps in VOC biomarker research

Despite the growing volume of literature, our analysis reveals persistent and critical gaps in the biological interpretability and mechanistic understanding of exhaled VOC biomarkers. Recurrent candidate compounds in high‑relevance studies include short‑chain alkanes/alkenes, aldehydes (e.g., hexanal, nonanal), ketones (e.g., acetone), alcohols, and sulfur‑containing species. Their putative origins map plausibly to lipid peroxidation and oxidative‑stress pathways (alkanes/aldehydes), altered energy metabolism (ketones), and microbiome‑host co‑metabolism (volatile sulfur compounds). However, disease specificity remains incompletely established; many signals are not unique to cancer and may reflect inflammation, comorbidities, or environmental exposures [22]. Furthermore, cross‑platform reproducibility is uneven: while GC‑MS affords compound‑level specificity, electronic‑nose or nanomaterial sensors capture composite patterns susceptible to sensor drift and matrix effects.

Strikingly, even among the most‑cited studies (Table S1), very few systematically investigate the biological origin of candidate VOCs or examine correlations with tumor stage or burden [23]. This represents a major translational barrier. The field has also developed with a pronounced disease‑specific bias, whereas research on other cancer hallmarks, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, has systematically identified pan-cancer biomarkers (e.g., COA1, COX18) with prognostic and therapeutic relevance [24, 25]. Breath analysis has shown significant potential for respiratory and gastrointestinal cancers, owing to the direct connection of these organs to the exhaled air‑stream and the gut–lung axis [22, 26]. In contrast, our bibliometric data highlight a surprising scarcity of research focused on oral‑cavity tumors, such as oral squamous‑cell carcinoma (OSCC), despite their clinical significance [27, 28]. This gap may arise from anatomical and metabolic factors (e.g., dilution/metabolization of oral‑tumor VOCs before exhalation) as well as methodological traditions that have prioritized systemic diseases. Filling this gap will require innovative sampling strategies and a deeper mechanistic interrogation of tumor‑specific volatile metabolomes. Future studies must move beyond correlative observations to employ stable‑isotope tracing, perturbation models, and harmonized multi‑platform validation in blinded multicenter cohorts. Therefore, future studies in breathomics must move beyond correlative observations and emulate the mechanistic depth seen in other oncology domains, which ranges from elucidating organelle-specific death mechanisms and conducting integrative pan-cancer biomarker discovery, to deciphering the immunometabolic circuits within the tumor microenvironment [29].

Positioning breathomics within the comparative non‑invasive diagnostic landscape

When evaluated alongside other non‑invasive diagnostic modalities, exhaled‑VOC testing occupies a distinct but complementary niche. Relative to plasma cell‑free DNA (cfDNA) profiling and urine‑based molecular assays, breath analysis is minimally invasive, potentially low‑cost, and offers rapid turnaround. However, it currently faces challenges in standardization (sampling, environmental controls), compound‑level specificity, and cross‑platform harmonization. cfDNA provides high analytical specificity and direct genomic linkage but at higher cost and with pre‑analytical constraints; urine assays balance feasibility and molecular specificity but are often disease‑site dependent. Cytology‑enhanced AI tools can deliver strong performance in anatomically accessible sites but require specialized collection and labeling pipelines.

In this context, breathomics appears most promising as a complementary triage and longitudinal‑monitoring modality, especially where repeated sampling is needed and low invasiveness is paramount. A comparative matrix summarizing invasiveness, specificity, maturity of standards, turnaround time, and scalability is provided in Supplementary Figure S2. To realize this potential, the field must address the standardization and mechanistic gaps outlined above, thereby strengthening the biological plausibility and clinical interpretability of VOC signatures.

Limitations, future perspectives, and conclusion

Our study has several limitations. First, as a bibliometric and evidence‑mapping synthesis, it integrates published evidence at the corpus level; all interpretations are association‑based and hypothesis‑generating, not causal. We do not evaluate patient‑level diagnostic performance or clinical utility, which require prospective validation beyond the scope of this work. Second, the AI‑assisted screening framework, while rigorously validated, inherits limitations of the underlying commercial LLMs (Tongyi Qianwen, Hunyuan, Yi). Their proprietary architectures limit full algorithmic transparency, and model‑specific decision patterns can introduce bias. We mitigated these risks by employing deterministic decoding (temperature = 0.0, top_p = 0.1), multi‑model consensus, expert adjudication on a stratified sample, and threshold‑sensitivity analyses, which showed stable macro‑level patterns. Nevertheless, future work could benefit from domain‑tuned models, standardized reporting checklists, and larger adjudication sets to further enhance reproducibility and generalizability.

Looking forward, we identify two parallel research trajectories. For the breathomics field itself, priorities should include: (i) mechanistic studies to elucidate the origin and disease‑specificity of VOC biomarkers; (ii) harmonized, multi‑center validation across analytical platforms; and (iii) targeted exploration of under‑studied cancer types, such as oral‑cavity tumors. For the methodology of evidence synthesis, future developments may incorporate domain‑adapted LLMs, cross‑modal fusion (e.g., linking breath‑VOC patterns to histopathology or other omics data), and federated learning pipelines to accelerate reproducible, multicenter research.

In conclusion, this 20‑year bibliometric analysis, enabled by a novel multi‑LLM consensus framework, documents the dynamic evolution of exhaled‑breath research for cancer diagnosis. The field exhibits sustained growth, technological diversification, and expanding global collaboration. At the same time, our findings highlight critical translational gaps—particularly in biological plausibility, disease‑specificity, and cross‑platform standardization—that must be addressed to advance breathomics from a promising research tool toward a robust, clinically viable component of the non‑invasive diagnostic arsenal.

Conclusion

This 20-year bibliometric analysis underscores the dynamic evolution of exhaled breath detection research for cancer diagnosis. The findings reveal a rapidly growing field driven by technological advancements, interdisciplinary collaboration, and a shift towards integrating computational methods with traditional analytical techniques. The study highlights the United States and China as dominant contributors, with European countries playing a crucial role in international collaborations.

Future research should prioritize the integration of machine learning and AI to enhance diagnostic accuracy and develop real-time analytical capabilities. Emphasis should also be placed on fostering global collaborations and leveraging multidisciplinary approaches to advance the clinical application of breathomics. As the field continues to evolve, these strategies will be instrumental in realizing the full potential of exhaled breath analysis as a non-invasive, cost-effective tool for early cancer detection.

Beyond literature screening, LLMs can support standardized interpretation of cytological and histological datasets by (i) enforcing reporting checklists (structured diagnostic synopses with controlled vocabularies), (ii) aligning pathology narratives with quantitative image features from computational pathology (linking free text to nuclei morphology, gland architecture, or tumor–stroma interactions), and (iii) enabling cross-modal fusion that relates breath-VOC signatures to cytology/histology patterns and ancillary omics (e.g., cfDNA, proteomics). Coupled with weakly supervised learning and federated pipelines, these approaches may accelerate multicenter reproducibility and generate testable mechanistic hypotheses (e.g., VOCs associated with specific histopathological phenotypes).

By maintaining a focus on innovation and collaboration, researchers can drive significant advancements in this field, ultimately contributing to improved cancer diagnostics and better patient outcomes. The continued exploration of emerging trends and technologies will ensure that breathomics remains at the forefront of non-invasive diagnostic research, paving the way for groundbreaking developments in precision medicine.

Methods

Data collection

This study performed a systematic analysis of publications spanning from January 1, 2005, to March 20 at 09:00 Beijing Time (01:00 UTC) on March 20, 2025, to capture the evolving landscape of breath detection research for cancer and tumor diagnosis. Data were extracted from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database, a widely recognized and comprehensive resource for bibliometric analyses (Fig. 6).

PRISMA-style flowchart of literature identification and selection. After exclusions, 2,625 records remained (2,083 ART; 542 REV). ART were de-duplicated to 2,007; the combined corpus (2,549) underwent LLM scoring, yielding 808 core publications (31.7%). Partial-year 2025 counts were excluded from annual trends

A carefully designed search strategy was employed using the following combination of keywords and terms within different matching scopes: (1) Topic Matching Scope (includes Title, Abstract, Author Keywords, and Keywords Plus): (Exhaled-breath OR Breathomic OR Exhale OR “Breath-analysis” OR “Breath-sample” OR “Breath Test” OR “Breath Biomarker*” OR “Breath Profile*”)* (Cancer OR Neoplas* OR Tumor* OR Oncolog* OR Carcinoma*)*; (2) Topic Matching Scope: (VOC OR VSC* OR “Volatile Organic Compound*” OR GC-MS OR “Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry” OR “E-nose” OR “Electronic Nose” OR PTR-* OR “Proton Transfer Reaction” OR “Volatile-metabolite*” OR Biomarker* OR Diagnos* OR Detection OR Identification OR Monitoring OR “Clinical-trial*” OR Predict* OR “Early Screening” OR “Risk Assessment” OR “Disease Progression” OR “Metabolic Profiling” OR “Biochemical Marker*” OR “Breathomics” OR “Non-invasive Test*”)*; (3) All Fields Matching Scope: The broader keyword-based search incorporated relevant synonyms and terms to capture as many related records as possible, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the field.

Of the initial search results, all proceedings papers, early access articles, book chapters, and retracted publications were excluded, yielding a final corpus of 2,625 relevant records. These comprised 2,083 original research articles (ART) and 542 review articles (REV), all of which were peer-reviewed. The deduplication process via reference management tools removed 76 redundant records, yielding 2,007 distinct ART entries. Subsequently, an AI-driven filtering process was applied to assess topic relevance, ultimately retaining 808 core publications (31.7% of the deduplicated corpus).

To address the inherent trade-off between recall and precision, we deliberately adopted a broad initial retrieval strategy to minimize selection bias and maximize recall. This approach, while inclusive, inevitably introduced a substantial number of irrelevant records. To subsequently enhance precision, we applied an AI-assisted semantic screening workflow using large language models to score each record’s relevance based on titles, abstracts, and keywords. By applying a calibrated relevance threshold, the models automatically filtered out clearly irrelevant entries while retaining records with marginal but potential value. This two-step approach balances recall and precision, substantially reduces manual burden, and minimizes subjective bias in the screening process.

The final dataset, consisting exclusively of high-quality peer-reviewed publications, was selected to ensure a rigorous bibliometric analysis and to provide reliable insights into trends, contributions, and collaborations in the field of exhaled breath analysis for cancer detection.

Although the search spanned publications up to 09:00 Beijing Time (01:00 UTC) on March 20, 2025, annual publication trends excluded partial data for 2025 to ensure consistency and avoid skewed interpretations. This systematic approach enabled a comprehensive analysis of publication trends, key contributors, and collaborative networks within this rapidly advancing field.

Overview of LLM architectures and decision mechanisms used in this study

To streamline the identification of key studies related to breath analysis for cancer diagnosis, we employed a relevance evaluation system guided by large language models (LLMs): Tongyi Qianwen (qwen-plus), Hunyuan (hunyuan-pro), and Yi (yi-large-turbo). The selection was based on three considerations: (1) Originating from different developer teams and trained on diverse pre-training corpora helps mitigate the knowledge blind spots inherent in any single model, thereby improving information completeness, particularly on specialized or marginal topics. (2) Features high compatibility with the OpenAI SDK format for seamless integration. (3) Adjusting parameters such as temperature and top_p enables near-deterministic outputs, which constitutes the prerequisite for the reproducibility of this framework. Using the provided prompts, experiments can be fully replicated via each model’s corresponding API endpoints. (4) Offers highly cost-effective pricing.

A carefully designed prompt was used to guide the models in evaluating the relevance of titles, keywords, and abstracts to the research topic. The prompts directed the models to analyze each document’s alignment with the core themes of “exhaled breath” and “cancer diagnosis” and to assign an overall relevance score based on these analyses.

In natural language processing (NLP) models, temperature and top_p are two key parameters used to control the randomness and diversity of generated text. These parameters adjust the distribution of the output, balancing between diversity and determinism. For this study, we set the parameters to temperature = 0.0 and top_p = 0.1, ensuring highly deterministic results with minimal randomness. Specifically, setting the temperature to 0.0 eliminates randomness, prompting the model to consistently generate fixed and predictable responses. Additionally, the top_p value of 0.1 constrains the model to consider only the top 10% of cumulative probability mass for candidate words, further reducing variability and enhancing the determinism of the generated outputs. This approach ensures consistency and reproducibility in the relevance scoring process across all evaluated documents [30].

This systematic approach leverages the unique strengths of each LLM. Hunyuan, optimized for complex reasoning and domain-specific tasks, tended to assign higher scores, reflecting its broader inclusivity. Qianwen excelled in detailed analysis of technical content, while Yi offered cost-effective yet precise general assessments. This multi-model strategy ensures a robust and reproducible literature selection process, facilitating comprehensive bibliometric analysis.

Literature relevance assessment

The scoring process entailed evaluating the alignment of titles, keywords, and abstracts with the research topic. The scores from the three components were aggregated into a single model-specific score for each publication using a fixed weighting scheme: Title (30%), Keywords (20%), and Abstract (50%). Titles were assessed for explicit mentions of key terms such as “breath analysis” and “cancer diagnosis.” Keywords were evaluated for their technical and contextual fit, including terms like VOCs, GC-MS, and biomarkers. Abstracts were analyzed for depth and connection to the topic, focusing on methodological details and clinical applications. Based on these assessments, each model provided a relevance score ranging from 0 to 10 (detailed in Table S2). The final score for each document was then aggregated and averaged across the three models. Documents with an average score of 6 or higher were classified as “highly relevant,” while those below 6 were deemed “low relevance”. All models used deterministic decoding (temperature = 0.0; top_p = 0.1). Core prompts and the scoring rubric are provided in Table S2 to support reproducibility. A stratified random sample (n = 100) was double-labeled by two domain experts with adjudicated consensus; validation metrics (Cohen’s κ, precision, recall, F1) are reported in Fig. 1c and Table S3. The prompting strategy, scoring rubric, and ensemble voting mechanism proposed in this study are model-agnostic, thereby endowing the framework with strong transferability and adaptability to other emerging LLMs in the future.

What’s new?

We present a domain-specific, multi-LLM consensus framework (Tongyi Qianwen, Hunyuan, Yi) for evidence mapping in exhaled-breathomics. Using deterministic decoding, an explicit 0–10 relevance rubric, and threshold-sensitivity analysis, we achieve robust agreement with expert labels on a stratified validation set, and deliver a 20-year map of technologies, themes, and collaborations, balancing recall and precision while maintaining reproducibility.

Statistical analysis and visual analysis

The collected literature was analyzed using VOSviewer 1.6.18 [31], Citespace 6.1.6 [3], Gephi, Bibliometrix (the R package) to evaluate publication trends, key authors, research institutions, countries, study topics, methodologies, and citation metrics. Through these tools, we generated visual representations to explore the current state, research hotspots, and emerging trends in breath testing for cancer and tumor diagnostics. This integrative approach provides a comprehensive view of the field’s development and highlights its dynamic evolution over time.
