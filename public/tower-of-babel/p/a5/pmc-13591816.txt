Clinical characteristics and an admission-time predictive model for severe scrub typhus and secondary HLH in adults
Authors: Changqi Guo, Xin Zhang, Chonghuan Li, Yibin Huang, Jiawei Su, Yijing Wang, Chao Zhuo, Ling Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScrub typhus can rapidly progress to severe disease with multi-organ dysfunction. Secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is an increasingly recognized hyperinflammatory complication associated with high mortality, but adult data and simple admission-time risk tools remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of hospitalized adults with scrub typhus at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (May 2013–July 2025). Severe scrub typhus was defined by major organ involvement, shock, or in-hospital death. Variables available at admission were compared between severe and non-severe groups. Independent predictors were identified using multivariable logistic regression, and model performance was evaluated using ROC analysis. Among severe cases, HLH was identified according to standard diagnostic criteria, and clinical characteristics were compared between HLH and non-HLH patients.ResultsAmong 135 patients, 44 (32.6%) developed severe scrub typhus and overall in-hospital mortality was 2.2% (3/135). Lower hemoglobin (Hb) (OR 0.965, 95% CI 0.947–0.982), higher serum creatinine (SCr) (OR 1.019, 95% CI 1.008–1.029), and lower fibrinogen (FIB) (OR 0.629, 95% CI 0.435–0.909) were independent predictors of severe disease. A parsimonious three-variable model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.843). At the sensitivity-prioritized ROC threshold, sensitivity was 88%, specificity 46%, and the Youden index 0.34. HLH was identified in 6 patients overall, including 5 patients among the severe cases. Among severe patients, HLH cases showed higher observed in-hospital mortality than non-HLH cases (40.0% vs. 2.6%). They also had more severe thrombocytopenia and greater coagulation and organ-function abnormalities.ConclusionsA simple admission-time triad (Hb, SCr, and FIB) provides an interpretable tool for early identification of severe scrub typhus in hospitalized adults. HLH cases showed higher observed mortality and more severe thrombocytopenia/coagulation derangement; however, these findings should be interpreted descriptively due to the small number of cases.

Introduction

Scrub typhus(ST), caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, is a neglected tropical rickettsial disease transmitted through the bite of infected larval trombiculid mites (“chiggers”). It remains endemic throughout the Asia–Pacific region and represents a major cause of acute undifferentiated febrile illness in endemic communities. The clinical spectrum ranges from mild self-limiting fever to rapidly progressive multi-organ failure.

Mild cases typically respond well to doxycycline or azithromycin, whereas severe infections can lead to pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute kidney injury (AKI), encephalitis, myocarditis, shock, or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Reported mortality in severe scrub typhus ranges from 10% to 30%, underscoring the disease’s potential lethality [1].

In recent years, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) has been increasingly recognized as a life-threatening immunopathologic complication of scrub typhus [2–4]. HLH is characterized by uncontrolled macrophage activation and a cytokine storm that result in cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, and progressive organ failure [5–7]. Emerging evidence indicates that HLH may occur early during hospitalization rather than as a late sequela, representing a key inflection point in the continuum of immune dysregulation underlying severe scrub typhus [2, 8, 9].

Given this marked clinical heterogeneity, there is a pressing need for early, admission-time stratification to identify patients at high risk for deterioration—facilitating timely monitoring, organ-supportive interventions, and, when appropriate, immunomodulatory therapy.

In response, multiple research groups have sought to develop predictive models or key clinical indicators for severe scrub typhus using routine bedside and laboratory parameters. Early studies proposed pragmatic risk-scoring systems based on clinical and biochemical features such as elevated body temperature and pulse rate, pulmonary crepitations, lower lymphocyte percentage, elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST), hypoalbuminemia, increased serum creatinine (SCr), and urine albumin positivity [10–16]. These models provided a feasible framework for bedside risk forecasting.

Subsequent cohort studies demonstrated that predictors of severity may vary across age groups. In comparative pediatric and elderly cohorts, severe disease was associated with hematologic and hepatorenal dysfunction: in children, decreased hemoglobin (Hb) and platelet (PLT) count with elevated bilirubin and occasionally alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were key indicators, whereas in older adults, elevated bilirubin, decreased platelet and hemoglobin levels, and increased creatinine were more strongly correlated with severity [12].

More recent pediatric-focused modeling has expanded the biomarker spectrum to include tissue injury and cardiac–renal surrogates. A 2025 pediatric nomogram study identified six admission-time predictors—Hb, PLT, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB), and total protein—demonstrating robust discrimination in both training and validation cohorts [14]. In parallel, a 2025 machine-learning study utilizing objective admission variables highlighted BUN, age, eosinophil count (EOC), PLT, neutrophil count, and SCr as major predictors of progression. However, coagulation indices prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), previously suggested as potential markers, were excluded due to data incompleteness, reflecting persistent limitations of real-world clinical datasets [11].

Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. Many predictive tools have been optimized for specific populations (children, elderly, or regional cohorts) and may not generalize to broader hospitalized populations. Furthermore, while existing models often define “severity” in terms of organ failure or ICU admission, few explicitly address hyperinflammatory phenotypes such as secondary HLH, which carry disproportionately high mortality though underdiagnosed. Because HLH can closely mimic sepsis while rapidly evolving toward shock, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction, and coagulopathy, its early recognition within scrub typhus cohorts is clinically crucial [5, 6, 17].

Against this backdrop, the present study was designed to develop a clinically applicable, admission-time framework serving two interlinked objectives: (1) Early prediction of severe scrub typhus using readily available clinical and laboratory variables; and (2) Characterization of the HLH phenotype within severe scrub typhus, identifying admission-time markers that may signal this high-risk subgroup.

By integrating general severity prediction with HLH-focused risk profiling, and by relying solely on routinely obtainable hospital variables, this work aims to address a critical unmet need—bridging the gap between existing severity models that lack immunologic granularity and those requiring specialized biomarkers unavailable in most clinical settings.

Methods

Study design and population

A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University from May 2013 to July 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) in accordance with one of three diagnostic criteria [18] based on Expert consensus on the clinical diagnosis and treatment of scrub typhus (2024) issued by the Chinese Society of Microbiology Committee on Zoonotic Pathogens; Biodiagnostic Technology Branch of China Medical Biotechnology Association: 1) patients who had a history of field activities in an epidemic region/ season within 3 weeks prior to illness onset and presented sudden high fever with characteristic eschar or ulcer, rash, lymphadenopathy and other typical clinical manifestations can be clinically diagnosed ST.2) At least one laboratory test is positive: specific IgM antibody positive or double serum antibody titer increased by more than 4 times (Weil-Felix test) or pathogen detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [19, 20]. 3) ST was highly suspected but could not be diagnosed, and fever was reduced within 48 h after diagnostic treatment with azithromycin or doxycycline. Patients were excluded if alternative diagnoses for other endemic infections with overlapping clinical presentations (e.g., dengue fever, hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome, and leptospirosis) were established based on compatible epidemiology, clinical features, and confirmatory laboratory tests; (3) sufficient clinical and laboratory data to determine diagnosis, disease severity, and key model variables. Exclusion criteria included: severe comorbidities causing organ failure independent of scrub typhus, and patients transferred or discharged within 24 h of admission.

Diagnostic criteria and grouping

Severe scrub typhus was defined by at least one major organ involvement or critical condition [13]: (1) Central nervous system: altered consciousness, seizure, cerebral hemorrhage/infarction; (2) Respiratory: bilateral pulmonary infiltrates with PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 250 mmHg, respiratory rate ≥ 30 /min, or mechanical ventilation; (3) Cardiac: myocarditis, ischemia, or new severe arrhythmia; (4) Renal: Scr ≥ 176 µmol/L; (5) Shock: systolic BP < 90 mmHg or ≥ 40 mmHg drop from baseline after excluding other causes; (6) Gastrointestinal bleeding without pre-existing ulcer; or Death during hospitalization. Cases not meeting these criteria were considered non-severe.

HLH was diagnosed based on the HScore [21], a validated tool to assess the probability of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH) in adult patients. The HScore comprises nine parameters (points in the parenthesis): known underlying immunosuppression (18); high temperature: 38.4–39.4 °C (33), ≥ 39.4 °C (49); organomegaly: hepatomegaly or splenomegaly (23), hepatomegaly and splenomegaly (38); number of cytopenia: two lineages (24), three lineages (34); ferritin (FER): 2000–6000 ng/mL (35), > 6000 ng/mL (50); triglyceride (TG): 132–354 mg/dL (44), > 354 mg/dL (64); fibrinogen (FIB) ≤ 2.5 gm/L (30); and AST ≥ 30 U/L (19); and hemophagocytosis in the bone marrow (35). The score ranges from 0 to 264, with an HScore of ≤ 100 and ≥ 241, indicating a probability of sHLH of ≤ 1 and ≥ 99%, respectively. We defined HLH as an HScore of 200 or above, corresponding to a probability of 88% or higher for HLH, instead of a widely used cutoff of 169, corresponding to a probability of about 54%. Additionally, we applied the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria to all patients, which include eight parameters: fever, splenomegaly, cytopenia, high FER, high TG or low FIB, hemophagocytosis detected in bone marrow, spleen, or lymph nodes, reduced natural killer cell activity, and elevated soluble interleukin-2 (IL-2) receptor (also soluble cluster of differentiation 25 (CD25)). At least 5 of 8 parameters are required to fulfill the criteria.

Data collection and variable definitions

Data were extracted from the hospital electronic records, including: (1) Demographics: age, sex; (2) Clinical symptoms: fever, cough, headache, vomiting, abdominal/back pain, outdoor exposure, fever duration; (3) Comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease, hematologic disorder, malignancy, long-term steroid/immunosuppressant use; (4) Physical signs: eschar/ulcer, lymphadenopathy, edema, hepatomegaly; (5) Complications: pneumonia, hepatic injury, hypoalbuminemia, electrolyte disturbance, renal impairment, meningitis/encephalitis, coagulopathy, gastrointestinal bleeding, serous effusion, myocarditis, HLH, thrombosis, shock, MODS, and death ; (6) Laboratory indices: white blood cell (WBC), EOC, Hb, PLT, CRP, procalcitonin (PCT), ALT, AST, total bilirubin (TBil), SCr, BUN, uric acid (UA), creatine kinase (CK), LDH, PT, APTT, FIB, Weil–Felix reaction, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) results; (7) Treatment: days from onset to doxycycline/azithromycin, use of corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG); (8) Outcome: discharge status and mortality.

Operational definitions followed standard criteria: pneumonia = fever/respiratory symptoms + new infiltrate on chest X-ray/CT; hepatic injury = ALT or AST > upper limit of normal (ULN); hypoalbuminemia = albumin < 30 g/L; renal impairment = SCr rise and/or oliguria; electrolyte disturbance = Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺ out of range; coagulopathy = PT/APTT prolongation; shock = requirement of vasoactive agents. Thrombotic events (deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), stroke, peripheral occlusion) were imaging-confirmed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 and R software version 4.5.0. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent-samples t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median [interquartile range, IQR] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) and analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis and considered clinically relevant were entered into multivariable binary logistic regression using the forward likelihood ratio (forward LR) method to identify independent predictors of severe disease. The Box–Tidwell test was used to assess the linearity assumption between each continuous variable and the logit-transformed outcome. In addition, locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) curves were used to visually evaluate the linear relationship between continuous variables and logit(P). For variables with missing observations, analyses were performed using available cases, and the number of valid observations was reported where applicable.

Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Internal validation was conducted using two complementary approaches. First, the cohort was randomly split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio to evaluate model discrimination and potential performance decline. Second, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions was performed to assess optimism-corrected calibration. Model discrimination was evaluated by AUC, and calibration was assessed using calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and mean absolute error. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots. Clinical utility and net benefit were evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA), with severe scrub typhus defined as the outcome event. Net benefit was calculated across a threshold probability range of 0.10–0.80. The prediction model was compared with two reference strategies: (1) the treat-all strategy, in which all patients were assumed to receive intervention, and (2) the treat-none strategy, in which no patients were assumed to receive intervention.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The P values from univariate analyses were considered exploratory, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. In the multivariable logistic regression model, only variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion, and the final model underwent a series of diagnostic assessments, including evaluation of multicollinearity, the linearity assumption, and calibration. Therefore, the absence of multiple-comparison correction was not expected to affect the robustness of the main conclusions.

Results

General clinical characteristics

A total of 135 adult patients with confirmed scrub typhus were enrolled. There were 72 men (53.3%), with a mean age of 59 ± 13.5 years. Fever was present in 129 cases (95.6%), cough in 70 (51.9%), and headache in 42 (31.1%). Eschar or ulceration was identified in 105 patients (77.8%). The mean duration of fever before admission was 10 ± 10.3 days and peak temperature 39.1 ± 1.2℃.

Major complications included pneumonia (47.4%), hepatic dysfunction (83.7%), renal impairment (37.0%), fecal occult blood positivity (20.7%), electrolyte imbalance (76.3%), HLH (4.4%), and MODS (12.6%). Overall, in-hospital mortality was 2.2% (3 deaths).

Comparison between non-severe and severe groups

According to severity criteria, 91 patients (67.4%) were classified as non-severe and 44 (32.6%) as severe.

Severe cases exhibited a higher prevalence of metabolic and systemic comorbidities than non-severe patients, including diabetes mellitus (20.5% vs. 5.5%, P = 0.008), whereas there were no significant differences between the two groups in the prevalence of hypertension, fatty liver disease, or primary immunosuppression.

With regard to complications, severe cases demonstrated markedly higher incidences of pneumonia (75.0% vs. 34.1%, P < 0.001), renal impairment (59.1% vs. 26.3%, P < 0.001), fecal occult blood positivity (50.0% vs. 6.7%, P < 0.001), hypoalbuminemia (70.5% vs. 47.3%, P = 0.011), serous effusion (56.8% vs. 22.2%, P < 0.001), HLH (11.4% vs. 1.1%, P = 0.023), MODS (38.6% vs. 0%, P < 0.001), and shock (31.8% vs. 0%, P < 0.001). Mortality occurred exclusively in the severe group (6.8% vs. 0%, P = 0.033).

In terms of laboratory findings, severe patients exhibited significantly elevated leukocyte counts [13.15 (9.65) ×10⁹/L vs. 8.30 (5.75) ×10⁹/L; P < 0.001], PCT levels [2.22 (4.17) ng/mL vs. 0.62 (1.21) ng/mL; P < 0.001], and biochemical indicators of tissue injury, including TBil, SCr, BUN, and LDH (all P < 0.001). Conversely, Hb [78.5 (40.5) g/L vs. 116.0 (27.0) g/L; P < 0.001], PLT [67.5 (96.25) ×10⁹/L vs. 125 (98) ×10⁹/L; P < 0.001], and FIB levels [2.32 (2.10) g/L vs. 3.34 (1.50) g/L; P < 0.001] were significantly reduced, while PT was notably prolonged (15.95 s vs. 14.20 s; P < 0.001).(Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 1).

Predictive model for severe scrub typhus

Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis included age, headache, peak body temperature, diabetes, edema, pneumonia, hypoalbuminemia, coagulopathy, renal impairment, fecal OB test, serous effusion, thromboembolic events, MODS, shock, mortality, WBC, EOC, Hb, PLT, PCT, γ-glutamyl transferase(GGT), TBil, SCr, BUN, UA, LDH, FIB, and PT. All continuous variables were entered into the analysis in their original continuous form. To minimize multicollinearity, exclude redundant indicators, and account for clinical relevance, age, Hb, SCr, FIB, PLT, PCT, and TBil were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Ultimately, three independent predictors of severe scrub typhus were identified: Hb (OR = 0.965, 95% CI 0.947–0.982, P < 0.001), SCr (OR = 1.019, 95% CI 1.008–1.029, P < 0.001), and FIB (OR = 0.629, 95% CI 0.435–0.909, P = 0.014).

The logit-linearity assumption was further assessed for Hb, SCr, and FIB, and potential collinearity among predictors was evaluated. Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis showed that all VIF values were < 2 (Hb: 1.08, SCr: 1.01, FIB: 1.08), indicating no significant multicollinearity. The Box–Tidwell test demonstrated that Hb and SCr satisfied the linearity assumption with the logit-transformed outcome (P > 0.05), whereas FIB did not (P < 0.05). LOESS smoothing curves yielded consistent results (Fig. 2).

In the multivariable regression model, the equation was: Logit(P) = 2.235 + (0.019 × SCr) − (0.036 × Hb) − (0.464 × FIB); where P represents the probability of severe scrub typhus (Fig. 3). ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.843, indicating good discriminative ability (Fig. 4). Clinically, every 10 g/L decrease in Hb was associated with a 43% increase in the risk of severe scrub typhus, whereas every 10 µmol/L increase in SCr corresponded to a 21% increase in severe disease risk.

To evaluate the potential impact of circular reasoning, “renal-only severe cases” were defined as patients meeting the criteria for renal involvement without concomitant septic shock, respiratory failure, impaired consciousness, or death. According to this definition, 3 patients (6.8% of severe cases) were classified as renal-only severe cases. After excluding these patients, logistic regression and ROC analyses were repeated to assess the predictive performance of the Hb–SCr–FIB model for non-renal severe outcomes. SCr remained an independent predictor (OR = 1.017, 95% CI 1.007–1.031, P = 0.004), and ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.833 (95% CI 0.751–0.915), which was comparable to the primary analysis (AUC = 0.841). These findings suggest that the impact of circular reasoning was minimal and that the predictive value of SCr was genuine rather than driven by overlap with the outcome definition (Fig. 5).

The optimal cut-off probability of 0.64 yielded a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 95%. However, for triage purposes, sensitivity was prioritized over specificity. Based on the ROC curve, an alternative sensitivity-oriented cut-off was selected, achieving a sensitivity of 88%, specificity of 46%, and Youden index of 0.34. This threshold identified 88% of severe cases with a missed diagnosis rate of only 12%, making it suitable for rapid screening in emergency departments or fever clinics. Although specificity was relatively low (46%), false-positive cases could be further differentiated through subsequent clinical evaluation.

In the 7:3 train–test split validation, the model achieved an AUC of 0.858 in the training set and 0.833 in the testing set, with a performance decline of 0.025, suggesting limited overfitting. Bootstrap-corrected calibration showed close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with a mean absolute error of 0.034. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test also indicated acceptable model fit (χ² = 10.39, P = 0.239) (Fig. 6).

HLH vs. non-HLH in severe scrub typhus

Among 44 patients with severe scrub typhus, 5 (11.4%) fulfilled diagnostic criteria for HLH. In-hospital mortality was higher in the HLH subgroup than in patients without HLH (40.0% vs. 2.6%, P = 0.029).

Compared with non-HLH severe cases, patients with HLH had lower systolic blood pressure and more severe thrombocytopenia (95 [43] vs. 111 [34] mmHg, P = 0.035; 21 [22.5] vs. 73 [95.5] ×10⁹/L, P < 0.001). The HLH subgroup also showed higher total bilirubin, BUN, and D-dimer levels (117.6 [417.2] vs. 18.1 [28.5] µmol/L, P = 0.013; 24.69 [26.3] vs. 13.3 [19.4] mmol/L, P = 0.016; 8.81 [2.89] vs. 5.44 [6.13] µg/mL, P = 0.035) (Table 3). Detailed clinical characteristics and diagnostic data of the six patients with scrub typhus-associated HLH are also presented (Table 4).

Thromboembolic events were observed in 7 patients (15.9%), and overt disseminated intravascular coagulation (ISTH score ≥ 5) was present in 15 patients (34.1%). Collectively, these patterns indicate that HLH is accompanied by profound cytopenia and deranged coagulation.

Discussion

This study identified low Hb, elevated SCr, and lower FIB as independent predictors of severe scrub typhus, forming a practical model with AUC = 0.843. Unlike complex multivariable models, this triad uses routine indicators easily available in most hospitals. Unlike previous models primarily developed for pediatric or regional cohorts, this model is tailored for adult populations and features a simplified variable structure. And it provides practical advantages in clinical settings, including rapid evaluation, low cost, and feasibility for bedside application.

Decreased Hb in scrub typhus reflects more than nutritional anemia—it signals systemic inflammation, bone-marrow suppression, endothelial injury, and, in some cases, hemophagocytosis or consumptive coagulopathy. Lower Hb therefore mirrors the intensity of immune activation and vascular damage. Similar findings were reported by Guan et al. [12] and Luo et al. [14], who both incorporated Hb into predictive nomograms for severe disease in children and elderly patients. SCr elevation indicates renal involvement and systemic endothelial dysfunction. O. tsutsugamushi infection triggers monocyte-derived cytokines (notably TNF-α) that activate distal endothelium [22, 23]. The renal glomerulus, with its dense capillary network, is highly susceptible to such “inflammation–endothelium–coagulation axis” injuryThis study excluded the possibility that the significance of SCr was overestimated due to circular reasoning, confirming SCr as an independent predictor of severe scrub typhus. Each 10 µmol/L rise in SCr increased severe-disease risk by ≈ 21%.

Although FIB was retained in the parsimonious prediction model, the Box–Tidwell test and LOESS curve suggested a nonlinear relationship between FIB and the logit of severe scrub typhus. Therefore, the OR for FIB should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as a strictly linear effect across the entire range of FIB values. In a sensitivity analysis in which FIB was categorized into tertiles, the coefficients of SCr and Hb remained essentially unchanged, supporting the robustness of the main findings. Larger studies are warranted to model the nonlinear effect of FIB using flexible approaches such as restricted cubic splines.

FIB exhibits a nonlinear association with the risk of severe scrub typhus, which is clinically reasonable (Box-Tidwell test, P < 0.05; an inverted U-shaped trend observed in LOESS smoothing curve). As an acute-phase reactant, mildly and moderately elevated FIB indicates inflammatory activation, while excessively increased or decreased levels may suggest disseminated intravascular coagulation or consumptive coagulopathy. Hence, the odds ratio of FIB should be interpreted cautiously. Restricted cubic spline can be adopted in further studies to fit its nonlinear effect.

Together, these predictors integrate hematologic oxygen transport, immune-endothelial response, and organ damage into a biologically coherent framework for risk stratification.

In our cohort, HLH developed in 11.4% of severe scrub typhus cases and was associated with a disproportionately high mortality (40%), consistent with a severe immune-dysregulation phenotype. This observation aligns with prior reviews [2, 3, 24] describing scrub-typhus–associated HLH as infrequent but often life-threatening.

In the present study, HLH cases were characterized by severe thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, azotemia, and markedly elevated LDH—laboratory patterns compatible with extensive tissue injury and macrophage activation. Notably, Hb and SCr did not differ significantly between HLH and non-HLH patients. This may indicate that, while Hb and SCr capture overall severity, HLH reflects an additional layer of immune activation beyond organ dysfunction alone.

Prior studies in rickettsial infections suggest that serum FER may be a useful biomarker to support the recognition of HLH. In prior studies, a serum FER concentration > 2,000 ng/mL has shown approximately 90% diagnostic sensitivity, while levels > 6,000 ng/mL increased specificity to 88% and yielded a negative predictive value of 97%, substantially outperforming traditional markers such as hypertriglyceridemia (specificity 52%) and hypofibrinogenemia (specificity 65%) [25]. In addition, FER kinetics may carry prognostic information; a smaller early decline (e.g., < 50% within 7 days) has been associated with higher mortality risk in prior work [26].

In our cohort, FER was missing for a substantial proportion of patients and therefore was not included in the statistical analyses. Nonetheless, based on existing evidence, serial monitoring of serum FER should be considered a pivotal diagnostic and prognostic tool for the early recognition of HLH in patients with severe scrub typhus, even before the complete diagnostic criteria are fulfilled [7]. Early detection of FER elevation may prompt timely immunomodulatory therapy, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Severe scrub typhus was associated with significant coagulation abnormalities characterized by reduced FIB levels and prolonged PT, consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Notably, thrombotic events (≈ 16%) co-existed with gastrointestinal bleeding manifestations (fecal occult blood positivity ≈ 50%), revealing a paradoxical state of concurrent hyper- and hypocoagulability. This dual phenomenon likely reflects immune-mediated endothelial dysfunction and microangiopathy, in which inflammatory cytokines and monocyte-derived tissue factor (TF) expression trigger localized microthrombosis, followed by consumptive coagulopathy and secondary bleeding. Consistent with Tantibhedhyangkul et al. [23] excessive secretion of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and other proinflammatory mediators may induce endothelial TF expression, thereby promoting intravascular thrombosis and depletion of coagulation factors. This explanation should be interpreted as a literature-based hypothesis rather than a conclusion directly supported by the data of the present study.

This endothelial-centric inflammatory process is not limited to the systemic vasculature but extends to the central nervous system. Kim et al. [27] reported cases of scrub-typhus–associated polyneuropathy and cerebral infarction, demonstrating that Orientia tsutsugamushi infection can precipitate cerebral vasculitis and occlusive microangiopathy. Histopathologic and imaging findings in that study revealed small-vessel inflammation, perivascular edema, and thrombotic occlusion, supporting the notion that direct endothelial invasion and immune-mediated vasculitis underlie neurological complications. These observations align with our findings of a systemic prothrombotic and hemorrhagic imbalance, further confirming that scrub typhus can induce diffuse endothelial injury across multiple organ systems, from cerebral microcirculation to systemic vasculature.

Large-scale epidemiologic evidence also supports this vascular paradigm. In a nationwide Taiwanese cohort, Chung et al. [28] demonstrated that scrub typhus increased the risk of developing acute coronary syndrome (ACS) by 37% within one year of infection, independent of metabolic comorbidities. This reinforces the view that the infection triggers persistent endothelial activation and a sustained procoagulant state even beyond the acute febrile phase. Collectively, these findings delineate a unifying “inflammation–endothelium–coagulation” axis dysregulation, bridging acute microvascular thrombosis, neurologic and cardiac ischemic events, and chronic vascular sequelae.

In this context, HLH likely acts as an accelerator of the same immunothrombotic cascade.

The cytokine storm characteristic of HLH augments macrophage and endothelial activation, leading to accelerated fibrin consumption and increased hemorrhagic risk. Understanding this intertwined immunoinflammatory and prothrombotic network is essential for guiding management. Early recognition of endothelial activation and tailored interventions—such as balanced anticoagulation, timely transfusion support, and judicious immunomodulatory therapy—may mitigate both thrombotic and bleeding complications and improve overall outcomes in severe scrub typhus.

Existing pediatric nomograms include 5–6 variables (Hb, PLT, LDH, BUN, etc.) and require numerous laboratory assays. The current model’s simplicity—using only Hb, SCr, and FIB—makes it well suited for rapid bedside assessment, particularly in endemic, resource-limited settings. With AUC 0.843, sensitivity 70%, and specificity 95%, it can serve as both an early screening and exclusion tool for high-risk patients.

Limitations

This model has only undergone internal validation; external multicenter validation is required to confirm generalizability. HLH subgroup size was small, limiting statistical power. Key immunologic parameters (e.g., FER, IL-6, TNF-α) were unavailable, preventing molecular validation of the proposed pathophysiological pathway. Due to the limited sample size, this study was not powered to draw definitive conclusions regarding scrub typhus-associated HLH. The cohort comprised hospitalized patients, likely underrepresenting community mild cases.

Conclusions

This study proposes a simple and interpretable admission-time triad for early risk assessment in adults with scrub typhus. Decreased Hb, increased SCr, and lower FIB were independent predictors of severity, and the three-variable model showed good discrimination and calibration in internal validation. HLH cases showed higher observed mortality and more severe thrombocytopenia/coagulation derangement; however, these findings should be interpreted descriptively due to the small number of cases. Clinicians should remain alert to possible HLH in severe scrub typhus, but the prognostic and therapeutic implications require confirmation in larger cohorts.
