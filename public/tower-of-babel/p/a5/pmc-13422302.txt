Contezolid and polymyxin B nonapeptide combination reveals antibacterial synergy against multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii strains
Authors: Danrui Hao, Yuhang Huang, Tianzhu Zhao, Runyu Wang, Wei Jiang, Xiangting Yuan, Xinrong Wang, Yiwen Chu, Kelei Zhao, Ting Huang, Zhenxing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Acinetobacter baumannii is a notorious clinical pathogen that predominantly causes nosocomial infections. The multidrug resistant Acinetobacter baumannii clinical isolates are becoming more prevalent on a global scale. In this study, we aimed to determine the potential synergistic antibacterial activity of contezolid in combination with polymyxin B nonapeptide (PBNP) against various A. baumannii strains.ResultsContezolid, a commonly-used agents targeting Gram-positive bacteria, in combination with PBNP (CP) was used to explore the potential synergistic antibacterial activity against A. baumannii in vitro and Caenorhabditis elegans models. We found that CP treatment exhibited a strong synergistic effect on A. baumannii or multidrug resistant A. baumannii. CP also inhibited the biofilm formation and alter morphology of A. baumannii compared to the control group. Moreover, the residual bacteria were significantly decreased in the CP-treated murine alveolar macrophages (MH-S). Importantly, CP treatment improved the survival of C. elegans compared to the control or monotherapy group.ConclusionsThis strategy improved antibacterial activity spectrum of agents targeting Gram-positive bacteria like contezolid. Nevertheless, CP combination therapy requires further validation in mammalian infection models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05237-8.

Introduction

The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) raises a top-priority challenge to global and regional public health in the 21st century. By 2050, approximate 8 million deaths have been predicted annually by the AMR infections [1]. The over-usage and mis-usage of existing antibiotics are the important drivers to accelerate the AMR, which has greatly reduced the therapeutic effectiveness of numerous existing drugs [2]. As a nosocomial Gram-negative human pathogen, Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) is widely distributed in the healthcare environment and clinical settings [3]. This notorious opportunistic bacterium causes diverse severe infections in hospital patients, for instance, meningitis, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and blood stream infections [4]. It is now recognized as the worldwide threat in the healthcare institution, because of its special propensity to obtain and sustain multidrug resistant (MDR) phenotype in the past few years [5]. Notably, the MDR A. baumannii clinical isolates are becoming more prevalent on a global scale at previously unforeseen rates [6].

Until now, polymyxins are considered as the last-line therapy for treating infections caused by the widespread MDR A. baumannii [7]. Polymyxins are cationic, non-ribosomal peptide antibiotics that is produced by Paenibacillus polymyxa and exhibit potent bactericidal to Gram-negative bacteria [8]. Although the polymyxins exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against a large part of A. baumannii isolates, only polymyxin B and polymyxin E are mainly applied for therapeutic practice [9]. In addition, the nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity are significant side effects preventing widespread clinical application of polymyxins. Consequently, it is in urgent need of developing more effective antibacterial agents for combating A. baumannii infection. Polymyxin B nonapeptide (PBNP) is a potent permeabilizers with power to disorganize the outer membrane of Gram-negative pathogens in different types of bacterial isolates [10]. To reduce toxicity as well as retaining permeabilization activity, some studies demonstrated that PBNP in combination with common antibiotics or the anti-Gram-positive pathogen agent (linezolid) showed great synergistic activity against in different bacterial models [10–13]. These critical findings emphasize that combination of PBNP with anti-Gram-positive bacterial medical drugs is alternative strategy to cure or to relieve of the symptoms of A. baumannii-caused diseases.

Contezolid (MRX-I) is a novel member of oxazolidinone class antibiotics that serves as a powerful inhibitor of bacterial protein synthesis and consequently inhibiting bacterial proliferation [14]. Contezolid is now widely used for treating multidrug-resistant Gram-positive bacterial infections and has improved efficacy and safety compared to linezolid [15]. Recent studies have shown that contezolid combined with other antibacterial agents exhibited great synergistic activity against MDR bacterial infections, indicating that contezolid can act as the potential candidate to constructing novel combination therapeutic agents [16–18]. Despite these promising results, whether combinations of contezolid and PBNP could be used for the treatment of A. baumannii infections is uncertain.

In this study, the synergistic antibacterial activity of contezolid and PBNP (CP) combination against A. baumannii were determined via a checkerboard test and time-kill assay. The biofilm production of A. baumannii was detected by treated with CP combination. Moreover, we investigated the protective effect of CP against A. baumannii in a MH-S cell model and a nematode model (Caenorhabditis elegans, C. elegans). The present work supplies an alternative strategy to promote the development of potential therapeutic agents against A. baumannii infections.

Materials and methods

Antibacterial agents

The polymyxin B nonapeptide (PBNP), contezolid, and linezolid were bought from MedChem Express (Shanghai, China). The antibiotics used in this study were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem technology Co., LTD or Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) as follows: cefepime, ampicillin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, tobramycin, ceftazidime, levofloxacin, amikacin, cefotetan, ceftriaxone, ertapenem, cefazolin, imipenem, nitrofurantoin, aztreonam. Antimicrobial stock solutions were prepared as follows: Contezolid, linezolid, and other non-water-soluble agents were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Water-soluble antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone) and polymyxin B nonapeptide (PBNP) were dissolved in sterile distilled water.

Bacterial strains, cell lines, and C. elegans strain

The A. baumannii type strain ATCC 19,606 (Ab19606) and a multidrug-resistant A. baumannii ATCC BAA1605 (Ab1605) was obtained from the global resource center American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The clinical strain A. baumannii 3-aR8-10 (Ab3aR810) and carbapenem-resistant (CR) A. baumannii 9-R6-5 (Ab9R65) were kindly provided by Dr. Qianglin Zeng (The Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu University). These strains were cultured in Muller-Hinton broth (MHB)/agar at 37 °C. The C. elegans strain N2 was preserved in the laboratory and the MH-S cells (murine alveolar macrophage cell line) were kindly provided by Prof. Xikun Zhou (Sichuan University) and were cultured referring back to published studies [12, 19].

Minimal inhibitory concentration assay

The A. baumannii strains were inoculated in MHB and the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 105 CFU/ml (final concentration). The MIC (minimal inhibitory concentration) value of different compounds were determined for the various A. baumannii strains via a dilution series according to the standard guidelines [20]. The tested concentrations for the antimicrobial agents followed a standard two-fold serial dilution series: 1 µg/ml, 2 µg/ml, 4 µg/ml, 8 µg/ml, 16 µg/ml, 32 µg/ml, 64 µg/ml, 128 µg/ml, and 256 µg/ml. The MHB containing the same final concentration of solvent (DMSO or water) as the test wells, but without antimicrobial agents, served as the negative (growth) control. All tests were incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h and performed in technical triplicate.

Antibiotic synergy test

Synergy effects of contezolid combined with PBNP on Ab1605 strains, Ab19606 strain and A. baumannii Ab9R65 clinical isolates were tested by a checkerboard assay as mentioned before with minor modifications [21]. Briefly, the strains were seeded in MH broth and adjusted to 1 × 105 CFU/ml (final concentration). The two-dimensional array in the 96-well plate was carried out to set up the serial concentrations of drugs based on the MIC of contezolid and PBNP. The plates were cultured at 37 °C for 16–18 h. The checkerboard assay was performed in biological triplicates. The value of synergy (S) was analyzed by the Bliss independence model according to published studies [22, 23]. The interaction score S is calculated as: S = fX,0 · f0,Y - fX, Y. An S value of zero signifies independent drug action. A positive S value corresponds to a synergistic interaction, while a negative S value indicates an antagonistic interaction.

Time-kill assays

Time-kill assays were applied to determine the antibacterial effect of drugs in the tested strains as follows: contezolid alone (32 µg/ml), PBNP alone (8 µg/ml), or contezolid combination with PBNP (CP). PBS (phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4) treatment was set as a control group. The strains (Ab19606, Ab1605, Ab9R65) were seeded in MH broth reach to 1 × 105 CFU/ml (final concentration) in 96-well plates. The drugs were added to the plates and cultured at 37 °C overnight, followed by the measurement of OD600. Optical density at OD₆₀₀ was monitored in parallel for the growth control cultures only. The viability of bacteria was evaluated at time points of 1, 6, 12, and 24 h by 10-fold serially-diluted method and plated on MH agar at 37 °C. The antibacterial effect was defined based on a relevant threshold for growth inhibition (a ≤ 2 log₁₀ CFU/mL change from the initial inoculum over 24 h). The experiments were performed with technical triplicates.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The Ab1605 strains (1.0 × 106 CFU) were seeded in MH broth and respectively treated with PBS, contezolid (32 µg/ml), PBNP (8 µg/ml) or CP (contezolid+PBNP) at 37 °C for 16–18 h. A scanning electron microscope was applied to detected the morphological characteristics of Ab1605 strain after drug treatments as stated previously [12]. Briefly, bacterial pellets were collected after treatment, washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and fixed with 3% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 2 h at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS (10 min each), samples were subjected to post-fixation with 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature. After another series of buffer washes, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% twice) for 15 min at each concentration. The dried samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a 10-nm layer of gold-palladium. Finally, the prepared samples were sent to Chengdu Lilai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) for high-resolution imaging using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT700HR).

Assessment of biofilm detection

Biofilm formation was assessed by a crystal violet staining following previous work with minor modifications [6, 24]. Briefly, the Ab1605 strains were seeded in 96-well plate (1.0 × 105 CFU) or 24-well plate (1.0 × 106 CFU) with MH medium and added with PBS, contezolid (16 µg/ml), PBNP (4 µg/ml) or CP at 37 °C overnight (about 16 h). The unattached bacteria were removed and the plates was dried in a clean bench. After dried, crystal violet solution (0.04%) was added in the wells for 20 min. The dye was removed and washed three times with distilled water. The absorbance was measured at OD595 after dissolution by 95% ethanol. Three technical replicates for each treatment were performed.

In vitro A. baumannii-infected cell model

The Ab1605 strain was cultured in MH medium at 37°C for 16 h. The Ab1605 pellet was collected by centrifugated bacterial medium at 4,000 g and subsequently was softly resuspended in sterilized PBS solutions. MH-S cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which was supplement with fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10%) and 1% (w/v) penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2. An infection assay was performed in MH-S cells model as shown in a published study with minor modifications [25]. MH-S cells were changed to fresh RPMI-1640 medium without antibiotics. Then, bacterial resuspensions were added to the medium and cocultured with MH-S cells at an MOI of 10:1 (bacteria-cell ratio) for 1 h, followed by treatment with CP or monotherapy for 1 h, 6 h, and 12 h. The residual bacterial CFU were determined via plate count method at different time point [26].

Cytotoxicity assay in MH-S cells

To determine the potential cytotoxicity of contezolid, PBNP, or CP, a cytotoxicity assay in MH-S cells was performed. Briefly, MH-S cells were cultured in 96-well plates reach to a final concentration of 1 × 105 cells/ml and treated with PBS, contezolid, PBNP or CP for 1 h, 6 h, and 12 h. The cell viability of MH-S cells was detected via CCK-8 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Solarbio Life Science) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at OD450 by a microplate reader. Three replicates for each treatment were performed.

C. elegans infection model

To further examine the protective efficacy of CP combination group, killing assays were performed to analyze the survival rate of nematode in the presence of two different A. baumannii strains as previously described [13]. Briefly, the L1 worms of C. elegans were seeded in E. coli plates to L4 stage. The L4 worms of C. elegans (10 worms/group) were infected with Ab1605 or Ab19606 strains at 37 °C for 24 h. The infected C. elegans was moved to a new plate, which contains fresh M9 medium. After adding the nematodes, PBS, contezolid (32 µg/ml), PBNP (8 µg/ml) or CP were added into the plate respectively. Dead nematodes were microscopically counted every 12 h for the subsequent 5 days. All the experiments were performed in biological triplicate (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data and statistical tests via a GraphPad Prism software (8.0). A Tukey-Kramer post hoc test was utilized to compare the means (a one-way analysis of variance) and a Mantel-Cox log rank test was used to determine the survivability of nematodes.

Results

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

All tested A. baumannii strains demonstrated resistance to PBNP and the two anti-Gram-positive antibiotics, contezolid and linezolid (Table 1, Table S1). The MICs for both PBNP and contezolid were ≥ 128 µg/mL against all strains, indicating a lack of direct antimicrobial activity. In addition, strains Ab19606 and Ab3aR810 were susceptible to most anti-Gram-negative agents at low concentrations.

The synergistic activity of contezolid and PBNP in a checkboard assay

The polymyxin B nonapeptide PBNP is antibacterial sensitizer that can restore the antibacterial activity of many antibiotics including anti-Gram negative or anti-Gram-positive antibiotics. However, whether combinations of PBNP and contezolid has synergistic activity against A. baumannii remains an open question. To investigate the synergistic activity of contezolid and PBNP (CP), the checkerboard assay was carried out to determine the degree of synergy for the type strain A. baumannii Ab19606, MDR A. baumannii Ab1605, and CR A. baumannii Ab9R65 (Table S2). As shown in Fig. 1A, a dose-dependent synergistic activity of CP was detected on the Ab19606 strain. PBNP could decrease the MICs of contezolid, which is smaller than the susceptibility breakpoint (256 µg/ml). Likewise, PBNP enhanced the contezolid susceptibility of Ab1605, as the MIC of contezolid against Ab1605 decreased from 256 µg/ml to 32 µg/ml-64 µg/ml and the MIC of PBNP against Ab1605 decreased from > 256 µg/ml to 8 µg/ml-16 µg/ml (Fig. 1B). In addition, PBNP enhanced the contezolid susceptibility of CR Ab9R65, as the MIC of contezolid against Ab9R65 decreased from 256 µg/ml to 16 µg/ml-64 µg/ml (Fig. 1C). Given that combination of conezolid (32 µg/ml) and PBNP (8 µg/ml) (CP) exhibited the significant synergistic effects at lower concentrations in all the tested group, CP was chosen as a new drug combination to explore the synergistic antibacterial activity for combating A. baumannii in this study.

Combined contezolid and PBNP enhanced antibacterial activity in culture media

To confirm the synergism of CP, the time-kill assay was performed in three different A. baumannii strains. As shown in Fig. 2A, the Ab19606 strain grew normally from 1 h to 24 h by treated the drug alone. Neither contezolid nor PNBP monotreatment killed type strain A. baumannii Ab19606. Nevertheless, the combination CP led to a significant reduction of bacterial CFU compared to the PBS-treated group from 1 h to 24 h time point (Fig. 2A). To verify the findings, the synergistic antimicrobial effects of CP was also tested on the MDR strain Ab1605 (Fig. 2B) and clinical strain CR A. baumannii Ab9R65 (Fig. 2C). Compared to the control group, treatment with CP led to remarkably declined bacterial viability from 1 h to 24 h (Fig. 2B and C). The obtained results above demonstrated that PBNP drastically improved the antibacterial effect of contezolid against drug-resistant A. baumannii.

CP altered the morphology and biofilm production of A. baumannii

In order to test whether the morphology of A. baumannii was altered by CP, Ab1605 strain was cultured in MH medium, following treatment with the contezolid or PBNP monotherapy, and CP drug combination. As can be seen from the Fig. 3D, it is apparent that CP drug combination remarkably altered the morphology of Ab1605 strain and leading to cell shrinkage or cell lysis. Nevertheless, no significant change was observed in the contezolid or PBNP monotherapy group compared to the control group (Fig. 3A-C). To rule out the possibility that CP treatment might affect the biofilm production of A. baumannii, Ab1605 strain was cultured in plates and were added with PBS, contezolid, PBNP or CP in a lower concentration (contezolid (16 µg/ml), PBNP (4 µg/ml)). From the data in Fig. 4A and C, CP treatment reduced the biofilm production of Ab1605 in 96-well plate or 24-well plate, and statistical tests further confirm the antibiofilm activity of CP (Fig. 4B and D).

CP reduced bacterial burdens in MH-S cells

Alveolar macrophages play an essential role in host responses to bacterial infections. To investigate the potential antibacterial activity of CP against Ab1605 infection, we examined its protective effects in an in vitro MH-S cell model by counting the bacterial loads. As shown in Fig. 5, it is apparent that contezolid or PBNP monotherapy exhibited no significant protection in MH-S cells compared to the control group at 1 h (Fig. 5A), 6 h (Fig. 5B), or 12 h (Fig. 5C) post infection. The most interesting aspect of this graph is that drug combination group CP enhanced clearance of Ab1605 in MH-S cells compared to the control group from 1 h to 12 h post infection (Fig. 5A-C). Notably, there is no significant difference of cell viability between the drug-treated groups and blank group at different time points by a CCK8 kit (Fig. 5D-F), suggesting that neither monotherapy nor drug combination treatment has obvious toxicity in mammalian cells.

CP promoted survivability of C. elegans against A. baumannii infection

To further explore the antibacterial activity of contezolid combination with PBNP, C. elegans were infected with A. baumannii in SK plate, following treatment with CP or monotherapy. As shown in Fig. 6A, the C. elegans from PBS group died at 60 h. Most C. elegans did not survive in the contezolid or PBNP monotherapy group within 120 h. However, the approximate 70% of C. elegans in the CP-treated group survived by infected with Ab1605 strain at days 5 post infection. Likewise, contezolid combination with PBNP greatly improved the survivability of C. elegans against Ab19606 infection (Fig. 6B). Importantly, uninfected C. elegans survived in the CP-treated group within 120 h, indicating that CP has no toxicity in C. elegans and therefore did not affect the growth of C. elegans.

Discussion

As a critical member of the ESKAPE pathogen group, the MDR A. baumannii poses a serious and growing challenge to global public health. Notably, the incidence of CR A. baumannii infections is increasing in life-threatening hospital-acquired settings, where treatment options remain severely limited [27]. The development of new antibiotics is further hampered by a stark pipeline disparity; by the end of 2020, only 43 phase 1–3 clinical trials involved novel antibiotics, compared with nearly 1,300 trials for anticancer agents [28]. Given the diminishing efficacy of available antibiotics, it is imperative to develop alternative strategies against which A. baumanniimay not readily develop resistance. Antibiotic synergy is a promising strategy to treat the severe infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria, particularly those antibiotics that have become less effective and toxicity [11–13, 29]. In this study, we constructed a new combination CP, which exhibited a strong synergistic activity against type strain A. baumannii and CR A. baumannii clinical isolates. Intriguingly, the combination agents CP greatly enhanced the antibacterial activities in vitro and provided protection from A. baumannii infections in the nematode model. Hence, this potential drug combination treatment expands the spectrum of antibiotics and provides an innovative strategy for controlling A. baumannii infections.

Polymyxin B nonapeptide is an important candidate for the antibiotic combinations due to the less toxicity compared to polymyxins and retaining strong membrane-permeabilizing activity [10–13]. To the best of our knowledge, there was limited evidence for the synergistic activity of CP to combat A. baumannii. First of all, the susceptibility of several antibiotics and PBNP were detected in A. baumannii Ab19606, Ab1605 or clinical CR A. baumannii strains Ab9R65 and to determine the synergistic antimicrobial activity of alternative antibiotic combinations. One interesting finding was that all the tested A. baumannii isolates were remarkably resistant to the anti-Gram-positive compounds (contezolid and linezolid) alone and PBNP alone. Another important finding was that the A. baumannii strains were susceptible to CP compared with the single drug treatment. The observed synergy is consistent with the well-documented membrane-permeabilizing activity of PBNP, which may facilitate the uptake of contezolid and enhanced the antibacterial activity. Given that contezolid has improved efficacy and safety compared to linezolid, hence, we focus on the synergistic antibacterial activity of CP drug combination in this study.

To assess the potential synergy between contezolid and PBNP, antibacterial activity was evaluated by comparing single-agent contezolid treatment with the CP combination in a time-kill assay. The results showed that the CP combination significantly reduced bacterial viability in both the Ab19606 strain and clinical CR-A. baumanni compared to monotherapy. While PBNP alone exhibited limited bacteriostatic activity, its combination with contezolid produced a pronounced antibacterial activity, which was further supported by observable morphological damage to A. baumannii, including cell lysis. Biofilm can consist a structured community of bacteria in self-produced exopolysaccharide matrices, which is adherent to abiotic or biotic surfaces [29]. A. baumannii possess various virulence traits, and especially possess a strong ability to form biofilms on medical devices such as catheters and ventilators. Within a biofilm community, this notorious bacterium is highly tolerant to antibiotics than their planktonic counterparts, which leads to increased treatment failures [30]. As a result, disrupting established biofilm formation of A. baumannii is extremely crucial against A. baumannii infections. In the present study, we found that CP combination has a remarkably inhibitory effect on the biofilm formation of A. baumannii strains. These findings were similar to several previous reports, in which the phenotypes of MDR Gram-negative bacteria were significantly altered by treated with a drug combined strategy associated with PBNP [10, 11, 13]. While the combination of contezolid and PBNP demonstrates promising in vitro synergy against the A. baumannii strains, the MIC of contezolid alone (32 µg/ml) is high and likely exceeds established clinical susceptibility breakpoints for this antibiotic class. However, the primary significance of the observed synergy lies not in restoring standalone susceptibility to contezolid, but in demonstrating that PBNP can potentiate the activity of contezolid at a low concentration. This may inform potential combination regimens where lower, more clinically achievable doses of both agents could be effective, potentially mitigating toxicity or overcoming resistance mechanisms. Future studies utilizing pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling are warranted to explore the feasibility of such a combination approach.

To further determine the potential protection of CP combination against A. baumannii strains, the MH-S cell infected model and the C. elegans infected model were used as targets. One interesting finding was that CP combination therapy exhibited better microbicidal activity against A. baumannii infection in MH-S model. However, a large number of A. baumannii burdens were determined from the MH-S cell in the control group or the monotherapy group, which further confirmed that either contezolid or PBNP alone treatment did not affect the growth of bacteria. Another important finding was that MH-S cell viability in the drug-treated groups was no statistically significant difference compared with the blank group, indicating either contezolid or PBNP treatment was safe for cells. For the C. elegans infection model, the better protective effect against A. baumannii strains was determined in the CP combination therapy group compared to the control group. Indeed, contezolid and PBNP worked synergistically, and protective effects in the C. elegans infection model were enhanced as shown in the 120 h of post treatment. Altogether, our findings further confirm the in vitro results of antibacterial activity by the CP treatment, indicating contezolid was a potent candidate for treating A. baumannii infections when it was incorporated with PBNP. However, a direct experimental validation of the proposed mechanism (e.g., intracellular drug accumulation studies or NPN uptake assay) was not performed in this study. Therefore, while the synergy is clearly demonstrated, the precise molecular mechanism within this specific combination remains to be conclusively established.

In conclusion, PBNP sensitized A. baumannii to contezolid, and the novel combination CP demonstrated significantly synergistic activity against A. baumannii, including the MDR and CR strains, across multiple experimental models used in this study. PBNP significantly enhanced the antibacterial effect of contezolid, suggesting its potential to expand the therapeutic utility of contezolid. Notably, this combination allows for a reduction in contezolid dosage, which may help mitigate selective pressure on A. baumannii populations. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations, such as the limited number of strains tested and the lack of genotypic data, without polymyxin B comparison, and the absence of rodent infection models to further evaluate the efficacy of CP. Collectively, combining antibacterial agents in this manner may help counteract the rise of bacterial resistance and contribute to control of A. baumannii-related infections.
