Stress-coping phenotypes shape adipose tissue function and plasticity and modulate metabolic resilience
Authors: Saumya Kishor Mehta, Michaella Ben-Shachar, Sharmila Govindaraj, Suparna Sen, Adrian Segev, Oshrit Rahimi, Valid Gahramanov, Galia Luboshits, Michael A Firer, Ayala Wollman, Albert Pinhasov, Tovit Rosenzweig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur previous findings demonstrated that stress resilience confers metabolic protection in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice. Recognizing the central role of adipose tissue in the pathogenesis of obesity comorbidities, this study aimed to define how stress resilience shapes adipose tissue function. Stress-resilient (Dominant, Dom) and stress-vulnerable (Submissive, Sub) mice were examined under standard (STD) or HFD. Metabolic, molecular, and transcriptomic analyses were performed in epididymal and inguinal white adipose tissue (eWAT and iWAT), and the effects of PPARgamma activator (pioglitazone) and antidepressant (paroxetine) were investigated. Stromal vascular fraction (SVF) cells were isolated to assess adipogenic potential. Sub mice developed glucose intolerance under HFD, accompanied by impaired insulin-induced Akt phosphorylation, and reduced glucose uptake in WAT, whereas Dom mice remained metabolically protected. Sub WAT exhibited adipocyte hypertrophy, an unfavorable leptin-to-adiponectin ratio, and depot-specific suppression of BCAA catabolic enzymes. RNA sequencing revealed transcriptomic divergence between strains, with Sub mice showing downregulation of adipogenesis under STD conditions. Functional analyses confirmed reduced proliferative and adipogenic potential of SVF cells, derived from Sub mice under both dietary conditions. In contrast, Dom-derived adipocytes displayed higher Glut4 and Ucp1 and an elevated adiponectin-to-leptin mRNA expression ratio. Pioglitazone enhanced adipogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and prevented hepatic steatosis in Sub mice, although this was not accompanied by improved glucose tolerance. Paroxetine treatment normalized behavioral deficits in Sub mice and improved adipogenesis toward a Dom-like profile. In summary, this study demonstrates that impaired stress coping drives defects in adipose progenitor function, leading to maladaptive WAT expansion and increased susceptibility to obesity-related metabolic disease. Subject terms: Physiology, Depression

Introduction

Obesity is a global health concern and a leading risk factor for metabolic syndrome and its associated complications. Most individuals with obesity develop comorbidities such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, classifying them as metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO). However, there is a considerable variability in both the risk and severity of cardiometabolic diseases among obese individuals. Notably, a subset of this population maintains relatively normal metabolic health, therefore known as metabolically healthy obese (MHO) [1]. While the prevalence and long-term stability of the MHO phenotype remain debated [1–3], it is agreed that identifying the mechanisms underlying differential susceptibility to obesity-related comorbidities is critical for developing effective public health strategies.

Among the factors proposed to influence metabolic health, stress was shown to increase the risk of obesity and cardiometabolic morbidities [4–7]. However, individual differences in stress-coping abilities might also play a role in determining vulnerability to metabolic dysfunction [8–11]. It can be hypothesized that in a stressogenic environment, stress-vulnerable individuals are prone to the development of depression and face a higher risk of developing obesity-related comorbidities, whereas stress-resilient individuals may maintain healthier behavior as well as a better metabolic profile [11–15]. However, the association between impaired stress-coping ability and the MUO phenotype has received limited investigation, and the underlying mechanisms have not been elucidated.

To address this gap, we have employed a unique mouse model exhibiting two distinct stress response phenotypes: stress-resilient (Dominant, Dom) and stress-vulnerable (Submissive, Sub). Mice were tested for dominance or submissiveness using a validated test (the Dominance-Submissiveness Relationship, DSR), in which mice compete for sweetened milk. By a series of selective breeding, the phenotypes of Sub and Dom mice were stabilized, with Sub mice presenting high levels of anxiety, depressive-like behavior, and stress vulnerability, compared to the Dom strain, which was resilient to these behavioral alterations [16]. Our previous studies showed that Sub mice were metabolically unhealthy under HFD feeding. They developed severe glucose intolerance, insulin and leptin resistance, and fatty liver, whereas Dom mice were protected from these pathologies [13]. Similarly, in a different rodent model of dominance and subordination, weight gain was influenced by this trait, with HFD-fed subordinate mice exhibiting greater body weight and an elevated visceral adipose mass [17]. Notably, while these metabolic alterations in Sub mice emerged only under HFD conditions, their inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) exhibited changes even when fed a standard diet (STD) [13]. This suggests that the iWAT is inherently impaired in Sub mice and may play a key role in the pathogenesis of their metabolic dysfunction.

White adipose tissue (WAT) is a key regulator of energy homeostasis and insulin sensitivity and plays a central role in shaping metabolic phenotype [2, 18–21]. It is generally agreed that adipose tissue function, rather than mass, determines metabolic health. Cross-sectional studies contrast MHO with MUO by the presence of lower plasma levels of pro-inflammatory adipocytokines, and increased adiponectin [22–24]. It was also shown that WAT of MHO individuals tend to drive adipogenesis, enabling adipocyte hyperplasia and the production of small, insulin-sensitive, and healthy adipocytes. Conversely, in most MUO subjects, adipose tissue is hypertrophic, inflamed, and more localized in the viscera [25, 26].

The present study aimed to determine whether stress vulnerability promotes adipose tissue dysfunction, and to characterize the functional differences in adipose tissue between mice with distinct stress-response phenotypes. To address this, we combined hypothesis-driven analyses of key features of adipose tissue function, including insulin sensitivity, adipocyte hypertrophy versus hyperplasia, metabolic and adipokine profiles, with hypothesis-free approach of transcriptomic profiling of iWAT and eWAT. These approaches identified adipogenesis as a central pathway differentially regulated in stress-vulnerable and stress-resilient mice, which we further investigated using functional analyses and pharmacological interventions. Collectively, these findings provide insight into the mechanisms underlying susceptibility or resistance to MUO versus MHO, and establish a functional link between behavioral phenotype, tissue adipogenic potential, and its functionality.

Methods

Animals

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health) and adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments at Ariel University (approval number: AU-IL-21101023). We employed unique mouse models that display distinct behavioral phenotypes of social dominance and stress resilience (Dom) and social submissiveness and stress vulnerability (Sub). These lines were originally derived from the Sabra strain (Envigo) [27] and developed through 48 generations of selective breeding using the Dominant-Submissive Relationship (DSR) test [28].

Study design

Assessment of adipose function in STD and HFD-fed mice

Dom and Sub mice (males) were randomly assigned at 6 weeks of age into standard diet (STD, 18% of total calories from fat) or high-fat diet (HFD, 60% of total calories from fat; Research Diets Inc. D12492) groups for omics analyses or metabolic characterization (n = 10 males, with 5 mice per cage). Glucose tolerance test (GTT) was conducted at the age of 12 weeks. Mice were sacrificed at the age of 14 weeks and adipose tissues (iWAT and eWAT) were collected for analyses.

Assessment of adipose function in response to pharmacological intervention

Male Sub mice aged 6 weeks were fed a STD and were divided into three treatment groups: vehicle control, paroxetine, and pioglitazone (Cayman Chemicals). Drugs were administered via drinking water at a dose of 15 mg/kg, adjusted to the body weight of the mice and drinking volumes [29, 30]. At 10 weeks of age, mice were divided into two cohorts. One cohort was sacrificed, and iWAT was collected for primary adipose tissue culturing (section 2.2.11). The second group underwent a battery of behavioral tests, then sacrificed, and adipose tissue was collected for gene expression analyses.

Behavioral assessment

To evaluate anxiety-like, depressive-like behaviors, locomotor activity, and social interaction, a series of standardized behavioral tests was conducted over four consecutive days. All tests, including the Three-Chamber Test (TCT), Open Field (OF), Hole Board (HB) test, and Forced Swimming Test (FST), were performed according to established protocols [31].

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests (GTT and ITT)

An intraperitoneal GTT was conducted after six weeks of dieting. Following a 6-h fast, mice were injected intraperitoneally with D-glucose (1 g/kg body weight). ITT was conducted a week after GTT. Fasting mice were injected intraperitoneally with insulin (0.5 U/kg body weight). Glucose levels were measured from tail blood using an ACCU-CHEK Go glucometer (Roche) at fasting and after glucose or insulin administration at the indicated times.

Fasting insulin, leptin, adiponectin and branched-chain amino acids (BCAA)

Fasting serum insulin, leptin and adiponectin levels were measured using ELISA kits (Mercodia and Abcam, respectively) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fasting BCAA was measured in serum using BCAA Assay Kit (Abcam), in which BCAA are oxidatively deaminated, producing NADH, which generates a colored product. A background control measurement was included for each sample to subtract in-sample NADH/NADPH.

Glucose uptake

Isolated iWAT and eWAT were harvested from 14-week-old STD and HFD-fed mice. Explants were prepared by mincing the tissues into small pieces in KRH/Ca2+-BSA buffer in 24 well plates [32]. Explants were treated with 100 nM human insulin for 20 min at 37 °C and then washed with warm PBS. Next, 2-DG (0.2 mM in KRH/Ca2+-BSA buffer) and [3H]-2-DG (2µCi/ml) were added to each well. Cytochalasin B (40 µM) was used to measure non-specific uptake. The explants were then incubated for another 10 min at 37 °C and washed three times with ice-cold PBS. To measure radioactivity, the explants were dissolved in NaOH (1 N) and SDS (0.1%) at 50 °C for 2 h. Basal glucose uptake was measured as 2-DG uptake without insulin [32].

Western blot analysis

Adipose tissue explants were incubated with or without 100 nM human insulin for 20 min at 37 °C. After treatment, the explants were washed with ice-cold PBS and either stored at -80 °C or immediately processed for lysate preparation. Protein lysates were extracted, and Western blot analysis was conducted as described before [13].

RNA isolation

For RNAseq analysis, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For gene expression analysis in primary adipocyte culture, cells were lysed and homogenized in TRIzol Reagent. RNA isolation was performed according to the User Guide (Invitrogen).

Real-time PCR

RNA (4 µg) was reverse transcribed using oligo dT primers (GoScript, Promega), as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. Real-time PCR was carried out using the SYBR Fast Universal Ready-Mix Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Inc.) and the MxPro QPCR system (Stratagene). The primers were synthesized by Sigma (Israel) [13]. Primers sequences are detailed in Supplement (Suppl. 1).

RNA-Seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted as described above, and RNA quality was measured using a TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies). RNA-seq libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, cat. E7760), following the manufacturer’s protocol, and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform at the Technion Genomics Center, Haifa, Israel, as single-end 100 bp reads. Raw data and processed files have been deposited in NCBI GEO under accession number GSE336483.

Raw reads underwent quality control using the FASTQC (v.0.11.7) [33] available at http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and Trim Galore (v.0.5.0) [34] as used to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. High-quality reads were aligned to the mouse genome (build GRCm38/mm10) using the HISAT2 (v.2.2.1) [35] and gene-level read counts were quantified using the featureCounts (v.1.6.3) [36].

For gene expression comparisons, TMM normalization was performed using the edgeR package [37]. The voom transformation was applied for differential expression analysis using linear models in the Limma package [38]. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. Genes with an FDR < 0.05 and a log fold change |log2FC | > 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Heatmaps of DEGs were created using the pheatmap package in R [39], with Euclidean distance and complete clustering method.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the plotPCA function in the DESeq2 package [40], and visualized using the ggplot2 package in R [41]. Volcano plots were generated to visualize the DEGs. The ggplot2 package was used to create scatter plots with the log2 fold change on the x-axis and the negative log10 of the adjusted p-value on the y-axis [41]. Genes with a log2 fold change greater than 1 and an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 were colored red (upregulated), while genes with a log2 fold change less than -1 and an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 were colored blue (downregulated).

Pathway analysis

The ranked gene list based on fold-change was used as input for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the hallmark gene-set signature and curated gene sets (C2) from the Molecular Signature Database v7.2. Significant gene sets were defined as those with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an FDR cutoff of 25%, considering the minimal number of gene sets analyzed [42, 43].

Primary adipose tissue culturing

Stromal vascular fraction (SVF) was extracted from adipose tissue through collagenase digestion, as described [44]. Adipose-derived progenitor cells (ADPC) proliferation was assessed 72 to 96 h after seeding using the XTT cell proliferation assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Biological Industries, Israel), and adipocyte differentiation was evaluated 7 days after induction of differentiation by Oil Red O staining and qPCR analysis of adipogenic markers expression. In addition, supernatant was collected from differentiated cultures, centrifuged, and adiponectin level was measured by ELISA (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Oil red O staining

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Merck) for 1 h at room temperature, after which the media was removed, and the cells were rinsed with warm PBS. After fixation, cells were incubated for 1 h with 60% ORO solution and then washed with distilled water. To quantify staining intensity, cells were incubated for 15 min in 0.4 mL of isopropanol. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions of SVF cells were prepared by collagenase digestion. The eluant was treated with 1x RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience) for 3 min, centrifuged at 300 x g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the cells were resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/ml. Fc receptors were blocked by the addition of anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (clone 93, eBioscience). The cells were stained for 30 min at RT in the dark with combinations of six fluorescently labeled antibodies (see Supplement S2) and 7aad for dead cells exclusion. Fluorescence was measured using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Waters Biosciences, USA). To specifically characterize adipocyte progenitor populations, a sequential flow cytometry gating strategy was employed based on established methodologies [45, 46].

Initially, total and viable cells were selected using forward and side scatter properties (FSC/SSC) and dead cells were excluded based on 7aaD staining. Hematopoietic and endothelial cells were subsequently excluded using CD45 and CD31 markers, respectively, allowing isolation of the non-hematopoietic, non-endothelial stromal compartment (CD45⁻ CD31⁻), which represents the stromal fraction [47]. Within this population, CD29 (integrin β1) was used as a stromal/adhesion-associated marker. The progenitor pool was further defined by co-expression of CD34 and Ly6 (Sca-1), which identifies a stromal progenitor compartment within adipose tissue [45, 48]. A critical distinction within this progenitor compartment is based on CD24 co-expression, which represents a population with high adipogenic potential [47]. Gating scheme is shown in Supplement S3.

Statistical analysis

All measurement data followed a normal distribution (mean ± SE). Brown-Forsythe test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance. For unequal variances, Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test was used. Sample size was based on prior experiments and power estimation. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s post hoc tests, as appropriate. All analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 software. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A series of tests confirmed the behavioral phenotype of Sub and Dom mice (Fig. 1A). Anxiety-like behavior of Sub mice was validated, showing that Sub mice spent less time than Dom in the center of the OF arena, and had reduced explorative behavior in HB test. Swimming time in the FST was low in Sub, indicating impaired stress-coping and depressive-like behavior, whereas Dom mice presented a contrasting behavior. Lastly, impaired sociability, an additional feature of depressive-like behavior, was observed by TCT in Sub mice.

Our previous study demonstrated that stress-vulnerable Sub mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD/Sub) developed glucose intolerance, while stress-resilient Dom mice were almost unaffected by the obesogenic diet. This observation was also confirmed in the experimental groups of the current study (Fig. 1B). Our previous study indicated that adipose tissue may play a central role in developing obesity-related metabolic alterations in the Sub strain. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the effects of stress coping abilities on adipose tissue function.

Strain-dependent differences in adipose insulin sensitivity under HFD

Insulin sensitivity was measured in adipose tissue explants. Insulin-induced glucose uptake was higher in both iWAT and eWAT of Dom than in Sub mice under STD feeding (Fig. 1C). Insulin failed to stimulate glucose uptake in iWAT and eWAT of Sub mice upon HFD feeding. In contrast, insulin-induced glucose uptake remained preserved in the iWAT of Dom mice, while being impaired in the eWAT of these mice.

The binding of insulin to its receptor activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade that involves the phosphorylation and activation of the major pathway node, Akt/PKB, which subsequently phosphorylates and blocks PRAS40 inhibitory activity towards mTORC1. Akt was similarly phosphorylated in STD/Sub and STD/Dom mice in response to insulin stimulation in both iWAT and eWAT (Figs. 1D and 1F respectively). However, HFD feeding markedly reduced insulin-induced Akt phosphorylation in Sub, but not in Dom mice. In contrast, PRAS40 phosphorylation was higher in Sub compared to Dom mice, with insulin-induced phosphorylation being elevated under STD feeding and basal PRAS40 phosphorylation tends to be elevated upon HFD feeding (Figs. 1D and 1F).

HFD-induced strain- and depot-specific changes in BCAA metabolism

The increase in PRAS40 phosphorylation could be explained by Akt-independent pathways such as phosphorylation of PRAS40 in response to amino acid signaling, especially branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs). Therefore, the expression of genes, encoding BCAA metabolizing enzyme was measured. BCKDHA, BCAT1, BCAT2 and HMGCL, which are BCAA catabolizing enzymes, were all stimulated by HFD in iWAT of Dom mice (Fig. 1E). In eWAT of both Sub and Dom mice, HFD repressed the expression of BCKDHA and HMGCL, while the expression of BCKDHB and BCAT2 was attenuated by HFD only in Sub mice (Fig. 1G). Fasting BCAA levels were lower in STD-Sub mice than in Dom. However, HFD increased BCAA in Sub, while not affecting Dom (Fig. 1H).

HFD differentially affects adipocyte size and adipokine expression in Sub and Dom mice

The functionality of adipose tissue is also manifested by the diameter of cells, small adipocytes being healthier than large cells. As shown in Figs. 2A and 2E, HFD feeding increased adipose mass in both HFD/Sub and HFD/Dom mice. However, adipocytes’ diameter was increased in Sub mice, while being unchanged in Dom, indicating a hyperplasia of Dom’s adipose tissue (Figs. 2B and 2F). In addition, leptin mRNA expression was significantly higher in iWAT and eWAT of HFD/Sub than in Dom mice (Figs. 2C and 2G). An opposite effect was found regarding adiponectin mRNA expression in iWAT, demonstrating a higher expression in HFD/Dom than in Sub mice (Fig. 2D). Adiponectin expression was inhibited by HFD in eWAT of Sub mice, while not affected in Dom (Fig. 2H). Serum leptin levels were higher in HFD-SUB than in Dom (Fig. 2I), while adiponectin levels were similar between strains (Fig. 2J), indicating a higher leptin-to-adiponectin ratio in Sub.

Adipose transcriptomes of Sub and Dom mice exhibit divergent expression profiles

The results so far demonstrate a better adipose tissue function in Dom than in Sub mice. To further elucidate the specific differences in adipose tissue functionality between Sub and Dom, RNA seq analysis was performed on iWAT and eWAT of STD- and HFD-fed mice. On average, 23 million reads per sample were obtained (n = 4 per group) and mapped to the Mus musculus genome (build mm 10) with an average base call quality Q score of 33. PCA of gene expression data showed distinct clustering of Sub and Dom samples, even under STD feeding (Fig. 3A). HFD did not alter the clustering of Dom iWAT samples, while generating different cluster of Sub samples. A similar profile was observed in eWAT, although some difference was observed in the clustering of samples of STD and HFD-fed Dom mice (Fig. 3C).

Pathway enrichment analysis of DEG (p-value < 0.05, FDR cutoff of 25%) was conducted by Hallmark analysis (Figs. 3K, L). A comparison between STD/Sub and STD/Dom indicates that pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, adipogenesis and fatty acid metabolism were elevated in Sub mice, whereas pathways related to inflammation were downregulated (Fig. 3K). On the contrary, in response to HFD, pathways related to inflammation were upregulated, whereas pathways of oxidative phosphorylation and adipogenesis were downregulated in Sub mice (Fig. 3L). Thus, the pattern of gene expression in eWAT of Sub and Dom is opposite to that observed in iWAT.

Collectively, the results so far reveal that adipogenic pathways are differentially expressed in Sub and Dom mice. This conclusion is also demonstrated by heatmaps of adipogenesis pathway (Fig. 4A and B) and enrichment plots, which show an inverse expression profile between iWAT and eWAT (Fig. 4C and D). However, analysis of the genes of the adipogenesis pathway (Hallmark), filtered by adjusted p-value < 0.05 and LFC higher than ±1, revealed a set of genes presenting a similar expression pattern between the two adipose depots under STD feeding (Fig. 4E). STRING network analysis showed that these genes clustered together to the acyl-CoA biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 4F). This similarity in gene expression in iWAT and eWAT of Sub and Dom mice was not observed under HFD feeding (Fig. 4E).

In contrast, pathways directly related to insulin signaling or BCAA metabolism were not among the most significantly enriched pathways in the transcriptomic analyses. While functional assays demonstrated alterations in insulin sensitivity and circulating BCAA levels, the lack of strong transcriptomic enrichment suggests that these processes may reflect downstream or secondary features of adipose tissue dysfunction, rather than primary drivers in this model.

Pioglitazone stimulated adipogenesis and improved metabolic health in Sub mice

To support the functional link between reduced adipogenesis and the development of metabolic alteration in HFD-fed Sub mice, we treated mice with pioglitazone (Pio), a known adipogenic stimulator [50]. Pio treatment did not significantly affect body weight (Fig. 5A), or adipose tissue weight (iWAT and eWAT; Figs. 5B, C). However, adipocyte diameter was smaller in response to Pio administration, compared with untreated HFD-fed mice (Figs. 5D, F), indicating that PPARγ activation induced adipogenesis through hyperplasia of adipocytes, as expected [51]. While a reduced adipocyte diameter was also observed in eWAT of Pio-treated mice, this effect was less remarkable (Fig. 4E). Pio also stimulated the expression of Pgc1a, Lpl, and Fasn, supporting the induction of adipogenesis and lipogenesis (Fig. 5G). In addition, the expression of Ucp1, a biomarker of beige tissue, was elevated by Pio. Despite these improvements in adipose tissue functioning, Pio did not improve glucose tolerance, as measured by the glucose tolerance test (Fig. 5H). However, while GTT failed to be improved in Pio-treated mice, other obesity-related pathologies were abolished by this treatment; The early glucose-lowering effect of insulin was enhanced by Pio (Fig. 5I), and both hyperinsulinemia and hyperleptinemia, developed in HFD/Sub mice, were ameliorated, indicating that insulin and leptin sensitivity were improved (Figs. 5J and 5K). In addition, HFD-induced steatosis, developed in the liver of Sub mice, was eliminated by Pio (Fig. 5L).

Sub and Dom mice exhibit a distinct adipogenic potential

Functional analysis of the adipogenic potential of iWAT and eWAT of Sub and Dom mice was conducted. The stromal-vascular fraction (SVF) was isolated from the adipose tissue of mice, and its proliferation rate and capability to differentiate into adipocytes were measured. The proliferation rate was measured four days after SVF isolation and seeding, while adipogenesis was measured seven days after induction, which was initiated upon reaching confluency (Fig. 6A). These measurements were conducted on SVF isolated from two cohorts of mice. In the first cohort, representing STD-fed conditions, SVF was isolated from 6–7-week-old mice. In the second cohort, which was used to assess the effect of HFD, SVF was isolated from 11–12-week-old mice after six weeks of HFD feeding. Under STD conditions, the proliferation rate of both iWAT and eWAT-derived preadipocytes was lower in Sub than in Dom mice (Fig. 6B). In the second (older) cohort, the proliferation rate was lower overall in both strains compared with the younger cohort. However, proliferation of STD-fed Dom’s SVF, isolated from iWAT, was still higher than Sub (Fig. 6F). After achieving confluency, differentiation was induced, and the magnitude of differentiation was measured by Oil-red O (ORO) staining of lipids (Figs. 6C and 6G). In addition, gene expression analysis was performed on adipocyte culture, derived from iWAT SVF (Fig. 6E). ORO staining was higher in differentiated adipocytes of young Dom than those isolated from Sub mice (Fig. 6C). Adipocyte morphology was observed in over 90% of Dom-derived differentiated culture, while a lower percentage of cells achieved this morphology in culture of Sub-derived cells (Fig. 6D). Analysis of gene expression (Fig. 6E) showed that Cebpa and Fabp4, key regulators of adipocyte differentiation, were similarly stimulated in differentiated adipocyte cultures of both Sub and Dom mice (STD-fed), while Ppargamma expression was higher in Dom-derived cultures than in Sub. The expression of Lpl, Fasn and Acaca, genes encoding for lipogenic enzymes, was similarly increased after differentiation in both strains; however, the expression of Fasn and Acaca genes was lower in Dom than in Sub. Glut4 expression was significantly induced in the differentiated culture of Dom but not in Sub. Interestingly, Lep and Adipoq presented an opposite expression pattern: Lep was higher in Sub adipocytes, while Adipoq was higher in Dom. Finally, Ucp1 mRNA expression was stimulated in differentiated adipocytes from Dom mice, while no induction of this gene was observed in adipocytes derived from SVF of Sub mice.

The effect of HFD feeding on adipogenesis was measured in 11-12-weeks-old mice. SVF proliferation of iWAT was stimulated by HFD in Dom, while eWAT proliferation was increased in HFD-fed Sub (Fig. 5F). ORO staining was higher in culture derived from eWAT of HFD-fed Sub than in Dom-derived culture (Fig. 6G). Finally, adiponectin was measured in the supernatant of the differentiated cultures (Fig. 6H). Levels of adiponectin were higher in the cultures obtained from SVF of iWAT than in SVF of eWAT. In iWAT cultures, adiponectin levels were reduced when SVF was isolated from HFD-fed Sub, whereas in eWAT cultures, adiponectin levels were reduced when SVF was isolated from Sub mice, regardless of diet.

Sub and Dom mice had different abundance of adipocytes’ progenitors

The proportion of preadipocyte stem cells in iWAT from Sub and Dom mice was assessed by flow cytometry. Cells were identified by negative staining for CD45 (an immune cell marker) and CD31 (an endothelial cell marker) and positive staining for Ly6, CD29, CD34, and CD24, all markers of preadipocytes at different levels of commitment. Dom mice exhibited a higher proportion of CD45⁻CD31⁻ cells compared to Sub mice (Fig. 7A), indicating the presence of a greater population of cells encompassing preadipocytes at different stages of commitment in Dom. An HFD increased the proportion of this population in both groups; however, levels remained higher in Dom mice. Within this parent population, the proportion of Ly6⁺CD29⁺ cells were similarly increased by HFD in both Sub and Dom mice (Fig. 7B). A comparable pattern was observed for CD34⁺CD24⁻ cells (Fig. 7C), whereas HFD reduced the relative abundance of CD34⁺CD24⁺ cells in both strains (Fig. 7D). Therefore, while Dom mice have a higher number of adipocyte progenitors, there is no difference between strains in terms of the relative abundance of the distinct progenitor subpopulations.

Paroxetine (Pxt), given to STD-fed Sub mice, significantly restored most behavioral alterations of this strain (Fig. 8A). Exploratory behavior, swimming time, and sociability, measured in the HBT, FST, and TCT, respectively, were all improved, while time in the center, measured in the OF test, was not affected. Expression of pro-adipogenic genes was elevated in the iWAT of Pxt-treated Sub mice, as well as the expression of Glut4, AdipoQ, and UCP1, all are markers of a functional and a healthy adipose tissue (Fig. 8B). SVF isolated from Pxt-treated Sub mice (11-12 weeks old) successfully differentiated into mature adipocytes, while SVF isolated from control Sub mice failed to achieve the morphology of differentiated adipocytes (Fig. 8C). SVF differentiation in Pxt-treated mice was comparable to the effect of pioglitazone, a known inducer of adipogenesis [51], which was used as a positive control (Fig. 8C). Pxt also enhanced basal and insulin-induced Akt phosphorylation in adipose tissue explants, indicating improved insulin sensitivity (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the physiological and molecular differences between adipose tissues of mice with contrasting stress response profiles. Our findings reveal inherent differences in adipose tissue functionality among the strains. We propose that stress vulnerability impairs adipogenic capacity, limiting healthy adipose expansion and thereby promoting insulin resistance under obesogenic conditions. Moreover, our study demonstrated that restoring behavioral impairments improved adipogenesis and other characteristics of adipose tissue function.

Differences in stress coping induce variations in adipose insulin sensitivity, adipokine expression, BCAA metabolism, and adipogenesis. These differences exist even under STD feeding and may predispose Sub mice to different severities of metabolic alterations upon HFD regimen. On the other hand, stress-resilient Dom mice showed a healthy adipose function and are protected from HFD-induced glucose intolerance, in line with our previous work [13]. In Dom mice, iWAT preserved a better insulin responsiveness under HFD than eWAT, suggesting that the functionality of iWAT is better correlated with the metabolic health. This observation is in line with the vast number of publications, demonstrating the protective effects of healthy iWAT on insulin sensitivity and other cardiometabolic disorders in comparison to eWAT [52, 53].

Interestingly, while Akt phosphorylation was reduced in Sub mice, phosphorylation of PRAS40, a regulatory component of mTORC1, was elevated. While mTORC1 activity is necessary to stimulate protein synthesis and cell growth, its hyperactivation is linked to insulin resistance [54]. Insulin-independent activation of mTORC1 can be driven by elevated levels of BCAA. One of the most robust and consistent changes in obesity is a marked decrease in adipose tissue expression of genes involved in BCAA catabolism, leading to elevated levels of these amino acids and insulin resistance [55, 56]. In line with this, our data demonstrated that HFD repressed the expression of genes encoding BCAA catabolic enzymes in eWAT of both Sub and Dom mice, supporting the conclusion that eWAT function is similar between the strains, and negatively affected by the obesogenic diet. In contrast, expression of genes encoding BCAA catabolic enzymes was increased in the iWAT of Dom mice in response to HFD, suggesting enhanced disposal of these amino acids. While BCAA levels were lower in Sub mice at baseline, they increased in response to HFD, whereas remained unchanged in Dom. These results are consistent with the body weight and fasting glucose profiles of the strains under STD and HFD conditions, showing lower values in Sub at baseline, and a greater increase in body weight and glucose in response to HFD than observed in Dom [13]. While lower BCAA levels at baseline may reflect a distinct metabolic set point, the increase in their levels following HFD suggests reduced metabolic flexibility and impaired capacity to regulate BCAA catabolism under conditions of stress vulnerability. Thus, the results are consistent with a compensatory increase in BCAA catabolism occurs in iWAT of Dom mice, potentially contributing to their protection against HFD-induced insulin resistance. However, pathway enrichment analysis of the RNA-seq data did not identify BCAA metabolism as one of the top differentially regulated pathways. Therefore, the findings related to BCAA metabolism are interpreted as secondary to adipogenic potential, which appears to be the major driver of adipocyte health in this model.

In addition to the difference in insulin sensitivity and BCAA metabolism, the changes between the strains in adipose hypertrophy vs. hyperplasia, and leptin/adiponectin gene expression ratio, represent the dysfunctional adipose tissue of HFD/Sub compared to Dom [57]. Therefore, while both iWAT and eWAT depots of HFD-fed Sub mice appeared metabolically unhealthy, Dom mice maintained functional adipose tissue, particularly in iWAT. Notably, Adipoq mRNA expression was higher in iWAT of Dom compared to Sub mice even under STD feeding, indicating that adiponectin may contribute to their resistance to HFD-related metabolic alterations.

Identifying the changes in gene expression presented in WAT under STD conditions might uncover the mechanisms predisposing Sub mice to obesity and its comorbidities. A lower activation of adipogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid metabolism pathways was observed in iWAT of STD-Sub compared to Dom mice, whereas HFD strongly stimulated these pathways in the Sub population. The changes in gene expression were accompanied by reduced adipogenesis in adipocyte culture generated from the SVF of both iWAT and eWAT of Sub mice. Interestingly, because iWAT and eWAT displayed a similar expression pattern of genes involved in the acyl-CoA biosynthesis pathway, this pathway may represent a key regulator of adipogenic potential and could contribute to the strain-specific differences observed. Notably, inhibition or loss-of-function of several acyl-CoA pathway genes has been shown to protect mice from obesity-induced metabolic alterations [58–61]. supporting the notion that lower activity of this pathway may underlie the healthier metabolic phenotype of the Dom mice.

Adipogenesis is a key process, enabling adipocytes hyperplasia and a healthy WAT expansion for safe storage of surplus energy [62]. While adipocyte hyperplasia is correlated with improved metabolic health, hypertrophy-driven WAT expansion leads to WAT dysfunction, ectopic lipid accumulation in the liver, muscle, and other tissues, and poor metabolic health [63–65]. The capacity to induce adipogenesis on demand acts as a protective mechanism against adipocyte hypertrophy and is closely associated with cellular insulin sensitivity [66, 67]. The association between adipocyte hypertrophy and insulin resistance is multifaceted, involving both indirect and direct mechanisms. Indirectly, hypertrophic adipocytes secrete elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which impair insulin signaling in multiple cell types, including adipocytes themselves [68]. Directly, hypertrophy is associated with defects in GLUT4 trafficking, likely due to disruptions in cortical actin organization within the adipocyte [50].

Antidepressant treatment was associated with parallel improvements in behavioral indices, adipogenic capacity, and insulin signaling, suggesting that modulation of stress-related pathways may influence adipose tissue function. Furthermore, the central role of adipose tissue dysfunction in the development of MUO in the Sub mice, and the beneficial effect of an elevated adipogenic potential on insulin sensitivity, was also supported by the improvement in insulin sensitivity observed in response to PPARgamma activation in Sub mice. It was shown before that PPARgamma agonist enhances insulin-induced glucose uptake in a cell-size dependent manner, showing a higher glucose transport specifically in small, newly differentiated adipocytes[50]. Together, these findings are consistent with a functional association between adipogenic capacity and insulin sensitivity within adipose tissue and support the notion that these processes are mechanistically linked. However, despite these improvements at the tissue level, glucose tolerance was not enhanced in pioglitazone-treated Sub mice. This may reflect alterations in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, which has been reported to be affected by pioglitazone [69, 70], as well as the limited contribution of adipose tissue to whole-body glucose disposal [71]. Indeed, while improvements in adipose insulin sensitivity are likely to influence systemic metabolism, glucose homeostasis is largely determined by additional tissues, particularly skeletal muscle, liver, and pancreatic β-cells. Thus, the dissociation between improved adipose tissue function and unchanged glucose tolerance suggests that adipose-specific adaptations alone may be insufficient to normalize systemic glucose handling. It should also be noted that other aspects of adipose insulin action, such as insulin-mediated suppression of lipolysis, were not assessed in the current study and may further contribute to whole-body metabolic regulation. These parameters should be addressed in future investigations.

Upon HFD feeding, stress resilience selectively induced adipogenesis of iWAT, while stress-vulnerability induced eWAT adipogenesis. These results indicate that stress-coping abilities modulate SVF characteristics. The capacity to induce adipogenesis depends on adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), which are differentiated into preadipocytes and subsequently mature adipocytes [72]. ADSCs are heterogenous and composed of several distinct subpopulations [72–74] with distinct proliferation and differentiation capacity, and also direct the differentiation fate of the tissue, either toward white or beige adipocytes [75]. Our data demonstrates that stress-coping abilities affect adipose progenitor cell (ADPC) populations, with a lower abundance of preadipocyte precursors in stress-vulnerable mice. These findings suggest that the reduced adipogenic potential in Sub mice may be partly attributable to a lower abundance of adipocyte progenitors. However, given the observed differences in gene expression in differentiated adipocytes, alterations in the differentiation program are also likely to contribute. Future studies might focus on the effects of various stress stimuli and stress manipulation strategies on specific subpopulations of ADPC and address the mechanisms mediating the effect of stress coping behavior on the ADSC population.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the differences in SVF composition and adipogenic potential between Sub and Dom mice, including differential glucocorticoid responsiveness in adipose tissue and variations in sympathetic tone [76–79]. Furthermore, our previous study demonstrated distinct gut microbiome profiles between Sub and Dom mice [80]. Notably, fecal microbiota transplantation from Sub and Dom mice into germ-free mice induced different variations in adipokine levels [80]. Together, these findings suggest that gut microbiome play a role in regulating adipose function, and thus, the potential involvement of the gut microbiome in the regulation of adipogenesis should be further investigated in future studies.

This study also demonstrated different patterns of activation of pro-inflammatory pathways in mice with contrasting stress responses. This aspect should be investigated thoroughly in future studies. Interestingly, several studies reported an interaction between ADPC and adipose tissue inflammation [81–83]. This interaction may explain the negative correlation observed between the adipogenic and proinflammatory pathways. The biological relevance of the reduced inflammatory signaling in iWAT of HFD-fed Sub mice, a phenomenon which was also observed in our previous publication [13], and its relevance to the susceptibility to metabolic disorders should be elucidated as well.

In summary, these findings suggest that stress-coping capacity may contribute to adipose tissue plasticity and metabolic resilience, with adipogenesis representing a potential mechanistic link between behavioral phenotype and metabolic health. The association between stress-related traits and reduced adipogenic potential appears to involve both alterations in progenitor cell abundance and differentiation capacity, although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully defined. Further studies are required to determine whether targeting these pathways can improve adipose tissue function and metabolic outcomes in susceptible individuals.
