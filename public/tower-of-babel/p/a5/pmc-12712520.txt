Modified Lower Trapezius Tendon Transfer Using an Achilles Tendon–Bone Allograft With Acellular Dermal Matrix Augmentation for Irreparable Posterior-Superior Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Chang Hee Baek, Bo Taek Kim, Jung Gon Kim, Chaemoon Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLower trapezius (LT) transfer has shown promising clinical outcomes in the treatment of posterior-superior rotator cuff tears. Numerous surgical modifications of LT transfer have been developed, including variations in interpositional graft choices, fixation methods, and graft anastomosis techniques. The most widely adopted method involves the use of an Achilles tendon allograft to bridge the gap between the humerus and the LT. Unlike conventional tendon-only grafts, the technique described in this article retains the calcaneal bone block to facilitate bone-to-bone healing at the humeral footprint. The bony portion is shaped to approximately 5 mm in thickness for optimal fit and is secured using a triple-row suture anchor configuration. To enhance the subacromial spacer effect and reduce friction on the bursal side, an acellular dermal matrix patch is applied over the graft. This technical note outlines a step-by-step approach to arthroscopically assisted LT transfer using an Achilles tendon–bone allograft augmented with an acellular dermal matrix patch for the treatment of irreparable posterior-superior rotator cuff tears.

Lower trapezius (LT) transfer has become a reliable surgical option for treating irreparable posterior-superior rotator cuff tears, owing to its anatomic alignment with the native infraspinatus and its capacity to restore external rotation and overall shoulder function.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Among various interpositional graft options, the Achilles tendon allograft is most commonly used to bridge the gap between the LT and the humerus.2,6, 7, 8 Conventional LT techniques typically remove the calcaneal bone portion of the allograft, resulting in a tendon-to-bone interface at the humeral insertion.2,6 Yet, tendon-to-bone healing could be biologically less favorable than bone-to-bone healing—particularly in patients with poor bone quality at the humeral footprint.9

Baek et al.10 recently reported 4 cases of revision LT transfer after failure of the primary procedure. In 3 of these cases, failure was attributed to retear at the humeral footprint, highlighting the critical importance of secure graft incorporation on the humeral side to ensure long-term structural integrity. To address these limitations, this technical note presents a modified arthroscopically assisted LT transfer technique using an Achilles tendon–bone allograft. This approach retains the calcaneal bone block, which is reshaped to fit the humeral footprint and facilitate bone-to-bone healing. Additionally, an acellular dermal matrix (ADM) patch is applied over the graft to reinforce the suture construct, increase graft volume, and reduce bursal-side friction.

This technique is indicated in cases in which bone-to-bone healing is preferred over tendon-to-bone healing, particularly in patients with compromised humeral footprint quality. The indications are as follows: irreparable posterior-superior rotator cuff tear, characterized by severe tendon retraction (stage 3 per Patte classification11) and advanced fatty infiltration (grade 3 or 4 per Goutallier classification12); minimal glenohumeral arthritis (grade <3 per Hamada classification13); poor bone stock or deficient footprint at the greater tuberosity; and revision surgery after failed rotator cuff repair, failed LT transfer, or failed superior capsular reconstruction. The contraindications are as follows: active shoulder infection, fixed anterosuperior escape of the humeral head, advanced glenohumeral arthritis (grade ≥3 per Hamada classification13), neurologic disorders affecting scapular stability or muscle control, and inability to adhere to postoperative rehabilitation protocols.

Surgical Technique

A step-by-step surgical demonstration is provided in Video 1.

Patient Positioning and Arthroscopic Setup

Under general anesthesia, the patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position. Standard posterior, lateral, anterior, and posterolateral arthroscopic portals are established.

The footprint of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus on the greater tuberosity is then debrided, decorticated, and medialized by approximately 5 to 10 mm to create a flat, bleeding surface (Fig 1A). The prepared footprint measures approximately 3 cm in the medial-lateral direction and 2 cm in the anterior-posterior direction, providing an optimal bed for bone-to-bone healing (Fig 1B). A 5-mm suture anchor (Healicoil; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is placed at the midpoint of the footprint, near the articular margin (Fig 1C). For side-to-side suturing, one suture limb is passed through any remaining posterior cuff tissue (such as infraspinatus or teres minor), whereas the other limb is preserved for subsequent graft fixation (Fig 1D).

(A, B) The footprint (white asterisk) is prepared with an arthroscopic burr and measures approximately 3 cm in the medial-lateral direction and 2 cm in the anterior-posterior direction. (C) A triple-loaded suture anchor (yellow asterisk) is placed at the midpoint of the prepared footprint. (D) One suture limb is threaded using an arthroscopic lasso suture loaded with a wire loop (white arrow) for side-to-side suturing with the remnant posterior rotator cuff (red asterisk).

Graft Preparation

An Achilles tendon allograft with an attached calcaneal bone block is selected (Fig 2A). The bony portion is trimmed into a 3-cm × 2-cm rectangular shape, matching the dimensions of the prepared humeral footprint (Fig 2B). The bone block is uniformly shaped to a thickness of approximately 5 mm to ensure a snug and stable fit onto the footprint. Two bone holes are drilled centrally within the bone block to allow fixation of the ADM patch to the bone. Additionally, 4 bone holes are created in the proximal portion of the bone block, serving as passageways for suture fixation during graft attachment to the humerus (Fig 2C). The ADM patch (SureDerm; Hans Biomed, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) is sutured to the superficial surface of the Achilles tendon using two No. 2 nonabsorbable sutures in a Krackow configuration (Fig 2D).

Achilles tendon–bone allograft augmented with acellular dermal matrix (ADM) patch. (A) Bone section of Achilles tendon–bone allograft (white asterisk) marked with dimensions of 3 cm × 2 cm. (B) A mini-saw (yellow arrow) is used to cut the bone at the marked location. (C) Two bone holes (red arrows) are drilled for fixation of the ADM patch to the bone, and 4 bone holes (white arrows) are created for suture fixation. (D) Final prepared Achilles tendon–bone allograft with ADM patch (red asterisk).

LT Harvest

A 5- to 6-cm horizontal incision is made just inferior to the medial third of the scapular spine. After dissection through the subcutaneous tissue and removal of the overlying fat pad, the lateral border of the LT muscle is identified. The LT tendon is carefully detached from the scapular spine, with care taken to avoid injury to the spinal accessory nerve. A No. 2 free traction suture is placed at the distal end of the tendon to facilitate subsequent graft attachment (Fig 3).

Lower trapezius harvest in right shoulder in the lateral debubitus position. (A) Prepared lower trapezius muscle (white asterisk). (B) Underlying fascia tissue (white asterisk) of lower trapezius muscle when reflected.

Graft Delivery and Fixation

An anteroinferior portal is established approximately 2.0 cm anterior and 1.5 cm inferior to the standard lateral portal. All suture limbs are retrieved through this portal. A soft-tissue tunnel is then created through the infraspinatus fascia for graft passage. With a long suture retriever, the prepared graft is introduced through the anteroinferior portal toward the soft-tissue tunnel and advanced into the subacromial space. For suture preparation, the distal portion of the prepared graft should remain outside of the shoulder joint suture (Fig 4A). Outside the joint, the suture limbs are passed through the 4 predrilled bone holes in the graft for humeral fixation. The remaining suture limb from the side-to-side suture is passed through the posterior and medial aspects of the tendinous portion of the graft for the side-to-side repair. The graft is then reinserted into the subacromial space under arthroscopic guidance and carefully positioned over the prepared humeral footprint, ensuring maximum bone-to-bone contact. All sutures are securely tied to compress the graft firmly onto the bone.

A suture-bridge construct is created using three 5.5-mm knotless anchors (SwiveLock anchor; Arthrex, Naples, FL). The first anchor, preloaded with the suture limbs from the two bone-fixation sutures and the anterior traction sutures from the graft, is placed in the anterolateral portion of the greater tuberosity (Fig 4B). The second anchor, loaded with the remaining suture limbs from the bone-fixation sutures and posterior traction sutures, is placed posterolaterally (Fig 4C). The remaining suture limbs are secured with a third anchor positioned anterior to the first anchor (Fig 4D).

Graft-to-LT Anastomosis

With the shoulder positioned at 45° of abduction and 60° of external rotation, the proximal portion of the Achilles tendon graft is wrapped securely around the distal LT muscle. A No. 2 free suture is applied using a Krackow configuration both anteriorly and posteriorly to the lower border of the LT muscle to achieve a strong, stable muscle-to-tendon anastomosis (Fig 5). Finally, the shoulder is taken through a full range of motion to confirm appropriate tension and mobility, and the surgical wounds are closed in layers.

Lower trapezius anastomosis with graft in right shoulder. (A) The proximal part of the Achilles tendon–bone allograft (white asterisk) is fixed with a free suture along the lower border of the lower trapezius. (B) Final view after securing lower trapezius with graft (white asterisk).

Postoperative Care

The arm is immobilized in a neutral rotation brace for the first 4 weeks after surgery. During this period, continuous passive motion therapy is initiated to promote early joint mobility. From weeks 4 to 8 postoperatively, patients begin active-assisted range-of-motion exercises, followed by progressive strengthening exercises. Forceful or high-impact activities are discouraged for at least 12 weeks after surgery to ensure proper graft healing and minimize the risk of complications.

Discussion

A critical challenge in LT transfer remains achieving secure graft healing at the humeral footprint. Baek et al.10 recently reported 4 cases of revision LT transfer after failed primary procedures, with 3 of these cases showing retear of the tendon graft attributed to poor healing at the humeral attachment. This underscores the importance of bone-to-bone healing in ensuring long-term structural success.

Ek et al.14 described an arthroscopic-assisted LT transfer using an Achilles tendon–bone allograft with a bone tunnel in the humerus. Although this inlay method provides strong fixation and avoids the killer-turn phenomenon, it requires sacrificing part of the humeral bone and carries risks such as bone block fragmentation during insertion and incomplete docking. The current technique differs by using an onlay fixation method, wherein the calcaneal bone block is reshaped and placed onto a decorticated, medialized greater tuberosity, secured with a double-row suture-bridge construct. A similar study has addressed LT transfer using a tendon-bone construct; however, it was performed in the context of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. Eckers et al.15 reported a technique using an Achilles tendon–bone allograft for proximal humeral reconstruction during reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. In their technique, the calcaneal bone block was shaped to match a proximal humeral implant, with the graft secured using locking sutures.

LT transfer using an Achilles tendon–bone allograft augmented with ADM may be a reliable technique for treating irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, particularly in cases in which bone-to-bone healing is preferred over tendon-to-bone healing, such as patients with compromised humeral footprint quality.
