Systematic review and meta-analysis of predictive accuracy of prognostic models for poor treatment outcome of drug resistance tuberculosis
Authors: Denekew Tenaw Anley, Melaku Ashagrie Belete, Ermias Sisay Chanie, Anteneh Mengist Dessie, Melkamu Aderajew Zemene, Asaye Alamneh Gebeyehu, Ermiyas Alemayehu, Natnael Moges, Natnael Kebede, Sintayehu Simie Tsega, Zufan Alamrie Asmare, Rahel Mulatie Anteneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) remains a global health challenge, with drug-resistant (DR) forms of the disease posing a significant threat to effective disease control. Prognostic models are crucial tools that aid in clinical decision-making by predicting the likelihood of adverse treatment outcomes. Evaluating the accuracy of these models is essential to ascertain their reliability and effectiveness in guiding healthcare interventions and optimizing patient outcomes. This study aimed to systematically review and meta-analyze the predictive accuracy of the DR-TB poor treatment outcome prediction models. A systematic search was conducted in four databases (Scopus, Embase, PuBMed, and HINARI) to identify studies based on Population, Index model, Comparator, Outcome, Timing, and Setting (PICOTS) approach until May 20, 2023. We extracted data using the CHARMS checklist and appraised risk of bias using PROBAST tool. Discrimination and calibration performance were meta-analyzed when appropriate. A total of 11 studies with 13 models were found to be eligible for this study. The cohort sizes vary from 102 to 2,441 participants involved in model development, while the number of events per parameter (EPP) in the model remains consistently below 10 across the majority of studies. The individual studies underwent assessment for Risk of Bias (ROB) and Applicability using PROBAST. The primary reason for the high concern of ROB was predominantly within the Analysis domain. The pooled Area Under the Curve (AUC) from the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis of the eligible models was 0.77 (95% CI 0.73, 0.81), which is within an acceptable range. The poor treatment outcomes of DR-TB prediction models manifest in various forms, characterized by diverse predictors and methodological approaches. While the pooled predictive accuracy of eligible models is deemed acceptable, concerns arise regarding a higher risk of bias, notably within the analysis domain, and a deficiency in external validation. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a global health challenge, with drug-resistant (DR) forms of the disease posing a significant threat to effective disease control1–3. Among DR-TB patients, poor treatment outcomes, characterized by treatment failure, relapse, or death, present critical concerns for both individual patient care and public health efforts4,5.

Despite advancements in TB management, the emergence of DR strains, particularly multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB), has heightened the complexity of TB treatment1,6,7. Managing DR-TB demands not only an understanding of the drug resistance patterns, but also an accurate assessment of the factors influencing treatment outcomes of patients5,8–10. The problem is unacceptably high in developing countries like Ethiopia. This has multi-dimensional consequences on the lives of affected individuals, productivity of the nation and over all development of countries in general8,11–13.

Hence, prognostic models, derived from various clinical and demographic variables, offer a direction for predicting which patients are at a higher risk of experiencing poor treatment outcomes. These models, designed to predict the likelihood of poor treatment outcomes, play a crucial role in guiding clinical decision-making and resource allocation. However, the landscape of these models and their predictive accuracy in the context of DR-TB remains a topic of ongoing research and scrutiny14.

The need for a systematic evaluation of externally validated prognostic models in the realm of DR-TB arises from the heterogeneity of patient populations and healthcare settings. Externally validated models, tested in populations different from those in which they were originally developed, provide a measure of generalizability and applicability across diverse settings. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize existing evidence on the predictive accuracy of externally validated prognostic models for poor treatment outcomes among DR-TB patients.

Understanding the performance of prognostic models is essential for healthcare providers, policymakers, and researchers working towards improved TB outcomes. By critically appraising the existing literature and conducting a meta-analysis, this study seeks to identify the strengths and limitations of prognostic models. The findings are expected to inform clinical practice, guide the development of more robust models, and contribute to the ongoing efforts to enhance the precision of risk stratification and treatment optimization for individuals facing the challenges of DRTB.

Methods

Review question and search strategies

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined prediction models for poor treatment outcome of DR-TB. The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number of CRD42024505439. Population, Index prediction model, Comparator, Outcome, Timing, and Setting (PICOTS) approach was used for prediction studies to declare eligibility15; P (drug resistance tuberculosis patients, patients having TB bacteria that are resistant to at least one of the most effective TB medicines used in treatment regimens), I (index poor treatment outcome prediction models), C (not applicable), O (treatment failure or death or lost follow up), T (prediction of poor outcome during treatment), and S (Individualized risk stratification for clinical set up).

A systematic literature search was conducted on PubMed, Embase, HINARI, and Scopus databases without the restriction of publication years. The last search was performed and updated on May 10, 2024. Google Scholar grey literature search was conducted and the reference lists of all included studies were explored. The search strategies were developed following search filters for prognostic and diagnostic studies16. The Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) and free text words were used to locate potential studies. Boolean operators (AND, OR, and NOT) and truncation were used to combine the search strategies.

The final result was reported in accordance with Transparent reporting of multivariable prediction models for individual prognosis or diagnosis: checklist for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (TRIPOD-SRMA) proposed by Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group and other researchers17.

Eligibility identification

Poor treatment outcome prediction studies including both drug resistance and susceptible tuberculosis patients were included. However, we excluded studies that focus on the effect of particular factors or variables, such as studies examining the prognostic effect of a factor or meta-analyses summarizing the interaction between a factor and treatment effect. It also didn’t cover reviews of prediction model impact studies, such as comparative studies (eg, randomized trials) evaluating the downstream consequences of using a prediction model in practice compared with not using a model, because these studies are more akin to intervention reviews.

Study selection

Once the literature search was completed, all identified articles were imported into an EndNote Library and all the duplicates were removed. Three independent reviewers (DTA, EA and AMD) screened the studies by the title and abstract and then review the full text of the papers based on the eligibility criteria. All potentially included studies were assessed for full text eligibility by two authors (ESC and MAZ). Differences were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (RMA). Data from the included studies was independently extracted by the same two investigators, and information was collected in a Microsoft Excel Spread sheet. The selection and exclusion of the studies were presented by using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA)−2020 flow diagram for the summary of the systematic review study selection process.

Data extraction

The extraction of data from included studies were done based on Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modeling Studies (CHARMS) Checklist18. Authors, publication year, source of data, outcome(s) to be predicted, candidate prognostic factors, sample size, model development, model discrimination performance, and calibration measures, and results including final multivariable models were extracted. Two reviewers (DTA and ZAA) independently extracted the data, and discrepancies were managed through discussion.

Assessment of methodological quality of the included articles

For evaluating the quality and applicability of prediction model studies; Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) was used19. The four domains that comprise PROBAST are participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. To aid in the organized evaluation of ROB—which is described as the occurrence of systematic distortions in estimates of model predictive performance due to flaws in research design, conduct, or analysis—these domains comprise a total of twenty signaling questions. The degree of certainty in the evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendation Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) technique. The GRADE approach has five different criteria’ (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias) to evaluate the certainty of evidence20.

Data synthesis

In conducting the meta-analysis of predictive performances of prediction models for DR-TB poor treatment outcomes, a systematic approach was employed to synthesize relevant studies. Comprehensive tables and graphs were utilized to organize and visually represent the included prediction models. Discrimination and calibration performances of the developed models were rigorously assessed and compared narratively. These models were described in terms of methodology, clinical utility, and transferability, nature of predictors, modelling method and presentation. Forest plots were utilized to visually represent the performance of various models, facilitating easy comparison. To assess discrimination ability, the AUROC was used, where values below 0.5 indicate no discrimination, 0.7 to 0.8 denote good/acceptable discrimination, 0.8 to 0.9 signify excellent discrimination, and values above 0.9 indicate outstanding discrimination performance21. Calibration, crucial for model reliability, was evaluated by examining both prediction slope and intercept. A well-calibrated model demonstrates a non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow p-value and/or a calibration slope close to one. These parameters were extracted to assess overall calibration performance. For studies that presented multiple models with the same set of predictors and outcomes, but different methods, the best-performing method was included in data synthesis. For studies presenting multiple models with different sets of predictors (i.e. baseline data vs. longitudinal data), the model developed using only baseline data was included. If studies developed multiple models for different outcomes or with different populations, all models were included.

A meta-analysis of the predictive performance of prediction models was conducted using Stata version 17. We estimated summary discriminative performance using a random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. I2 was used to quantify the proportion of variability in effect estimates attributable to heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses stratified by modelling methods, number of features, method of feature selection, method of model presentation, and year of publication were conducted. Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by EPP to address overfitting in prediction models for poor outcomes in DR-TB. This approach allowed us to account for the potential impact of overfitting by considering the ratio of events (poor outcomes) to the number of model parameters.

Results

Study selection

In this study, a comprehensive search across databases such as PubMed (1,149 articles), Scopus (141 articles), Embase (61 articles), and Hinari (125 articles), as well as Google Scholar (128 articles) and citation searching (5 articles), yielded a total of 1,609 articles. Following removal of duplicates and screening, 11 pertinent articles, comprising 13 reports, were deemed eligible for inclusion, providing a foundation for the study’s analysis and conclusions (Fig. 1). The search was conducted without filters and developed for PubMed, with adaptation to other databases accordingly (S1File).

Study characteristics

In this review cohort studies both retrospective and prospective were included. The publication years of the studies included in the analysis span 2016 to 2023, reflecting a recent focus on predicting poor treatment outcomes. Notably, most of these publications originate from middle and high-income countries22–30, with only two studies from Ethiopia, a developing country31,32. The primary data sources utilized across these studies predominantly comprise retrospective cohort data, with a minority drawing from registries such as the TB portal. The size of the cohorts varies, ranging from 102 to 2,441 participants involved in model development, while the number of EPP in the model remains consistently below 10 for the majority of studies. Among the total of 13 models predicting poor treatment outcome among DR-TB patients, four of them were development-only models23,26. Eight of the models were internally validated by bootstrapping, split half or cross validation methods22,24,25,27,28,30–33. One of the studies conducted in Portugal was externally validated25 (Table 1).

Predictors/features in the models

Prediction models for poor treatment outcomes of DR-TB encompass an array of features spanning from straightforward demographic variables to intricate genomic factors (Fig. 2).

Type of prognostic determinants involved in the development of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

The range of predictors utilized in the development of prediction models across the included studies varies significantly, spanning from 2 to 27. The timing of predictor ascertainment emerges as a pivotal factor in the prognostication of DR-TB patients. Notably, the majority of models outlined in the included studies were constructed based on prognostic determinants ascertainable at the time of diagnosis or patient enrollment, facilitating early preparedness and enabling timely decision-making processes23,25–28. However, a subset of studies deviated from this trend by incorporating predictors assessable both at diagnosis and throughout the course of treatment (9 studies) (Fig. 3). This nuanced approach underscores the importance of accounting for temporal dynamics in predictor selection, thereby enhancing the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of prognostic models tailored to DR-TB management.

Timing of predictor ascertainment for the development of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

Among the studies analyzed, a notable subset, comprising a total of 4 investigations, opted for the implementation of the List absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm in predictor selection for model development25,27,30,32. The LASSO algorithm was instrumental in mitigating the risk of overfitting, particularly in scenarios characterized by limited sample sizes and lower EPP ratios, which pose significant challenges to the robustness and generalizability of prediction models. Conversely, the remaining studies employed diverse methodologies for predictor selection, including forward or backward stepwise selection, Univariate Feature Selection (UFS), Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), and Information Gain and Ranking strategies (Table 2).

Modelling methods and presentation

The modeling methods employed across the studies included in this review exhibit a diverse array of approaches. While a majority of the investigations predominantly utilized logistic regression-based prediction modeling due to its established utility and interpretability, one notable model diverged from this trend, opting for a Cox regression-based approach. Additionally, a subset of studies demonstrated a departure from traditional regression techniques, instead leveraging a combination of machine learning algorithms. Notably, methodologies such as Random Forest, XGBoost, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Deep Learning utilizing Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), as well as variations of SVM such as linear and polynomial kernels, were utilized to develop predictive models22,24,28. This variety in modeling techniques reflects a concerted effort to explore and harness the strengths of diverse methodologies in elucidating the complex dynamics underlying DR-TB treatment outcomes (Fig. 4).

Modelling methods used in the development of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

The models described in the encompassing studies employed a range of presentation methods, reflecting the diverse approaches used to convey predictive insights. Notably, various studies presented their models through distinct modalities such as nomograms23,27,30,32, which offer a visually intuitive depiction of predictive probabilities and facilitate clinical decision-making. Others utilized risk scores to quantitatively assess the likelihood of poor treatment outcomes in DR-TB patients. Additionally, the incorporation of advanced techniques such as Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Random Forest22,24 further diversified the presentation landscape, underscoring the versatility and adaptability of these methodologies in encapsulating the complexity of predictive modeling in clinical contexts (Fig. 5).

Model presentation methods used in the development of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

Risk of bias and applicability assessment

The individual studies underwent assessment for ROB and Applicability using PROBAST. Some of the studies were identified as having a high concern for ROB23,24,26,28,30,31, while 3 studies raised concerns regarding Applicability25,26,28. The primary reason for the high concern of ROB was predominantly within the Analysis domain. Studies categorized as development only, internally validated with small sample sizes, and featuring less than 10 EPP – prone to overfitting – were flagged as having high ROB. Conversely, studies encompassing both development and validation, with larger sample sizes and EPP greater than 10, were deemed to have lower ROB concerns. Furthermore, the applicability assessment focused on the eligibility of the studies for our research. Three of the studies were labeled as having high concern of applicability mainly in the participants domain of applicability, because of the mix of drug susceptible tuberculosis patients with DR-TB patients in the prediction model development (Table 3).

Meta-analysis

In this systematic review and meta-analysis examining prediction models for poor treatment outcome of DR-TB, a total of 11 studies featuring 13 models were analyzed. One study by Bastos et al25. was excluded from the meta-analysis due to the absence of reported events. Additionally, two studies by Sauer et al28. and Asad et al24. were deemed outliers due to substantial methodological differences from the majority of the models and were therefore excluded from the meta analysis. The standard errors of the AUC for the eligible models were calculated, taking into account both the AUC values and the numbers of cases and non-cases. While all models reported AUCs, only a few studies provided data on sensitivity and specificity. The AUC values across individual studies ranged from 0.69 to 0.89. Inclusive of the two outliers, the pooled AUC for the 12 models was determined to be 0.77 (95% CI 0.74, 0.81). However, with evidence from the Eggers test (bias | −53.88974, P-value = 0.003) and highly asymmetric funnel plot; it was decided to exclude these outliers. Subsequently, pooling the remaining 10 homogeneous prediction models yielded a pooled AUC of 0.77 (95% CI 0.73, 0.81), almost mirroring the pooled AUC of the 12 models (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of the pooled discriminatory performance of models predicting MDR TB poor treatment outcome.

Publication bias and subgroup analysis

Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s test were conducted to assess the presence of publication bias. The funnel plot showed high symmetry (Fig. 7), suggesting minimal bias, while the Egger’s test yielded a p-value of 0.187, further indicating the absence of publication bias.

Funnel plot and Egger test of the 10 models included in the meta-analysis of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

In addition, subgroup analysis was conducted based on various factors including EPP (Range of pooled AUCs; 0.76 to 0.79), year of publication (Range of pooled AUCs; 0.72 to 0.82) (Fig. 8), feature/predictor types (Range of pooled AUCs; 0.69 to 0.83) and number of predictors involved (Range of pooled AUCs; 0.69 to 0.89) (Fig. 9).

Forest plot of the subgroup analysis by EPP and year of publication of MDR TB poor treatment outcome prediction models.

Discussion

The findings of this study shed light on the current landscape of prediction models for poor treatment outcomes (treatment failure, death, or loss to follow-up) among patients with DR-TB. Through a comprehensive review and meta-analysis of 11 pertinent articles spanning the years 2016 to 202322–32. This research highlights several key observations regarding the development and evaluation of these models. One notable trend observed is the recent emergence of prognostication as a significant research area among scholars, as evidenced by the majority of included studies being published in recent years. This indicates a growing recognition of the importance of predicting treatment outcomes in DR-TB patients and reflects the increasing efforts to address this pressing public health challenge.

A total of 11 studies comprising 13 models were deemed eligible for this study. The cohort sizes ranged from 102 to 2,441 participants used in model development. In most studies, the number of EPP was consistently below 10. Additionally, while most models were validated internally, external validation was not performed. The studies were assessed for ROB and Applicability using the PROBAST tool. High ROB concerns were primarily associated with the Analysis domain. The AUC from ROC analysis for the eligible models was 0.77 (95% CI: 0.73 to 0.81).

A critical aspect illuminated by this study is the heterogeneity among prediction models in terms of data sources, sample sizes, modeling methods, feature selection techniques, and performance metrics. Remarkably, despite the diversity, all studies adhere to similar definitions of the outcome of interest, elucidating a unified approach toward understanding and predicting poor treatment outcomes. While some models rely on retrospective cohort data, others utilize national tuberculosis registry data, highlighting the diverse sources of information available for model development. However, the predominance of models developed using small sample sizes raises concerns about overfitting and compromises the robustness and transferability of these models.

Furthermore, the study reveals the wide range of modeling algorithms employed, including regression-based methods and various forms of machine learning such as Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, and Deep Learning. Notably, models developed using machine learning techniques tend to incorporate a higher number of predictors and demonstrate superior performance, underscoring the potential of advanced computational methods in prognostic modeling.

The range of predictors utilized in the development of prediction models across the included studies varies significantly, spanning from a modest 2 to a complex 27. While models with only 2 predictors may offer simplicity and efficiency in usage, their limited scope raises concerns regarding robustness, as they may fail to capture the multifaceted nature of poor treatment outcomes DR-TB. Conversely, employing an extensive set of 27 features suggests a potential issue with feature selection, wherein the inclusion of redundant or irrelevant predictors could compromise model performance. To address these challenges, it is imperative to employ rigorous feature selection methods like LASSO, which can identify the most influential and easily ascertainable predictors while mitigating the risk of overfitting and enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy and transferability.

Models developed using logistic regression typically involve parsimonious selection of predictors, resulting in fewer variables. In these models, the contributions or odds ratios of each predictor are reported, providing insight into their individual impact on the outcome variable. Conversely, models developed using other machine learning methods often include more predictors. However, unlike logistic regression-based models, the effect sizes of each predictor are not explicitly reported in these models. Instead, these methods focus on predictive accuracy rather than the specific contribution of individual predictors, thus offering a different perspective on model development and interpretation.

Feature selection emerges as a crucial aspect of predictive analytics, with studies employing techniques like LASSO algorithm to identify potent predictors and mitigate the risk of overfitting. However, the variability in the number and complexity of predictors across models underscores the challenge of balancing model simplicity with predictive accuracy and comprehensiveness. Conversely, studies included in our review employed diverse methodologies for predictor selection, including forward or backward stepwise selection, UFS, RFE techniques, as well as IGR strategies. Each of these approaches offers unique advantages in identifying salient predictors while navigating the intricacies of model complexity and data dimensionality, contributing to the diverse landscape of predictive modeling methodologies in the realm of DR-TB research.

The timing of predictor ascertainment is identified as a pivotal factor in prognostication, with most models focusing on predictors ascertainable at the time of diagnosis or patient enrollment. This facilitates early preparedness and timely decision-making, essential for optimizing patient outcomes. However, the inclusion of predictors assessable throughout the treatment course in some models highlights the potential for dynamic prognostication and personalized intervention strategies22,24,31,32.

Importantly, the study emphasizes the importance of considering the transferability of prediction models, particularly in resource-limited settings. Models were found to be built incorporating highly invasive laboratory variables24,28,33. However, the inclusion of predictors that necessitate highly invasive procedures raises significant concerns regarding the transferability, practicability, and applicability of these models across various settings. Particularly, the incorporation of genomic predictors poses challenges, as models reliant on such data are less feasible for transfer or utilization in developing countries lacking adequate laboratory infrastructure. Thus, while genomic predictors may enhance predictive accuracy, their practical implementation in resource-constrained settings remains limited, emphasizing the need for broader consideration of predictors that align with the accessibility and capabilities of diverse healthcare environments. Besides, the evaluation of risk of bias and applicability reveals significant concerns, particularly regarding the validation of models, small sample sizes, and low EPP ratios. Addressing these biases is crucial for ensuring the reliability and validity of prediction models in real-world clinical practice.

Individual studies were evaluated for their performance, with two studies showing subpar discrimination performance with AUC values of 0.6523 and 0.6931. Conversely, the remaining models exhibited satisfactory to very good levels of discrimination. In terms of calibration, six models22,25,27,30–32 provided data on calibration plots, slope, or Hosmer–Lemeshow tests, all of which demonstrated good calibration. However, half of the models did not report calibration measures. Additionally, only four studies25,27,28,32 reported sensitivity and specificity, while clinical utility was addressed by just three included studies27,30,32. These findings suggest that there is limited reporting of performance assessment metrics in prediction models for poor treatment outcomes in DR-TB.

Initially, the review identified one study that did not report events/cases, and substantial methodological differences in two others, leading to their exclusion from the meta-analysis. After calculating the standard errors of the AUC and conducting subgroup analyses, the pooled AUC for the remaining 10 homogeneous models12,23,25–27,30,32,33 was determined to be 0.77 (95% CI 0.73, 0.81), indicative of good/acceptable discrimination power. Importantly, the assessment for publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot and Egger’s test yielded insignificant results, reinforcing the robustness and reliability of the findings of the included models.

Subgroup analysis was done based on various factors such as; EPP, number of predictors, year of publication, and type of predictors. Notably, the subgroup analysis by number of predictors showed a trend: as the number of predictors increases, model performance tends to increase. However, this shouldn’t be in expense of overfitting, a threat of prediction modelling. In addition, prediction models for identifying poor treatment outcomes among DR-TB patients that integrate genomic variables exhibit significantly improved discrimination performance.

However, this study was not without limitations. The systematic review assessing the predictive accuracy of prognostic models for poor treatment outcomes among drug-resistant tuberculosis patients confronts certain limitations inherent to the original models under scrutiny. One notable constraint stem from the heterogeneous prognostic predictors utilized in the development of current models, which can introduce variability and diminish the comparability of findings across studies. This would also affect the precision of pooled performance estimates, though random effects model was used to account for the variability between studies. Additionally, the absence of models tailored to ethnically diverse and low-income settings presents a notable weakness, as these populations may exhibit unique risk factors and disease dynamics that are not adequately captured by existing models. Furthermore, this review included models predicting different specific outcomes; death, treatment failure, or loss to follow up synthesized together under the broader category of poor treatment outcomes, even though the predictors and model performance may differ across these distinct endpoints. These limitations underscore the need for further research and refinement of prognostic models through external validation to ensure their applicability and effectiveness across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings.

In conclusion, these findings underscore the promising potential of prediction models in prognosticating poor treatment outcomes in DR-TB patients. This study is the first of its kind in reviewing and meta-analyzing the DR-TB poor treatment outcome prediction models and has brought a compressive and synthesized evidence of these prediction models for the scientific community and the clinicians. We meticulously delineated the included studies and employed a coherent methodology, enhancing comprehension of prediction models concerning poor treatment outcomes in DR-TB. Moreover, the abundance of models facilitated a meta-analysis of performance metrics, enabling comparisons across models via subgroup analysis. However, further research is warranted to validate and refine these models, considering factors such as sample size, methodology, and predictors, to enhance their reliability and applicability in clinical practice. By addressing these challenges, researchers can help develop more accurate, clinically relevant predictive tools, ultimately improving patient care and outcomes in the management of DR-TB.

Implications

One clear implication of this study is the potential to enhance clinical decision-making in the treatment of DR-TB patients. By synthesizing and analyzing existing prognostic models, this research provides healthcare practitioners with valuable insights into the predictive accuracy of these models and their limitations. Armed with this knowledge, clinicians can make more informed decisions about treatment strategies, potentially leading to improved outcomes for DR-TB patients. Additionally, the findings of this research could inform the development of more accurate and robust prognostic models, further advancing the field of TB treatment and care.
