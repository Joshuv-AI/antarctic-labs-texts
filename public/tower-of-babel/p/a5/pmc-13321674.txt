Artificial intelligence for environmental sustainability across sectors: a scoping review and evidence map for nursing and health policy
Authors: Fatma M Ibrahim, Ghada Shahrour, Abdulkarem Radhi Alanazi, Husain Mohammed Al Hakami, Asmaa Alsuwayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth systems are expected to reduce environmental impacts while preserving safe, continuous and equitable care. Artificial intelligence (AI) may help optimize energy, water, waste and circular-economy processes, but the evidence is dispersed across sectors and its relevance to nursing and health policy is uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sector scoping review and evidence map of studies published from January 2020 to April 2025. Searches were conducted in Scopus and Web of Science, with backward reference-list checking. Eligible studies evaluated an AI-enabled intervention, decision-support pathway or optimization process and reported a quantified environmental sustainability endpoint, sustainability-relevant operational proxy or clearly linked circular-economy process outcome. Outcomes were mapped as real-world direct environmental endpoints, simulated or modeled direct endpoints, indirect operational proxies or technical enabling metrics. Full platform-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary file 1 (Supplementary Table S1).ResultsAfter a tightened eligibility audit, 11 studies were included. No study evaluated AI-enabled sustainability interventions in routine healthcare delivery or nursing practice. Energy-management studies were concentrated in building heating, ventilation and air-conditioning control or microgrid scheduling. Indirect evidence included demand-response flexibility, operating-cost reductions, productivity or profit improvements, operational-efficiency gains, battery reuse-pack performance and bioleaching process optimization. Technical metrics such as forecasting or classification accuracy were treated as implementation-enabling evidence and were not interpreted as environmental effects.ConclusionsThe current evidence base is better characterized as a cross-sector evidence map than as a focused healthcare effectiveness review. Background

Climate change, biodiversity loss and resource depletion are major threats to population health and health-system resilience. Nursing services are increasingly affected as climate-related hazards disrupt care delivery, intensify demand and widen inequities [1, 2].

Health systems also contribute to environmental harm through energy use, procurement, transport, waste generation, water use and digital infrastructure. Health Care Without Harm and Arup estimated that healthcare’s climate footprint is equivalent to 4.4% of global net greenhouse gas emissions [3]. Reducing this footprint is therefore both a health-policy and care-delivery challenge.

Artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning, computer vision and selected computational-intelligence optimization methods, can analyze complex data streams and support adaptive decisions. These methods are being applied to building energy management, demand response, renewable-energy scheduling, irrigation, waste forecasting, route optimization, material sorting and battery or material recovery. However, the environmental costs of AI computation, hardware and data infrastructure also require attention [4, 5].

For nursing and health-system decision-makers, the key question is not only whether AI can improve sustainability indicators in any sector, but whether the evidence provides sufficiently direct and methodologically transparent information to inform nursing leadership, service delivery, education and policy. Because the available evidence is heterogeneous, cross-sectoral and largely indirect to healthcare, a scoping review and evidence map is used to clarify the nature, maturity and directness of the evidence and to distinguish evidence-derived findings from interpretive implications for nursing and health policy.

Aim and review questions

The aim was to map recent cross-sector evidence on AI-enabled applications that report measurable environmental sustainability outcomes or sustainability-relevant operational proxies, and to assess the directness of that evidence for health systems, nursing leadership and health policy.

The review questions were: (1) What AI-enabled sustainability applications have been evaluated from 2020 to 2025? (2) Which outcomes are direct environmental endpoints, indirect operational proxies or technical enabling metrics? (3) Which outcomes are measured in real-world settings versus simulated or modeled? (4) What can and cannot be inferred for nursing and health-system implementation?

Methods

Design and reporting framework

This study is reported as a cross-sector scoping review and evidence map in line with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), with relevant PRISMA 2020 flow-diagram elements used to present study selection [6, 7]. The broad search and heterogeneous outcomes were designed to map evidence maturity and gaps rather than to estimate intervention effectiveness. A protocol was not registered.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were empirical field, experimental, quasi-experimental, laboratory, simulation or modeling evaluations published from January 2020 to April 2025. Studies had to evaluate an AI-enabled intervention, controller, decision-support pathway, optimization process or algorithmically generated action that could plausibly change an environmental sustainability outcome.

Eligible sustainability domains were energy efficiency, emissions reduction, water conservation, waste management, recycling, circularity and resource or material recovery. To avoid over-interpreting technical performance as environmental benefit, studies were required to report at least one direct environmental endpoint or a sustainability-relevant proxy explicitly linked to resource use, emissions, waste reduction, circularity or recovery. Prediction-only or classification-only studies without a linked intervention pathway were not treated as environmental-effect evidence.

Reviews, commentaries, opinion articles, purely conceptual frameworks, studies without quantified outcomes, and studies using only conventional deterministic optimization or rule-based control were excluded. Borderline optimization methods were eligible only when they were explicitly framed as AI or computational intelligence (for example, ant colony optimization, genetic algorithms or particle swarm optimization) or when paired with learning/adaptive components.

Operational definition of AI-enabled applications

An application was considered AI-enabled when an AI or computational-intelligence method was central to producing an action, recommendation or automated decision. Eligible methods included supervised or unsupervised machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning, support vector machines, random forests, gradient boosting, self-organizing maps, computer vision and metaheuristic computational-intelligence optimization where the algorithm generated sustainability-relevant decisions such as set-points, schedules, routes, allocation plans, grouping or process parameters.

Outcome classification

Outcomes were classified before synthesis to prevent direct environmental endpoints, proxies and technical metrics from being rhetorically merged.

Level 1 outcomes were direct environmental endpoints, measured or modeled: energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions or explicitly converted emissions indicators, water volume, waste mass, waste diversion or recycling rate, material recovery yield or energy/water use per unit output. Monetary carbon-cost indicators were treated as Level 1 only when the primary study explicitly linked them to emissions calculations; otherwise, they were treated as Level 2 proxies.

Level 2 outcomes were indirect sustainability-relevant operational proxies: operating cost when linked to energy scheduling or resource use, demand-response flexibility, route distance or time, productivity or yield when paired with resource conservation, operational efficiency, reuse-pack performance and process-optimization indicators for recovery.

Level 3 outcomes were technical or implementation-enabling metrics: forecasting accuracy, classification accuracy, comfort or indoor-environmental-quality indicators, model stability and computational performance. Level 3 metrics were mapped as context only and were not interpreted as environmental effects.

Information sources and search strategy

Searches were conducted in Scopus and Web of Science for publications from January 2020 to April 2025, with backward reference-list checking of included reports and relevant reviews. Full platform-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary file 1 (Supplementary Table S1). Search terms combined AI concepts (for example, “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “reinforcement learning”, “computer vision”, “support vector machine”, “random forest”, “gradient boosting”, “self-organizing map”, “ant colony optimization”, “genetic algorithm” and “particle swarm optimization”) with sustainability concepts (for example, “energy”, “carbon”, “emissions”, “water”, “waste”, “recycling”, “circular economy”, “resource recovery” and “environmental sustainability”) and intervention concepts (for example, “control”, “scheduling”, “routing”, “optimization”, “decision support” and “process optimization”). Database syntax was adapted to each platform.

This evidence map was built from the archived de-duplicated screening set used in the original review process. The archived screening file preserved the verified unique-record set but not all platform-specific pre-de-duplication export counts; accordingly, the PRISMA flow diagram begins with unique records after de-duplication. We report all verified screening-stage counts and full-text exclusion categories and explicitly avoid claiming exhaustive coverage of healthcare or engineering literature. The term evidence map therefore denotes a bounded mapping exercise rather than proof that relevant studies do not exist outside the searched sources. A definitive health-system review should extend searching to MEDLINE/PubMed and CINAHL, and a definitive cross-sector engineering review should additionally consider IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library and Engineering Village.

Study selection and data charting

Records were screened by title and abstract followed by full-text assessment. Two reviewers independently screened and charted data using a piloted form, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Charted items included domain, sector, setting, study design, comparator or baseline, AI method, outcome type, reported result, measurement mode, healthcare directness and any reporting of the AI system’s own environmental footprint.

Eligibility audit and critical appraisal

Before synthesis, all full-text inclusion decisions were re-audited against the tightened AI and outcome definitions. Empirical non-randomized implementation studies were appraised using ROBINS-I where applicable [8]. Modeling, simulation and process-optimization studies were appraised narratively with attention to transparency of assumptions, baseline choice, sensitivity analyses, outcome definitions, external validity and whether the AI system’s own environmental footprint was reported. Because the purpose was evidence mapping rather than pooled effect estimation, critical appraisal was used to qualify interpretation rather than to exclude studies.

Synthesis

Findings were synthesized narratively by outcome level, measurement mode and sustainability domain. We did not conduct meta-analysis because outcomes, baselines, sectors and study designs were too heterogeneous. Reported numerical results are presented as primary-study percentage differences or reported values, not as comparable review-level effect sizes.

Use of AI-assisted editorial support

AI-assisted editorial support using ChatGPT (OpenAI) was used after study selection, data charting, eligibility audit, critical appraisal and synthesis to improve clarity, organization and language. The tool did not select studies, chart data, appraise evidence, generate analyses or draw independent conclusions. All AI-assisted wording was reviewed, verified and approved by the authors, who remain fully accountable for the manuscript.

Results

Study selection

After de-duplication, 54 unique records were screened by title and abstract. Thirty-four records were excluded at title/abstract stage and 20 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Following the tightened eligibility audit, 11 studies were included in the scoping synthesis. Full-text exclusions were categorized as no eligible quantified sustainability outcome or proxy (n = 3), prediction-only without an intervention pathway (n = 2), method not AI-enabled under the operational definition (n = 2), no original empirical or modeling evaluation (n = 1) and overlapping report (n = 1). The audit removed prediction/classification-only reports without an intervention pathway and conventional deterministic optimization studies that did not meet the revised AI-enabled definition. Because the archived screening log preserved the verified de-duplicated set rather than all platform-level export totals, the flow diagram reports unique records after de-duplication as the first fully auditable count. Characteristics of included evidence

The 11 included studies covered energy management and emissions-relevant optimization (n = 5), water conservation and irrigation (n = 2), waste management and circularity (n = 2), and battery reuse or material recovery (n = 2). No included study evaluated AI-enabled sustainability interventions in routine healthcare delivery, nursing services or clinical health-system operations. Therefore, nursing and health-policy implications are interpretive and hypothesis-generating, based on plausibility of transfer rather than direct healthcare evidence.

The evidence was methodologically heterogeneous. Most studies used simulations, modeling or laboratory/process optimization. Real-world implementation evidence was limited, most notably in building energy-control studies; however, only a subset of real-world implementation outcomes were direct environmental endpoints rather than energy-cost or comfort proxies. Few studies reported the environmental footprint of the AI system itself, such as training energy, inference energy, hardware requirements or data-centre impacts.

Critical appraisal and evidence credibility

Critical appraisal indicated an early-stage and indirect evidence base. Non-randomized implementation evidence was limited to building-control studies and was vulnerable to confounding, short evaluation periods and site-specific baseline choices. Simulation and modeling studies frequently depended on investigator-selected baselines, weather or usage scenarios, objective functions and conversion assumptions. Across studies, common limitations were limited external validation, limited uncertainty or sensitivity analysis, and minimal reporting of training/inference energy, hardware lifecycle or data-centre impacts. These appraisal findings were used to moderate interpretation and are reflected in the separation of L1, L2 and L3 outcomes. Synthesis by outcome category

Real-world direct environmental outcomes

Real-world direct environmental evidence was extremely limited. The clearest implementation evidence came from non-healthcare building-control studies in which AI-generated control actions were tested in occupied or real-building settings [9, 10]. However, not all real-world implementation outcomes were direct environmental endpoints: Kurte et al. [9] reported real-house energy-cost reductions, which were treated as an indirect proxy, whereas Silvestri et al. [10] provided building energy-control implementation evidence alongside indoor-temperature outcomes. No real-world study measured emissions, water, waste or material-recovery outcomes in a healthcare or nursing-service setting.

Simulated or modeled direct environmental outcomes

Simulated and modeled direct environmental endpoints were more common than measured endpoints. A reinforcement-learning whole-building HVAC study reported a maximum weekly energy reduction of up to 22% compared with a handcrafted baseline controller [11]. Precision-irrigation optimization reported 20–30% lower water consumption compared with fixed irrigation [12]. A municipal waste-route optimization case study modeled reductions in travel distance and a route-derived carbon-emission cost indicator compared with actual waste-collection operations [13]. These findings suggest plausible environmental gains, but their magnitude is highly dependent on baseline choice, model assumptions, sector context and implementation constraints.

Indirect operational proxies

Several studies reported indirect proxies rather than environmental endpoints. These included demand-response power shifts [11], simulated and real-house energy-cost reductions [9, 14, 15], profit or productivity gains in irrigation [12, 16], operational-efficiency gains in waste-management planning [17], improved reuse-pack capacity and cycle numbers [18], and optimization of bioleaching process conditions [19]. These outcomes may support sustainability pathways, but they should not be described as direct emissions, water, waste or recovery impacts unless validated conversion factors or measured environmental endpoints are provided.

Technical enabling metrics

Technical metrics were treated as contextual evidence. For example, forecasting accuracy in waste-management planning may support better resource allocation, but accuracy alone does not demonstrate reduced waste, emissions or resource use [17]. This distinction was central to the revised synthesis and prevents a classification or prediction metric from being interpreted as a sustainability outcome.

Directness to nursing and healthcare

No included study evaluated routine healthcare delivery, nursing services, hospital waste streams, clinical water use, sterilization, laundry, medical-device procurement, infection-prevention constraints or nursing workflow. As a result, the review cannot support claims that AI-enabled sustainability interventions are effective in nursing practice. Instead, it identifies cross-sector AI functions that could be tested in health-system infrastructure and operations. Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review and evidence map found that AI-enabled sustainability applications are being evaluated across building energy control, microgrid scheduling, irrigation, waste-management planning, route optimization, battery reuse and material-recovery processes. The evidence base is promising as a set of candidate technologies, but it is not a direct evidence base for nursing practice or routine healthcare sustainability interventions.

The strongest signals were in energy and water applications that reported quantified reductions against explicit baselines [11, 12] and in waste-route optimization that modeled reductions in distance, cost and a carbon-emission cost indicator [13]. The clearest real-world deployment evidence was in non-healthcare building control, but even there some reported outcomes were energy cost or comfort rather than direct environmental endpoints [9, 10]. Many studies used simulations or models, and many outcomes were indirect proxies such as operating cost, profit, productivity or process performance. The revised synthesis therefore separates measured direct endpoints, modeled direct endpoints, indirect proxies and technical metrics rather than presenting them as a single category of AI-enabled sustainability effects.

Implications for nursing leadership and service delivery

AI has not yet been shown to produce sustainability benefits in nursing practice. The defensible implication is that nurses should help design, govern and evaluate any health-system adoption of AI-enabled sustainability tools. Nurses are close to patient care, infection prevention, ward routines, equipment use and waste-handling practices. Their involvement can help ensure that environmental goals do not compromise patient safety, dignity, comfort, equity or staff workload.

The most plausible near-term applications are in infrastructure and operations rather than bedside clinical decision-making. Examples include HVAC control, demand response, water-use monitoring, non-infectious waste logistics, procurement analytics and device take-back programs. These applications should be evaluated as sociotechnical interventions because decisions about temperature, air changes, humidity, cleaning, waste segregation or device reuse can affect patients and nursing workflows.

Policy and governance implications

Health-system leaders should establish joint sustainability-digital governance with nursing leadership, facilities management, infection prevention, procurement, data science, occupational health and patient representatives. Governance should define acceptable use cases, safety thresholds, escalation pathways, accountability for model updates and procedures for suspending automated recommendations when they conflict with clinical requirements.

Procurement policies should require vendors to disclose model performance, data requirements, cybersecurity safeguards, human-override mechanisms and AI lifecycle impacts, including training or inference energy where feasible. This is consistent with Green AI principles and with the need to balance the environmental benefits of AI applications against the environmental costs of computation and hardware [4, 5].

Research implications

Future research should move from cross-sector modeling to pragmatic healthcare evaluations. Priority designs include implementation studies, interrupted time-series evaluations, controlled before-after studies and mixed-methods evaluations that measure environmental endpoints together with patient safety, infection-prevention compliance, indoor environmental quality, nursing workload, workflow disruption and equity. Studies should report baselines, time horizons, units, conversion factors and uncertainty, and should distinguish measured outcomes from simulated estimates. These implications should be treated as hypotheses for healthcare implementation research, not as evidence that AI-enabled sustainability interventions are effective in nursing practice.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this review is the explicit outcome classification, which prevents technical metrics and indirect proxies from being interpreted as direct environmental benefits. The revised evidence-map framing also makes the cross-sector and indirect nature of the evidence transparent.

Limitations are substantial. The search was limited to Scopus and Web of Science and was not updated beyond April 2025. MEDLINE/PubMed, CINAHL, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library and Engineering Village were not searched, so the absence of healthcare or nursing studies should be interpreted as absence from this audited evidence map rather than evidence that no such studies exist. The archived screening file preserved the de-duplicated screening set but not all platform-specific pre-de-duplication counts, so the flow diagram begins with unique records after de-duplication rather than original database yields. The review may therefore have missed healthcare, nursing or engineering studies indexed outside Scopus and Web of Science. Included studies were heterogeneous in sector, design, baseline and outcome definition, precluding meta-analysis. No included study evaluated routine healthcare delivery, and few reported AI lifecycle footprints. Consequently, implications for nursing and health policy are interpretive and should be treated as hypotheses for healthcare implementation research rather than as evidence of effectiveness in nursing practice.

Conclusions

AI-enabled environmental sustainability applications have been evaluated across several sectors, especially building energy management, irrigation, waste logistics and circular-economy processes. This bounded evidence map identifies candidate functions that may be relevant to health-system infrastructure and operations, but it does not establish effectiveness in nursing practice or routine healthcare delivery. A healthcare research agenda should test AI-enabled sustainability interventions in real clinical and operational settings while measuring environmental outcomes, patient safety, infection prevention, nursing workload, equity and the lifecycle environmental footprint of AI systems.
