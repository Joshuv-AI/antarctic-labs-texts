Protocol to quantify glioblastoma cell invasion and nuclear deformations in 3D hydrogels
Authors: Emma J van Bodegraven, Yekta Kesenci, Jean-Christophe Olivo-Marin, Aleix Boquet-Pujadas, Sandrine Etienne-Manneville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol to quantify 3D invasion and nuclear deformation from time-lapse imaging of U251 glioblastoma spheroids embedded in Matrigel. We describe steps for computing a time-resolved radial invasion index through epifluorescence imaging and extracting per-nucleus deformation from 3D+time data using spinning-disk confocal microscopy with an optical flow-based pipeline. This adaptable protocol links nuclear mechanics to invasion.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to van Bodegraven et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cancer, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Innovation

This protocol integrates, in a single workflow, quantitative 3D invasion assays with live, 3D+time measurements of nuclear deformation in invading cells. The invasion module combines a time-resolved Radial Invasion Index with standardized leader/follower counting normalized to spheroid size, providing a clear readout of collective and single-cell invasion dynamics.

Unlike end-point shape descriptors or 2D contour tracking, our optical flow–based analysis of nuclear deformations leverages the spatiotemporal pattern of fluorescent signal within the nucleus to compute internal deformation fields from live z-stacks. Each nucleus is assigned an overall deformation value and a deformation map with minimal manual input (∼1 min per nucleus; 10–20 s computation).

The pipeline is fully open-source and reproducible, from mask generation in Icy to automated statistics. Methodologically, the assay requires only common reagents (e.g., Matrigel, SiR-DNA, CellTracker) and widely available microscopes (epifluorescence for long-term invasion; spinning-disk for fast 3D imaging), making it accessible and scalable without microfabrication, bead embedding, or traction measurements. In principle, the approach should be broadly adaptable to any spheroid-forming, invasion-competent cell type and readily supports perturbation studies (e.g., drugs, ECM changes, genetic manipulations), positioning it as a practical platform to mechanistically connect nuclear mechanics with invasion in physiologically relevant 3D environments.

The protocol below describes the specific steps for the analysis of invasion of U251 glioblastoma cells. However, the method described here can in principle be applied to other cells able to form spheroids, invade 3D hydrogels, and endure fluorescent labeling with nuclear and cytoplasmic dyes.

Note: For optimal performance, the acquisition speed should be chosen such that the apparent motion between consecutive frames is on the order of a few pixels. Larger displacements can still be handled due to the multiresolution strategy used in the optical-flow estimation. However, the limits at which tracking fails depend on image texture and signal quality and therefore must be assessed empirically for example by inspecting the results after running the analysis.

Acquisition speed must also be balanced against signal-to-noise ratio to preserve sufficient image texture. Optical-flow methods rely on spatial intensity variations to estimate motion; therefore, images with richer structural heterogeneity generally yield more reliable deformation estimates. In contrast, perfectly uniform nuclear labeling would contain information primarily at the nuclear boundaries and little information within the interior. Maximizing informative texture (arising from genuine structural heterogeneity rather than noise) improves motion estimation. In practice, imaging conditions that allow visible tracking of internal nuclear features across frames typically provide sufficient texture for reliable analysis. Uniform global intensity changes, such as gradual photobleaching, are typically well tolerated because they affect the entire image similarly. Fluorescence imaging with confocal microscopy is often well suited to this analysis, as it enables relatively fast 3D acquisition together with optical sectioning that preserves spatial structure.

Image analysis for cell invasion quantification using ImageJ

Timing: 4–6 h

This section describes the manual quantification of cell invasion from phase-contrast live-cell acquisitions of 40 hours in two distinct ways (Figure 3A). First, we describe the analysis of a Radial Invasion Index (RII) at different time-points of invasion (8h, 16h, 32h, 40h) which gives both information on the overall capacity of the cells to invade as well as on the temporal dynamics of the invasion initiation (as assessed by the analysis of 8h and 16h time-points) and the overall capacity of invasion (as assessed by the analysis of 32h and 40h time-points). Second, we describe a manual quantification method of the number of leader and follower invading cells. In both analyses, we define invading cells as cells which have lost their lateral cell-cell contacts and have invaded out of the spheroid.

Optional: Before the start of the analysis, use a filename randomizer plugin to blind the images when comparing different experimental conditions.

Live-cell analysis: Quantification of deformation using fluorescent flow

Timing: 1 min of manual processing per nucleus + 10–20 s computation time per nucleus

This step describes how to quantify nuclear deformation in live cells from fluorescent time-lapse image sequences (2D + time or 3D + time z-stacks). Individual fluorescently labeled nuclei are cropped, segmented, and converted into binary masks for each frame. These image sequences are then processed using an optical flow–based deformation analysis pipeline to compute frame-to-frame displacement fields within the nucleus. From these measurements, a single scalar figure of merit is extracted for each nucleus, summarizing the magnitude of deformation it undergoes over the entire sequence (i.e., integrated over time and space). This scalar value enables direct quantitative comparison between cells and experimental conditions. Accurate segmentation, mask generation, and consistent file organization are critical to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the deformation metric.

Expected outcomes

This protocol yields two complementary datasets acquired in the same 3D hydrogel context: Equation 1 time-lapse invasion movies of spheroids and Equation 2 3D+time image sequences of fluorescently labeled nuclei in invading cells. Successful datasets of the invasion movies, show well-separated spheroids with non-overlapping invasion fronts and clearly discernible single-cell paths emanating from the spheroid core.

Users obtain a Radial Invasion Index (RII) at multiple time points which directly reflects cell invasive capability. For invasive conditions, RII typically increases over time and may plateau at later time points as invasion slows; a near-constant RII indicates low or no invasion.

From the nuclear deformation analysis, users obtain for each nucleus: (i) one overall deformation value that summarizes the magnitude of nuclear deformation over space and time across the 3D+time sequence, (ii) a per-nucleus 2D map highlighting where deformation is strongest within the z-stack, and (iii) condition-level summary plots comparing populations (box plots with associated p-values). Successful executions are characterized by stable nuclear segmentation across frames (no flickering or abrupt size jumps), smooth and spatially coherent deformation maps (continuous fields rather than speckled noise), and consistent trends across biological replicates. More deformable cells, or cells experiencing stronger mechanical constraints (e.g., during confined 3D invasion) are expected to display higher deformation metrics than less constrained or non-invasive cells. All analysis outputs are automatically collated by the pipeline into a Figures folder, including a spreadsheet (deformations.xlsx) listing per-nucleus deformation values, per-nucleus deformation maps (Mean_norms PDFs), and population-comparison figures (Population_1_vs_Population_2.pdf).

These deliverables support direct statistical comparisons between conditions and correlation analyses linking invasion metrics (RII, leader/follower counts) with nuclear deformation in the same assay. As a practical guideline for robust statistics, we recommend analyzing multiple spheroids per condition and tens of nuclei per condition; expected trends should be reproducible across independent experiments. If expected outcomes are not observed, common indicators include: interacting invasion fronts due to overly dense spheroid seeding (compromising RII and leader/follower scoring), excessive photobleaching or z-undersampling (leading to unstable segmentations and noisy deformation maps), or off-target labeling (poor nucleus-to-background contrast).

Limitations

Physiological relevance. This assay uses Matrigel as the 3D matrix, which does not recapitulate the biochemical composition, stiffness range, architecture, or viscoelasticity of human brain extracellular matrix. Batch-to-batch variability in Matrigel protein content and mechanical properties can introduce differences in invasion and deformation readouts. However, it can be applied to other matrices, such as collagen matrix, as long as they allow 3D fluorescence imaging with sufficient spatial and temporal resolution. The spheroid model lacks stromal, vascular, and immune components and does not include interstitial flow, so cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions relevant in vivo are only partially represented. Generalizability may require re-optimization for other cell types or labels: some cells may not form uniform spheroids or tolerate prolonged fluorescent labeling, and dye choice or cell-cycle state can alter nuclear signal and mechanics, affecting both segmentation quality and biological interpretation.

Invasion quantification is partly manual. Delineating spheroid borders, selecting distal invasion paths, and classifying leader versus follower cells are labor-intensive and subject to observer bias, especially when invasion fronts overlap or when spheroids are not perfectly round. Invasion behavior also depends on spheroid size and compactness at t0; residual heterogeneity in these features can influence the Radial Invasion Index and leader/follower counts even after diameter normalization.

The nucleus deformation workflow relies on high-quality 3D+time fluorescence imaging. Long time-lapse acquisition and repeated z-stacks can induce photobleaching and phototoxicity, forcing trade-offs between temporal resolution, z-sampling, and cell health. Axial resolution is lower than lateral resolution; undersampling may reduce segmentation accuracy and bias deformation estimates. Focus drift, stage instability, or abrupt focal shifts violate assumptions used for flow estimation and degrade segmentation and deformation accuracy. Fast 3D imaging with spinning-disk confocal and long working distance objectives is not universally available and limits throughput.

Accurate nuclear segmentation is critical because errors in mask definition directly affect the computed deformation fields and the resulting scalar metric. Highly irregular or touching nuclei, or nuclear envelope blebbing can be difficult to segment robustly. The optical flow–based analysis assumes relatively small, smooth frame-to-frame intensity displacements and approximate brightness constancy; strong noise, lack of texture, channel bleed-through, or very rapid morphological changes can thus reduce reliability. This can also complicate comparisons across datasets acquired with different optics or sampling schemes.

The deformation readout is reported as a single overall deformation value per nucleus, which simplifies comparisons but compresses rich spatiotemporal information and may obscure localized or transient events. The metric is relative and image-based; it does not yield absolute strain, stress, or material properties and should not be directly interpreted as nuclear stiffness.
