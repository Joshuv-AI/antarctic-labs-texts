Potential Involvement of Sulfotransferase in the Mechanism of Lamotrigine-induced Skin Rash
Authors: Yanshan Cao, Ahsan Bairam, Ming-Cheh Liu, Jack Uetrecht
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanism of drug-induced skin rash is not well understood. Circumstantial evidence suggests that the covalent binding of a reactive metabolite is involved in the mechanism of most idiosyncratic drug reactions. However, there is a limited quantity of drug metabolizing enzymes in the skin, except for sulfotransferases. It is possible that some drugs are metabolized to reactive sulfate metabolites that are responsible for skin rashes. For example, nevirapine-induced skin rash involves metabolism of nevirapine to 12-hydroxy-nevirapine, which is further metabolized by sulfotransferase in the skin to a reactive benzylic sulfate that covalently binds to proteins. The working hypothesis is that lamotrigine, valdecoxib, and sertraline skin rashes involve the formation of reactive sulfate in the skin. Lamotrigine-N-oxide, hydroxy-valdecoxib, and hydroxy-sertraline were tested as substrates with known human sulfotransferases. Hydroxy-valdecoxib and the benzylic alcohol metabolite of sertraline were not substrates for human sulfotransferases. Therefore, this pathway is presumably not involved in the mechanism by which they cause skin rashes. In contrast, lamotrigine-N-oxide is a substrate for several human sulfotransferases and the sulfate is chemically reactive. Furthermore, lamotrigine-N-sulfate not only alkylates proteins as we described previously but also forms the sulfate of tyrosine, suggesting another possible mechanism for protein modification. This study has further added to the understanding of the potential of the sulfotransferase pathways and protein sulfation to play a role in drug-induced skin rash.

Introduction

The basic mechanism(s) of idiosyncratic adverse drug reactions (IDRs) are not well understood, in part, because of their idiosyncratic nature and because there are few valid animal models. Circumstantial evidence suggests that in most cases reactive metabolites are responsible.1 A drug may form many metabolites, and it is difficult to determine which one, if any, is responsible for an IDR. Reactive metabolites are usually too reactive to reach sites far from where they are formed. This is presumably why the liver, where most drug metabolism occurs, is a common target of IDRs. However, the skin is also a common target of IDRs in the form of skin rash, and the concentration of most drug metabolizing enzymes is low in the skin.2 One exception is the sulfotransferases (SULTs), which are present in the skin at relatively high concentrations.3 Sulfation is generally considered a detoxification pathway; however, it can also make a molecule more reactive, because sulfate is a good leaving group. We previously demonstrated that nevirapine-induced skin rash is caused by the formation of a reactive benzylic sulfate in the skin.4 Nevirapine is metabolized to a benzylic alcohol in the liver, and this alcohol is bioactivated to a benzylic sulfate by SULTs in the skin. The reactive sulfate covalently binds to proteins, and application of a topical SULT inhibitor prevented covalent binding and skin rash in a rat model. There are other drug metabolites that can potentially be substrates for SULTs leading to reactive metabolites that could be responsible for skin rashes.

Lamotrigine (LTG) is an antiepileptic drug that is associated with a high incidence of skin rashes. The incidence of rashes is approximately 10.7 and 12.9% in adults and children, respectively, and severe, life-threatening skin rashes including Stevens Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis account for 1.0 and 0.3% of the rashes in children and adults, respectively.5,6 LTG is metabolized to several metabolites, including LTG N2-oxide. It is plausible that this N-oxide would be a substrate for SULTs because other N-oxides such as minoxidil are metabolized to a sulfate in the skin.7 Previously, the chemically synthesized sulfate was found to be reactive toward alcohol groups, but no covalent binding was detected when LTG-N-oxide was incubated with rat liver cytosol and 3′phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate (PAPS).8 However, human SULTs were not studied, and under the conditions of the experiment, most of any sulfate that might have formed may have hydrolyzed before it covalently bound to proteins.

Another metabolite of LTG that has been proposed to be responsible for LTG-induced skin rash is an epoxide.9 This is based on finding a glutathione conjugate formed by rats or rat hepatocytes; however, no significant covalent binding was observed when radiolabeled LTG was incubated with hepatic microsomes in the presence of added NADPH. The glutathione adduct was also observed when human epidermal keratinocytes was incubated with LTG, but the amount formed was very low and only observed at very high concentrations of LTG.10 The lack of covalent binding, even with hepatic microsomes, and the low activity of P450 in the skin make this pathway doubtful as a cause of LTG-induced skin rash.

Valdecoxib (VLD) and sertraline are also associated with life-threatening rashes, although the incidences are lower.11 VLD is metabolized by cytochromes P450 to various metabolites in the liver including hydroxy-VLD metabolite, which is further metabolized by UDP-glucuronylsyltransferases to a glucuronide.12 Sertraline is metabolized by cytochromes P450 to a ketone metabolite that is further reduced to a hydroxy-sertraline metabolite.13,14

We hypothesized that if the N-oxide of LTG and the hydroxymetabolites of VLD and sertraline were substrates for SULTs, then the sulfate should have sufficient reactivity to covalently bind to proteins, and this could be the pathway that leads to drug-induced skin rash (Figure 1). In this study, we tested whether the three metabolites were substrates for human SULTs.

Experimental Reagents and Procedures

Chemical Reagents, Cells, and Cell Culture

Sodium carbonate, ammonium formate, formic acid, acetic acid, m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid, sulfur-trioxide pyridine complex, anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF), 1,2-dichlorobenzene, aluminum chloride, sodium borohydride, dihydroxybenzoic acid, tyrosine, and isoamyl alcohol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Oakville, ON). Solvents including acetone, ethyl acetate, hexanes, acetonitrile, and methanol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Oakville, ON) or Caledon (Georgetown, ON). Lamotrigine (TCI America) was purchased from Fisher Scientific, CA. Lamotrigine N-oxide and hydroxysertraline were synthesized and hydroxy-VLD was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, ON). Silica (silica 60 and 70-230 mesh) was purchased from Chromatographic Specialties (Brockville, ON). HepG2 human hepatoma cells (ATCC HB-8065) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). 3′-Phosphoadenosine-5′-phospho[35S]sulfate (PAP[35S]) was prepared using ATP and carrier-free [35S]sulfate based on a previously established protocol.15 X-ray films were products of the Research Products International Corporation (Mt Prospect, IL). Ecolume scintillation cocktail was obtained from MP Biomedical LLC (Irvine, CA). A Sciex API 3000 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer was used for ESI-MS analysis.

Synthesis of Lamotrigine N-oxide

LTG (100 mg) was dissolved in acetone. To the stirring solution m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid (110 mg) was added. The mixture was stirred for 4 h at RT. LTG N-oxide precipitates as a yellow solid. The precipitate was filtered, washed with acetone, and dried. On mass spectrometry, LTG N-oxide gave [M + H]+ ions at m/z 272, 255, 242.

Synthesis of Lamotrigine-sulfate and Studies of Its Reactivity with Alcohols, Acids, and Tyrosine

Synthesis of LTG N-sulfate was adapted from Lu and Uetrecht8. Briefly, 15 mg of LTG-N-oxide was dissolved in 1 mL of anhydrous DMF. Sulfur trioxide-pyridine complex (24 mg) was added to the solution and stirred under nitrogen for 30 min. The reaction mixture was purified by preparative HPLC using an Ultracarb 5 ODS (30), 60 × 10 mm 5 μm column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) at flow rate of 1 mL/min with a mobile phase of acetonitrile: 1 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid; 25:75 (v/v) buffer. The LTG N-sulfate fractions were analyzed on an ESI-LC/MS with a Gemini 5 μm C18 110A 150 × 3.00 mm column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA). The mobile phase was a buffer containing acetonitrile: 0.1 mM ammonium formate, 1% formic acid (20:80, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The LTG N-sulfate gave ions at m/z: 274, 257, 244, 242, 207, 187; yield ∼85% and RT 7.5 min.

To the LTG N-sulfate sample collected from preparative HPLC, a solution of 180 μL of methanol was added to study the reaction of LTG N-sulfate with alcohols. The pH was adjusted to ∼ pH 8 by adding small increments of solid sodium carbonate. pH was monitored and maintained at ∼pH 8 by checking the reaction mixture using pH test strips (BDH, VWR). The reaction was stirred and periodically analyzed by LC/MS. The LTG N-sulfate [M – H]− 274 m/z peak was no longer detectable at 8 h. The reaction was stirred and sampled for 24 h to determine the stability of sulfated molecules formed. This procedure was repeated using 100 μL of acetic acid or 1.5 mg of tyrosine to study the reactions of acetic acid and tyrosine with LTG N-sulfate, respectively. A sulfated-isoamyl alcohol adduct was synthesized for the ESI-MS comparison. Isoamyl alcohol (100 μL) was dissolved in 2 mL of DMF. SO3-pyridine (135 g) was added to the stirring solution and stirred at 4 °C. The reaction was analyzed by HPLC and ESI-MS at multiple time points.

Synthesis of Hydroxysertraline

Anhydrous 1-naphthol (3 g) was dissolved in 1,2-dichlorobenzene (18 mL), 5.55 g of aluminum chloride was added, and the mixture was stirred at 100 °C under nitrogen for 1 h. The reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and then purified using silica chromatography (hexanes/ethyl acetate 4:1; v/v). HPLC and ESI-MS were performed using a Gemini 5 μm Phenyl 110A column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) using a mobile phase of acetonitrile: 0.1 mM ammonium formate, 1% formic acid buffer (60:40, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Sertraline-ketone [M + H]+m/z 293.3, 291.2; RT 8.7 min, yield ∼27%.

Oven-dried sertraline-ketone (0.2 g) was dissolved in 15 mL of anhydrous methanol, sodium borohydride (0.019 g) was added, and the reaction was refluxed for 2 h. The solvent was evaporated and purified on silica chromatography (hexanes/ethyl acetate 4:1; v/v). There were two fractions of hydroxysertraline that came off the column. HPLC and ESI-MS were performed using a Prodigy 5 μm ODS 100 A column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) using a mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile: 0.1 mM ammonium formate, 1% formic acid buffer (55:45, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Hydroxysertraline [M + H]+m/z 292, RT 11.6 and 17.4 min. Yield for the two diastereomers was ∼80–95% total.

Preparation of Human Cytosolic Sulfotransferases (SULTs) and Sulfotransferase Assay

Recombinant human SULTs (SULT1A1, SULT1A2, SULT1A3, SULT1B1, SULT1C2 SULT1C3, SULT1C4, SULT1E1, SULT2A1, SULT2B1a, and SULT2B1b), expressed using pGEX-2TK or pET23c prokaryotic expression system, were prepared as previously reported.16−19 The sulfating activities of the above-mentioned SULTs toward LTG-N-oxide were assayed based on a previously established protocol (Bairam et al.), with radioactive PAP[35S] as the sulfate donor. The assay mixture, following a 15 min reaction at 37 °C, was separated by TLC and analyzed for the [35S]sulfated LTG-N-oxide. The generation and release of [35S]sulfated LTG-N-oxide by HepG2 human hepatoma cells labeled with [35S]sulfate in the presence of different concentrations of LTG-N-oxide was examined based on a previously established procedure.20 Upon an 18 h labeling period, the labeling media were collected and analyzed by TLC.

Results

Reactivity of LTG N-sulfate

As previously shown in Lu and Uetrecht,8 and in our current study, the synthesized LTG N-sulfate reacts with methanol; [M + H]+m/z 286. In this study, we also showed that the sulfate reacted with carboxylic acids. LTG N-sulfate reacted with formic acid (source: HPLC mobile phase) and acetic acid; [M – H]−m/z 300 and 314, respectively. These adducts were detectable by LC/MS at 24 h suggesting they are stable for at least 24 h.

We also found that LTG N-sulfate can sulfate tyrosine. Sulfo-tyrosine gave ions: [M + H]−m/z 260, and [M + formic acid]− 306 in negative ion mode (Figure 2A). To further test the ability of LTG N-sulfate to sulfate alcohols, a sulfated-isoamyl alcohol adduct was synthesized to compare the mass spectrum with that of isoamyl alcohol sulfated by LTG N-sulfate. The mass spectra of the two sulfated alcohols were similar to a parent ion of m/z 167 in the negative ion mode (Figure 2B).

LTG-N-sulfate reacts with nucleophiles, including alcohols, carboxylic acids, and tyrosine. Therefore, it likely reacts with proteins, although that was not demonstrated.

Analysis of the Sulfating Activity of Human SULTs Toward LTG-N-oxide

A set of 11 human SULTs previously prepared were evaluated for sulfation activity toward LTG-N-oxide as a substrate. As shown in Figure 4, three (SULT1A1, SULT1A2, and SULT1C4) of the 11 human SULTs examined displayed significant sulfating activity toward LTG-N-oxide. Of the three, SULT1C4 showed the strongest LTG-N-oxide-sulfating activity (0.222 nmol/min/mg enzyme), followed by SULT1A1 and SULT1A2 (at 0.104 and 0.021 nmol/min/mg enzyme, respectively).

The final concentration of LTG-N-oxide in the assay mixture was 50 μM. The solvent system used for the TLC analysis was 6:2 (n-butanol/acetonitrile). A similar procedure was used to determine if hydroxy-VLD or hydroxysertraline is a substrate for SULTs, but no activity was detected.

Discussion

The skin is a metabolically competent organ, and understanding the ability of the skin to metabolize drugs and foreign molecules is important to understanding the mechanism of drug-induced skin rash. There are limited cytochrome P450 enzymes in the skin,2 but a relatively high concentration of SULTs. The SULT pathway is an important metabolic pathway in the liver for drug detoxification,21 while in the fetus, SULTs are important for steroid and thyroid metabolism in addition to detoxification.22 In the skin, in addition to protein and mRNA expression of various SULTs, there is evidence of SULT activities such as the sulfation of minoxidil and 12-hydroxy-nevirapine to reactive sulfates. The hypothesis that the SULT pathway is involved in the mechanism of some drug-induced skin rashes is a plausible mechanism because of the limited metabolic enzymes in the skin with the exception of SULTs.

Previously we did not observe covalent binding using radiolabeled LTG-N-oxide and rat liver cytosol with added PAPS, which was evidence against the SULT pathway being responsible for LTG-induced skin rash.8 In our current study, we determined if the N-oxide was a substrate for human SULTs. There are several reasons that suggest the SULT pathway may be responsible for lamotrigine-induced skin rashes. LTG-N-oxide is a significant metabolite, which can reach the skin through general circulation. LTG-N-oxide is a good substrate for several human SULTs: SULT1A1, SULT1A2, and SULT1C4. SULT1A1 is a major isoform in the liver accounting for more than 50% of SULTs activity; it also has a high interindividual variability between individuals and is expressed in various tissues,23,24 while SULT1A2 has similar functions to SULT1A1, but less efficiency.25 SULT1C4 is expressed mainly in the fetus, and expression in the skin is yet to be determined.22 To date, there have not been studies that have investigated the concentration of various SULT isoforms in the skin, but it is likely that SULT1A1 is involved in the activation of the N-oxide to N-sulfate in the skin, similar to the sulfation of minoxidil.

In addition, LTG N-sulfate is chemically reactive and reacts with alcohol and carboxylic acid groups. Serine accounts for 8.3 mol % of amino acid composition in the human epidermis keratin,26 this suggests that there should be available alcohol groups in the skin for binding to LTG N-sulfate. The observation that unlike the putative epoxide the N–O sulfate does not react with glutathione may make it more likely to covalently bind to proteins. We also found sulfation of hydroxyl groups by LTG N-sulfate including tyrosine sulfation. This suggests that LTG N-sulfate can potentially sulfate proteins, which can lead to protein modification and subsequently be involved in LTG-induced skin rash. Protein sulfation is a common post-translational modification process important in processes such as protein–protein interactions, viral cell entry and visual functions.27−29 Aberrant sulfation by reactive sulfate metabolites may cause abnormal protein modification.

Hydroxy-VLD was not a substrate for human SULTs; therefore, the corresponding sulfate is very unlikely to be responsible for VLD-induced skin rash. Unlike LTG, the incidence of VLD-induced skin rash is much less common.30 Similarly, hydroxy-sertraline was not a substrate for SULTs; therefore, a reactive sulfate metabolite is very unlikely to be responsible for sertraline-induced skin rash. We are not sure what reactive metabolite(s) may be involved in the mechanism of VLD and sertraline-induced skin rashes, but one other possibility involves interaction with the drug itself, without metabolism, through the P–I hypothesis.31 Thus, even though the SULT pathway is a plausible mechanism for drug-induced skin rash, this pathway is limited to some drugs, such as nevirapine and LTG.
