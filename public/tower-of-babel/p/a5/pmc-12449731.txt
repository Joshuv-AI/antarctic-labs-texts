Rapid Elimination of Culturable SARS-CoV-2 With Intramuscular or Intravenous Administration of Antiviral Monoclonal Antibody Therapy
Authors: Rinki Deo, Manish C Choudhary, Owen T Glover, Rachel Bender Ignacio, Julie Boucau, Kara W Chew, Carlee Moser, Judith S Currier, Joseph J Eron, Arzhang Cyrus Javan, Mark J Giganti, Evgenia Aga, Michael Gibbs, Taylor Cohen, Katie Streicher, Karina Soboleva, Courtney V Fletcher, Eric S Daar, Alexander L Greninger, Robert W Coombs, William Fischer, Michael D Hughes, Davey Smith, David Alain Wohl, Amy K Barczak, Jonathan Z Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated intramuscular (IM) versus intravenous (IV) administration of tixagevimab/cilgavimab in early COVID-19. Both routes achieved rapid elimination of culturable virus and minimal emergence of resistance. These results support IM delivery as a viable alternative to IV, with important implications for scalable deployment in future viral pandemics.Keywords: intramuscular administration, monoclonal antibodies, SARS-CoV-2, tixagevimab/cilgavimab, viral culture conversion

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have been an important treatment option for individuals at risk of severe COVID-19, especially those ineligible for oral antivirals, due to their targeted mechanism of action and rapid antiviral effects [1]. Both single mAb [2–4] and combination mAb therapies [5–7] have demonstrated clinical efficacy. However, resistance has been reported in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals following treatment [8–14]. Beyond COVID-19, mAbs have also been successfully deployed against viral infections such as respiratory syncytial virus [15] and Ebola virus disease [16].

Despite their benefits, the scalability of mAb treatments has been limited by their dependence on intravenous (IV) administration, which requires infusion infrastructure and trained personnel. In contrast, intramuscular (IM) administration offers a more practical and scalable approach that can be implemented in outpatient clinics and community-based settings. Pharmacokinetic studies have shown that IM delivery of certain mAbs, including tixagevimab and cilgavimab (T/C), achieves systemic exposures comparable to IV infusion [17]. Similarly, IM administration of sotrovimab demonstrated noninferior pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes compared with IV delivery [18], further supporting its feasibility. However, that study did not assess virologic endpoints such as viral culture clearance or resistance emergence—key measures of antiviral efficacy and transmission risk. Determining whether IM administration maintains virologic effectiveness comparable to IV delivery is critical for informing treatment strategies and improving pandemic preparedness. With the continued threat of emerging viral pathogens, streamlined and scalable therapeutic delivery models are urgently needed.

In this substudy of the ACTIV-2/A5401 trial, we evaluated the virologic impact of IM versus IV administration of T/C in individuals with early COVID-19, focusing on viral culture conversion and resistance emergence. While subcutaneous (SC) formulations (eg, casirivimab/imdevimab, REGEN-COV) [19, 20] have also been used, IM delivery offers further simplification. This study addresses a key evidence gap by directly comparing the virologic efficacy of IM and IV administration of mAbs in early SARS-CoV-2 infection.

METHODS

This virologic substudy was nested within the ACTIV-2/A5401 platform trial (NCT04518410), which enrolled nonhospitalized adults with mild-to-moderate COVID-19. Participants were randomized to receive IM or IV T/C or matching placebo. Inclusion criteria included symptom onset within 10 days and detectable nasopharyngeal (NP) SARS-CoV-2 RNA at baseline. Anterior nasal (AN) swabs were collected on study days 0, 1–3, and 7 for viral culture from a subset of participants with baseline viral loads ≥6 log₁₀ RNA copies/mL (N = 8/group). NP swabs were collected on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 for RNA quantification and spike (S) gene sequencing. Viral cultures were performed using TCID₅₀ assays in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells. Culture positivity was defined as cytopathic effect in at least 1 of 24 wells. Sequencing of the SARS-CoV-2 spike gene was performed to detect emergent resistance-associated mutations at a threshold of ≥1% variant frequency. Full methods are described in the Supplementary Information.

RESULTS

Among the 313 participants, baseline characteristics were well balanced between the active treatment and placebo groups within each administration route (IM and IV). However, differences were observed between the IM and IV cohorts themselves, particularly in COVID-19 risk status and baseline viral load (Table 1). We previously reported that individuals receiving IM T/C had a higher proportion of participants with unquantifiable NP SARS-CoV-2 RNA levels versus placebo at day 7, but no significant differences were seen in the IV T/C-treated group (Supplementary Figure 1) [1]. Among the participants who underwent viral culture testing, baseline AN SARS-CoV-2 RNA levels and proportion of participants with positive viral culture were not statistically different between IV/IM T/C versus placebo groups (Figure 1A). Following treatment, the differences between groups became evident. One day after mAb infusion (Day 1), SARS-CoV-2 RNA levels were not statistically different between groups (IV/IM vs placebo: 5.8 vs 7.5 log₁₀ copies/mL; P = .2), but those receiving mAbs were less likely to have culture positivity (6.3% vs 62%; P = .007). Amongst participants with >6 log₁₀ RNA copies/mL at day 1, all five (100%) placebo participants were culture positive, whereas only 1 of 7 (14%) mAb-treated participants was culture positive (an IV recipient). On day 2, 50% of placebo participants were still culture positive, compared with none in the combined mAb group. These findings underscore the utility of viral culture as a more sensitive early endpoint for mAb efficacy than RNA quantification alone. Median time to culture conversion was ≤1.0 day in the T/C group versus 2.5 days for placebo (Figure 1B). This rapid clearance of viable virus not only reflects antiviral potency but also suggests a potential public health benefit in terms of reducing transmissibility. The prolonged culture positivity observed in the placebo arm (median 2.5 days) further supports the virologic impact of T/C therapy.

Treatment-emergent resistance mutations were infrequent and occurred at similar rates between placebo and treatment groups in both the IM (1/98 [1.0%] vs 2/111 [1.8%] in placebo) and IV arms (0/54 [0%] vs 0/50 [0%] in placebo) (Figure 1C). We also examined the emergence of additional S gene sequence variations beyond the primary resistance sites in the same set of participants. These polymorphisms were rare and observed similarly across all study groups (Figure 1D). These data suggest that combination mAb therapy may mitigate the development of escape variants, a key concern in the widespread use of antiviral agents.

DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrate that both IM and IV administration of T/C lead to rapid elimination of culturable SARS-CoV-2 with minimal emergence of resistance-associated mutations. These results are consistent with prior studies showing that mAb therapy reduces viral infectivity earlier than measurable reductions in viral RNA and highlights the importance of culture-based assessments in evaluating treatment efficacy [21]. The comparable virologic effects of IM and IV administration are particularly relevant for clinical and public health planning. During the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, the need for infusion centers and trained staff limited the availability of IV-administered mAbs [22]. IM delivery offers a practical alternative that can be more easily implemented in outpatient or mass-vaccination-style settings. This could be particularly impactful in the early phases of future pandemics, where rapid scale-up of treatment capacity is essential [18]. The pharmacokinetic profiles of IM and IV T/C, as demonstrated in prior studies, support the biological plausibility of our findings [17]. Comparable systemic drug levels suggest that observed clinical and virologic effects are not route-dependent, enabling a shift toward simpler and more accessible delivery models without sacrificing efficacy.

Our results also reinforce the role of combination mAb therapy in reducing the risk of treatment-emergent resistance [23]. In contrast to monotherapy, which has been associated with the selection of escape variants, dual-targeted approaches like T/C appear more robust [13, 24]. The low rate of emergent resistance across both IM and IV arms suggests that route of administration does not significantly impact resistance dynamics.

Although T/C has been withdrawn from clinical use due to reduced activity against circulating Omicron variants [6], the broader implications of this work are highly relevant. In future viral pandemics, mAbs are likely to represent a critical first-line response, given their speed of development and adaptability [25]. The ability to deliver mAbs via IM route could greatly facilitate widespread administration, particularly in nonhospital settings and low-resource regions [26].

Our results complement pharmacokinetic studies showing that IM administration of T/C and other mAbs (eg, sotrovimab) achieves systemic drug exposures comparable to IV infusion [18]. Together, these findings argue for further development of IM mAb platforms. Limitations of this study include small sample size in the viral culture subset and the focus on pre-Omicron variants. Still, the consistency of culture conversion across both IM and IV arms strengthens the robustness of the findings. As the field prepares for future emergent pathogens, ensuring access to scalable, effective therapies is critical. IM mAb administration represents a promising strategy to meet that goal.
