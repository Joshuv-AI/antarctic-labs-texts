Practice patterns and outcomes of local and systemic therapy for single-site metastatic non–small cell lung cancer in the era of immunotherapy
Authors: Gabriela Esnaola, Maureen Canavan, Giorgio Caturegli, Abigail Lynch, Benjamin Resio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrognosis of lung cancer with single metastatic sites in the era of immunotherapy remains poorly described. We used the National Cancer Database to assess how metastatic site and the use of local therapy with systemic immunotherapy is associated with prognosis in the United States.MethodsPatients with clinical stage IVA lung cancer treated with immunotherapy were identified in the National Cancer Database (2018-2023). Patients were stratified by metastatic site and treatment: systemic immunotherapy with or without local therapy (immunotherapy + local therapy or immunotherapy). Prognosis was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier and Cox survival analysis landmarked at median time from diagnosis to local therapy.ResultsAmong included patients (N = 20,214), metastases included lung (N = 5308; 26.3%), brain (N = 3516; 17.4%), bone (N = 3142; 15.5%), distant lymph node (N = 1219; 6.0%), liver (N = 874; 4.3%), and other metastases (N = 6155; 30.4%). A total of 13,890 patients (68.7%) were treated with immunotherapy, and 6324 patients (31.3%) were treated with immunotherapy + local therapy (70.2% received chemotherapy, 27.2% radiation, and 9.8% surgery). Poor 5-year overall survival was associated with bone (20.9%) and liver (15.9%) metastases compared with brain (33.4%). Five-year survival was greater in patients selected for local therapy (30.3% vs 20.5%, P < .001); thoracic and extrathoracic local therapy was associated with greater overall survival compared with extrathoracic local therapy alone (37.7% vs 28.2%, P < .001). Differences in 5-year survival with local therapy varied by metastatic site (10.4% lung, 7.1% brain, 0.1% liver).ConclusionsThe addition of local therapy to a systemic regimen containing immunotherapy was common for patients with single metastatic sites. Patients selected for local therapy had a more favorable prognosis, which varied by metastatic site.Key Words: local therapy, non–small cell lung cancer, oligometastatic disease

Survival of patients with single-site metastatic lung cancer with and without LT.

Central Message

LT use in patients with single-site metastatic lung cancer receiving IO was common and associated with relatively favorable prognosis, which varied by metastatic site.

Perspective

Prognosis of lung cancer with single metastatic sites in the era of IO remains poorly described. Our analysis of the National Cancer Database demonstrated relatively favorable survival for patients selected for LT in addition to IO-containing ST. Further study to determine the appropriate use of LT in this setting is warranted.

Two-thirds of patients with non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis, contributing to poor overall survival (OS) despite treatment.1 The benefit of local therapy (LT), primarily delivered as radiation therapy (RT) or surgical resection, in addition to systemic therapy (ST) in patients with metastatic NSCLC remains unclear, and distant metastases have historically been considered an exclusion criteria for surgical resection of the primary tumor.2

Patients with a lower metastatic burden have a more favorable prognosis than those with diffuse metastatic disease and patients with single metastatic sites, particularly brain or adrenal lesions, have been considered candidates for LT.3, 4, 5 However, traditional treatment paradigms are shifting with the introduction of more effective ST, such as systemic immunotherapy (IO) and targeted therapy, that have been shown to improve survival outcomes in select patients with metastatic NSCLC.6 In addition, most studies demonstrating a differential response to therapy based on single metastatic site, and thus the rationale behind the selective use of LT, originated before the widespread adoption of IO in clinical practice.7,8 Randomized clinical trials evaluating the benefit of LT in addition to IO in patients with oligometastatic disease are ongoing (LONESTAR) or have suggested that there is no advantage with LT in patients with less than 3 metastatic sites (NRG-LU002).9, 10, 11 Furthermore, the benefit of treating distant metastases versus the primary tumor is not well understood.

The role of LT in conjunction with ST in treatment-responsive patients with limited metastatic NSCLC will be of increasing relevance as the therapeutic landscape continues to evolve. We used the National Cancer Database (NCDB) to assess how metastatic lesion location, treatment with LT in conjunction with ST including IO, and type of LT are associated with the prognosis of patients with single-site metastatic NSCLC in the United States.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

The NCDB is a hospital-based tumor registry sponsored by the American Cancer Society and American College of Surgeons that includes data from approximately 1500 accredited hospitals and captures more than 80% of newly diagnosed cancer in the United States. Data are coded and reported according to established protocols coordinated under the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries.

Study Population and Design

For our retrospective cohort study, we included all patients aged 18 years or more in the 2023 NCDB participant user file treated nonpalliatively with ST that included IO for single-site synchronous oligometastatic NSCLC (clinical stage IVA, American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition) diagnosed between 2018 and 2022. For the purpose of this study, single metastatic site refers to single site of organ metastasis, not overall tumor burden or lesion number. This cohort was stratified based on whether patients received IO without LT or a combination of IO and LT (IO+LT). Patients were assigned to the IO+LT group if they received IO in addition to undergoing (1) therapeutic thoracic RT 45 Gy or more (minimum recommended dose per National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines) or a complete course of extrathoracic RT more than 0 Gy or (2) surgery on the primary site or another nonprimary site.12 Patients in either group could receive chemotherapy. Patients in the IO+LT group were stratified based on receipt of thoracic LT, extrathoracic LT, or thoracic and extrathoracic LT. We evaluated the clinical significance of 6 metastatic sites for survival: bone, brain, lung, liver, distant lymph node (LN), and other sites. Patients who did not have clinical stage IVA NSCLC, had more than 1 documented metastatic site, did not receive IO, or were treated with palliative intent were excluded (Figure E1).

Data Elements

The following patient, treatment, and tumor variables were included in our analysis as independent covariates based on clinical relevance: age, sex, race/ethnicity, Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index (CCI) score (modified to a grouping of 0, 1, 2, or ≥3), insurance status, income level (median household income at the US zip code level), facility type and region, year of diagnosis, histologic type, EGFR/ALK mutation status, type of surgery or RT, and chemotherapy use. Patients with missing data for independent variables and outcomes of interest were excluded. A complete NCDB variable list can be found online.13

Statistical and Survival Analysis

Patient, treatment, and tumor characteristics were compared across metastatic sites and treatment cohorts using chi-square tests for categorical variables or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. OS was defined as time in months from date of metastatic diagnosis to date of death or last follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves for 5-year OS were stratified by therapy type and landmarked to exclude patients that did not survive to median time from diagnosis to receipt of LT (42 days). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the independent effects (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]) of metastatic site and treatment strategy on OS adjusted for patient, tumor, and treatment variables. Assumption of proportional hazards were visually assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves. Analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Cohort Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 20,214 patients with single-site synchronous oligometastatic NSCLC treated with IO were included in the study (Figure E1). Sites of metastasis included lung in 26.3% (N = 5308), brain in 17.4% (N = 3516), bone in 15.5% (N = 3142), distant LN in 6.0% (N = 1219), liver in 4.3% (N = 874), and other sites in 30.4% (N = 6155) of patients. Patients with brain metastases were younger, with a median (interquartile range) age at diagnosis of 65 (59-72) years compared with those with distant LN (68 [61-75]), bone (69 [62-76]), liver (69 [64-76]), other (70 [63-77]), or lung (71 [64-77], P < .001) metastases. Adenocarcinoma was most common (66.9-83.4%) except in patients with liver metastases, who had a greater proportion of squamous cell carcinoma compared with other sites (47.5% vs 26.5% overall, P < .001). A summary of patient and tumor characteristics by metastatic site is included in Table E1.

Treatment Patterns

A total of 68.7% (N = 13,890) of patients received ST with IO and 31.3% (N = 6324) were treated with IO+LT. Patients treated with IO+LT were younger (median [interquartile range] age 66 [60-73] vs 70 [63-77] years at diagnosis, P < .001), had higher rates of private insurance (28.7% vs 21.6%, P < .001), and were more likely to receive chemotherapy (74.7% vs 68.1%, P < .001). Of patients treated with IO+LT, 26.0% (N = 1467) received thoracic, 55.4% (N = 3128) received extrathoracic, and 16.2% (N = 916) received thoracic and extrathoracic LT. Patients treated with thoracic and extrathoracic LT were younger, with a median [interquartile range] age of 65 [59-72] years at diagnosis, compared with extrathoracic (median 66 [60-73] years) and thoracic LT (median 67 [61-74] years, P < .001) groups. LT was often delivered as RT 87.2% (68.5% RT, 18.7% surgery and RT) (Table 1).

Patients with brain and bone metastases were frequently treated with IO+LT (85.4% and 42.2% vs other sites 11.0%-22.2%). These patients were more likely to undergo RT (brain 51.5% and bone 36.9% vs other sites 8.5%-15.3%) or RT + surgery (brain 28.5% and bone 3.0% vs other sites 0.5%-0.8%), primarily to extrathoracic sites alone (brain 74.5% and bone 61.3% vs 14.6%-35.8% in other sites, P < .001). In contrast, patients with all other metastatic sites more commonly received thoracic LT, typically as RT. There was no significant difference in chemotherapy use across metastatic sites (P = .27). Unadjusted Survival Analysis

Patients with brain and LN metastases had higher median OS (28.2 and 24.1 months) and 5-year survival (33.4% and 28.5%) compared with patients with lung (median 22.0 months, 23.5% 5-year survival), other (median 18.1 months, 19.5% 5-year survival), bone (median 17.5 months, 20.9% 5-year survival), or liver metastases (median 16.0 months, 15.9% 5-year survival, P < .001, Figure 1, A). Five-year survival in the IO group was 20.5% (median 18.5 months) compared with 30.3% in the IO+LT group (median 27.6 months, P < .001, Figure 1, B). Patients selected for LT had more favorable median OS for all sites. Survival was generally longer in patients who received thoracic and extrathoracic LT (median 39.7 months, 37.7% 5-year survival) compared with thoracic (median 29.4 months, 29.7% 5-year survival) or extrathoracic LT (median 23.3 months, 28.2% 5-year survival); however, differences varied by metastatic site (Figure 2, A-F). In patients with brain metastases, thoracic and extrathoracic LT (median 46.8 months, P < .001) were associated with longer OS compared with other strategies (median 30.8 months IO+LT, 17.1 months IO, P < .001). Although LT was associated with longer median OS in patients with liver metastases (22.9 vs 15.4 months, P = .003), it was not associated with greater 5-year survival (15.9% vs 15.8%), which remained poor regardless of LT type.

Factors Associated With All-cause Mortality in a Multivariable Model

Several covariates associated with survival were identified in multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for patient and tumor characteristics. Brain, lung, and distant LN metastases had comparably favorable survival, whereas bone (HR, 1.28, 95% CI, 1.20-1.36, P < .001), liver (HR, 1.28, 95% CI, 1.17-1.41, P < .001), and other metastases (HR, 1.25, 95% CI, 1.19-1.31, P < .001) had the least favorable survival. Thoracic LT was associated with lower mortality for all sites Table 2). Extrathoracic LT alone was associated with lower mortality only in patients with brain metastases (HR, 0.73, 95% CI, 0.64-0.84, P < .001). Thoracic and extrathoracic LT were associated with lower mortality compared with thoracic or extrathoracic LT alone in brain (HR, 0.44, 95% CI, 0.37-0.53, P < .001) and bone metastases (HR, 0.67, 95% CI, 0.56-0.80, P < .001).

Discussion

Survival outcomes of patients with NSCLC with single metastatic sites in the era of IO remain poorly described. This scenario is becoming increasingly relevant as more patients with metastatic lung cancer survive due to overall improvement in available therapies. This study demonstrates practice patterns and outcomes of patients with single-site metastatic NSCLC in the United States treated with IO, with and without LT. The presented data suggest that treatment with LT in the setting of a single metastasis was common at Commission on Cancer hospitals in the United States, with some form of LT used in approximately one-third of patients receiving IO.

The use of LT was associated with increased survival for all single metastatic sites. There is reasonable concern for selection bias when interpreting outcomes, because LT may have been offered to more robust patients that could tolerate the toxicities associated with surgery or radiotherapy, live long enough to demonstrate a potential therapeutic response, or have disease that could be managed with LT. The current analysis cannot be interpreted as showing a causal relationship between LT and favorable outcomes, and it is likely that some of the perceived difference is due to selection bias. Specifically, although patients treated with palliative intent were excluded from this analysis, younger age and higher rates of private insurance and treatment with chemotherapy in the IO+LT group could suggest patients not treated with LT had poorer prognosis at baseline and were offered LT less often due to poor health status, which may have impacted survival comparisons. Notably, patients who received LT had slightly lower CCI scores overall (63.3% vs 60.8% had CCI score of 0). Additionally, most patients treated with LT received RT, which is generally well tolerated even in patients with poorer functional status at baseline and thus perhaps less of a selection criteria.

There is currently no level 1 evidence that the addition of LT in NSCLC with limited metastatic disease receiving IO improves survival outcomes.14, 15, 16 Studies have shown benefit with LT in patients treated with other ST, particularly targeted therapy (NORTHSTAR).17 In contrast, a recent large, randomized trial of patients with 3 or less metastatic sites after response to initial treatment with largely IO-containing ST (NRG-LU002) did not demonstrate a survival advantage with local consolidative therapy. However, authors of the NRG-LU002 trial noted that biologically driven patient selection may optimize therapeutic benefit from LT.10 NSCLC with an initial single metastatic site may represent a different clinical condition compared to the 3 or less sites evaluated in NRG-LU002. Small randomized controlled trials studying LT use with adjuvant IO primarily in patients with solitary oligometastases and a large database study evaluating outcomes after primary tumor resection for patients with single metastatic sites have shown promising results.18, 19, 20 Our study aims to further contextualize this prior literature on the role of LT in patients with single-site oligometastatic disease, more specifically in those receiving IO. However, additional clinical trials may be needed to determine if and to what extent LT is actually beneficial in the setting of systemic IO.

The efficacy of LT may vary depending on the organ site of metastasis. Our study findings suggest that LT is associated with a larger difference in 5-year survival in patients with lung or brain metastases, compared with minimal if any difference with liver metastases (although this sample was small, limiting interpretability). Prolonged survival after LT to brain metastases in patients with NSCLC has been well documented, particularly in the absence of extracranial metastases. The most favorable survival appears to be in patients treated with LT to both the primary tumor and intracranial metastases.21,22 Our study similarly found that in patients with brain metastases, the combination of thoracic and extrathoracic LT was associated with the greatest difference in median survival compared with IO ST.

Although the NCDB does not provide additional detail on the organ sites included in the “other” metastatic site category (30.0%), the frequencies of organ sites of metastasis represented in the NCDB are consistent with those reported by other studies: bone in 15.4% (vs 13-28%), brain in 17.4% (vs 15-38%), liver in 4.6% (vs 6-15%), and lung in 26.3% (vs 17-32%) of patients; therefore, based on descriptive studies of other databases of patients with NSCLC, “other” sites may include adrenal (10-17%), pleural (14-24%), and skin or soft tissue (0-3%) metastases.4,23, 24, 25, 26 Patients with adrenal metastases in particular may represent good candidates for metastasectomy, and a series of smaller studies have shown that long term-survival can be achieved with resection of adrenal metastases after successful surgical treatment of the primary tumor.27,28 In addition, although patients with pleural dissemination have been considered poor candidates for surgery according to standard treatment guidelines, recent studies have found that primary tumor resection is associated with prolonged survival, particularly in patients receiving nontargeted therapy.29,30 A similar difference is observed with RT to the primary tumor and stereotactic body radiotherapy to metastases in patients with pleural and adrenal metastases, respectively.31,32 Although the composition of the “other” metastasis category is unknown, these studies are consistent with our findings that a combination of thoracic and extrathoracic LT in addition to systemic IO is associated with improved survival in patients with other metastatic sites in the NCDB.

Limitations

Because this was a retrospective database study, certain limitations were inherent to the nature of data collection, including possible selection bias. In addition, the NCDB only documents metastatic sites at time of diagnosis and first treatment course, and included patients may have developed additional metastases to other sites after diagnosis or treatment with ST or LT, which could have implications for long-term survival. EGFR and ALK mutation status was unavailable before 2021, potentially affecting regression model accuracy and limiting clinical applicability to patients with driver mutations. Additional limitations include the lack of granular information in the NCDB regarding overall tumor burden, subtypes of ST, the anatomic site of the nonprimary surgery, RT received in addition to the primary volume, residual disease burden, duration of treatment response, or several other key covariates, such as smoking status. Additionally, cancer-specific survival and progression-free survival are not reported in the NCDB; thus, long-term time-to-event analysis is limited to OS. We recommend that our results be interpreted in the context of these limitations as well as likely selection bias.

Conclusions

The addition of LT to a systemic regimen containing IO was common for patients with single metastatic sites at Commission on Cancer hospitals in the United States. Patients selected for LT had a relatively favorable prognosis, which varied by metastatic location. Further study to determine the appropriate use of LT in the setting of single-site metastatic lung cancer is warranted.
