Congenital microtia–atresia in Iran: a case report
Authors: Mahsa Shiravani, Marzyeh Alipour, Ahmad Reza Mortajez, Sirous Naeimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrotia–atresia is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by an abnormally small external ear (microtia) with a narrow or blocked ear canal or its absence (atresia). Its incidence is between 3 and 10 cases per 10,000 live births. Microtia is bilateral in 10% of cases and in unilateral cases, the right ear is affected twice as often. Although genetic elements like chromosomal abnormalities and mutations are known to significantly contribute to the development of microtia, various environmental factors have also been linked to its occurrence.Case presentationThis article reports a term female infant of Iranian ethnicity with microtia–atresia resulting from cesarean delivery from a 25-year-old mother. On initial examination by a pediatrician, the right external ear of the infant was underdeveloped, with an abnormal appearance and an absence of the ear canal. On closer examination, no craniofacial abnormalities or signs of possible syndromes associated with microtia were observed.ConclusionThis report describes a rare case of isolated unilateral microtia with aural atresia in a newborn without additional congenital anomalies. Early recognition of such cases is crucial to ensure appropriate hearing evaluation, multidisciplinary management, and optimal developmental outcomes. Case reports of isolated microtia may provide valuable insights into its clinical variability and underlying etiological factors.

Introduction

Microtia and atresia are congenital abnormalities that affect the external ear and the ear canal, with an occurrence rate of approximately 3 to 10 cases per 10,000 live births [1] Microtia describes an underdeveloped or abnormally shaped outer ear and is frequently associated with atresia or malformation of the external auditory canal. It is bilateral in about 10% of cases, and in unilateral presentations, the right ear is affected twice as often as the left [2]. Both conditions exhibit varying degrees of severity and potential complications.

Microtia is most commonly classified using the Marx grading system, which ranges from Grade I to Grade IV [3] Grade I includes deformities where all auricular subunits are present, but the ear is at least two standard deviations smaller than normal, with a typically open ear canal [3]. Grade II involves deformity of the upper half to two-thirds of the auricle, while the remaining parts of the ear have identifiable structures; canal atresia may be present. Grade III often called "peanut-shaped auricular remnant" is the most frequent form and is characterized by a small cartilage remnant with an anterosuperiorly rotated lobule and commonly complete ear canal atresia. Grade IV, or anotia, is marked by a total absence of the external ear and ear canal [3].

The severity of aural atresia varies widely. Mild forms may involve a narrow ear canal and a basic tympanic membrane, whereas severe forms usually lack a visible canal and may have malformed or missing middle ear structures [4]. Other classification systems for microtia–atresia, such as those by Tanzer and Nagata, focus on the surgical reconstruction methods and techniques required [5].

Although the etiology of microtia is not fully understood, several chromosomal abnormalities, including trisomy 13, 18, and 22, as well as chromosomal deletions, microdeletions, and translocations with a hereditary background, have been identified as potential risk factors [6]. Similar to many other structural malformations, microtia with aural atresia may present either as an isolated anomaly or as part of a broader clinical syndrome, most commonly Treacher Collins and Goldenhar syndromes, which follow autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance patterns [6–8]. Common ear anomalies, such as prominent ear (prominauris), cryptotia, cup or lop ear deformities, Stahl’s ear, and lobular malformations can sometimes be misidentified as microtia [9].

Although genetic causes, including chromosomal defects and mutations, play a major role in the development of microtia, various environmental risk factors have also been linked to its occurrence. These include prenatal exposure to maternal medications [10], maternal illness [11], advanced parental age [11, 12], high number of previous births [11, 12], race or ethnicity [12], high-altitude living [13], urban residence [14], low maternal education [15, 16], multiple births [12, 13], and low birth weight [11–13]. Owing to the rarity of this condition, we present a case that was diagnosed at birth. Case report

A 25-year-old Iranian woman, gravida 3, abortion 1, living 1(G3P1A1L1), with a history of one previous cesarean delivery, was referred to the Women's Department of Vali Asr Hospital (AJ) in Kazerun city, accompanied by a referral letter from her gynecologist and in accordance with her expected delivery date. She was promptly admitted to the department. The gestational age was determined to be 38 weeks based on the first day of her last menstrual period and confirmed by ultrasound.

At 10:15 AM, following initial evaluations and preparation for cesarean section, the patient was taken to the operating room, where the delivery was performed. The newborn was a live female infant weighing 3700 g, with a head circumference of 37 cm, a height of 53 cm, and an Apgar score of 9 to 10. A deformity of the right ear was observed at birth.

The infant’s parents were consanguineous and had one previous healthy child. There was no family history of congenital ear abnormalities. The parents also reported no history of illness or allergy to any specific medications.

Upon physical examination, the pediatrician identified a Grade III microtia–atresia, characterized by underdevelopment of the external ear, a smaller than normal size, absence of the auditory canal, and a distinctive “peanut-shaped auricular remnant” appearance (Fig. 1). A comprehensive evaluation was conducted to investigate possible syndromic associations with microtia–atresia, including assessments of the infant’s quality of life indicators, hairline, mandible, oral cavity, cervical spine, and eyes. The pediatrician stressed the importance of early hearing assessment and timely surgical reconstruction of the external ear to prevent future speech and social development issues. The parents were advised to consult with otolaryngologists and specialists in plastic, reconstructive, and cosmetic surgery for further evaluation and management.

Discussion

Many patients with microtia–atresia experience some degree of hearing loss, which should be evaluated early ideally during infancy using auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing [3].

In the case of our patient, the newborn was born with Grade III microtia–atresia of the right ear. Although the contralateral ear appeared normal, early hearing assessment is crucial to determine the presence of any conductive or sensorineural deficits.

In this case, hearing screening (such as ABR or OAE) was not performed before discharge due to local hospital limitations; however, the parents were counseled about the importance of early auditory evaluation and were advised to schedule testing shortly after discharge.

The Grade III classification indicates severe underdevelopment of the external ear with complete absence of the auditory canal, which has important implications for both functional outcomes and the planning of surgical reconstruction.

Parental consanguinity, as in this case, increases the risk of recurrence in future offspring and highlights the need for genetic counseling, even in the absence of a known family history of congenital ear abnormalities. Families should be informed about potential inheritance patterns and the benefits of genetic evaluation for future pregnancies.

Most affected individuals have moderate-to-severe conductive hearing loss, typically ranging from 40 to 65 dB. In addition, up to 15% of patients may present with coexisting sensorineural hearing loss [3, 4]. Therefore, monitoring both ears is essential even if the deformity is unilateral, as in this patient.

In cases where the child is too young for surgical intervention, early auditory rehabilitation can be initiated using bone-conduction or conductive hearing aids [4].which can support proper speech and cognitive development during infancy.

Around the age of 5 to 6 years, a high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scan of the temporal bone is recommended to further evaluate the microtia deformity and determine the patient’s eligibility for surgical reconstruction.

CT imaging plays a crucial role in grading the severity of aural atresia using the Jahrsdoerfer grading system a 10-point scale used to predict surgical outcomes after atresiaplasty [4].

A score of 7 or above suggests an 85% to 90% likelihood of successful hearing improvement, whereas a score below 6 indicates a poor surgical prognosis [3].

A similar case reported a term male neonate presented with unilateral grade III microtia–atresia resembling a peanut-shaped auricle, without any associated craniofacial or syndromic anomalies, supporting the occurrence of isolated, non-syndromic forms of this malformation [17].In another report a 15-year-old with bilateral congenital outer ear malformations and conductive hearing loss treated with Bonebridge implants, highlighting the relevance of individualized management even in non-syndromic, unilateral cases like ours [18].

The primary goal of microtia treatment is to restore the aesthetic appearance of the external ear. In cases with minimal cosmetic or functional impairment, conservative management may suffice, and prosthetic ear placement can be a satisfactory alternative [3].

However, given the severity of microtia in our patient, early referral to otolaryngologists and reconstructive surgeons is warranted to discuss optimal timing and surgical options, including reconstruction using autologous rib cartilage or synthetic porous polyethylene (PPE) implants [19].

This individualized approach ensures both functional and aesthetic outcomes are considered, which is particularly important for supporting the child’s psychosocial development.

Prosthetic ear placement may be an alternative if surgical reconstruction is not immediately feasible or desired.

Addressing hearing loss remains equally important; bone-anchored hearing devices, whether implantable or headband-mounted, can provide effective auditory support during early childhood.

Early and proactive intervention is particularly critical in this patient due to the unilateral but severe nature of the deformity, to minimize potential delays in speech and social development [1].

Conclusion

Early hearing assessment and timely multidisciplinary management are crucial in patients with microtia–atresia. Prompt evaluation, imaging, and individualized intervention surgical or non-surgical help prevent developmental delays and optimize functional and psychosocial outcomes.
