BRD9-p53-E2F1 circuit orchestrates cell growth and DNA damage repair in gastric cancer
Authors: Qingqing Zhou, Qi Wang, Yantao Duan, Chi Zhang, Tengfei Liu, Hengrui Liu, Mindi Zhao, Zhihuang Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBRD9 is involved in multiple physiological and pathological pathways, yet its functional role and molecular mechanisms in gastric cancer (GC) remain largely unexplored. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical given the persistent global mortality burden of GC and the limited efficacy of current therapeutic strategies.MethodsBRD9 expression in GC patients was systematically analyzed using immunohistochemical (IHC) assays and transcriptomic datasets. Comprehensive functional validation, employing cellular and murine tumor models, elucidated BRD9’s role in GC progression. Molecular pathways underlying BRD9-mediated gastric carcinogenesis were delineated through integrated approaches, including RNA sequencing, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), subcellular fractionation, and luciferase reporter assays.ResultsBRD9 was significantly overexpressed in GC and associated with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, BRD9 promoted GC cell proliferation and enhanced DNA damage repair capacity. Mechanistically, elevated BRD9 expression inhibited p53 nuclear translocation via direct binding, subsequently activating the E2F transcription factor family. Notably, we identified that E2F1 directly bound to and transactivated the BRD9 promoter, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains BRD9 expression. Additionally, BRD9 knockdown sensitized GC cells to cisplatin and oxaliplatin treatment.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the critical role of BRD9 in GC progression and its therapeutic potential. The BRD9-p53-E2F1 axis acts as a crucial regulator of GC cell proliferation and DNA damage response. Targeting BRD9 pharmacologically could be a novel therapeutic approach to enhance chemotherapy efficacy and improve treatment outcomes in GC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02449-w.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a major global health burden, ranked as the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Its incidence and mortality exhibit significant geographical disparities, with high-risk regions including East Asia (notably China, Japan, and South Korea), Eastern Europe, and parts of Central and South America [3]. Early detection is challenging due to the lack of specific symptoms in the initial stages, resulting in a high proportion of cases diagnosed at advanced disease. The overall 5-year survival rate for GC is approximately 32%, but it plummets to as low as 6% for advanced-stage patients [4]. China carries a particularly severe burden, accounting for nearly 43.9% of global new GC cases [5]. Given this substantial disease burden, further research into the novel genetic alterations in GC development and its aggressive features are essential.

GC is a complex tumor characterized by substantial molecular and cellular heterogeneity. Its development and progression involve multiple signaling pathways interacting extensively with transcriptional and epigenetic regulators, forming a highly dynamic regulatory network. A key player within this network is the p53-E2F pathway. The relationship between E2F and p53 is complex and involves a fine balance that regulates cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and DNA damage response. E2F transcription factors, particularly E2F1, play a critical role in promoting cell cycle entry and proliferation by activating genes required for DNA synthesis and cell division [6]. On the other hand, p53, a well-known tumor suppressor, acts as a guardian of the genome by inducing cell cycle arrest to promote DNA repair or apoptosis in response to cellular stress or damage [7]. Under normal conditions, p53 can inhibit E2F1 activity to prevent uncontrolled cell proliferation [8, 9]. However, loss of functional p53 can lead to dysregulated E2F1 activity, contributing to tumorigenesis [10]. Conversely, E2F1 can also activate p53 under certain stress conditions, creating a feedback loop that amplifies p53’s tumor-suppressive functions [11]. This intricate interplay underscores their critical roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing carcinogenesis.

BRD9, an essential component of the non-canonical BAF (ncBAF) complex, has been identified as an oncogene in several malignancies [12, 13]. In SMARCB1-deficient malignant rhabdoid tumors and synovial sarcomas driven by SS18-SSX fusion proteins, BRD9 promotes tumor cell survival, and its depletion inhibits tumor growth [14, 15]. Within complexes like ncBAF, esBAF, and npBAF, BRD9 collaborates with the TGFβ/Activin-SMAD2/3 pathway to regulate cancer stem cell self-renewal, chemoresistance, and invasiveness in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [16]. BRD9 plays a pivotal role in regulating androgen receptor signaling and is essential for driving prostate cancer progression [17]. However, a tumor-suppressive role has been identified in uveal melanoma, where BRD9 depletion disrupts ncBAF complex localization at CTCF-associated sites, promoting melanoma genesis [18]. The antitumor efficacy observed with BRD9 interference across tumor entities establishes BRD9 inhibitor I-BRD9 as a scientifically grounded therapeutic candidate for testicular germ cell tumors [19]. Combining I-BRD9 with platinum agents or PARP inhibitors represents a promising therapeutic strategy for ovarian cancers [20]. While therapeutic approaches targeting BRD9 are under active investigation, the precise mechanisms by which BRD9 contributes to tumorigenesis remain poorly understood, particularly in GC.

Materials and methods

Multi-omics data collection and visualization

Standardized and log2-transformed RNA sequencing profiles, accompanied by the corresponding clinical metadata from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression projects, were retrieved from the UCSC Xena database. Concurrently, single-nucleotide variation (SNV), copy number variation (CNV) datasets, and methylation data were also procured from this repository. The “GeoTcgaData” R package was utilized to translate ensemble IDs into gene symbols. After the acquisition of the GC single-cell dataset HRA001689 from the National Genomics Data Center (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/), the “Seurat V5” R package was applied for the analysis and visualization of the data. The spatial transcriptome slice information of GC was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) resources, and gene expression information was mapped following spatial annotation. Simultaneously, GC cohort data, encompassing GSE13861, GSE57303, GSE84426, GSE84433, and GSE84437, were downloaded from the GEO database to serve as the validation set. A comprehensive set of 50 hallmark pathways was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) for pathway-associated scoring. The computation of the BRD score was predominantly achieved using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm implemented within the “GSVA” R package. The “survival” and “survminer” R packages were harnessed for the Kaplan-Meier analysis, and the optimal cut-off point was ascertained via the “surv_cutpoint” function.

Cell lines and cell culture

NCI-N87 and AGS as well as HEK-293T cells, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Other gastric cancer cell lines (MKN-45, MKN-74, HGC-27, NUGC3, NUGC4) and the immortalized gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 were sourced from the Chinese Academy of Science. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (BasalMedia, China, catalog no. L100KJ) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA, catalog no. 10099141), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (BasalMedia, China, catalog no. S110JV), cultured at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Lentiviral vector generation and transduction

The plasmid pLVX-BRD9 was provided by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). Plasmids encoding E2F1, E2F4, and E2F6 were obtained from Miaoling (Wuhan, China). The BRD9-specific lentiviral shRNA construct was subcloned into the pLKO.1 vector. The target sequences used for these constructs are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with target vectors, packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene) using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, France; catalog no. 1010000046) per manufacturer’s protocol. Supernatants containing viral particles were harvested 72 h post-transfection. GC cells were then infected with 1 × 10⁶ transducing units of recombinant lentivirus in the presence of 6 µg/ml polybrene (Beyotime, China; catalog no. C0351).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time RT-PCR (RT-qPCR)

TRIzol-based RNA extraction was followed by reverse transcription of normalized RNA yields to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) templates for qPCR using HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China, catalog no. R133). RT-qPCR analyses were conducted on a QuantStudio (TM) 6 Flex System using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China, catalog no. Q311). Relative gene expression (2 − ΔΔCt) was calculated following normalization of target Ct values to endogenous GAPDH controls. The primer sequences (BRD9, GAPDH) were provided in Supplementary Table 2. The primers (E2F1, E2F2, E2F3, E2F4, E2F5, E2F6) were provided by Beyotime (Shanghai, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed on a 78-sample GC tissue microarray. Two independent pathologists semi-quantitatively assessed immunostaining. The proportion of positive cells was scored: 0 (0%), 1 (< 10%), 2 (10%−35%), 3 (35%−75%), or 4 (> 75%). Staining intensity was graded: 0 (none), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). A final immunoreactivity score (proportion score × intensity score) ≤ 4 indicated low expression, while > 4 denoted high expression. This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from cells with lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1.0% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; Abmart, catalog no. AWB-0105) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Abmart, catalog no. A-10014 and A-10004) and resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels. After SDS-PAGE, proteins were wet-blotted onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA/TBS-T, then exposed to primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following TBS-T washes, HRP-linked secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence. Antibody-related information is summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Proliferation profiling and clonogenicity assays

Ninety-six-well plates were seeded with cells (1,000/well) and incubated overnight for subsequent proliferation quantification. After overnight incubation, proliferation was quantified using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Bimake, USA, catalog no. B34304). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Multi-mode Microplate Reader. For clonogenicity assessment, we cultured cells in 6-well plates (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 10–14 days. Resultant colonies were formalin-fixed (4%, 30 min) and Giemsa-stained for quantification.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were plated at a suitable density in 6 cm dishes and cultured overnight. After incubation with 10 mM DCHF-DA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, catalog no. C6827) for 30 min, the cells were washed twice with PBS. Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a BD FACS Celesta or BECKMAN COULTER CytoFLEX, flowJo v10 facilitated subsequent data analytics.

Immunofluorescence confocal imaging

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at RT, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS (30 min), then sequentially incubated with: (i) primary antibody overnight, (ii) secondary antibodies (1 h, RT) and DAPI. Antibody specifics are in Supplementary Table S3.

DNA Damage Assay Kit Using γH2AX Immunofluorescence (Beyotime, China, catalog no. C2037S) was applied to detect the γH2AX level.

Subcellular fractionation of nuclear, cytosolic, and mitochondrial compartments

Mitochondrial isolation from GC cells was conducted using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China, catalog no. C3601). Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separated with a nuclear extraction kit (Abmart, China, catalog no. A10009) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Co-IP assay

Cell lysates were prepared by ice-cold lysis buffer incubation (30 min), followed by centrifugation (12,000 g, 10 min). Antibodies against BRD9, p53, or IgG control were pre-bound to Protein A/G beads (Abmart, China, catalog no. A10001) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Washed immunocomplexes were denatured (10 min boiling) and subjected to immunoblotting.

Luciferase assay

The E2F and BRD9 promoters were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China) and IGEbio (Guangzhou, China), respectively. Cells were cultured in 24-well plates and co-transfected with the corresponding reporter plasmids and the indicated plasmids in each experiment according to the standard protocol. Luciferase activity was quantified with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega, USA, catalog no. E1910) per manufacturer guidelines.

In vivo growth assays

To evaluate tumor growth in vivo, nude mice received subcutaneous engraftment of 1 × 10^6 cells (resuspended in 200 µl PBS) into the left axillary fossa. Tumors were excised and weighed four weeks later. All procedures strictly adhered to the ethical guidelines approved by the Shanghai Medical Experimental Animal Care Commission.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Hypothesis testing employed two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA with post hoc corrections for multi-group comparisons. BRD9-clinicopathological correlations were evaluated by χ² analysis. Statistical significance thresholds were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01. All computations were executed in SPSS 24.0.

Results

A comprehensive dissection of the BRD gene family uncovers its profound significance in the pan-cancer context

Considering the burgeoning interest in the BRD family genes in recent times, we embarked on an extensive pan-cancer analysis with the aim of discerning both the shared characteristics and idiosyncrasies of this family genes during tumorigenesis and disease progression. Employing the GSVA algorithm, we assigned scores to common cancers by considering the BRD family genes as an integrated unit and designated this cumulative metric as the “BRDscore”. The BRDscore manifested as positive in malignancies such as colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), kidney chromophobe (KICH), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC). Conversely, it was negative in tumors like kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), and thyroid carcinoma (THCA) (Fig. 1A). To elucidate these disparities, we performed an enrichment analysis of the BRDscore at the pan-cancer scale, primarily leveraging gene sets typified by “Hallmarks”. The most striking outcomes was the activation of the E2F Targets pathway, intimating that this pathway might play conserved roles in the BRDs - mediated tumor - promoting cascade (Fig. 1B and C). We delved deeper into the expression profiles of the BRD family genes across diverse malignant neoplasms. The findings revealed that the expression patterns of different family members diverged markedly among various tumor types. Notably, genes such as BRD9 and BRD4 exhibited significant expression across the pan-cancer spectrum (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the expression of BRD family genes varied substantially between different cancer entities. For instance, in THCA and STAD, these genes were more highly expressed, whereas in BLCA and ESCA, their expression levels were comparatively low. Subsequently, we investigated the impact of these expression discrepancies on patient survival, concentrating specifically on overall survival. Genes such as BRD4 and BRD9 exerted a significant influence on the survival of patients with diverse malignancies (Fig. 1E). To unearth the underlying mechanisms responsible for the heterogeneity in BRD, we conducted functional enrichment analyses on each of the nine family genes, which demonstrated that BRD significantly modulated processes such as the cell cycle and responses to DNA damage (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, we scrutinized the genomic architecture of the BRD family genes, with a particular emphasis on the pan-cancer differences in CNV and SNV. At the pan-cancer level, BRD4 and BRD1 were associated with a higher frequency of SNV mutation events, while BRD7 and BRD9 were more prone to CNV mutation events (Fig. 1G and H). We further probed the CNV landscape of these genes in cancers. The results corroborated that distinct cancer types harbored divergent CNV patterns, with heterozygous amplification and deletion being the predominant mutation modalities. Nevertheless, this generalization pertained solely to the pan-cancer level. Tumors such as ACC, STAD, and TGCT exhibited unique mutation landscapes (Supplementary Figure S1A). We also dissected the differences in the heterozygous CNV profiles, which presented the percentages of heterozygous CNVs, including the amplification and deletion frequencies of heterozygous BRD CNVs in each cancer type. The results disclosed that the heterozygous copy number variations of BRD were prevalent in the vast majority of cancers, potentially implicating their involvement in aspects such as cancer initiation and progression (Fig. 1I). The SNV landscape of the BRD family genes in cancers delineated the distribution of diverse SNV types among different cancer entities. The data revealed that the majority of SNVs were missense mutations, with the nonsense mutations of BRD7 being particularly conspicuous. Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) events occurred with high frequency and represented the most prevalent form of genetic variation (Supplementary Figure S1B-1 C).

Elevated BRD9 expression is significantly associated with an unfavorable prognosis in GC patients

A comprehensive dissection of the BRD gene family uncovers its profound significance within STAD. Notably, the value of this family in STAD is particularly concentrated in BRD9, a member that exhibits differential expression across most cancer types in the pan-cancer landscape. Consequently, we initiated an investigation by probing the expression patterns of BRD family genes in GC. Transcriptomic data sourced from TCGA unambiguously demonstrated that all BRD family genes were significantly overexpressed in GC (Fig. 2A). Analysis of single-cell datasets further revealed that, except BRD2, these genes were overexpressed in cancerous epithelial cells (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we harnessed UMAP for dimensionality reduction to explore the expression of BRD9 in diverse cell populations within the GC microenvironment. Our findings indicated a preferential and concentrated expression of BRD9 in malignant tumor cells (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Figure S2A). Given the established role of BRD family genes in influencing the transcription factor pathway, we delved deeper into comparing the expression of BRD9 under distinct chromatin accessibility conditions. Strikingly, in contrast to normal epithelial cells, BRD9 in malignant cells exhibited enhanced chromatin accessibility, suggesting an associated elevation in transcriptional activation (Fig. 2E). Data derived from spatial transcriptomics disclosed the spatial - distributional specificity of BRD family genes in STAD, with BRD9 emerging as a particularly prominent contributor (Fig. 2F-G and Supplementary Figure S2B).

To validate these findings, we detected the gene expression of BRD9 in clinical samples. In contrast to normal tissues, BRD9 was significantly overexpressed in GC samples (Fig. 2H). The results from the IHC assay further supported these observations (Fig. 2I). Notably, the expression level of BRD9 correlated with different stages of GC, with its expression increased as the tumor stage progressed (Fig. 2J). We evaluated BRD9 expression in 78 GC tissues through IHC staining, and based on the results, we categorized the patients into high-expression and low-expression groups. The results demonstrated that BRD9 expression was associated with tumor size, T stage, and differentiation grade (Table 1).

BRD9 drives the growth of GC cells in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the role of BRD9 in GC progression, we assessed BRD9 expression levels in GC cell lines by Western blot analysis (Supplementary Figure S3A). GC cell lines with either low or high endogenous BRD9 expression were chosen for gain and loss-of-function studies, respectively. As shown in the results, BRD9 overexpression promoted GC cell proliferation and colony formation (Fig. 3A-C and Supplementary Figure S3B), while BRD9 knockdown inhibited these cellular processes (Fig. 3D-F and Supplementary Figure S3C). Moreover, EdU incorporation assays revealed that DNA synthesis increased in GC cells overexpressing BRD9 and decreased in BRD9-knockdown GC cells (Fig. 3G-I).

To verify the in vitro observations, we investigated the impact of BRD9 on tumor growth through a xenograft tumor model. As depicted in Fig. 3J and K, overexpression of BRD9 in NCI-N87 cells accelerated tumor growth in nude mice. In contrast, tumors derived from NCI-N87 cells with shBRD9 displayed significantly reduced volumes and weights compared to those with shNC. Elevated BRD9 expression persisted in tissues of BRD9-overexpressing xenografts, contrasting with the reduced levels observed in BRD9 knockdown models (Supplementary Figure S3D-3E). In the meantime, expression of Ki-67 and PCNA cells was significantly higher in the BRD9-overexpressed group and markedly lower in the shBRD9 group compared to their respective negative controls (Fig. 3L and M).

BRD9 enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of GC cells

Given the fundamental role of metastasis in malignancy, we assessed BRD9’s effects on GC cell migration and invasion. The results demonstrated that the overexpression of BRD9 significantly enhanced the migratory and invasive capabilities of GC cells, while the knockdown of BRD9 markedly suppressed these behaviors (Supplementary Figure S4A-4D). Additionally, the results revealed that filopodia were significantly more numerous and elongated in BRD9-overexpressing cells compared to control cells, while filopodia were significantly fewer and shorter in BRD9-knockdown cells compared to control cells (Supplementary Figure S4E-4 F).

BRD9 is essential for regulating DNA damage repair

Disruptions in the DNA damage response pathway can lead to genomic instability, driving oncogenesis [21, 22]. Previous studies have indicated that BRD-containing proteins, such as BRD4 and ZMYND8, may play significant roles in DNA repair processes [23, 24]. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether BRD9 is involved in regulating DNA damage repair in GC.

The results indicated that overexpression of BRD9 led to a reduction in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, while knockdown of BRD9 increased ROS levels (Fig. 4A and B). The level of γ-H2AX, produced by the phosphorylation of H2AX, serves as an indicator of the extent of DNA damage repair. Western blot analysis conducted between 8 and 16 h after cisplatin treatment revealed a significantly elevated level of γH2AX in BRD9-knockdown GC cells compared to control cells (Fig. 4C). To confirm the above results, we conducted HR (homologous recombination) and NHEJ (non-homologous end joining) reporter assays. The results revealed a significant impairment in HR efficiency in BRD9-knockdown cells, while NHEJ activity remained largely unaffected. Treatment with the BRD9-specific inhibitor, I-BRD9, led to a pronounced reduction in HR activity (Fig. 4D). Additionally, BRD9 overexpression in GC cells promotes HR activity (Fig. 4E).

Cisplatin-induced γH2AX foci can be visualized and quantified using immunofluorescence. The gradual resolution of these foci over time following DNA damage serves as an indicator of the cell’s capacity to perform double-strand break repair. The absence of BRD9 did not affect the formation of γH2AX foci at 2 h after cisplatin treatment. However, between 8 and 16 h after cisplatin treatment, we observed a significant delay in the clearance of γH2AX foci in BRD9-knockdown GC cells, indicating reduced functional double-strand break repair activity (Fig. 4F and G). Collectively, these data suggest that BRD9 plays a positive regulatory role in DNA double-strand break repair through HR.

BRD9 inhibits p53 nuclear translocation, thereby activating the E2F signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanisms by which BRD9 functions in GC cells, RNA sequencing was performed on total RNAs extracted from BRD9-overexpressing and their respective control cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis demonstrated that the E2F pathway was significantly modulated in response to BRD9 overexpression (Fig. 5A). GSEA further revealed that the E2F signaling pathway was significantly altered in response to BRD9 overexpression (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 1B and C, the pronounced activation of E2F transcriptional targets provides empirical validation of its evolutionarily conserved role in BRD-mediated oncogenic processes. Thus, we hypothesized that BRD9 might promote the malignant behavior of GC by activating the E2F signaling pathway. qPCR analysis revealed that BRD9 overexpression in AGS and NCI-N87 cells upregulated the expression of E2F1, E2F2, and E2F6 (Fig. 5C). Conversely, knockdown of BRD9 in NCI-N87 suppressed the expression of E2F1, E2F2, E2F4, E2F5 and E2F6 (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, BRD9 overexpression promoted E2F transactivation, while BRD9 knockdown inhibited E2F transactivation, suggesting that BRD9 plays a role in mediating E2F activity (Fig. 5E). Additionally, we found a significant positive correlation between the expression of BRD9 and E2F family members (E2F1, E2F2, E2F3, E2F4, E2F5, and E2F6) in GC tissue (Fig. 5F).

BRD are essential for p53 to exert its biological functions [25, 26]. Given the complex and tightly interconnected regulatory relationship between p53 and E2F, we hypothesize that BRD9 may activate the E2F signaling pathway through a p53-dependent mechanism. While Co-IP assays revealed an interaction between BRD9 and p53 (Fig. 5G), the BRD9 inhibitor I-BRD9 markedly diminished BRD9-p53 binding affinity (Fig. 5H). Moreover, confocal colocalization analysis further confirmed that BRD9 directly interacted with p53 (Fig. 5I). Next, we analyzed the expression level of p53 and its subcellular localization. BRD9 knock-in significantly reduced the expression of p53 in the nucleus and mitochondria, but not in the cytoplasm, whereas BRD9 knockout markedly increased the expression of p53 in the nucleus and mitochondria, without affecting its levels in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5J and K). Of note, BRD9 overexpression decreased p53 phosphorylation at Ser15, while BRD9 depletion potentiated this modification. Phosphorylation at Ser392, however, was largely resistant to BRD9 modulation (Supplementary Figure S5A-5B). Besides, IHC also demonstrated that p53 was downregulated in murine xenografts from BRD9-overexpressing NCI-N87 cells (Fig. 5L). Pifithrin-α reversibly inhibits p53-dependent transcriptional activation of target genes. Notably, Pifithrin-α treatment significantly reversed the level of E2F1, E2F2, E2F4 and E2F6 that were decreased by BRD9 knockdown (Fig. 5D). Pifithrin-α treatment also significantly reversed the E2F transcriptional activity decreased by BRD9 knockdown in tumor cells (Fig. 5M). These findings demonstrate that BRD9 activates the E2F signaling pathway in a p53-dependent manner.

BRD9 drives GC progression through the p53-E2F pathway

To confirm the role of the p53 signaling pathway in BRD9-mediated GC cell proliferation and DNA damage repair, we employed Pifithrin-α, a p53 inhibitor, to suppress p53-dependent transcriptional activation in BRD9-knockdown GC cells (Fig. 6A). The inhibition of cell growth induced by BRD9 knockdown was partially reversed by Pifithrin-α treatment (Fig. 6B-D). Pifithrin-α treatment could hinder ROS accumulation induced by BRD9 knockdown (Fig. 6E and F). Moreover, we found significantly delayed clearance of γH2AX foci in BRD9 knockdown was partially reversed by Pifithrin-α treatment (Fig. 6G and H).

BRD9 knockdown increases the sensitivity of GC cells to cisplatin and oxaliplatin

Building on these findings, we investigated whether BRD9 could serve as a potential therapeutic target for GC treatment. Multiple GC cohort data demonstrated that the expression level of BRD9 is positively correlated with cisplatin IC50, suggesting that BRD9 may be a critical factor influencing cisplatin resistance (Fig. 8A). Cell viability assays demonstrated that treatment with the BRD9-specific inhibitor, I-BRD9, or BRD9-deficient GC cells resulted in increased sensitivity to cisplatin, while BRD9 overexpression in GC cells enhanced resistance to cisplatin (Fig. 8B and C). The perioperative FLOT protocol—combining 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel—constitutes the standard-of-care for locally advanced gastric adenocarcinoma [27–29]. Therefore, we wonder whether inhibiting BRD9 increases the susceptibility of GC cells to oxaliplatin treatment. The results demonstrated that I-BRD9 treatment or BRD9 knockdown sensitized GC cells to oxaliplatin, whereas BRD9 overexpression conferred resistance (Fig. 8D and E). Additionally, the results showed that treatment with I-BRD9 or BRD9 knockdown enhanced cell apoptosis induced by cisplatin (Fig. 8F). Thus, these findings indicate that BRD9 knockdown enhances the sensitivity of GC cells to cisplatin and oxaliplatin, offering a promising therapeutic strategy.

Discussion

This study elucidates the role of BRD9 in GC progression and its underlying molecular mechanisms. We observed BRD9 upregulation in GC, which was associated with unfavorable patient outcomes. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated that BRD9 facilitates proliferation and HR repair in GC cells by suppressing p53 nuclear translocation, resulting in E2F pathway activation. Additionally, genetic depletion of BRD9 or pharmacological inhibition using I-BRD9 enhanced the sensitivity of GC cells to cisplatin and oxaliplatin.

The BRD family comprises evolutionarily conserved modules that recognize acetylated lysine residues on histone tails and other proteins [30]. Dysregulation of these proteins is frequently implicated in cancer, contributing to oncogenic gene fusions [31]. BRD9 exemplifies the context-dependent functionality of BRD protein. While often acting as an oncogene, BRD9 loss-of-function significantly inhibits multiple myeloma cell growth in vitro and in vivo [32]. BRD9 plays a critical role in delineating a distinct SWI/SNF sub-complex and represents a unique therapeutic target in malignant rhabdoid tumors [14]. BRD9 represents a selective functional dependency in synovial sarcoma, underscoring its potential as a novel therapeutic target for this disease [33]. BRD9 also regulates human embryonic stem cell self-renewal and differentiation and is implicated in pancreatic and breast cancer progression [34]. However, in uveal melanoma, BRD9 acts as a potent tumor suppressor. Restoring proper splicing of BRD9 in SF3B1-mutant cells through antisense oligonucleotides or CRISPR-mediated mutagenesis effectively inhibits tumor growth [18]. Despite increasing research interest, BRD9’s role in GC remained poorly characterized. Our study demonstrated that BRD9 is markedly overexpressed in GC and that its elevated expression is associated with poorer prognosis, suggesting that BRD9 may serve as an independent prognostic marker. While BRD9 is widely recognized as an oncogene in many cancers, it can also exhibit tumor-suppressive properties in specific cancer types. This study further demonstrates that BRD9 promotes GC growth in vitro and in vivo. While ROS and DDR pathways are essential for maintaining genomic integrity, their dysregulation in cancer can drive tumor progression and therapy resistance [35–37]. We identified BRD9 as a key factor promoting genomic stability and efficient DDR. This is mediated through BRD9-dependent reduction of ROS levels and enhancement of HR repair capacity.

As a non-BET bromodomain protein, BRD9 sustains oncogenic transcription by binding acetylated histones, driving proliferation and survival [38, 39]. Proteomic studies have identified BRD9 as a specific component of the mammalian SWI/SNF complex, the most frequently mutated chromatin-regulatory complex in human cancers [40]. BRD9 actively participates in transcriptional regulation by interacting with key transcription factors, including Smad2/3, POU2F3, and STAT5A [16, 41, 42]. Studies reveal that in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the BRD9 component of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling complex plays a crucial role in sustaining MYC expression, promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation, and maintaining a differentiated state [43]. BRD9 orchestrates the stemness of pancreatic CSCs by interacting with the TGFβ/Activin-SMAD2/3 signaling pathway [16]. BRD9 interacts with androgen receptor and CCCTC-binding factor to modulate AR-dependent gene expression, establishing it as a critical regulator of androgen receptor signaling and prostate cancer progression [17]. BRD9-mediated regulation of the RAD54–RAD51 complex facilitates homologous recombination repair, linking it to chemoresistance [20]. Our investigation revealed a novel regulatory axis involving BRD9, p53, and E2F1. We demonstrate that elevated BRD9 inhibits p53 nuclear translocation via direct binding, leading to activation of the E2F family, including E2F1, E2F2, and E2F6. More importantly, upregulated BRD9 appeared to inhibit p53 nucleus translocation via direct binding with p53, which was the underlying mechanism for BRD9 in activating the E2F family.

Huang et al. demonstrated that miR-140-3p directly targeted BRD9 mRNA, hindering its protein translation. The downregulation of BRD9 subsequently decreased expression of c-myc, a key oncogene involved in cell proliferation [44]. We discovered that E2F1 directly binds to and transactivates the BRD9 promoter, establishing a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop. This finding provides a molecular basis for BRD9 overexpression in GC, which is consistent with its established oncogenic role with E2F1, a transcription factor regulating genes involved in cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and DNA repair. Overexpression of E2F1 promotes cell proliferation and enhances DNA damage repair, which were effectively reversed by treatment with BRD9 shRNA, indicating a functional dependency on BRD9. Importantly, correlation between E2F1 and BRD9 expression in GC tissues supports the co-existence and functional significance of the BRD9-p53-E2F1 axis in GC pathogenesis, suggesting that this axis mediates key oncogenic properties downstream of E2F1.

Platinum-based chemotherapy remains standard for GC despite its toxicity and limiting clinical efficacy [28, 45–47]. Platinum drugs affect gastrointestinal cancer cell metabolism, induce ROS and cell senescence, cause DNA damage, depolarize mitochondrial membranes, and induce apoptosis to achieve anti-tumor activity [48, 49]. Emerging studies demonstrate that m⁶A RNA methylation drives gastric carcinoma pathogenesis by promoting tumor initiation, progression, and chemotherapy resistance [50]. H3K18la was up-regulated in cisplatin-resistant bladder cancer and heightened expression of transcription factors such as YBX1 and YY1, ultimately driving cisplatin resistance [51]. Autophagic dysregulation participates in modulating, either increasing or decreasing, cisplatin resistance in cancer cells [52]. Significantly, our findings position BRD9 as a novel contributor to chemoresistance. We show that targeting BRD9, either genetically or pharmacologically with I-BRD9, markedly enhances sensitivity of GC cells to both cisplatin and oxaliplatin. This positions BRD9 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy to potentiate the efficacy of standard platinum-based regimens in GC.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings establish a pivotal role of BRD9 in driving GC progression via a functionally integrated BRD9-p53-E2F1 signaling axis (Fig. 9). BRD9 directly binds to p53, sequesters it from the nucleus, and inhibits its tumor-suppressive functions. This derepresses E2F activity, primarily through E2F1, fueling cell growth and enhancing DDR capability. Furthermore, the reciprocal transcriptional regulation–where E2F1 transactivates the BRD9 promoter–creates a potent self-sustaining oncogenic loop. This BRD9-p53-E2F1 axis represents a critical vulnerability in GC. Consequently, targeting BRD9 (e.g., with inhibitors like I-BRD9) emerges as a highly promising therapeutic strategy, particularly to sensitize GC tumors to platinum chemotherapies. This study not only uncovers a fundamental mechanism underlying BRD9 oncogenic activity in GC but also provides a foundation for the development of novel therapeutic interventions.
