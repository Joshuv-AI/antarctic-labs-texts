Codonopsis pilosula polysaccharide suppresses the progression of non-small cell lung cancer by triggering NLRP3/GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis
Authors: Shaoyan Huang, Quanxing Li, Yaobo Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most common and lethal malignancies worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases. NSCLC encompasses various histological types, including adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [1]. Due to the lack of early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, making treatment challenging and leading to poor prognosis. Traditional treatment methods include surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, but they have limitations in addressing the complexity and heterogeneity of NSCLC [2]. These treatment methods often face challenges such as recurrence, radiation-induced lung injury, patient tolerance issues, and drug toxicity [3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new therapeutic strategies to improve the survival rates and quality of life for NSCLC patients.

In recent years, the induction of pyroptosis has garnered significant attention as a novel cancer treatment strategy [4]. Pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory programmed cell death closely associated with immune regulation [5]. During pyroptosis, the release of intracellular components such as nuclear DNA and other inflammatory signaling molecules can induce the infiltration and activation of immune cells, thereby promoting inflammatory responses within the tumor microenvironment [6]. Studies have shown that inducing pyroptosis can enhance antitumor immune responses and inhibit tumor growth. For instance, Zhou et al. [7] discovered that targeting the mevalonate pathway can enhance antitumor immunity through the induction of pyroptosis. Su et al. [8] demonstrated in lung and pancreatic cancer models that Src administration can induce pyroptosis and reverse chemotherapy resistance. Lin et al. [9] found that Gasdermin E (GSDME) plays a critical role in vaccinia virus-induced tumor cell pyroptosis and subsequent antitumor immune activation. Ding et al. [10] identified that one of the mechanisms by which ZIF-8 nanoparticles efficiently inhibit tumor growth is through the activation of pyroptosis. Moreover, studies have found that inducing pyroptosis in NSCLC cells can effectively inhibit tumor growth and metastasis [11].

In the mechanism of pyroptosis, ROS accumulation and activation of inflammatory pathways play critical roles. ROS are important cellular signaling molecules that can regulate various cellular functions, including apoptosis and pyroptosis [12]. High levels of ROS can induce pyroptosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and the NF-κB signaling pathway [13]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key regulator of pyroptosis, capable of recognizing and responding to various intracellular and extracellular stress signals, such as ROS accumulation, thereby activating caspase-1. This activation subsequently cleaves Gasdermin D (GSDMD), forming pyroptotic pores that lead to cell membrane perforation and cell death [14]. Additionally, the activation of NF-κB can promote the expression of various inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β and IL-18. These factors not only induce the infiltration and activation of immune cells but also further enhance pyroptosis through autocrine and paracrine pathways [15].

Codonopsis pilosula polysaccharides (CPP), one of the main components of the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Codonopsis pilosula, have been reported in numerous studies to exhibit significant antitumor activity and immunomodulatory effects [16, 17]. Additionally, CPP is closely associated with ROS accumulation and inflammatory responses [18]. Therefore, CPP may induce pyroptosis by promoting ROS accumulation and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling pathway. This mechanism not only inhibits tumor cell proliferation but also further suppresses tumor growth and metastasis by enhancing the immune response.

In summary, we propose the following research hypothesis: CPP may inhibit the proliferation of NSCLC cells and promote apoptosis by inducing pyroptosis. Specifically, we hypothesize that CPP can promote the occurrence of pyroptosis by upregulating the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins such as NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, and caspase-1. Additionally, we hypothesize that CPP can enhance pyroptosis and antitumor immune responses by promoting ROS accumulation and activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the release of inflammatory factors.

To validate these hypotheses, we will conduct a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments to investigate the effects of CPP on the proliferation, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and inflammatory response of NSCLC A549 cells. Through these experiments, we aim to elucidate the multifaceted roles of CPP in inhibiting NSCLC progression and provide a theoretical foundation and experimental evidence for its potential as a novel therapeutic agent for NSCLC.

Methods

Cell culture and cell treatment

Purchase and thaw NSCLC A549 cells (C6053, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cells will be cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (PM150110B, Pricella, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (MBS2568758, MyBioSource, San Diego, California, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin mixture (PSF-1, Zen-Bio, Inc., North Carolina, USA). Logarithmically growing cells will be randomly divided into control and treatment groups. The treatment groups will be treated with different concentrations (10, 20, 40, and 80 µmol/L) of CPP, while the control group will be supplemented with the same volume of medium.

Cell viability

Logarithmically growing A549 cells were harvested to prepare a cell suspension. The cells were treated with 10, 20, 40, 80 µmol/L CPP, and a control group was established (treated with an equivalent volume of culture medium), using DMEM as the zero-setting medium. After 48 h of incubation, CCK8 reagent (C0037, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was put into each well, followed by incubation. Cell viability was calculated via measurement of the OD values.

TUNEL

TUNEL assay for detecting cell apoptosis involved harvesting logarithmically growing cells, fixing them, and washing them with PBS. The cells were permeabilized via the addition of 1% Triton X-100, and then they were incubated for 50 μL under Shading protection (green fluorescence) with TUNEL reagent (C1086, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cell nuclei were counterstained with 200 μL of DAPI reagent (blue fluorescence). Following the sealing of the slides, TUNEL and DAPI pictures from the same field were superimposed, and staining patterns were examined under a microscope.

Tumor tissues were fixed, permeabilized, incubated on ice with PBS consisting of 0.1% Triton X-100, and then treated in TUNEL solution (C1091, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C under Shading protection for 60 min in order to detect TUNEL in the tissues. Subsequently, the cells were sealed with Anti-fluorescence quenching agent, and the image was observed under a fluorescent microscope.

Levels of ROS detection

Cells that grew logarithmically were taken out and given the appropriate treatments based on their grouping. For each specimen, three replicate wells were prepared in accordance with the guidelines provided in the Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Detection Kit (S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The quantity of ROS in each group’s cells was measured.

Western blotting (WB)

Collect logarithmically growing cells from each group and lyse them by adding lysis buffer (R0278, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Utilizing the BCA protein analysis kit, ascertain the total protein concentration following centrifugation. Subsequently, aliquots of the lysed protein were separated using SDS-PAGE (B7021S, New England Biolabs, USA) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking the membrane with a buffer containing 0.5% TBS Tween20 and 5% BSA, incubate primary antibodies anti-β-actin (AC004, 1:2000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), cleaved caspase-1 (A25308, 1:2000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), NLRP3 (A5652, 1:2000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), ASC (A1170, 1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), GSDMD (A17308, 1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), NF-κB p65 (A6667, 1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), and p-NF-κB p65 (AP0125, 1:2000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). After washing the membrane with TBST, add secondary antibodies, capture images, and analyze the grayscale values of protein bands using ImageJ software.

Detection of cellular pyroptotic morphology

Logarithmically growing cells were cultured under the presence or absence of CPP (40 µmol/L). Photomicrographs were captured using an inverted microscope.

ELISA

Following the collection of supernatant from each batch of treated cells, the Human IL-1β ELISA Kit (EK0392) and Human IL-18 ELISA Kit (EK0864) were utilized to assess the amounts of IL-1β and IL-18, respectively. The kit manuals’ directions were followed during the testing processes. An ELISA reader was employed to measure the optical density (D value) of each sample across all groups at a wavelength of 450 nm. The supplier of all test kits was BOSTER Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan).

LDH release experiment

Gather the supernatant from each batch of cells. The instructions supplied were followed to assess the LDH levels in the cell supernatant using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C0016, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Using an EnVision microplate reader, the optical density (D value) of each sample in each group was determined at a wavelength of 490 nm.

Animals and subcutaneous tumor mouse model

A549 cells were cultured in vitro, and logarithmically growing cells were harvested, counted using a counting plate, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 107/mL. Each BALB/c nude mouse (hnslkjd006, Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd) received a subcutaneous injection of the NSCLC cell suspension into the right dorsal skin. mice were randomly separated into two groups for CPP and control. Intragastric administration of 100 mg/kg of dissolved CPP was given to the mice in the CPP group once a day, whereas animals in the control group were provided with an equivalent amount of physiological saline. For 7 days, both groups had constant treatment. Following the initiation of therapy, the tumors’ longest and shortest diameters were measured daily using calipers to track their development. The formula to compute the tumor volume was V (mm3) = 1/6 × π × length × width2. The mice were euthanized the day following the last injection, and the tumor tissues were taken out, fixed, dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned (thickness: 4 μm). The study strictly followed the 3R principles and was approved by the Animal Committee of Hunan Evidence-based Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Ethics No. XZ20240027). The maximum tumor size allowed by the Ethics Committee for mice is 2000 mm3, which was not exceeded in this study.

HE staining

Standard processing techniques for tumor samples included dehydration, embedding, and sectioning. Appropriate staining was carried out using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, C0105S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to aid in visibility. The slices were inspected under an optical microscope to assess the tissues’ histological alterations.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After dehydration, embedding, and sectioning, the acquired tumor tissues were fixed, and paraffin sections were generated. Following the application of primary antibodies NLRP3 (A5652, 1:100, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) and IL-1β (FNab04209, 1:50, Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd.), secondary antibodies were incubated for 60 min. DAB staining, hematoxylin counterstaining, xylene transparency, neutral gum mounting, and optical microscope inspection were the next procedures.

Statistical analysis

Data will be statistically analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and results will be expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons among multiple groups will be conducted using one-way ANOVA. For pairwise comparisons, Tukey’s test will be used if variances are equal, and Dunnett’s T3 test will be used if variances are unequal. A P-value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Results

The proliferation of NSCLC A549 cells is suppressed, and their apoptosis is encouraged by CPP

Initially, we bought NSCLC A549 cells and subjected them to treatment with varying concentrations of CPP (0, 10, 20, 40, 80 µmol/L) for subsequent analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, we noted that when CPP concentrations in A549 cells increased, there was a progressive suppression of cell viability within the concentration range of 40 µmol/L (P < 0.05). However, as the concentration of CPP reached 80 µmol/L, the negative impact on cell viability plateaued. To uncover further concerning the apoptotic rate, we opted for doses of 0, 10, 20, and 40 µmol/L. The findings of the experiment, which are exhibited in Fig. 1B, point to a strong pro-apoptotic effect at 20 µmol/L CPP (P < 0.05). No discernible increase in efficacy was seen at higher concentrations.

The proliferation of NSCLC A549 cells is suppressed, and their apoptosis is encouraged by CPP. A Cell viability. B Apoptosis rate. *p < 0.05 and ns means no statistical difference. Results are illustrated as mean ± SD

CPP activates NF-κB protein and appeals to ROS accumulation in A549 cells

Following this, as Fig. 2A illustrates, we noticed that as the concentration of CPP raised the expression of both p-NF-κB p-p65 and total NF-κB p65 protein elevated. Additionally, the p-p65/p65 ratio progressively rose (P < 0.05). In addition, we discovered that CPP could encourage the buildup of ROS (Fig. 2B) (P < 0.05).

CPP activates NF-κB protein and appeals to ROS accumulation in A549 cells. A The protein expression of p65 and p-p65. B ROS level. *p < 0.05 vs the control group, #p < 0.05 vs the 10 µmol/L group, and ^p < 0.05 vs the 20 µmol/L group. Results are illustrated as mean ± SD

CPP promotes pyroptosis and pyroptosis-related proteins in A549 cells

By analyzing the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, we identified that CPP might upregulate the production of proteins such as NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, and caspase-1 (P < 0.05). Notably, the most pronounced effect was observed at a CPP concentration of 40 µmol/L (Fig. 3A) (P < 0.05). Following treatment, we analyzed the cellular morphology of the cells treated with and without 40 µmol/L CPP. Figure 3B conveys that, in contrast to the CPP-treated cells, which showed swelling, the release of inflammatory substances, and finally permeabilization that resulted in cell rupture and death, the control group’s cells retained their normal cellular shape. All things taken into account, our research indicates that CPP promotes pyroptosis in A549 cells.

B Cell morphology. *p < 0.05 vs the control group, #p < 0.05 vs the 10 µmol/L group, and ^p < 0.05 vs the 20 µmol/L group. Results are illustrated as mean ± SD

CPP aids in the release of IL-1β, IL-18, and LDH in A549 cells

As illustrated in Fig. 4, we noticed a noticeable enhancement in the release of IL-1β, IL-18, and LDH with CPP treatment (P < 0.05). Notably, after reaching a concentration of 20 µmol/L CPP, further increases in concentration did not result in a more pronounced promotion of IL-1β and IL-18 release.

CPP limits tumor growth and fosters apoptosis in tumor-bearing mice

As depicted in the following figure, we established an in vivo tumor model through subcutaneous implantation and then administered the mice with CPP. The experimental results following CPP administration demonstrate a considerable suppression of tumor development, displayed in Fig. 5A. A discernible reduction in the tumor’s length, width, volume, and weight is the sign of this (P < 0.05). Additionally, compared to the control group, the CPP-treated group exhibited evident cell shrinkage, cell death, and an expanding necrotic area (Fig. 5B). In addition, there was a noticeable difference between the CPP-treated group and the control group based on the TUNEL immunohistochemistry data, which disclosed an obvious rise in apoptosis (Fig. 5B) (P < 0.05).

CPP enhances the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins in tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice

Subsequently, through experiments, we noticed that CPP boost the expression of cleaved caspase-1, NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD proteins in tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6A) (P < 0.05). The immunohistochemistry results for NLRP3 further corroborated that CPP promotes pyroptosis in tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6B) (P < 0.05). These results are consistent with in vitro experiment results.

CPP fosters the release of IL-1β and IL-18 in tumor tissues of tumor-bearing mice

Additionally, our research revealed that in comparison to the control group, the tumor tissues of the mice in the CPP group had considerably higher levels of IL-1β and IL-18 secretion (Fig. 7A) (P < 0.05). Additional evidence was supplied by IL-1β immunohistochemistry results, which exhibited a noteworthy distinction from the control group (P < 0.05). The result is in line with the findings from in vitro research (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

Codonopsis pilosula polysaccharides (CPP) are one of the main components of Codonopsis pilosula. CPP exhibit various biological activities, including immunomodulation, antioxidant, anti-aging, and antitumor effects [19]. Chen et al. demonstrated that pectic polysaccharides isolated from Codonopsis pilosula can activate apoptotic signaling pathways, promoting apoptosis in NSCLC A549 cells [20]. Similarly, our research has shown that CPP can effectively inhibit the proliferation of A549 cells and exert significant pro-apoptotic effects. Furthermore, our in vivo studies revealed that CPP administration significantly suppressed tumor growth, increased tumor cell apoptosis, and resulted in a marked reduction in tumor size and weight, further emphasizing the therapeutic potential of CPP as an agent to inhibit NSCLC cell growth.

Our study demonstrates that CPP effectively inhibits the proliferation of A549 cells and exerts a significant pro-apoptotic effect. Furthermore, the notable suppression of tumor growth, accompanied by a substantial increase in cellular apoptosis, as well as the reduction in tumor size and weight following CPP administration, validates these observations. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of CPP as an agent for inhibiting the growth of NSCLC cells.

In recent years, pyroptosis has garnered significant attention in cancer therapy. Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death that triggers an inflammatory response in the body, characterized by continuous swelling of the cell membrane and eventual rupture. This process is accompanied by the massive release of cellular contents and the activation of the immune system [5]. This inflammatory response is crucial in many diseases and is a critical factor in cancer immunotherapy [4].

NLRP3 and GSDMD are key regulatory molecules in the process of pyroptosis. Activation of the NLRP3 pathway can trigger inflammasome formation, ultimately leading to the activation of GSDMD, which forms pores and induces pyroptosis [14]. Yang et al. found that STING mediates hepatocyte pyroptosis in liver fibrosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [21]. Liu et al. also demonstrated that the levels of NLRP3 and GSDMD were significantly elevated in the serum of systemic sclerosis model mice induced by macrophage pyroptosis, and inhibitors of NLRP3 and GSDMD significantly alleviated cell pyroptosis in these mice [22]. ASC is an adaptor protein that links the inflammasome NLRP3 to caspase-1. In cancer, ASC is crucial for the assembly of the inflammasome complex, promoting the activation of caspase-1 and the downstream inflammatory response [23]. ASC coordinates cellular events leading to pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory mediators [24]. During cell rupture, pyroptosis releases a large amount of inflammatory cytokines, enhancing the cytotoxic effect on surrounding tumor cells and further boosting the immune response [25].

In this study, we explored for the first time the potential of CPP to regulate pyroptosis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells. In vitro experiments showed that CPP significantly promoted the expression of cleaved caspase-1, NLRP3, ASC, and GSDMD proteins in A549 cells. We observed cancer cell swelling, the release of inflammatory substances, and cell rupture and death due to increased permeability. In vivo experiments indicated that CPP upregulated the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1, promoting inflammasome activation and assembly, leading to GSDMD activation, enhanced tumor cell pyroptosis, release of inflammatory mediators, and activation of the immune response. Ultimately, this inhibited tumor growth. These findings suggest that CPP can activate the NLRP3/GSDMD pathway to promote pyroptosis in NSCLC cells, thereby inhibiting NSCLC tumor development. This further underscores the potential of CPP as a therapeutic agent for NSCLC.

Recent evidence suggests that increased ROS levels in cells are among the factors that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. For example, crystalline silica activates the NLRP3 inflammasome through an ROS and caspase-1 dependent pathway. However, in NLRP3-deficient macrophages, silica-induced ROS levels are reduced [26]. Additionally, the NF-kB pathway is considered a regulator of NLRP3 inflammasome activation [27]. The activation of the NF-kB pathway is also closely related to ROS [28]. In this study, we found that CPP not only promoted pyroptosis in NSCLC cells but also increased ROS levels and activated the NF-kB pathway. This further demonstrates the close connection between ROS, NF-kB pathway activation, and NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis.

Although this study has made some significant findings, there are still some limitations. This study lacks rescue experiments to further verify whether inhibitors of the NLRP3/GSDMD pathway can reverse the therapeutic effects of CPP. Future studies can be conducted to validate this result.

In summary, our findings highlight the potential of CPP in modulating cell death pathways, suppressing tumor growth, and influencing the inflammatory microenvironment, underscoring its promising candidacy as a therapeutic agent. These discoveries pave the way for the development of interventions based on CPP, addressing challenges associated with traditional NSCLC treatments. This research not only provides evidence supporting the anticancer properties of CPP but also emphasizes its potential as a novel approach to addressing the complexity of NSCLC. As the field of cancer treatment continues to evolve, CPP emerges as a promising participant in the quest for effective and targeted NSCLC therapies, offering a potential avenue for innovative interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CPP emerges as an effective agent in inhibiting the progression of NSCLC through the induction of NLRP3/GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis. A comprehensive analysis of CPP’s impact in both in vitro and in vivo settings provides a robust foundation for considering CPP as a potential therapeutic intervention for NSCLC.
