Do video animations and 3-D skull models reduce preoperative anxiety in bimaxillary orthognathic surgery patients? Randomized controlled trial
Authors: Uğur Boz, Ayşegül Mine Tüzüner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative information before orthognathic surgery is usually provided through verbal and written explanations; however, the effectiveness of supplementary visual tools, such as video animations and skull models, remains understudied. This study aimed to evaluate whether video animations and skull models effectively reduce preoperative anxiety in patients undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery.MethodsA single-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted at Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry between January 2024 and January 2025. Patients aged 18–36 years, classified as ASA I, and scheduled for elective bimaxillary orthognathic surgery were eligible. Exclusion criteria included previous orthognathic surgery, psychiatric disorders, or anxiolytic medication use. Patients were randomized equally into three groups: Group A, standard verbal information; Group B, verbal/video animation information; and Group C, verbal/3-D skull model information. The primary outcome was the change in State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State score (STAI-I) between before information time (BIT) and one hour before surgery time (BST); secondary outcomes included changes in Visual Analog Scale anxiety scores. One-way ANOVA was conducted for intergroup comparisons.ResultsThirty patients were included. There was no significant difference among groups in mean age (X² = 3.44, P = 0.18) or trait anxiety (F = 2.21, P = 0.13). For STAI-I, between-group comparisons were not significant at BIT (F = 1.38, P = 0.27) or BST (F = 1.04, P = 0.37). BIT-to-BST changes were not significant in Group A (P = 0.82), Group B (P = 0.67), or Group C (P = 0.51), and no significant group × time difference was observed (F = 0.31, P = 0.74). For VAS scores, a baseline difference was observed at BIT (F = 3.78, P = 0.04), with lower scores in Group B than in Group C (adjusted P = 0.04); no significant differences were found at BST (F = 1.81, P = 0.18) or in the group × time analysis (F = 1.06, P = 0.36).ConclusionNo statistically significant differences were observed among standard verbal information, video animation, and 3-D skull model information in reducing preoperative anxiety before bimaxillary orthognathic surgery.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07394881. Introduction

Patients seeking treatment for dentofacial deformities anticipate enhancements in both facial appearance and functional abilities, particularly in mastication and speech [1]. In addition to these reasons, psychosocial expectations play a role in patients’ decisions regarding surgical treatment [2].

As orthognathic surgery is associated with multiple potential complications, oral and maxillofacial surgeons must demonstrate comprehensive knowledge of these complications and ensure thorough preoperative patient counseling [3].

Patients preparing for orthognathic surgery often experience increased levels of anxiety, which can adversely affect both the postoperative recovery process and satisfaction with treatment [4]. Although preoperative anxiety can be considered a normal aspect of the surgical experience, it is recognized as a significant issue with the potential to exert negative effects on the healing process and surgical outcomes [5].

It is well established that one of the most crucial factors contributing to preoperative anxiety is a lack of information. Therefore, comprehensive psychological preparation of the patient and adequate preoperative procedural information are critically important [6]. Preoperative anxiety is known to be associated with slower recovery and increased postoperative symptoms in other types of elective surgery. Various methods aimed at alleviating preoperative anxiety may contribute to improved treatment outcomes [4].

Preoperative information is typically delivered through verbal and/or written explanations; however, there are insufficient data regarding the effectiveness of verbal presentation supplemented by video animation and a 3-D skull model in reducing preoperative anxiety in patients undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. The current study aimed to evaluate preoperative anxiety levels in patients undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery by comparing standard preoperative information methods (verbal and written information) with supplementary visual tools, including video animation and a 3-D skull model. This study hypothesized that supplementary visual tools would reduce preoperative anxiety compared with verbal counseling alone.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and sample size calculation

This study was approved by the Ankara University Health Sciences Sub‑Ethics Committee (Decision No. 159, Meeting No. 24, dated December 25, 2023). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was designed prospectively and registered retrospectively at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07394881.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), with a calculated effect size of 0.95, an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a power of 0.95. According to this analysis, a minimum of total 27 participants was required. Anticipating potential dropouts, more than 9 patients were included in each group.

Study design and sample

This single-blinded randomized controlled trial was conducted between January 2024 and January 2025 in the operating room of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, recruitment, or conduct of this study. A total of 30 patients, aged 18 to 36 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I and without any diagnosed psychological disorders, were included in the study.

The trial adhered to the CONSORT 2025 guidelines for reporting randomized trials, and a participant flow diagram is provided (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flow diagram illustrating the recruitment, randomization, and follow-up process of the participants

Inclusion criteria were patients scheduled for bimaxillary orthognathic surgery who agreed to participate. Exclusion criteria included refusal to watch the video or receive information using the skull model, incomplete forms, missing data, previous orthognathic surgery, prior negative experience with maxillofacial procedures, anxiety disorder or use of anxiolytics, inability to cooperate, language or comprehension difficulties, significant visual or hearing impairments, tobacco use, pregnancy, or lactation. Additionally, patients who chose to withdraw from the study were excluded. Sociodemographic characteristics (age and sex) were recorded. All patients were informed about the study procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Intervention and randomization

Participants were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio by an independent researcher who was not involved in the educational intervention, outcome assessment, or statistical analyses. Group assignments (A, B, or C) were prepared in advance on allocation cards, placed in an opaque box, and thoroughly mixed. Upon enrollment, each participant drew one card; the selected card determined the group assignment.

After the surgical appointment was scheduled, all patients were invited to the outpatient clinic at least two days prior to surgery. Information sessions were conducted in a quiet setting by a single researcher according to group assignment. Each information session was delivered in a standardized verbal context and lasted approximately 10 min. In all three groups, the same researcher verbally explained five identical core content domains in the same sequence: surgical preparation, osteotomy procedures of the maxilla and mandible, jaw repositioning, fixation techniques, and postoperative expectations. The verbal information was identical in wording across groups.

The video animation and 3-D skull model demonstration contained identical educational content and followed the same sequence as the predefined verbal script. In Group B, the video animation was shown on a standard screen. In Group C, the same procedural steps were demonstrated on a generic 3-D skull model by the same researcher.

After the information session, all participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and any questions raised were answered by the same researcher using the same standardized wording; no additional explanatory content beyond the predefined verbal script was introduced.

Blinding and data collection

All anxiety assessments and demographic data collection were performed by a single blinded researcher who was not involved in the information sessions or statistical analyses. This blinded researcher conducted questionnaire administration.

The Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I and STAI-II) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) were used to assess anxiety. These instruments were administered at two time points: Before the delivery of information (BIT) and one hour prior to surgery, on the day of the procedure (BST). Patients completed the forms without being informed of the surgical details prior to the first assessment.

Surveys

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) consists of two 20-item subscales evaluating state (STAI-I) and trait (STAI-II) anxiety. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a total score between 20 and 80. In this study, STAI-I was used to assess patients’ current anxiety levels, while STAI-II measured their baseline anxiety disposition. STAI scores were interpreted as follows: 20–37 (low or no anxiety), 38–44 (moderate anxiety), and 45–80 (high anxiety).

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) used in this study was a 100 mm horizontal line, anchored with “no anxiety” on the left and “the highest anxiety imaginable” on the right. Patients were asked to mark a point on the line that best represented their current anxiety level.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed with Levene’s test. Parametric tests were used when both assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the non-parametric equivalent was applied. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, median (min–max), and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) of the mean. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Comparisons across the three information-format groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance when parametric assumptions were met, or the Kruskal-Wallis test otherwise. When the omnibus test was statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni adjustment. Bonferroni-adjusted P values were reported and considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Comparisons between two independent groups (e.g., female vs. male within each group) were performed using the independent-samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Within-group changes from BIT to BST were assessed using paired-samples t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests otherwise. To assess whether the change from BIT to BST differed among the three groups, individual change scores were calculated as BST minus BIT and compared using one-way analysis of variance; this comparison was reported as the group × time effect. Relationships between continuous variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients (r). All statistical tests were two-sided, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05 unless an adjusted threshold was specified a priori.

Results

Participants and demographics

Of the 36 patients assessed, 4 did not meet the inclusion criteria, leaving 32 who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and consented to participate in the study. 1 patient in the group B and 1 patient in the group C refused to complete preoperative information with visual aids. Therefore, 30 patients (15 females and 15 males; 18 to 36 aged) were included in the study. The patient recruitment process is explained in the Consolidated Standards of Report- ing Trials flow chart (Fig. 1).

Female and male participants were equally divided among the groups. Descriptive statistics for age by group and sex are presented in Table 1. In Group A, age ranged from 18 to 36 years (mean: 26.7 ± 5.76); in Group B, from 18 to 34 years (mean: 25.2 ± 5.25); and in Group C, from 20 to 30 years (mean: 23.0 ± 3.2). No statistically significant difference in age was found among the groups (P = 0.18). When analyzed according to sex, female participants had a mean age of 25.4 ± 5.33 (range: 18–34), and male participants had a mean age of 24.53 ± 4.66 (range: 18–36). The statistically significant baseline difference was observed at BIT between Groups B and C, with higher VAS scores in Group C. This finding represents a pre-intervention baseline imbalance.

Discussion

The main aim of the current study was to evaluate the effect of different preoperative information techniques related to bimaxillary surgery on patients’ anxiety levels preoperatively. The study’s hypothesis was rejected since verbal information with visual tools did not reduce preoperative anxiety in bimaxillary orthognathic surgery patients.

Preoperative information is determinative to alleviate stress and foster a sense of control. Preoperative anxiety may be mitigated through standardized preoperative educational protocols delivered by the clinical team. Leotti et al. suggested that adequate preoperative information can reduce anxiety by enhancing predictability and supporting patients’ ability to anticipate and cope with postoperative pain [7].

Patient populations have included not only those undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery but also individuals undergoing different surgical procedures such as tooth extraction and the treatment of odontogenic cysts and tumors [8–11]. This current study was conducted on patients scheduled for bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. To the best of our knowledge, this topic has not been investigated in the context of bimaxillary orthognathic surgery; therefore, the present study aimed to contribute to this under-researched area.

Standard verbal counseling may already approach its anxiolytic ceiling in this population. Ng and colleagues demonstrated in an oral surgery cohort that the mere provision of structured preoperative information about the recovery process is itself anxiolytic across unselected surgical patients under local anesthesia [12]. Process-simulation studies have similarly shown that additive interventions beyond an adequate consultation yield diminishing rather than incremental returns [13]. In the present study, all three groups received identical structured verbal content, delivered by a single researcher over approximately 10 min — a design that ensures that incremental visual adjuncts are tested on top of an already-effective verbal channel [6]. The clinical implication is that qualitative refinement of the verbal content, rather than visual supplementation, may be the more productive next step in this surgical context [5].

Beyond verbal content saturation, moderate-to-high baseline trait anxiety places a ceiling on the responsiveness of state anxiety to short informational interventions. Spielberger conceptualizes trait anxiety as a stable, disposition-like tendency to perceive situations as threatening, in contrast to state anxiety, which is situation-evoked [14, 15]. In the current cohort, trait anxiety scores were comparable across groups and fell within the moderate-to-high range (Group A: 45.1 ± 8.63; Group B: 38.3 ± 4.74; Group C: 42.6 ± 7.97; F = 2.21, P = 0.13). Trait anxiety has been shown, meta-analytically across 35 clinical studies of dental and oral-surgical populations, to moderate both pre-treatment state anxiety and procedure-related pain expectation [16]. Patients with elevated dispositional anxiety therefore exhibit higher pretreatment state anxiety and respond less predictably to short informational interventions than do patients with low trait anxiety [14, 17]. Even an optimally designed visual intervention may shift state anxiety only marginally in this trait-loaded population.

An additional consideration is the duration of the educational exposure, which may be insufficient to overcome anticipatory anxiety generated in the 24–48 h preceding bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. Each intervention lasted approximately 10 min and was delivered at least two days before the procedure. Trials that have demonstrated significant anxiety reduction in comparable surgical cohorts have generally delivered multiple sessions or extended the cumulative exposure time beyond a single 10-minute encounter [18, 19]. Comparative trials of preoperative education in other surgical contexts further indicate that dose (number of sessions and cumulative duration), modality intensity (passive viewing vs. interactive handling), and timing relative to surgery all moderate intervention efficacy [6, 20]. Although no within-group change reached statistical significance, the direction of change differed across groups, with small increases in STAI-I scores in Groups A and B and a small decrease in Group C. Given the wide confidence intervals and small sample size, these descriptive changes should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical benefit [21].

Orthognathic surgical anxiety is itself multifactorial and only partly driven by procedural knowledge gaps. Cross-sectional work on 70 orthognathic patients showed that information satisfaction, social support, resilience, and coping style independently explained a substantial portion of preoperative anxiety variance, with procedural knowledge being relevant but not the sole or strongest determinant [17]. More recent survey data from patients scheduled for combined orthodontic-orthognathic treatment identified surgical-risk perception, postoperative pain, social/professional impact, and social media-driven anticipatory comparisons as predominant anxiety drivers [22]. Earlier work on recovery perceptions in orthognathic cohorts further indicated that overly negative preoperative expectations prolong postoperative burden — meaning that what patients expect, rather than how much they are told, may shape the anxiety response [23]. Informational adjuncts that lack personalization, interactivity, or aesthetic-prediction content may therefore be unable to displace the deeper psychosocial drivers that sustain preoperative anxiety in orthognathic candidates [1, 21, 24]. The 3-D skull model used in Group C was a commercial generic anatomical model, not a patient-specific photorealistic soft-tissue or skeletal prediction; photorealistic patient-specific content has been reported to reduce presurgical anxiety by concretizing the anticipated aesthetic result, whereas a generic skull model lacks this self-referential anchor [25–27]. Systematic review evidence across multiple specialties indicates that 3-D printed models, in isolation, generally do not reduce anxiety in randomized comparisons — a finding concordant with the present null result [25, 28].

While the current study cohort exhibited a balanced sex distribution (5 females and 5 males per group), large case-series of orthognathic surgical candidates have frequently enrolled proportionally more female participants, reflecting both epidemiological sex differences in dentofacial deformity presentation and the female-predominant pattern observed in elective cosmetic-related surgical cohorts [24, 29]. Sex does not, however, consistently predict preoperative anxiety in orthognathic cohorts; although some reports describe higher mean state-anxiety scores among female patients than among males [15], the present cohort showed a largely balanced sex × anxiety pattern. The single sex-related signal that approached but did not reach the conventional threshold for statistical significance was observed at pre-procedural VAS scores in Group A (female: 63.80 ± 18.50; male: 34.40 ± 27.00; t = 2.01, P = 0.08; 95% CI of the difference − 4.36 to 63.16). This localized finding, supported by a baseline STAI-I × VAS correlation of r = 0.413 (P = 0.023), suggests that the female–male anxiety gradient — when present — may be more readily captured by single-item subjective instruments like the VAS than by multi-item state-anxiety inventories [30, 31]. A definitive sex-stratified analysis will require a pre-specified, adequately powered sample.

Despite the negative primary outcome, some descriptive patterns may be useful for designing future studies. However, these observations are exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical effectiveness. First, the within-group state-anxiety trajectory from BIT to BST showed a downward (rather than upward) trend in patients receiving verbal counseling supplemented by a 3-D skull model, despite the use of a generic — not patient-specific — anatomical model. Second, the strong positive correlation between BIT VAS and BST VAS (r = 0.835, P < 0.001) indicates that early subjective anxiety predicts late subjective anxiety; therefore, interventions targetable at the BIT timepoint may have clinical reach even when their effect on BST state anxiety fails to reach statistical significance [14, 17]. These exploratory patterns warrant confirmation in larger, multi-center, adequately powered cohorts.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a small, single-center cohort of 30 patients (10 per group), the trial lacks statistical power to confirm or refute modest intervention effects, and a larger adequately powered replication — ideally via a multi-center design — is needed before firm clinical claims can be made. In addition, the 3-D skull model used in Group C was a generic anatomical model rather than a patient-specific photorealistic soft-tissue or skeletal prediction; this design choice — adopted because patient-specific 3-D printing entails additional CBCT scans, software licensing, and printing time — may have attenuated the potential benefit of the 3-D arm [25–27]. Compounding this, anxiety was assessed only at two time points without follow-up into the early postoperative period, precluding evaluation of whether any modest informational gain persisted across the perioperative trajectory [4]. Beyond these design considerations, the cohort was restricted to ASA I patients aged 18–36 years at a single tertiary university hospital, limiting external validity to older or medically comorbid patients, to private-practice settings, and to non-Turkish cultural contexts [9]. Finally, although trait anxiety was measured, the trial did not screen for coping styles, resilience, or social-support buffers — all of which have been independently associated with preoperative anxiety variance and could confound the response to information-based interventions [17, 21].

A further consideration relates to trial registration and reporting transparency. The study was retrospectively registered, which—while a pragmatic response to the ethics-approval timeline—introduces a residual risk of outcome-reporting bias and underscores the need for prospective registration in future replication trials.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this pilot randomized controlled trial, supplementary video animations and 3-D skull models did not produce statistically significant reductions in preoperative anxiety compared with standard verbal counseling in patients undergoing bimaxillary orthognathic surgery. Larger, prospectively registered multicenter trials are needed to determine whether these visual tools offer clinically meaningful benefits.
