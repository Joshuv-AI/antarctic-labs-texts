GeoEvoBuilder: A deep learning framework for efficient functional and thermostable protein design
Authors: Jiale Liu, Hantian You, Zheng Guo, Qin Xu, Changsheng Zhang, Luhua Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEnhancing enzyme catalytic efficiency and thermal stability is crucial for biocatalytic and industrial applications. While structure-based protein sequence design can improve thermal stability, its success in enzyme engineering is limited by complex reaction mechanisms and a lack of evolutionary information. Protein language models have shown promise in generating active sequences but rarely surpass natural enzymes. Improving enzyme activity and stability typically requires iterative experimental testing cycles with optimization designs based on few-shot or active learning approaches. To overcome these limitations, we developed a zero-shot deep learning framework that simultaneously improves thermal stability and activity. Our model has successfully generated high-performance glutathione peroxidase 4 and dihydrofolate reductase variants in one run, demonstrating its potential for rapid and efficient functional protein design.

The ultimate goal of protein design is to create novel protein sequences that can perform the desired function. De novo protein sequence design for a given protein target structure (1–15) and novel structure design (16–20) have shown promising outcomes. For example, trained on known protein three-dimensional structures and corresponding sequences, ProteinMPNN (14) uses message passing neural networks to generate sequences compatible with the desired protein target. ABACUS-R (11) and ProDesign-LE (21) use a graph transformer network to iteratively design sequences for the given protein backbone. We have previously developed GeoSeqBuilder (22) that utilizes a multiscale graph network to iteratively generate sequences and highly accurate side-chain conformations for the designed sequences. RFdiffusion (17) and Chroma (18) adopt a diffusion model to generate highly confident novel protein backbones. Although these methods have achieved remarkable success in protein binder design (23–26) and de novo enzyme design (27, 28), designing highly active enzymes remains challenging, and only as few as 1% of mutations are beneficial (29), which is a very small ratio in the immense sequence space. Recently, Sumida et al. attempted to use ProteinMPNN to design sequences for Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease to improve its enzyme activity (30). They found that even when fixing all the residues in the active sites and allowing other positions to mutate, the designed sequences were all inactive. Only when additional conserved residues were fixed could the designed sequences show enzyme activity. Their work demonstrated that evolutionary information plays a key role in redesigning natural enzymes for better properties using structure-based deep learning sequence generative models.

On the other hand, protein language models (PLMs) (31–34) have emerged which were trained on vast protein sequence datasets to capture the sequence grammar and evolutionary information. One notable example is ESMFold (35), which was developed based on ESM2, a series of pretrained PLMs with varying parameters, for protein structure prediction. ESMFold has demonstrated performance comparable to the groundbreaking algorithm AlphaFold2 (AF2) (36). Moreover, these PLMs are not limited to structure prediction (37–39), but can also be utilized for a wide range of downstream property predictions and functional protein design (40–46). By harnessing the power of PLMs, researchers can tap into the rich knowledge embedded in protein sequences, enabling advancements in protein engineering to improve the desired function with multiple rounds of experiments and active or few-shot learning (47, 48).

The integration of structural information and PLMs holds great promise for enhancing the success rate of functional protein design and improving properties in concern. While structure-based protein design methods often yield proteins with high thermal stability, they may overlook conserved functional information. By combining structural folding insights with the knowledge captured by PLMs, better outcomes in functional protein design may be achieved.

Recently, COMPSS (49) was developed as a scoring function over three rounds of multiple sequence alignment-based design and structure-based sequence design to improve the success rates of enzyme design, providing starting points for engineering with directed evolution. Despite these efforts, the enzymatic activity of the designed sequences did not exhibit significant improvement. InstructPLM (50), on the other hand, was developed with an adaptive attention module that integrates structural encoding networks and PLMs to design sequences, which was experimentally validated on two enzymes, showcasing the potential of integrating different sources of information for protein design. The recently developed ESM3 (45) showed enhanced performance using data distillation to jointly learn the information of protein sequence and structures. Although PLMs have been used to design proteins that have activities similar or less than natural proteins, several rounds of experimental guided active learning are necessary to improve their stability and activity (47, 48). Computational algorithms that can directly generate highly active proteins with desired properties and a high success rate by zero-shot learning are in high demand in protein engineering.

We have developed GeoEvoBuilder that integrates a structure-based sequence design algorithm, GeoSeqBuilder (22), with ESM2 for efficient zero-shot functional protein design. GeoEvoBuilder adaptively learns the weights from the structural encoding module GeoSeqBuilder and the evolutionary module ESM2. GeoEvoBuilder gives a high sequence recovery rate (57%) in a large test set and recalls the functional residues. We further experimentally tested GeoEvoBuilder on three protein targets with diverse functions, including the green fluorescent protein and two enzymes, and the success rate of design is significantly better than the current leading methods. The protein sequences generated by GeoEvoBuilder showed improved enzymic activity, elevated melting temperature (Tm), or fluorescent intensity. We solved several high-resolution crystal structures which are consistent with the designed models. The functional protein sequences generated by GeoEvoBuilder with over 30% multiple site mutations provide rich information of the protein evolution study for understanding protein sequence–structure–function relationship. We demonstrate that GeoEvoBuilder can be generally applied directly to simultaneously increase both thermal stability and protein activity for zero-shot learning without training on temperature and/or activity-guided information.

Results

Workflow of GeoEvoBuilder.

GeoEvoBuilder consists of three parts: GeoSeqBuilder, ESM2, and the adaptive concatenating module. GeoSeqBuilder is a structural encoding module, which learns the compatibility between the protein backbone and the sequence. ESM2 provides the sequence evolutionary information for the target. The adaptive concatenating module is a multilayer perception (MLP) which learns the joint distribution adaptively coupling the structural and evolutionary information. In other words, the MLP layer (trained via gradient descent) implicitly learns to reweight features from each embedding to resolve possible conflicts between the structural module and evolutionary information, favoring the more reliable model for specific input patterns, which is pushed by the training objective (cross-entropy) to approximate the joint distribution by balancing information flow from both embedding, effectively acting as a learned “consensus mechanism.” To make every position in the backbone optimal, GeoEvoBuilder is designed to iteratively update the nonoptimal residue types until all positions are convergent. The workflow of GeoEvoBuilder is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Before experimental validation (Fig. 1C), the generated sequences are evaluated using AF2 with both parent structural template and multisequence alignment (MSA) information. The MSA was generated by searching the UniRef30_2023 database using HHblits (51), with the parent sequence as the query (see SI Appendix for details). These inputs are essential, as AF2 cannot produce confident models without them. High-confidence candidates are selected based on two key metrics: pLDDT scores and Cα rmsd values between the predicted models and the template structures. Additionally, these candidates with large exposed hydrophobic patches (defined as three or more hydrophobic residues clustered on the surface) are filtered out to minimize the risk of protein aggregation (Fig. 1B).

GeoSeqBuilder is used to extract the structural information of the given backbone to enhance the folding ability for the designed sequence. ESM2 provides the evolutional information to keep the function. (B) Structure prediction using AF2 to evaluate the quality of designed sequences and select high confidence sequences. Performance of GeoEvoBuilder.

We train GeoEvoBuilder using the same dataset of GeoSeqBuilder. Specifically, we used 24,000 single-chain proteins from CATH4.3 (52) to train the model and evaluated the performance using 1,000 proteins (TS1000). GeoEvoBuilder achieved an overall sequence recovery rate of 57.07%, representing an improvement of about 5% over GeoSeqBuilder. The confusion matrix (Fig. 2A) illustrates that the majority of residues lie along the diagonal line, indicating high-quality predictions by GeoEvoBuilder. Some predictions were replaced by other residue types with similar physicochemical properties. This suggests that GeoEvoBuilder generates accurate predictions. We further analyzed the difference between the predicted distribution and the natural distribution for individual residue type. As shown in Fig. 2B, the difference between the GeoEvoBuilder-generated and natural distributions is much smaller than that of GeoSeqBuilder and the natural ones for most residues, especially Cys, His, Asp, and Glu. Previous research (53) noted that the frequency for residues in enzyme active sites is significantly different from the overall distribution for other residue types. For instance, His, which is often involved in enzymatic reactions, constitutes up to 18% in active sites compared to its overall frequency of about 2.7%. GeoEvoBuilder gives a higher frequency of His compared to GeoSeqBuilder and ProteinMPNN (Fig. 2B). As the proportion of active sites in the loop region (50%) is much higher than the overall distribution (30%), we further computed the predicted accuracy for the residues in the loop region using GeoEvoBuilder. The accuracy of GeoEvoBuilder for almost all residues is better than GeoSeqBuilder and ProteinMPNN (Fig. 2C). This may be due to the fact that GeoSeqBuilder and ProteinMPNN were trained only on structural data and might be inclined to generate sequences with high thermal stability in the loop region, without considering the functional sites. The predicted accuracy for the residues in the helical and extended regions is given in SI Appendix, Fig. S3. GeoSeqBuilder tends to only generate hydrophobic residues in the core region while natural proteins often have a few polar residues forming satisfactory hydrogen bonds in the core region. To check whether this phenomenon is also exhibited in GeoEvoBuilder, we computed the accuracy for the buried residues and found that residues predicted by GeoEvoBuilder in the buried region are more correct than GeoSeqBuilder, especially for residues that are polar and widely involved in function, including Cys, Arg, and Trp (Fig. 2D). Except for these residues (for example, Cys and His), ProteinMPNN seems to achieve better performance than GeoEvoBuilder in the buried region.

As ESM2 was trained by randomly masking 15% residues and then recovering it, it could also be used to design protein sequences with iterations. We wonder how it contributes to the performance of GeoEvoBuilder, especially for the generation of functional sites. To this end, we tested GeoEvoBuilder, as well as ESM2 alone on the CAMEO41 dataset (22) which contains 41 protein targets from CAMEO (54) that were released between 2022-12-09 and 2023-06-03. For every protein target, we first randomly masked 100% (i.e., random initialization), 70%, 50%, 30%, 15%, and 0% (i.e., starting from native sequence) native sequences and generated 10 sequences for each situation. As ESM2 actually does not know whether the initial sequences are the same as natural ones (i.e., 0% masking rate) or from random initialization, we then computed the identity of the generated sequences relative to the native sequences. It is expected that the generated sequences have decreased sequence identity with the native sequences when more positions are masked. For GeoEvoBuilder, the sequence recovery rates change from 82.11% (0% masked) to 45.39% (100% masked) (Fig. 2E). In contrast, ESM2 could only recover native sequences when at least 70% native residues were provided with a larger variance among targets (Fig. 2F). The predicted structures using ESMFold for these sequences further confirmed that as the masking rate increases, the sequences refined by ESM2 progressively fail to fold into the desired targets (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This suggests that the structural module is important to guide GeoEvoBuilder to generate sequences compatible with the desired target and only when the intermediate sequences are close to the target sequence, the EMS2 module starts to play its role to refine sequences for function.

GeoEvoBuilder Recalls Enzyme Functional Sites.

We have shown that GeoEvoBuilder gives a high recovery rate for residues that are widely involved in protein function. We next analyze how the concatenating module adaptively operate the weights from sequence–structure consistency to sequence–structure–function agreement using TEV protease as an example, which is an enzyme from tobacco etch virus that is widely used to cleave the peptide motif Glu-Xaa-Xaa-Tyr-Xaa-Gln-|-(Ser or Gly). Its catalytic sites mainly contain three residues, including 46H, 81D, and 151C (Fig. 3A). We used GeoEvoBuilder to design sequences for TEV protease structure [PDB entry: 1LVM (55)] with random initialization to see whether it can generate the functional sites. As a comparison, we also used GeoSeqBuilder and ProteinMPNN to design sequences for it. Though all the three methods could recover two of the three functional residues (46H and 81D), only GeoEvoBuilder could recover the third one (151C) (Fig. 3B), indicating its capability of functional protein design. We then examined how GeoEvoBuilder recovered the catalytic residue 151C during the sequence design iterations. GeoEvoBuilder recovered 151C from step 29 and step 64 starting from 50% native sequence randomly masked and random initialization, respectively (Fig. 3 C and D). We further predicted the structures for the generated sequences at each iteration step. It can be seen from Fig. 3E that correct structures appeared earlier than the correct residue type for position 151. After several tens of iterations, sequences that produce structures similar to the target (TM-score around 0.8) appeared, and then, several iterations later, the correct functional residue emerged.

Experimental Validations on Three Protein Targets.

To further test the performance of GeoEvoBuilder, we redesigned sequences and carried out experimental validation for three protein targets, including green fluorescent protein (GFP), Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). The GFP forms a fluorescent chromophore by a self-catalyzed reaction (56). Redesigning the GFP might result in better properties. Glutathione Peroxidase 4 is an enzyme that plays a crucial role in protecting cells from oxidative stress (57). In addition to that, GPX4 also plays a vital role in cell survival and regulation, particularly in processes such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, and lipid metabolism (58). Redesigning GPX4 with higher catalytical activity may have potential medical application to remove the excess hydroperoxide. DHFR reduces dihydrofolic acid to tetrahydrogen folic acid using NADPH as the reductant. The protein-fragment complementation assay (PCA) based on DHFR is widely used for studying protein–protein interactions and high-throughput protein binder screening (59).

We first used GeoEvoBuilder to design sequences for GFP using the structural template of the original GFP from jellyfish (PDB entry: 1GFL (56) with two protocols. In the first protocol, we fixed the residues within 8 Å to the chromophore (77/230, see SI Appendix for more details) and randomized the initial sequence for other positions. In the second protocol, we fixed the residues within 8 Å to the chromophore and randomly masked 50% natural sequences at the initial state. Initially, we set the radius value to 7 Å, consistent with the value used in the literature (30). Upon careful inspection of the wild-type structure, we observed several exposed residues (168H, 147S, and 146 N in PDB entry 1GFL) that form robust hydrogen bonds, that may serve as a protective barrier to prevent the chromophore from exposure to the solvent. Consequently, to incorporate these critical residues, we expanded the radius from 7 Å to 8 Å.

Subsequently, 200 sequences were generated for each protocol, and AF2 was utilized for structure prediction. Notably, AF2 in single sequence mode or with the structural target template yielded models with low confidence. However, AF2 can give highly confident models for the designed sequences when using the structural template and MSA information. We selected 13 and 19 candidate sequences with pLDDT ≥ 95 and all Cα rmsd ≤ 0.6 Å from these two protocols. We further inspected the protein surface and eliminated the candidates with three or more exposed hydrophobic residue patches to minimize the potential risk of protein aggregation. Finally, we selected 5 and 17 sequences from the first and second protocols, respectively, synthesized their corresponding genes, and expressed them in Escherichia coli.

Most of the E. coli cells expressing these proteins exhibited green color under fluorescence microscope. Fig. 4A showed the fluorescence imaging of the live E. coli (1GFL-15). SDS-PAGE analysis showed that 20 out of the 22 overexpressed proteins are soluble (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Only one protein (1GFL-8, from the first protocol) was found in the inclusion bodies, and one protein (1GFL-7, from the second protocol) was not observably expressed. The supernatants of the 17 designed GFPs showed significant fluorescence. We further measured their melting temperatures by monitoring the green fluorescence changes. The Tm for the wild type (76.6 °C) is similar to the previously reported value (76 °C) (60), and the designed GFPs exhibited various Tm ranging from 67.5 to 78.3 °C (Fig. 4B). We selected two proteins with high expression levels for purification and further characterization, including 1GFL-15 and 1GFL-19. The main positions of the elution peak from the gel column for 1GFL-15 and 1GFL-19 were similar to the wild-type GFP, indicating similar homogeneous states (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We measured the excitation and emission maxima of the two designed GFPs and observed that 1GFL-15 exhibits a red-shifted excitation wavelength (470 nm) compared to the wild-type GFP (395 nm) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). When comparing fluorescence intensity at each protein’s maximum excitation wavelength (at 1 μM protein concentration), both 1GFL-15 and 1GFL-19 demonstrated fluorescence intensities comparable to wild-type GFP (0.94 vs. 1.00 and 0.80 vs. 1.00, respectively; SI Appendix, Table S1). The red-shifted excitation (470 nm) is particularly advantageous for live-cell imaging, as it minimizes background interference from endogenous fluorophores (which are more prevalent at wild-type GFP’s 400 nm excitation) and reduces UV-A-induced cellular damage. Notably, when excited at 470 nm, 1GFL-15 and 1GFL-19 exhibited 1.5- to 2.5-fold higher fluorescence intensities (measured at their respective emission maxima) than wild-type GFP (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Table S1). This enhancement at a biologically favorable wavelength makes these variants superior for imaging applications. We also measured the absolute quantum yields, revealing that 1GFL-15 is comparable to 1GFL-WT, whereas 1GFL-19 exhibits only one-third of the quantum yield of 1GFL-WT (SI Appendix, Table S2). These two proteins shared 86.52% sequence identity to the wild type (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), and 87.83% between themselves. We further solved the crystal structures for the two proteins with 2.61 Å resolution for 1GFL-15 (Fig. 4D) and 1.90 Å resolution for 1GFL-19 (Fig. 4E), respectively. The overall structures of the designed proteins are highly consistent with the wild type with the all Cα rmsd of 1GFL-15 and 1GFL-19 to the wild type of 0.91 Å and 0.52 Å. We further compared the local environment of the chromophore to the wild type and found that the lengths of hydrogen bond between the 66Y and 148H are shorter than the wild type. In addition, we also found that 203T formed hydrogen bond to 66Y in the GFP-19, which is not exhibited in the wild-type GFP. The backbone carbonyl oxygen atom of residue 67G formed hydrogen bond to the side chain hydroxyl group of 92Y in GFP-15, which are not appeared in the wild-type GFP. We also noticed that two residues in the hydrophobic core have been changed (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) from the polar residues (169H and 177Q) to hydrophobic ones (169L and 177L), which might improve protein stability for avoiding the buried unsatisfactory hydrogen bonds. These environmental specificities might result in the differences for the fluorescent intensity. We further performed sequence homology analysis for the designed sequences and built a phylogenetic tree. As shown in Fig. 4F, the branches were abundant and most designed sequences were dissimilar to the wild-type GFP. We further computed the identity confusion matrix for these sequences to the wild type, 1GFL-15, and 1GFL-19. The results revealed that the designed protein GFP-22 only shared 67.39% identical sequence to the wild-type GFP (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), which is a relatively low sequence identity. In other words, when 75 residues were mutated, the designed protein GFP-22 can still retain the function. However, the absolute quantum yield for this variant is only 2.6% (SI Appendix, Table S2). We wonder whether our designed sequences are closer to these mutations and other natural GFPs identified previously. To this end, we searched the authoritative fluorescent protein database (61), and found that the closest GFP to our designed sequences was the wild type, i.e., the template we used, indicating the novelty for the designed sequences.

We further used GeoEvoBuilder to design sequences for GPX4 [PDB entry: 2OBI (62)] utilizing two protocols similar to that used for GFP redesign. In the first protocol, the residues within 7 Å to the catalytic residues were fixed (47/165, see SI Appendix for details) with random initialization. The second protocol is similar to the first protocol, but with 50% sequence randomly masked at the initial state. We generated 200 sequences for each protocol and used AF2 metrics to select the designed sequences with high confidence (pLDDT ≥ 92 and all Cα rmsd ≤ 1.5 Å) and all selected designs were further checked to eliminate those with three or more surface hydrophobic residue patches. Subsequently, 10 and 9 sequences were selected from the two protocols, respectively. Of the 19 proteins designed, 16 were well expressed and soluble (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

We first roughly measured the enzyme activity of the supernatant of cell lysate and found that 7 proteins showed evident activities (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). We then purified these 7 proteins and tested their enzyme activity and thermal stability. The Tm values of designed proteins range from 53 to 65 °C (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12), and three of them showed increased thermal stability compared to the wild-type protein. We further performed enzyme kinetic analysis for these designed proteins and the wild-type protein (Fig. 5C). The most active protein 2OBI-10, showed enhanced binding affinity for the cofactor GSH with a KmGSH value that was 4.74-fold lower than the wild-type protein. Its catalytic efficiency kcat/(KmGSH. KmTBH) to GSH and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBH) improved by a factor of 8.49 compared to the wild-type protein and its Tm was also 3 °C higher than the wild type (Fig. 5B). The catalytic constant kcat of 2OBI-10 is about 1.79-fold to the wild type (Fig. 5C). This indicates that 2OBI-10 can work well under low GSH concentration. The details of the fittings of Michaelis–Menten kinetics for the cofactor GSH and substrate TBH can be found in SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14, respectively.

Among the 7 active designs, 6 are from the second protocol and 1 is from the first protocol (2OBI-13). This indicates that partial knowledge of the wild-type residues helps the algorithm reach an optimal state fast. At the same time, random initialization with fixed active sites can also generate active sequences, like the case of 2OBI-13, which only shares 67.9% identity to the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). As currently 200 sequences were generated for each protocol, we expect to have more active and diverse designs from the first protocol when more sequences are designed.

We were able to solve three crystal structures for the designed GPX4 proteins with high resolution (1.32 Å for 2OBI-1, 1.34 Å for 2OBI-3, and 1.53 Å for 2OBI-10). The overall Cα rmsd between the two designed proteins, 2OBI-3 and 2OBI-10, and the wild type were rather small at 0.43 and 0.54 Å, respectively (Fig. 5 E and F). Though the overall Cα rmsd between 2OBI-1 and the wild type was 2.17 Å, when the flexible loop region (residue index 119 to 127, Fig. 5D) was ignored, the rmsd became only 0.70 Å. We noticed that some of the core residues of the three designed proteins are different from the wild type. As shown in Fig. 5G, 14M, 37C, and 120M in the wild-type GPX4 have been changed to 14L, 37V, and 120L.

In order to further understand why the protein 2OBI-10 increases GPX4 enzyme activity, we performed 200 ns molecular dynamic simulations for it and the wild-type protein and an inactive variant (2OBI-9). The residue motion correlations of the three proteins displayed distinct patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S16), and 2OBI-10 is better modularized. We also performed protein community network analysis (63) which reveals the connections and distributions between remote sites and catalytic sites throughout the molecular dynamics trajectory. The detailed methodology for this analysis can be found in our previous publication (64). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S17, residues in proteins were clustered into 7 communities. The catalytic residues were mainly divided into two communities. In the case of wild-type GPX4 (2OBI-WT) (SI Appendix, Fig. S17A), the communities that contain catalytic residues were represented by green and purple clusters. For 2OBI-10 (SI Appendix, Fig. S17B), the communities that contain catalytic residues expanded to include an additional light-blue cluster. However, for the inactive protein 2OBI-9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S17C), the communities that contain catalytic residues became smaller with less interactions. The active protein 2OBI-10 also exhibits denser connections between neighboring communities for the region of catalytic sites, which is different from the wild-type and inactive protein 2OBI-9. We also found that several remote residue pairs exhibited strong motion correlations where one is in the catalytic pocket and the other one is in the allosteric pocket we previously identified (65). We displayed all the mutations on the protein structure and found that several of them are located around the known allosteric site (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), which might impact the enzyme activity by allosteric regulation. Besides that, these allosteric sites in 2OBI-10 had strong connections to the catalytic sites (refer to purple and yellow ball in SI Appendix, Fig. S17) while those in the inactive protein 2OBI-9 did not.

The previous results showed that fixing the pocket residues and randomly masked 50% native sequences at initial state achieved the highest success rate (15/17 for GFP, and 6/9 for GPX4). We then used GeoEvoBuilder to design sequences for the DHFR template [PDB entry: 3D80 (66)] using this setting. As the substrate and cofactor pocket of DHFR is a long groove (Fig. 6A), simply fixing residues within 7 Å to the pocket residues would make too many residues fixed and too little residues (53/186) changeable (see SI Appendix for more details). To increase the design diversity, residues within 6 Å (66/186 changeable) to the pocket residues were fixed, and 50% natural sequences were randomly masked at the initial state. We generated 200 sequences and used AF2 for structure prediction. Homologous sequences and structural template are necessary to correctly predict structures for the designed sequences using AF2. We used the metrics (pLDDT ≥ 94 and all Cα rmsd ≤ 1.3 Å) to select 26 candidates for further inspection and selected 23 sequences for experimental validation. The preliminary DHFR enzyme activity screen was performed using a PCA (please see SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20), and all the designs were found active. The 7 designs that demonstrated high activity were then purified for further experimental study. All the designed proteins were homogenous and monomeric on size exclusion column (SI Appendix, Fig. S21). Five of the 7 designs exhibited higher Tm values than the wild type (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S22). One of them, 3D80-15, displayed Tm that is 10 °C higher than the wild type.

We further carried out a comprehensive kinetic analysis of the cofactor NADPH and the substrate dihydrofolic acid (DH) for the selected active proteins and the wild-type DHFR. The catalytic efficiency of these designed proteins showed a range of improvements from 1.53 to 21.05 times (Fig. 6 B and C) compared to the wild-type DHFR. The most active protein, 3D80-15, not only boosted the catalytic efficiency more than 20-fold but also increased the thermal stability by 10 °C. The kinetic parameters for individual cofactor NADPH and substrate DH were also available in SI Appendix, Figs. S24 and S25, respectively.

Discussion

Deep learning algorithms offer powerful tools to efficiently learn the relationship between protein–sequence, structure, and function from the protein sequence and structure information. In this study, we introduced GeoEvoBuilder, an algorithm that integrates structural encoding with sequence evolutionary information from a protein language model for functional protein design. GeoEvoBuilder achieves a high sequence recovery rate in benchmark datasets and recaptures protein functional sites in case studies. Experimental validation demonstrates its ability to design diverse protein sequences with improved thermal stability, enhanced fluorescence of GFP, or increased enzymatic activity of GPX4 and DHFR with a high success rate without the need of fine-tuning or iterative learning cycles. GeoEvoBuilder provides a highly efficient and easy-to-use tool for protein function design. Our study showcases that integrating structure-based sequence design with PLM transforms protein functional design into a faster, smarter, and more accessible process.

While a number of deep learning based protein design tools have been developed, de novo designed enzymes (27, 28) usually exhibit catalytic efficiencies lower or comparable to their natural counterparts. Achieving the real-world protein engineering requirements remains challenging. Recent works (45, 47, 48, 67) have shown promising results, which typically require multiple rounds of labor-intensive experiments and active or few-shot learning circles to identify potentially beneficial single mutation or combinational mutations. In this study, we showed that the zero-shot learning method, GeoEvoBuilder, can be used to significantly enhance protein activity and thermal stability in a single round of experiments. GeoEvoBuilder expands the exploration of sequence space beyond the reach of traditional directed evolution. The novel sequences generated by GeoEvoBuilder can serve as starting points for further evolution or as sources of multiple combinational mutations, providing a foundation for achieving superior outcomes in protein engineering.

The first shell residues from the substrate binding pocket play a crucial role in performing the catalytic function. To maintain this function as much as possible, we fixed these residues while conducting experimental studies for the three protein targets. We were curious to see how effectively GeoEvoBuilder could recover these residues if no constraints were placed on sequence design. To investigate this, we generated 200 sequences with random sequence initialization for GPX4, computed the sequence profile for these fixed 47 positions, and compared them with the corresponding native residue types. GeoEvoBuilder was able to recover 45 (95.7%) residues (the two that were not recovered are marked in red in SI Appendix, Fig. S26), while GeoSeqBuilder and ProteinMPNN only recover 34 (72.3%) and 37 (78.7%) residues under identical protocol (i.e., without any constraints), respectively. This trend consistently appeared in other two targets (see SI Appendix, Fig. S27 for GFP and SI Appendix, Fig. S28 for DHFR, and SI Appendix, Table S8 for summary). Our results demonstrate that evolutionary information derived from ESM2 provides an orthogonal dimension to geometric and structure-based design paradigms. However, functional protein sequence design without any constraints may lower the success rate due to the complicated relationships between protein enzymes and substrates. One potential way to address this issue is to introduce substrate, the activation intermediate state, and molecule encoding and retrain the deep learning model using the protein complex data. This is a strategy we plan to explore in our future work.

As the evolutionary information for sparse protein families may become diluted in the unified protein language model ESM2, we hypothesized that integrating target-specific evolutionary information may amplify relevant signals and improve the design success rate. To investigate this, we trained an alternative network, GeoEvoBuilder-MSA (refer to SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for additional details). This network adaptively and automatically learns the distribution between GeoSeqBuilder, ESM2, and target-specific MSA. As GeoEvoBuilder-MSA fully accounts for the entire natural homologous sequences, we expect it to achieve the optimal state easily without the need for information of remote sites. We then employed GeoEvoBuilder-MSA to design sequences for GPX4. We fixed the same residues for sequence design targeting GPX4 as used in GeoEvoBuilder, but with random sequence initialization. Seven sequences were selected for further experimental validation, and all of them can be expressed as soluble proteins in E. coli (SI Appendix, Fig. S29). Preliminary enzyme activity testing using the lysate supernatant showed that four of them are active. We then purified them for further analysis. All the four proteins showed similar size to the wild-type GPX4 as indicated by the size exclusion chromatography (SI Appendix, Fig. S30), but displayed reduced thermal stability (SI Appendix, Fig. S31). The catalytic efficiencies of the four designed proteins were approximately five to seven times higher than that of the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S32 and Table S9). Pairwise sequence identity and sequence information can be found in SI Appendix, Fig. S33 and Table S10, respectively. These results suggest that incorporating target-specific evolutionary information can achieve a similar success rate to GeoEvoBuilder with 50% masked random initialization. However, the increase of enzyme activity is less than the designs from GeoEvoBuilder. The use of MSA information seems to constrain the exploration of sequence space and lowers the chances to find novel designs with high activity and stability.

For application convenience, when designing sequences for the target proteins, we did not remove their highly homologous sequences from the training set to retrain GeoEvoBuilder. To evaluate whether the homologous sequences in the training set significantly impact design performance (see SI Appendix, Tables S11–S13 for details on high identity sequences in the training set), we systematically analyzed their potential influence. Our sequence alignment results indicate that these highly homologous sequences primarily originate from designed variants of the target proteins, featuring key mutations near chromophore or substrate-binding sites for functional characterization (SI Appendix, Figs. S34–S36). In contrast, our design strategy fixes first shell residues around the functional site while only altering remote residues for designing proteins with better properties. The success of our designs demonstrates that functional regulations are complicated and concerted changes of distal sites contribute significantly. The underlying mechanisms could be further explored in the future. Furthermore, the residue types in our designed sequences differ from those at the mutation sites present in the homologous sequences from the training set. To rigorously test for possible data leakage, we removed all training sequences with ≥40% identity to the targets (1LVM, 1GFL, 2OBI, and 3D80) and retrained the model, yielding GeoEvoBuilder40. Notably, GeoEvoBuilder40 retained the ability to recover the catalytic site 151C in the TEV protease (1LVM), despite the removal of all homologous sequences (SI Appendix, Fig. S37). It also achieved comparable sequence recovery rates to the original GeoEvoBuilder across the three experimentally tested targets (GFP: 0.46 vs. 0.45; GPX4: 0.59 vs. 0.60; DHFR: 0.60 vs. 0.61; SI Appendix, Figs. S38 and S39). We further applied GeoEvoBuilder40 with the same second protocol to design sequences for the target GPX4 and selected six newly designed sequences for experimental validation (SI Appendix, Figs. S40–S44 and Table S14). Remarkably, all of them exhibited activity, and most of them showed substantial improvements in thermal stability and/or catalytic efficiency over GeoEvoBuilder’s outputs (SI Appendix, Fig. S45). These results suggest that the PLM (ESM2) seems to provide much functional information, while inclusion of highly homologous sequences and MSA information (MSA-based approach GeoEvoBuilder-MSA) may inadvertently restrict the model’s exploration capacity. Overall, both GeoEvoBuilder and GeoEvoBuilder40 can generate functionally improved protein variants regardless of whether high-identity sequences are included or excluded during the design process. This flexibility broadens GeoEvoBuilder’s applicability across diverse scenarios. Importantly, it is convenient for users as no retraining is needed for their designed targets if there are homologous sequences in the training set.

Conclusion

In summary, our unified deep learning model, GeoEvoBuilder, has demonstrated exceptional capability in generating highly active and stable sequences for proteins of interest. As a zero-shot learning model, it only needs a single design and experimental round, significantly reducing experimental efforts and increasing research efficiency. In addition to its direct application in protein engineering, GeoEvoBuilder also serves as a powerful tool for advancing our understanding of the intricate relationships between protein sequence, structure, function, and evolution.

Materials and Methods

Model Training.

The pseudocode, training details, and interpretation can be found in SI Appendix. Here is the main description. To efficiently configure GeoEvoBuilder parameters utilizing data from CATH4.3 and enable adaptive learning of the interplay between structural and evolutionary data, we maintained flexibility by not fixing any parameters. Only the initial parameters of the GeoSeqBuilder module were sourced from the pretrained model GeoSeqBuilder. The learning rate was set to 1e-4, facilitating rapid convergence to the optimal state within 5 epochs of iteration. We did not train GeoEvoBuilder without any parametric constraints as our iterative manner could potentially bias the model toward the evolutionary ESM2 while diminishing the importance of structural insights from GeoSeqBuilder. The pretrained model esm2_t36_3B_UR50D was used. The adaptive concatenating module is a multilayer perception network, which learns the distribution:[1]Φ=φWGeoSeqBuilderPGeoSeqBuilder+WESM2PESM2,

where φ is a nonlinear activation function ELU. W and P are the learnable parameters and logits distributions from different modules, respectively. Other details can be found in the corresponding paper ESM2 (35), GeoSeqBuilder (22), and SI Appendix.
