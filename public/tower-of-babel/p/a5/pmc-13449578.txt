Leveraging the non-contrast CT component of PET/CT: an AI-driven delta-radiomics approach to monitor treatment response in metastatic breast cancer
Authors: Emir Gokhan Kahraman, Olcun Umit Unal, Halil Taskaynatan, Ozlem Ozdemir, Emine Budak, Mustafa Alper Selver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose18 F-FDG PET/CT is the standard modality for monitoring treatment response in metastatic breast cancer. This study aims to evaluate the predictive value of delta-radiomics derived solely from the low-dose, non-contrast CT component acquired during routine PET/CT imaging—without requiring an additional dedicated CT examination or extra contrast administration—for monitoring response to CDK4/6 inhibitors in de novo metastatic hormone receptor-positive (HR+)/HER2-negative breast cancer.MethodsThis retrospective study included 33 patients with bone-predominant metastatic breast cancer. Delta radiomic features were extracted from the non-contrast CT component of paired baseline and follow-up 18 F-FDG PET/CT scans. Patients were stratified into Responders (Complete or Partial Response) and Non-Responders (Stable or Progressive Disease) based on standard PERCIST criteria. We developed an integrated machine learning model using logistic regression with elastic net regularization, validated via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV).ResultsThe cohort consisted of 25 Responders and 8 Non-Responders. Non-Responders exhibited distinct longitudinal increases in Delta_Pct_shape_Elongation and Delta_Pct_firstorder_90Percentile compared to Responders. The integrated model, combining these features with clinical variables, achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.930, significantly outperforming the baseline clinical-only model (AUC = 0.775). While the default threshold prioritized sensitivity (96.0%) with limited specificity (25.0%), post-hoc threshold optimization maximizing the Youden index demonstrated a highly balanced performance, achieving 88.0% sensitivity and 87.5% specificity.ConclusionsDelta radiomics analysis of the routinely acquired non-contrast CT component of PET/CT provides substantial incremental prognostic value over standard clinical variables. This approach demonstrates the potential of utilizing existing low-dose CT data as a cost-effective, supportive biomarker for the early prediction of therapeutic resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02463-4.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide [1]. The hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) subtype accounts for the majority of cases, comprising approximately 60% to 75% of breast cancers [2]. In the metastatic setting, this subtype shows a marked propensity for skeletal involvement, and bone metastases are frequently encountered in patients with HR+/HER2- disease [3].

The therapeutic landscape of HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer has been substantially transformed by cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors combined with endocrine therapy, which now represent the standard first-line approach in most patients [4, 5]. Randomized trials have shown significant improvements in progression-free survival, and in several settings overall survival, with these agents [4, 5]. Nevertheless, a subset of patients exhibits primary resistance, and acquired resistance remains a major limitation even among initial responders [5]. Given the cost, treatment duration, and toxicity profile of CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy, early identification of patients who are unlikely to benefit remains clinically important [6, 7].

Response assessment in bone metastases remains challenging [8–11]. RECIST 1.1 is often suboptimal for skeletal disease because most bone lesions are categorized as non-measurable unless they are associated with a measurable soft-tissue component [8]. In addition, the heterogeneous morphologic patterns of bone metastases, including osteolytic, osteoblastic, and mixed lesions, complicate conventional size-based evaluation [8, 9]. The flare phenomenon, characterized by transient increases in sclerosis or radiotracer uptake during treatment response, may further mimic progression on conventional computed tomography (CT) or bone scintigraphy [8, 9]. Although ^18F-FDG PET/CT provides valuable functional information and is widely used in metastatic breast cancer, repeated PET/CT for close longitudinal monitoring may be limited in routine practice by cost, accessibility, and cumulative imaging burden [10–12].

Radiomics provides a framework for extracting quantitative features from medical images that may reflect tumor phenotype, spatial heterogeneity, and treatment-associated microenvironmental remodeling [13]. Delta radiomics extends this concept by evaluating temporal changes in imaging features and may therefore be particularly relevant in treatment settings in which biologic response evolves gradually over time [14]. In metastatic breast cancer, radiomic texture biomarkers have shown promise for predicting early response to CDK4/6 inhibitors in patients with visceral metastases, supporting the broader premise that longitudinal imaging phenotypes may capture treatment sensitivity before conventional anatomical progression becomes evident [15]. Similarly, CT-derived radiomic signatures have demonstrated prognostic and predictive value in other systemic therapy settings, suggesting that quantitative imaging biomarkers may have broader applicability across tumor types and therapeutic contexts [16].

More broadly, advances in hybrid molecular imaging and theranostic oncology have increased interest in imaging-derived biomarkers that can complement clinical decision-making. Radiolabeled targeted agents, technetium-99 m-based imaging strategies, and emerging theranostic platforms highlight the growing convergence of quantitative imaging and precision oncology [17, 18]. Within this broader context, the low-dose non-contrast CT component of routine PET/CT represents an attractive but underused source of quantitative information.

Importantly, the potential advantage of this approach is not the absence of radiation within the PET/CT examination itself, but rather the opportunity to extract additional response-related information from imaging that is already being acquired, without requiring a separate dedicated CT examination or additional contrast administration. This may be particularly relevant in patients with bone-predominant metastatic disease, in whom structural change may complement metabolic assessment.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of delta radiomics derived exclusively from the low-dose, non-contrast CT component of routine PET/CT scans in patients with HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy. We hypothesized that longitudinal changes in CT-derived radiomic features from bone metastases may provide complementary information regarding treatment response. Given the retrospective design, limited cohort size, and lack of external validation, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating pending validation in independent datasets.

Materials and methods

Study population and clinical design

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the Medical Oncology Clinic of İzmir City Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of İzmir City Hospital (Decision No: 2025/468; Date: 10/09/2025), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

The study cohort consisted of patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic hormone receptor-positive (HR+), HER2-negative (HER2-) breast cancer between November 2023 and November 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathologically confirmed de novo metastatic HR+/HER2- breast cancer; (2) the presence of at least one bone metastasis; (3) treatment with a CDK4/6 inhibitor (palbociclib, ribociclib, or abemaciclib) combined with endocrine therapy as first-line systemic therapy; and (4) availability of paired ^18F-FDG PET/CT examinations obtained at baseline (pre-treatment) and at first follow-up (post-treatment). Patients were excluded if they had received prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy, had another active malignancy, or had fractures, orthopedic implants, or prior surgical interventions within the vertebral or truncal regions of interest that could interfere with radiomic analysis.

Response assessment

Treatment response was determined using the first follow-up ^18F-FDG PET/CT examination relative to baseline and served as the reference standard for model development. Response categories were assigned according to PERCIST (PET Response Criteria in Solid Tumors) [19]. For binary classification, patients were grouped as Responders, including complete response (CR) and partial response (PR), and Non-Responders, including stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD).

CT source images and segmentation workflow

Radiomic analysis was performed exclusively on the low-dose, non-contrast CT component of routine ^18F-FDG PET/CT examinations. A total of 66 CT scans from 33 patients were analyzed. Because all scans were acquired in a single-center setting using the same routine PET/CT platform and a consistent low-dose non-contrast CT protocol, acquisition-related heterogeneity within the cohort was relatively constrained. However, this design does not establish robustness across different scanners, vendors, or reconstruction settings.

Segmentation was performed in 3D Slicer (version 5.8) using a hybrid workflow designed to balance automation and anatomical control. Skeletal structures were initially segmented using the TotalSegmentator deep-learning module [20]. All automatically generated skeletal masks were then reviewed individually on a case-by-case basis by an experienced nuclear medicine specialist, and minimal manual refinements were applied where needed before radiomic feature extraction. The forearm and lower leg bones were excluded because of the frequent presence of artifacts and inconsistent field-of-view coverage in these regions.

Radiomic feature extraction

Radiomic features were extracted from the segmented skeletal regions on both baseline and follow-up CT scans using the PyRadiomics library in Python [21]. Feature extraction followed an IBSI-informed workflow, and a total of 107 radiomic features were extracted from each scan, including first-order, shape, and texture features derived from the GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, GLDM, and NGTDM feature families.

Feature extraction was performed on the original images using a fixed bin width of 25.0, without additional intensity normalization and without isotropic voxel resampling. The interpolator was set to sitkBSpline. A detailed summary of extraction settings, preprocessing parameters, delta feature generation rules, and modeling workflow is provided in Supplementary Table S1 (Table 1).

Machine learning pipeline

All computational analyses were performed in Python (version 3.8) using pandas, numpy, scikit-learn, and SciPy. A structured machine-learning workflow was used to identify the integrated predictive signature with the highest discriminatory performance.

Feature selection

To address the high dimensionality of the radiomic dataset relative to cohort size, delta radiomic features were first ranked according to univariate discriminatory ability using Mann-Whitney U testing and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) analysis. The top 15 ranked features were retained for multivariable exploration. Subsequently, an exhaustive combinatorial search was performed across feature subsets ranging from 1 to 6 candidate radiomic variables to identify the best-performing integrated model.

Model development

Predictive models were constructed using logistic regression with elastic net regularization. This approach was selected to reduce overfitting risk and address collinearity among predictors by combining L1- and L2-based shrinkage. The logistic regression model was fitted using the saga solver with penalty=’elasticnet’, l1_ratio = 0.5, C = 1.0, max_iter = 5000, and random_state = 42.

To evaluate the incremental contribution of radiomics beyond routine clinical data, a clinical-first integration strategy was adopted. Age, menopausal status, and visceral metastasis category were included as baseline clinical variables, and radiomic features were subsequently added to generate integrated models. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, whereas continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization. The final best-performing model was defined by comparison of the baseline clinical model with integrated clinical-plus-radiomics models.

Given the limited sample size and imbalanced outcome distribution, synthetic oversampling and class-weighted learning were deliberately not used. Instead, class imbalance was addressed through elastic net regularization and internal cross-validation, and this methodological choice was taken into account in the interpretation of model performance.

Statistical analysis and internal validation

Because of the limited sample size (n = 33), leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was used for internal validation. Model performance was assessed primarily by AUC, together with accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Out-of-fold predicted probabilities were generated using LOOCV, and binary class predictions were derived using a default probability threshold of 0.5.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in radiomic feature distributions between Responders and Non-Responders were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test. To further characterize uncertainty around observed differences, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was performed to estimate 95% confidence intervals. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patterns observed in the leading delta features

Among the highest-ranked features, several texture-, intensity-, and shape-related variables showed different distributions between Responders and Non-Responders. The ngtdm_Complexity feature showed median percentage changes of -0.9% in Responders and + 1.2% in Non-Responders (p = 0.117). The glrlm_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis feature also differed between groups (p = 0.074).

Among intensity-based variables, firstorder_Range (p = 0.081), firstorder_Maximum (p = 0.084), and firstorder_90Percentile (p = 0.150) were among the leading ranked features. In addition, the shape descriptor shape_Elongation was identified among the top-ranked variables (p = 0.127).

Machine learning-based prediction

Integrated best-performing model

To identify the best-performing predictive signature, standard clinical variables were combined with top-ranked delta radiomic features using an integrated modeling strategy. The final best-performing model consisted of three clinical variables (age, menopausal status, and visceral metastasis category) and two delta radiomic features (Delta_Pct_firstorder_90Percentile and Delta_Pct_shape_Elongation).

Using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), the integrated model achieved an AUC of 0.930. The confusion matrix for the final model is shown in Fig. 11. Of 25 Responders, 24 were correctly classified, corresponding to a sensitivity of 96.0%. Of 8 Non-Responders, 2 were correctly classified, corresponding to a specificity of 25.0%. Overall accuracy was 78.8%. To further evaluate the model’s operating characteristics, a post-hoc threshold optimization was performed. When the decision threshold was adjusted to maximize the Youden index (optimal threshold = 0.69), the model achieved a more balanced performance with a sensitivity of 88.0% and a specificity of 87.5% (Fig. 12).

Discussion

This retrospective single-center pilot study suggests that delta radiomics derived from the low-dose, non-contrast CT component of routine PET/CT may provide complementary information for treatment response assessment in patients with HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer with bone-predominant disease receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy. In the present dataset, the integrated model combining clinical variables with two delta radiomic features achieved higher discriminatory performance than the clinical-only model. These findings support the feasibility of extracting longitudinal response-related information from routinely acquired CT data in a disease setting in which conventional response assessment remains challenging [8, 10, 11, 13, 14].

A major clinical rationale for this approach is the persistent difficulty of monitoring bone metastases using conventional morphologic criteria alone. In routine practice, skeletal lesions are often difficult to assess with size-based methods, whereas repeated metabolic imaging may be constrained by cost, access, and cumulative imaging burden [8–12]. Within this context, the low-dose CT component of PET/CT represents an underused source of potentially informative quantitative data. Our results suggest that longitudinal structural information extracted from this routinely acquired CT component may complement, rather than replace, established metabolic assessment [10, 11].

One conceptual strength of the present study is the use of delta radiomics rather than static single-timepoint radiomic descriptors. By focusing on within-patient change between baseline and follow-up examinations, delta features may better reflect therapy-associated structural evolution over time [14, 15]. This rationale is broadly aligned with prior work showing that longitudinal imaging phenotypes may capture treatment sensitivity before overt anatomic progression becomes evident. In particular, Khorrami et al. reported that delta CT radiomic features could predict early response to CDK4/6 inhibitors in metastatic HR-positive breast cancer with liver metastases [15]. Although the metastatic site and clinical endpoint differ from those of the present study, that work supports the broader premise that longitudinal CT-derived imaging biomarkers may be informative during CDK4/6 inhibitor-based treatment [14, 15] (Table 2).

These findings suggest that longitudinal changes in intensity- and shape-related descriptors may capture response-associated remodeling patterns not fully reflected by routine clinical features alone. At the same time, these observations should be interpreted in an exploratory framework. Several of the leading radiomic variables did not reach conventional statistical significance on univariate testing, which is not unexpected given the limited sample size and imbalanced outcome distribution. Accordingly, the selected feature patterns are best interpreted as candidate imaging signals requiring confirmation rather than as definitively established biomarkers [14, 25, 26].

The shape-related findings are particularly interesting, but they should not be overinterpreted mechanistically. We observed a tendency toward increasing elongation in Non-Responders, suggesting that longitudinal morphologic descriptors may contain information not fully captured by intensity-based features alone. One possible explanation is that these changes reflect heterogeneous structural remodeling of bone metastatic involvement under ineffective treatment, potentially including mixed lytic-sclerotic evolution. However, because our study did not include lesion-level sub-classification according to lytic, blastic, or mixed metastatic phenotype, and did not include paired histopathologic or molecular correlates such as serial biopsy or circulating tumor DNA analysis, any interpretation linking delta shape features to a specific biologic escape pattern should remain hypothesis-generating. Prior studies in other imaging settings support the relevance of shape-related descriptors, but not a specific mechanism in this disease context [22–24].

The comparison against the clinical-only baseline remains one of the most relevant findings of this study. In our dataset, adding delta radiomic features increased the AUC from 0.775 to 0.930, suggesting incremental predictive value beyond age, menopausal status, and visceral metastasis category. This observation is encouraging, particularly because the analysis was based on routinely acquired imaging rather than additional dedicated imaging or invasive biomarker sampling. While longitudinal biochemical markers such as CA 15 − 3 or alkaline phosphatase may also be informative, they were not included in the current model in order to preserve statistical parsimony in this small exploratory cohort and to avoid introducing additional heterogeneity related to marker secretion patterns [25, 27].

At the operating threshold used in the present analysis, the model showed high sensitivity for Responders but substantially lower specificity for Non-Responders. This pattern should be interpreted carefully. On one hand, high sensitivity may be useful for identifying patients with imaging patterns associated with treatment benefit. On the other hand, lower specificity implies a risk of false reassurance, whereby a patient with ineffective treatment could be misclassified as responding. This issue is particularly relevant in the setting of CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy, where unnecessary continuation of ineffective treatment may delay alternative therapeutic strategies and prolong exposure to cost and toxicity [4–7]. Our primary analysis utilized a pre-specified default probability threshold of 0.5. This operating point reflects a sensitivity-prioritized strategy, which may be clinically acceptable in early treatment assessment settings where missing a true responder could lead to premature discontinuation of effective therapy. Nevertheless, a post-hoc exploratory analysis maximizing the Youden index demonstrated that the model can be tuned to achieve a high specificity (87.5%) without a substantial loss in sensitivity (88.0%). This confirms that the low specificity observed at the default threshold is a function of the selected cut-off rather than an inherent failure of the model’s underlying discriminatory power.At the same time, the specificity estimate in the present study is inherently unstable because it is based on only eight Non-Responders. Accordingly, the current model should be viewed as a supportive and exploratory stratification tool rather than a standalone decision instrument, and future studies should evaluate threshold optimization in larger and more balanced cohorts [25, 27].

Our findings should also be interpreted within the broader landscape of quantitative imaging biomarkers in oncology. Beyond metastatic breast cancer, CT-derived radiomic signatures have shown prognostic and predictive value in other disease settings, supporting the broader biologic plausibility of radiomics as a treatment-response framework [13, 14, 16]. Prior work in breast cancer, spinal bone metastases, and other systemic therapy settings further supports the relevance of quantitative texture and shape descriptors, although direct comparison across modalities, metastatic sites, and endpoints should be made cautiously [15, 22–24].

The broader context of hybrid molecular imaging and theranostics is also relevant, although it should be framed appropriately. Our study does not evaluate theranostic radiopharmaceuticals, radiolabeled vitamins, or targeted radionuclide therapy directly. Nevertheless, recent literature in hybrid molecular imaging highlights the increasing convergence of quantitative image analysis and precision oncology. In this sense, CT-based delta radiomics may be viewed as part of a wider movement toward extracting biologically relevant information from clinically acquired imaging data, even when the analytic strategy differs from radiopharmaceutical-based theranostic approaches [17, 18].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population was small (n = 33), and the class distribution was imbalanced (25 Responders vs. 8 Non-Responders), increasing the risk of model instability despite the use of elastic net regularization and internal cross-validation [22, 24]. Second, the study was retrospective and single-center, and no independent external validation cohort was available [14, 22, 23]. Third, all scans were acquired using the same institutional PET/CT workflow, which likely reduced within-cohort acquisition heterogeneity, but robustness across other scanners, vendors, and reconstruction settings remains unproven [9, 23]. Fourth, segmentation was performed using a hybrid workflow in which all TotalSegmentator-derived skeletal masks were individually reviewed by an experienced nuclear medicine specialist and minimally refined when needed before feature extraction. This approach strengthened segmentation quality control; however, formal inter-observer and intra-observer reproducibility analyses were not performed, and the exact frequency of minor manual boundary adjustments was not prospectively quantified. Finally, percentage delta calculations were stabilized using a predefined threshold rule for near-zero baseline values. Although this approach avoided unstable estimates, alternative formulations of delta feature construction may also be explored in future work [14, 23].

Despite these limitations, the study has several practical strengths. It focuses on a clinically important subgroup in whom response monitoring remains difficult, it leverages routinely acquired imaging without requiring additional dedicated CT acquisition or contrast administration, and it uses a transparent code-based workflow with explicitly reported extraction and modeling parameters [8, 10, 11, 13, 14]. In addition, the present findings extend the application of delta radiomics to a bone-predominant metastatic HR+/HER2- population receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy, a setting that remains underexplored in the imaging biomarker literature. Viewed in this context, the current work may serve as a foundation for larger validation studies designed to test whether CT-based delta radiomics can contribute to clinically meaningful response stratification in metastatic breast cancer [14, 15].

Future research should focus on external validation across institutions and scanner settings, prospective confirmation in larger cohorts, and integration with complementary biologic markers such as circulating tumor DNA, serum biomarkers, or lesion-level phenotype annotation. Such work will be necessary to determine whether CT-based delta radiomics can evolve from an exploratory imaging biomarker into a clinically useful decision-support tool in metastatic breast cancer [14, 15, 23].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this pilot study suggests that delta radiomics derived from the low-dose, non-contrast CT component of routine ^18F-FDG PET/CT may provide complementary information for treatment response assessment in patients with HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer with bone-predominant disease receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy. In this dataset, integration of selected delta radiomic features with baseline clinical variables improved discriminatory performance compared with a clinical-only model, supporting the feasibility of this approach in a clinically challenging setting.

At the same time, these findings should be interpreted in light of the retrospective single-center design, limited sample size, class imbalance, lack of external validation, and absence of formal segmentation reproducibility analysis. Accordingly, the present results should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Nevertheless, the study supports the concept that routinely acquired CT data may contain clinically relevant longitudinal imaging information beyond conventional assessment alone and may help inform future response-stratification strategies in metastatic breast cancer.

Further validation in larger, prospectively collected, and multi-institutional cohorts will be necessary to determine the robustness, generalizability, and potential clinical utility of CT-based delta radiomics across different acquisition settings and patient subgroups. To support transparency and facilitate future validation, the source code used in the present study has been made available as Supplementary File 2.
