Extract of endophytic fungus Cepalotheca foveolata (N11) inhibits lytic bacteriophage: a study on activity, cytotoxicity, and chemical profiling
Authors: Aman Ullah, Talmeez UR Rehman, Shahzar Khan, Qurban Ali, Mahwish Ali, Abdul Haleem, Sajid Iqbal, Mohammed Raashid, Abdul Haq, Safia Ahmed, Abebe Bogale, Fatmah M. Alqahtani, Omar A. Almohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viral infections are one of the leading causes of disease worldwide. The continuous emergence of new viruses has limited the effectiveness and availability of antiviral drugs. Fungal endophytes are considered important sources for the discovery of novel antiviral agents. In this study, a bacteriophage was used as a safe and economical viral model to screen for antiviral compounds from endophytic fungi. Bacteriophage AU-1 and its host bacteria (Escherichia coli) were isolated from sewage water. The endophytic fungus Cepalotheca foveolata (N11) was screened using a plaque reduction assay. The fungal extract was fractionated via liquid-liquid partition, and its antiphage activity was evaluated against the isolated lytic bacteriophage using a plaque reduction assay. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the Artemia salina model, and chemical profiling of active fractions was performed using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). C. foveolata crude extracts exhibited strong anti-phage activity, showing 100% plaque reduction at 1 mg/100 µL. Following pilot-scale cultivation, the extract was purified by silica-gel column chromatography. Active pooled fractions (F31–F40) showed > 93% plaque inhibition at 0.4 mg/100 µL. GC-MS analysis revealed four major compounds, including benzophenone, a known antiviral agent. The results demonstrate the utility of bacteriophage-based assays for the preliminary screening of antiviral compounds from fungal extracts. This approach is simple, safe, and cost-effective, and the identified extracts warrant further investigation for the isolation of potential antiviral agents.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10123-026-00788-2.

Introduction

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that depend entirely on host cell machinery for their replication and survival. Their genomes consist of either single or double-stranded DNA or RNA, arranged in linear or circular forms. Viruses are responsible for a wide range of human diseases, with RNA viruses often causing outbreaks and pandemics. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), major global viral threats include Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (SARS-CoV), Human Immunodeficiency viruses (HIV), Hepatitis C Virus (HCV), Zika virus, Dengue virus, Nipah virus, Influenza virus, Ebola virus, Smallpox virus, and the Spanish flu virus (Lu et al. 2021). The treatment of viral infections remains challenging due to the limited number of antiviral drugs. Although existing antiviral drugs are beneficial, the production of novel antiviral drugs is important due to viral mutation, rapid transmission, shortage of drugs, and the emergence of resistance. Three basic problems of current antiviral pharmacotherapies are: (i) severe toxicity, (ii) emergence of resistance, and (iii) low bioavailability (Chaudhuri et al. 2018).

To overcome these issues, scientists are trying to find out antiviral drugs having broad-spectrum activity (Vigant et al. 2015). The rapid evolution of viruses often leads to resistance, rendering available drugs ineffective. Therefore, natural products are an attractive source for the isolation and production of antiviral agents (Adamson et al. 2021). Endophytes are rich sources of unexplored bioactive compounds and have the potential to be used as a useful source for novel antiviral compounds (Pradeep et al. 2019). Plants, animals, or microbes have long been used as natural sources for drug discovery, therapeutic, and valuable compounds. Among these, microorganisms have significantly contributed to production of pharmaceutically important bioactive secondary metabolites (Kumar et al. 2017).

Endophytic fungi are those microorganisms that reside inside different tissues of plants. They colonized the intracellular and intercellular spaces of host plant tissues without causing any harm. Host plants and endophytic fungi have a mutualistic relationship, where both partners benefit. Microorganisms isolated from medicinal plants, especially endophytic fungi, have proved promising capability to produce compounds with high therapeutic properties (Xie and Zhou 2017). Most of the secondary bioactive metabolites produced by endophytes act as antimicrobial agents against many etiological agents. The discovery of novel and unique secondary metabolites as antibiotics from endophytic fungi has played a significant role in combating drug resistance against pathogenic microbes of humans, animals, and plants.

Endophytic fungi are one of the potent sources for the discovery of new antiviral drugs. Several compounds have been isolated from endophytic fungi with potent antiviral activities against HIV, influenza virus, Dengue virus, and human cytomegalovirus (Lacerda et al. 2022). For instance, Cytonic acid A (C32H36O10) and B (C32H36O10) from Cytonaema spp have been reported to have antiviral activity (Kheiralla et al. 2023). In addition, the endophytic fungus Penicillium speciesproduces Brefeldin A with antiviral potential against HCV, Hepatitis B virus (Lacerda et al. 2022). Furthermore, it was reported that themangrove plant fungi called Streptomyces sp. produce a compound Xiamycin, which is selectively active against HIV. Pleospora tarda is another endophytic fungus, which produces antiviral compounds like alternariol-(9)-methyl and alternariol.

Despite high potential, the reported antiviral compounds from endophytic fungi are very limited. However, some promising bioactive substances have already been isolated as an alternate source of antiviral drugs. A study has reported about 360 isolates of morphologically different fungi from 81 different species of Thai medicinal plants. Among these, 40 isolates exhibited strong inhibiting activity against Herpes simplex virus type (Wiyakrutta et al. 2004). However, there is a significant gap in exploring endophytic fungi from underexplored regions, particularly from the diverse ecosystem of Pakistan. Additionally, there are very few studies on the antiviral potential ofCepalotheca Foveolata (N11) from Pakistan.

This research aims to evaluate the antiviral activity of endophytic fungus Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) growing on medicinal plants of the Bajaur district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, through a bacteriophage model system. The objectives are: (1) screening Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) crude extracts for its antiviral effects by plaque reduction assays, (2) improving growth conditions to produce more active metabolites, and (3) partially identifying the active antiviral compounds of interest by chromatography and spectroscopy analyses. The study is unique as it describes for the first time the antiviral activity of Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11), isolated in Pakistan, using a bacteriophage-based assay as a simple, safe, and efficient initial screening method for antiviral compounds of fungal origin.

Wastewater samples (200 mL each) were collected in sterile containers from sewage water channels and treatment systems across different regions of Quaid-i-Azam University (QAU), Islamabad, Pakistan. Multiple samples were obtained from each location (Table 1; Fig. 1) and stored at 4 °C until further processing. Sampling was conducted in the morning (09:00–10:00). The specific collection site (Latitude 33.746° N, Longitude 73.136° E) receives predominantly domestic wastewater from university residential blocks, with minimal direct input from agricultural or industrial sources. Isolation of cacteria

Bacterial isolation was performed from wastewater samples using standard microbiological techniques. Initial screening was conducted on MacConkey agar, a selective and differential medium. Presumptive Escherichia coli isolates were selected based on the characteristic pink colony morphology indicative of lactose fermentation. Biochemical profiling included Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) agar, catalase test, oxidase test and gram staining.

Bacteriophage isolation, purification, and titration

Bacteriophages were isolated from wastewater samples collected from different regions of Islamabad, Pakistan, during October-November 2020. Several Escherichia colistrains were isolated from wastewater, among which strain AUB-1 was used as the host bacterium. Phage isolation was performed with slight modifications of a previously described method (Rahimian et al. 2025). To suspend particles, impurities, and algae, the wastewater was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant, which still contained bacteriophages but a lower level of bacteria and particulate matter, was carefully collected. Afterwards, the clarified sample was transferred to a sterile flask containing 40 mL LB broth, 25 mL wastewater, and 20 mL of bacterial culture with an OD600 of 1.0. Subsequently, the mixture was incubated in a shaking incubator at 120 rpm at room temperature for 15 h. Following incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane syringe filter. After that, a spot assay was performed to analyze bacteriophage activity. For this purpose, 100 µL of overnight bacterial culture was mixed with 3 mL of soft agar and spread on a Luria broth (LB) agar plate. Following solidification, 10 mL of the filtered phage suspension was deposited on the plate, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Plates with distinct areas of lysis were chosen to undergo further analysis after incubation.

Further, a plaque assay method was carried out following the spot assay. In plaque assays, 100 µL of the phage filtrate was combined with 100 µL of pre-grown bacterial culture in a 15 mL sterile falcon tube and incubated at temperatures of 37 °C and 15–20 min. Then, 3 mL of soft agar was put in, mixed carefully, and poured into LB agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The plaques were removed aseptically with a sterile blade and put in 50 mL of LB broth that contained bacteria. Phage amplification of the suspension was done by incubation at 37 °C at 120 rpm. This purification procedure was carried out thrice. Lastly, the phage suspension was centrifuged at 6, 000 rpm and incubated at 4 °C to eliminate bacterial cells. This supernatant was then filtered using a 0.22 mm syringe filter and stored at 4 °C till further use.

Characterization of bacteriophage

Host range determination

To determine the host range of phage AU-1, different bacterial strains were isolated from the wastewater and tested. A total of 15 bacterial strains were evaluated using a spot assay with slight modifications of a previously described method (Kutter et al. 2009). For this purpose, 100 µL of overnight bacterial culture was mixed with 3 mL of soft agar and then spread onto LB agar plates. After solidifying agar on each plate, 10 µL filtrate was spotted and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the bacterial sensitivity was assessed based on the presence of clear lysis zones.

Thermal stability of phage

For finding out the temperature tolerance of phages, the previously used protocol was adapted with some modifications (Kuo et al. 2025). Bacteriophage suspensions were placed in different types of Eppendorf tubes and incubated at various temperatures (30 °C, 33 °C, 37 °C, 42 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C,60 °C and 65 °C) for 1 h. Following incubation, the soft agar overlay technique was used to assess the determination of the survival rate of the treated phage.

pH stability of phage

Bacteriophage stability was tested on different pH levels (3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11), using previous protocols with some modifications (Kuo et al. 2025). For each condition, 1 mL of bacteriophage filtrate was added to 9 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) with a pH of 1–9. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature overnight. The viability of the phage was assessed using the soft agar method.

Endophytic fungal strains

The Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11)endophytic fungal strain was taken from the Department of Microbiology, Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad, Pakistan, where it was previously isolated. It was initially a local isolate of the Bajaur district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. Prior characterization of this strain has been reported (Jakubovska et al. 2018). The culture was inoculated on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for two weeks. These cultures were used for obtaining fungal extracts.

Preparation of inoculum and fungal metabolites extraction

Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11)was subcultured on Sabaraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) plates and incubated for two weeks at 28 ± 2 °C. Then three discs (1–3 mm) of the selected endophytic fungi were cut with the help of a sterile blade and cultured in a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 200 mL of Sabouraud dextrose broth (SDB) and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C in a shaking incubator at 120 rpm for 2–3 weeks. After incubation, the fungal cultures were filtered through a sterile gauze piece followed by a Whatman filter paper. For metabolite extraction, the culture filtrate was mixed with ethyl acetate in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio in a separating funnel and left undisturbed for 3–4 h to allow phase separation. The organic phase was collected, and ethyl acetate was evaporated at 40–45 °C using a rotary evaporator to obtain the crude extract (Said et al. 2025).

Plaque assay and screening of crude extract

For initial screening of antiviral compounds, a plaque reduction assay was performed using the double-layer agar method (Sonmez et al. 2025). In the first step, 1 mg/100 µL of fungal crude extract was prepared in ethyl acetate. Afterwards, the whole extract was mixed with 100 µL of bacteriophage filtrate of known PFU in a sterile Eppendorf tube and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. Subsequently, 200 µL of an overnightE. coli culture was added to the mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The suspension was then mixed with 3 mL of soft agar and overlaid onto LB agar plates. After drying, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Laboratory-scale cultivation and extraction

After screening, the Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) was chosen, as it was found to have high antiviral potential. The fungal culture was aseptically isolated with a sterile blade into 6 discs (1–3 mm) before inoculation into 3,000 mL Erlenmeyer flasks having 2000 mL of SDB. The culture was incubated at 28 ± 2 °C at 120 rpm. Following the incubation, the fungal crude extract was retrieved as described earlier.

Purification of crude extracts

The crude extract was further purified into active antiviral fractions using column chromatography. A glass column (2 × 35 cm) was packed with 16 g of silica gel C60 (particle size 0.063–0.200 mm) as the stationary phase. The crude extract was carefully loaded onto the top of the column, followed by an additional thin layer of silica gel to protect the sample. Elution of bioactive compounds was carried out using mobile phases consisting of different solvent systems (n-hexane, ethyl acetate, and methanol) at varying ratios (from 100:0:0 to 0:0:100, v/v/v), with a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min, until complete separation was achieved. The collected fractions were stored separately in vials. Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) was performed to compare the fractions, and those with identical retention factors (Rf) values were pooled together. Based on the TLC profile, similar fractions of Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) were combined (Table 2). Cytotoxicity activity assay

The cytotoxicity of the partially purified compound from Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11)was evaluated using the brine shrimp lethality assay (Sukkum et al. 2025), with some modifications. In artificial sea salt, brine shrimp (Artemia salina) eggs were hatched for 24 h. The swimming nauplii were harvested using a Pasteur pipette, and 10 nauplii were placed in each vial containing 10 mL of sea salt solution. The dissolved test compound was introduced in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at the concentrations of 0.2 mg, 0.4 mg, and 1 mg/vial. The control groups were only treated with DMSO. The mortality was recorded by observing the nauplii after 24 h. Further experiments were conducted at 48 h and 72 h to determine the time-dependent cytotoxic activity of the metabolite under test.

GC-MS analysis

Fractions F31-F40 were analyzed using gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (GC-MS) at the Department of Chemistry at Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad. A 7890B GC system with a 5977 A mass selective detector from Agilent Technologies was utilized. The separation was done through an HP-5MS fused-silica capillary column (30 m x 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). The carrier gas was helium (99.999% purity) with a constant flow rate of 1.5 mL/min.

The injector temperature was kept at 250 °C, and samples (5 uL) were injected in splitless mode. The column oven was first heated to 120 °C for 2 min, ramped at 10 °C/min up to 280 °C, and maintained for 10 min. The effective run time was 28 min. The MS used electron ionization (EI) mode at an energy of 70 eV, ion source temperature of 230 °C, and quadrupole temperature of 150 °C. The mass spectra were obtained at 40–550 m/z.

Chromatographic peaks were automatically deconvoluted and identified on the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Mass Spectral Library. This was done by confirming compounds with a minimum spectral match quality index of 80 or more. Each compound was observed, and the retention time, molecular weight, molecular formula, and patterns of fragmentation were documented.

Results

Isolation of and identification of bacteria

Bacterial strains were isolated from wastewater samples and screened for Escherichia coli using standard microbiological methods. On MacConkey agar, presumptive E. coli colonies were identified by their characteristic pink coloration, indicative of lactose fermentation. Subsequent confirmatory testing of purified isolates included Gram staining, Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) agar assays, and catalase and oxidase tests. All isolates exhibited a phenotypic profile consistent with E. coli: they were Gram-negative rods, produced an acidic butt and slant with gas formation on TSI agar, tested positive for catalase, and were oxidase negative. These biochemical and morphological characteristics confirm the identification of the isolates as Escherichia coli. Detailed results from all biochemical tests are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

Plant endophytic fungi are a significant reservoir of novel bioactive compounds with considerable pharmaceutical potential (Manganyi and Ateba 2020). This study focused on isolates fromTeucrium stocksianum, selecting the strain Cepalotheca foveolata N11 for detailed investigation based on promising initial screening. While previous work on this strain has explored other properties, its antiviral potential remained uncharacterized. Fungi from the Cepalothecagenus and related taxa are increasingly recognized for their biosynthetic diversity, capable of producing antimicrobial and antiviral secondary metabolites (Sarwar et al. 2025). Our investigation intoC. foveolata N11 was therefore driven by the hypothesis that it harbors untapped chemical defenses with therapeutic relevance.

Furthermore, in this study, we use bacteriophage as a viral model for preliminary screening of antiviral compounds. For this purpose, a plaque reduction assay was performed for the evaluation of the antiviral potential of different compounds isolated from endophytic fungal strains. The use of phages as viral models has been reported by several research groups. In such assays, antiviral activity is determined by comparing plaque numbers between treated and control samples, where the absence or reduction of plaques in the treated group indicates antiviral activity (Dembitsky 2006). For instance, (Ramezani et al. 2008) investigated the antiviral effect ofEuphorbia helioscopiaagainst bacteriophages, while some studies reported antiviral activity of borage extract against free phages, showing that at 5 mg of extract, a 50% reduction in phage numbers was achieved (Abolhassani 2010). Their findings suggested that the active material of the extract can affect the coat protein or tail of the phage, which are most important in phage absorption to their bacterial hosts.

In this study, the cell-free broth was extracted using ethyl acetate and methanol. The crude extracts were further purified, and the fractions obtained showed more than 70% PFU reductions even at a low concentration of 0.1 mg/150 µL. The antiviral activities of the crude extracts and their purified fractions were dependent on concentration. More inhibition with the concentration of the product increased, but many of the samples even showed 70% PFU reduction at a lower concentration of 0.1 mg/150 µL.

From the metabolite analysis by GC-MS, we found that the Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) has significant potential to produce a diverse range of bioactive metabolites. Among the compounds identified, the major metabolite was Benzophenone. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of benzophenone being isolated from Cephalothecales. Benzophenone derivatives have been reported to function as viral non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (Ferris et al. 2005). Additionally, these compounds have been described as selective inhibitors of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) reverse transcriptase (Ma et al. 2011). Moreover, In silico studies suggest that benzophenone derivatives exhibit inhibitory activity against the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) of SARS-CoV-2 (Patil et al. 2022).

In the present research work, benzophenone detected through GC-MS analysis has potent antiviral activity against Bacteriophage AU-1. Benzophenone is also characterized by the capability to intercalate or react with nucleic acids because of their plane aromatic rings (Jakubovska et al. 2018). This property indicates a possible mechanism in which the compound inserts itself into the phage DNA or RNA which may block the process of packaging, transcription or replication of the genome. Alternatively, the amphiphilic nature of the molecule can be the cause of attachments with the phage capsid proteins, causing conformational alterations that disrupt virion stability or inhibit adequate adherence of the virions to the host bacterium (Bai et al. 2023).

In previous studies, four different antiviral compounds were isolated from the marine fungus species Aspergillus terreus. These compounds showed inhibitory activity against HSV-1 with IC50 Values of 12.76, 68.16, 62.08, and 68 µM. Similarly, another antiviral compound Asperterrestide A was isolated from A. terreus, which exhibits antiviral effect on H3N2and H1N1 with values of 8.1µM and 15µM, respectively (Zorofchian Moghadamtousi et al. 2015).

In the present study, metabolites of Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) were extracted using ethyl acetate. Among the isolated compounds, oryzaeins A-D, at 20 µM concentration, showed different inhibition rates at different concentrations against bacteriophages. In another related study, three new sesquiterpenoid and xanthone derivative compounds have been isolated from the fungus Stachybotry sp. The antiviral activity of these compounds has been evaluated against enterovirus. They exhibited inhibitory activity with IC50values of 40.3 and 50.0, and 30.1 µM against in vitro replication of Enterovirus-71 (Qin et al. 2015).

The novelty of our work lies in the identification of benzophenone from Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) and the demonstration of its potent activity against a bacteriophage model. The limitation of this study is that while the phage model provides a robust initial screen, the translational relevance of benzophenone’s activity against human pathogenic viruses requires direct validation in mammalian cell-based assays against relevant viral targets. Future work will focus on the large-scale purification of benzophenone derivatives from C. foveolata N11, followed by in vitro testing against clinically important viruses to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential and precise molecular mechanism. While Artemia salina provided a preliminary toxicity screen, cytotoxicity should be confirmed in mammalian cell models to better predict human safety.

Another recognized limitation is that the quantitative plaque assay data, while performed with internal technical controls, are derived from a single representative experiment. This is appropriate for the primary screening phase reported here but necessitates further validation. The critical next step is to perform full biological triplicate assays with the purified benzophenone compound to generate definitive IC₅₀ values and robust statistical analysis. This will solidify the potency metrics and pave the way for mechanistic and in vivo studies.

It is important to note that the GC–MS analysis, while identifying Benzophenone as a major component, provides a selective profile. The derivatization (silylation) and detection parameters are optimized for volatile and semi-volatile metabolites and thus may not capture the full spectrum of non-volatile or highly polar secondary metabolites produced by C. foveolata N11. Consequently, the four compounds reported represent the major detectable constituents under these conditions, with Benzophenone being the most abundant. Future studies employing complementary techniques such as LC-MS/MS are warranted to provide a more comprehensive metabolomic profile.

Conclusion and future directions

In conclusion, the endophytic fungus Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11), isolated from the Teucrium stocksianum plant, represents a potent source of antiviral secondary metabolites. These bioactive compounds have great therapeutic potential to be used in the pharmaceutical industry as effective antiviral agents against different pathogenic and infectious viral diseases. Nonetheless, additional research on improving strains, standardization, and clinical validation is necessary to maximize their therapeutic utility. Both the crude extract and partially purified fraction of the fungal strain Cepalotheca Foveolata (N11) were found to have an extraordinary antiphage effect against lytic bacteriophages. These endophytic fungal metabolites can be used as natural and cost-effective antiviral drugs with minimal side effects. While the bacteriophage model provides a valid preliminary screening tool, these promising results warrant further investigation. Future work must focus on isolating the pure active compound, elucidating its mechanism of action, and evaluating its efficacy and safety against human pathogenic viruses using mammalian cell culture models to assess its true therapeutic potential.
