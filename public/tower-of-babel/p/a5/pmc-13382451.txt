Real-world implementation of a pre-consultation patient educational tool for intermediate risk prostate cancer
Authors: Mariana Borras-Osorio, Roman O Kowalchuk, Heather R Preston, Anne W Rajkumar, Andrea M Uptmor, Brian J Davis, C Richard Choo, Brad J Stish, Ryan M Phillips, Timothy D Malouff, Bryan J Traughber, Jessica Wilson, Yuliia Lozko, Federico Mastroleo, Mohammad-Javad Namazi, David M Routman, Daniel K Ebner, Mark R Waddle

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate Cancer (PCa) decision-making requires patients to understand their diagnosis and therapeutic options, balancing care nuances and risk-to-benefit profiles. The complexity of information and a wide range of treatments often exceed what can be covered in consultation. Our team developed a PCa Educational Tool to enhance patient understanding and support decision-making process.MethodsThe PCa Educational Tool was designed using plain language principles and evidence-based content. The tool was distributed automatically to newly diagnosed intermediate-risk PCa patients. A post-use questionnaire assessed usability, satisfaction, and perceived educational value. Qualitative feedback was categorized by sentiment and themes. Treatment selection was explored between sequential cohorts before and after implementation.ResultsA total of 395 patients received the tool, and 79.7% completed the questionnaire. Among respondents, 88.1% felt more prepared for discussion with their care team, and 74.8% indicated improved understanding of their diagnosis. Satisfaction rates were high, with 93.9% of respondents recommending the tool to other patients. In exploratory analysis, its use is correlated with a statistically significant shift in treatment choices in sequential cohorts (p = 0.048). Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most prevalent non-cutaneous malignancy among men worldwide and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Approximately 70% of PCa patients in the US are diagnosed at localized stage with excellent survival rate (97.5% 5-year survival) [2]. For localized PCa, a wide range of treatment options are available, including active surveillance, radiation therapy, radical prostatectomy, and focal therapies with or without systemic therapies [3], [4], [5], [6].

Most PCa patients prefer a shared decision-making approach to understand their condition and treatment options, and to ensure their needs, preferences, and priorities are considered [7]. Due to all available treatment options, the initial patient education during consultation is inherently lengthy, often exceeding the time available during medical appointments [8], [9]. Although there has been a steady increase in medical information on PCa, its readability and accessibility for non-medically trained individuals remains limited. This is further compounded by the variable quality of information available in online searches. These shortcomings compromise patients' understanding of their disease, prognosis, and treatment options, all associated with decision regret [7].

Effective communication strategies and educational tools are essential for improving shared decision-making in cancer care. These resources bridge gaps in patients' understanding of medical information, empowering them to make informed decisions, enhancing satisfaction, and improving outcomes, while minimizing decision regret. Our team developed an online PCa Educational Tool to guide newly diagnosed intermediate risk PCa patients, improving their understanding of the disease and treatment options prior to consultation.

Methods

Study design

This prospective single arm study assessed the feasibility, usability, and patient-perceived value of PCa Educational Tool, among newly diagnosed intermediate risk PCa patients scheduled for initial consultation with the Radiation Oncology Department at our institution. Both the intervention and historical control cohorts were restricted to the same eligibility criteria. Treatment selection patterns were compared with a retrospective historical control cohort.

PCa educational tool development

For the PCa Educational Tool development, interviews with four board-certified radiation oncologists specializing in genitourinary cancers were performed, simulating real-life in-person consultations for recently diagnosed intermediate risk PCa following the typical structure of the clinical encounters. Using the highest evidence-based treatment information, relevant data was added, including efficacy, general considerations, and adverse event profiles, derived from the ProtecT Randomized Trial and other sources [5], [6]. Content related to surgical interventions and treatment-related adverse events was reviewed and approved by the Urology Department. Careful attention was given to the complexity, volume, and readability of material with the goal of designing an educational tool in anticipation of their upcoming consultation. The tool was developed in collaboration with Health Education and Content Services with healthcare-specific expertise, using plain language principles and best practices in health literacy, making complex information more accessible, readable, and actionable [10], [11], [12]. Readability analysis reported Flesch Reading Ease Score of 66.0 and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 7.0, consistent with recommendations for patient-facing health education materials. By reducing cognitive burden and emotional distress, the tool facilitates patients' progress towards better understanding of options and treatment decisions that align with their values and needs. At the end of the tool, additional links were provided for patients seeking more in-depth information. The tool distribution was fully automated, programmed to be sent to all newly diagnosed intermediate risk PCa patients the day before their initial radiation oncology consultation, beginning on March 7, 2024.

Questionnaires

A questionnaire was developed to evaluate the patients' experience (Supplement 1). The link to the questionnaire was embedded at the end of the PCa Educational Tool, requiring patients to navigate through the content to access it. Questionnaire completion therefore serves as a reasonable proxy for at least partial engagement with the tool, though it does not confirm the depth or duration of interaction. The questionnaire included seven multiple-choice questions grouped in three categories: Engagement and Tool Use, exploring the level of baseline knowledge, learning interest, and continued interaction with the tool; Perceived Educational Value, evaluating the tool's effectiveness in enhancing patients' understanding of their diagnosis and treatment options, and its effects on their preparedness to engage in informed discussion with their cancer care team; and Content Usability and Satisfaction, exploring patient's satisfaction with the structure, design, and content of the tool, and willingness to recommend it to other patients. Additionally, three open-ended qualitative questions captured patient feedback on what they liked most, liked least, and additional comments about the tool. Questionnaire response was encouraged but not mandatory, allowing to skip questions to facilitate compliance. Thus, the response rates for each question vary. Importantly, due to confidentiality and information security limitations, questionnaire responses were anonymous and no identifying information was collected. As a result, comparison between respondents and non-respondents was not feasible.

Statistical analysis plan

Questionnaire response analysis: For categorical questions, a univariate descriptive analysis with absolute frequencies and percentages was performed. For open-ended questions, a detailed content analysis was used. Non-informative responses (i.e., NA, none, nothing, all okay, everything, thanks) were excluded, and the remaining were manually classified into three “opinion” categories (positive, negative, and neutral), based on overall sentiment expressed. The responses classification was completed by a single reviewer, following a codebook which included category definitions and examples. For responses with more than one opinion category, the response was divided into sections, and each opinion was classified separately and analyzed independently. Responses were then grouped into subcategories determined by topics the patient addressed. This process allowed a structured analysis of qualitative data, enabling identification of key components of the tool that were a strength and those that might require further development.

Exploratory analysis on treatment choice: To assess potential effects of the tool on patient's understanding of treatment options, risk-to-benefit profiles, and general considerations, an exploratory analysis was conducted comparing treatment selection in two cohorts. The intervention group included PCa patients scheduled for initial consultation with the Radiation Oncology Department at our institution between March and August 2024, during which PCa Educational Tool was distributed automatically the day before the appointment. Even though all eligible patients received access to the tool, confidentiality and information security limitations prevented us from tracking whether individual patients accessed the tool and the extent of their interaction (number of times used and time in each session) in a way that could be linked to their identification or personal information. For these same limitations, sociodemographic data including educational level, race/ethnicity, health literacy, native/primary language, and insurance status were not collected. The control group included the retrospective cohort of PCa patients scheduled for initial consultation with the Radiation Oncology Department, between June 2023 and early March 2024, prior to the tool's implementation. Final treatment decision for both cohorts was manually retrieved from each patient's clinical care team's documentation in the electronic health records and categorized in treatment groups. Comparative analysis was performed using chi-square test. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Software (Version 29.0.2) – © 2024 IBM Corporation – Armonk, NY, USA.

Results

Sequential cohorts of newly diagnosed intermediate risk PCa patients, before and after the launch of PCa Educational Tool, were included, resulting in a total of 791 patients in the control group and 395 in the intervention group. There was no statistically significant difference in age between the two groups, with a median of 69 and 70 years respectively (p = 0.16). The median duration of radiation oncology consultation, calculated using electronic health record check-in and check-out timestamps as a surrogate measure, was slightly shorter after the launch of the tool at 101 min (vs 106 min, p = 0.015).

For patients with access to the tool, a total of 315 responses (79.7% response rate) of post-use questionnaire were collected. Most patients (88.6%) spent an hour or more reading about PCa prior to using the tool. The majority (88.1%) expressed feeling more prepared for discussions with their PCa care team, and 74.8% reported better understanding of their diagnosis after using the tool. Additionally, 91.7% agreed the course was the right length, 80.5% felt the level of detail in information was appropriate, and 93.9% would recommend the tool to other PCa patients. Table 1 summarizes multiple-choice questionnaire responses. The qualitative analysis of the open-ended responses included 458 answers, of which 62 were excluded for being non-informative, resulting in 396 unique answers. Of these 396 answers, 18 responses had more than one opinion category or subcategory, each opinion being classified independently, resulting in a total of 414 classified opinions. The 414 opinions were classified as positive (n = 289, 69.8%), negative (n = 113, 27.3%), and neutral (n = 12, 2.9%). Of these classified opinions, the most frequently addressed theme was the amount of information, with the majority considering the content to be comprehensive yet concise (n = 188, 45.4%), while others expressed they would have preferred access to more information or resources (n = 88, 21.3%). The structure/format of the tool was also considered ideal by some (n = 32, 7.7%), while others stated they would prefer other types of designs (n = 10, 2.4%). The neutral responses included several topics with the most frequently related to disease related anxiety or treatment nuances, being unrelated to the tool itself. Discussion

The questionnaire results confirm that PCa Educational tool was overall well-received by the patients. The notably high 93.9% of patients that would recommend the tool to other PCa patients highlighted the perceived educational value to the participants. The negative opinions expressed by 27.3% of the patients provide valuable insights into potential areas and topics for refinement to further enhance patient engagement, comprehension, and satisfaction with the decision-making process and their final treatment choice. Interestingly, while some patients registered negative opinions of the tool, a substantial proportion therein would recommend the tool for other PCa patients.

A qualitative analysis of the patient's responses suggests that many patients learned from internet sources about focal therapy options and were interested in learning more about them. This information was not included in the PCa Educational Tool, as focal therapies are not currently classified as a curative treatment option for localized PCa by national or international medical guidelines. However, recognizing the increased interest in this topic, a future update will include information on focal therapies as an emerging treatment alternative, while clearly cautioning that these are not guideline-recommended interventions.

Several factors have been significantly associated with treatment choice. For instance, elderly patients and those with significant medical comorbidities often opt for less aggressive treatments, prioritizing quality-of-life and minimizing the risk of treatment-related adverse events. In contrast, younger and/or healthy patients are more likely to pursue a treatment associated with curative intent [7]. In addition, many patients prefer to reflect on their therapeutic options, discuss with family members, and seek insights from other patients who have undergone similar treatments.

After the initial PCa diagnosis, patients have limited time to make treatment selection decisions, considering all available treatment options, the nuances for each one, and the associated adverse effects. Given the number of options and the amount and complexity of information, decision conflict and regret are common and widely described challenges in PCa care, with studies reporting rates ranging from 10% to 20%, depending on the specific treatment modality and follow-up duration [13], [14], [15]. Evidence suggests that treatment-related adverse events, related to decreased quality-of-life during survivorship, limited involvement in the initial decision-making process, and limited understanding of the disease and available treatment options, are associated with increased decision regret [15]. Importantly, these findings suggest that decision regret in PCa patients may be modifiable by improving pretreatment counseling, helping patients better understand treatment options, frequency and duration of adverse events, helping align expectations with anticipated outcomes. Consistent with this, Gattrell et al. described that the increase in patients' active engagement in their medical decision-making evidenced in recent years, has been associated with significantly lower decision regret [7].

The exploratory treatment selection analysis between the sequential cohorts revealed a small but statistically significant difference in treatment selection with the implementation of PCa Educational Tool, with a higher proportion of patients in the intervention group opting for less invasive treatment alternatives. There was a slight increase in focal therapy selection among the intervention group. While this may partially reflect evolving trends in PCa treatment, it is also possible that the PCa Educational Too indirectly encouraged patients to seek information on other emerging treatment alternatives beyond its content In contrast, we hypothesize that the increased selection of brachytherapy in the intervention group may have been in part, influenced by the tool, though this association cannot be confirmed given the non-randomized design. Since brachytherapy is only available at select centers and performed by a limited number of radiation oncologists, many patients are not fully informed about this option during standard consultations. For example, the tool was distributed to patients seen by more than 15 radiation oncologists, but only 4 perform brachytherapy regularly.

There are currently other PCa educational resources available for patients, including those developed by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) [16] and the American Cancer Society (ACS) [17], which offer comprehensive information on PCa diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. However, these resources are designed to address all PCa disease stages, including advanced and metastatic cancer, and include all treatment options, which could inadvertently contribute to information overload for newly diagnosed patients seeking a more focused overview for their specific disease stage and treatment alternatives. In contrast, the PCa Educational Tool was designed specifically for intermediate risk patients, providing a structured comparison of treatment-related adverse events, expected frequency and duration, and other practical treatment considerations across the different treatment options, aiming to reduce cognitive burden while supporting individualized decision-making. The tool's Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 7.0 falls within the sixth- to eighth-grade range recommended for patient health education materials. It is important to highlight that all resources and tools available are useful in patients' decision-making, and without direct comparative-controlled studies, firm conclusions regarding the relative utility of one educational format versus another cannot be made.

Future steps for PCa Educational Tool include enhancements based on feedback, to improve engagement, increasing personalization, improve patient understanding of the disease and treatment options, aiming to support an enhanced decision-making process. Tiered information structure will be explored, allowing patients to access different levels of detail. A randomized controlled trial is being evaluated to investigate the tool's impact on treatment choice, decision regret, and patient satisfaction, incorporating also tool engagement metrics, and sociodemographic and health literacy data collection.

While this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, patients and advocates were not formally involved in the development of the tool, and being it developed based on simulated consultations. Second, the exploratory treatment selection analysis has a sequential cohort design, comparing a retrospective historical control group with a prospective intervention group, which can be subject to temporal confounding. Third, while all eligible patients received access to the PCa Educational Tool, due to confidentiality and information security limitations, the study did not track which individual patients accessed the tool and the usage and engage metrics. For these same reasons, collection of sociodemographic data, such as race/ethnicity, education level, baseline health literacy, native language, insurance status, and objective extent of interaction with the tool was not possible. This limited assessment of patient-specific factors associated with tool engagement and perceived value, as well as baseline comparison between the cohorts, and should be considered when interpreting findings. Fourth, the qualitative analysis was performed by a single reviewer, preventing inter-rater reliability assessment, which may limit the reproducibility assessment of sentiment and themes categorization. Fifth, the questionnaire response was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias, as patients with stronger opinions might have been more likely to respond. Although the questionnaire link was embedded at the end of the tool, suggesting that respondents engaged with at least part of the content, the extent of interaction for non-respondents and the broader intervention group remains unknown. Finally, this tool was designed for our specific patient population, considering their educational level, health literacy, and English language proficiency. Generalizability to other populations may be limited, as effective educational interventions require tailoring to the specific population and context to optimize engagement and understanding.

Conclusions

PCa Educational Tool was designed to provide patients with a comprehensive self-paced overview of their disease and treatment options prior to the clinical encounter. The tool aims to enhance patient understanding, providing the necessary background while reducing information burden, enabling them to better engage in shared decision-making with their clinical care team. Although questionnaire responses suggested improved patient preparedness and high satisfaction, direct measures of shared decision-making were not assessed. While exploratory analysis of treatment selection suggested differences in treatment patterns between sequential cohorts, a randomized trial will be needed to validate relationship. The tool's fully automated distribution process ensured consistent access to all eligible patients without disrupting existing workflows or requiring additional effort from the care team.
