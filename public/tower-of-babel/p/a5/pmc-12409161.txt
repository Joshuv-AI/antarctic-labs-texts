The Triglyceride-Glucose Index: A Practical Tool for Enhanced Cardiovascular Risk Stratification in Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Jang Won Son
Published: 2025

The global burden of type 2 diabetes continues to increase, underscoring the urgent need for effective tools to stratify cardiovascular (CV) risk. Although traditional risk factors such as hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia are well established, they often fail to capture the full spectrum of metabolic dysfunction, especially insulin resistance, that drives atherosclerosis in patients with diabetes. The study by Kwak et al. [1], published in this issue of Endocrinology and Metabolism, provides compelling evidence that the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a pragmatic and robust predictor of CV outcomes across the spectrum of diabetes duration.

The TyG index, calculated as ln[triglyceride (mg/dL)×fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2], reflects insulin resistance using only routine laboratory parameters. Unlike the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance, which requires fasting insulin measurements that are often unavailable or unreliable, the TyG index is cost-effective, reproducible, and readily integrated into clinical workflows. A growing body of evidence supports the utility of the TyG index across diverse populations and clinical settings. Oh et al. [2] demonstrated its association with CV risk and mortality in type 1 diabetes, while Zhang et al. [3] showed stronger predictive power in individuals with diabetes than in those without diabetes for ischemic heart disease. Dakota et al. [4] reported robust associations with CV events in hypertensive patients, and the meta-analysis by Liu et al. [5] in patients with acute myocardial infarction found that elevated values of the TyG index consistently predicted major adverse CV and cerebrovascular events and mortality, regardless of diabetes status or reperfusion therapy, indicating applicability even in acute care settings.

Beyond CV outcomes, Nayak et al. [6] demonstrated associations of the TyG index with chronic kidney disease, diabetic retinopathy, and cognitive impairment, suggesting broader implications for systemic metabolic health. This expanding evidence base establishes the TyG index as a dependable, scalable surrogate for insulin resistance across varied clinical contexts.

In their nationwide analysis of more than 1 million Korean patients with type 2 diabetes (mean follow-up of 5.6 years), Kwak et al. [1] examined TyG quartiles and their association with myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality. Higher TyG levels were associated with significantly increased CV and mortality risks. After adjustment for conventional risk factors, those in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had an 18% higher risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and 16% higher mortality compared with the lowest quartile (Q1). Importantly, the TyG–CVD association became stronger with longer diabetes duration. Patients with diabetes for ≥10 years had a 44% higher CVD risk in Q4 versus Q1, compared with 18% in newly diagnosed individuals. Mortality risks also increased progressively with disease duration. The index’s predictive power was most pronounced in patients with fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, where Q4 was associated with 33% higher CVD risk and 23% higher mortality. These associations were weaker in well-controlled patients.

Subgroup analyses revealed that the TyG–risk relationship was especially strong in younger patients, non-obese individuals, and non-smokers. Notably, lean patients with diabetes and high TyG had greater relative CVD risk than their obese counterparts, challenging body mass index (BMI)-based risk assumptions.

This large cohort study supports the TyG index as a meaningful CV risk marker in type 2 diabetes that adds predictive value beyond traditional factors. The persistent predictive power across the entire spectrum of diabetes duration, from diagnosis through decades of disease, fills an important knowledge gap and suggests that insulin resistance remains clinically relevant throughout disease progression. The study’s stratification by diabetes duration is novel, marking the first assessment of the prognostic role of the TyG index across the full disease spectrum.

The subgroup findings also refine risk assessment by identifying high-risk phenotypes. Young patients with high TyG may require intensive preventive strategies, and lean patients should not be assumed low-risk based solely on BMI. Incorporating TyG and similar metabolic markers into clinical algorithms could improve risk detection in patients who might otherwise appear low-to-moderate risk by conventional criteria.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. TyG was measured at baseline only, without accounting for longitudinal changes. Hemoglobin A1c data were unavailable, allowing for possible residual confounding by chronic glycemic control. The study population consisted solely of Koreans, limiting generalizability. Moreover, CVD outcomes were restricted to major atherosclerotic events. As with all observational studies, causality cannot be definitively established.

Despite these caveats, the TyG index remains a practical risk-stratification tool. Its predictive utility extends to early detection, as shown by Wen et al. [7], who reported TyG’s ability to predict prediabetes in normal-BMI individuals, particularly younger women. Clinicians can use TyG to uncover high-risk patients who might be overlooked based on glucose or lipid parameters alone, enabling timely intervention through lifestyle modification, earlier cardioprotective therapy, or intensified metabolic management.

Across the diabetes-duration spectrum, TyG offers actionable insight: in newly diagnosed individuals, it identifies opportunities for early intervention; in long-standing disease, it underscores the importance of ongoing risk-factor control. Future inclusion of TyG in formal CVD prediction models could capture insulin-resistance–related risks that traditional calculators miss.

This study also raises key research questions. Interventional trials are needed to determine whether reducing TyG can lower CV event rates. Validation in multi-ethnic populations is essential to broaden applicability. Future studies should evaluate TyG’s predictive role for additional outcomes such as heart failure and microvascular complications.

Given the substantial impact of unrecognized insulin resistance on CV outcomes, there is a clear need for improved risk identification. As diabetes management becomes increasingly complex, practical and scalable tools such as the TyG index can help clinicians navigate evolving metabolic risks.

Overall, the findings highlight that addressing insulin resistance through combined glycemic and lipid management represents a crucial strategy in diabetes care, encouraging a more holistic approach to metabolic control for CV risk reduction. The TyG index is emerging as a valuable addition to the risk-stratification arsenal, offering enhanced precision in identifying patients with diabetes who are most vulnerable to CV events.
