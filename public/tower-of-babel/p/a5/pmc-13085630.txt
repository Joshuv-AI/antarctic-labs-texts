Progress in research on the effects of Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) ovarian injection on early ovarian reserve dysfunction and premature ovarian insufficiency
Authors: Min Hu, Liangdan Tang, Ying Chen, Tian Gao, Yanna Ban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) pose major challenges in reproductive medicine, as few interventions can truly restore ovarian function or fertility. Conventional hormone replacement therapy alleviates symptoms but fails to rejuvenate the ovarian microenvironment, prompting growing interest in regenerative approaches such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy.ObjectiveThis review critically evaluates the therapeutic potential of intraovarian PRP injection for women with EORD and POI, integrating mechanistic insights, preclinical findings, and clinical evidence to clarify its biological rationale and translational challenges.MethodsWe systematically synthesized literature on PRP composition, preparation methods, and reproductive applications. Mechanistic data from animal and cell models were combined with evidence from clinical studies to elucidate how PRP modulates angiogenesis, follicular activation, apoptosis, and immune regulation through pathways such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and mTOR.ResultsPRP delivers a rich concentration of growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles that orchestrate angiogenesis, suppress apoptosis, and remodel the ovarian niche. Preclinical and early clinical studies consistently report improved ovarian reserve markers, partial hormonal recovery, and resumption of menses in some patients, although heterogeneity in protocols and limited sample sizes hinder definitive conclusions.ConclusionIntraovarian PRP represents a biologically plausible and minimally invasive regenerative strategy for ovarian rejuvenation. Future research should prioritize the development of standardized preparation protocols, biomarker-guided patient selection, and large multicenter randomized trials to confirm its safety, efficacy, and long-term clinical value.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), Early ovarian reserve decline (EORD), Ovarian rejuvenation, Regenerative medicine, Folliculogenesis

Introduction

Ovarian reserve dysfunction, which includes early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), constitutes a significant clinical concern in the field of reproductive medicine, bearing substantial implications for both fertility and long-term health outcomes. The ovarian reserve denotes the quantity and quality of oocytes available at a specific point in time. Although it naturally diminishes with advancing age, it can also be prematurely affected by pathological or iatrogenic factors. EORD is defined by an accelerated depletion of follicles before the anticipated age of menopause, while POI is characterized by ovarian failure occurring before the age of 40, typically manifesting as amenorrhea, elevated gonadotropin levels, and hypoestrogenism [1]. These conditions not only lead to infertility and menstrual irregularities but also have systemic repercussions, including osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and neuropsychological disturbances, thereby imposing considerable psychosocial and economic challenges [1].

Contemporary diagnostic methodologies predominantly utilize biomarkers such as anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and antral follicle count (AFC) to facilitate risk stratification. Traditional management strategies, including hormone replacement therapy (HRT), effectively mitigate hypoestrogenic symptoms but do not succeed in restoring follicular reserves or enhancing fertility. Invasive procedures, such as ovarian tissue cryopreservation, are constrained by limited clinical applicability. Consequently, there is a pressing demand for minimally invasive, regenerative interventions that can both alleviate symptoms and address the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms.

Regenerative medicine has emerged as a promising field, with particular emphasis on platelet-rich plasma (PRP). PRP is an autologous plasma fraction that is enriched with platelets and bioactive growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs). These components facilitate processes such as angiogenesis, cellular proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immunomodulation [2, 3]. Originally developed within the realm of hematology, PRP has been effectively utilized in orthopedics, dermatology, and plastic surgery [4]. More recently, its application has extended to gynecology and reproductive medicine, addressing conditions such as endometrial receptivity, Asherman syndrome, and ovarian rejuvenation [5, 6]. The autologous nature of PRP reduces the risk of immunogenic reactions, thereby enhancing its appeal as a therapeutic option.

Initial evidence indicates that intraovarian platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections may enhance anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels, follicle counts, and oocyte quality, potentially leading to improved outcomes in assisted reproductive technology (ART) [7]. The modulation of angiogenesis, inflammation, and oxidative stress by PRP may also mitigate the adverse ovarian microenvironment associated with aging and injury. Nonetheless, the current body of research is constrained by small sample sizes, methodological variability, and the absence of standardized protocols.

Current fertility-preservation options and their limitations

Fertility preservation represents an essential component of reproductive management for women at risk of ovarian insufficiency. Established techniques—including oocyte cryopreservation, embryo cryopreservation, and ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC)—aim to preserve reproductive potential but do not restore ovarian function. Their effectiveness is further limited in women who already present with significantly diminished ovarian reserve, as is commonly observed in EORD or POI, which reduces the feasibility and clinical benefit of these interventions. OTC has gained increasing clinical relevance. While slow freezing remains the conventional method [8], vitrification has emerged as a promising alternative. Notably, the first successful delivery after retransplantation of vitrified ovarian tissue in Europe has recently been reported [9].Because prepubertal girls cannot undergo controlled ovarian stimulation, OTC is the only established fertility-preservation method, endorsed by ASRM and ESHRE for this population. A recent two-decade cohort involving 451 children and adolescents demonstrated long-term endocrine restoration and over 200 live births worldwide [10]. These preventive strategies differ fundamentally from regenerative approaches such as PRP, which seek to restore ovarian function after decline has already occurred.Because existing fertility-preservation strategies do not restore ovarian function and are often not feasible for patients already experiencing ovarian decline, regenerative approaches such as PRP have gained increasing attention.

This review seeks to synthesize the existing body of knowledge regarding intraovarian platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection for the treatment of early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI). We will examine the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, provide a comprehensive summary of both clinical and preclinical evidence, and elucidate mechanistic insights. Additionally, we will address challenges related to patient selection, safety, and standardization. Finally, we propose future research directions, including the discovery of biomarkers and the optimization of therapeutic strategies, to facilitate the responsible integration of PRP into reproductive medicine.Within this context, regenerative medicine has emerged as a promising discipline, with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) demonstrating distinct potential in the rejuvenation of ovarian function.

Pathophysiological basis of early ovarian reserve dysfunction and premature ovarian insufficiency

Definitions and diagnostic criteria

Early ovarian reserve dysfunction (EORD) and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) are significant reproductive disorders affecting women under the age of 40, characterized by compromised ovarian function and endocrine abnormalities. International guidelines define POI as oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea persisting for a minimum of four months before the age of 40, accompanied by serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels typically exceeding 25 IU/L on two separate occasions at least one month apart [11, 12]. EORD represents a milder spectrum of ovarian dysfunction, often identified by declining biomarkers of ovarian reserve prior to the onset of complete ovarian failure. Accurate assessment of these conditions relies on a combination of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), FSH, and antral follicle count (AFC). AMH, secreted by granulosa cells of pre-antral and small antral follicles, is considered the most stable and sensitive marker of ovarian reserve [13, 14], with significantly reduced levels observed in POI [15]. Elevated basal FSH levels indicate impaired ovarian feedback and serve as a classical diagnostic marker [11]. AFC, determined through transvaginal ultrasonography, provides a direct measure of follicle quantity [16]. Clinically, EORD and POI manifest as menstrual irregularities, infertility, and symptoms of estrogen deficiency, including vasomotor instability and osteoporosis [17, 18].The etiologies of early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) encompass genetic mutations, autoimmune disorders, gonadotoxic therapies (such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy), and environmental exposures [15, 19]. Recent guidelines suggest that a single follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) measurement exceeding 25 IU/L may be sufficient for diagnosis, with anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels and repeat FSH tests providing additional clarity in ambiguous cases [11, 20]. Endocrine comorbidities, including thyroid autoimmunity, have been linked to diminished ovarian reserve and negative reproductive outcomes [16, 21]. In summary, EORD and POI are characterized by clinical symptoms and biomarker abnormalities occurring before the age of 40. Early and precise diagnosis is crucial to facilitate timely intervention and mitigate both reproductive and systemic consequences. Collectively, these criteria form a clinical framework for identifying individuals who may benefit from novel regenerative therapies, such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP).

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of Early Ovarian Reserve Decline (EORD) and Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) is complex and multifactorial, encompassing genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, environmental factors, and iatrogenic damage. Genetic predisposition involves mutations that affect folliculogenesis, DNA repair, or hormonal signaling, thereby accelerating follicular depletion. Autoimmune injury is characterized by the presence of autoantibodies against ovarian antigens, leading to follicular destruction and impaired steroidogenesis. Environmental and iatrogenic factors, such as exposure to toxins, chemotherapy, and radiation, contribute to oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis of granulosa cells and oocytes [22, 23]. At the molecular level, follicular apoptosis and oxidative stress are pivotal. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) inflict damage on mitochondria and activate pro-apoptotic pathways, while impaired angiogenesis limits the delivery of oxygen and nutrients, further compromising follicular survival [22, 24, 25].Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has been suggested as a therapeutic intervention to mitigate these processes by delivering growth factors that facilitate angiogenesis, diminish oxidative damage, and inhibit apoptosis [26, 27]. Preclinical studies indicate that PRP may activate dormant primordial follicles, enhance the proliferation of granulosa cells, and modulate signaling pathways such as MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and mTOR, thereby contributing to the restoration of ovarian function [22, 26].

Impact on fertility and quality of life

Diminished ovarian reserve and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) significantly compromise fertility potential, leading to reduced rates of natural conception and decreased success in assisted reproductive technologies (ART) due to a lower number of follicles and diminished oocyte quality. Beyond reproductive implications, estrogen deficiency is associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis, cardiovascular diseases, and neuropsychological disorders, thereby substantially affecting quality of life [17, 18]. Experimental regenerative strategies have demonstrated potential; in animal models, intraovarian administration of platelet-rich plasma (PRP), occasionally in conjunction with stem cell factors, has been shown to enhance oocyte maturation, embryo development, and blastocyst formation [28].The mechanisms involved encompass enhanced mitochondrial function, decreased apoptosis, and beneficial modulation of the ovarian microenvironment. Although these findings remain preliminary, they underscore the dual impact of Early Ovarian Reserve Decline (EORD) and Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) on fertility and systemic health, while highlighting the potential of regenerative therapies to address both reproductive and endocrine consequences. Collectively, these observations advocate for the development of therapeutic strategies that not only alleviate symptoms but also target the fundamental biological processes underlying ovarian dysfunction.

Biological characteristics and preparation of Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP)

Components and bioactivity of PRP

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous plasma fraction characterized by an elevated concentration of platelets beyond physiological levels. Its therapeutic efficacy is primarily attributed to the presence of growth factors stored within platelet α-granules, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs). These factors collectively facilitate angiogenesis, cellular proliferation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and immunomodulation [27, 29, 30]. In addition to growth factors, PRP comprises cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular vesicles (PRP-EVs), which convey proteins, messenger RNAs (mRNAs), and microRNAs to target cells, thereby promoting intercellular communication and augmenting tissue repair processes [31].The biological profile of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is subject to variation based on its leukocyte content (distinguishing between leukocyte-rich PRP [L-PRP] and leukocyte-poor PRP [LP-PRP]), which affects inflammatory responses, as well as the methods of activation, such as the use of calcium chloride, which influence the kinetics of growth factor release [32, 33]. Alternative sources, such as umbilical cord blood-derived PRP, may present unique regenerative profiles in comparison to PRP derived from peripheral blood [30, 34]. Advances in preservation techniques, including cryopreservation and lyophilization, enhance clinical applicability by allowing for long-term storage without significant loss of bioactivity [35, 36]. In conclusion, PRP functions as a biologically active delivery system rather than a stem cell-based therapy, exerting regenerative effects through a complex composition of growth factors, cytokines, and vesicles [37–39].The regenerative potential of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is fundamentally rooted in its bioactive composition; however, the methodologies employed in its preparation and quality control are equally pivotal in influencing its therapeutic efficacy. This complex composition underscores PRP as a dynamic regenerative system, as opposed to a mere single-agent therapy.

Preparation methods and quality control

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is generally prepared through the centrifugation of autologous blood, employing either single-spin or double-spin protocols with fixed-angle or horizontal rotors. The parameters of centrifugation—such as rotor type, speed, and duration—exert a direct impact on platelet yield and the concentrations of essential bioactive factors, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [40]. Among the various techniques available, optimized fixed-angle protocols have been shown to achieve superior platelet recovery and enhanced growth factor content. Nonetheless, there is considerable variability among commercial devices and laboratory methodologies, leading to inconsistent concentrations of platelets and leukocytes. Although higher platelet counts are generally associated with increased growth factor release, the morphology and functionality of platelets are also critical determinants of biological efficacy [41–43].Furthermore, activation strategies, including the use of calcium chloride or thrombin, can significantly affect the structural properties of fibrin networks and the kinetics of growth factor release [44]. These factors add complexity to the efforts aimed at standardization. To mitigate this heterogeneity, innovative quality control (QC) systems have been proposed. For instance, artificial intelligence-based computer vision and scoring systems, such as the William-Eqram Scoring System for PRP Quality Reporting (WESS-PQR), provide advanced tools for consistent assessment [45, 46]. In conclusion, the variability associated with preparation methods remains a substantial obstacle to clinical translation. The development of standardized protocols and robust quality control measures is therefore crucial, especially in the field of reproductive medicine, where precision in treatment is of utmost importance. Consequently, the implementation of standardized protocols and effective QC systems is imperative to ensure clinical consistency, particularly in reproductive applications.

Applications of PRP in regenerative medicine

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has been extensively utilized across various medical disciplines. In the field of orthopedics, PRP contributes to the enhancement of tendon healing, the management of osteoarthritis, and the promotion of bone regeneration [47, 48]. Its efficacy is further amplified when used in conjunction with biomaterial scaffolds, which facilitate sustained release of growth factors [49]. In dermatology, PRP has demonstrated positive outcomes in the treatment of androgenetic alopecia and vitiligo [50]. Within dentistry, PRP supports the regeneration of bone and soft tissue, particularly in implantology and periodontal therapy [51, 52]. The application of PRP in veterinary medicine further highlights its adaptability, although variability in preparation remains a notable challenge [53]. Mechanistically, PRP functions as a reservoir of growth factors, stimulating endogenous repair pathways rather than directly supplying stem cells [54]. Regulatory bodies in Europe and Japan have developed manufacturing standards to ensure the safety and consistency of PRP products [55, 56]. Recent investigations into PRP-derived extracellular vesicles (PRP-EVs) indicate their potential as cell-free alternatives, offering enhanced stability and targeted therapeutic effects [57].In summary, PRP is an established regenerative therapy in several disciplines. However, methodological heterogeneity and lack of standardization limit the comparability of outcomes, reinforcing the need for harmonized protocols before broad application in gynecology and reproductive medicine [45, 58].These multidisciplinary successes underscore PRP’s potential as a regenerative therapy, warranting deeper exploration in reproductive contexts.

Mechanisms of PRP Action in Ovarian Tissue

Promotion of angiogenesis

Adequate vascularization is critical for follicular survival, oocyte competence, and the maintenance of an optimal ovarian microenvironment. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) contains pro-angiogenic growth factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), fibroblast growth factors (FGFs), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which promote endothelial proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix remodeling [59]. In animal models of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), intraovarian administration of PRP has been shown to enhance the expression of angiogenesis-related genes, such as angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2) and kinase insert domain receptor (KDR), reduce follicular atresia, and improve follicular survival [60]. Combination therapies, such as PRP with peripheral blood mononuclear cells, have further augmented angiogenesis and glycolytic activity, thereby restoring estrous cycles and fertility in aged rats [61]. Enhanced microvascularization also mitigates hypoxia and oxidative damage, potentially improving embryo quality in assisted reproductive technologies (ART) [62]. Clinically, intraovarian PRP has been associated with increases in anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels and antral follicle count (AFC), consistent with angiogenesis-mediated functional recovery [63].

Follicle activation and anti-apoptotic effects

Building upon the angiogenic effects described above, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) further exerts anti-apoptotic and follicle-activating actions within the ovarian cortex. Key growth factors such as PDGF, IGF-1, and TGF-β promote granulosa cell proliferation, inhibit caspase-mediated apoptosis, and may awaken dormant primordial follicles through activation of PI3K/Akt and mTOR signaling cascades [60–64].

Experimental evidence suggests that PRP can restore mitochondrial function and reduce oxidative stress, thereby enhancing oocyte competence and follicular viability. These effects collectively contribute to the partial reactivation of ovarian endocrine activity and improved folliculogenesis in both animal and clinical settings.

Immunomodulation and inflammatory regulation

The ovarian immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in folliculogenesis and the maintenance of homeostasis. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) influences this niche by augmenting anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, while simultaneously mitigating oxidative and inflammatory damage. Preclinical studies have shown that PRP administration results in a reduction of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels and vascular congestion, alongside enhanced ovulation and fertility outcomes [60, 65]. Additionally, PRP may alleviate autoimmune-related primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) by reprogramming cytokine profiles and modulating toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated pathways, such as TLR2, TLR6, and TLR7, which are associated with granulosa cell apoptosis [66]. Moreover, growth factors derived from PRP facilitate the polarization of macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby restoring immune homeostasis and providing protection against ovarian aging [67, 68].

Summary of mechanistic insights

Collectively, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) exerts multifaceted effects on ovarian tissue by promoting angiogenesis, thereby enhancing follicular oxygenation and nutrient supply; activating dormant follicles and preventing apoptosis through the PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and mTOR pathways; and modulating immune and inflammatory responses to restore a favorable ovarian microenvironment. These mechanisms provide a robust biological rationale for the use of intraovarian PRP therapy in the treatment of early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI). However, the heterogeneity in preparation protocols presents a significant challenge, highlighting the necessity for standardization and translational studies to validate these effects in clinical practice. Collectively, these mechanisms create a microenvironment conducive to follicular survival and hormonal restoration.

The effect of PRP ovarian injection on ovarian function

Hormonal changes

Intraovarian platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections have been consistently linked to beneficial hormonal modulation. Clinical studies have documented reductions in serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), coupled with increases in anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and estradiol levels, indicative of a partial restoration of ovarian reserve [69–71]. Animal models of chemotherapy-induced ovarian damage support these findings, showing preserved granulosa cell function and enhanced endocrine balance [72, 73]. These results imply that PRP may aid in reestablishing a more physiological hormonal environment in women experiencing ovarian insufficiency.

Follicle number and ovarian morphology

Ultrasound evaluations suggest that platelet-rich plasma (PRP) can enhance antral follicle count (AFC) and ovarian volume, especially in individuals with mild to moderate ovarian impairment [74]. Nonetheless, outcomes in women with severely diminished ovarian reserve are variable, with certain studies indicating limited efficacy [75, 76]. Preclinical investigations also propose that PRP may enhance follicle quality and developmental competence more significantly than follicle quantity itself, thereby supporting its role in qualitative rather than purely quantitative restoration [77].

Menstrual recovery and reproductive outcomes

Clinical observations indicate that PRP therapy facilitates the resumption of menstrual cycles and improves fertility outcomes in women with early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) [78, 79]. The restoration of menstruation has been associated with enhancements in ovarian reserve markers, while assisted reproductive technology (ART) studies have demonstrated increased oocyte yield, fertilization rates, and embryo quality following PRP administration [72]. Experimental models corroborate these findings, showing that PRP, whether used alone or in conjunction with stem cell factors, enhances oocyte activation and pregnancy rates [80]. Overall, PRP appears to improve ovarian function through hormonal normalization, morphological recovery, and the enhancement of reproductive outcomes, although its efficacy varies according to patient characteristics. The primary advantage of PRP may lie in improving follicular quality and the microenvironmental conditions rather than significantly increasing follicle numbers. These findings suggest that PRP’s main benefit may be in enhancing follicular quality, particularly in cases of early-stage dysfunction.

Clinical research progress of PRP ovarian injection in EORD and POI

Evidence from clinical studies

Clinical research on platelet-rich plasma (PRP) for early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) encompasses randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, and meta-analyses. The results are heterogeneous: some RCTs have reported modest improvements in oocyte numbers, whereas most have demonstrated limited effects on embryo quality or pregnancy rates [76, 81]. Observational studies and systematic reviews suggest enhancements in anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels, antral follicle count (AFC), oocyte yield, and pregnancy outcomes in selected patient populations [69, 81–84]. Safety data across studies are reassuring, with no severe adverse effects reported [80, 85]. These findings suggest potential clinical benefits but also underscore variability arising from differences in study design, PRP protocols, and patient selection.

Patient selection and treatment optimization

The efficacy of PRP treatment appears to be significantly influenced by the initial ovarian status of the patient. Younger women and those with remaining follicular reserves generally exhibit more pronounced improvements in antral follicle count (AFC) and oocyte retrieval rates [86–88]. Conversely, patients with extremely diminished ovarian reserve or prolonged primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) often experience limited benefits. Additionally, there is considerable uncertainty regarding the optimal treatment protocols, including the number of injections, dosing intervals, and the potential advantages of combination strategies, such as PRP with stem cells [70, 89, 90]. Therefore, standardizing patient selection criteria and treatment parameters is essential to enhance the reproducibility of results. Future protocols should focus on refining patient inclusion criteria and optimizing dosage schedules.

Safety and adverse reactions

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is widely regarded as a safe treatment modality, with predominantly mild and transient adverse events, such as local discomfort, being reported [80, 85]. Due to its autologous nature, the risk of immune rejection or infection is minimized; however, the implementation of strict aseptic techniques is imperative. Despite this, long-term safety data remain limited, and the potential for placebo or procedural effects cannot be completely ruled out [81]. Therefore, large-scale prospective studies with extended follow-up periods are required to comprehensively establish the risk profile of PRP.

Summary

In summary, clinical research indicates that intraovarian platelet-rich plasma (PRP) may enhance ovarian reserve markers and reproductive outcomes in certain patient populations, while demonstrating a favorable short-term safety profile. Nonetheless, the conclusions are limited by small sample sizes, variability in preparation protocols, and the absence of standardized regimens. To establish efficacy, optimize protocols, and ensure reproducibility, future multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are necessary.Despite promising hormonal and follicular improvements, meta-analyses indicate that live birth rates remain inconclusive, with reported improvements ranging from 10% to 25% compared with baseline or control groups [83, 90]. These findings emphasize the need for adequately powered RCTs with standardized endpoints.

Molecular mechanisms and basic research progress of PRP ovarian injection

Insights from animal models

Preclinical studies present robust evidence indicating that platelet-rich plasma (PRP) exhibits antioxidative, antiapoptotic, and pro-angiogenic properties in ovarian tissue. In murine and rat models of chemically or radiation-induced primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), intraovarian administration of PRP has been shown to reduce follicular atresia, enhance angiogenesis, restore hormonal equilibrium, and improve oocyte quality, thereby facilitating embryo development and blastocyst formation [28, 60, 72, 77]. These findings highlight PRP’s potential to restore the ovarian microenvironment and maintain follicular viability. Beyond the isolated effects observed in animal models, the interaction between PRP and stem cell-mediated pathways provides a more comprehensive understanding of the cellular mechanisms involved in ovarian regeneration.

Interaction with stem cells and ovarian microenvironment

Recent studies have demonstrated synergistic effects between PRP and stem-cell-derived factors, further promoting angiogenesis and follicular restoration [91].Stem cells, particularly mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), are recognized for their role in facilitating ovarian repair predominantly through paracrine signaling mechanisms rather than direct cellular differentiation. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) appears to augment these reparative effects by supplying a concentrated source of growth factors and extracellular vesicles, which can influence angiogenesis, immune regulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling [92–95]. Recent studies have notably identified MSC-derived exosomes as pivotal mediators of ovarian regeneration. These exosomes transport a variety of bioactive molecules, including microRNAs (miRNAs) such as miR-29a and miR-205-5p, which have been demonstrated to enhance granulosa cell survival and proliferation. Mechanistically, these miRNAs activate signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, which are crucial for folliculogenesis and the maintenance of ovarian homeostasis [95–97].Moreover, the interaction between platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and stem cells appears to be synergistic. PRP may function as a supportive scaffold or biochemical enhancer, augmenting the paracrine effects derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), thereby leading to improved tissue repair outcomes. Experimental models that integrate PRP with MSCs have shown enhanced follicular development and restoration of endocrine function. Collectively, these findings advocate for an integrated regenerative approach that harnesses both the biochemical richness of PRP and the reparative capabilities of stem cells, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for women experiencing ovarian insufficiency. Building upon these cellular interactions, emerging molecular biomarkers are elucidating the underlying mechanisms of action and offering potential tools for therapeutic monitoring. This synergy underscores the potential for developing combined regenerative strategies.

Emerging molecular biomarkers

Recent studies highlight the significance of microRNAs, exosomes, and proteomic/metabolomic signatures as both mechanistic mediators and potential predictive biomarkers for the response to platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy. Specifically, decreased expression of miR-144-3p has been associated with granulosa cell apoptosis and ovarian insufficiency [98–101]. Exosomal profiling in patients with primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) has revealed alterations in pathways associated with oxidative stress and energy metabolism [102].Advances in biosensing technologies may facilitate real-time monitoring of ovarian responses to PRP therapy, thereby supporting the personalization of treatment [102–109]. These biomarkers offer promising potential for patient stratification and therapeutic monitoring, which could guide individualized interventions.

Summary

Fundamental research underpins the regenerative function of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in the ovary through a variety of mechanisms: mitigating oxidative stress and apoptosis, promoting angiogenesis, modulating immune responses, and remodeling the ovarian microenvironment. Interactions with stem cells and the identification of novel biomarkers further enhance the understanding of PRP’s mechanisms of action and may inform patient selection. Future research should incorporate multi-omics approaches and translational studies to elucidate these mechanisms and validate predictive biomarkers, with the ultimate goal of improving clinical outcomes for women with early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI).

Advantages and limitations of PRP treatment for EORD and POI

Advantages compared with conventional therapies

In contrast to hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and assisted reproductive technology (ART), which predominantly offer symptomatic relief or circumvent ovarian dysfunction, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) presents a regenerative strategy. PRP specifically targets the ovarian microenvironment, potentially enhancing hormonal markers, folliculogenesis, and ART outcomes. Furthermore, it may exhibit synergistic effects when used in conjunction with stem cell-based interventions [72, 78, 80]. The autologous origin of PRP reduces the risk of immunogenicity and infection, thereby rendering it a promising candidate for clinical application.

Limitations of current evidence

Despite promising outcomes, the existing evidence is limited by several factors: small sample sizes and short follow-up durations, which restrict statistical power; variability in platelet-rich plasma (PRP) preparation and administration, complicating inter-study comparisons; a predominance of preclinical or observational data, with a scarcity of well-designed randomized controlled trials (RCTs); and an unclear impact on live birth rates, the most clinically relevant endpoint [69, 76, 85, 108–110]. Although safety data are reassuring, the long-term risks have not been thoroughly investigated [81, 103]. These challenges underscore the necessity for rigorous trial design and extended follow-up periods.

Practical and ethical considerations

Numerous practical challenges impede the clinical translation of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapies. These include the absence of standardization in platelet concentration, injection volume, and dosing frequency [80, 86, 104–111]; issues related to informed consent and patient expectations, particularly given the lack of definitive evidence regarding efficacy [85]; and regulatory deficiencies, as there are currently no established professional society guidelines for the use of PRP in reproductive medicine [105–113]. These obstacles highlight the necessity for harmonized technical protocols and robust ethical oversight prior to the routine implementation of PRP therapies.

Summary

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) emerges as a promising regenerative therapy for early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), potentially providing advantages over traditional management approaches. Nonetheless, its clinical application is constrained by methodological variability, a lack of comprehensive long-term safety data, and ethical considerations. To ascertain whether PRP can transition from an experimental treatment to a validated evidence-based therapy in reproductive medicine, it is imperative to conduct rigorous multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs), establish standardized preparation protocols, and develop clear professional guidelines.

Conclusion

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) ovarian injection has gained recognition as a promising regenerative approach for women experiencing early ovarian reserve decline (EORD) and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). Through the delivery of a concentrated array of bioactive factors, PRP exerts a range of effects, including the promotion of angiogenesis, activation of dormant follicles, reduction of apoptosis, and modulation of local immune responses. Preclinical studies and preliminary clinical observations indicate enhancements in ovarian reserve markers, menstrual recovery, and reproductive outcomes, suggesting that PRP may serve as a potential adjunctive therapy in the field of reproductive medicine.

Nonetheless, the existing body of evidence is still in its nascent stages and exhibits considerable heterogeneity. The majority of studies conducted to date are constrained by limited sample sizes, brief follow-up durations, and a lack of standardized protocols, which collectively impede the ability to derive definitive conclusions regarding efficacy, especially in relation to live birth rates. Furthermore, the long-term safety profile necessitates additional validation.

Future advancements in this field will depend on the implementation of standardized preparation and administration protocols, multicenter randomized controlled trials, and long-term safety evaluations. Integrating biomarker discovery and multi-omics analyses may enable personalized therapeutic strategies, while ongoing mechanistic studies will clarify how PRP modulates the ovarian microenvironment. Concurrently, professional societies should establish ethical and regulatory frameworks to guide the responsible clinical translation of PRP therapy in reproductive medicine.
