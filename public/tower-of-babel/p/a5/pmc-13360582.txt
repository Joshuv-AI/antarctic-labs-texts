Clinical Outcomes of Decompression Versus Decompression With Instrumented Fusion for Lumbar Spinal Stenosis Secondary to Degenerative Spondylolisthesis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Lama N. Alqahtani, Ahmed K. Alzahrani, Razan A. Almutairi, Abdulaziz M. Salah, Shahad F. Khan, Mohammad G. Sindi, Khalid E. Fassi, Abdulrahman M. Alshami, Ibrahim A. Hakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To compare decompression alone with decompression with fusion in patients with degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis.Methods:PubMed, Google Scholar, Ovid, and Web of Science databases were searched up to March 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The primary outcomes were the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores. The secondary outcomes included operative time, length of hospital stay, intraoperative blood loss, reoperation, complications, and cost. Pooled analysis was performed using a random-effects model in Review Manager following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020. The quality of included randomized trials was evaluated using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool.Results:Of the 4,507 studies, 5 RCTs (n = 898) were included. Both interventions improved ODI and VAS scores, with no significant differences for ODI (mean difference [MD], –3.21; 95% confidence interval [CI], –6.59 to 0.17, p = 0.06) or VAS leg pain (MD, –1.67; 95% CI, –8.85 to 5.51, p = 0.65). Decompression showed greater improvement in VAS back pain (MD, –7.48; 95% CI, –14.23 to –0.73, p = 0.03). Perioperative outcomes favored decompression, with a shorter operative time (95 min), shorter hospital stay (1.7 days), and lower blood loss (322 mL). Complications were more frequent with fusion, whereas reoperation rates did not differ. One study reported lower decompression costs (5,400 versus 12,200 USD).Conclusions:Both interventions significantly improved pain and disability. Decompression alone was associated with a lower perioperative morbidity and some evidence of greater back pain relief, supporting its role as a reasonable initial surgical option, with fusion reserved for patients requiring stabilization.

Introduction

Degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) is characterized by the anterior or posterior displacement of one vertebral body relative to the adjacent caudal vertebra [1,2]. It is a common etiology of back pain across all age groups, with higher prevalence in females and predominant involvement of L4–L5 segments [3]. According to Global Burden of Disease 2021, this condition ranks among the leading contributors to years lived with disability [4]. The pathophysiology of DS is initiated by degenerative spinal changes beginning with spinal canal narrowing and resulting in secondary spinal stenosis. Symptoms manifest according to the severity of stenosis and include back pain, leg pain, claudication, and paresthesia [1,2]. The initial treatment for low degrees of stenosis is typically conservative and involves pain-relieving medications and exercise. As the disease progresses and the degree of stenosis increases, surgical intervention becomes the most effective option for symptomatic relief [5]. The cornerstone treatment for spinal stenosis secondary to DS is lumbar decompression surgery, which relieves the entrapped spinal cord and provides symptomatic relief. This procedure is performed either as decompression alone or as decompression with fusion of the spine [6]. Although both methods are accepted treatments for DS, debate remains as to whether decompression alone is superior to decompression with fusion [7,8,9]. The relative benefits and drawbacks of these methods are assessed by evaluating the occurrence of medical and surgical complications, patient satisfaction with symptom relief, and long-term clinical outcomes. Several studies have found decompression alone to be superior, with lower rates of surgical and medical complications, lower rates of spinal stability, and higher rates of reoperation [10,11,12,13,14]. However, one study claimed that decompression with fusion was superior on some measures [12]. Conversely, other studies have reported no difference between the two techniques [7,8,9]. Given this conflicting evidence, our objective in this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare the clinical outcomes of decompression alone and decompression with fusion in the management of spinal stenosis secondary to DS.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020 guidelines [15]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews. A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Google Scholar, Ovid, and Web of Science from database inception to March 2025 was performed to identify studies that compared clinical outcomes between decompression alone and decompression combined with instrumented fusion in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis secondary to DS. Gray literature, clinical trial registries, and preprint platforms were excluded from the search strategy. All search terms were selected to ensure broad capture of relevant studies. The full PubMed string was: (“lumbar spinal stenosis” or “lumbar stenosis”) and (“degenerative spondylolisthesis”) and (“decompression” or “laminectomy”) and (“fusion” or “spinal fusion” or “instrumented fusion”). Equivalent adaptations were applied to Ovid, the Web of Science, and Google Scholar.

Inclusion criteria were defined according to population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes. Eligible studies included adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with lumbar spinal stenosis associated with low-grade DS. The intervention of interest was posterior decompression alone, and the comparator was posterior decompression surgery followed by instrumented fusion. Studies were required to include at least one of the following outcomes: visual analog scale (VAS) scores for leg and back pain, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores, walking capacity, radiological outcomes, length of hospital stay, intraoperative blood loss, surgical complications, or reoperation rates. Additional patient-reported outcomes, including EuroQol 5 dimension (EQ-5D), physical component summary (PCS), and numeric rating scale, were extracted when available; however, these were considered exploratory and were excluded in the primary meta-analysis.

The exclusion criteria encompassed studies involving non-degenerative or traumatic spondylolisthesis, those evaluating anterior interventions only, and those assessing non-instrumented fusion techniques. Studies not reporting functional or surgical outcomes were also excluded. Case reports, case series, retrospective or observational studies without control groups, conference abstracts, editorials, and non–peer-reviewed publications were not eligible for quantitative synthesis but could be narratively described if exploratory outcomes (such as radiological progression or cost analyses) were reported.

All identified studies were compiled into a shared excel spreadsheet. Titles and abstracts were independently evaluated by 2 reviewers for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion criteria. A full-text review of the included studies was conducted by 2 reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through a discussion between the 2 reviewers or, if needed, by a third reviewer. The study selection process was illustrated in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analysis flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data were independently extracted by 2 reviewers using a standardized excel spreadsheet. Extracted study characteristics included study identifier (first author's last and year of publication), article title, journal name, study design, year of publication, country of origin, total sample size, number of patients in each group, and outcomes measured including complications and quality of life. Patient-related variables included age range, mean age with standard deviation, and follow-up duration (months). Intervention-related data comprised decompression only, decompression + type of fusion, and treatment duration. Primary outcomes included the ODI, VAS for leg and back pain. Secondary outcomes included reoperation rates and radiological findings.

Exploratory perioperative outcomes encompassed length of hospital stay, intraoperative blood loss, operative time, and complications. When numerical outcome data (means and standard deviations) were not available in tabular form but were presented graphically, we extracted values using WebPlot Digitizer (version [5.2], Automeris LLC). This method has been validated for accurate data extraction from figures in systematic reviews and meta-analysis.

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study using an appropriate tool according to study design. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we applied the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2), which evaluates 5 domains, including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result [16]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. The overall risk of bias for each study was classified as low risk, some concern, or high risk based on the assessment of these domains.

The meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 7.2.0. Continuous outcomes (ODI, VAS back, and VAS leg) are presented as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and dichotomous outcomes (reoperation, complications) are expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. We used a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird, restricted maximum likelihood) and applied Hartung–Knapp adjustment in the sensitivity analysis.

Heterogeneity was measured using the I2 statistic. Thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicated low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. If I2 exceeded 50%, we explored potential sources of heterogeneity by excluding high-risk studies or conducting subgroup analysis (by fusion type or follow-up duration) when at least 3 studies were available. Outcomes with an I2 greater than 75% were considered highly heterogeneous.

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger's test when at least 10 studies were available; however, this analysis was not performed because only 5 studies were included. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Certainty of evidence was not formally assessed. This review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and the risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 [15,16].

Results

Our systematic search yielded 4507 publications (1266 from PubMed, 200 from Google Scholar, 1383 from Ovid, and 1658 from Web of Science). After removing 2339 duplicates, 34 full-text publications were identified. After applying the exclusion criteria, 5 articles published between 2016 and 2024 were included in the review (Fig. 1).

All 5 studies included in the analysis were RCTs. A total of 898 patients were enrolled in this study. The sample sizes ranged from 66 to 267 patients per study. The follow-up durations ranged from 24 to 60 months. The patient population largely comprised adults aged 50–80 years, with 1 study including broader age ranges from 18 to 80 years. The studies originated from 4 countries, with the United States, Norway, and Japan contributing one each and Sweden contributing 2 (Table 1).

The treatment arms primarily compared decompression alone (group 1) with decompression combined with fusion (group 2), the latter encompassing various techniques such as posterolateral fusion (PLF), posterolateral instrumented fusion (PSF), transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), and interspinous process devices (such as Coflex, ILS, and IFD).

Patient-reported outcomes were assessed using the ODI and VAS for back and leg pain. Across the studies, baseline pain scores were significantly reduced following surgical intervention. Posterolateral fusion, posterior lumbar interbody fusion, instrumented fusion, and decompression procedures were consistently associated with improved pain relief and function. Improvements were also observed in the EQ-5D and PCS scores, indicating enhanced health-related quality of life and decreased pain-related disability.

The clinical outcomes varied across procedures. Four studies reported the intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, and operative time. The meta-analysis demonstrated significantly lower blood loss with decompression alone compared with decompression combined with fusion (n = 520; MD = –322.5 mL, 95% CI: –396.7 to –248.2, p < 0.001, I2 = 65%, moderate heterogeneity). Furthermore, the length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in those with decompression alone compared with those with decompression combined with fusion (n = 617; MD = –1.70 days, 95% CI: –1.75 to –1.65, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%, low heterogeneity).

This result was largely driven by 1 large study, which contributed to nearly all the statistical weight. Mean operative time was significantly shorter in patients undergoing decompression alone compared with those undergoing decompression with fusion in studies reporting this outcome (n = 519; MD = –95.22 minutes, 95% CI: –142.6 to –47.9, p < 0.001, I2 = 98%, extremely high heterogeneity, pooled analysis may be unreliable). Overall, the perioperative outcomes favored decompression alone, with lower blood loss, shorter operative times, and reduced length of hospital stay compared with decompression combined with fusion.

This meta-analysis found no significant difference in ODI scores between decompression alone and decompression with fusion (MD = –3.21, 95% CI: –6.59 to 0.17, p = 0.06). The forest plot showed overlapping CIs across the studies, indicating no clear superiority of either approach. Low heterogeneity (I2 = 23%) suggested consistent findings across studies (Fig. 2).

Similarly, VAS scores for leg pain yielded comparable outcomes between decompression alone and decompression with fusion. The pooled effect size (MD = –1.67, 95% CI: –8.85 to 5.51, p = 0.65) showed no significant difference between the groups. Importantly, the heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%), suggesting that the included studies were consistent in their findings. These results reinforce the notion that neither decompression alone nor decompression with fusion demonstrates clear superiority in improving patient-reported pain outcomes (Fig. 3).

The results of this analysis showed a significant trend favoring decompression alone over decompression with fusion for VAS back pain (MD = –7.48, 95% CI: –14.23 to –0.73, p = 0.03). Low heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%), indicating consistency across studies. (Fig. 4).

The complication rates varied between the decompression alone and decompression with fusion groups. Dural tears, reported in 3 studies, ranged from 0% to 11% in the decompression-alone group and from 6.5% to 13.3% in the decompression with fusion group. Wound infections occurred in 2.3% to 4% of patients undergoing decompression alone and in 0% to 10% of patients undergoing decompression with fusion. Pneumonia was reported in 2 studies, with rates ranging from 0% to 0.8% in the decompression-alone group, and 1.6% to 3.2% in the decompression with fusion group. Overall, complication rates tended to be higher in the decompression with fusion group.

Four studies demonstrated that the risk of reoperation was slightly higher in the decompression-alone group compared with the decompression with fusion group (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.70 to 1.45, p = 0.98). However, the difference was not statistically significant, and the CI crossed unity, indicating no clear superiority of either surgical approach. Heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%), suggesting consistent results across studies. Overall, these results imply that decompression alone and decompression with fusion carry comparable risks of reoperation.

Radiologically, patients typically presented with degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis associated with grade I spondylolisthesis, characterized by anterior vertebral slippage. Although the radiographic outcomes were mentioned, none of the included studies provided relevant data. Therefore, radiographic success could not be analyzed in this review.

Economic outcomes were reported in only one study, limiting conclusions regarding cost differences. Limited evidence suggests that decompression alone was more cost-effective than decompression with fusion. Reported mean costs were 12,200 USD for decompression with fusion and 5,400 USD for decompression alone, highlighting a substantial difference in direct expenses. Although not all studies provided detailed cost analysis, the available data consistently suggested that decompression alone offered a lower-cost alternative than decompression with fusion.

Five studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 tool for RCTs. Three studies were classified as having a low risk of bias, 2 as having a high risk, and no study was classified as having some concerns in the overall risk domain. The most common sources of bias were missing outcome data (2 studies), which may have influenced pain and disability outcomes, and outcome measurement (2 studies), where limitations in blinding and subjective outcome measures (VAS and ODI) increased the risk (Table 2).

Given these assessments, the overall quality of the evidence was moderate. The presence of 2 high-risk studies introduced uncertainty in the pooled estimates, particularly for ODI and VAS leg and back pain outcomes, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Discussion

This systematic review compared clinical outcomes between decompression alone and decompression with fusion in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis. Across studies, both procedures were associated with significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes, including the ODI and VAS for leg and back pain, indicating improved quality of life and reduced pain-related disability. Importantly, pooled analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences between decompression alone and decompression with fusion with respect to disability and leg pain. These findings suggest that both surgical approaches provide comparable clinical benefits, with no clear superiority. Decompression alone demonstrated a statistically significant but modest advantage in back pain improvement (p = 0.03), although the CI was close to the null effect.

However, significant differences were observed in complication profiles. Decompression with fusion was consistently associated with a higher incidence of perioperative complications, including dural tears, wound infections, and pneumonia. These results are consistent with the greater invasiveness, longer operative times, and increased postoperative morbidity typically associated with fusion procedures, highlighting the importance of tailoring surgical decisions to individual patient factors such as comorbidities, functional status, and overall surgical risk.

Radiologically, patients with degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis and grade I spondylolisthesis commonly present with anterior vertebral slippage. However, none of the studies included in this review reported radiographic outcome data, precluding evaluation of radiographic success or spinal stability. Economically, only 1 study reported cost outcomes, demonstrating that decompression alone was substantially more cost-effective than decompression with fusion, with average costs of 5,400 USD and 12,200 USD.

Overall, the evidence supports consideration of decompression alone as the standard surgical approach for most patients, with fusion reserved for selected cases presenting with mechanical instability, deformity, or predominantly axial back pain. Optimal outcomes rely on careful patient selection, radiographic assessment, and informed discussions of the relative risks, benefits, and costs of each procedure.

The results of this review have important clinical implications. Instrumented fusion has traditionally been favored because of its perceived advantages in spinal stabilization and prevention of further slippage. However, the present findings suggest that routine fusion may not reflect best practice for all patients with DS and lumbar spinal stenosis. For patients without radiographic evidence of instability, uncomplicated decompression seems to provide comparable long-term relief of pain, as well as improvements in function and reoperation rates. Given the higher surgical risks, longer operation durations, and increased perioperative complications associated with fusion, a more individualized approach to surgical decision-making is warranted. Therefore, fusion should be reserved for patients with clear instability or deformity.

This review includes only 5 studies, which limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, variability in patient selection and surgical indications, differences in operative techniques across centers and surgeons, and the inability to blind participants or investigators may introduce bias. Missing outcome data and inconsistent outcome measurements further represent methodological limitations.

Several studies have included mixed populations with and without spondylolisthesis, restricting subgroup analysis and reducing applicability to patients with greater spinal instability. Although some trials have reported follow-up durations of up to 5 years, this may still be insufficient to fully capture long-term complications and reoperation risks. Variations in the definitions and adjudication of outcomes, such as reoperation, further hinder direct comparability. Furthermore, access to 5 potentially relevant articles was not possible, which may have excluded evidence that could have influenced the findings. Collectively, these limitations make it difficult to draw universal conclusions and underscore the importance of individualized surgical decision-making based on patient-specific risk profiles and preferences.

To address these limitations and strengthen the evidence, future research should focus on high-quality multicenter RCTs with uniform surgical protocols, standardized procedures, defined outcome measures, and extended follow-up periods. Meta-analysis of individual patient data could facilitate more detailed subgroup analysis, helping to identify patients who derive the greatest benefit from fusion compared with decompression alone. Sensitivity analyses should also be performed to assess the impact of the study quality and potential biases. Expanding the inclusion criteria to include non- English publications and fostering international collaboration may help reduce language bias and strengthen the impact of the findings. Finally, regular updates of systematic will be essential as new information becomes available to ensure that clinical recommendations remain current, and evidence based.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis compared decompression alone and decompression combined with fusion for the treatment of DS. Both procedures achieved comparable outcomes in terms of leg pain and disability. Decompression alone was associated with greater back pain improvement, shorter operative times, and reduced length of hospital stay, in addition to lower blood loss and treatment costs in limited data. Radiological outcomes were not reported consistently across studies. Overall, the current evidence suggests that decompression alone may be a reasonable initial treatment approach given its lower perioperative morbidity and favorable clinical outcomes. Further studies are warranted to compare long-term functional outcomes of both techniques as this may inform refinement of current treatment protocols.
