Provider perspectives of clinical decision support for chronic kidney disease management: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Robert Goldberg, Henry Li, Cory E Goldstein, Kevin Yau, Shannon M Ruzycki, Maoliosa Donald, Meghan J Elliott, Brenda R Hemmelgarn, Matthew T James, Pietro Ravani, Neesh Pannu, Derek Chew, Dilaram Acharya, Tyrone G Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately one in ten adults globally. Multiple studies have identified underuse of effective therapies in eligible patients with CKD despite guideline endorsement. Clinical decision support (CDS) may increase confidence in prescribing recommended medications, but how end-users perceive it is unknown.ObjectivesTo evaluate clinician-reported perspectives and acceptability of CDS elements for ambulatory CKD management and inform the development of a CKD-specific CDS intervention.MethodsEligible participants for this electronic cross-sectional study were prescribing clinicians from Alberta, Canada, that practiced in ambulatory care settings, including physicians, nurse practitioners, and pharmacists. The survey presented examples of a proposed CDS’ content and format, and collected information on clinician demographics, CKD workflows, perceptions of CDS design, perceived acceptability and clinical utility. Knowledge, comfort, and prescribing patterns were examined for renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi), statins, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor agonists (nsMRA). Participant comfort prescribing each medication class was compared using mean paired differences before and after viewing the proposed CDS.ResultsBetween May and June 2025, 137 clinicians engaged with the survey, 131 completed demographics and 111 completed all survey elements. Most were nephrologists (n = 42; 32%) or general internal medicine specialists (n = 36; 27%). Overall, 88 (79%) supported CDS integration. RASi had the highest self-reported comfort with prescribing followed by statins, SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, and nsMRA. Clinicians reported higher comfort prescribing GLP-1RA and nsMRA with CDS support (median +1 on 9-point scale; p < 0.001), but no change for RASi, statins, and SGLT2i. Participants with low baseline comfort (≤6 on 9-point Likert scale) had statistically significant improvements in comfort with CDS for GLP-1RA and nsMRA (p < 0.05), but no change was observed for those with high baseline comfort.ConclusionsClinicians support CDS integration to enhance CKD care in ambulatory settings. The proposed CDS increased perceived prescribing confidence, particularly for GLP-1RA and nsMRA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03721-9.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Clinical decision support, Guideline-recommended care, Electronic health record

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), defined by either a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or kidney damage, affects over 10% of the global population, amounting to over 840 million individuals [1, 2]. As the population ages and the prevalence of CKD risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease continue to increase over time, this medically complex population is expected to continue to grow [3]. Multiple evidence-based pharmacotherapies exist that can slow CKD progression and reduce adverse outcomes [1].

Despite strong evidence from randomized clinical trials and endorsement by international guidelines, many established therapies, such as renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) and statins, remain underutilized in clinical practice [1, 4]. More recently available disease-modifying medications, such as the sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (nsMRA) have demonstrated benefit in reducing CKD progression [1]. Despite this, their uptake remains limited even with their recommendation in recent international clinical practice guidelines for CKD management [1, 4–6]. This underutilization may be due to a lack of physician knowledge of indications, monitoring requirements, workflow complexity, competing priorities, or their comfort with prescribing these agents, all of which have been identified as key barriers to implementation in multiple studies [7–9]. Interventions to address this evidence-to-practice gap should be guided by implementation science principles.

One viable option to address these barriers is the implementation of clinical decision support (CDS) tools designed to promote guideline recommendations and help facilitate adoption of these evidence-based interventions. CDS tools are designed to enhance clinical decision-making by providing clinical information, patient-specific assessments, or recommendations for clinicians [10, 11]. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the integration of CDS tools into electronic health records (EHRs) to support the implementation of guideline-recommended interventions. However, their effectiveness varies depending on their intensity and design [12–16]. Among the most commonly used forms of CDS are interruptive alerts that can prompt and promote prescribing at the point of care [11, 17]. Before implementing a tool for use in CKD ambulatory management, it is essential to understand the perspectives and preferences of clinicians to ensure that such an intervention is acceptable, feasible, aligned with their needs, and easily integrated into clinical workflows.

To inform the development and optimization of a CKD-specific CDS intervention, we conducted a cross-sectional survey of ambulatory clinicians across Alberta, Canada. Our primary objective was to assess clinician acceptability and support for integration of the proposed CDS. Secondary objectives evaluated prescribing comfort, workflow preferences, perceived productivity burden, and preferred CDS design elements.

Methods

This cross-sectional electronic survey study was administered and reported following recommendations from the consensus-based checklist for reporting survey studies (CROSS; Supplementary Table 1) and the checklist for reporting results of internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES; Supplementary Table 2) [18, 19]. The study protocol was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board.

Selection of participants and recruitment

We used purposive sampling to recruit clinicians from Alberta, Canada across provider types and specialty, genders, and geographic regions. Respondent characteristics were monitored during recruitment to support balanced representation among specialities, gender identities, and locations of practice. Recruitment efforts targeted various regions across Alberta, recognizing that clinician experiences and priorities may differ by geographic context [20, 21]. The majority of recruitment targeted Calgary and Edmonton, two most populous cities in Alberta, with a combined population of over 2.3 million per the 2021 Canadian Census [22].

Eligible participants were licensed clinicians practicing in Alberta ambulatory care settings that use the Epic Systems-based EHR called Connect Care, including physicians, nurse practitioners, and pharmacists. Participants were invited through direct email contact as well as through departmental leaders of targeted specialty groups at the University of Calgary, the University of Alberta, and ambulatory clinics within Alberta. Recruitment occurred between May and June 2025, and one reminder email sent if no response was received. Snowball recruitment of participants was encouraged. Because of this, the total number of clinicians who received the survey invitation could not be assessed and a complete response rate cannot be determined.

Sample size targets were informed by the need to capture variation in provider perspectives and geographic settings rather than statistical power. Recruitment concluded once each key specialty group was represented by at least five participants. The survey was completed through Qualtrics, and participants who completed the survey received a $25 electronic gift card. All participants provided electronic consent prior to participation. Internet protocol (IP) addresses were evaluated to identify multiple responses from the same participant [23]. Participant identifiers were not recorded to ensure anonymity and only select study team members had access to the responses.

Survey topics and format

We followed the Burns methodology for the design of self-administered surveys which emphasizes systematic item development, pretesting, and transparent reporting [24]. Survey items were initially generated through literature review and iterative discussion among the multidisciplinary study team to ensure relevance to CKD management and CDS implementation. Items were then pre-tested and refined through multiple consensus meetings by the authors, followed by pilot testing including study members who had not participated in item development to assess clarity and relevance. Authors involved in pre-testing represented a variety of demographics and disciplines, including clinicians in Alberta involved in CKD care, ensuring contextual alignment with the target survey population. The survey included items across three domains: participant demographics, current clinical CKD management practices, and perspectives on the proposed CDS (Supplementary Item 1).

Participant demographics included location, language, age, sex, gender identity, racial identity, clinical role, specialty, and years of practice. Items were developed using guidance from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) and Statistics Canada to ensure inclusive, standardized demographic measurement [25, 26]. All questions were mandatory, and propagation to subsequent survey items was dependent on answering preceding questions. Survey questions assessed clinicians’ current CKD management practices, including the number of patients with CKD that they manage, the clinical metrics they use to guide decision-making, and use of an existing CKD management resource at CKDpathway.ca which was developed in Alberta and is used as a provincial clinical pathway [27]. Additional items evaluated clinician confidence and comfort in prescribing goal directed medical therapies for CKD and discussing prognosis.

Participants reviewed an example of the proposed CDS tool and were asked about preferred content, format, and whether the CDS would increase confidence in prescribing each medication class (Figs. 1 and 2). Throughout the survey, the CDS was referred to as a Best Practice Advisory (BPA), a term commonly used in Epic Systems-based EHRs and familiar to clinicians working within Connect Care. The proposed CDS in its current form includes an alert to notify clinicians of their patients’ CKD and provides medication recommendations in a patient-specific order set based on their comorbidities, laboratory measures (eGFR, albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), and serum potassium), current list of medications, and contraindications. Confidence and acceptability items were measured using a 9-point Likert scale, where 1 represented the lowest possible response and 9 the highest.

Analysis

Participant demographics were summarized descriptively and stratified by primary provider specialty. For survey items, we reported the median and interquartile range (IQR) for Likert scale responses, and proportions for categorical questions, both overall and stratified by provider group. Each question was analyzed using the total number of participants that responded to the question (i.e., complete case analysis). Primary analyses included responses related to acceptability whereas subgroup, prescribing comfort, and design elements were considered as exploratory analyses. Mean paired differences (MPD) were reported descriptively to summarize the average magnitude and direction of change in prescribing comfort with and without CDS support from the 9-point Likert scale responses. Because Likert responses are ordinal and paired differences were not normally distributed, statistical significance was assessed using a Wilcoxon sign rank test (using p < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance). We further examined pre-post comfort stratified by initial comfort pre-CDS comfort, comparing within-person changes among those with baseline comfort ≥ 7 versus <7 using a Wilcoxon sign rank test. Given multiple related comparisons across medication classes, sensitivity analyses using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate adjustment were performed. All analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.5.0 [28, 29].

Results

A total of 137 participants consented to participate in this survey. Of these, 131 (96%) completed the demographics section and 111 (81%) completed the full survey (Supplementary Figure 1), reflecting a mid-survey attrition rate of 19%.

Participant characteristics

Among the 131 participants who completed the demographics portion (Table 1), 88 (67%) were based in Calgary, 39 (30%) in Edmonton, and 4 (3%) identified their location as rural or other (Table 1). English was reported as the primary language for 125 of 131 participants (95%). Most participants were physicians (n = 127; 96%), including attending physicians (n = 122, 92%), fellows (n = 4; 3%), and a resident (n = 1; 1%). There were also nurse practitioners (n = 2; 2%) and pharmacists (n = 2; 2%) included. The most common specialties were nephrology (n = 42; 32%), general internal medicine (n = 36; 27%), and other internal medicine subspecialties (n = 26; 20%). Most had been working in healthcare for over a decade; many reported 11-to-20 years of experience (n = 50; 38%), and several had more than 20 years (n = 36; 28%, Table 1). Mid-survey attrition (n = 20) differed across observed participant characteristics and was high among general internists (n = 14; 55%) and those with 11–20 years of experience (n = 10; 50%).

Current clinic and CKD processes

Of the 123 participants who completed this section, most reported dedicating more than half of their time to clinical practice (n = 84; 68%; Table 1). They reported participating in an average of 3.9 (Standard deviation (SD) 2.36) half-day clinics per week, with 3.4 (SD 2.32) of these typically managed using the Epic Systems-based Connect Care. Of 123 participants, many reported that their clinic employs or has access to a clinic-based pharmacist (n = 50; 41%), half reported no access (n = 62; 50%), and the remainder selected ‘Other’ (n = 11; 9%), clarifying that they had access to a clinic-based pharmacist in some, but not all, of their practice settings. Participants estimated managing a median of 10 (IQR 15) patients with CKD per week, of whom 81% were managed through Connect Care.

Participants reported visiting CKDpathway.ca a median of 1.0 (IQR 7.5) times annually. When making clinical decisions involving medication initiation with eGFR cutoffs, 45 (37%) participants reported using the most recent eGFR/ACR value, while 57 (46%) used average values over the past 3 months. Fewer participants relied on longer-term averages, with 9 (7%) using 12-month averages and 12 (10%) selecting ‘Other’ and noting context-specific or hybrid approaches.

Confidence in the ability to diagnose CKD was highest (Median 7, where 9 represents maximum confidence; IQR 2.5), followed by the ability to monitor CKD (Median 7, IQR 3) and ability to manage CKD (Median 6; IQR 3.5, Table 1). Many participants prescribed RASi and SGLT2i frequently, including 62 and 50% reporting at least one daily prescription (Table 2). Some participants reported never prescribing the medication classes included in the proposed CDS, however this varied significantly by medication class and physician specialty (Supplementary Table 3). When assessing familiarity with specific aspects of each CDS-targeted medication, participants reported the highest knowledge for RASi (Median 8; IQR 2); statins (Median 7; IQR 2) and SGLT2is (Median 7; IQR 3). Knowledge was rated lower for GLP-1RA (Median 7; IQR 3), and nsMRA (Median 5; IQR 5). Additionally, participants’ comfort with prescribing these interventions was highest for RASi, statins, and SGLT2i (Median 9; IQR 2 for all), followed by GLP-1RA (Median 7; IQR 4), and was lowest for nsMRA (Median 6; IQR 6, Table 2).

CDS elements and preferences

After providing an explanation of the proposed CDS (Supplementary Figures 2 and 3), 79% of participants indicated full support for using the automated order sets and referrals as described in the survey (Supplementary Table 4). This included 85% of nephrologists, 80% of general internists, 70% of internal medicine subspecialists, 80% of cardiologists, 100% of family physicians, 50% of surgical specialists, and 50% among those who selected other/prefer not to say.

Participants were then asked to rate their comfort prescribing five classes of medications after receiving patient-specific advice from the proposed CDS. For RASi, statins, and SGLT2i, no significant difference in comfort was observed (MPD = −0.06 ± 0.17, −0.05 ± 0.19, 0.04 ± 0.22; p = 0.58, 0.63, 0.70; Fig. 3, Table 3). However, mean comfort prescribing GLP-1RA increased from 6.45 to 7.02 (MPD = 0.53 ± 0.26; p = 0.00013; BH-adjusted p = 0.0003), and for nsMRA increased from 5.12 to 6.14 (MPD = 0.99 ± 0.32; p < 0.0001; BH-adjusted p < 0.0001). When stratified by high and low comfort without CDS support, significant increases were observed for SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, and nsMRA, in participants who initially ranked comfort as 6 or less (p = 0.036, 0.0007, <0.0001, Table 3); however after adjustment for multiple testing, the increase in perceived comfort only remained significant for GLP-1RA and nsMRA (Table 3). No significant difference was observed for RASi or statins upon stratification (p = 0.33, 0.42).

Participants most frequently wanted the CDS to be provided ‘when opening order entry’ (n = 46; 41.4%) and ‘when first opening the patient chart’ (n = 33; 29.7%) (Table 4). Free-text responses from participants reflected preferences for less disruptive implementation, including manual triggering or the ability to suppress alerts for specific patients. In terms of CDS elements to include, participants supported inclusion of ‘contraindications to interventions’ (n = 91; 82.0%) and ‘monitoring and follow-up recommendations’ (n = 75; 67.6%) (Table 4).

Participants most frequently indicated that interaction times of up to 30 seconds where acceptable (n = 88; 79.3%), while 56 (50.4%) of these respondents also considered longer interaction times acceptable (Table 4). For both items, the median response was 3, which corresponds with ‘Minimal loss of productivity’. There was a small difference between the mean scores for acceptable productivity loss (2.92) and anticipated productivity loss (3.69). Similar results were observed upon stratification by clinician specialty (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey of clinicians from a large Canadian province suggests general acceptability and informs the design of a CDS tool to assist with improving prescribing guideline recommended medications for patients living with CKD in ambulatory care settings. Most participants were supportive of CDS integration across clinical specialties, and there were perceived improvements in comfort prescribing CKD guideline-recommended therapies if included in the CDS. These findings are derived from a predominantly urban, physician-based sample with substantial representation from nephrology and general internal medicine. The insights provided may help guide clinician-informed design and optimization of CDS elements prior to implementation.

Our findings suggest that although there was comfort with prescribing RASi and statins, there was relatively less comfort in prescribing SGLT2i, and particularly GLP-1RA and nsMRA, which are more recently approved medication classes for treatment of CKD (Table 3). However, participants also reported the greatest increase in prescribing comfort for these classes following exposure to the proposed CDS, providing a potential opportunity to support their uptake, particularly among those with low baseline prescribing comfort. Some clinicians may lack sufficient knowledge regarding GLP-1RA and nsMRA, and thus may be less likely to prescribe these medications when indicated despite established clinical benefit [8]. In our study, 19.5% of participants reported never prescribing GLP-1RA and 44.2% never prescribed nsMRA. Outside of nephrology, these numbers increased to 27.7% and 60.8%, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). Despite the high co-occurrence of CKD, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and the ability to reduce morbidity and mortality with GLP-1RA or nsMRA, these medications appear to be largely under-prescribed [1, 9]. These findings suggest that a CDS may help address these therapeutic gaps where they exist, particularly for more recent medication classes such as GLP-1RA and nsMRA.

CDS tools are increasingly being used to help clinicians make informed decisions for their patients. Between 2006 and 2013, uptake in Canada grew from 20% to 62% of practitioners [30]. By providing individualized diagnostics, alerts, and interventional suggestions to improve individual outcomes, CDS tools are paving the way for precision medicine in electronic systems [11]. CDS tools may also improve coordination of care for patients through targeted notifications and automated referrals [11]. In this study, preferences for the timing of the CDS delivered as an alert varied substantially, with no single timepoint receiving majority support. The most endorsed option, triggering the alert upon opening order entry, was selected by only 41.4% of participants. This highlights the challenge of designing any single alert that integrates seamlessly into diverse clinical workflows, as individual clinician needs and desired usage varies. Several free-text comments emphasized the need for alerts to be minimally disruptive, customizable, and suppressible for irrelevant patient contexts. These findings align with broader concerns in the literature about alert fatigue and resistance to intrusive CDS interventions, reinforcing the importance of offering flexible alert logic and opt-out mechanisms where feasible [31]. Moreover, other investigators have similarly reported that identifying an optimal timepoint for CDS alert presentation can be challenging, with effectiveness influenced by clinical context, workflow integration, and user receptiveness [32].

Participants also prioritized the inclusion of actionable, clinically relevant information within the CDS. The most strongly endorsed features were contraindications to interventions, monitoring and follow-up recommendations, side effect profiles, and guidance on the timing of medication initiation when multiple therapies are considered. These selections suggest that clinicians are more likely to engage with the CDS if it informs rapid prescribing decisions rather than simply presenting prompts or risk scores. Additionally, participants expressed a desire for embedded links to the CKD Pathway (http://www.ckdpathway.ca), relevant clinical guidelines, and patient education materials, as these features may further increase the utility of the tool by providing key resources at the point of care. Given the number of items that received support for inclusion, the proposed CDS should use embedded links and interfaces to balance information depth with the need to minimize workflow interruption which is a commonly cited issue among other CDS tools designed to improve medication prescribing [33].

The survey responses indicated a possible discrepancy between anticipated and acceptable productivity loss due to CDS engagement. The median values for both measures corresponded to ‘Minimal loss of productivity’. However, the mean scores suggested that the expected burden slightly exceeds what participants reported as acceptable, suggesting that the perceived impact on productivity may be unacceptable. Although subtle, the observed difference highlights the potential risk to uptake and sustained engagement if the tool is too cumbersome to integrate within clinic flows. This may be particularly true in high-volume settings where cumulative seconds can scale substantially across the day. As such, the tool must ensure that clinician interaction time remains minimal. A 30-second maximum usage was considered tolerable by roughly 80% of participants, although it may not be feasible to deliver a meaningful alert with paired action in such a short period of time. Strategies to minimize interaction burden while preserving informational depth may therefore be important for implementation success. Additionally, a less disruptive CDS approach for RASi and statin-related alerts may be considered given the limited perceived benefit observed for these medication classes.

A key consideration in maintaining the pragmatism of our CDS intervention is ensuring that the alert logic aligns with how clinicians currently use eGFR and ACR to inform decision-making. In our sample, most participants based prescribing decisions on either the most recent (37%) or 3-month average (46%) eGFR/ACR values. Very few reported relying on 12-month trends or more complex longitudinal data. This preference highlights that decision-making around nephroprotective therapies tends to prioritize recent kidney function and albuminuria status over longer-term averages. Structuring the CDS to match existing decision-making norms therefore supports its integration into routine care, and aligns with commonly used tools such as the KD Predict and Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE), in addition to Kidney Disease | Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO)-based guidance, which also typically rely on more recent eGFR/ACR data in determining risk of kidney disease progression [1, 34].

Our study has several limitations. Despite targeted outreach across Alberta, rural participation was minimal, with only a small number of participants identifying a rural or non-urban practice setting. This likely reflects the lower overall number of rural physicians in the province who may be less connected with academic centres but nonetheless limits generalizability to these contexts [20]. Additionally, increased participation among primary care physicians would have strengthened our findings as they care for many of the patients this tool would provide recommendations for. Participation among non-physicians was also low. Nonetheless, because Connect Care implementation is predominantly within specialist and academic-affiliated settings, the sample likely reflects many of the clinicians that the CDS is intended to impact once implemented. Further, the generalizability of our findings to other jurisdictions without widespread EHR use or without similar comprehensive drug coverage, is unknown. However, as an estimated 93% of primary care is delivered using EHRs in Canada and other countries, our results are applicable to many settings [35]. It is possible that the length of the survey may have contributed to response fatigue, as evidenced by the gradual decline in completion across sections. Although 95.6% of initial participants completed the demographic portion, only 81.0% completed the CDS-related items. This missingness may not have been completely at random and could reflect mild response bias, particularly if later items preferentially retained more engaged clinicians. Because purposive and snowball recruitment via email was utilized, we are unable to determine a response rate for the survey. Finally, while the survey captured comfort and preferences, it did not assess actual prescribing behavior or CDS usage and this should be evaluated at a more appropriate stage once the CDS is tested.

Conclusion

In summary, the proposed CDS for outpatient CKD management in a province-wide EHR was generally well supported and was associated with greater self-reported confidence in prescribing of important CKD therapies. Our results suggest that future implementation success likely depends not only on the perceived clinical utility of the alert content, but also on the extent to which it respects user autonomy and accommodates different workflow styles. This work highlights the need to incorporate stakeholder input when designing any population-level CDS tool. Given the high global prevalence of CKD, and recent advancements in CKD care, studies evaluating the implementation of a CDS for CKD are warranted.
