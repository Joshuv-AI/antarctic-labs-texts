Prognostic prediction in primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma with serum squamous cell carcinoma antigen ≥ 10 ng/mL: development and validation of a nomogram model based on inflammatory biomarkers and clinical factors
Authors: Yiwei Zhao, Chutong Zhao, Yujie Liu, Yuan Wang, Yunyan Zhang, Sijia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer remains a significant health burden worldwide, particularly in patients with markedly elevated pretreatment serum squamous cell carcinoma antigen levels (≥ 10 ng/mL), who often have poor outcomes. Accurate prognostic tools for this high-risk population are limited.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study including 355 patients with primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma who received radiotherapy between 2020 and 2023. Clinical characteristics and inflammation-related hematological indices, including hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, were collected. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors for overall survival. A nomogram incorporating these variables, along with clinical stage and treatment modality, was developed and validated.ResultsHemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, clinical stage, and treatment modality were independent predictors of survival. The nomogram achieved a concordance index of 0.729 in the training cohort and 0.704 in the validation cohort. The area under the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves for overall survival at 1, 2, and 3 years were 0.76, 0.81, and 0.79 in the training cohort, and 0.80, 0.75, and 0.71 in the validation cohort. Decision curve analysis demonstrated a consistent net clinical benefit, and risk stratification based on total scores effectively distinguished high- and low-risk groups.ConclusionsThis study developed and validated a clinically useful nomogram integrating inflammation-related hematological indices and clinical parameters to predict survival in high-risk cervical cancer patients. The model demonstrates favorable predictive accuracy and may guide individualized treatment strategies, supporting its potential for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15824-7.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy of the female reproductive system. It poses a serious threat to women’s health worldwide [1]. Persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) is the main cause of the disease. In recent years, advances in cervical screening technologies and the widespread implementation of HPV vaccination have contributed to a gradual decline in both the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer globally [2, 3]. However, in certain developing countries and resource-limited regions, the number of new cases and deaths remains substantial and cannot be overlooked [4]. Of particular concern, a subset of patients present with advanced-stage disease at diagnosis or possess adverse prognostic features—such as a primary tumor diameter ≥ 4 cm or pelvic lymph node metastasis—which are associated with poor responses to standard treatments and a higher likelihood of treatment resistance and disease recurrence [5, 6]. Effectively controlling disease progression and reducing the risk of relapse and metastasis in this patient population remain major challenges in clinical practice.

In clinical practice, dynamic monitoring of serum squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag) has become a crucial indicator for evaluating treatment response and assessing recurrence risk in cervical cancer [7, 8]. Previous studies have reported that pretreatment SCC-Ag levels, particularly those exceeding the clinical threshold (≥ 10 ng/mL), often indicate increased tumor biological aggressiveness and a significantly higher likelihood of recurrence [9]. In patients with primary cervical cancer, pretreatment SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL has been confirmed as an independent risk factor affecting survival outcomes [10]. Therefore, these patients urgently require more precise and individualized prognostic tools to guide clinical decision-making. However, specific research focusing on patients with markedly elevated SCC-Ag remains limited, and existing clinical prediction models have yet to effectively integrate relevant prognostic factors. This limitation results in inadequate risk assessment and constrained individualized guidance in clinical practice. Hence, developing an accurate prognostic model tailored for patients with primary cervical cancer and high SCC-Ag levels holds significant clinical value and practical importance.

In recent years, the role of systemic inflammatory response in the development, progression, and prognosis of various malignancies has gained increasing attention [11]. Inflammation represents an essential part of the tumor microenvironment. It contributes to tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [12–14]. Based on this understanding, several indicators reflecting systemic inflammatory status—such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—have been reported in many studies. These indices show a close association with survival outcomes in different cancers, including cervical cancer. They also carry potential prognostic value [15–19].

Meanwhile, hematological parameters such as the hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio (HRR) have been linked to unfavorable prognosis and the advancement of solid tumors in multiple studies [20]. Red cell distribution width (RDW), reflecting the heterogeneity of erythrocyte volume, is often elevated in conditions indicating underlying inflammation or malnutrition [21]. Low hemoglobin levels suggest anemia, which may result in inadequate tissue oxygenation [22]. Considering that hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment is closely linked to radiotherapy resistance, anemia is regarded as an important factor influencing tumor treatment outcomes [23]. HRR, which reflects inflammation, nutritional status, and oxygenation, has demonstrated potential prognostic value across multiple cancer types [24, 25].

Although the aforementioned inflammatory markers and hematological indices have demonstrated some prognostic value, research gaps remain. Currently, there is a lack of multidimensional and individualized prognostic models that effectively integrate inflammatory markers, blood parameters, and key clinical factors specifically for patients with primary cervical cancer and pretreatment SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL. Existing models are mostly developed based on the overall cervical cancer population and do not provide subgroup-specific analyses. Consequently, their predictive performance and clinical applicability in this particular patient subset have yet to be validated.

Based on the above background, this study focuses on patients with primary cervical cancer who had pretreatment SCC-Ag levels ≥ 10 ng/mL. By systematically evaluating and selecting inflammatory markers, hematological parameters, and key clinical variables significantly associated with survival outcomes, we aim to build and validate a multivariable individualized prognostic model. The establishment of this model is expected to enable refined risk stratification for this specific patient population, thereby providing a robust theoretical foundation and clinical decision-support tool for tailoring personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Patient information and inclusion criteria

This study retrospectively enrolled 355 patients with primary cervical cancer. All patients received radiotherapy at our center between January 2020 and December 2023. Each patient had a pretreatment serum squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag) level of at least 10 ng/mL. All patients were pathologically confirmed as having cervical squamous cell carcinoma and received a radiotherapy regimen centered on external beam pelvic radiotherapy combined with intracavitary brachytherapy [26]. In addition, an independent external cohort was selected from patients treated at our center in 2018 with SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL. After excluding patients lost to follow-up, 51 individuals remained for validation. This external cohort was not used for model training and served solely to evaluate the predictive performance of the nomogram in an independent population.

External beam pelvic radiotherapy employed advanced precision techniques. These included volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), and helical tomotherapy (TOMO). The conventional pelvic irradiation dose was 45–50 Gy delivered in 25 fractions. An extra local boost of 10–15 Gy was applied for patients showing parametrial invasion or metastasis in pelvic or para-aortic lymph nodes on imaging or pathology. Intracavitary brachytherapy utilized iridium-192 isotope sources. Treatments were delivered 1–2 times per week. The common regimens were 6 Gy in 5 fractions or 7 Gy in 4 fractions. The selection of the specific radiotherapy technique was made after comprehensive evaluation by the treating physician and thorough discussion with the patient, taking into account tumor characteristics, patient preference, and institutional resources.

The definitive radiotherapy target volumes for cervical cancer include the primary cervical lesion, parametrial tissues, part of the vagina, and the lymphatic drainage regions. Prophylactic irradiation of lymphatic drainage areas encompasses the common, external, and internal iliac lymph nodes, together with the obturator, presacral, and lower abdominal lymph node regions. For patients with a high risk of lymph node metastasis—such as those with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or suspicious lymph nodes—the irradiation field should be extended to cover the common iliac lymph node region. In cases with metastases in the pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes, the radiation field is extended to include the pelvic extended field and para-aortic lymph nodes [27]. When the lower one-third of the vagina is involved, prophylactic irradiation of the inguinal lymphatic drainage region should be considered. The choice of specific treatment modality depends on the patient’s stage, comorbidities, and general physical status, and mainly includes three approaches: (1) Radiotherapy alone: Only local radiotherapy is administered without the combination of any chemotherapeutic agents; (2) Concurrent chemoradiotherapy: Radiotherapy is synchronously combined with chemotherapeutic agents during treatment. Cisplatin, recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines, is the preferred foundational chemotherapeutic agent. For patients who are elderly, have significant chemotherapy-related adverse reactions, or whose physical condition cannot tolerate cisplatin, carboplatin or nedaplatin are used as alternatives. The dosage of chemotherapeutic drugs follows the standardized regimens recommended by the guidelines and is adjusted based on individual patient conditions; (3) Concurrent chemoradiotherapy combined with additional systemic chemotherapy: Additional systemic chemotherapy is administered before or after concurrent chemoradiotherapy; The selection and dosage of all chemotherapeutic agents are in accordance with the NCCN Guidelines and are individually adjusted based on the patient’s specific conditions.

Patients entered a regular follow-up phase after completing treatment: follow-up visits were scheduled every three months during the first two years post-treatment, and every six months from the second to the fifth year. Follow-up assessments included gynecological examinations, imaging studies (such as MRI and CT), and hematological marker monitoring (including SCC-Ag) to comprehensively evaluate disease control. Recurrence was defined as follows: local recurrence referred to tumor reappearance at the primary sites (cervix, uterine corpus) or pelvic lymph nodes occurring six months or more after treatment completion; distant metastasis was defined as metastatic lesions appearing in sites such as bone, liver, lung, or supraclavicular lymph nodes.

Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) patients with primary cervical cancer; (2) histologically confirmed cervical squamous cell carcinoma; (3) SCC-Ag level ≥ 10 ng/mL; (4) complete treatment and follow-up data; (5) complete clinical and inflammatory marker data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) non-squamous histology (e.g., adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma); (2) SCC-Ag < 10 ng/mL; (3) missing survival or follow-up information; (4) absence of key clinical variables or laboratory indicators; (5) recurrence after prior cervical cancer-related treatments (such as surgery or chemotherapy); (6) concurrent diagnosis of other malignancies; and (7) pregnancy or lactation.

Data collection and variable definitions

This study systematically collected patients’ baseline clinical characteristics and peripheral blood inflammation-related markers, including the NLR, PLR, SII, HRR, serum albumin level, blood type (classified according to the ABO system), treatment modality, age at diagnosis, stage, lymph node metastasis status, and maximum tumor diameter.

NLR represents the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. It indicates the body’s systemic inflammatory response and immune condition [28]. PLR, calculated by dividing the platelet count by the lymphocyte count, reflects the inflammatory burden [29]. SII is a composite inflammation index computed as (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the patient’s inflammatory and immune balance [30]. HRR, derived by dividing hemoglobin by red cell distribution width, is commonly used to reflect nutritional status and potential anemia risk, and has prognostic significance [31].

Lymph node metastasis was assessed using imaging methods such as ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT). For cases with suspected involvement of superficial lymph nodes, pathological confirmation via biopsy was required. Tumor size was determined based on the maximum diameter measured on imaging. Clinical staging of all patients was uniformly conducted according to the 2018 revised FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) cervical cancer system.

Statistical analysis procedure

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). Survival outcomes were first evaluated with Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Differences between groups were tested using the log-rank method to determine statistical significance.

To better illustrate the impact of inflammation-related continuous variables on survival, the surv_cutpoint function was applied to determine optimal cutoff values for NLR, PLR, SII, HRR, and albumin. Based on these cutoffs, patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to visualize survival differences between groups. Hazard ratios (HRs) calculated from Cox regression in the training cohort were used solely to quantify the effect size of each variable between high- and low-risk groups, and served as input parameters for statistical power analyses in the validation cohort. In subsequent univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses, all variables were treated as continuous to retain maximal information and to enhance model robustness and interpretability.

To minimize the potential influence of extreme values on statistical analyses, distribution visualization and outlier treatment were performed on selected continuous variables before univariate Cox regression analysis. Specifically, boxplots of continuous variables—including NLR, PLR, SII, HRR, albumin, age, and tumor size—were generated to preliminarily evaluate their distribution characteristics and identify potential outliers (see Additional file 1). Based on these visualizations, PLR and SII exhibited marked skewness and extreme values and were therefore subjected to Winsorization. The Winsorization thresholds were defined using the interquartile range (IQR) method, where the lower limit was set to the 25th percentile (Q1) − 1.5 × IQR and the upper limit to the 75th percentile (Q3) + 1.5 × IQR, with IQR = Q3 − Q1. Values beyond these bounds were replaced with the corresponding boundary values. To maintain consistency between datasets, the Winsorization thresholds were calculated from the training cohort and subsequently applied to the validation cohort.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were carried out sequentially to identify clinical variables that might influence overall survival. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model to evaluate their independent prognostic significance. Variables retaining statistical significance in the multivariate model were then used to construct a nomogram for predicting patient survival probabilities.

To avoid potential multicollinearity among included variables, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was conducted to assess multicollinearity levels. All VIF values were maintained within acceptable limits, ensuring model stability.

The performance of the model was evaluated using several metrics. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated to measure the model’s discriminative ability. To reduce bias from overfitting, internal validation was conducted using the bootstrap method with 100 resamples. This approach helped to calibrate and stabilize the C-index estimation. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values were used to examine the model’s predictive accuracy at different time points. Higher AUC values indicated better discrimination. In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the model across various risk thresholds. These analyses further confirmed the model’s clinical usefulness [32].

Survival outcomes and subgroup analyses in patients with primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma with SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL

The survival profile of the study cohort was as follows: the 1-year overall survival (OS) rate was 96.9%, declining to 86.3% at 2 years and further to 81.1% at 3 years (Fig. 3A). To further explore prognostic heterogeneity across clinical subgroups, we performed stratified survival analyses. First, patients were grouped according to treatment modality (Fig. 3B). The results indicated that those receiving radiotherapy alone had poorer survival outcomes compared with those undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy. To minimize the confounding effect of disease stage, survival analyses stratified by treatment type were performed separately within FIGO stage II, stage III, and stage IV subgroups. In stage III patients, OS remained significantly inferior in the radiotherapy-alone group, consistent with the overall stratification results. In contrast, the Kaplan–Meier curves for stage II and stage IV patients showed no statistically significant differences. This result may be due to the limited sample size (see Additional file 2A-C).

Furthermore, subgroup analysis by clinical stage revealed a clear stage-dependent gradient in survival, with the most favorable outcomes observed in stage II disease and the poorest in stage IV, a difference that was statistically significant (Fig. 3C). This finding aligns with the established correlation between tumor stage and prognosis in cervical cancer.

Analysis of recurrence patterns in patients with primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma and baseline SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL

The overall recurrence rate in the study cohort was 13.52%, with local recurrence and distant metastasis each accounting for 50% of the recurrent cases. Local recurrences predominantly involved the cervix, uterine corpus, and pelvic lymph nodes, whereas distant metastases were most frequently observed in the liver, lungs, and supraclavicular lymph nodes. These findings indicate a pronounced spatial heterogeneity in recurrence patterns of cervical cancer. Further stratified analysis of recurrence patterns across clinical subgroups revealed a stage-dependent shift in recurrence risk toward distant metastasis. In stage IV disease, distant metastases occurred more frequently than in earlier stages. In contrast, local recurrence was more common in patients with stage II and stage III disease. Regarding treatment, patients who received radiotherapy alone had a higher recurrence rate than those treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy or combined modalities. Subgroup analysis showed that local recurrences were particularly more frequent in the radiotherapy-alone group. This finding suggests that radiotherapy alone may have limitations in controlling recurrence, especially for local disease.

Interestingly, we observed a possible link between ABO blood type and recurrence risk. As shown in Fig. 4B, patients with blood type O had a higher recurrence rate, while those with blood type A had a lower rate. These results indicate that blood type might influence tumor behavior. Among patients with blood type B, the proportion of distant metastases was comparatively higher, while those with blood types A and O were more prone to local recurrence, further supporting the notion that blood type may, to some extent, shape the metastatic pattern of relapse (Fig. 4C). Taken together, these findings indicate that the risk and pattern of tumor recurrence are influenced by multiple factors, including clinical stage, treatment modality, and ABO blood type. These factors should be carefully considered in clinical decision-making to enable more individualized follow-up schedules and targeted interventions for recurrence.

In the validation cohort, only albumin remained significantly associated with overall survival, consistent with the trend observed in the training cohort (Fig. 5F). Other markers showed similar directional tendencies but did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size (see Additional file 3A–3D).

To determine whether these nonsignificant results were due to insufficient statistical power, effect sizes (HRs) obtained from the training cohort were used to calculate the corresponding statistical power in the validation cohort. Power values were low for all indicators (PLR 24.5%, NLR 42.8%, SII 31.0%, HRR 26.5%), suggesting that the absence of significance may stem from inadequate power rather than a lack of true prognostic association.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that elevated NLR, PLR, and SII, along with reduced HRR and albumin, are associated with unfavorable survival outcomes in cervical cancer patients. Albumin showed consistent prognostic value across both cohorts, underscoring its potential as a reliable clinical biomarker.

The results demonstrated that albumin, HRR, NLR, SII, clinical stage, treatment modality, and tumor size were significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 6). Validation of the prognostic model for primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma with SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL

To comprehensively assess the predictive performance of the constructed nomogram, multiple validation approaches were employed. First, Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated to assess the model’s ability to discriminate between patients. The C-index was 0.729 in the training cohort and 0.704 in the validation cohort. These values indicated good accuracy in stratifying patients by prognostic risk.

Next, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the model’s predictive performance at different follow-up times. In the training cohort, the area under the curve (AUC) values at 1, 2, and 3 years were 0.76, 0.81, and 0.79, respectively (Fig. 9A). In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the model. The DCA results demonstrated that the model consistently yielded a substantial net benefit across all time points, further supporting its potential and significance for practical clinical application (Fig. 9C). Finally, patients in both the training and validation cohorts were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the total nomogram score. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed significant differences in survival between these two groups in both datasets. This result indicates that the model can reliably stratify risk and distinguish patients with different prognoses (Fig. 9D–E).

To evaluate the practical performance of the combined model in predicting patient prognosis, we calculated C-index for each individual clinical variable and for the integrated model in both the training and validation cohorts (Table 2). The analysis demonstrated that the predictive ability of all single-variable clinical models was inferior to that of the nomogram constructed by integrating multiple variables. Among the single-variable models, HRR, NLR, clinical stage, and treatment modality had C-index values ranging from 0.561 to 0.656 in the training cohort. In the validation cohort, their C-index values ranged from 0.499. In contrast, the integrated model reached C-index values of 0.729 in the training cohort and 0.704 in the validation cohorts. To comprehensively evaluate the generalizability of the nomogram, an independent cohort was selected from patients treated at our center in 2018 with primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma and SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL. After excluding patients lost to follow-up, a total of 51 cases were included. Model performance was assessed using 100 bootstrap resamples to provide a robust evaluation. The results showed that the average C-index in this external cohort was 0.624, indicating that the nomogram retained a certain degree of prognostic discrimination in an independent patient population.

Overall, the nomogram demonstrated consistent predictive performance across the training set, internal validation set, and external independent cohort. Discussion

Cervical cancer remains one of the most common malignant tumors and continues to pose a serious threat to women’s health, with a mortality burden that should not be ignored [4]. In particular, patients with primary cervical cancer presenting markedly elevated pretreatment squamous cell carcinoma antigen levels (≥ 10 ng/mL) often have an unfavorable prognosis [9, 10]. However, precise prognostic tools and individualized therapeutic strategies specifically tailored for this population are still lacking. Addressing this gap, the present study focused on patients with primary cervical cancer and pretreatment SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL, and developed as well as validated a multidimensional prognostic nomogram model by integrating hematological parameters, systemic inflammatory markers, and key clinical characteristics, with the aim of improving prognostic prediction accuracy for this high-risk group.

During the variable selection stage, we built univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. These models were used to identify independent prognostic factors related to survival in cervical cancer patients. The selected factors were then included in a nomogram model. The final variables in the model were HRR, NLR, FIGO stage, and the primary treatment modality received by each patient.

Low hemoglobin levels typically indicate poor nutritional status or ongoing blood loss, both of which have been previously demonstrated to be closely associated with decreased radiosensitivity and unfavorable prognosis [33, 34]. On one hand, anemia reduces oxygen delivery to tissues, creating a hypoxic tumor microenvironment. Oxygen enhances the DNA-damaging effects of ionizing radiation, whereas hypoxia attenuates the cytotoxic impact of radiotherapy on tumor cells. On the other hand, hypoxia can activate anti-apoptotic pathways and DNA repair mechanisms in tumor cells, thereby increasing resistance to radiotherapy, ultimately compromising therapeutic efficacy and contributing to poor clinical outcomes.

RDW, a parameter reflecting the heterogeneity of erythrocyte volume, has been shown in multiple studies to be associated with adverse outcomes in various diseases [35]. A low HRR may indicate a distinct hematologic profile that is significantly correlated with more aggressive tumor biology and more advanced disease stages [20, 24].

As an easily accessible marker of systemic inflammation, the NLR has been widely applied in prognostic assessment across various malignancies [15, 16, 36]. Previous studies have developed a nomogram model integrating NLR and SCC-Ag levels, and demonstrated its favorable predictive performance in patients with locally advanced cervical cancer [37]. Building on this work, the present study further validated the clinical utility of NLR, with a specific focus on patients with primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma and SCC-Ag levels ≥ 10 ng/mL—a high-risk subgroup with poorer prognosis, for whom model construction is particularly challenging yet of greater clinical relevance. Moreover, we incorporated the HRR, an emerging hematological biomarker, alongside key clinical factors such as FIGO stage and treatment modality, to establish a more comprehensive nomogram model.

It is noteworthy that serum albumin, a classical marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, also demonstrated prognostic relevance in this study. Consistent patterns were observed in both the training and validation cohorts through Kaplan–Meier analyses, and statistical significance was achieved in the univariable Cox analysis. However, albumin did not enter the final multivariable model, potentially due to collinearity with variables such as HRR. Nevertheless, its performance supports its potential value in other predictive models or clinical contexts.

Our findings suggest that these hematological parameters are not merely physiological indicators but may serve as biological markers reflecting the complex interactions between tumor and host, thereby providing a quantitative basis for elucidating prognostic heterogeneity in this high-risk population. To a large extent, these indicators capture the systemic inflammatory status, immune response, and nutritional-metabolic profile of the patient, conferring substantial clinical utility. Overall, the model developed in this study demonstrates advantages in variable selection, target population specificity, and clinical applicability, offering robust data to support precise risk stratification and individualized treatment in high-risk cervical cancer patients.

This study further investigated prognostic differences among subgroups defined by various clinical characteristics. Stratified survival analysis based on treatment modality revealed that patients receiving radiotherapy alone had significantly worse survival outcomes compared to those undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy. To exclude potential confounding effects of stage on treatment efficacy, survival analyses were conducted separately for patients at stages II, III, and IV. The results demonstrated that among stage III patients, those treated with radiotherapy alone continued to exhibit significantly lower survival rates, consistent with the overall trend. However, the differences in survival for stages II and IV were not statistically significant. This may be due to the limited number of patients. Further analysis indicated a degree of selection bias in the radiotherapy-alone group: the cohort was relatively small (n = 28), with nearly 40% aged 70 years or older. Additionally, some patients were unable to tolerate concurrent chemotherapy due to comorbidities or poor performance status, while others declined chemotherapy based on personal preference. These factors may collectively have influenced the overall survival outcomes in this group. Therefore, the inferior survival observed in the radiotherapy-alone cohort may not be solely attributable to the treatment regimen itself but could reflect the underlying poorer baseline health status of these patients. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensively assessing patient age, functional status, and comorbid conditions when formulating treatment strategies, in order to balance therapeutic benefits with tolerability and achieve more precise, individualized care.

Beyond the key variables incorporated in the current model, this study also identified several noteworthy potential associations. Previous research has demonstrated links between ABO blood groups and the incidence as well as metastasis of various malignancies. For instance, a case–control study reported differences in both the occurrence and metastatic rates of melanoma among patients with different blood types [38]. Additionally, the distribution of blood groups in patients with gynecologic cancers has been associated with survival outcomes, with individuals of blood type A possibly exhibiting a survival advantage [39]. Our study further revealed that blood type may be correlated with recurrence risk and metastatic patterns in cervical cancer: patients with blood type O showed a relatively higher recurrence rate, whereas those with blood type A had lower recurrence rates. Moreover, blood type B was more frequently associated with distant metastases, while patients with blood types A and O predominantly experienced local recurrence. These disparities may be related to the multifaceted biological functions of blood group antigens within the tumor microenvironment.

Recent studies have suggested that ABO blood group antigens, beyond being expressed on red blood cells, are also present in various epithelial tissues and solid tumor cells. Alterations in their glycosylation patterns can influence tumor cell adhesion, migration, and immune recognition. Importantly, specific blood group antigens (such as the A antigen) may interact with corresponding antibodies to activate the complement cascade, generating membrane attack complexes (C5b–9) that directly lyse tumor cells and enhance natural killer (NK) cell infiltration and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. Differences in the expression of ABO antigens may therefore modulate the intensity of these immune responses, potentially contributing to the divergent recurrence and metastasis risks observed among blood types [40].

Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that ABO blood groups may influence T-cell–mediated immune regulation. For example, in patients infected with SARS-CoV-2, individuals with blood type O exhibited significantly higher frequencies of CD4+CD8+ double-positive T cells compared to non-O types, whereas non-O individuals generated stronger but less sustained CD4+T-cell responses. The level of natural anti-B immunoglobulins in type O individuals was inversely correlated with TNF-α–producing CD4+T-cell responses [41]. Integrating these findings with existing cancer immunology data, it is plausible that ABO blood groups modulate tumor-specific T-cell responses by shaping the natural antibody landscape, thereby influencing T-cell activity and immune surveillance within the tumor microenvironment.

In blood type O individuals, the absence of A/B antigens is accompanied by high titers of natural IgM anti-A/B antibodies, which can cross-react with O-GalNAc–containing structures (e.g., Tn antigen, T antigen, and aberrantly glycosylated MUC1) on tumor cells. This cross-recognition contributes to tumor immune surveillance. However, studies in pancreatic cancer have shown that patients with blood type O display significantly lower anti-A/B IgM antibody titers, which may weaken immune recognition of tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens and facilitate tumor immune escape [42]. This mechanism may partially explain the higher recurrence rates observed among type O individuals. Collectively, ABO blood groups may not only serve as inherited genetic markers but also actively participate in tumor progression by regulating complement activation, NK cell infiltration, and T-cell immune responses. These findings suggest that ABO blood type could influence recurrence risk and metastatic pathways in cervical cancer, providing mechanistic insight into disease heterogeneity and potential directions for individualized monitoring and therapeutic strategies.

Previous prognostic models for cervical cancer have primarily focused on inflammatory biomarkers, often with limited integration of clinical variables. Additionally, some studies have inadequately addressed patient heterogeneity reflected by variations in squamous cell carcinoma antigen levels, which may limit the generalizability of these models across different risk subgroups. In the present study, we specifically targeted a cohort of primary cervical cancer patients with SCC-Ag ≥ 10 ng/mL and constructed a multidimensional nomogram incorporating systemic inflammation markers (HRR, NLR) along with key clinical parameters (FIGO stage, treatment modality). This approach integrates biological indicators with routine clinical practice, thereby enhancing individualized prognostic assessment and providing a practical tool to support treatment planning.

The nomogram developed in this study demonstrates both strong clinical applicability and translational potential. Its advantages are mainly reflected in several aspects: first, the model relies on parameters commonly obtained from routine laboratory tests and clinical records, allowing clinicians to rapidly estimate individualized risk scores using accessible hematologic markers (HRR, NLR) and standard clinical information (FIGO stage and planned or ongoing treatment regimens). Second, the model offers meaningful risk stratification, effectively distinguishing patients across low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. Patients identified as high-risk may benefit from intensified adjuvant therapies, such as the combination of standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted agents, aiming to improve tumor control and survival. Conversely, low-risk patients may avoid unnecessary aggressive treatments, reducing treatment-related toxicity and enhancing quality of care. Additionally, the risk stratification results can guide tailored follow-up schedules, providing closer monitoring for high-risk patients while allowing a less intensive approach for low-risk individuals.

Moreover, to facilitate real-world application, the model could be implemented as an interactive online calculator or mobile application, enabling clinicians to input patient parameters and obtain individualized survival predictions in real time, thereby improving accessibility and practical utility.

The predictive performance of the nomogram was assessed using multiple statistical metrics. The C-index reached 0.729 in the training cohort and 0.704 in the internal validation cohort, demonstrating minimal differences and confirming stable discriminative ability without apparent overfitting. Time-dependent ROC curves further showed high AUC values at 1-, 2-, and 3-year intervals, with the training cohort achieving 0.76, 0.81, and 0.79, and the internal validation cohort showing 0.80, 0.75, and 0.71. While the slight decline in 3-year AUC in the validation cohort may be due to limited long-term follow-up events and the single-center sample size, the overall discriminative consistency supports robust short- to mid-term predictive performance. Decision curve analysis indicated superior net clinical benefit across different time points compared with “treat all” or “treat none” strategies, confirming the practical value of the model. Risk stratification based on total nomogram scores revealed significant differences in survival among low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, confirming the model’s effective risk discrimination.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, the findings may be influenced by selection bias and require validation in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts. Second, the applicability of our model is mainly restricted to patients with good performance status (ECOG performance status 0–1), as patients with poorer functional status were generally not eligible for the treatment strategies assessed. Future studies should evaluate the model across a wider range of functional statuses. Third, although the model is based on readily available clinical and routine laboratory variables to facilitate practical use, it does not include more specialized inflammatory markers—C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)—or molecular features such as gene mutations and protein expression, which could offer additional prognostic insights and better capture tumor heterogeneity. These markers were not incorporated because they are not routinely measured in our cohort, and including them could increase patient burden. Finally, the external validation cohort was relatively small and contained incomplete follow-up data, which may limit the assessment of the model’s generalizability. Further research incorporating these elements is warranted to refine and validate the predictive performance of the model.

Overall, this nomogram provides a quantitative and practical tool for predicting outcomes in primary cervical cancer patients with elevated SCC-Ag (≥ 10 ng/mL). It demonstrates robust discrimination, stable performance, and effective risk stratification in both training and validation cohorts. By offering individualized prognostic predictions, the model can support informed decisions regarding adjuvant therapy, follow-up intensity, and overall treatment planning. Prospective multicenter validation and development of a user-friendly online calculator are warranted to bridge the gap between model construction and real-world clinical application, further facilitating precision management of cervical cancer patients.
