Learning to reflect together: a video-facilitated reflective inquiry and linguistic analysis of collaborative clinical reasoning in intensive care
Authors: Ching-Yi Lee, Hung-Yi Lai, Ching-Hsin Lee, Mi-Mi Chen, Sze-Yuen Yau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollaborative clinical reasoning (CCR) is essential to safe and effective practice in intensive care units, where decisions are shaped by uncertainty, high cognitive load, and interprofessional dynamics. Although CCR is widely recognised as a collective cognitive process, little is known about how clinicians learn to reason together or how reflective educational interventions may influence the language and social organisation through which CCR is enacted.MethodsThis exploratory mixed-methods Groundwork study examined the association between a video-facilitated reflective (VFR) inquiry, informed by Video Reflexive Ethnography (VRE) principles, and the linguistic features of CCR within a neuro-intensive care unit (NICU) in Taiwan. Twelve clinicians (4 attending physicians, 2 resident physicians, 5 registered nurses, and 1 respiratory therapist) participated. Eight bedside ward-round sessions were video-recorded across two phases (4 pre-intervention; 4 post-intervention). Audio was transcribed in Mandarin and translated into English by bilingual clinicians. English transcripts were analysed at the session level using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC-22). Five a priori focal constructs—analytical thinking, clout, authenticity, tentativeness, and insight—were selected on theoretical grounds linked to dual-process reasoning, epistemic stance, and team cognition; 5 additional categories were retained as descriptive context. Independent-samples Welch t-tests, Hedges’ g effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals, and Mann–Whitney U robustness checks were used to compare pre- and post-intervention sessions. Quantitative trends were interpreted alongside qualitative reflections from retrospective think-aloud sessions in which participants reviewed selected video excerpts of their own practice.ResultsOf the 5 pre-specified focal constructs, only insight showed a robust post-intervention elevation, consistent across parametric (Welch t(5.87) = -3.78, p = .010) and non-parametric (Mann–Whitney U = 0.0, p = .029) tests, with Hedges’ g = 2.33 (approximate 95% CI [0.53, 4.12]); this finding survives a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of α = .01. Tentative language showed a parametric trend in the predicted direction (t(5.08) = -2.57, p = .049; g = 1.58, 95% CI [-0.01, 3.17]) that did not survive non-parametric testing (Mann–Whitney p = .114) or multiplicity considerations, and is therefore reported as suggestive rather than detected. No effect was detected for clout (g = -0.18), authenticity (g = 0.41), or analytical thinking (g = 0.47); all confidence intervals crossed zero. Confidence intervals are wide, reflecting only 4 session-level observations per phase. During reflective sessions, participants described heightened awareness of their own communicative habits, greater openness to uncertainty, and increased willingness to invite alternative perspectives from other team members.ConclusionsIn this exploratory single-site study with only 4 session-level observations per phase, video-facilitated reflective inquiry was associated with elevated use of insight-related vocabulary in subsequent ward-round talk, while a parametric increase in tentative language did not survive non-parametric robustness testing. No effect was detected for clout, authenticity, or analytical thinking. Wide confidence intervals, translation-related validity considerations, and the absence of a controlled comparison group warrant caution; the findings are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The combined use of computational linguistic analysis and reflective dialogue offers a tractable approach to surfacing subtle changes in collaborative reasoning that should be tested in larger, multi-site samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09861-w.

Introduction

Clinical reasoning lies at the heart of competent medical practice, enabling clinicians to interpret complex data, generate diagnostic hypotheses, and select appropriate management strategies under uncertainty [1]. In intensive care units (ICU), such reasoning rarely occurs in isolation. Instead, diagnostic and therapeutic judgments emerge through collaborative clinical reasoning (CCR)—the shared cognitive process by which physicians, nurses, and allied professionals negotiate patient understanding and collective action [2, 3]. Because ICU work is distributed, time-pressured, and often ambiguous, the quality of CCR directly influences patient safety. Communication failures and unchecked assumptions remain recurrent sources of error [4, 5]. Thus, strengthening CCR represents not merely a teamwork enhancement but an educational imperative: clinicians must learn to reason together, cultivating mutual awareness and adaptive dialogue amid cognitive load and hierarchical constraint.

Collaborative clinical reasoning as team cognition and learning

CCR can be conceptualized as an integration of individual cognition—hypothesis generation, evidence appraisal, and decision justification—with team cognition, which involves building shared mental models, maintaining transactive memory, and dynamically monitoring collective understanding [6]. Drawing on dual-process theory, clinical reasoning alternates between intuitive, rapid System 1 judgments and analytical, effortful System 2 deliberation [1, 7]. Within teams, CCR serves as a social mechanism that distributes cognitive load and counterbalances bias by exposing reasoning to collective scrutiny. From a sociocultural perspective, reasoning is enacted through interaction: talk, gesture, documentation, and tool use constitute the medium through which knowledge is constructed [8, 9]. Language, therefore, is not ancillary to reasoning but constitutive of it—it organizes attention, conveys epistemic stance, and structures how clinicians jointly navigate uncertainty.

Despite the theoretical promise of CCR, its enactment in clinical environments is impeded by entrenched hierarchies and asymmetries of voice. Senior physicians often dominate rounds, while nurses and junior trainees may defer even when possessing critical contextual knowledge [10]. Such patterns shape epistemic stance—the degree of certainty, authority, or tentativeness expressed in language [11]. High-clout, declarative speech reinforces top-down decision-making, whereas tentative or insight-oriented expressions invite inquiry and shared reasoning. Edmondson’s [12] construct of psychological safety underscores that equal participation requires an environment where questioning and dissent are safe; yet psychological safety alone is insufficient without deliberate reflection on how discourse itself reproduces hierarchy. Overconfidence has been implicated in diagnostic error [13], and recent work on physician humility highlights epistemic openness as a professional virtue [14]. Consequently, educational interventions must move beyond communication "skills training" toward practices that surface and reorganize the linguistic and cultural structures of talk through which authority and uncertainty are negotiated.

Reflection and the promise of video-facilitated reflective inquiry

Reflection offers a means to transform such entrenched communicative patterns. Schön’s [15] reflective practitioner model and Kolb’s [16] experiential learning cycle both posit that learning occurs when practitioners critically examine their own "knowing-in-action," experiment with new understandings, and re-enter practice with altered frames of reference. In complex systems like the ICU, however, much of professional performance is tacit and inaccessible to self-report. Video-facilitated reflective inquiry addresses this limitation by using recordings of authentic practice as stimuli for structured collective reflection [17, 18]. Drawing on principles from Video Reflexive Ethnography and related video-based professional development traditions, this approach re-presents naturally occurring clinical interactions to practitioners, enabling them to observe habitual communication patterns, question implicit hierarchies, and notice micro-moments of reasoning that would otherwise remain invisible [17]. The video recording serves not as surveillance but as a shared reference point for inquiry, redistributing attention from individual performance to the collective evidence of practice. Empirical work informed by these traditions has demonstrated improvements in situational awareness, interprofessional respect, and learning culture [17]. The present study draws on these principles in a methodologically constrained form: video served as a recording instrument for naturalistic ward-round talk and as the stimulus for retrospective reflective think-aloud sessions, but the analytic centre of gravity rests on transcribed language rather than on full multimodal ethnographic analysis. Yet, the mechanisms through which video-facilitated reflection may reshape cognition and communication remain insufficiently specified, particularly regarding linguistic indicators of reflective learning and collaborative reasoning.

Language as a window into learning: Computational linguistic analysis

If reasoning and reflection are enacted through language, then examining linguistic patterns offers a powerful lens for understanding how learning manifests in interaction. Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) is a validated computational tool that categorizes words into psychologically meaningful domains such as analytical thinking, authenticity, insight, and clout [19]. Function words—articles, pronouns, conjunctions—are especially revealing of cognitive style and social orientation [20]. In educational contexts, shifts toward tentative and insight-related language can signify metacognitive engagement, while decreased clout may index redistributions of authority and improved communicative equity [21]. Thus, LIWC provides scalable, theory-aligned indicators to complement qualitative ethnographic interpretation. However, computational metrics cannot capture turn-taking or pragmatic nuance; triangulating them with reflective interviews or think-aloud data allows richer interpretation of learning mechanisms. This mixed-methods integration responds to growing calls for multi-modal evidence of how reflection translates into cognitive and behavioural change [22, 23].

Despite increasing adoption of VFR in health professions education, little is known about how reflective video inquiry alters the linguistic and cognitive ecology of CCR. Empirical studies often describe improved teamwork qualitatively but seldom trace the micro-level transformations in discourse that signal deeper epistemic change. Moreover, most VFR research originates from Western academic centers, leaving under-examined how hierarchical and cultural dynamics in East Asian critical-care settings mediate reflective learning. Addressing this gap, the present exploratory study investigates whether and how a video-facilitated reflective intervention is associated with changes in the linguistic features of multidisciplinary ICU discussions using LIWC-22 analysis, supplemented by participants’ reflective commentaries.

The study aims to elucidate, in an exploratory and hypothesis-generating manner, how reflection may relate to the cognitive and social mechanisms of CCR. Specifically, it asks:Do linguistic markers associated with analytical thinking, insight, tentativeness, authenticity, and clout shift following VFR?How do participants interpret observed linguistic changes in relation to their reasoning, roles, and participation during reflective think-alouds?What do convergences between quantitative and qualitative findings reveal about how VFR fosters communicative equity and epistemic humility in interprofessional teams?

Materials and methods

Design and theoretical orientation

This Groundwork study employed an exploratory mixed-methods, video-facilitated reflective inquiry design informed by Video Reflexive Ethnography principles [17, 18] to explore how reflective viewing and discussion of clinical encounters is associated with the linguistic and cognitive dimensions of CCR in an ICU. The design aligns with the constructivist view that learning emerges from reflection on authentic practice [15, 16] and the distributed cognition perspective that reasoning is co-constructed through interaction, artifacts, and shared context [9]. Quantitative linguistic data were integrated with qualitative reflections to examine how the reflective process may mediate communicative and epistemic change. The study was framed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; it did not seek to establish causal effects but to examine whether reflective viewing of recorded clinical interactions is associated with linguistic features of CCR that can be interpretively integrated with participants’ own reflections. Setting and participants

The study took place in the neuro-intensive care unit of a tertiary teaching hospital in Taiwan. The ICU operates as a multidisciplinary environment where clinical reasoning unfolds dynamically through rounds, briefings, and informal discussions.

Purposive sampling identified 12 participants—4 attending physicians, 2 resident physicians, 5 registered nurses, and 1 respiratory therapist—representing a range of professional roles and hierarchical positions involved in team-based clinical reasoning. Participants were invited through departmental announcements, and all agreed to participate in the study. Inclusion criteria required at least one year of ICU experience and fluency in Mandarin Chinese.

The NICU was selected as the study setting because it represents a high-acuity clinical environment in which collaborative clinical reasoning is essential. The unit manages a complex neurosurgical caseload encompassing cerebrovascular, neuro-oncological, and spinal pathologies, requiring clinicians to engage in multi-system decision-making during rounds—including ventilator weaning, intracranial pressure management, hemodynamic support, and post-operative neurological assessment. Physicians had between 4 and 18 years of clinical experience, nurses had between 5 and 11 years, and the respiratory therapist had 6 years. This diversity in experience levels and professional roles enabled examination of how hierarchical dynamics and expertise asymmetries shape CCR.

Data collection

Video recording of team interactions

The primary method of data collection involved video and audio recordings of NICU team conversations during bedside patient rounds. Video served two specific functions: (i) the audio track was transcribed to produce the linguistic corpus analysed using LIWC; and (ii) selected video clips served as the stimulus for the Stage 2 reflective think-aloud sessions described below. The study did not undertake systematic analysis of gaze, gesture, spatial positioning, or sequential turn organisation; such multimodal interaction analysis was outside the analytic scope of this work. Researchers shadowed the NICU team, recording bedside patient rounds using hand-held video cameras for approximately one hour at a time. Recordings were conducted once per month for six months, yielding 8 ward-round sessions in total, distributed across two phases (4 pre-intervention; 4 post-intervention). The pre-intervention phase comprised first-time recordings; the post-intervention phase recorded the same NICU team after each member had completed at least one reflective think-aloud session reviewing their earlier ward-round footage. Session length and team composition varied with the clinical schedule of the unit, reflecting the unit’s usual rounding practice rather than a fixed sampling frame. Researchers also maintained field notes documenting chronological accounts of observed interactions, contextual factors influencing communication, and any emergent themes related to clinical reasoning.

Reflective and retrospective think-aloud sessions

Reflective practice is a core component of professional development in healthcare, enabling practitioners to critically examine their reasoning and decision-making processes. In this study, reflective and retrospective think-aloud sessions were integrated as a structured educational strategy to support critical reflection on collaborative clinical reasoning.

To examine potential changes in clinical reasoning and team-based decision-making, video-recorded data from bedside observations were analysed before and after participants engaged in reflective think-aloud sessions. The retrospective think-aloud approach was designed to prompt participants to articulate their reasoning while reviewing selected video excerpts of their own patient-round interactions.

Researchers reviewed the video recordings and edited short segments (10–20 minutes each) that captured key moments of collaborative clinical reasoning. These edited clips were used as stimuli during the think-aloud sessions, during which participants were invited to reflect on and explain their reasoning processes. Specifically, participants were encouraged to:Describe their thought processes in reaching a diagnosis or treatment plan.Explain the reasoning behind specific statements, questions, or actions observed in the footage.Identify any factors that influenced their decisions during clinical encounters.Reflect on interactions with other team members and their impact on shared reasoning.

Participants were able to pause, replay, or revisit video segments as needed to support articulation of their thought processes. Reflective discussions were conducted either concurrently with the think-aloud activity or in a subsequent facilitated discussion. Facilitation was guided by a semi-structured interview guide developed specifically for this study, informed by ethnographic observations and relevant literature on collaborative clinical reasoning and reflective practice. An English version of the interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

All reflective think-aloud sessions were audio-recorded and lasted approximately 20 to 30 minutes. Recordings were transcribed verbatim and analysed to identify patterns in collaborative clinical reasoning, decision-making strategies, and communicative behaviours among neuro-intensive care unit team members.

Participant flow and unit of analysis

Across the 8 recorded ward-round sessions, the 12 participants contributed at varying frequencies, reflecting the clinical roster: most participants appeared in one or two recorded sessions, with several contributing across both phases (Supplementary File 2). Because session composition varied day-to-day and no participant was present in every session, individual-level pre/post linguistic scores could not be reliably computed. The unit of analysis was therefore the ward-round session, with each session-level transcript yielding one set of LIWC scores. This yielded 8 session-level observations: 4 pre-intervention (A1–A4) and 4 post-intervention (B1–B4). Pre- and post-intervention sessions were treated as independent groups for statistical comparison; given partial participant overlap across phases, this treatment is approximate, and we therefore report effect sizes and a non-parametric robustness check alongside parametric tests (see Statistical analysis).

Data analysis

A total of eight recordings were collected. The video recordings were transcribed in Mandarin, the participants’ primary language, and then translated into English. The translation process involved a team of bilingual experts who ensured accuracy and retention of original meanings.

Transcription, translation, and language version of LIWC

LIWC-22 (English dictionary) was applied to the English-translated session transcripts. We acknowledge that this is a methodologically consequential choice. LIWC categories are computed from word counts in language-specific dictionaries, and the construct validity of LIWC indices—particularly clout (which depends on first-person plural, second-person, and first-person singular pronoun ratios) and tentative language (hedging adverbs and modal verbs)—depends on the structural correspondence between source and target language. Mandarin Chinese and English differ in pronoun expression (Mandarin permits pro-drop and uses fewer overt subject pronouns), in hedging mechanisms (modal sentence-final particles such as 吧/嘛/呢/啦 and discourse markers such as 也許/或許/可能 have no one-to-one English equivalents), and in markers of social authority. Translating from Mandarin into English can therefore alter the surface forms LIWC counts, even when pragmatic meaning is preserved.

Three safeguards were used to mitigate this threat to validity. First, translators were briefed on the LIWC categories of interest and asked to attempt to render (a) pro-dropped Mandarin subjects as the contextually appropriate explicit English pronoun, (b) Mandarin hedges through English hedge approximations (e.g., 也許 → "perhaps," 可能 → "might/may"), and (c) modal sentence-final particles through adjacent English hedge approximations where pragmatically equivalent. These are mitigations of, not solutions to, the structural cross-linguistic differences identified above; we cannot empirically demonstrate that LIWC-relevant features were retained at a constant rate across translation. Second, a second bilingual reviewer independently checked a subset of the translated segments for retention of these features; the review was conducted as a qualitative spot-check, and no quantitative inter-rater agreement statistic (e.g., percentage agreement, kappa) was computed because the review was not a systematic coding procedure. The proportion of segments checked was not pre-specified. This is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Third, the analytic frame was structured to treat translation-mediated LIWC indices as approximate, theory-guided indicators rather than precise psychometric measurements; we therefore privilege effect sizes and triangulation with participant reflections over null-hypothesis significance testing. We return to translation-related validity limitations in the Discussion. A traditional Chinese LIWC dictionary (C-LIWC [24]) exists; replicating this analysis directly on Mandarin transcripts using C-LIWC is an important direction for future work.

This study employed LIWC to perform a systematic, computational analysis of language use [19]. LIWC enables researchers to analyse both psychological processes and linguistic content by categorizing words into predefined semantic and cognitive domains. LIWC operates by comparing each word in a transcript to an established dictionary of linguistic categories, calculating the frequency of words related to cognitive, emotional, and social processes. For example, the word encourage may fall under multiple categories such as social, emotional, positive emotion, and cognitive processing. Similarly, the word remorseful may be categorized under negative emotion, sadness, and discrepancy words. By quantifying linguistic features, LIWC provides objective insights into team communication, cognitive reasoning patterns, and emotional tone.

Selection of LIWC focal constructs

LIWC-22 reports indices across more than 80 lexical and psychological categories. Reporting and interpreting all categories would invite multiple-testing problems and obscure the theoretical relationship between linguistic markers and the constructs of interest. We therefore distinguished a priori between 5 focal constructs, selected before data analysis on theoretical grounds linked to the study’s research questions, and 5 additional categories retained as descriptive context but not used as primary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Because the unit of analysis was the ward-round session, statistical comparisons were conducted on session-level LIWC scores (n = 4 pre-intervention; n = 4 post-intervention). For each focal LIWC index, we computed: (i) the independent-samples t-test with Welch’s correction (given small and potentially unequal variances), reporting t, Welch–Satterthwaite df, and exact p values; (ii) Hedges’ g with the small-sample correction factor and approximate 95% confidence intervals computed from the normal approximation as the principal estimand, in line with editorial guidance to prioritise effect sizes and uncertainty intervals over p values in small samples; and (iii) the Mann–Whitney U test as a non-parametric robustness check. Statistical reporting follows American Psychological Association (7th edition) conventions: italicised statistical symbols (t, p, M, SD, n, g, U); exact p values to three decimal places without a leading zero; and 95% confidence intervals for all effect sizes. Analyses were conducted in Python (SciPy 1.x).

This is an exploratory, hypothesis-generating study with eight session-level observations. Three limitations of the inferential framework warrant explicit acknowledgment. First, with n = 4 per group the small-sample t-distribution is sensitive to departures from normality, and confidence intervals on Hedges’ g should be interpreted as approximate exploratory uncertainty intervals rather than precise bounds. Second, with 5 pre-specified focal constructs the family-wise type I error rate at α =.05 is approximately.23 if the tests were independent. We did not apply a formal multiplicity correction because the study is positioned as exploratory and effect-size estimation rather than confirmatory hypothesis testing; however, we explicitly report which focal findings would survive a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of α =.01 (p =.05/5), and we treat conventional significance markers with corresponding caution. Third, at n = 4 per group the Mann–Whitney U test has only 70 possible two-sided outcomes; the minimum achievable two-sided p value (under perfect separation of the two groups) is.029. Readers should therefore interpret Mann–Whitney p values at this sample size as floor-bounded rather than as fine-grained probabilities. Fourth, although the unit of analysis was the session, several participants contributed to sessions in both phases (no participant appeared in every session, and several contributed to one or two sessions across the two phases). Independent-samples tests were used because no principled basis existed for pairing specific A-phase and B-phase sessions, but this partial overlap mildly violates the independence assumption and is reported as a limitation of the analytic strategy.

Qualitative interpretation

Verbatim transcripts of the reflective think-aloud sessions were read iteratively by two members of the research team. The interpretive aim was not full inductive thematic analysis but theory-informed identification of accounts that illuminated or qualified the linguistic patterns observed in the LIWC analysis. Where a participant’s reflective account converged with a LIWC pattern (e.g., a participant describing increased openness to uncertainty alongside an increase in tentative language for the relevant session), the convergence was treated as supportive triangulation; where accounts diverged, the divergence was preserved and reported.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the hospital’s institutional review board (Approval No. 202002453B0). Participants’ confidentiality was maintained through anonymized transcripts and secure data storage. Informed consent was obtained before recording, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Results

This section presents the findings from the study, focusing on the changes in linguistic patterns observed through LIWC analysis, as well as qualitative insights derived from reflective think-aloud sessions. The results are organized to reflect the primary and secondary research questions, emphasizing the influence of the reflective practice intervention on CCR.

Participant demographics

A total of 12 participants were recruited for this study, consisting of 4 attending physicians, 2 resident physicians, 5 registered nurses, and 1 respiratory therapist. These participants engaged in bedside clinical case discussions during ward rounds, with each case discussion lasting approximately 3 to 5 minutes. Each ward round included at least two different professional roles, ensuring interprofessional engagement in CCR. All 12 participants completed at least one reflective think-aloud session; individual session composition varied with the clinical roster, with most participants appearing in one or two recorded sessions and several contributing across both phases (Supplementary File 2). The primary data source comprised clinical video recordings, with 4 pre-intervention sessions (A1–A4) involving first-time participants, and 4 post-intervention sessions (B1–B4) including members who had previously participated in post-video interviews and were now engaging in a second recording session. Participants were also involved in one-on-one interviews lasting 20 to 30 minutes, during which they reviewed their own clinical video recordings. These retrospective reflections provided insights into their reasoning processes and discussions related to CCR. Each session-level English-translated transcript was analysed as a single LIWC document; comparisons used independent-samples Welch t-tests on session-level LIWC scores (n = 4 per phase), with Hedges’ g effect sizes (95% CI) and the Mann–Whitney U test as a non-parametric robustness check.

Eight ward round sessions were recorded across two phases: four pre-intervention (A1–A4) and four post-intervention (B1–B4). Each session captured a different set of patients, but the overall case mix across both phases was comparable in diagnostic complexity and management demands. Pre-intervention recordings included cases involving ruptured and unruptured cerebral aneurysms requiring intracranial pressure monitoring, post-craniotomy patients with cerebral oedema complicating ventilator weaning, and intracerebral haemorrhage with persistent unconsciousness. Post-intervention recordings included cases of traumatic spinal cord injury complicated by neurogenic shock, high-grade brain tumours (glioma grade IV, parasagittal meningioma, anaplastic ependymoma) with post-operative motor deficits, multi-aneurysm rupture requiring staged endovascular intervention, and respiratory failure following stent placement. Across both phases, clinical discussions routinely involved multi-system decision-making—including external ventricular drain threshold management, vasopressor titration, airway management, and goals-of-care conversations with families. Although the specific cases differed between sessions, the consistently high acuity across all eight recordings provides a comparable clinical backdrop against which to interpret the observed linguistic changes.

Quantitative analysis: linguistic changes post-intervention

Statistical analysis of the 5 focal a priori constructs identified one robust post-intervention elevation (insight), one suggestive parametric trend that did not survive non-parametric robustness testing (tentative language), and three null findings (analytical thinking, clout, authenticity). The 5 additional descriptive categories also showed no detectable change. Increase in insight: deeper reflection on cases

What this elevation reflects at the level of underlying cognition or interaction practice cannot be resolved from LIWC indices alone and requires future work integrating discourse and interaction analysis.

Clout: No detected change, with high post-intervention variability

A small decline in clout scores (49.34 to 46.38) was observed, but the effect size was small (Hedges’ g = −0.18, 95% CI [−1.57, 1.21]) and the confidence interval clearly crosses zero (Welch t(4.59) = 0.30, p =.780; Mann–Whitney U = 8.0, p = 1.000). Authenticity: No detected change

Authenticity scores were higher post-intervention than pre-intervention (41.76 to 47.38), but the effect was modest and the confidence interval included zero (Hedges’ g = 0.41, 95% CI [−0.99, 1.81]; Welch t(5.95) = −0.67, p =.529). These data do not support inferential claims about authenticity.

Discussion

This study examined the influence of a video-facilitated reflective intervention on CCR in multidisciplinary teams within a NICU. By integrating transcribed video-recorded observations with computational linguistic analysis using LIWC, and triangulating with reflective think-aloud accounts, this research sought to identify linguistic and cognitive shifts following a structured reflective intervention. Of the 5 pre-specified focal LIWC constructs, only insight-related vocabulary showed an estimated post-intervention elevation whose approximate 95% confidence interval did not cross zero ([0.53, 4.12]) and whose parametric p value would survive a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of α =.01 for the 5 focal tests; the corresponding Mann–Whitney p value of.029 reflects perfect separation of the 4 pre-intervention sessions below the 4 post-intervention sessions on this index and is the minimum two-sided value achievable at this sample size. This pattern is consistent with — but does not establish — a post-intervention shift in linguistic register on the insight category. Tentative language showed a parametric trend in the predicted direction that did not survive non-parametric testing or multiplicity considerations and is reported as suggestive rather than detected. The remaining three focal constructs (analytical thinking, clout, authenticity) showed no detected change; all confidence intervals crossed zero. Reflective think-aloud accounts converged with the linguistic pattern for insight and helped characterise the heterogeneity behind the null clout finding. These findings contribute to ongoing discussions on the role of video-facilitated reflective inquiry in interprofessional ICU settings and are framed throughout as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of intervention effectiveness.

The post-intervention elevation of insight-category vocabulary should be read as a change in linguistic register — an increase in the frequency of words such as "think," "consider," and "check" in post-intervention transcripts — rather than as a direct measurement of underlying cognitive change. Insight-category words have been associated in prior literature with metacognitive talk [19, 20], but the present data establish only that these lexical markers were used more frequently in post-intervention than pre-intervention sessions; whether their elevated use reflects altered cognitive processing, altered conversational convention, observer effects, or some combination cannot be determined from word counts alone. Markowitz [20] cautions that LIWC indices reflect cognitive style rather than cognitive ability; we extend this caution by noting that LIWC indices reflect linguistic surface form rather than the cognitive moves that surface form may, in some contexts, accompany. The pre-specified prediction that reflective inquiry would elevate analytical-thinking scores was not supported (Hedges’ g = 0.47, CI crossing zero). We therefore do not interpret these data as evidence of a System 1–to–System 2 shift at the level of overall analytical-thinking style; the insight-category finding is compatible with the narrower observation that post-intervention transcripts contained more frequent use of insight-category vocabulary.

The parametric trend toward more tentative language post-intervention (e.g., words such as "seems," "might," and "possibly") is in the theoretically predicted direction. In traditional ICU settings, clinicians are often expected to communicate with confidence and minimise expressions of doubt [13]; an increase in hedging vocabulary would, in principle, suggest a shift toward a more open and exploratory reasoning style in which team members are willing to consider alternative explanations and discuss diagnostic uncertainty [30]. However, this finding did not survive non-parametric robustness testing or multiplicity considerations, and we therefore treat it as a hypothesis to be tested in larger samples rather than a result to be built upon. If replicated, an increase in hedging vocabulary alongside the more robust insight finding would together suggest that reflective inquiry primarily affects the metacognitive register of clinical talk; the present data are consistent with that hypothesis but do not establish it.

The small decline in clout scores in post-intervention did not reach statistical significance, and the confidence interval crosses zero (Hedges’ g = −0.18, 95% CI [−1.57, 1.21]). We therefore do not interpret these data as evidence of a redistribution of discursive authority within the NICU team, departing from our originally hypothesised direction. Several explanations are plausible. First, our sample is too small to detect modest shifts in a noisy summary index. Second, LIWC’s clout index depends on pronoun ratios; translation from Mandarin (a pro-drop language) into English may have introduced systematic noise into this specific index. Third, structural features of NICU hierarchy may be sufficiently entrenched that brief reflective interventions do not measurably alter the linguistic surface of authority within a few weeks of practice. Whether reflective inquiry produces context-dependent changes in discursive authority that aggregate to no net effect at the LIWC summary-index level, or whether NICU clout patterns are stable on the timescale of this study, cannot be determined from these data and would require a different study design — larger samples, multi-site, with explicit measurement of authority distribution at the case-discussion level rather than aggregated to session-level summary scores.

Read together, these findings are most consistent with a narrow rather than a broad effect: structured video-facilitated reflection appears to elevate metacognitive vocabulary (insight-related language) in subsequent ward-round talk, while leaving overall analytical style, authority distribution, and self-disclosing language largely unchanged within the limits of this small corpus. Reflection is a key component of experiential learning theory, which posits that deep learning occurs when individuals analyse their experiences, extract insights, and apply them to future decision-making [31, 32]; the present data are compatible with the more specific claim that structured reflection on recorded practice helps clinicians rehearse and articulate metacognitive moves, rather than the broader claim that reflection reorganises team communication overall. To translate these preliminary findings into practice, reflective faculty development in ICU teams could specifically scaffold metacognitive moves—asking clinicians during debrief to articulate what they were uncertain about, what alternatives they considered, and what they would now check—while subjecting any claims of broader team-level change to further empirical test.

Methodological reflections: translation, scope, and what video did and did not do

Two methodological choices shape the interpretation of these findings. First, LIWC-22 was applied to English translations of Mandarin transcripts. This procedure inevitably introduces a translation-mediated layer between the participants’ actual speech and the linguistic indices reported. Mandarin and English differ in pronoun realisation, hedging mechanisms, and modal-particle systems that LIWC depends on, particularly for pronoun-driven summary indices such as clout. Our mitigations — translator briefing on LIWC-sensitive features, second-reviewer spot-checks, and analytic privileging of effect sizes and triangulation over precise psychometric inference — reduce but do not eliminate this concern, and we cannot empirically demonstrate the rate at which LIWC-relevant features were retained across translation. The insight finding rests on a lexical category (cognitive-process verbs such as "think," "know," "consider") for which Mandarin–English correspondence is typically expressed through forms with reasonably direct equivalents (我覺得/我認為 → "I think"; 想 → "think/consider"), though this correspondence is not perfect and some pragmatic nuance is inevitably lost in translation. Clout-related interpretations should be treated as the most translation-affected, given the index’s dependence on pronoun ratios in a pro-drop source language. Future replication using C-LIWC [24]applied to Mandarin transcripts is the appropriate next step.

Second, although the study draws on Video Reflexive Ethnography principles, it does not constitute a full VRE design in the sociological sense. Video served two specific functions: as a recording instrument for naturalistic ward-round audio that was transcribed for linguistic analysis, and as the stimulus for retrospective reflective think-aloud sessions. The study did not systematically analyse gaze, gesture, spatial positioning, or sequential turn organisation, and it did not engage in iterative ethnographic immersion in the field. We therefore characterise the design as "video-facilitated reflective inquiry"—a more modest framing that matches what the analysis delivered.

Research limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the study involved 12 participants from a single neurosurgical ICU, which constrains both statistical power and generalisability. The small sample size limits the ability to detect statistically significant differences across LIWC categories, and findings from one institutional context may not transfer directly to other clinical settings, team structures, or cultural environments. Furthermore, the pre- and post-intervention recordings captured different patient cases with varying clinical presentations. Although both phases involved cases of comparable neurosurgical acuity, the specific diagnoses differed—for example, post-intervention sessions included cases requiring particularly explicit multi-system reasoning, such as neurogenic shock management and seizure with diagnostic ambiguity—which may have independently influenced linguistic patterns. Similarly, natural variation in team composition across recording days means that shifts in communicative dynamics cannot be attributed solely to the reflective intervention. These findings should therefore be interpreted as suggestive trends warranting further investigation with larger, multi-site samples and controlled designs, rather than as evidence of causal effects.

Second, the high-stakes, time-sensitive nature of ICU practice may have shaped participant behaviour. Awareness of being recorded may have led clinicians to modify their communication styles, potentially affecting the authenticity of the observed interactions. Although efforts were made to minimise observer effects, future research could employ longitudinal designs with extended recording periods to allow participants to habituate to the presence of cameras and capture more naturalistic communication patterns.

Third, while LIWC-22 provides a validated, scalable approach to quantifying linguistic features, it categorises words based on predefined lexical databases and does not capture the rhetorical strategies, pragmatic functions, or contextual meaning of utterances. A word such as "maybe" is counted as tentative regardless of whether it signals genuine diagnostic uncertainty or a politeness convention. Future research could integrate LIWC with qualitative discourse analysis or conversation analysis to examine how linguistic patterns relate to the sequential organisation of clinical reasoning in situ.

Fourth, although the study used video recordings as both the primary data source and stimuli for reflective sessions, the analysis did not systematically engage with the visual, embodied, or sequential dimensions of the recorded interactions. The analytic focus was on transcribed language rather than on gaze, gesture, spatial positioning, or turn-taking. While the video-facilitated reflective intervention draws on principles from Video Reflexive Ethnography (VRE), the study does not constitute a full ethnographic design in the sociological sense. Future research could integrate interaction analysis or multimodal discourse analysis to provide a richer account of how collaborative clinical reasoning is accomplished in practice. Additionally, patient and family contributions to clinical reasoning were not captured in the current design, representing a further limitation that future studies should address.

Fifth, LIWC-22 was applied to English translations of Mandarin transcripts. Despite translator briefing on LIWC-sensitive linguistic features and second-reviewer checks, translation can alter the surface forms LIWC counts, particularly for pronoun-dependent summary indices such as clout. Future replication using the traditional C-LIWC applied directly to Mandarin transcripts is needed to verify translation-mediated findings.

Sixth, the unit of analysis was the ward-round session (n = 4 per phase), yielding 8 session-level observations in total. At this sample size, conventional inferential statistics carry substantial uncertainty: confidence intervals on effect sizes are wide; the Mann–Whitney test’s minimum two-sided p value is floor-bounded at.029; and 5 focal tests imply a family-wise type I error rate of approximately.23 at α =.05 if no correction is applied. We did not apply a formal multiplicity correction because the study is exploratory and estimation-based, but report Bonferroni-comparison status alongside each focal finding so that readers can apply a stricter inferential standard if they prefer. Because session composition varied and several participants appeared in both phases, the independent-groups assumption is mildly violated; this is a limitation of the analytic strategy. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as initial evidence to be tested in larger, ideally multi-site corpora.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a small but growing literature applying computational linguistic analysis to ward-round talk in interprofessional ICU teams that have engaged in video-facilitated reflection. In this exploratory single-site mixed-methods design with 4 session-level observations per phase, we observed elevated use of insight-category vocabulary in post-intervention sessions compared with pre-intervention sessions — a finding whose approximate 95% confidence interval did not cross zero and whose parametric p value survived a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold for the 5 pre-specified focal constructs. A parametric trend toward more tentative language did not survive non-parametric robustness testing and is reported as suggestive rather than detected. No effect was detected for analytical thinking, clout, or authenticity. Confidence intervals were wide, translation-related validity considerations warrant caution, and the design does not support causal inference. These preliminary findings should be tested in larger, multi-site samples with controlled designs before recommendations for educational practice can be drawn. Future research should explore scalable implementations of video-facilitated reflective inquiry across different clinical environments, longitudinal effects on reasoning patterns, and integration with emerging AI-driven linguistic analysis tools. Replication using the C-LIWC Mandarin dictionary on the original Chinese transcripts, multi-site sampling with controlled designs, and integration with interaction analysis to examine embodied and sequential dimensions of CCR alongside its linguistic surface are the highest-priority next steps.
