Likelihood-based calibration improves the clinical utility of JAG1 functional data for variant classification
Authors: Tristan J Hayeck, Christopher J Sottolano, Justin J Blair, Markos N Xenakis, Nancy B Spinner, Melissa A Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMultiplexed assays of variant effects (MAVEs) represent a powerful approach to providing functional information for variants at scale. To harness the full utility of these systems, assay readout must be translated into a language that is accommodating to the clinical genomics community. We previously performed a MAVE to characterize variants in JAG1, the primary cause of the autosomal-dominant, multisystemic disease Alagille syndrome. Using this existing dataset, we calculated log-likelihood ratios of pathogenicity for each variant score, allowing for direct translation of variant data into recognized evidence weights utilized by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) in clinical variant classification. Calibration resulted in improved separation of known benign and pathogenic variants and increased the classification rate of abnormal missense variants from 486 to 610, providing clear binning for strong (n = 1), moderate (n = 340), and supporting (n = 269) evidence toward pathogenicity. Retrospective application of this evidence to a cohort of 29 individuals with a JAG1 variant of uncertain significance (VUS) identified from clinical diagnostic sequencing yielded nine (31%) variants with abnormal data meeting supporting (n = 3) and moderate (n = 6) weight for pathogenicity, of which six (21%) were upgraded to likely pathogenic or pathogenic. Calibration of MAVE data to comply with the ACMG/AMP variant classification framework improves the diagnostic yield for JAG1 variants for Alagille syndrome. Moreover, our results support the broader application of these models to additional MAVEs, suggesting that they are likely to strongly impact variant classification across genes associated with disease.Keywords: MAVE, Alagille syndrome, ALGS, JAG1, variant classification, LLRp, VUS, variant of uncertain significance

Introduction

Multiplexed assays of variant effects (MAVEs) have revolutionized our ability to characterize the function of DNA variants at scale.1 These assays, which utilize variant libraries to simultaneously measure the effects of thousands of DNA changes in a single experiment, have become increasingly valuable as a tool to reduce the reporting rates of variants of uncertain significance (VUSs), which have seen exponential growth as sequencing technologies have matured.1,2 To fully realize the utility of MAVEs, assay results must be translated into a language that is readily accessible to the clinical genomics community. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) provide guidelines for the classification of DNA variants that follows a qualitative, systematic framework where evidence is collected, stratified into strength levels (supporting, moderate, strong, or very strong) and combined to direct variant classifications (benign, likely benign, VUS, likely pathogenic, or pathogenic).3 Evidence from functional assays that support normal or abnormal function is included in this framework and utilizes the criterion codes BS3 (for benignity) and PS3 (for pathogenicity).

In recognition of the need to provide data that are meaningful and useful, and as part of the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Working Group, a Bayesian framework was developed to translate the qualitative ACMG/AMP criteria into quantitative evidence weights supporting either pathogenicity or benignity.4 This was further adapted using log-likelihood ratios of pathogenicity (LLRp) derived from MAVE score distributions to translate functional scores directly into ACMG/AMP evidence-weight categories (very strong, strong, moderate, or supporting) that can be used in support of benignity or pathogenicity during clinical variant classification.5 Similar strategies that develop gene-specific calibration frameworks have been proposed6,7 and show promise for enhancing variant classification within the ClinGen/ACMG guideline structure.

In this study, we applied this approach to a previously generated MAVE dataset measuring membrane localization of JAG18 to compute LLRps. Pathogenic variants in Jagged1 (JAG1 [MIM: 601920]) are the primary cause of the autosomal-dominant disorder Alagille syndrome 1 (ALGS1 [MIM: 118450]).8 ALGS1 is a multisystemic disease associated with hepatic, cardiac, ocular, vertebral, and facial anomalies.9 JAG1 encodes a transmembrane ligand that binds to the NOTCH2 receptor, expressed on adjacent cells, to activate the Notch signaling pathway. The pathomechanism of JAG1-related disease is haploinsufficiency, and consequently, most variants are protein truncating.10 However, over 900 JAG1 missense variants have been reported in ClinVar, with >85% classified as a VUS or with conflicting classifications.11 Some JAG1 missense variants are known to result in protein misfolding and consequent intracellular retention.8,12,13,14,15 Our MAVE, which measured membrane localization, was developed to test the effects of all nucleotide changes occurring in the first 7 exons of JAG1, a region with a significantly higher proportion of reported missense variants.10,16,17 Using a reference set of 175 variants (n = 69 known pathogenic and n = 106 known benign), we established a threshold for abnormal membrane localization and classified variants as either abnormal or likely abnormal depending on their mean membrane localization scores and confidence intervals. Through this method, 486 variants were characterized as functionally abnormal.18

To improve upon the utility of this previously generated JAG1 MAVE dataset, we calculated LLRps to allow for clear translation of membrane localization scores into evidence-strength categories recognized in the ACMG/AMP framework. We then applied these data to a retrospective cohort of individuals with JAG1 VUSs identified through clinical genome sequencing to assess clinical utility.

Material and methods

LLRp calibration

Study cohort

Individuals with a classified variant in JAG1 were identified through the Genetic Cholestasis Panel Testing Program (funded by Mirum Pharmaceuticals) through PreventionGenetics, a CLIA-certified testing laboratory. All individuals (or their parent/guardian) provided informed consent for genetic testing and for the use of their de-identified data for research purposes, as part of the clinical testing program. Test results were de-identified by PreventionGenetics prior to sharing. The use of anonymized data for this study was considered non-human subjects research in accordance with applicable institutional and national guidelines. Individuals received testing between February 24, 2021, and March 31, 2025. Results were filtered to include only those variants occurring between amino acids 2 and 336 (JAG1 exons 1–7), for which a MAVE dataset is available.8 Variants included those with classifications of VUS, likely pathogenic, and pathogenic.

Structural and stability analysis

AlphaFold2 (DeepMind) was used to predict structures for wild-type and mutant JAG1 variants by aligning amino acid residues 31–333 in PyMOL (PyMOL v.2.5.4, Schrödinger). Residue-wise structural perturbation was quantified using local alpha carbon (Cα) root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) within ±15 residues of the variant site. FoldX19 v.5.1 was used to estimate ΔΔG values following structure optimization with RepairPDB mutation modeling using BuildModel. Mutation-induced changes in folding free energy (ΔΔG) were also computed using the Cartesian ΔΔG protocol implemented in Rosetta v.2026.06.20 The wild-type structure was first subjected to Cartesian relaxation using the ref2015_cart energy function and a full-atom interaction cutoff distance of 9.0 Å. Cartesian ΔΔG calculations were subsequently performed with local backbone relaxation around the mutation site (backbone neighbors, bbnbrs = 1). Predicted ΔΔG values are reported in Rosetta energy units (REUs).

Results

Calculation of LLRps and mapping to ACMG/AMP evidence weights

JAG1 membrane localization scores were previously calculated by computing variant frequencies for each variant after flow sorting, calculating the weighted variant frequencies, and normalizing the scores as described.8 We originally calibrated MAVE functional scores into ACMG/AMP evidence categories using the odds of pathogenicity (OddsPath) framework,4,8,18 where all variants with an abnormal MAVE readout were assigned PSt and all variants with a likely abnormal readout were assigned PM.8 Variants with a normal readout were not assigned evidence weight due to a high potential for the assay to yield false negative results because of the importance of this region in ligand-receptor binding, a function not captured by this MAVE. Variants with a normal readout were therefore defined as normal or likely normal (LNorm).

To calculate LLRps, we used the statistical approach called maveLLR to convert MAVE functional scores into likelihood ratios.5 Each LLRp was then mapped to an ACMG/AMP evidence category using gene-specific thresholds. To compare the original approach with the LLRp-based calibration described here, we restricted the analysis to variants with definitive evidence weights and excluded indeterminate variants. For performance comparisons (sensitivity, specificity, and OddsPath), a classification was considered concordant if a model correctly identified a pathogenic variant (PSt, PM, or PSu) or a benign variant (normal or LNorm). Using this definition, the original calibration showed slightly higher sensitivity (91.8% vs. 87.3%) and specificity (90.0% vs. 87.3%), although overall performance was similar. The original calibration framework yielded an OddsPath of 24.6 for variants with abnormal membrane localization, consistent with strong evidence of pathogenicity, while inclusion of variants with likely abnormal membrane localization lowered the OddsPath to 11.5, consistent with moderate evidence of pathogenicity.8 Using the new LLRp-based framework, variants assigned PM or PSt evidence weight yielded an OddsPath of 18.4.

However, these summary metrics do not fully capture the behavior of the two calibration methods, which differ in the fraction of reference variants retained for analysis. Notably, the original calibration abstains on a substantially larger fraction of reference variants (n = 66 indeterminate for original OddsPath calibration compared to n = 34 indeterminate for LLRp), which affects interpretation. Among 175 reference variants, 71% (n = 124) were assigned concordant evidence categories under the LLRp-based approach compared with 57% (n = 99) in the original analysis (Figures 1A–1C; Table S1). This improvement is largely driven by increased coverage, with the LLRp-based model assigning evidence to 81% of variants vs. 62% in the original model.

When applied to all variants in the library, the LLRp model binned all those previously categorized as PSt or PM by OddsPath as having either strong (n = 1), moderate (n = 340), or supporting (n = 145) evidence for pathogenicity (Figure 1D). Of the 340 variants with LLRps assigned as PM, 273 were previously categorized as PSt and 67 as PM. Of the 269 variants with LLRps assigned as PSu, three were previously classified as PSt and 142 as PM. The remaining 124 variants were not previously assigned evidence weights, and the LLRp calibration model now recognizes these variants as having supportive evidence of pathogenicity (PSu) (Figures 1D and 1E). This represents an increase in the number of variants present in the library, with abnormal membrane localization data supporting pathogenic evidence weights from 486 (17%) to 610 (22%).

Application of functional evidence to reclassify JAG1 VUSs

To measure the effect of applying these evidence weights during clinical variant classification, we ascertained a retrospective cohort of 46 individuals who received panel-based clinical genome testing. From this list, 39 unique JAG1 variants were reported, of which JAG1 MAVE data existed for 38 (Figure 2; Table S2). The majority of these variants (n = 29) were classified as VUSs, while the remainder were classified as either likely pathogenic (n = 6) or pathogenic (n = 3). Of the 29 variants originally classified as a VUS, MAVE data supported abnormal membrane localization for nine (31%) (Table 1). Data for six variants were recommended to be applied at an evidence weight of moderate for the PS3 criterion (ACMG/AMP classification code for data supporting abnormal protein function3), while data for the remaining three were recommended to be applied at an evidence weight of supporting (Table 1). When these data were incorporated during variant reclassification, six variants were upgraded from VUS to either pathogenic (n = 1) or likely pathogenic (n = 5) (Figure 3A). MAVE evidence for the remaining three was not enough to support a classification upgrade, and these variants remained VUSs.

Review of the six VUSs that were upgraded to pathogenic or likely pathogenic indicated that most of these had not been previously reported in individuals, or were reported as a VUS or with conflicting classifications,11 and were either not present, or were present at a very low allele count, in gnomAD21 (Table 1). Therefore, in the absence of the functional data applied here, these variants did not have enough evidence to support either pathogenicity or benignity. However, three VUSs were suspicious for pathogenicity before the addition of the MAVE data, with one variant, c.851G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Cys284Tyr), having been reported in an additional individual with ALGS1 since its original VUS classification22 (Table 1). These three variants all involve the loss of a cysteine within an epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domain (c.813C>G [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Cys271Trp], c.851G>A [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Cys284Tyr], and c.878G>A [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Cys293Tyr]), an alteration that has been widely recognized as disruptive to protein structure.8,10 Cysteines are critical components of EGF-like domains, where they are necessary to establish disulfide bonds that support proper folding, and cysteine loss has been associated with ALGS1.10,15 Cysteine loss has also been associated with pathogenic variants in NOTCH3 (MIM: 600276) (associated with CADASIL [MIM: 125310]) and Fibrillin-1 (FBN1; MIM: 134797) (associated with Marfan syndrome [MIM: 154700]).23,24,25 We have previously shown that the Cys271 residue is intolerant of tryptophan variation based on protein-folding modeling, which supports our MAVE data.8 To model the effects of the two tyrosine changes, we used AlphaFold226 to measure the RMSD within ±15 residues of the variant site. RMSD measures the distance between the backbone atoms (Cα) of the wild type and mutant, where a larger distance corresponds to a greater disruption of protein structure. Both c.851G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Cys284Tyr) and c.878G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Cys293Tyr) have RMSD values >1.5 Å, indicating effects on protein folding (Figure 4; Table S3). Consistent with this, stability predictions using FoldX19 and ROSETTA,20 computational tools that estimate the energetic impact of mutations on protein stability, indicate substantial destabilization for both variants, with ΔΔG values suggesting strong effects, particularly for the p.Cys293Tyr variant (Figure S1; Table S3). Together, these findings further validate the MAVE data for these three variants, supporting that they are likely to disrupt protein folding and substantiating their reclassification as likely pathogenic.

For the remaining 20 VUSs without abnormal MAVE data, five had data supporting indeterminate membrane localization and retained their original classification. For the 15 variants with MAVE data supporting normal membrane localization, evidence weights supporting benignity were not calculated. This is because successful Notch signaling requires both JAG1 cell surface localization and NOTCH2 binding; thus, the presence of JAG1 on the membrane is required, but not sufficient, for JAG1 function. Importantly, the JAG1 region of study (exons 1–7) encompasses the entire NOTCH2-binding domain and is enriched for variants with binding defects compared to the remainder of the protein.8,27 We reviewed the amino acid locations of all pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants included in the reference set that had a normal MAVE readout (n = 4). All four variants were adjacent to or within seven amino acids from a known or predicted NOTCH2-binding residue, indicating their likely involvement in a separate functional property that is not measured in this assay (Table S4). Given this context, we felt it was important to stratify variants as having LNorm or normal function rather than utilizing the ACMG-recognized evidence weights of benign moderate and benign supporting.

Although we did not calculate or apply evidence weight for variants with MAVE data supporting normal membrane localization, we did review population data from gnomAD for these 15 variants, which indicated that four (26.7%) had allele counts higher than expected for ALGS1 (c.5G>T [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Arg2Leu], c.74G>A [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Arg25Gln], c.506C>T [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Thr169Met], and c.925G>C [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Gly309Arg]) (Table S2). Based on this evidence, these four variants should be reclassified as likely benign, which is in concordance with their MAVE results (Figure 3A). Interestingly, of the four variants that were seen in more than one individual, one of them is included in this group (c.925G>C [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Gly309Arg], n = 4 probands), indicating that the higher representation for this variant in our cohort is likely accounted for by population frequency and not disease. The remaining three variants observed in more than one proband were also all present in gnomAD but at allele frequencies that were low enough to account for the incidence of ALGS1. For these three variants and the additional remaining eight VUSs with normal membrane localization data, more evidence is needed before reclassification can be considered.

Application of functional evidence for likely pathogenic and pathogenic JAG1 variants

We next reviewed MAVE evidence for the nine variants originally classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (Table S5). We were surprised to see that only four variants (44%) had data supporting abnormal membrane localization (n = 3 supporting, n = 1 moderate), consistent with their classification (Figure 3B; Table S5). Of the remaining five variants, four had data that were calibrated at an indeterminate evidence weight, and one had data that supported normal membrane localization. For three of these variants, the discordance between MAVE data and variant classification is likely due to the inability of the MAVE to identify abnormal functioning of the protein outside of membrane localization (Table S5). The c.755G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Arg252Lys) variant occurs at the last nucleotide of exon 5 and is predicted to affect splicing (SpliceAI28 and Pangolin29).30,31 Since this MAVE utilized a cDNA overexpression system, it is unable to identify effects caused by splice variants, and this effect would be missed. An additional two variants are located at c.806C>T (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Pro269Leu) or near c.241G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Glu81Lys), a NOTCH2-interacting residue, and it is possible that these changes interfere with NOTCH2 binding rather than membrane localization, which is not measured in this assay.8

A fourth variant (c.701G>A [GenBank: NM_000214.3] [p.Cys234Tyr]) occurs at the first cysteine in the first EGF-like repeat. We have previously shown that this residue is tolerant of tryptophan variation based on protein-folding modeling.8 Using AlphaFold2,26 we modeled the predicted structures for wild-type JAG1 and the c.701G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Cys234Tyr) mutant using local Cα RMSD within ±15 residues of the variant site. The c.701G>A (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Cys234Tyr) variant is predicted to have a very similar structure to wild-type JAG1 (<1.2 Å), while other cysteine-to-tyrosine changes within EGF-like domains have greater deviations, up to ∼5 Å, which is more supportive of protein misfolding (Figure 4; Table S3). Consistent with this, predictions using FoldX19 and ROSETTA20 indicate minimal changes in stability, with ΔΔG < 0, further confirming that this variant is unlikely to substantially disrupt protein folding (Figure S1; Table S3). These data suggest that cysteine loss at this residue may not be as damaging as loss of other EGF-like cysteines.8 Although not available for this study, phenotypic information from the individual in whom this variant was identified could be important in finalizing a classification decision.

The remaining variant, c.550C>T (GenBank: NM_000214.3) (p.Arg184Cys) is a known pathogenic variant that is expected to affect protein folding and impair membrane localization.10,14,32 It is noteworthy that the MAVE data for this variant support indeterminate rather than normal membrane localization and that the LLRp for this variant is just slightly above the cutoff for the PSu evidence-weight bin (Table S1). Although our model provides improved guidelines for classifying functional effects into evidence weights for clinical use, this highlights a limitation for those data that are at or near a bin threshold. It is possible that future improvements to either the model or the functional assays themselves could help to better refine these thresholds.

Discussion

High-throughput assays, such as MAVEs, are a powerful approach to provide functional information for variants at scale. The translation of a functional readout into a language that is accommodating to the clinical genomics community is needed to realize the full clinical potential of these assays. Calculation of LLRps enables direct translation of results into the ACMG/AMP classification framework.4,5 Here, we modeled a previously generated MAVE dataset measuring the membrane localization of JAG18 using this method, which improved the discrimination between our pathogenic and benign reference sets, resulting in an additional 124 abnormal variants with evidence supporting pathogenicity. With this calibration, we now provide recommended ACMG/AMP evidence weights for 610 abnormal variants. Using these evidence weights, we reanalyzed a cohort of 29 previously classified VUSs, resulting in the reclassification of six (21%) to likely pathogenic or pathogenic for ALGS1.

Increasingly, the field has recognized the need for calibration frameworks for variant classification that enable direct, transparent, and clinically actionable interpretation. Recent studies have begun extending these concepts to more scalable and generalized settings,6,7 highlighting growing momentum toward standardized analytic solutions for high-throughput functional assays. Notably, much of the existing MAVE literature relies on OddsPath and forms of the positive likelihood ratio, specifically the LLRp.4,5,33 This emphasis largely reflects the availability of clear, well-established guidance from the ClinGen SVI Working Group, which formalized the use of these metrics within the ACMG/AMP framework, building on earlier Bayesian formulations of variant classification.4 At their core, both LLRp and OddsPath provide direct, interpretable measures of evidence strength for pathogenicity, making them highly attractive for clinical translation. However, there are important distinctions in how these approaches incorporate and interpret data. OddsPath and early likelihood ratio approaches relied on calculating a global positive likelihood ratio, using a fixed score threshold.4,34 While straightforward, this is a coarse summary that does not support variant-level interpretation.33,35 Local positive likelihood ratios, which provide a variant-specific measure of evidence strength, address this limitation,33 allowing evidence to be assigned at the level of individual variants based on their exact score rather than via threshold groupings. Instead of replacing odds-based frameworks such as OddsPath, LLRp refines how evidence is quantified within them, moving from coarse, threshold-based summaries toward score-resolved, variant-level interpretation.

As with any analytical approach, there are limitations. Both the OddsPath and LLRp-based calibration frameworks rely on establishing decision cutoffs using known reference variants treated as truth. Therefore, evaluating them on the same reference set they were calibrated against is inherently circular. Regardless, we found increased utility in the new calibration framework. This framework improved concordance between experimental readout and known clinical classifications. This benefit is driven largely by the higher coverage of reference variants in the new analysis compared with the original analysis. Clinically, this translates to 20% more variants in the library being assigned definitive (non-indeterminate) functional evidence compared to the original calibration. Furthermore, the new model was designed to directly conform to ClinGen SVI Working Group guidelines for variant classification.4 We feel that these advantages outweigh the subtle reductions in sensitivity, specificity, and OddsPath and recommend using the new approach because of its improved overall accuracy and its ability to support retrospective interpretation of real-world clinical observations while conforming to ACMG guidelines. Ultimately, as high-throughput multiplexed assays become more standardized and widely adopted, calibration or alternative classification strategies will require benchmarking across multiple genes or genomic regions, ideally using sets of known variants that were not included during calibration to avoid circular evaluation.

Additional calibration refinement should also focus on more effectively integrating information across multiple lines of evidence (functional assays, structural predictions, evolutionary constraints, etc.). Point-based schemes have been proposed to aggregate evidence weights across these domains,36 offering a straightforward and interpretable way to combine disparate signals into a single classification. However, as we have shown using structural and stability prediction tools corroborating JAG1 functional effects, these evidence types are not independent and can encode overlapping information. In this setting, naive aggregation via additive point contributions can introduce unintended effects, including double counting, attenuation, or even cancellation of signals rather than a true increase in confidence. While point-based frameworks remain aligned with current ACMG guidance, these considerations highlight the need for approaches that more explicitly model dependence across evidence types, particularly as increasingly correlated datasets become available.

Although calibration is critical, the overall utility of a functional dataset depends on multiple elements.37 Understanding the biological and mechanistic relevance of a MAVE for disease is critical for clinical translation.1,18 In the Notch signaling pathway, JAG1 function requires both cell surface localization and binding to NOTCH2. As a result, membrane localization of JAG1 is required, but not sufficient, for normal function. The NOTCH2-binding region encompasses protein domains located within the N-terminal region of JAG1, including the C2-like domain, Delta/Serate/Lag2 (DLS) domain, and EGF-like repeats 2 and 3.27 Therefore, variants located in this region are more likely to affect binding than those located in more distal regions of the protein. This is particularly relevant to our MAVE, which utilized a variant library encompassing the entirety of this region.8,27 Of the four pathogenic variants in our reference set that had normal membrane localization, all are located adjacent to or near NOTCH2-binding residues. Therefore, while variants with abnormal membrane localization provide evidence toward pathogenicity, the converse is not necessarily true, and we expect a higher false negative rate for this region than for other regions of JAG1 when assayed for membrane localization. For this reason, we intentionally use the classifications of normal and LNorm rather than asserting explicit evidence for benignity, reflecting the biological and interpretive limits of the assay. Reports for MAVEs in other genes have recognized similar limitations. A MAVE measuring protein abundance of ASPA, a gene encoding an enzyme involved in myelin lipid synthesis, reports that not all pathogenic variants affect protein abundance, highlighting the potential for false positives and the importance of additional functional assays, such as testing enzyme activity.38 In recognition of this, many groups have performed multiple MAVEs to test various functional properties for genes to improve mechanistic resolution.39,40,41,42,43,44,45,46

In conclusion, we provide improved modeling of a previously characterized JAG1 MAVE dataset measuring membrane localization. This new model is better able to discriminate known pathogenic from known benign variants in a reference set, enabling the establishment of thresholds that allow for binning data into evidence-weight categories recognized by ACMG/AMP and useful to the clinical genomics community. In this analysis, we describe 610 of 2,832 (22%) variants with abnormal membrane localization, dividing them into strong (n = 1), moderate (n = 340), and supporting (n = 269) evidence weights for use in assessing variant pathogenicity. Although 1,416 variants are described as having normal function, we do not recommend these data to be used as evidence for benignity since additional functional properties, such as the ability to bind NOTCH2, were not assessed with this MAVE. Retrospective application of this evidence in a cohort of individuals with a JAG1 VUS resulted in the reclassification of 21% (n = 6) to pathogenic or likely pathogenic. We found that our JAG1 MAVE data, when appropriately translated into a language recognized by clinical genomic scientists, strongly impacts variant classifications and improves diagnostics for ALGS1. This approach represents an important step toward the systematic calibration of MAVEs and the assessment of their clinical utility. As these methods mature and converge on shared standards, they have the potential not only to substantially improve variant classification but also to reduce the burden of manual curation faced by clinical laboratories.
