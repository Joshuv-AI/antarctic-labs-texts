Man in his third decade with a giant coronary artery aneurysm
Authors: Weihao Chen, Cheng Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report details a rare instance of chronic rupture in a 27-year-old male with a giant coronary artery aneurysm (GCAA) in the mid-left anterior descending artery (mid-LAD). Presenting with 4-month chronic chest pain and 3-day hemoptysis, cardiac computed tomography angiography (CTA) and catheter angiography revealed a 3.8 cm × 2.5 cm aneurysm with adjacent 60% stenosis. Etiological workup excluded Kawasaki disease, infective endocarditis, vasculitis, and hereditary vasculopathies via negative serological, infectious, and genetic screening, suggesting an idiopathic origin with chronic inflammatory changes. Surgical management involved coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with saphenous vein and aneurysm resection, with no stent implantation attempted. The patient showed favorable recovery with 3-month postoperative follow-up, free of complications. Introduction

Giant coronary artery aneurysm (GCAA) is defined as a luminal diameter > 2 cm or ≥ 1.5 times the adjacent normal segment [1]. While childhood coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) often relate to Kawasaki disease, adult-onset GCAA in young patients (aged < 35 years) may involve idiopathic, inflammatory, or genetic etiologies. Chronic rupture of GCAA remains underreported, especially in individuals without typical risk factors. This case adds to the limited literature on GCAA rupture in young adults, emphasizing the need for comprehensive diagnostic workup and tailored surgical management [2].

Case presentation

Clinical presentation

A 27-year-old male presented with a 4-month history of intermittent retrosternal chest pain described as dull and exertion-exacerbated, accompanied by 3-day hemoptysis. He had no prior vascular risk factors, family history of cardiovascular disease, or childhood febrile illnesses (including no documented Kawasaki disease). Initial physical examination and electrocardiogram were unremarkable, with laboratory tests indicating mild anemia.

Diagnostic evaluation

Chest CT at initial presentation showed a 10.3 × 7.5 cm mixed-density mass adjacent to the left pulmonary artery, with patchy hyperdense areas suggesting hemorrhage; the lesion had unclear boundaries with the heart and adjacent vessels, and the mid-LAD was poorly visualized (Fig. 1A and B).

The lesion has unclear boundaries with the heart and adjacent vessels, and the left anterior descending artery (LAD) segment is poorly visualized. Adjacent left pulmonary artery branches show mild wall irregularity

Cardiac CTA confirmed a giant saccular aneurysm in the mid-LAD (3.8 cm×2.5 cm) with 60% luminal stenosis at the proximal neck, accompanied by a mediastinal hematoma (CT value 45 HU, indicative of organized blood) and significant compression of the main pulmonary artery(Fig. 2A and B). Catheter angiography was performed to further characterize the lesion, verifying a single mid-LAD aneurysm with patent distal LAD, circumflex, and right coronary artery, with no other aneurysmal changes (Fig. 3 and Video).

Etiological workup included serological tests negative for ANA, ANCA, ASO, and Kawasaki disease-related antibodies (anti-endothelial cell antibodies, anti-cardiolipin antibodies); inflammatory markers were normal (ESR 12 mm/h, CRP 0.8 mg/L). Blood cultures (×3) and procalcitonin were negative, and transesophageal echocardiogram showed no vegetations, excluding infective endocarditis. Genetic screening for Marfan syndrome (FBN1 gene), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (COL3A1 gene), and other hereditary vasculopathies yielded no abnormalities. Pathological analysis of the aneurysm wall revealed vascular fibrosis and chronic inflammatory infiltration.

Surgical intervention

Under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB time: 85 min, aortic cross-clamp time: 42 min), the patient underwent saphenous vein grafting to the mid-LAD (end-to-side anastomosis with 7 − 0 Prolene), resection of the aneurysm, and debridement of the mediastinal hematoma, followed by pericardial patch reinforcement at the resection site. Stent implantation was not attempted due to the aneurysm size exceeding 25 mm (risk of stent-graft mismatch), concurrent proximal stenosis requiring combined revascularization, and fibrotic nature of the aneurysm wall, which favored surgical resection for mechanical stability [3].

Postoperative follow-up

The patient was discharged 7 days postoperatively with no perioperative complications (e.g., arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, or bleeding). At 3-month follow-up, he reported no chest pain or hemoptysis; postoperative CTA showed patent saphenous vein graft, resolved mediastinal hematoma, and no recurrent aneurysm.

Discussion

The differential diagnosis of GCAA in young patients included Kawasaki disease, which was excluded based on the absence of childhood history and negative serological markers (e.g., anti-endothelial cell antibodies) [4]. Infectious vasculitis was ruled out by normal inflammation indices, negative cultures, and absence of vegetations on echocardiogram. Genetic disorders were excluded through negative screening for FBN1 and COL3A1 mutations. Idiopathic etiology, accounting for approximately 30% of adult GCAA cases, is the most plausible diagnosis here, supported by the patient’s negative workup and pathological findings of chronic inflammation [4].

The adjacent mid-LAD stenosis likely induced turbulent blood flow, increasing wall shear stress on the aneurysm, as supported by prior computational fluid dynamics studies [5]. The mediastinal hematoma with a CT value of 45 HU, MRI T1WI hyperintensities, and fibrotic aneurysm wall indicated a gradual rupture process over weeks to months, consistent with the patient’s subacute clinical presentation. This chronic rupture mechanism differs from acute ruptures typically associated with cardiac tamponade, highlighting the importance of integrating imaging findings with symptom onset duration for accurate characterization [6].

The initial complex presentation underscores that multi-modality imaging is critical: chest CT identified the mediastinal mass and hemorrhage, CTA detailed the aneurysm-artery relationship, and catheter angiography confirmed the single-vessel involvement. Surgical CABG with aneurysm resection provided definitive revascularization and mechanical stabilization, aligning with guidelines for complex GCAA in young patients where endovascular approaches may be suboptimal. The favorable 3-month follow-up further supports the efficacy of this surgical strategy.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare presentation of idiopathic GCAA chronic rupture in a young adult, highlighting the need for multi-modality imaging (CT, CTA, and catheter angiography) in diagnosis and surgical intervention in management. The negative etiological workup and favorable postoperative course contribute to understanding the natural history and treatment of idiopathic GCAA in young populations.
