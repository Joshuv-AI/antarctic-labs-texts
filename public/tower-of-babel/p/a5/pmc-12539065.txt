Different detection rates of brain metastasis in different pathological types of lung cancer by 18F-FAPI PET/CT
Authors: Haoqian Li, Pei Li, Shouhui Zhu, Wenhong Hou, Wei Zhao, Qingxi Yu, Jingjie Qin, Xudong Hu, Yuchun Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the utility of fluorine-18-fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (18F-FAPI) PET/CT for detecting brain metastasis (BM) in different pathological types of lung cancer using craniocerebral MRI as the standard.MethodsFrom December 2020 to October 2021, patients with pathologically confirmed lung cancer and suspected BM were prospectively enrolled and underwent paired 18F-FAPI PET/CT and MRI. The number of BMs and maximum tumor diameter were measured by MRI. The maximum and peak standardized uptake values (SUVmax and SUVpeak, respectively) and tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) on 18F‐FAPI PET/CT in BMs were evaluated.ResultsA total of 76 BM lesions from 18 patients (11 males and 7 females) were evaluated. Among these, 23 lesions were detected by 18F-FAPI PET/CT. The detection rate of BM in adenocarcinoma was 48.28%, which was significantly higher than that in large cell carcinoma (16.67%, P = 0.016) and small cell carcinoma (0%, P = 0.009), but showed no significant difference from that in squamous carcinoma (35.71%, P = 0.437). The detection rate in squamous carcinoma was significantly higher than that in small cell carcinoma (P = 0.043), while no significant differences were observed between large cell carcinoma and small cell carcinoma (P = 0.183), or between large cell carcinoma and squamous carcinoma (P = 0.191).ConclusionsThis study revealed differences in the detection rates of BM in lung cancer types by 18F-FAPI PET/CT, with the highest and lowest detection rates in adenocarcinoma and small cell carcinoma, respectively, which may be valuable for predicting the prognosis of lung cancer patients with BM.Trial registrationInstitutional review board approval NO. Background

Lung cancer is the world’s second most deadly malignant tumor with the second highest incidence rate, accounting for 24% of all cancer deaths [1]. Brain metastasis (BM) is one of the main causes of death for lung cancer patients. About 8%–25% of lung cancer patients have BM at the time of diagnosis, and 45%–50% of patients have BM at some point in the course of disease progression [2].

At present, MRI is commonly used in the clinical diagnosis of BM and offers a high detection rate, but the information provided by this imaging method is limited to anatomical structures and contains no biological data. BM tissue is difficult to obtain by puncture, which inhibits the analysis of its biological characteristics [3]. The lack of biological data for BMs is one of the key reasons for the poor treatment effect and short survival time observed in patients with BM [4]. PET/CT is a relatively newer imaging approach that combines functional metabolic imaging (PET) with anatomical imaging (CT). With the use of different imaging agents, PET/CT can show biological heterogeneity within tumor tissue. For example, fluorine-18-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/CT has become a common screening method for tumor metastasis, except in the case of BM. This is because the inherent characteristics of cerebral glucose metabolism, combined with the lack of obvious 18F-FDG uptake in some small lesions, result in its low specificity for detecting BM [5]. Therefore, a new functional imaging approach is needed for improving the diagnosis of BM and will be of great significance to the treatment and prognosis of patients.

Fibroblast-activating protein (FAP), a membrane-anchored peptidase belonging to the dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) family, has been shown to be expressed on the surface of tumor cells, interstitial cells, and tumor-associated macrophages in more than 90% of epithelial tumors at much higher levels that those observed in normal human tissues [6]. FAP inhibitor (FAPI) binds specifically to FAP and the radioisotope-labeled FAPI PET/CT scan allows to pinpoint FAP expression in the tumor microenvironment, offering several unique advantages in brain tumor imaging. Multiple studies have shown that 68Ga- or 18F-labeled FAPI-PET/CT provides a high tumor to background ratio (TBR) in BM on imaging [7–9].

In our hospital we observed that not all BMs are detected by 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging and the this different detection rate varies, also according to different lung cancer types. With a prospective study, we evaluated and analyzed the clinical utility of 18F-FAPI PET/CT for detecting BMs of different pathological types of lung cancer, using craniocerebral MRI as the standard imaging.

Methods

Patients

Patients were enrolled in this prospective study according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) Primary or recurrent lung cancer confirmed by biopsy and histopathology and BM confirmed by cranial MRI; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) ECOG performance status of 0–1; and (4) voluntary participation in the study. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) pregnancy; (2) inability to tolerate 18F-FAPI PET/CT or MRI examination; and (3) inability or unwillingness of the patient, patient’s parent or patient’s legal representative to provide written informed consent for participation in the study.

From December 2020 to October 2021, a total of 23 lung cancer patients were selected for inclusion in the final analysis. Our investigation was conducted under the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration (unproven interventions in clinical practice) guidelines. This prospective study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to their inclusion in the study.

Compound synthesis and radiochemistry NOTA (1,4,7-triazacyclononane-N, N’,N″-triacetic acid)-FAPI was labeled with 18F using a synthesis process previously described in reference [10]. The chemical structure of 18F-FAPI was consistent with the literature [10]. 18F-FDG was synthesized by the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Shandong Cancer Hospital.

18F‑FAPI PET/CT scanning

Neither fasting nor glucose measurement was required or requested prior to the imaging examinations. The patients were given an intravenous injection of 4.81 MBq/kg (0.12 mCi/kg) 18F-FAPI and allowed to rest for approximately 60 min before imaging. Scans were acquired using an integrated online PET/CT system (Gemini TF Big Bore; Philips Healthcare, Cleveland, OH, USA). A low-dose CT scan (300 mAs, 120 kV, 512 × 512 matrix, rotation time of 1.0 s, pitch index of 0.688, soft-tissue kernel reconstruction) was used for attenuation correction. PET data were acquired in three-dimensional mode with a 200 × 200 matrix and 1-min imaging time per bed position. After random, decay, and scatter correction, the data were reconstructed using Body-CTAC-SB. Lstcln, Biograph 3D iterative reconstruction software with TOF correction. During the image acquisition process, patients continued with normal shallow breathing.

MRI protocols

All of the patients were imaged in the supine head-first position on a 3.0T system (Magnetom Verio, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a sixteen-channel head and neck combined phased-array surface coil. Axial T2-weighted (T 2 W) turbo spin-echo imaging was performed (repetition time [TR], 3000 msec; echo time [TE], 102 msec; section thickness, 5 mm; field of view [FOV], 38 cm; matrix, 320 3 224). Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) was performed using the single-shot echo-planar imaging technique (b = 0 and b = 800 s/mm2; TR, 6300; TE, 75 msec; section thickness, 5 mm; FOV, 38 cm; matrix, 128 3 102); the center of the slice groups was the same as those used for the axial T2W images. ADC maps were computed on a workstation system (Syngo Multimodality Workplace, Siemens).

Imaging analysis

CT transmission data were used for attenuation correction of the images. Attenuation-corrected PET images, CT images, and fused PET/CT images were viewed and analyzed on a nuclear medicine information system (Beijing Mozi Healthcare, Ltd., Beijing, China), displayed in coronal, sagittal, and transverse planes. Two experienced board-certified nuclear medicine physicians visually assessed the 18F-FAPI PET/CT images and reached a consensus on the image interpretation for primary and metastatic tumors. The location and intensity of 18F-FAPI uptake within tumor tissue were recorded.

Radiographic findings consistent with tumor characteristics were defined as a positive finding, compared to background uptake and anatomical MRI imaging. For controversial tumor lesions, two physicians discussed these findings, with consideration of the findings of MRI imaging studies, and reached an agreement.

A single nuclear medicine physician quantified tumor tracer uptake based on the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and peak standardized uptake value (SUVpeak) at 1-hour post-injection. To calculate SUVmax and SUVpeak, a circular region of interest (ROI) was drawn around the tumor lesion, with focal uptake increasing on transverse sections and automatically adapting to a three-dimensional volume with a 30% isocontour. Lesions for which 18F-FAPI uptake was higher within the lesion than in the surrounding tissue were considered positive for suspected malignancy. The TBR represented the difference in tracer uptake between the lesion and background. Each ROI was drawn along the edge of a lesion on axial PET images and automatically adapted to a three-dimensional volume at the 60% isocontour for measuring SUVmax. The TBR was calculated by dividing the lesion’s SUVmax by the mean SUV of the background (brain background for brain lesions).

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs), and nonnormally distributed data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The detection rates of BM in different pathological types of lung cancer by 18F-FAPI PET/CT were analyzed by chi-square test. 18F-FAPI PET/CT uptake was compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and correlations were analyzed using the Spearman test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristic

From December 2020 to October 2021, 23 patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of lung cancer and suspected BM at Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute were enrolled in this study. Five patients were later excluded, including 1 patient who underwent BM resection surgery. 2 patients for whom no BMs were found on MRI examination, and 2 patients for whom only meningeal metastasis was found in MRI. Finally, a total of 18 patients with 76 brain metastatic lesions were evaluated (Fig. 1). The proportion of patients with brain metastases detected by 18F-FAPI PET/CT was 66.67% (12/18). Detection rate of brain metastases in patients with different pathological types of lung cancer by 18F-FAPI PET/CT

A total of 76 BM lesions were detected by MRI in 18 patients. Of these 76 lesions, 23 were detected by 18F-FAPI PET/CT, and 53 were not. According to the pathological type of lung cancer, the BM detection rates in adenocarcinoma (ADC), squamous carcinoma (SCC), large cell carcinoma, and small cell carcinoma by 18F-FAPI PET/CT were 48.28% (14/29), 35.71% (5/14), 16.67% (4/24), and 0% (0/9) (Table 2). Figure 2 showed that only large cell carcinoma showed a significantly greater diameter compared to the other histological types, while no statistically significant differences were observed among the remaining types. The diameters of the detected metastatic tumors were significantly larger than those that were not detected, 10.01 TQR (7.28–14.48) vs. 18.97 TQR (13.29-25.00), P < 0.0001. Whether detected was moderately positively correlated with tumor diameter (r = 0.465, P < 0.0001).

Correlation between tumor diameter and 18F-FAPI uptake of BMs

On 18F-FAPI PET/CT, the median diameters of the detected BM lesions were 19.62 mm (12.33–23.94 mm) in ADC cases, 15.26 mm (13.84–25.01 mm) in SCC cases, and 22.91 mm (18.57–28.38 mm) in large cell carcinoma cases. These diameters of BMs on 18F-FAPI PET/CT did not differ significantly among the different pathological types of lung cancer (P = 0.42).

The SUVmax of 18F-FAPI in BMs showed a moderate positive correlation with tumor diameter (r = 0.42, P = 0.04) (Fig. 5A), whereas the SUVpeak and TBR showed no significant correlation with tumor diameter (r = 0.39, P = 0.07; r = 0.29, P = 0.18) (Fig. 5B and C).

Discussion

In this prospective study, 18F-FAPI PET/CT showed better detection accuracy for BM in ADC and SCC compared with other pathological types of lung cancer, and poor detection accuracy in large cell carcinoma and small cell lung cancer, which may help guide prognosis prediction for patients with BM of lung cancer.

In recent years, studies have shown that amino acid PET/CT brain imaging exhibits low physiological uptake in the brain and a better tumor-to-background ratio, with the potential for diagnostic accuracy [11]. Unlike 18F-FAPI, amino acids, as PET imaging agents, specifically target amino acids highly expressed in brain metastases for disease imaging [12]. That is to say, different imaging targets reveal different biological characteristics of brain tumors. Distinguishing between treatment-related changes and disease progression is a current clinical challenge and difficulty. Castello A. et al. reported that O-(2-[18F]F-fluoroethyl)-L-tyrosine ([18F]-FET) PET/CT is an artificial amino acid characterized by transient incorporation into cells and can visualize brain metastases from acute myeloid leukemia and provide accurate imaging guidance for puncture [13]. Unterrainer et al. reported that [18F]-FET has good diagnostic value for brain metastases from lung cancer [14]. 3,4-dihydroxy-6-[18F]F-L-phenylalanine ([18F]-DOPA), an amino acid-related compound labeled with [18F], can be used to distinguish radionecrosis from tumor progression in brain metastases after stereotactic radiotherapy [15]. The application of 18F-FAPI PET/CT is novel and innovative. Technically, it allows assessment of both the whole body and the brain using a single tracer, which is not feasible with most amino acid tracers. Certainly, further research is warranted to explore its guiding role in stereotactic radiosurgery and its value in evaluating treatment efficacy. The combined use of new amino acid or 18F-FAPI PET imaging with MRI may have better clinical application value in terms of biological characteristics, activity assessment, and efficacy evaluation for patients with brain tumors.

18F-FAPI PET/CT produced different detection rates for BM of lung ADC, SCC, large cell carcinoma and small cell carcinoma. Studies have confirmed that 18F or 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT is superior to 18F-FDG PET/CT for the diagnosis of primary tumors, lymph node metastases, bone metastases and liver metastases [8, 9, 16]. Several studies have reported that 18F or 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT can also be used for BM imaging [17, 18], but the accuracy of this imaging technique for BM detection has not been reported. In the present study, with the finding on craniocerebral MRI as the standard, the accuracy of 18F-FAPI PET/CT for detecting BM was compared among different pathological types of lung cancer for the first time. Several case reports [19, 20] have indicated that 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT shows higher sensitivity in detecting brain metastases. The uptake of ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI in brain metastases exhibited considerable variability, with nearly half of the lesions (11 out of 23) demonstrating low tracer uptake (SUVmax 2.5) [9]. To date, no reports have described the use of any radiolabeled FAPI PET/CT for the diagnosis rate of brain metastases in small cell lung cancer. Previous research found that the incidence of BM in small cell lung cancer is higher than that in non-small cell lung cancer, and that 30%–40% of non-small cell lung cancer cases have BM [21]. However, the incidence of BM in patients with a higher malignant degree of small cell lung cancer was 10% at the first visit and as high as 40–50% during diagnosis and treatment [22]. However, the present study found that the detection rate of BM by 18F-FAPI PET/CT in non-small cell lung cancer was higher than that in small cell lung cancer, indicating that most BM lesions in cases of small cell lung cancer did not express FAP or had low expression of FAP.

For BMs detected by 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging, no difference in 18F-FAPI uptake was observed according to the pathological type of lung cancer. In this study, BMs can be divided into two types: detectable by 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging and not detectable by 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging. In other words, the expression level of FAP did not differ in the BMs. FAP is expressed in macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts and the tumor cell membrane and cytoplasm [23–29], and plays a very important role in tumorigenesis, development and treatment.

The SUVmax of BMs was positively correlated with the maximum tumor diameter, while TBR and SUVpeak showed no statistically significant correlation with tumor size. During the progression of metastatic tumors, the tumor microenvironment undergoes dynamic changes, particularly in the interstitial components that support and protect tumor cells [30]. These changes may partly explain the observed relationship between 18F-FAPI uptake and tumor diameter.

No correlation was observed between gene mutation and 18F-FAPI imaging of BM in this study. About 40%–50% of ADC cases are complicated by gene mutation, and gene mutation is an important factor affecting the prognosis of patients. In patients who harbor epidermal growth factor receptor mutations, the reported incidence rate of BM has reached as high as 60% [31], and targeted therapy can significantly improve survival for patients with these mutations [32]. Kraman et al. reported that FAP-expressing stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment can prevent immune cells from attacking cancer cells, and destroying the FAP-expressing stromal cells can cause tumors to lose their resistance to the immune system, suggesting that FAP-positive stromal cells play an important role in immunosuppression [25]. Hu et al. found that high 18F-FAPI uptake is associated with poor efficacy of simultaneous radiotherapy and chemotherapy in patients with esophageal cancer [33]. Therefore, the prognosis of patients with gene mutations and BMs detected by 18F-FAPI imaging is also worthy of further exploration.

The present study has some limitations. First, the number of participants included in this study was relatively small, which leaves statistical uncertainty. Second, only patients with lung ADC underwent genetic analysis, while the patients with the other pathological types of lung cancer did not. Thus, the correlation between 18F-FAPI PET/CT BM imaging and gene mutation in more types of lung cancer requires further exploration. Third, because is difficult to obtain biopsy tissue of BMs, histopathological examination cannot be carried out to verify the expression level of FAP in BMs from different pathological types of lung cancer.

Conclusion

In this study, 18F-FAPI PET/CT produced different BM detection rates for different pathological types of lung cancer. Therefore, it is worthwhile and of far-reaching clinical significance to carry out related studies to explore the differences in prognosis between lung cancer patients with BMs that are or are not detectable on 18F-FAPI PET/CT.
