Isopropyl paraben targets type III secretion to inhibit Salmonella enterica serovar typhimurium infection
Authors: Jinli Ge, Xueyu Li, Qian Lu, Siqi Li, Jiayang Liu, Xuming Deng, Hongtao Liu, Jiazhang Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSalmonella enterica, a food- and water-borne pathogen, triggers food poisoning and enteric infections. The effectiveness of antibiotics against Salmonella infections is decreasing due to bacterial resistance. Developing novel antimicrobial agents is crucial and urgent. Here, we screened 550 natural compounds and found that isopropyl paraben (IPPB) effectively inhibited Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (ST) invasion of host cells without impacting bacterial growth, reducing the risk of developing bacterial resistance. Further investigation revealed that IPPB bound to the regulatory protein HilD and inhibited the transcription of the type III protein secretion system 1 (T3SS–1) regulatory genes hilD, hilC and rtsA. This interference blocked the production of T3SS–1 effectors. Importantly, IPPB exerted certain therapeutic effects on both G. mellonella larvae and murine models infected with ST and improved the ST-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis in mice. These findings suggest that IPPB has potential as a novel antimicrobial agent, targeting the T3SS–1 of Salmonella.KEYWORDS: Salmonella, isopropyl paraben, invasion, T3SS-1 inhibitor, anti-virulence

Introduction

Salmonella enterica is widely distributed and transmitted through foodborne routes and is capable of infecting both humans and animals, resulting in a range of diseases, including foodborne gastroenteritis and typhoid fever [1,2]. The annual mortality rate attributed to Salmonella infection-induced food poisoning has exhibited an upward trend in global incidents of foodborne illnesses [2,3]. Salmonella species are ubiquitously distributed and frequently reside in a variety of foods, such as vegetables, eggs and poultry products. To date, a variety of Salmonella serotypes have been identified, with Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium, ST) emerging as the predominant serotype isolated from food sources [4–7]. In recent years, the issue of Salmonella contamination in poultry products has become increasingly pressing, exacerbated by the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains resulting from the overuse of antibiotics. Developing novel anti-infective strategies for the prevention and management of clinically resistant bacterial infections is imperative, given the waning efficacy of antibiotics in treating these infections [8,9].

The development of novel antimicrobial agents is crucial but time-consuming and costly. Anti-virulence strategies offer an effective approach to address the therapeutic challenges posed by antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections [10,11]. Anti-virulence strategies typically target nonessential bacterial life processes that are less crucial for growth, thereby exerting minimal selective pressure on bacteria and reducing the likelihood of inducing resistance. Such approaches can also effectively preserve the integrity of the intestinal microbial flora [12,13]. The virulence of Salmonella depends on its ability to deliver effectors into host cells through its 2 type III protein secretion systems upon interaction with host cells. These effectors interfere with normal host cell signaling, facilitating invasion of host cells and intracellular proliferation of Salmonella [14–17]. The T3SS–1 encoded by Salmonella pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) plays a crucial role in Salmonella invasion of host cells [18]. The secreted effectors, such as Sips (SipA, SipB, SipC and SipD), influence various stages of membrane ruffling and actin cytoskeleton rearrangement, thereby increasing the efficiency of Salmonella entry into host cells [19,20]. Additionally, Sops (SopA, SopB, SopD, SopD2, SopE and SopE2) are involved in controlling various stages of polymorphonuclear leukocyte influx and cytoskeletal rearrangement [21–23]. The Salmonella T3SS–1 has emerged as an optimal target for the development of anti-virulence inhibitors against Salmonella infection. In recent years, the development of T3SS–1 inhibitors has become a trend. Cytosporone B (Csn-B) is a compound isolated from fungi that inhibits the expression of SPI-1 effectors [24]. Cinnamaldehyde, an aldehyde organic compound, has been found to inhibit the expression of key effectors such as SipA, SipB and SipC in ST [25]. Hyperoside (HYP) inhibits the activity of the Salmonella T3SS needle protein InvG, thereby preventing host cell damage and conferring protection to mice against ST [26]. Although some inhibitors targeting the T3SS have been reported, they still do not fully meet the needs for the prevention and control of Salmonella infection.

Using a gentamicin protection assay, we found that isopropyl paraben (IPPB) inhibited ST invasion of host cells. This study was the first to demonstrate that IPPB acts as an anti-virulence agent specifically targeting the T3SS–1 of Salmonella. The therapeutic efficacy of IPPB against ST infection was assessed utilizing a murine infection model. Taken together, these data suggest that IPPB may be a potential novel antibacterial candidate for the treatment of Salmonella infections, providing evidence-based support for its clinical use.

Results

IPPB, MPB and IBPB inhibit ST invasion into HeLa cells

In this study, we first conducted a comprehensive screen of 550 natural compounds to identify potent small molecules that can effectively inhibit the invasion of ST into HeLa cells by employing a gentamicin protection assay. IPPB (Figure 1(A)), a preservative, was identified to dramatically weaken the invasion of HeLa cells by ST (Figure 1(B-C)). The infected cells were subjected to immunostaining using a Salmonella-specific antibody, and the uptake of bacteria by the host cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner upon incubation with increasing concentrations of IPPB (Figure 1(B)). Since methyl paraben (MPB) and isobutyl paraben (IBPB) are structural analogs of IPPB (Figure 1(A)), we also tested the effects of MPB and IBPB on the invasion of ST into HeLa cells. Similar inhibitory effects on ST invasion were obtained for MPB and IBPB (Figure S1A-B). We also assessed the impacts of IPPB, MPB and IBPB on bacterial invasion of HeLa cells by performing a gentamicin protection assay. Pretreating the bacteria with 8 μg/mL IPPB for 4 hours prior to infection resulted in a reduction of 30% in the invasion rate (Figure 1(C)), and 8 μg/mL of both MPB and IBPB also suppressed the invasion rate of ST to approximately 77% and 82% (Figure S1C-D). As expected, HeLa cells challenged with the T3SS–1-defective mutant ΔinvA showed little bacterial invasion in both assays. We also found that IPPB, MPB, and IPBP inhibit the invasion of ST into Raw264.7 cells. Notably, 32 μg/mL IPPB and 64 μg/mL MPB and IPBP exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect on the invasion (Figure S2A-C). Due to the active phagocytic nature of Raw264.7 cells, the invasion rate of ST, as indicated by ΔinvA, was elevated (Figure S2A-C). Moreover, IPPB, MPB and IPBP did not affect the intracellular replication of ST in HeLa and Raw264.7 cells (Figure 1(D), S2D-H). The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of IPPB and IBPB against ST were 512 μg/mL and 1024 μg/mL, respectively, and the concentrations of MPB were greater than 2048 µg/mL (Figure 1(E), S1E). Furthermore, these three compounds had no impact on the growth of ST (Figure 1(F), S1F-G). These findings indicate that IPPB, MPB and IBPB hinder bacterial entry into HeLa cells but do not influence bacterial viability or growth.

IPPB, MPB and IBPB are T3SS–1 inhibitors of ST

The injectisome and effectors of T3SS–1 are known to primarily induce the rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton in host cells, leading to membrane ruffling. This process aids in the invasion of nonphagocytic cells by bacteria during infection [27,28]. We detected the levels of secreted proteins in the supernatant using Coomassie brilliant blue staining and western blotting to further explore the effect of IPPB on the function of the T3SS–1. The application of 8 μg/mL IPPB resulted in a significant decrease in the concentrations of the T3SS–1 effectors (SipA, SipB, SipC and SopB) within the liquid medium, while having no impact on the secretion and expression of the protein FliC (Figure 2(A-B), S4A). Moreover, significant inhibition of SipA and SipB effectors secretion was observed upon exposure to 64 μg/mL MPB and IBPB (Figure S3A-B, S4B-C). We further substantiated the aforementioned findings by employing the β-lactamase translocation reporter system to detect SipA secretion. The results showed that when HeLa cells were infected with ST expressing SipA-β-lactamase (TEM), SipA-TEM was transported into HeLa cells. However, coincubation of ST with IPPB, MPB and IBPB notably inhibited SipA-TEM translocation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2(C-D), S3C-F). Furthermore, the SipA-TEM expressed by the ΔinvA mutant strain was unable to translocate SipA into HeLa cells. These results provide evidence that IPPB, MPB and IBPB significantly inhibit the secretion of ST T3SS–1 effectors.

IPPB suppresses the transcription of key SPI-1 genes in ST, hindering the expression of T3SS–1 effectors

To elucidate the mechanism of by which IPPB inhibits the translocation of ST T3SS–1 effectors, the expression levels of the plasmid-derived SipA-TEM and endogenous SipA and SipB were assessed by western blotting. The results showed that the expression of these proteins was significantly inhibited by IPPB in a dose-dependent manner with the inhibitory effect persisting for at least 48 h (Figure 3(A-B), S5), whereas the inhibitory effects of MPB and IBPB were relatively modest (Figure S6A-D).

The expression of the T3SS–1 effectors is manipulated by an intricate regulatory network (Figure 3(C)). We assessed the transcriptional levels of T3SS–1 effector-encoding genes using real time RT–qPCR to further determine the mechanism of IPPB action. The mRNA levels of sipA, sipB and sipC in the 64 μg/mL IPPB were reduced approximately 5-fold compared with the DMSO group (Figure 3(D)). In addition, compared to the untreated control, the mRNA levels of T3SS–1 component genes, including prgH, prgI, prgK and invG, were significantly reduced by 5.9-, 21-, 13- and 30-fold, respectively (Figure 3(E)). These findings prompted us to examine the impact of IPPB on the T3SS–1 regulatory genes. Hence, we assessed the transcript levels of hilA, hilC, hilD and rtsA in ST following an incubation with IPPB. Remarkably, IPPB dose-dependently suppressed the transcription of these genes (Figure 3(F)). When treated with 64 μg/mL IPPB, the transcription of hilA, hilC, hilD and rtsA decreased approximately 2.1-, 1.9-, 2.2- and 4.1-fold, respectively. Given the cross-talk between SPI1 and SPI2 [29], we also examined the transcriptional levels of SPI2-related genes in ST following treatment with IPPB. The data showed that IPPB did not affect the transcription of SPI2-related genes (Figure S7). These results indicate that IPPB reduces the production of T3SS–1 component and effectors by inhibiting the transcription of the regulatory genes.

Analysis of binding between IPPB and HilD

The gene expression of SPI1 T3SS is governed by three homologous transcriptional regulators, namely HilD, HilC and RtsA [30]. Among these proteins, HilD acts as the primary regulator, while HilC and RtsA function as amplifiers of HilD. Additionally, HilD also undergoes self-regulation [31,32]. We constructed a recombinant plasmid for the overexpression of HilD and subsequently introduced it into ST. Following the treatment of ST (pJL03-hilD) with increasing concentrations of IPPB, the inhibition of SipA was restored with the induction of HilD derived from the plasmid (Figure 4(A)). This finding suggests that IPPB is likely to interact with HilD. The fluorescence quenching assay provides a distinct approach for investigating the potential interaction between IPPB and HilD. After an incubation of purified HilD and commercial bovine serum albumin (BSA) with 4 to 32 μg/mL IPPB, the change in the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of HilD and BSA was measured. As shown in Figure 4(B), the excitation wavelength of HilD was 220 nm, and its emission wavelength was 280 nm. Thus, it could produce the strongest fluorescence at 310 nm, which gradually decreased after the peak, until the fluorescence disappeared above 400 nm. The intrinsic fluorescence intensity of HilD decreased continuously with increasing IPPB concentrations (Ka = 6.157x10−4 L/mol). However, the incubation of BSA with diverse doses of IPPB does not alter the fluorescence intensity of BSA (Figure 4(C)). In addition, the interaction between IPPB and HilD in ST was confirmed by drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay. Treatment of ST (pJL03-hilD) with increasing concentrations of IPPB resulted in a significant decrease in the susceptibility of HilD proteolysis (Figure 4(D)). These data establish that IPPB could specifically bind HilD. Based on the results above, we investigated whether IPPB affected the binding of HilD to the promoter. We employed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using recombinant His6-HilD and relevant promoter probes to investigate this possibility (Figure 4(E-F)). In the absence of IPPB, HilD effectively interacted with the hilA and hilD promoters, as evidenced by the formation of HilD – promoter complexes. However, the addition of IPPB to the reaction did not affect HilD binding to the DNA probes, as observed from the gel wherein the number of HilD – DNA complexes did not decrease. These data suggest that IPPB binds to HilD but does not affect its ability to bind to the hilA and hilD promoters.

IPPB can protect G. mellonella larvae from infection with ST

G. mellonella larvae have been used for complex studies of host – microbe interactions [33]. Hence, the effect of IPPB treatment on ST infection was comprehensively evaluated. A diagrammatic representation of the basic methods used in G. mellonella–pathogen infection studies is shown in Figure 5(A). The larvae were determined to be dead when they did not respond to stimulation, and specific black nodules or disseminated dark pigmentation covered the entire body of the larvae (Figure 5(B)). When infected with ST, all G. mellonella larvae were dead at 36 h (Figure 5(C)). In contrast, 40% of G. mellonella larvae treated with IPPB (250 ug/kg) were alive at 72 h post-infection. Histopathological changes in G. mellonella larvae were investigated by performing H&E staining of sections. Notably, the histopathological analysis revealed submucosal edema in the WT-infected group, and a large number of exfoliated goblet cells and intestinal villi appeared in the gut. However, the IPPB-treated group showed mild submucosal edema, which was similar to that of the PBS-treated group (Figure 5(D)). These results show that IPPB protects G. mellonella from infection with ST.

IPPB can protect ST-infected mice

The potent inhibitory activity of IPPB against ST invasion in HeLa cells prompted us to investigate its potential for systemic protection. We established a murine model to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of IPPB against ST infection. At day 8 post infection, the mortality rate of the ST-infected mice reached 100%. In contrast, the infected mice treated with IPPB (25 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days presented a survival rate of 30% (Figure 6(A)). Mice in the infection control group exhibited prolonged body weight loss as the experiments progressed. As expected, the IPPB-treated group gained weight compared to the infection control group (Figure 6(B)). A comprehensive evaluation of the effects of IPPB treatment on pathological lesions in the intestinal tract and other organs resulting from ST infection was conducted. At 5 days post-infection, the infection control group exhibited congestion and edema with reduced contents upon gross inspection of the mouse cecum, while these changes were alleviated in the IPPB treatment group (Figure 6(C)). Moreover, histopathological analysis revealed central vein congestion in the livers of the infection control mice, which was accompanied by a large number of necrotic and inflammatory foci and neutrophil infiltration. The spleen showed infiltration of inflammatory cells and obvious necrotic foci. The cecum displayed submucosal edema, with a significant accumulation of exfoliative necrotic epithelial cells and intestinal villi in the lumen (Figure 6(D)). However, IPPB treatment alleviated pathological damage to the spleen, liver and cecum (Figure 6(D)). Compared with the infection control group, the IPPB-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in the bacterial load in both the liver and spleen (Figure 6(E)). Taken together, these results indicate that IPPB effectively protects mice from ST infection.

Effects of IPPB on gut microbiome in ST-infected mice

To determine the impact of IPPB on the gut microbiota structure following ST infection, we conducted 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to analyze the gut microbiota composition within the intestinal contents. Alpha diversity is an indicator of richness, diversity and evenness. The results showed that the Chao1, Dominance, Goods coverage, Shannon and Simpson indices of the gut microbiota were not significantly different among the control, IPPB, ST and ST+IPPB groups, indicating that the variation of the microbial community was not significantly altered in each group (Figure 7(A)). The Venn diagram showed that the control group shared more common operational taxonomic units (OUT) with ST and ST+IPPB groups than the IPPB group. This discrepancy may be attributed to the reduced OTU within the IPPB group (Figure 7(B)). Furthermore, the Bray – Curtis-based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) analysis showed significantly different trends between the ST and ST+IPPB groups, while the IPPB group was also separated from the control group, indicating different microbial communities among the four groups (Figure 7(C)). To further investigate the differences in the composition of each group, we analyzed different taxa in the four groups. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes and Patescibacteria were the major phylums of microbiota in the contents of the mouse gut (Figure 7(D)). At the genus level, an increase in the abundance of Erysipelatoclostridium in the ST group was observed in mice gut, and treatment with IPPB reversed this trend (Figure 7(E-F)). Notably, the abundance of Ligilactobacillus and Candidatus_saccharimonas was decreased in the ST group but increased with IPPB treatment (Figure 7(G)). These results suggest that treatment with IPPB ameliorated ST-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis.

Discussion

Antibiotics have catalyzed the evolution of modern medicine. Nevertheless, increasing numbers of bacteria are developing resistance to multiple currently utilized antibiotics [34–36]. The scarcity of novel antimicrobial agents coupled with the increasing incidence of multidrug-resistant bacteria has led to therapeutic challenges, and antibiotic resistance has emerged as a significant medical crisis in contemporary health care [37]. Developing novel antibacterial agents to meet this crisis is essential. In response to the increase in the prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria, anti-T3SS agents have gained prominence as a novel strategy for fighting Salmonella infection. In this study, using an invasion-based screening approach, we successfully determined the efficacy of IPPB, MPB and IBPB dissolved in DMSO in efficiently suppressing the invasion of host cells by ST, despite the fact that DMSO itself can also suppress the invasion of ST into host cells [38]. IPPB, MPB and IBPB are p-hydroxybenzoic acid ester compounds commonly employed as antimicrobial preservatives, mainly in food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical products [39,40]. Unlike traditional antibiotics, IPPB, MPB and IBPB did not possess direct bactericidal activity, thus exerted less pressure on bacterial survival. Therefore, these anti-T3SS drugs minimize the risk of drug resistance development and can maintain the integrity of the microbiome.

During the infection process of Salmonella, SPI-1 encodes the T3SS–1, which is responsible for delivering essential effectors crucial for intestinal invasion and enteritis induction [41,42]. The regulation of this process entails an intricate interplay between various environmental cues and genetic modulators within complex networks. The AraC-like proteins, HilD, HilC and RtsA, regulated the expression of hilA, with each protein able to stimulate the transcription of the hilD, hilC and rtsA genes. The predominant role of HilD in the hilA activation process stems from the entry of most regulatory signals impacting SPI1 expression through HilD within the regulatory network [30]. HilD acts as the central switch for activating hilC, rtsA, and hilA expression, while HilC and RtsA amplify these signals. HilA serves as the transcriptional regulator for SPI1, directly enhancing the expression of genes encoding T3SS–1 components and effectors [31]. We conducted extensive screening work in the preliminary stage and identified several Salmonella T3SS–1 inhibitors, including tannic acid, harmine, fraxetin and fisetin. These four compounds all targeted T3SS–1, thereby inhibiting the secretion function of T3SS–1, with no impact on the natural growth of bacteria. Further mechanistic studies revealed that tannic acid could inhibit the transcription of T3SS–1 regulatory and structural genes, suppressing SipA and SipB expression [43]. Fraxetin and harmine inhibited the transcription of the SPI-1 regulatory gene (hilA, hilC, hilD and rtsA), with affecting the expression of effectors [44,45]. Fisetin reduces hilA transcription by disrupting HilD-promoter interactions, leading to downregulated expression of T3SS–1 structural/effector proteins [46]. This study revealed that IPPB could inhibit the expression of the T3SS–1 effector protein, thereby inhibiting the invasion of Salmonella into host cells. The structural analog of IPPB, 3,4-dimethylbenzoic acid, could also suppress the invasion of ST into host cells, primarily by inhibiting the expression of hilA [47]. However, unlike 3,4-dimethylbenzoic acid, IPPB was bound to HilD but did not affect the ability to bind HilD with relevant target promoters, which was somewhat expected, as IPPB repressed the expression of the hilD gene. Therefore, the impact on invasion gene expression was likely mediated through the regulation of hilD transcription rather than its DNA binding capability. Furthermore, this study analyzed the pathological impacts of IPPB therapy on ST-infected mice and G. mellonella model, as well as its effects on the intestinal microbiota of ST-infected mice. It showed that IPPB significantly alleviated the intestinal damage caused by ST infection and effectively ameliorated the dysbiosis of the intestinal microbial community induced by ST. These results provide evidence of the therapeutic effect of IPPB on ST infection. While some inhibitors targeting T3SS have been reported, they still fall short of fully meeting the needs for preventing and controlling Salmonella infection. Therefore, we aimed to systematically screen for virulence-inhibiting compounds against Salmonella infection, laying the groundwork for developing future combination therapy strategies.

In this study, we highlight the novel finding that IPPB serves as a potent inhibitor of the ST T3SS–1. These results indicate that IPPB exhibits efficacy as an anti-infection agent for ST infection, revealing its molecular mechanism in targeting T3SS–1 functions. IPPB emerges as a promising candidate for the development of new anti-T3SS drugs.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmid, cell lines, culture methods and natural compounds

Table S1 lists the bacterial strains utilized in this study. Salmonella strains were grown at 37 °C either on Luria – Bertani (LB) agar plates or in LB liquid medium supplemented with the antibiotics (40 µg/ml ampicillin, 40 µg/ml streptomycin, 20 µg/ml gentamicin or 50 µg/ml kanamycin). Expression of the T3SS–1 gene was induced by supplementing the LB liquid media with an extra 0.3 M NaCl (high-salt LB).

For recombinant protein expression in E. coli, amplification products generated with Table S2 primers were directionally ligated into the pET28a vector.

HeLa and Raw264.7 cells, sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS) under 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Natural compounds ( > 98% purity, Chengdu Herbpurify Co., Ltd.) were dissolved in DMSO to generate stock solutions.

Minimal inhibitory concentration assay

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against ST were assessed via the broth microdilution method. Briefly, serial two-fold dilutions of IPPB (1024 to 2 µg/mL) were dispensed per well of a 96-well plate. Overnight Salmonella cultures were standardized to 1 × 108 CFUs/mL with LB broth supplemented with resazurin (0.02%). Bacterial inoculum (100 μL) containing the compound dilutions was aliquoted into a 96-well microplate, following 18-hour incubation under 5% CO2 at 37°C. The MIC of IPPB against ST was designated as the lowest test concentration eliciting a blueshift.

Growth curve

ST cultures grown overnight were standardized to an optical density (OD) at 600 nm of 0.1 in high-salt LB broth. Serially diluted concentrations of IPPB were subsequently introduced to the standardized suspensions. The OD600 was monitored hourly over 8 h period by a UV spectrophotometer to evaluate the IPPB-mediated effect on ST growth.

Bacterial invasion

HeLa or Raw264.7 cells were plated into 24-well plates. ST was subcultured [1: 20] in high-salt LB broth containing IPPB gradients or isovolumetric DMSO controls, followed by a 4-hour shake at 37°C. The suspensions of bacteria (MOI 30) were applied to cells. Following 5-minute centrifugation at 1,000 rpm, infected co-cultures were maintained for 30 min. Then, the replacement of gentamicin-supplemented medium (100 μg/mL) to eliminate extracellular bacteria, followed by 37°C incubation for another hour. After washing the plates three times with PBS, cellular lysis was induced with 0.02% saponin (Sigma). Lysates were serially diluted and cultured on solid bacteriological medium for quantitative bacterial CFUs.

For the immunofluorescence analysis of ST invasion, IPPB treatment was administered post-infection following ST culture as described above. Fixed cells (4% PFA, 30 min) were permeabilized (0.02% Triton X-100, 5 min) and blocked (4% goat serum, 30 min) at RT. Anti-Salmonella primary antibody was applied to ST for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with Alexa FluorTM 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using Hoechst 33,342 (5 min), and immunofluorescence was analyzed using an Olympus IX-83 fluorescence microscope.

Intracellular replication

Stationary-phase ST was introduced to HeLa or RAW 264.7 cells (1 × 105) in 24-well plates at MOI 20, followed by centrifugation (500 × g, 5 min) to synchronize invasion. After incubating for 20 min, cells underwent triple PBS washes. To eradicate extracellular bacteria, cells were subsequently treated with DMEM containing 100 µg/mL gentamicin for 1 h. Cells were incubated for 6 h in DMEM supplemented with 20 µg/mL gentamicin and escalating IPPB concentrations. Cellular lysis was achieved with 0.02% saponin, and intracellular bacteria were then determined by plating lysates on LB agar for enumeration.

SipA-TEM translocation assay

Overnight cultures of ST expressing the SipA-β-lactamase fusion were subcultured 1:20 in high-salt LB broth containing various concentrations of IPPB (0, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 μg/mL) for 4 h. At an MOI of 20, HeLa cells (1 × 104, 96-well plate) were exposed to either ST or invA mutant strains for 2 hour co-incubation period. After three washes with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS), the cells were covered with 6 × CCF4/AM substrate (K1095; Thermo Fisher) and incubated in darkness at RT for 2 h prior to fluorescence imaging using an Olympus I×83microscope (IX83; Olympus).

Western blotting

Overnight cultures of ST were subcultured at a 1:50 ratio into high-salt LB medium supplemented with varying concentrations of IPPB (0, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 μg/mL). The Cultures underwent an additional 4-hour incubation at 37°C. After pelleting cells (12,000 × g, 2 min), samples were resuspended in 1× loading buffer and boiled (100°C, 5 min). Protein separation was achieved via 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Blocking was performed with 5% nonfat milk (30 min, RT), then probed with primary antibody (4°C, overnight), followed by species-matched secondary antibody (1 h, RT). The antibodies used in this study were polyclonal antibodies specific for WB analysis of SipA generated by immunizing rabbits (AbMax Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), a mouse anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma, cat# F1804; 1:3000), and a rabbit anti-ICDH antibody (Sigma, cat# ABS2090; 1: 20000). The protein ladder employed in this study was purchased from Bio-Rad (Cat. No. 1610373). The immunoblot results were observed with an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR).

Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation assay

ST cultures, treated with or without concentration gradients of IPPB, were incubated in high-salt LB broth at 37°C with shaking (220 rpm) for 4 h. Bacterial pellets were then collected by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 20 min) and resuspended in 100 μL of 1× SDS loading buffer. Concurrently, culture supernatants were mixed with 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and precipitated at 4°C for 4 h. Following centrifugation (14,000 × g, 30 min) to pellet secreted proteins, the precipitates were resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer and boiled at 100°C for 5 min. Finally, the aforementioned samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized using Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining.

Protein expression and purification

E. coli BL21 (DE3) transformants were cultured at 37 °C in 1 L antibiotic-supplemented LB broth. When OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced by adding 0.2 mM IPTG followed by incubation at 16°C for 18 h. Cells were harvested b y centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 30 min), and the resulting pellets were resuspended and lysed by homogenization (JN-mini, JNBIO). Cellular debris was removed via centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4°C). The clarified lysate was incubated with Ni-NTA resin (QIAGEN) for 2 h at 4°C to facilitate binding. Bound proteins were eluted using a stepwise imidazole gradient. Finally, purified proteins were dialyzed and stored.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Real-time PCR was performed to determine the relative transcript levels and investigate the effects of IPPB on SPI-1 gene expression. ST was cultured in high-salt LB broth containing streptomycin (30 μg/mL) and IPPB gradients (0, 16, and 64 μg/mL) for 4 h at 37°C. Total bacterial RNA was isolated using Sangon Biotech’s extraction kit (B518625). cDNA synthesis employed Thermo Scientific RevertAid RT kit (K1691) and 10 pmol of primers for target sequences with a SYBR Green fluorescent dye (KTSM1401, AlpaLife) for analysis. Triplicate qPCR reactions were performed using previously described primers for sipA, sipB, sipC, hilD, hilC, hilA, rtsA, and gyrB [48], with additional primers (prgI, prgK, prgH, and invG) from Table S2. The gyrB functioned as the endogenous control.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

HilD-DNA interactions at hilA or hilD promoters were analyzed by EMSA following previously published protocols [49]. Purified His6-HilD (2.5 μg) was incubated with target DNA fragments (50 ng) in binding buffer containing increasing IPPB concentrations for 30 min at RT. Protein-DNA complexes were resolved on 6% native polyacrylamide gels, stained with SYBR Safe DNA dye (Invitrogen), and imaged using gel imaging system (Tanon MINI Space, China). Promoter regions (listed in Table S2) were PCR-amplified from ST genomic DNA.

Fluorescence quenching

Binding interactions between IPPB and HilD or BSA were analyzed by intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy (F-7100, Hitachi) [50]. Control groups contained HilD or BSA protein alone (0.5 mg/mL), whereas experimental groups consisted of protein solutions titrated with incremental concentrations of IPPB (4, 8, 16 to 32 μg/mL). During spectral acquisition, the excitation wavelength (λex) was set at 220 nm with emission wavelengths (λem) scanned from 280 nm (start) to 400 nm (end), using a slit width of 5 nm. HilD or BSA (0.5 mg/mL) alone was used as a control group; the experimental groups were sequentially spiked with IPPB at concentrations ranging from 4, 8, 16 to 32 μg/mL. The quenching constant is denoted as Ka.

DARTS assay

Overnight-cultured ST (pJL03-hilD) was subcultured [1: 20] in 10 mM arabinose LB broth containing IPPB gradients or isovolumetric DMSO controls. These cultures were incubated overnight at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, the cells were harvested via centrifugation (4000 rpm, 30 min). Subsequently, cellular lysis was achieved using a homogenizer (JN-mini, JNBIO, Guangzhou, China). Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4°C), and the supernatant was assayed using the Bradford method to confirm equal protein concentration across all samples. Pronase was added to the cell lysates at 1:200 based on the protein concentration, as previously described [51]. Proteolytic digestion proceeded for 20 min at RT before termination with SDS loading buffer and 100°C denaturation (10 min). The samples were then analyzed by SDS – PAGE on an 8–15% Bis-Tris gradient gel and subjected to western blotting to detect HilD. ICDH was included as a negative control in this experiment.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments presented in this report (permit number: SY202402302) were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University and complied with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health. The study followed the ARRIVE guidelines (https://www.nc3rs.org.uk/arrive-guidelines).

A total of 30 G. mellonella larvae (Tianjin Huiyude Biotechnology) were stored at 16°C in darkness (≤14 days). Healthy larvae with a uniform color, the absence of markings, and high motility were used for the experiments. Ten G. mellonella larvae were included in each group and were not fed during the experiment. ST cultures were pelleted (4,000 rpm, 5 min), washed, and resuspended in PBS to the desired density (1 × 105 CFUs in 10 μL of PBS, n = 10). ST and ST+IPPB groups larvae received 10 μL inoculations in the left posterior proleg via Terumo Myjector 29 G insulin syringes (Yeso-med) and the control group was injected with 10 μL of sterile PBS. After infection, the treated group of larvae received an injection of 10 μL of IPPB at a dose of 250 μg/kg. The larvae were incubated at 37 °C in darkness within lidded Petri dishes (9 cm). Survival status was assessed at 12-hour intervals, with death confirmed by absence of movement following shaking the container. For histopathological analysis, larvae were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde on day 1 post-infection, paraffin-embedded, and then stained with HE.

The BALB/c mice (n = 69, 6–8 weeks old) used in our study were purchased from Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). The mice had body weights of 19.3 ± 0.16 g, respectively (mean ± SEM). The mice were housed either in the same cages or in neighboring ones, and were attended to by a single caretaker. The mice were randomized into each experimental group and provided streptomycin in water (5 g/L) for 3 days before infection with ST. For the survival assays, a dose of approximately 1 × 107 CFUs of bacteria in 100 μL of PBS (n = 10) was administered to various groups of mice, and the control groups received sterile PBS (n = 10). Murine survival was evaluated through daily observations over an 8-day period. Mice were administered 25 mg/kg IPPB, dissolved in a solvent (40% PEG 400, 10% DMSO, 45% saline, and 5% Tween-80), two hours before the ST infection. Subsequently, oral administrations were administered at 24-hour intervals for an additional 3 consecutive days, amounting to a cumulative of four times via oral gavage. Concurrently, mice in both the control and ST-infection groups were given an equivalent volume of the solvent under an identical dosing regimen. Additionally, the loss of mice’s body weight was assessed on the 5th day following infection. To determine bacterial load in the organs of infected mice, each group of mice (n = 5) was infected with 5 × 106 bacterial cells. Representative organs (spleen, liver) were aseptically harvested, homogenized in PBS, and serially diluted (10-fold) in sterile PBS. Dilutions were cultured on streptomycin-supplemented LB agar (30 μg/mL) for 18 h at 37°C for colony enumeration. For histological evaluation, segments of the cecum, liver and spleen underwent 4% PFA fixation, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and HE staining. To facilitate 16S rRNA sequencing, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (control, IPPB, ST and ST+IPPB), each containing 6 mice. Mice in the ST and ST+IPPB groups were gavaged with 2 × 107 CFU s of ST in 100 μL PBS, whereas the control and IPPB groups received an equivalent volume of sterile PBS. The IPPB and ST+IPPB groups were administered IPPB via gavage following the same protocol as used in the survival rate experiment, while the control and ST groups received isovolumetric vehicle on an identical schedule. Fecal specimens were subsequently collected from all cohorts for 16S rRNA sequencing.

16S rRNA sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB/SDS method and amplified via PCR. Amplicons were gel-purified (Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit, Germany) and used to construct sequencing libraries with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (E7645). Library quality was verified using the Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Paired-end sequencing (250 bp) was performed on Illumina NovaSeq. Raw reads underwent demultiplexing, quality filtering, and denoising via DADA2 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Community diversity metrics (richness, evenness) were calculated from ASV abundance profiles.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data are presented as means ± SD. Intergroup comparisons employed Student’s t test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (≥3 groups). The survival of the mice was analyzed by log-rank test (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, CA, USA). Probability (P) values are shown as follows: p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. “*” p < 0.05, “**” p < 0.01, “***” p < 0.001, and “****” p < 0.0001, “ns” represents no significant difference.
