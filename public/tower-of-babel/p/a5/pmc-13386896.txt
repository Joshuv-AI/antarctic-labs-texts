Tumor lysis syndrome in a pediatric patient with Burkitt lymphoma following emergence from general anesthesia: a case report
Authors: Jiyong Yeom, Hoon Jung, Ye Ji Hwang, Jeong Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is a well-known complication after the treatment of hematologic malignancies, often occurring after cytotoxic therapy, with Burkitt lymphoma demonstrating a relatively high incidence of TLS. However, perioperative TLS is a rare phenomenon, typically associated with tumor dissection, biopsy procedures, and anesthesia induction.Case presentationA 14-year-old Korean male patient was diagnosed with Burkitt lymphoma, presenting with a left neck mass. The patient underwent chemoport insertion after starting chemotherapy on the day prior to surgery. The chemoport procedure itself was uncomplicated. However, following emergence from general anesthesia, the patient developed cardiac arrhythmia and electrolyte disturbances, prompting the diagnosis of TLS based on laboratory results and clinical findings. After the patient developed refractory ventricular tachycardia, laboratory tests revealed hyperkalemia and hypocalcemia. A cocktail treatment was promptly administered to manage the hyperkalemia-induced arrhythmia. Simultaneously, transthoracic echocardiography was performed to rule out surgical or anesthesia-related complications, which showed no abnormalities. Upon confirmation of TLS, standard management was initiated, including aggressive intravenous fluid therapy and administration of a xanthine oxidase inhibitor. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit and fully recovered by the following day. The patient was considered at high risk for TLS, and both the surgical and anesthesiology teams were vigilant to its potential development. Due to the timely initiation of TLS management, the patient was transferred back to the ward on postoperative day 1. Chemotherapy was resumed as initially planned.ConclusionTLS may be triggered by the stress associated with anesthetic emergence, a phenomenon that is rare but important to recognize in high-tumor-burden patients in the perioperative setting. Introduction

Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS) is a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by the rapid release of intracellular contents following the lysis of malignant cells, resulting in elevated blood levels of potassium, phosphate, and uric acid. TLS can lead to cardiac arrhythmia, acute kidney injury, and metabolic acidosis, contributing to increased mortality [1]. TLS is most commonly triggered by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or steroid therapy, with spontaneous onset being rare. The perioperative occurrence of TLS in pediatric patients has been reported primarily in the context of tumor biopsy, or immediately following anesthesia induction [2–5]. It is hypothesized that tumor cell lysis may be caused by the physical removal of tumor mass during surgery or by stress associated with anesthesia, which may accelerate the release of intracellular components from malignant cells.

Here, we report a case of TLS in a pediatric patient with Burkitt lymphoma, who developed the syndrome immediately following the emergence from general anesthesia after chemoport insertion. Notably, this was not the patient’s first exposure to general anesthesia during hospitalization. An excisional biopsy of a left neck mass had been performed under general anesthesia 4 days prior to the chemoport procedure, without any complications. Following the diagnosis of Burkitt lymphoma, chemotherapy was initiated on the day before surgery, and prophylactic allopurinol was administered, with ongoing monitoring for TLS through blood tests and urine output measurements. Despite these precautions, TLS developed immediately after extubation, which manifested with sudden-onset arrhythmia and electrolyte disturbances. This case highlights the rare but critical occurrence of TLS triggered by anesthetic emergence and underscores the importance of early recognition and prompt intervention.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old Korean male patient and his parents provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report. The patient (height: 175 cm, weight: 45 kg) was diagnosed with Burkitt lymphoma, presenting with a left neck mass (measuring 4.9 cm in the left submandibular area and 2.3 cm in the left peritonsillar area), and chemoport insertion was planned under general anesthesia (Fig. 1). Preoperative laboratory results on the day of surgery showed mild anemia and thrombocytopenia. Serum electrolytes were within normal limits (Table 1). Four days prior to this procedure, the patient had undergone general anesthesia for a needle biopsy of the neck mass, recovering without any complications.

Notably, on the day before surgery, the patient received triple intrathecal chemotherapy consisting of methotrexate, hydrocortisone, and cytarabine. The patient was started on prophylactic allopurinol due to elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels at diagnosis, and blood laboratory tests were performed twice daily to monitor for signs of TLS. No premedications were administered on the day of surgery to prevent TLS. In the operating room, standard monitoring was employed, including non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiography (ECG), pulse oximetry, bispectral index, and body temperature measurements. General anesthesia was induced with lidocaine, propofol, remifentanil, and rocuronium. Maintenance of anesthesia was achieved using the inhalation agent sevoflurane. After induction, the patient’s head was rotated to the left, and a sandbag was placed under the shoulders to facilitate neck extension. The chemoport was inserted through the right internal jugular vein under C-arm guidance. The procedure was successfully completed, and vital signs were stable without any complications.

At the end of surgery, spontaneous respiration was fully restored after the administration of 180 mg of sugammadex intravenously. The patient responded to verbal stimuli and the endotracheal tube was removed. However, as the patient was being transferred to the recovery room, ventricular tachycardia (VT) developed within about 5 minutes after extubation (Fig. 2). A palpable pulse was confirmed, and a defibrillator was immediately prepared. Upon detecting the VT, a 10 L/minutes oxygen mask delivering 100% O2 was applied. The VT recurred several times, each episode lasting approximately 30 seconds. Within 5 minutes of the initial VT, sinus rhythm spontaneously returned; however, systolic blood pressure dropped to 70 mmHg, and heart rate increased to 165 bpm. After sinus rhythm resumed, ECG showed no significant changes except for a slightly widened QRS complex. The patient’s mental status deteriorated, and within a minute, VT recurred, with a palpable pulse not detected from the femoral artery. Synchronized cardioversion at 100–150 J was administered twice due to the refractory pulseless VT. At the same time, patient’s respiration was not effective. Respiratory depression was suspected as the cause of VT, and manual bag-valve-mask ventilation was initiated. Another 180 mg of sugammadex was administered intravenously to ensure complete reversal of muscle paralysis. Despite the interventions, refractory VT persisted, and the cause of the arrhythmia remained undetermined. Therefore, the patient was reintubated with 40 mg of propofol, and attempts to cannulate the radial artery were made. However, pulseless VT was detected, and multiple attempts at synchronized cardioversion (150–200 J) were performed. Unfortunately, arterial pulse was not detected, and femoral arterial line placement was unsuccessful on repeated attempts. Point-of-care ultrasound was simultaneously used to assess for procedural complications related to the surgery. There were no abnormal findings in the heart and lungs.

A venous blood gas sample was drawn through the chemoport before the arterial line insertion attempt. The laboratory results revealed electrolyte abnormalities: hyperkalemia (K+ 8.5 mmol/L) and hypocalcemia (Ca2+ 7.7 mg/dL). Accordingly, TLS was clinically diagnosed. A potassium-lowering cocktail (60 mL of 50% dextrose, 10 IU of insulin, and 40 mL of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate) was administered immediately, and a Foley catheter was inserted to monitor urine output. Despite administering the cocktail, pulseless VT intermittently resumed, requiring cardioversion. In addition, intravenous amiodarone (300 mg diluted in 100 mL of normal saline) was administered. After 10 minutes, sinus rhythm was restored, and tall T waves were observed on ECG (Fig. 2). Systolic blood pressure stabilized above 100 mmHg, and the patient was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit.

In the intensive care unit, laboratory findings were consistent with TLS. Allopurinol, rasburicase, and a large amount of intravenous fluids were administered. Electrolyte abnormalities were corrected without renal replacement therapy. On postoperative day 1, blood electrolytes were normalized to K+ 4.0 mmol/L, Ca2+ 6.3 mg/dL, phosphate 3.7 mg/dL, and uric acid 0.5 mg/dL. ECG showed a stable sinus rhythm, and the patient was extubated. The patient subsequently transferred to the general ward without further complications.

Discussion

Burkitt lymphoma is sensitive to chemotherapy and carries a high risk of TLS following treatment. The incidence of TLS in Burkitt lymphoma is approximately 25%, which is higher than in other types of lymphoma [6]. TLS typically develops 2–3 days after cytotoxic agent administration, and the risk persists for up to 10 days thereafter [1, 7–10]. TLS can also occur spontaneously in Burkitt lymphoma, particularly in cases characterized by a bulky tumor mass and rapid tumor growth. In such cases, tumor lysis can be further exacerbated by factors such as steroid therapy, tumor biopsy, and induction of anesthesia [1, 4]. Rapid tumor cell breakdown leads to the release of intracellular potassium and phosphate, resulting in hypocalcemia, metabolic acidosis, and cardiac complications. Furthermore, TLS can lead to obstructive uropathy due to uric acid accumulation and acute kidney injury from tissue damage, making it a life-threatening condition. In this case, the time after extubation was within 1 day after chemotherapy. TLS was triggered immediately after emergence from general anesthesia despite prophylactic allopurinol administration and normal electrolyte levels immediately before surgery. The rapid recognition and prompt treatment of TLS were vital in ensuring a favorable outcome. This case underscores the importance of recognizing that anesthetic stress in high tumor burden patients—such as those with Burkitt lymphoma—can influence the development of TLS, especially during the perioperative period.

The patient had consistently elevated baseline levels of lactate dehydrogenase, uric acid, and phosphate upon admission, reflecting the rapid growth of the neck mass. Four days before the onset of TLS, a needle biopsy of the neck mass was performed under general anesthesia, and no significant changes were observed in preoperative and postoperative blood tests or urine output. Following the diagnosis of Burkitt lymphoma, chemotherapy was initiated the day before surgery, and allopurinol was administered to prevent uric acid accumulation. Blood tests performed twice daily during the course of treatment showed no significant changes, and in the morning before surgery, the patient’s electrolyte, lactate dehydrogenase, and uric acid levels remained stable. Chemoport was inserted through the right internal jugular vein, so it did not directly manipulate the mass. Although electrolyte levels were not monitored during the procedure, no significant ECG changes were observed until after anesthetic emergence. However, respiratory depression occurred immediately after tracheal tube extubation, and tall T waves appeared within 1–2 minutes, followed by the onset of VT, suggesting that the stress of emergence from anesthesia exacerbated TLS. Elevated lactate dehydrogenase and uric acid levels indicated a large tumor burden [5]. Moreover, factors such as dehydration from vomiting and dysphagia due to tumor invasion of the peritonsillar area may have contributed to TLS development. Continuous fasting before intrathecal chemotherapy and the chemoport procedure likely resulted in intravascular volume depletion, which is another factor that could have promoted the development of TLS [2, 4, 11].

TLS diagnosis relies on laboratory results and clinical symptoms according to the Cairo-Bishop criteria [12, 13]. In this case, the diagnosis was facilitated by the sudden onset of arrhythmia, accompanied by hyperkalemia and hypocalcemia. This allowed for immediate initiation of TLS management, which included aggressive intravenous fluid administration, a potassium-lowering cocktail, and reintubation. The standard treatment for TLS includes aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation and the use of diuretics to promote the excretion of toxins to prevent acute renal injury. In pediatric patients, a target urine output of 2–4 mg/kg/hours is recommended [13, 14]. Although allopurinol, a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, is generally used in the acute phase to prevent further uric acid production, it does not lower uric acid that has already been made. For active TLS, a recombinant urate oxidase enzyme (e.g., rasburicase) is preferred, as it facilitates the breakdown of uric acid into more easily excreted metabolites. If this treatment is insufficient, hemodialysis should be considered to protect renal function. Furthermore, electrolyte imbalances, particularly hyperkalemia and hypocalcemia, should be managed to prevent cardiac arrhythmia. Despite these interventions, the mortality rate for TLS in patients with solid tumors is approximately 50%, and it is even higher in patients with hematologic malignancies [15].

TLS is more common in patients with hematologic malignancies, typically diagnosed in the ward or intensive care unit; however, perioperative TLS is rare. Without strong suspicion of intraoperative TLS, proper treatment might be delayed while ruling out surgery or anesthesia related causes. In this case, the patient did not undergo tumor resection, and the procedure was contralateral to the tumor mass. Furthermore, no anesthetic agents known to induce TLS, such as succinylcholine, were administered. Therefore, diagnosing TLS in the operating room can be challenging, as its symptoms overlap with those of anesthesia-related complications, such as muscle weakness, fatigue, altered mental status, and shortness of breath. Initially, the arrhythmia was suspected to be caused by respiratory depression, and sugammadex was administered for reversal of the neuromuscular blocking agent. In addition, it was necessary to rule out other potential complications such as cardiac tamponade, hemothorax, or tension pneumothorax—conditions that could occur during the chemoport insertion procedure. Therefore, we had to use transthoracic echocardiography for differential diagnosis and TLS treatment could not started until the diagnosis was confirm. Although preoperative recognition of the risk of TLS and the use of allopurinol can help reduce the likelihood of TLS, no preventive measures can completely eliminate its development [7, 16]. Minimizing perioperative hypovolemia and ensuring close monitoring of electrolyte and uric acid levels, along with awareness of TLS risk among both anesthesiologists and surgeons, are essential for improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In this case, TLS immediately after extubation may be caused by the anesthetic stress and TLS has not previously been reported in the emergence from general anesthesia. Perioperative TLS is a rare and life-threatening condition that anesthesiologists may encounter, especially in high-risk patients with hematologic neoplasms. TLS progresses rapidly and requires prompt recognition and treatment. In patients with a high tumor burden, such as those with Burkitt lymphoma, preoperative allopurinol administration, adequate intravenous hydration, and close monitoring of urine output are critical preventive measures. If these measures fail to prevent TLS, immediate diagnosis and initiation of treatment with a recombinant urate oxidase enzyme or aggressive renal replacement therapies are essential to save the patient’s life.
