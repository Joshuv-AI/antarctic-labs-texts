Impact of hearing aid use on depression and quality of life in older patients with age-related hearing loss: A focus on social relationships
Authors: Woo-Ri Lee, Sungyoun Chun, Kangju Son, Hyun Seung Choi, You Young An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHearing loss is a sensory deficit prevalent in older adults. In South Korea, over 40 % of individuals aged 60 and older reportedly experience unilateral or bilateral hearing loss. An association between perceived social support and satisfaction with hearing aid use has been reported. Although most previous studies focused on the direct effects of hearing aid use, its impact on social relationships remains poorly explored. We analyzed data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging between 2006 and 2022 for those aged 65 years or above. Outcome measures in this study were depressive symptoms and self-reported quality of life (QoL) scores. The independent variable in this study was hearing aid use. A regression analysis was performed using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model. Additionally, the interaction effect between hearing aids and social relationships was examined by performing an interaction term analysis. Of the 554 participants, 177 (31.9 %) used hearing aids. GEE analysis revealed that participants who used hearing aids had a lower depressive symptoms score (β = −0.328, 95 % confidence interval (CI) = −0.625 to −0.031) and higher self-reported QoL (β = 2.193, 95 % CI = 0.060 to 4.327). The interaction term analysis demonstrated a significant interaction effect between hearing aid use and social relationships. Hearing aid use was associated with reduced depressive symptoms and enhanced QoL in older adults with hearing loss. These effects were further moderated by the strength of social relationships, underscoring the importance of both auditory and social interventions in promoting mental and emotional well-being in aging societies.Keywords: Hearing loss, Hearing aid, Depressive symptoms, Quality of life, Older patients

Introduction

South Korea has become an aging society, with the proportion of individuals aged 65 years and older exceeding 19.2 % of the total population in 2024 (Statistics Korea, 2024). Age-related hearing loss (ARHL) is a sensory deficit prevalent in older adults (Bow & Dawson, 2019; Huang & Tang, 2010). Approximately 33 % of the global population aged 65 and older reported hearing impairment in 2012 (Yang et al., 2023). In South Korea, over 40 % of individuals aged 60 and older reportedly experience unilateral or bilateral hearing loss, highlighting a high prevalence rate (Park et al., 2017). ARHL has been associated with diverse underlying causes, including frequent exposure to noise and genetic predispositions (Huang & Tang, 2010; Yang et al., 2023). It typically begins with diminished hearing ability in high-frequency ranges and progresses to affect mid-to-low-frequency ranges over time (Wang & Puel, 2020). Given its growing prevalence with age (Huang & Tang, 2010; Park et al., 2017), along with the trends in population aging and rising life expectancy, the number of individuals affected by ARHL is expected to increase (Yang et al., 2023).

ARHL poses various challenges owing to communication difficulties, leading to social isolation, depression, and cognitive decline (Bow & Dawson, 2019; Huang & Tang, 2010; Park et al., 2017; Wang & Puel, 2020; Yang et al., 2023). Hearing loss can also induce emotional conflicts within families, negatively impacting family satisfaction (Wallhagen et al., 2004). Hearing loss has been found to exacerbate social isolation, and adversely affect life satisfaction and mental health (Huang & Tang, 2010; Mick et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2023). In 2010, healthcare expenditures associated with hearing loss among individuals aged 65 and older in the United States were estimated at approximately $3.1 billion (Mahmoudi et al., 2018). Thus, ARHL not only contributes to health-related burdens, but also imposes substantial economic burdens (Mahmoudi et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2023).

The primary goal of addressing ARHL is to determine the functional need for hearing aids and subsequent improved hearing levels. Prior studies have consistently shown that hearing aid use enhances communication abilities and is associated with a wide range of benefits for older adults, including reduced depressive symptoms, improved quality of life (QoL), enhanced cognitive function, and better psychological well-being (Acar et al., 2011; Boothroyd, 2007; Chisolm et al., 2007; Dillard et al., 2023; Mener et al., 2013). Despite these benefits, the adoption rate of hearing aids remains low globally, with barriers such as cost, stigma, and low perceived need impeding access. In South Korea, healthcare for older adults is provided through the National Health Insurance (NHI), a government-run, single-payer system that ensures universal health coverage (National Health Insurance Service, 2024). All citizens are required to pay monthly insurance premiums based on income, and most medical services are accessed with partial out-of-pocket payments, while the remaining costs are reimbursed by NHI. Thus, older adults with hearing loss can access clinical evaluations and treatments through NHI. However, hearing aid purchases are not routinely covered by insurance. Only individuals who are officially diagnosed with hearing disability by an otolaryngologist can qualify for a government subsidy to partially support the cost of hearing aids (Byun et al., 2022). As a result, many older adults with hearing loss face financial barriers in obtaining hearing aids unless they meet the strict eligibility criteria for disability benefits.

While access to hearing aids is important, social support is also a crucial factor in determining the well-being of older adults with ARHL. A growing body of research emphasizes that social relationships play a protective role in the health of older individuals, particularly in mitigating depressive symptoms and enhancing QoL (Chao, 2011; Kang et al., 2018; Moya et al., 2021; Oxman et al., 1992; Penning, 1995; Unsar et al., 2016). Furthermore, older adults who live alone or are disconnected from family and community networks are more vulnerable to experiencing emotional distress and reduced life satisfaction (Kang et al., 2018; Koo et al., 2021; Van As et al., 2022). These findings suggest that the effectiveness of hearing aids may be influenced by the broader social environment in which they are used. Given this context, it is important to examine how social relationships may interact with hearing aid use in shaping mental health and quality of life outcomes. For example, prior research has demonstrated a notable association between perceived social support and satisfaction with hearing aid use (Singh et al., 2015). However, most previous studies focused solely on the direct effects of hearing aid use, and the interaction between hearing aid use and social relationships remains poorly explored. Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to investigate the effects of hearing aid use on depressive symptoms and QoL among older adults with hearing loss, while also examining how key aspects of social relationships may interact with hearing aid use to influence these outcomes.

Methods

Data and study population

South Korea is one of the most rapidly aging countries in the world. As of 2024, individuals aged 65 years and older account for approximately 19.2 % of the total population, placing the country on the verge of becoming a super-aged society (Statistics Korea, 2024). Among older adults in South Korea, women represent a greater proportion (55.9 %), and approximately 37.4 % live alone, meaning that one-third of older households are composed of living alone older adults (Statistics Korea, 2024). In addition to living along problem, many older adults face economic hardship, with a relative poverty rate of 39.7 % (Statistics Korea, 2024). These demographic challenges have drawn increasing attention to the psychosocial and health-related needs of the elderly population.

This study analyzed data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) provided by the Korea Employment Information Service. The KLoSA cohort data were derived from a nationally representative sample of working adults 45 years or older residing in private households in South Korea as of 2006. Data collection began in 2006 and continued biennially, with the ninth wave being completed in 2022. The current study utilized data from all nine waves (2006–2022).

The participant selection process in summarized in Fig. 1. From the initial cohort of 11,174 participants, we excluded those who did not meet the study criteria. We excluded participants from the initial cohort (N = 920), individuals without hearing loss (N = 8492), and those with hearing loss onset before the age of 65 or after age 80 (N = 527). Hearing loss was defined based on the survey question: “Do you experience difficulty in daily activities due to hearing problems?” Participants were classified as having hearing loss if they responded “Yes” at least once during the whole cohort period. Additionally, we excluded participants with missing health-related (N = 26) or self-reported QoL variables (N = 14), those who had used hearing aids before the onset of hearing loss (N = 98), and participants with non-continuous cohort observations (N = 234). The final analytical sample comprised 554 adults aged 65 years and older living in South Korea with hearing loss.

Variables

Outcome measures

The outcome measures in this study were depressive symptoms and self-reported QoL scores. Depressive symptom scores were calculated using the 10-item Korean version of the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), originally developed by Kohout et al. (Kohout et al., 1993) as a shortened form of the CES-D (Radloff, 1977). The version used in this study was the Korean-language adaptation included in the KLoSA dataset, which has been used in prior research (Kwon et al., 2021). Depressive symptom scores were calculated using the 10-item Korean version of the CES-D scale. Each item was scored as 1 if the respondent reported experiencing the symptom ‘frequently’ (3–4 days per week) or ‘almost always’ (5–7 days per week), and scored as 0 if they reported experiencing it ‘rarely’ (less than 1 day) or ‘occasionally’ (1–2 days). The total score ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Self-reported QoL was assessed using a single-item question included in the KLoSA survey: “Compared to individuals of the same age, how satisfied are you with your overall quality of life (happiness)?” Participants responded using a visual analog scale ranging from 0 to 100, in 10-point increments, with higher scores indicating greater QoL. This item was not drawn from a standardized global QoL instrument, but was developed and administered uniquely within the KLoSA dataset.

Independent variable

The independent variable in this study was hearing aid use, defined based on the health survey question, “Do you usually use a hearing aid?” Participants who responded “Yes” were categorized as hearing aid users, while those who responded “No” were classified as non-users for analysis.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and health-related factors were included as covariates. Sociodemographic factors included sex (male and female), age (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and over 80), highest education level (elementary, middle school, high school, and university or higher), marital status (no [including never married, divorced, or widowed], yes), region (metropolitan, urban, and rural), frequency of meetings with close people (rare, more than once a year, and more than once a month), frequency of participation in community activities (rare, more than once a year, and more than once a month), number of household members (continuous variable), and number of household members living nearby (i.e., within 30 min) (continuous variable). Health-related factors included physician-diagnosed conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, mental illness, and arthritis. These variables were coded as dichotomous indicators (1 = yes, 0 = no). Variables for the activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL (IADL) were also included as continuous scores. Other factors included health-related activity restrictions (e.g., working limitations) and time (cohort period).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses in this study were conducted in three stages. First, baseline characteristics were compared between hearing aid users and non-users. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, and continuous variables were analyzed using the independent samples t-test. Statistical significance was established at p < .05. In addition, effect sizes were reported using Cramér's V for categorical comparisons and Cohen's d for continuous variables to assess the magnitude of group differences. Second, to examine the effect of hearing aid use on depressive symptom scores and self-reported QoL, regression analysis was performed using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model. Statistical significance was determined by whether the 95 % confidence interval (CI) of the coefficient excluded 0. Third, the pure interaction effect between hearing aid use and social relationships was analyzed. Social relationship variables, including the number of household members, marital status, frequency of meeting close people, and frequency of participation in community activities, were used to explore the pure interaction effects. The regression analysis was adjusted for all covariates included in the study. All analyses were performed in 2023 using the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the study population. The participants included 554 older adults, of whom 52.2 % were female, and the largest age group was 75–79 years (40.8 %). Most participants had graduated from elementary school (75.3 %) and were currently married (67.1 %). A majority lived in metropolitan areas (41.3 %). The most common chronic conditions were hypertension (45.7 %), arthritis (33.0 %), and diabetes mellitus (24.7 %). Approximately 70.0 % reported health-related activity restrictions. Regarding social connections, 78.7 % met close people more than once a month, and 49.5 % participated in community activities more than once a month. The average CES-D score was 2.59 (SD = 2.68), and the mean self-reported QoL score was 51.88 (SD = 20.90). Of the total sample, 177 participants (31.9 %) reported using hearing aids. Among men, 37.4 % used hearing aids compared to 27.0 % of women—a statistically significant difference (p < .01), with a small effect size (Cramér's V = 0.111). A greater proportion of married participants used hearing aids (36.3 %) compared to unmarried participants (23.1 %) (p < .01; Cramér's V = 0.134). Hearing aid users also reported better functional outcomes. Their mean ADL score was 0.29 (SD = 1.15), compared to 0.66 (SD = 1.86) among non-users (p < .01), with a small effect size (Cohen's d = −0.24). For IADL, the respective scores were 1.27 (SD = 2.43) and 1.88 (SD = 3.23), also favoring hearing aid users (p = .01; Cohen's d = −0.20). The mean CES-D score was significantly lower among hearing aid users (2.20, SD = 2.48) than non-users (2.78, SD = 2.78) (p = .02), with a small effect size (Cohen's d = −0.22). In contrast, the mean QoL score was higher among hearing aid users (53.90, SD = 20.48) than non-users (50.93, SD = 21.09), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = .12; Cohen's d = 0.14).

The results of multivariable regression using the GEE model are presented in Table 2. Several sociodemographic and health-related variables were significantly associated with depressive symptoms and QoL. Several sociodemographic, health-related, and social variables were significantly associated with depressive symptoms and self-reported quality of life (QoL). Hearing aid users had significantly lower depressive symptom scores than non-users (β = -0.328, 95 % CI = -0.625 to -0.031), and significantly higher QoL scores (β = 2.193, 95 % CI = 0.060 to 4.327). Females had significantly higher CES-D scores compared to males (β = 0.449, 95 % CI = 0.085 to 0.812). Participants aged 80 years or older exhibited higher depressive symptom scores than those aged 65 to 69 (β = 0.556, 95 % CI = 0.098 to 1.013). Higher levels of education were positively associated with QoL: compared to those with only elementary education, participants with a high school (β = 6.273, 95 % CI = 2.511 to 10.034), and university or higher degree (β = 8.089, 95 % CI = 2.413 to 13.765) reported higher QoL scores. Rural residence, as compared to metropolitan residence, was also associated with higher QoL (β = 5.493, 95 % CI = 2.897 to 8.089). Having cardiovascular disease was associated with increased depressive symptoms (β = 0.503, 95 % CI = 0.091 to 0.915), while arthritis was associated with lower QoL (β = -2.984, 95 % CI = -5.756 to -2.600). Individuals with health-related activity restrictions showed both higher depressive symptoms (β = 0.473, 95 % CI = 0.259 to 0.687) and lower QoL (β = -4.178, 95 % CI = -5.756 to -2.600). Additionally, greater IADL limitations were significantly related to both higher depression scores and lower QoL. Social relationship variables showed meaningful associations as well. Participating in community activities more than once a month was associated with lower depressive symptoms (β = -0.299, 95 % CI = -0.544 to -0.053). Similarly, those who met with close people more than once a month reported both lower CES-D scores (β = -0.399, 95 % CI = -0.771 to -0.027) and higher QoL scores (β = 3.865, 95 % CI = 1.457 to 6.273). Regarding family structure, a greater number of household members was significantly related to higher QoL scores (β = 1.277, 95 % CI = 0.488 to 2.066).

The pure interaction term effects between hearing aid use, number of household members, and marital status are presented in Table 3. According to the results of the interaction analysis between hearing aid use and marital status, compared with unmarried hearing aid non-users, lower depression scores were recorded for married hearing aid non-users (β = -0.400, 95 % CI = -0.749 to -0.052), unmarried hearing aid users (β = -0.611, 95 % CI = -1.188 to -0.035), and married hearing aid users (β = -0.594, 95 % CI = -1.060 to -0.127). No significant interactions with self-reported QoL were observed. Considering the interaction between hearing aid use and the number of household members, we noted that an increase in the number of household members was associated with lower depression scores among hearing aid users (β = -0.150, 95 % CI = -0.276 to -0.024). Regarding self-reported QoL, an increase in household members resulted in higher QoL among hearing aid non-users (β = 1.189, 95 % CI = 0.377 to 2.001), with a more pronounced increase in QoL observed among hearing aid users (β = 1.694, 95 % CI = 0.658 to 2.730). Considering the frequency of meetings with close people, hearing aid non-users who met close people once a month (β = -0.451, 95 % CI = -0.873 to -0.030) and hearing aid users who met close people once a month (β = -0.709, 95 % CI = -1.207 to -0.211) had lower depression scores than those with rare meetings and hearing aid non-users. For self-reported QoL, compared with those with rare meetings and hearing aid non-users, higher QoL scores were recorded for hearing aid non-users who met close people once a month (β = 4.569, 95 % CI = 2.008 to 7.131), as well as for hearing aid users who had rare meetings (β = 4.314, 95 % CI = 0.282 to 8.346), met close people once a year (β = 9.862, 95 % CI = 4.335 to 15.389), or met close people once a month (β = 5.139, 95 % CI = 1.784 to 8.494). When comparing participants based on the frequency of community activity participation and hearing aid use, the lowest depressive symptom scores were observed among hearing aid users who participated activities at once a month (β = -0.604, 95 % CI = -1.061 to -0.147). Additionally, hearing aid users with rare participation (β = -0.388, 95 % CI = -0.759 to -0.016) and non-users who participated once a month (β = -0.315, 95 % CI = -0.572 to -0.058) also reported significantly lower depression scores compared to non-users with rare participation. However, no significant interactions were found for self-reported QoL outcomes.

Discussion

Key findings

In the current study, we utilized data from the KLoSA, a nationally representative cohort of older adults in South Korea, tracked over nine waves across 16 years, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the psychosocial dynamics associated with ARHL among this population. Compared with older South Korean adults with ARHL who did not use hearing aids, hearing aid users had significantly higher self-reported QoL scores and lower CES-D scores. Extending beyond previous studies that focused mainly on the direct cross-sectional effects of hearing aid use, we also identified notable pure interaction effects between hearing aid use and key indicators of social relationships, such as marital status, number of household members, and frequency of social engagement.

Interpretations

The underutilization of hearing aids among older adults has emerged as a global public health concern, given its adverse impacts on communication, mental health, and overall wellbeing (Chisolm et al., 2007; Cosh et al., 2019; World Health Organization, 2021). Hearing aid use has been associated with a wide range of positive outcomes for older adults with hearing loss. In our study, hearing aid use was significantly associated with reduced depressive symptoms, suggesting that improved auditory input may facilitate social communication and mitigate emotional distress and isolation among older adults. This finding is consistent with previous studies (Acar et al., 2011; Boothroyd, 2007; Chisolm et al., 2007; Dillard et al., 2023; Mener et al., 2013). Moreover, the improved self-reported QoL scores and reduced depressive symptom scores were consistent with the findings of previous reports, suggesting that hearing aids may contribute to better daily functioning and self-esteem in older adults (Chisolm et al., 2007; Dillard et al., 2023). Despite these benefits, the prevalence of hearing aid use remained low in our cohort (31.9 %), which is consistent with previous findings regarding the underutilization of hearing aids among older adults globally (Chisolm et al., 2007). Given the need for hearing aids, the South Korean government increased its hearing aid purchase subsidy from USD 300 in 2005 to approximately USD 1200 by 2014 (Byun et al., 2022). Although this subsidy policy boosted hearing aid adoption rates (Byun et al., 2022), barriers such as stigma and low awareness of hearing aids persist and need to be addressed. Therefore, public health strategies are required to further promote the use of hearing aids.

According to our pure interaction analysis, the psychological benefits of hearing aid use are more pronounced among individuals with supportive social structures. Married participants who used hearing aids reported the lowest CES-D scores, and an increased number of household members amplified the positive effects of hearing aids on depression and QoL. Frequent contact with close individuals and active participation in community activities can further enhance these benefits. These results underscore the synergistic interplay between auditory rehabilitation and social support, consistent with the socioecological model of health behavior (Chao, 2011; Kang et al., 2018; Moya et al., 2021; Oxman et al., 1992; Unsar et al., 2016). Given the rapidly aging population and increasing proportion of single-person older adult households in South Korea, social support from family members may become increasingly limited (Lee, 2021; Van As et al., 2022). The proportion of older adults living alone in South Korea steadily increased from 17.0 % in 2004 to 22.1 % in 2024 (Supplementary Table 1). In light of demographic challenges in South Korea, marked by rapid population aging and a growing number of single-person older households, it is crucial to promote social participation among older adults and strengthen community-level social support for hearing-impaired older adults.

Living alone, persistent depressive symptoms, and poor quality of life have all been identified as key risk factors for adverse health outcomes among older adults, including functional decline, social withdrawal, and suicide (Choi et al., 2024; Kim & Kihl, 2021; Lee et al., 2017; Olfson et al., 2022). As such, hearing aid use, in combination with social support, may play a critical role in promoting psychological resilience and preventing long-term mental health deterioration.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, hearing loss was identified through self-reported data rather than audiometric evaluation, potentially introducing misclassification bias. Second, the subjective nature of self-reported QoL and depressive symptom scores, rated on scales such as 0–10 or 0–100, may introduce response bias, thereby limiting the precision of the measured outcomes. Additionally, mental health conditions were not clinically assessed or diagnosed; only depressive symptoms and self-reported quality of life were measured, which may not fully capture the broader spectrum of mental health. Third, the study period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced participants’ psychosocial outcomes, particularly their social interactions and mental health status. Fourth, the study sample was limited to older adults with a history of employment, as defined by the KLoSA eligibility criteria, and thus may not represent unemployed individuals or those with untaxed sources of income. Finally, although our models were adjusted for numerous confounding variables, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive causal inferences. Nevertheless, the use of a longitudinal cohort with repeated measures across nine waves allowed the examination of dynamic pure interactions between hearing aid use and various social relationship factors over time. This strengthens the validity of our findings and provides important insights into the psychosocial mechanisms underlying hearing rehabilitation in aging societies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings revealed that hearing aid use is associated with reduced depressive symptoms and enhanced QoL in older adults with hearing loss. These effects were further moderated by the strength of social relationships, underscoring the importance of both auditory and social interventions in promoting mental and emotional well-being in aging societies.
