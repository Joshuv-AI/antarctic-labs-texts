The tissue distribution and pharmacokinetics of [14C]-olorofim following intravenous dosing in the rat
Authors: Derek Law, John Ayrton, Anthony Kennedy, Karen Cornelissen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the penetration of olorofim, a novel antifungal agent into the tissues of the rat.Materials and methodsQuantitative whole-body autoradiography was used to assess the distribution and tissue penetration in male and female rats following a single 2-hour IV infusion of [14C]-olorofim.ResultsRadioactivity was rapidly and extensively distributed throughout many tissues in both sexes with maximal concentrations observed soon after start of the infusion. Thereafter radioactivity declined and at 336 h (final timepoint), concentrations of radioactivity in most tissues were below the limit of quantification. The pattern of radioactive distribution was similar at all timepoints with concentrations greatest in liver, kidney cortex, adrenal cortex, abdominal and brown fat; the lowest concentrations were observed in the whole eye and at the bone surface (with levels similar to plasma in bone marrow and uveal tract/retina). Tissue:plasma radioactivity concentration ratios for most tissues were above unity during the period 2.25 to 72 h post-start of infusion. Radioactivity was detected in tissues frequently reported as sites of systemic fungal infections such as the lung, brain, kidney, bone and eye. Pharmacokinetic analyses showed that at early timepoints >75% of circulating radiolabelled material was intact olorofim confirming that olorofim is present in these key tissues at levels similar to or exceeding those seen in plasma.ConclusionsOlorofim distributes widely into rat tissues including those frequently infected by fungi, indicating its potential to treat systemic fungal infections caused by susceptible species, a finding in agreement with recent clinical reporting from a Phase 2 trial.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections (IFI) cause substantial morbidity and mortality particularly in immunocompromised individuals. The incidence of these infections is increasing, largely because of rising numbers of immunocompromised patients, including those with neutropenia and other immunodeficiency conditions although infections also occur in non-immunosuppressed patients with serious medical conditions1 including infections with respiratory viruses such as influenza2 or COVID-19,3 as well as patients treated with biological therapies.4 Invasive aspergillosis is the most common IFI caused by moulds, however, infections caused by azole-resistant Aspergillus spp.,5 driven by exposure to agricultural azoles in the environment which induces cross-resistance to clinically used azoles are on the rise.5,6 Other opportunistic moulds, such as Scedosporium/Lomentospora spp. and Scopulariopsis spp., are often multi-drug resistant and numbers of these infections are also increasing.7,8

Current antifungal treatment options for these IFI are limited to three classes of drugs: triazoles, polyenes and echinocandins. The increasing incidence of drug resistance among fungal pathogens to these drugs indicates a critical medical need for new antifungal agents with novel mechanisms of action and high efficacy against a wide range of fungal pathogens.9 Olorofim is a drug in clinical development for the treatment of IFI and has excellent activity against many important fungal pathogens, including Aspergillus spp., Lomentospora prolificans, Scedosporium spp. and Scopulariopsis spp., although it does not have activity against yeasts or the Mucorales.9 It does not show cross-resistance with other antifungal agents, has good tolerability and is dosed orally.10

Antifungal drugs must have good penetration into tissues such as the lungs, brain, kidney and bone as these are sites of infection in many IFI. To get a better understanding of the distribution of olorofim into tissues, a quantitative whole-body autoradiography (QWBA) study was carried out in the rat. Such studies are valuable in providing detailed insight into the distribution and persistence of a drug within a wide range of body tissues. In this study, the time course of plasma radioactivity and olorofim concentrations and the distribution of radioactivity in body tissues were determined following the administration of a single intravenous infusion of [14C]-olorofim to male and female rats.

Materials and methods

Test substances

Radiolabelled olorofim

Radiolabelled olorofim (molecular weight of 500.18) was synthesized by Selcia Ltd; as the stability of the amide link was not then certain, the drug was labelled with [14C] in two positions in case of cleavage across the amide link (Figure 1). However, subsequent data (in-house) have shown that cleavage at this bond does not occur. The chemical purity was 99.8% as determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and the radiochemical purity (99.9%) was determined by HPLC with on-line radioactivity detection. The specific activity of the material was 50.82 mCi/mmol (101.6 µCi/mg) and it was stored at −70°C.

Structure of [14C]-olorofim. Olorofim was labelled at two positions in case of cleavage across the amide link.

Non-radiolabelled olorofim

Non-radiolabelled olorofim (molecular weight of 498.18) had a purity of 99.4%. This was stored at 2–8°C.

Olorofim dosing formulation

Weighed amounts of [14C]-olorofim and non-radiolabelled olorofim (approximate ratio of 1:14) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (5% of total volume) and polyethylene glycol 400 was added (10% of total volume). The radio-diluted [14C]-olorofim solution was slowly added to 30% (w/v) aqueous solution of hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (85% of total volume) prior to filtering the final solution through a 0.22 µm filter.

Replicate aliquots of [14C]-olorofim dose formulation were analysed for total radioactivity content by liquid scintillation counting before, during and after dose administration. The radiochemical purity of [14C]-olorofim in the dose formulation was determined by HPLC with on-line radiochemical detection.

Animals

In total, 24 animals were used for QWBA evaluations (eight male and eight female Han Wistar albino rats plus eight partially pigmented male Lister Hooded rats), with a further 33 rats used for the pharmacokinetic assessments (15 male and 18 female Han Wistar albino rats). Animals were 11–15 weeks of age and their weights ranged from 183 to 362 g at the time of dosing. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant guidelines issued by the UK Home Office and approved by the Envigo Ethical Review Process Committee.

Quantitative whole-body autoradiography

In-life assessments

After a 2-hour intravenous infusion of [14C]-olorofim (10 mg/kg; 1016 µCi/kg) to eight male and eight female albino Han Wistar rats and eight partially pigmented male rats, one animal per sex was euthanized at: 2.25, 4, 8, 12, 24, 72, 168 and 336 h post-start of infusion. This dose level produces AUC values in both sexes that exceed those seen in humans following standard therapeutic oral dose of 90 mg q12h olorofim.11

Quantitative whole-body autoradiography

At euthanasia, each rat was anaesthetized, and a blood sample (approximately 1 ml) was collected from the orbital sinus into a tube containing heparin anticoagulant and centrifuged to obtain plasma.

Following euthanasia and rapid freezing at ∼−80°C, each carcase was stored at ∼−20°C until taken for sectioning where the carcase was set in a block of 2% (w/v) aqueous carboxymethylcellulose together with samples of human whole blood containing five different concentrations of [14C]-glucose to construct a calibration line. Sagittal sections (nominally 30 µm) were obtained at six different levels through the carcase: one section from each level was exposed to imaging plates and one section at each level was mounted on acetate sheets and used for visual reference purposes when evaluating the images.

After exposure in a refrigerated copper and lead-lined exposure box for 7 days, the imaging plates were scanned using a FLA5000 radioluminography system and the electronic images analysed using a validated image analysis package (Seescan Densitometry software, version 2.0). The limits of quantification for the procedure were taken to correspond to the lowest and highest calibration standards, 0.035 and 163 µg olorofim equivalents/g (µg equiv./g) tissue respectively. For the purposes of quantification, it was assumed that all tissues analysed had a similar density and quench characteristic to blood (used as calibration standards) and this was supported by the system validation. Exceptions to this rule were bone and abdominal fat where correction factors of 1/0.4 and 1/0.7×the actual measurement were applied, respectively, to compensate for quenching of radioactivity.

Tissue:plasma ratios were calculated using µg equiv./g (tissue): µg equiv./g plasma determined by direct liquid scintillation counting of the sample taken at time of animal euthanasia.

Pharmacokinetic evaluation

In-life assessments

After a 2-h intravenous infusion of [14C]-olorofim (15 mg/kg) to five subgroups of three male and female rats, two serial blood samples (target volume ∼0.5 mL) and one terminal blood sample (target volume ∼6–8 mL) per animal were taken across multiple timepoints: pre-dose, mid infusion (1 h), just before end of infusion (2 h), 2.25, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24 and 48 h post-start infusion. Further samples (two serial and one terminal blood sample/animal) were taken from an additional subgroup of three female animals at timepoints of 72 and 96 h post-start infusion. At each timepoint, blood samples were taken from three male and three female rats. Each animal contributed three samples across the time course of the study. Serial blood samples were collected by venepuncture of a caudal vein and terminal samples were taken by cardiac puncture under isoflurane/oxygen anaesthesia without recovery. All blood samples were taken into tubes containing lithium heparin anticoagulant and plasma was obtained by centrifugation (∼2000g at 4°C for 10 minutes). Before freezing at −80°C, duplicate aliquots were taken for liquid scintillation counting measurements, and an aliquot was dispensed for bioanalysis.

Bioanalytical analysis of olorofim in plasma

Plasma samples (10 µL) were mixed with internal standard (deuterated olorofim) and 0.1% formic acid added. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was diluted with 0.1% formic acid in methanol:water (20:80, v/v) and subject to LC-MS/MS analysis. Analysis was carried out using a Poroshell C18 column eluted with a 10 mM ammonium acetate/0.1% formic acid in methanol gradient on a Waters Acquity system with a Sciex API4000 spectrometer and MS/MS analysis using electrospray ionization (ESI), positive ion mode. No correction factor was applied in the LC-MS/MS assay for the [14C]-olorofim content, as that accounted for <10% of the total dose administered. The assay measured plasma concentrations of total olorofim; as olorofim is highly plasma protein bound (>99%), the small sample volumes available for analysis prevented development of a quantitative assay for free olorofim.

Measurement of radioactivity in plasma by liquid scintillation analysis

The radioactivity in plasma samples was measured by adding weighed volumes of plasma to scintillation cocktail. Total radioactivity was measured by liquid scintillation analysis, and radioactivity in amounts less than twice that of the background concentration in the sample under investigation was considered to be below the limit of quantification (LOQ).

Non-compartmental PK analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin version 6.3 (Pharsight Corporation, USA). Composite data (mean plasma concentrations per timepoint per sex) were used in the analysis. Pharmacokinetic parameters for total radioactivity in plasma and/or olorofim in plasma were reported as follows: maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to maximum plasma concentration (tmax), area under the curve to 48 hours after infusion start (AUC48), AUC to last quantifiable timepoint, clearance, volume of distribution and terminal elimination half-life (t½).

Results

Quantitative whole-body autoradiography

Following a single intravenous infusion (2 mL/kg/h over 2 h) of [14C]-olorofim (10 mg/kg) to male and female albino rats, radioactivity was rapidly distributed throughout the body with measurable concentrations observed in all tissues at 2.25 h post-start of infusion (first euthanasia time). Tissue concentrations were maximal at 2.25 and 4 h post-start of infusion for males and females, respectively. Concentrations of radioactivity declined over time and although some increases in concentrations were noted, these seem to be due to inter-animal variance rather than being indicative of retention of drug-related material. At 8 h post-start of infusion in males (Figure 3a) widespread distribution of radioactivity throughout the body is observed, although there is evidence of decreasing radioactivity throughout the animal compared with earlier timepoints. There was also evidence of penetration of radioactivity into tissues that are commonly sites of fungal infections such as the lung, brain and kidney, which typically had tissue:plasma ratios of 0.5:1 to 5:1 (Table 1). The greatest tissue: plasma ratio continued to be the liver (8:1 for males and 13:1 for females). Figure 3b shows a section of a partially pigmented male rat; overall staining intensity is similar to that of the albino rat, and while pigmented skin shows more intense staining than non-pigmented skin at this timepoint, there is no clear pattern at other timepoints (Table 1 and Table S2). Figure 3c shows that the staining of tissues in the female rat is more intense than in the male indicating slower clearance of radioactivity in the female.

By 24 h post-start of infusion (Figure 4, three different sagittal sections), while there is still evidence of radioactivity throughout most tissues in the male rat, levels continue to decline although tissue: plasma ratios remain >1 for many tissues including lung, kidney, liver, pancreas and stomach and small/large intestinal walls. (Table 1). There is also obvious concentration of radioactivity in faecal pellets, which exceeds that seen in the kidneys and/or bladder at earlier timepoints and suggests that a significant proportion of radioactivity is faecally eliminated.

At 72 h post-start of infusion there are clear differences in the intensity of radioactivity in the male and female rat, (Figure 5), radioactivity was still detectable in all female tissues at this timepoint (plasma level of 0.41 µg equiv/g) and tissue: plasma ratios were generally >1, with ratios of >5:1 being seen in liver, adrenals cortex, fat (abdominal and brown) and large intestinal wall. Circulating radioactivity was low in the male rat (plasma level of 0.03 µg equiv./g) and only liver and kidney had tissue levels >1 µg equiv./g and ∼30% of tissues showed radioactivity levels below the LOQ (0.035 µg equiv./g) (Table 1). At 168 h post-start of infusion in the male and female rat (Figure 6), tissue concentrations of radioactivity continued to decline, with <40% of tissues containing detectable levels of radioactivity (typically in the range of 0.3 to 1 µg/mL). At 336 h post-start of infusion (final euthanasia time), the concentration of radioactivity in the majority of tissues in both male and female rats was below the LOQ with radioactivity only measurable in kidney, liver, lung, spleen and large intestinal wall in males (concentration range 0.3 to 0.7 µg equiv./g), and in kidney, spleen, abdominal fat and small intestinal wall in females (concentration range 0.3 to 0.4 µg equiv./g).

Tissue:plasma radioactivity concentration ratios for most tissues were above unity during the period 2.25 to 72 h post-start of infusion (the last timepoint at which plasma concentrations were measurable) (Table 1). Tissue:plasma ratios were greater in males than in females and these were more prominent at later timepoints, e.g. 72 h where very high ratios were observed for some tissues, due to the very low levels of radioactivity in plasma at this timepoint. Tissue: plasma ratios in key tissues of male rats at various timepoints are shown in Table 1, with Figure 7 showing peak tissue: plasma ratios observed over the first 24 h post-infusion dose for male and female animals.

Following a single intravenous infusion of [14C]-olorofim to male partially pigmented Lister Hooded rats (10 mg/kg), the pattern of distribution was similar to that observed in male albino rats (Figure 3), indicating no difference in the distribution of drug-related material between the two strains of rat. Pigmented tissue levels of total radioactivity were typically up to 2-fold higher than in the same tissue in non-pigmented animals, although pigmented tissue levels were no more than 2-fold greater than those determined in plasma (Table 1). At 336 hours post-start of infusion, very small amounts of radioactivity were detected in a small number of tissues, including the uveal/tract and retina indicating some persistence of drug-related material in pigmented ocular tissue.

Of clinical interest is the penetration of pharmacologically-active drug-related material into the brain, and as key metabolites have been shown to have minimal or no activity against moulds (in-house data), it is brain penetration of unchanged olorofim that is clinically relevant. In both male and female rats over the first 12 h post-start infusion, brain:plasma radioactivity ratios ranged from 0.45 to 1.6 (Table 2); intact olorofim accounted for the majority of radioactivity present in plasma over this time period (53–91% in males and 65–100% in females) (Table 3), this would suggest that the much of the radiolabelled material in the brain is intact microbiologically active olorofim.

Pharmacokinetics

Following a 2-h IV infusion of 15 mg/kg [14C] olorofim, Tmax of both radioactivity and olorofim in plasma of female rats was earlier (Tmax = 1 h, mid infusion) than in male rats (Tmax = 2 h). However, in the female animals, blood sampling difficulties were encountered at the early timepoints that resulted in only one sample being collected for total radioactivity and olorofim analysis at 2 h post-start infusion (compared with three samples in male animals), and no sample was available for olorofim analysis at 2.25 h post-start infusion. As a consequence, determination of Tmax, Cmax and AUC in female rats for both plasma radioactivity and olorofim should be interpreted with caution as exposure for both total radioactivity and olorofim may have been underestimated; however, the AUC values derived for females were higher than those for males for both olorofim and radioactivity.

The plasma concentration: time profile of radioactivity and olorofim in male and female rats shows that olorofim accounts for the majority of total radioactivity in plasma [Figure 8; data up to last common timepoint (48 h) shown]. When comparing total radioactivity and olorofim plasma concentrations within individual timepoints, >75% of the total radioactivity present in plasma was accounted for by intact olorofim from 1 to 4 h post-start infusion in male rats and from 1 to 12 h post-start infusion in the female rats, with olorofim accounting for greater than 50% of plasma radioactivity for up to 48 h in males and 72 h in females [Table 3, data up to final timepoint (96 h) shown]. Overall, olorofim represented 89% and 90% of the total exposure to radioactivity (in terms of AUC48) in male and female rats respectively (Table 4). Elimination of both total radioactivity and olorofim was slower in female rats compared with males, with terminal elimination half-lives for both moieties of ∼10 h in the male and 13 to 16 h in the female. Olorofim clearance was also notably lower in female rats compared with male rats (74 versus 99 mL/h/kg), and the volume of distribution was higher (1.1 L/kg in females compared with 0.4 L/kg in males) (Table 4).

Discussion

Olorofim is a novel antifungal agent in clinical development for the treatment of IFI, excluding yeasts and the Mucorales. In this study, the time course of plasma radioactivity and olorofim concentrations and the distribution of radioactivity in body tissues were determined following the administration of a single 2 h intravenous infusion dose of [14C]-olorofim to male and female rats.

QWBA data demonstrated that radioactivity was rapidly and extensively distributed throughout both males and females with maximal concentrations observed at 2.25 h for males and 4 h for females. Thereafter concentrations declined over time and at 336 h (final timepoint), concentrations of radioactivity in most tissues were below the LOQ. While detection of some radioactivity in tissue at 336 h (14 days) post-start of dosing indicates some persistence of radioactivity in tissues, the concentrations measured were generally lower than at 168 h suggesting that drug-related material is slowly cleared from these tissues. A comparison of data from male and female rats showed there were no apparent significant differences in distribution of radioactivity between males and females. Olorofim appeared to be cleared more rapidly in male rats: this observation is consistent with the toxicokinetic data obtained in olorofim toxicity studies. This gender-related difference in PK does not appear to impact upon overall extent of distribution of total radioactivity.

The pattern of radioactive distribution was similar at all investigated timepoints with concentrations greatest in liver, kidney cortex, adrenal cortex, abdominal fat and brown fat. Radioactive concentrations in all other tissues, where measurable, were generally greater than those in plasma. Concentrations of drug-related material into the eye (as whole) and at the bone surface were lower than other tissues, although levels of radioactivity seen in the uveal tract/retina and bone marrow were similar to those seen in plasma suggesting that penetration within the bone and eye does occur. In the clinic, prolonged dosing of olorofim may allow concentrations to accumulate in these tissues to produce therapeutically active concentrations. Radioactivity was also observed in the brain and spinal cord providing evidence of drug-related material crossing the blood–brain barrier. At early timepoints (up to 4 h) most (>75%) of the circulating drug-related material was intact olorofim, which demonstrated that intact microbiologically active olorofim is present in tissues. The widespread distribution of olorofim into tissues is important if it is to be an effective antifungal therapy as many systemic fungal infections have infective foci in tissues such as the lung, brain, kidney, bone and eye. QWBA image data suggest the presence of radioactivity in urine, however, analysis of excreta samples collected from a mass balance study (in-house data) showed <7% of dose was renally cleared (where <0.3% was unchanged olorofim); most of the radioactivity was voided in faeces (where no unchanged olorofim was detected).

The extensive tissue distribution of olorofim seen in the rat provides a basis for efficacy seen in patients with IFI across a range of tissues. Following a single IV dose of olorofim to healthy volunteers, a high volume of distribution of 3.2 L/kg was determined, which exceeds total body water by >4-fold and suggests that olorofim is extensively distributed.10 Furthermore, in the 202 patients enrolled in the olorofim Phase IIb study, F901318/0032, sites of infection included lung, bone and CNS (each accounting for >10% of population) and efficacy was seen across all tissues.11 The distribution profile defined with [14C] olorofim in this study provides a strong indication that olorofim will penetrate well into tissues where fungi reside in man. This is further supported by mouse PK studies where potentially therapeutic levels of olorofim were detected in the tissues of mice including the brain following IV dosing of olorofim12 and data in an in vivo model of CNS coccidioidomycosis in the mouse, in which olorofim-treated animals had lower fungal brain loads and in some animals there was complete sterilization of the brain and prolonged survival.13 Taken together with the QWBA data, these studies demonstrate sufficient penetration of olorofim into the brain across the blood–brain barrier to achieve efficacious levels. Olorofim is a typical BCS Class II compound with low solubility but high permeability, where wide distribution, including CNS penetration is expected. In-house in vitro data show that olorofim is not a substrate of several transporter proteins, although there are insufficient data to determine whether transporter mechanisms account for the brain penetration seen. However, levels of drug-related material in the brain being similar to those in the systemic circulation would suggest that active transport across the blood–brain barrier may not be occurring.

Limitations of the study include use of a single animal per timepoint, which while typical for collection of QWBA data, does not allow for evaluation of inter-animal variation in tissue:plasma ratios at each timepoint. Furthermore, the study was performed following a single dose and the extent of accumulation in tissues following multiple dosing has not been determined. However, it is likely that tissue:plasma ratios will be similar to or higher than those seen after a single dose, particularly in those tissues where olorofim was more slowly cleared than from the plasma. Finally, while olorofim is known to be highly plasma protein bound in man, the same is also applicable to the rat, with both species showing >99% protein binding. Therefore, although all PK data generated in both species use total plasma concentrations of olorofim, the data available suggest that extensive protein binding found in man and rat does not restrict distribution of olorofim throughout the body.

In conclusion, following IV administration to rats, radiolabelled olorofim was found to distribute from plasma extensively into body tissues. Radioactivity in tissues at early timepoints was predominantly intact olorofim and therefore distribution of olorofim into tissues including tissues that are frequent foci of infections in humans was demonstrated.
