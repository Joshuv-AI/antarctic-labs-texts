A versatile yeast platform for N-demethylation of therapeutic alkaloids
Authors: Daniel J Yaw, Xu Li, Katrine B Kampmann, Kenny W L Lam, Siyi Xue, Jørgen Hansen, Tim A Bowser, Sandra E Kentish, Sally L Gras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlkaloid N-demethylation is a critical step in the production of many semi-synthetic alkaloid therapeutics and has traditionally relied on synthetic chemical routes. Microbial whole-cell bioproduction offers a promising alternate route for generating these valuable pharmaceuticals. This work demonstrated a greener N-demethylation platform applicable to morphinan and tropine alkaloid classes, employing an engineered yeast expressing a cytochrome P450 enzyme with N-demethylase activity, a cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase and purine permeases as transporters. This platform enabled the N-demethylation of the morphinan alkaloids, oripavine and thebaine with high activity, and the tropane alkaloids, atropine and scopolamine with moderate activity. The study systematically evaluated the contributions of substrate uptake and enzymatic conversion within the whole-cell system. Heterologous transporters enabled mass transfer across the cell membrane, with no significant difference detected for the four substrates, while in vitro enzyme catalysis differed between substrates and correlated with yield. Computational molecular docking, combined with deep learning-based protein structure prediction, found all four substrates had high affinity for the enzyme. This approach was also used to identify potential target substrates for future biotransformation. Background

N-demethylation is an essential reaction for the manufacture of many semi-synthetic alkaloid therapeutics, including buprenorphine used for treating opioid dependency and the opioid overdose therapeutic naloxone [1]. Opioids exert pharmacological effects primarily through the µ-opioid receptor [2]. Removal of the N-methyl group from opiates (e.g. oripavine and thebaine), followed by substitution with alternative groups (e.g. the cyclopropylmethyl group in buprenorphine), together with additional structural modifications, generates active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs, e.g. buprenorphine and naloxone) [3] that retain high receptor affinity while producing reduced or no receptor activation compared with the full opioid agonists [4]. Tropane alkaloids, such as atropine and scopolamine, can also be N-demethylated to form the precursors for therapeutically important APIs, such as ipratropium bromide, a bronchodilator listed on the 2025 WHO Model Lists of Essential Medicines and oxitropium bromide, also a clinically approved bronchodilator [5]. Other notable alkaloids requiring N-demethylation for therapeutic activity, or derivatisation of the N-methyl group for increased bioactivity, include noscapine, galantamine, vinblastine, lobeline and codeine, to name just a few [6, 7].

Historically, N-demethylation has been performed by synthetic chemistry, using harsh and unsustainable reagents, such as cyanogen bromide, via the von Braun reaction, as well as chloroformates [6, 8]. Biocatalysis has the potential to provide greener methods for industrial production [9], but so far this approach has only been explored to a limited extent for demethylation. One study describes the discovery of an N-demethylase from Methylobacterium that when expressed in E. coli and purified could be used to N-demethylate thebaine. The enzyme exhibited broad substrate specificity, with good to complete yields of N-demethylated products. Maximum product titres were very low, however, ranging from 2.0 µM to 7.5 µM for various isoquinoline, tropane, indole and piperidine alkaloids [6].

Recently, we reported on a new whole-cell N-demethylation platform that can convert oripavine to nororipavine with high efficiency [10]. This method utilises Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a chassis to express a mutated insect-derived N-demethylase (a cytochrome P450 enzyme), with a cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) as the redox partner and heterologous cross-membrane transporters for substrate uptake. A titre of 1.6 g L− 1 and a time-space yield, or productivity, of 1 g L− 1 day− 1 were achieved [10]. The use of whole-cell biotransformation provides advantages, such as superior stability of the endoplasmic reticulum-anchored N-demethylase and POR [11, 12] and the “free” regeneration of cofactor nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), which avoids expensive exogenous supply and reduction of reactive oxygen species [7, 13–15].

The natural function of many cytochrome P450 enzymes is the detoxification of xenobiotics, drugs, and pesticides [16]. Their utility lies in possessing broad substrate specificities combined with moderate catalytic velocities [16]. This purposeful promiscuity stems from the active site of this class of enzymes, where core elements including the heme-binding motif, are highly specific, while the regions involved in substrate recognition and binding are comparatively lax [17]. The activity of the cytochrome P450 N‑demethylase used in our whole-cell N-demethylation platform could therefore likely be extended to alkaloid structures besides oripavine, which would extend the utility of the platform greatly. The main purpose of the studies reported in this paper was therefore to examine whether our whole-cell N-demethylation platform could be applied to substrates such as thebaine, atropine and scopolamine.

Microbial N-demethylation consists of a series of sequential steps. The first step is the transfer of the substrate from the aqueous environment to the cell cytosol by crossing the cell wall and plasma membrane. The second step is the enzymatic transformation within the cytoplasm of the cell, which involves the donation of electrons from NADPH, transferred via the POR to the P450 enzyme. Finally, the alkaloid product must transfer back across the cell wall and plasma membrane to the culture medium.

The mass transfer of the xenobiotic substrates and products across the cell wall and plasma membrane is critical to ensure whole-cell biotransformation productivity [7]. This mass transfer can occur slowly by passive diffusion [18] and may also be restricted by adsorption of substrates onto the cell surface [7], thereby reducing substrate availability for enzymatic reaction. To mitigate such limitations, we and others have previously employed plant transmembrane purine permeases to facilitate substrate uptake through an active transport mechanism, allowing faster substrate uptake into the cell cytosol [10, 19, 20]. Little work has been done, however, to understand how the kinetics of transport affect introduced enzymes. Thus, a second goal of the current studies was to assess how improvements in substrate mass transfer combined with N-demethylation enzyme kinetics could optimize the bioconversion process.

We sought to determine the capacity of our N-demethylase cytochrome P450 enzyme to N-demethylate opioid and tropane alkaloids by whole-cell biotransformation. The uptake of substrate into yeast cells was assessed, together with in vitro enzyme kinetics and molecular docking using RosettaFold3 generated structure for examination of substrate binding affinity, with the goal of understanding the contribution of binding affinity to yield performance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Oripavine (99.3% purity) and thebaine (98.9% purity) were purchased from Extractas Bioscience (Australia). Atropine sulphate monohydrate (≥ 97% purity) and scopolamine hydrobromide (≥ 98% purity) were obtained from Merck (USA). Atropine is a racemic mixture of two enantiomers of L-hyoscyamine and D-hyoscyamine. Oripavine standard (99.5% purity) and nororipavine standard (87.9% purity) were purchased from the National Measurement Institute (Australia). Other chemicals were purchased from Merck (USA), Ajax Finechem (Australia) or ChemSupply Australia. All the experiments used ultrapure water with a resistivity of ≥ 18.2 MΩ∙cm (Merck Direct-Q).

Yeast strains

Two engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae) strains were used in this study. The first was a biotransformation strain described in our previous study [10] and also in the patent by Houghton-Larsen et al. [21], which expresses an N-demethylase, a cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) and purine permeases (PUP). The control strain expressed only the purine permeases with no P450 enzyme and no POR.

The biotransformation strain was constructed based on a S. cerevisiae derivative of S288C. Integration site X-3 was used for the chromosomal integration of two DNA fragments: (1) expressing a PUP under the control of the constitutive S. cerevisiae TDH3 promoter and (2) expressing a POR from Helicoverpa armigera under the control of the TEF2 promoter. The DNA sequence of PUP can be found in the patent by Houghton-Larsen et al. [21], reported with Sequence ID 235. The DNA sequence of POR can be found in the same patent, reported with Sequence ID 160. Ty element-based multiple insertion (using Ty1E and histidine auxotrophy complementation) was then used to insert a DNA fragment containing two differently codon-optimized mutant versions of the Heliothis virescens CYP6AE15 homologue (i.e., the N-demethylase), controlled by the TDH3 and TEF2 promoters, respectively. The DNA sequence of both mutants can be found in the same patent, reported with Sequence ID 255 and Sequence ID 257. The multiple insertion (using Ty1C and uracil auxotrophy complementation) was finally used to insert a DNA fragment containing another PUP and one of the above N-demethylases, controlled by the TDH3 and TEF2 promoters, respectively. The DNA sequence of the second PUP can be found in the same patent, reported with Sequence ID 249. The DNA sequence of the N-demethylase for this final insertion was the Sequence ID 257 described above. The final biotransformation strain contains 3 copies of the PUP gene (Sequence ID 235), 1 copy of the PUP gene (Sequence ID 249), 1 copy of the POR gene (Sequence ID 160) and a total of 9 copies of the N-demethylase genes (Sequence IDs 255 and 257).

Cell preparation

Cell density was determined based on the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) measured using a Nanodrop 2000c Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The samples were diluted such that the OD600 values fell between 0.2 and 1.0 to stay within the linear range of the Beer-Lambert law. S. cerevisiae cells were cultured under sterile conditions in two stages, using a seed medium (see Supplementary Materials and Methods) described in our previous study [10]. S. cerevisiae glycerol cryostock (100 µL) was grown in 5 mL of medium for 24 h at 30 °C and 180 rpm in a New Brunswick Innova® 40/40R incubator shaker (Eppendorf). In the second stage, the first culture was added into 20 mL of medium at an initial OD600 of 0.08 and was grown for 24 h at 30 °C and 180 rpm. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,000 g and 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and the cell pellets were washed with 25 mL of ice-cold ultrapure water, followed by a repeated wash with 5 mL of ice-cold ultrapure water and 5 mL of pH 6.0, 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer for the subsequent experiments.

Whole-cell biotransformation was performed for oripavine, thebaine, atropine and scopolamine using 0.5 mM substrate and yeast cells resuspended in potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 6.0) to a final OD600 of 10 and glucose of 0.5% w/w, with a final 20 mL working volume in 250 mL baffled shake flask (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 4110 − 0250) with an estimated lower kLa bound of 201 h− 1 (see Supplementary Materials and Methods). Reactions were conducted at 30 °C and 180 rpm with 50 µL samples taken at 0 h, 4 h, 8 h and 24 h. Five replicates were conducted for each biotransformation, with one outlier removed for the biotransformation using scopolamine. Intracellular alkaloid accumulation

The uptake of the four substrates by yeast cells was measured using a protocol adapted from a previous study [7].

The control strain was used to assess the alkaloid accumulation and was prepared as described above. This choice of strain allowed the effect of alkaloid transport to be examined without the influence of the biotransformation reaction. The cell pellet was resuspended into potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 6.0) to a final OD600 of 10 and alkaloid substrate was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, with a working volume of 500 µL. The test was performed in microtubes incubated at 30 °C and mixed at 750 rpm at discrete time periods of 1 min, 2 min, 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, 240 min and 480 min. Substrate uptake was stopped by quenching with 1 mL potassium phosphate buffer pre-chilled in a -4 °C ice and salt bath. The mixture was centrifuged immediately at 20,000 g for 30 s at 2 °C. The supernatant was then carefully removed and the pellet was washed with 1 mL of the pre-chilled buffer. The mixture was centrifuged again to remove the supernatant and collect the pellet. A volume of 100 µL of an extraction solvent (1% v/v formic acid in methanol), pre-chilled in a -4 °C ice and salt bath, was added to extract the alkaloid from the cell pellet by incubating on an ice and salt bath for 20 min, with very gentle vortexing every 5 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 18,000 g for 15 min at 2 °C and 50 µL of supernatant was collected for HPLC analysis. The assay was repeated at 4 °C to measure the substrate adherence to the cell wall, as the activity of purine permease transporters is expected to be reduced under these conditions; incubation was performed in an ice and salt bath inside a cold room, which is expected to reduce the activity of the transporters. Adhered substrate (µmol gDCW−1) was calculated as the average across all time points of the substrate adherence assay. Adjusted uptake was calculated by subtracting the average adhered substrate value from the cell uptake at each time point.

In vitro biotransformation with crude cell extract

Crude cell extracts were prepared with a protocol adapted from a previous study [22]. In brief, cell pellets of the biotransformation strain prepared as described above were resuspended to an OD600 of 1000 as the cell concentrate in a lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCL at pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, EDTA-free Complete Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail from Roche (1 tablet in 10 mL)). A volume of 1 mL of the cell concentrate was transferred into 2 mL Fastprep microtubes containing 200 µL glass microbeads (425–600 μm, Sigma Aldrich) pre-chilled on ice. The cells were lysed using a FastPrep homogeniser (MP Biomedicals) with 5 × 30 s cycles at 6.0 m s− 1 speed with breaks of 1 min on ice between each cycle. The lysed cells were centrifuged at 300 g and 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant (i.e. crude cell extract) was collected and used immediately for in vitro biotransformation.

The in vitro assays were performed in a working volume of 500 µL, with 12.5 µL of crude cell extract in 95 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 2 mM NADPH and substrate at varying concentrations (0 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, 400 µM, 600 µM, 800 µM and 1000 µM). The microtubes were incubated at 30 °C and 750 rpm for 30 min, with 100 µL samples taken for HPLC analysis. The control strain was tested in a same way, with a fixed substrate concentration of 1000 µM. The same batch of cells was used for testing across different substrates, where the mass of cellular material in the lysate was normalised based on OD600, providing a similar cell pellet and concentration of enzyme. Three replicates were conducted for each in vitro biotransformation, using different batches of cells grown under identical conditions. In each case, these were again normalised based on OD600 measurements and kinetic calculations performed for each replicate.

The cells were also lysed for total protein quantification using a protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCL at pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 5% SDS, EDTA-free Complete Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail from Roche (1 tablet in 10 mL)). The lysed cells were centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and used for BCA assay quantification with the Pierce™ BCA protein assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

HPLC analysis

A similar HPLC workflow was utilised from a previously published paper [10]. All samples were diluted to 0.03 mM − 0.6 mM with formic acid (FA) to a final FA concentration of 0.1% v/v. Samples were vortexed and incubated for 30 min at room temperature followed by centrifugation at 16,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for HPLC analysis, with a 10 µL injection volume. An Onyx Monolithic C18 column, 100 × 4.6 mm S/N (080660-38, Phenomenex Australia) was used for all tests. A Shimadzu liquid chromatograph mass spectrometer LCMS-2020 [buffer A: 0.1% v/v trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in water; buffer B: 0.1% v/v TFA in acetonitrile] was used for analysis of oripavine, thebaine and atropine biotransformation.

The following gradients were used for the respective substrate/product pairs: oripavine/nororipavine (1% − 13% (0.00–5.75 min)), 13% − 18% (5.75–7.00 min)), 18% − 32% (7.00–12.00 min), thebaine/northebaine (1–19% (0.00–7.00 min)), 19% − 26% (7.00–10.00 min)), 26% − 32% (10.00–12.00 min), atropine/noratropine (1% − 12% (0.00–4.50 min), 12% − 20% (4.50–7.00 min), 20% − 32% (7.00–12.00 min)). An Agilent 1200 HPLC [buffer: 40% acetonitrile, 10% methanol, 50% pH 6.5, 2 mM KH2PO4] was used for analysis of scopolamine biotransformation, with the method adapted from a previous study [23]. Peaks on the chromatogram were identified based on the retention time (oripavine 7.6 min, nororipavine 7.3 min, thebaine 10.0 min, northebaine 9.8 min, atropine 8.6 min, noratropine 8.4 min, norscopolamine 2.0 min and scopolamine 2.6 min) and the mass to charge ratio (m/z) [oripavine 298, nororipavine 284, thebaine 312, northebaine 298, atropine 290, noratropine 276, scopolamine 304 and norscopolamine 290]. The detector wavelength was set to 285 nm for oripavine and thebaine, 220 nm for atropine and 214 nm for scopolamine, with the reference wavelength at 360 nm.

Oripavine N-demethylation was quantified using both an oripavine standard (for substrate quantification) and a nororipavine standard (for product quantification). This latter standard was produced and validated commercially (National Measurement Institute). In contrast, no commercial standards are currently available for northebaine, noratropine and norscopolamine. Therefore, the nor-compound products generated from biotransformation of thebaine, atropine and scopolamine were quantified using the thebaine, atropine and scopolamine standards respectively, instead of the nor-compounds, assuming that the UV response factor is similar between the substrate and corresponding nor-compound product. A comparison between the standard curves for nororipavine and oripavine (molar concentration vs. absorbance value e.g. via HPLC peak area), showed that the slopes only varied by ~ 2%, suggesting that the change in absorbance at 285 nm, which reflects the aromatic ring structure, is similar for both compounds. We assume similar relationships for thebaine at 285 nm, atropine at 220 nm, and scopolamine at 214 nm.

Mass spectrometry

Higher resolution mass spectrometry was performed using a Thermo OrbiTrap Exactive Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For preparation, 10 µL of sample was diluted into 200 µL of pure methanol with 10 µL direct injection into the mass spectrometer. Isocratic flow at 0.1 ml min− 1 using a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). Data was acquired in positive electrospray ionisation mode over a scan range of 100 to 1000 m/z at a resolving power of 140,000 at m/z 200. The temperature was set to 22 °C with a maximum run time of 1 min.

P450 and heme group structure prediction

RosettaFold3 [24] was used to generate the 3D structure of the N-demethylase P450 isoform (CYP6-like), with the sequence of the enzyme, heme prosthetic group (chemical component dictionary (ccd) code: HEM) and multiple sequence alignment (MSA) result generated using MMseqs2 [25] submitted to the program. Model and model confidence scores are visualised alongside predicted structures, coloured by predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score, using ChimeraX [26]. For additional comparison of the generated structure, the same enzyme sequence and heme prosthetic group entity was also submitted to the AlphaFold3 webserver (https://alphafoldserver.com/) for structure prediction. ChimeraX (software for Biocomputing, Visualization and Informatics at The University of California, San Francisco) (MatchMaker plugin) was used to compare the overall structural similarity, based on all atom pairs root mean square deviation using the chain pairing method.

Molecular docking

The structure of the P450 with the heme group was predicted with RosettaFold3. Protonation states of all amino acid residues were predicted using PROPKA and subsequently used by PDB2PQR (v3.6.2) [27] to assign partial charges with the AMBER force field [28], at both pH 6.0 (the buffer pH for whole-cell biotransformation and for substrate uptake) and pH 7.4 (the pH for in vitro enzymatic assay) for two separate analyses. No residues were manually modified. All titratable residues adopted protonation states based on their PROPKA predicted pKa values, with tautomeric states assigned by PDB2PQR’s hydrogen-bond optimisation. All histidine residues were neutral at pH 7.4. At pH 6.0, the histidine residue at 8 sites (HIS 35, 148, 155, 193, 195, 337, 355, and 440) is positively charged, and two acidic residues (ASP375 and GLU321) were neutral. For the heme group, mixed quantum and molecular mechanics (QM/MM) simulated AMBER parameters by Shahrokh et al. [29] were used for assigning partial charges, the state selected for the P450 docking is Fe+ 3 (penta-coordinate ferric high-spin).

For the substrates, the Chemaxon Marvin pKa plugin (v24.1.2) was used to calculate microspecies and their distributions values at pH of 6.0 and 7.4 at 303.15 K. The SMILES data were then extracted for each microspecies. Using the cheminformatics toolkit RDKit [30], SMILES for all ligands were processed, hydrogens were added to the compound, and 3D conformation of all compounds was calculated. The 3D geometry of each ligand was then optimised using the Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF94) [31]. For the 4 tested substrates in this study: oripavine, thebaine, atropine (two enantiomers, D-hyoscyamine and L-hyoscyamine were separately examined) and scopolamine, 5 different seeds were used for 3D conformation generation in order to test model consistency. In addition to the tested substrates, 39 additional compounds, 1 potential negative control, and 5 nor-compounds (including two enantiomers for noratropine, D-norhyoscyamine and L-norhyoscyamine) were separately screened using molecular docking. In total, 3D conformations for 50 different compounds were obtained. Compounds were prepared using Meeko (v0.6.1) [32], where partial charges were assigned using the Gasteiger method.

Molecular docking was performed with SMINA (v2020.12.10) [33], which is based on AutoDock Vina [34] with improved ligand pose-scoring function. For the docking parameters of SMINA, a search box size of 30 × 30 × 30 Å3 was set to encompass the protein cavity around the heme group, identified using KVFinder [35]. The exhaustiveness parameter (number of docking trials with different ligand conformation) was set to 128 trials, which is well-above the empirical findings that docking results remain unchanged at ~25 trials [36]. For the four substrates tested in this study, 5 random seeds for SMINA were used to test model consistencies. For the other 39 substrates, the negative control and the 5 nor-compounds, only 1 random seed was used for SMINA. The optimal binding pose from the generated binding pose options was selected based on two criteria in this order: (1) a cutoff binding distance of 6 Å between the N-methyl carbon of substrate and the heme iron atom for electron transfer, consistent with the 4 – 7 Å optimal range required for electron transfer [37, 38]; (2) the lowest predicted binding affinity. With these selection criteria, the invalid binding poses were filtered out and the binding pose with the lowest predicted binding affinity, which represents the most favourable and spontaneous binding interaction, was selected for each compound [39]. The lowest predicted binding affinities obtained with and without filtering are both reported. The ligand docking pose alongside the protein was visualised using ChimeraX [40].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and curve fitting of kinetic data were performed using OriginPro 2023b. Experimental data were first determined to be normal using a Shapiro-Wilk normality test before conducting one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. Post-hoc Tukey HSD was performed to identify statistically significant pairwise differences.

Spearman correlation

Spearman correlations were computed in Python using a Monte Carlo sampling approach [41] to propagate measurement uncertainty into the correlation estimates for the apparent Vmax, apparent KM, apparent catalytic efficiency (calculated from the sampled Vmax and KM values, as Vmax/KM and is not an independent variable), nor-compound yield, and predicted binding affinity. L-hyoscyamine was used for the predicted binding affinity correlation analysis, as it is the biologically active enantiomer of the two enantiomers of atropine. Synthetic datasets were generated by Monte Carlo sampling (n = 500,000) to propagate measurement uncertainty and a Spearman correlation matrix was re-computed for each iteration. The final correlation coefficient and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were obtained from the distribution of Monte Carlo-derived correlations. As correlations were computed across four substrates (n = 4), this analysis is exploratory rather than confirmatory due to the low statistical power inherent in small sample sizes.

Results and discussions

N-demethylation of morphinan alkaloids and tropane alkaloids in engineered yeast

All substrates tested (oripavine, thebaine, atropine and scopolamine, see Fig. 1 for reaction schemes) were shown to be N-demethylated, using the engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain that expresses a cytochrome P450 N-demethylase enzyme, as shown by the HPLC chromatograms (Fig. 2) and mass spectra (Supplementary Figure S1). These findings confirm the substrate flexibility of the P450 enzyme, consistent with previously reported studies of other well characterised P450 enzymes [42]. In contrast, the control strain, which expresses only transporters for cellular uptake of substrate but not the cytochrome P450 enzyme needed for the N-demethylation, did not exhibit significant substrate consumption or nor-compound formation (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3).

Oripavine appears to be the optimal substrate for this whole-cell N-demethylation system, with the highest yield and titre of the N-demethylated product, nororipavine (72 ± 6% and 342 ± 25 µM), occurring at 24 h, followed by northebaine (60 ± 3% and 285 ± 32 µM) produced from thebaine, noratropine (43 ± 2% and 216 ± 16 µM) produced from atropine and norscopolamine (23 ± 1% and 110 ± 1 µM) (Fig. 3) produced from scopolamine. The differences in yield and titre were statistically significant for all substrates when determined by one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05). Yield and titre are inherently linked (see Eqs. 2 and 3), so yield was selected as the metric for further correlation of biotransformation performance in the following sections.

The difference in substrate consumption (see Eq. 1) between the four biotransformation reactions was also statistically significant (p < 0.05) in all pairwise comparisons, with a descending order of oripavine (84 ± 7%), thebaine (71 ± 6%), atropine (67 ± 5%) and scopolamine (45 ± 8%) consumption at 24 h (Supplementary Table S2a). The yield and titre of nor-compound and substrate consumption also followed the same relative order at earlier time points of 4 – 8 h (Supplementary Table S2a and S2b). Several factors may contribute to the differences in substrate consumption and N-demethylated product. The total weighted charge of scopolamine (+ 0.08) at an intracellular pH ~7.4 is markedly different from oripavine (+ 0.75), thebaine (+ 0.78) and atropine (+ 0.98) (Supplementary Table S3a). This difference in charge may be one contributing factor, as it could influence binding to the active site and the efficiency of enzyme catalysis, reducing the biotransformation of scopolamine relative to the three other substrates [43]. This is discussed further in the following section, where the in vitro enzyme performance is examined.

A low concentration of byproducts was observed in all reactions (Supplementary Table S1); these byproducts included the potential N-oxide (compound with + 16 Da mass shift) and potential dimer of N-oxide species of the substrate. N-oxides have previously been observed in P450-mediated N-dealkylation, arising from competing N-oxidation pathways that reduce the efficiency of N-demethylation [44]. N-oxides may also be formed from chemical reactions with reactive oxygen species (i.e. peroxide) [45] in yeast cells [46]. Additional byproducts with mass shifts of + 27 Da, + 41 Da and other molecular species were also detected for the respective substrates, suggesting a shared reaction mechanism. Further MS/MS analysis could be performed to identify these unknown byproducts. The quantification of all by-products (including unknown species) was not performed, due to the lack of standards and difficulty identifying all masses present in the reaction. These species were instead grouped into a single category referred to as ‘by-products and missing material’, which was determined by mass balance with the substrate consumed and the product yield (Supplementary Table S2a). This total mass was found to change slightly over time, for example for oripavine biotransformation, the mass of by-products and missing material changed from 3 ± 5% at 4 h to 12 ± 2% at 24 h, indicating a time dependent net increase. The mass of by-product and missing material within the reaction also differed between the different substrates, with the highest value observed for atropine (24 ± 5%) and lowest for thebaine (11 ± 7%) at 24 h. The selectivity reported in Supplementary Table S2a also showed a similar time and substrate dependence.

Other enzymes are also capable of N-demethylation of a variety of substrates, as an earlier study described an FAD-dependent oxidoreductase in a Methylobacterium that exhibited N-demethylation activity on isoquinoline (including opioids), indole, piperidine, and tropane alkaloids [6], similar to our present observations. This suggests that N-demethylation activity is not confined to a single enzyme family, as there is no structural homology between the two enzymes (Supplementary Figure S4) [6, 47]. The Methylobacterium enzyme gave mostly high yields, with 100% nororipavine, 100% northebaine, 65% norscopolamine and 18% noratropine reported [6]. The substrate concentrations employed, however, were relatively low, at 7.5 µM, which is 67-fold less than in this study, restricting industrial applicability.

The time space yield (TSY) (see Eq. 4) was highest at 4 h for nororipavine (296 ± 18 mg L− 1 day− 1), northebaine (229 ± 40 mg L− 1 day− 1), noratropine (139 ± 30 mg L− 1 day− 1) and norscopolamine (112 ± 10 mg L− 1 day− 1) (Supplementary Table S2b). TSY declined from 4 h to 24 h, with a TSY of 97 ± 7 mg L− 1 day− 1, 85 ± 10 mg L− 1 day− 1, 59 ± 5 mg L− 1 day− 1 and 31 ± 0 mg L− 1 day− 1 for nororipavine, northebaine, noratropine and norscopolamine respectively. This reflects a time-dependent reduction in volumetric productivity as cofactor and substrate are expected to be limiting over time. The pH decreased from 6.03 to 5.66 (i.e. ~6% reduction) over 24 h during oripavine biotransformation, which may be attributed to the H+ secretion from yeast cells into the buffer, as reported previously [48] (Supplementary Figure S5). P450 enzymes have been shown to exhibit pH dependent activity, with a reduction of ~15% in relative activity when the pH decreased from 6.5 to 6.0 [49]. Therefore, the pH decrease observed in this study may have an impact on the biotransformation performance. Upon upscaling to a bioreactor, where gas exchange, pH and substrate feeding profiles can be controlled, the TSY is expected to increase, as seen previously for the same N-demethylase enzyme using oripavine as a substrate [10].

The difference in the overall biotransformation performance observed for the different substrates here can potentially be attributed to two main factors: firstly mass transfer of the substrate into the cell or the performance of the enzyme [13], which were both explored in further detail below. The yield at 4 h was selected for consideration of these factors, as substrate and product concentrations vary more dynamically at later time points (i.e. > 4 h).

The uptake and adhesion of substrate to engineered yeast

The transport of each of the four substrates into the yeast cell was examined, as uptake can be a major bottleneck in biotransformation and the addition of transporters has previously been shown to improve overall N-demethylation activity relative to a control strain [19]. In the current transport experiments, we used the control strain expressing only purine permeases but not the N-demethylase enzyme to examine the transport in isolation.

The uptake of substrate supplied at a fixed concentration of 0.5 mM by the cell was similar for all substrates. All substrates accumulate rapidly in yeast cells, reaching a steady state within 15 min, with a total cell uptake of 25.6 ± 11.2 µmol gDCW−1 for oripavine, 22.4 ± 7.0 µmol gDCW−1 for thebaine, 17.7 ± 3.6 µmol gDCW−1 for atropine and 19.4 ± 2.6 µmol gDCW−1 for scopolamine (Fig. 4a-d navy blue circles). The slight fluctuations in concentration observed after 15 min (Supplementary Figure S6a) might be attributed to experimental noise or changes in cell physiology during prolonged incubation. The rate at which each of the substrates was taken up also appeared comparable (Fig. 4e). For example, the rate calculated at 15 min was 1.7 ± 0.7 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 for oripavine, 1.5 ± 0.5 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 for thebaine, 1.2 ± 0.2 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 for atropine and 1.3 ± 0.2 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 for scopolamine (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Figure S6b).

Substrate (supplied at 500 µM) uptake amount for (a) oripavine, (b) thebaine, (c) atropine and (d) scopolamine for the first 60 min. The substrate associated with the cell uptake is shown by the navy blue circles and the adjusted uptake, which has been adjusted to correct for mean substrate adherent to the yeast cell wall is shown in peach circles. The substrate uptake rate in units of µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 is presented for (e) cell uptake and (f) adjusted cell uptake for all substrates. The full data set is found in Supplementary Figure S6a-d. Data is the mean of three biological replicates and the error bars indicate one standard deviation

Subsequent experiments sought to determine the amount of substrate that was expected to adhere on the yeast cell wall, as the net negative charge of the yeast cell wall is expected to attract substrate molecules that are positively charged at pH 6.0 (Supplementary Table S3b) [50]. The concentration of adhered substrate was determined by performing the same transport assay at 4 °C, which is expected to greatly reduce biological activity within the cell and hence the intracellular uptake of substrate by the transporters [7, 19]. As a preliminary finding (n = 2), the amount adherent on the cell wall under these conditions appeared to be small and stable, when measured between 1 min and 30 min and adhesion occurred within the first minute. A total of 2.3 ± 0.4 µmol gDCW−1 adsorbed for oripavine, 4.3 ± 1.2 µmol gDCW−1 for thebaine, 1.4 ± 0.4 µmol gDCW−1 for atropine and 1.5 ± 0.4 µmol gDCW−1 for scopolamine (Supplementary Figure S7). At 15 min, which corresponds to the point where there is a plateau in substrate uptake (Fig. 4a-d), the amount of substrate found to adhere to the yeast cell wall represented between 8% and 19% of the total substrate found associated with the cell in the first assay, suggesting that most substrate, i.e., > 81% effectively passed through the yeast cell wall.

All substrates showed similar uptake rates after subtracting the mean adherent substrate from the cell uptake at each time point (Figure 4a-d peach circles and Supplementary Figure S6c). Uptake rates at 15 min were 1.6 ± 0.7 µmol gDCW-1 min-1 for oripavine, 1.2 ± 0.5 µmol gDCW-1 min-1 for thebaine, 1.1 ± 0.2 µmol gDCW-1 min-1 for atropine and 1.2 ± 0.2 µmol gDCW-1 min-1 for scopolamine (Figure 4f and Supplementary Figure S6d), indicating comparable transport rates of the different alkaloids across the yeast cell wall by the transporting purine permeases. At 15 min, the adjusted substrate uptake was 23.3 ± 11.2 µmol gDCW-1 for oripavine, 18.1 ± 7.0 µmol gDCW-1 for thebaine, 16.3 ± 3.6 µmol gDCW-1 for atropine and 17.9 ± 2.6 µmol gDCW-1 for scopolamine (Figure 4a-d peach circles and Supplementary Figure S6c). One-way ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences among substrates at 15 min for adjusted uptake and adjusted uptake rate (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S6e-f).

The Spearman correlation between the adjusted substrate uptake and biotransformation yield at 4 h was moderate (r = 0.24) but the uncertainty was high (95% CI of − 1.00 to 1.00), potentially reflecting the high variability in measurements of the substrate taken up by the cell that results in overlapping data ranges. Given this uncertainty, no firm conclusions can be drawn on whether substrate uptake is an indicator of biotransformation performance under the conditions examined.

It is not surprising that transport across the membrane is nonspecific for the four substrates tested, as purine permeases are reported to have a wide substrate range. A purine permease-family transporter from Nicotiana tabacum (nicotine uptake permease 1, NtNUP1), for example, has been shown to effectively transport anatabine, nicotine and vitamin B6 derivatives [51], while purine permease-type transporters from Papaver somniferum transport benzylisoquinoline intermediates from dopamine to morphine [52]. The rate of substrate uptake observed here (1.1 – 1.6 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 at the plateau at ~15 min; or 385–560 pmol mgfresh weight−1 min− 1, assuming ~65% water content [53]) appears one order of magnitude greater than the rate of nicotine uptake via NtNUP1 expressed in yeast of 31.3 pmol mgfresh weight−1 min− 1, based on the uptake of 50 µM tritium-labelled nicotine administered for 3 min [51]. The uptake, which may follow a substrate-proton symport mechanism, driven by the direction of the electrochemical proton gradient [54], appears lower, however, than the passive diffusion of the alkaloid noscapine through the peptidoglycan and phospholipid bilayer [55] that occurs for Bacillus megaterium cells (10.7 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 over 25 s, based on the uptake of 80 µM of noscapine incubated at intervals up to 90 s) [7]. The saturation of the uptake rate at ~15 min observed here is also similar to behaviour observed at ~30 min for uptake of the bioactive alkaloid narciclasine by an amino acid permease, lysine-histidine transporter 4 (AtLHT4) expressed in S. cerevisiae [56].

Glucose supplementation increased the adjusted rate of oripavine uptake from 1.6 ± 0.7 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 to 2.7 ± 0.3 µmol gDCW−1 min− 1 at 15 min (Supplementary Figure S8) using 0.5% w/w glucose (i.e., the glucose concentration used for whole-cell biotransformation in Fig. 3), suggesting that glucose addition may improve whole-cell biotransformation. These preliminary data on oripavine (n = 2) are consistent with glucose activation of Pma1, which maintains the proton gradient for permease-mediated substrate H+ symport [57], in addition to NADPH regeneration from glucose supplementation [58].

Overall, the expression of purine permease transporters enabled the uptake of all four substrates into the yeast cell for biotransformation by overcoming the natural uptake limitations; mass transfer rates did not differ significantly between the substrates for the substrate concentration tested (i.e. 500 µM, the initial concentration for whole-cell biotransformation), as could be determined within the variability of the experiment, indicating that substrate-specific differences in whole-cell biotransformation are not likely due to differences in mass transfer at earlier time points. In a dynamic whole-cell biotransformation system, however, substrate concentration decreases, which alters the concentration gradient for mass transfer and hence the substrate uptake rates. This may consequently differentiate intracellular substrate availability for different substrates. Future work could also assess transport behaviour across a range of substrate concentrations, enabling determination of kinetic parameters, such as substrate affinity for purine permeases and the maximum rate of uptake by these permeases [59], for which there is limited data to date [51, 52, 54]. These measures are relevant, as different substrates may display distinct substrate concentration dependent affinities for the permease transporters, impacting on transport rates at different substrate concentrations. Additionally, substrates uptake assays, such as radiolabelling and fluorescence assays, as well as binding assays, could be explored to further characterise the system in future work [60].

Assessment of in vitro enzyme performance

We next assessed the enzyme kinetics of N-demethylation in the absence of the intact cell wall and plasma membrane to characterise the contribution of enzyme kinetics to the performance of whole-cell biotransformation. Structural similarity, quantified by Tanimoto coefficients, was used as a preliminary assessment of predicted N-demethylase bioactivity [61] for the four selected substrates. Thebaine is highly similar to oripavine in structure, differing by only one methyl group (Fig. 1), so the N-demethylase is expected to N-demethylate thebaine with similar efficiency to oripavine. The average Tanimoto coefficient for thebaine was 0.88 ± 0.12, which is above the 0.85 threshold where false positives occur [62], so this measure provides no additional information for thebaine. In contrast, atropine and scopolamine have an average Tanimoto coefficient of 0.22 ± 0.07 and 0.23 ± 0.08, when calculated relative to oripavine using three different Tanimoto models (Supplementary Table S4). The N-demethylase is therefore expected to N-demethylate both substrates with an efficiency that differs to oripavine.

Crude cell extracts were made with lysed cells allowing enzyme activity to be examined in vitro. This method was selected, as further purification may alter the activity of the membrane-bound N-demethylase [63, 64], due to aggregation or denaturation [65], and would remove the cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) required for enzymatic reaction [66]. This approach also allows comparison based on the enzyme present in a cell pellet, reflecting the whole-cell approach to biotransformation. Cell lysates have similarly been used to study the enzymes aspartate carbamoyltransferase [67] and cytochrome P450s [68]. In this case, the same cell preparation was used across all substrates for each replicate to reduce variation. The total protein was measured to be 49.3 ± 4.0 mgprotein OD600−1 across replicate measurements, with an 8% coefficient of variation. While this approach prevents direct comparison with purified enzymes in the literature, it allows apparent kinetics parameters to be reported and comparisons to be made between substrates, reflecting whole-cell biotransformation rather than intrinsic enzyme constants.

A pH of 7.4 was also chosen to minimise any NADPH degradation, which can occur under acidic conditions [69]. A control cell lysate was prepared using cells not expressing N-demethylase and as expected, nor-compound was not observed in this assessment (data not shown). A 30-minute assay duration was selected for in vitro enzyme assessment with substrate at different concentrations (Supplementary Figure S9), as the in vitro time-scale N-demethylation showed a linear correlation between product formation and reaction time throughout 30 min, for oripavine both at low substrate concentration (100 µM, R2 = 0.98) and at high concentration (1000 µM, R2 = 0.98) (Supplementary Figure S10).

The in vitro enzymatic activity of the cell lysate observed with each of the four substrates could be described using apparent kinetic constants, i.e. apparent KM and apparent Vmax, determined by curve fitting [67] (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figure S9 and Supplementary Table S5). The apparent KM for oripavine and thebaine was similar and less than the KM for scopolamine, indicating an order of affinity of the N-demethylase for the alkaloids similar to the relative order of whole-cell biotransformation yield measured at 4 h, 8 h and 24 h ((Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table S5) vs. Fig. 3). Although the KM for atropine was not significantly different to the KM for other substrates. The similar KM for oripavine and thebaine is consistent with the structural similarity of these molecules and the difference in KM for scopolamine consistent with the estimated Tanimoto coefficient for this substrate. Differences in substrate charge at pH 7.4 (the pH used for in vitro assays; see Supplementary Table S3a) may also contribute to the observed variation in apparent KM. In particular, scopolamine is predicted with the lowest total weighted charge among all the compounds tested and may therefore be associated with the highest apparent KM, and correspondingly lower apparent affinity for the enzyme.

The (a) apparent KM, (b) apparent Vmax and (c) apparent catalytic efficiency were determined using crude cell extract with increasing concentrations of substrate and fitted using the curves shown in Supplementary Figure S9, with numerical values shown in Supplementary Table S5. The apparent Vmax, which indicates the maximum catalytic velocity at substrate saturation, was highest for thebaine, with significantly lower Vmax measured for oripavine and atropine (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table S5). The Vmax of scopolamine was not significantly different and the relative order of Vmax differed to the relative order of biotransformation.

The calculated apparent catalytic efficiency (apparent Vmax/apparent KM), a potentially more meaningful measure that incorporates both catalytic turnover and substrate concentration dependence, was highest for thebaine with both atropine and scopolamine having a significantly lower catalytic efficiency, while oripavine had a catalytic efficiency that was not significantly different from any other substrate (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table S5). This order is similar but not exactly the same as the relative order of biotransformation (Fig. 3).

A Spearman correlation between the apparent KM and biotransformation yield at 4 h gave r = − 0.72 (95% CI of − 1.00 to 0.20) and the correlation between apparent catalytic efficiency and yield gave r = 0.64 (95% CI of − 0.40 to 1.00). Apparent Vmax showed no correlation with yield (r = − 0.05 with 95% CI of − 0.80 to 0.60) (Fig. 5d). As the confidence intervals of these correlations cross zero, the results are exploratory, however, they suggest that substrates with higher enzyme affinity and catalytic efficiency tend to achieve greater biotransformation yield under the current condition using resting cells in a shake flask vessel. Future experiments could confirm this observation with a broader range of substrates.

Molecular docking using RosettaFold3-generated protein structure

Understanding the affinity between the four substrates and the N-demethylase is key to understanding biotransformation performance. To this end, molecular docking was performed using the SMINA molecular docking program [33]. The binding of POR was omitted from this docking, to simplify interactions of the N-demethylase, as substrate docking precedes electron transfer by the POR [70]. In addition, the substrate enters the active site on the distal side of the enzyme at the opposite end to the POR docking site, diminishing the need to model POR and N-demethylase interactions during substrate docking [71]. Similar assumptions have been made in prior studies, where substrate affinity to the active sites of other P450 enzymes has been studied by molecular dynamics [72].

The experimental crystal structure of our N-demethylase is not reported, therefore RosettaFold3 was utilised to predict its structure (Supplementary Figure S11a) for molecular docking, as this algorithm is capable of predicting protein structure and associated prosthetic group(s). The algorithm was used to jointly model the heme group located in the active site of the P450 enzyme [24], which is essential for oxygen activation to form the reactive oxoferryl species (Compound I) that extracts the methyl group for N-demethylation [73]. Model confidence metrics indicate generally high confidence for the predicted structure, with an overall pLDDT of 0.862 (Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Figure S11b) as well as an overall pDE of 0.655 Å and an overall pAE of 3.937 Å (Supplementary Table S6) [74]. AlphaFold3 [74, 75] was also used to predict the enzyme structure and both methods found to provide near identical predictions (i.e. an all atom pairs root mean square deviation of 0.727 Å), providing further confidence in the structural prediction (data not shown).

A total of 25 docking trials (5 random RDkit 3D conformation generation seeds × 5 random SMINA seeds) were performed for each of the five compounds (including L-hyoscyamine and D-hyoscyamine) analysed in this study. Chemaxon Marvin was used to predict different protonation microspecies at pH 7.4 (Supplementary Table S3). For the docking results, we focused on the dominant microspecies at pH 7.4, which is neutral for scopolamine and + 1 for oripavine, thebaine, and atropine (L-hyoscyamine and D-hyoscyamine). There is no study reporting negative control substrates for this enzyme, therefore we docked a potential non-substrate compound, dopamine, as an approximate negative control, which shows a predicted binding affinity of − 6.25 ± 0.03 kcal mol− 1. This molecule has a distinct structure to compounds studied here (lacking a methyl group) and is one of the two starting compounds for the benzylisoquinoline synthesis pathway in planta that produces oripavine and thebaine [76]. We acknowledge that the absence of a methyl group does not guarantee a compound is a true non-substrate, as CYP enzymes are known for their broad substrate promiscuity.

The molecular docking found all four substrates, or five including the different forms of atropine (L-hyoscyamine and D-hyoscyamine), bound strongly to the enzyme active site. Oripavine had a predicted binding affinity of − 9.05 ± 0.06 kcal mol− 1, when the molecular docking simulation was performed at pH 7.4 (i.e. the pH used for in vitro cell lysate tests) using the search box area shown in Supplementary Figure S11c. Thebaine had − 8.94 ± 0.16 kcal mol− 1, L-hyoscyamine − 8.90 ± 0.06 kcal mol− 1, D-hyoscyamine − 8.85 ± 0.09 kcal mol− 1 and scopolamine − 8.79 ± 0.1 kcal mol− 1 (Fig. 6, Supplementary Figure S12 and Supplementary Table S7a). Docking trials with the P450 set at pH 6.0 (i.e. the pH of the buffer used for whole-cell biotransformation) showed a similar high affinity of the substrates to the enzyme (Supplementary Table S7c), with a strong Spearman correlation of 0.99 between this data and the data obtained at pH 7.4. Subsequent analysis therefore used the predicted binding affinity at pH 7.4.

An important limitation of the AutoDock Vina (and SMINA) method applied here is the reported root mean squared error (RMSE) of ~1.4 kcal mol− 1 with experimental results [33]. All four substrates fall within this margin of error, so no reliable ranking can be drawn between the substrates in terms of binding affinity. The method nonetheless provides insights into substrate binding affinity for this enzyme. When comparing docking with and without the 6 Å filter, the predicted binding affinity order remains the same and the differences of predicted binding affinity values between with and without filter are within the model uncertainty range.

The Spearman correlation between the predicted binding affinity, determined by molecular docking and biotransformation yield at 4 h gave r = − 0.69 (95% CI of − 1.00 to 0.00) (Fig. 5c). The predicted binding affinity also correlated with apparent KM from the in vitro experiments giving an r = 0.54 (95% CI of − 0.40 to 1.00) (Fig. 5c), suggesting both parameters consistently reflect substrate-enzyme affinity. These exploratory findings suggest that molecular docking may serve as an indicator of whole-cell biotransformation yield under the conditions examined here, pending confirmation across a broader substrate range.

Molecular docking was also performed on each of the nor-compounds (Supplementary Table S8). The predicted binding affinity for nor-compounds was of similar magnitude for the substrates, when the error of AutoDock Vina (and SMINA) methods is considered (Supplementary Table S9). This may impede product release, reducing the effective rate of N-demethylase catalytic activity [77]. This effect is likely compounded in whole-cell biotransformation, where uncharacterised product egress pathways may represent a mass transfer resistance.

The potential substrate range of the N-demethylation reaction was tested in silico using 39 other compounds chosen from the literature based on the utility of their N-demethylated form for therapeutic synthesis [6, 8, 78–85] (Supplementary Table S10). Given the uncertainty of the docking methodology, future work could experimentally assess the promising candidates such as (-)-lobeline, apomorphine, and aporphine, as predicted by molecular docking for experimental validation.

Overall, the co-generated heme group and protein structure shown in Fig. 6 demonstrate the effective use of deep-learning models in the prediction of structures suitable for molecular docking [24, 74], which has applications for drug discovery and in the prediction of reaction performance for drug manufacture. It is also important to acknowledge that molecular docking operates under idealised conditions and does not account for environmental variables, such as solvent medium, temperature and receptor dynamics, which may influence the reaction in practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the transferability of the N-demethylation platform based on an engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain to chemically distinct substrates, with titre and yield in the descending order of oripavine, thebaine, atropine and scopolamine, indicating an enzyme preference for morphinan substrates but some moderate activity for tropane alkaloids. The rate of mass transfer of substrate into the cell via purine permease transporters was similar for all substrates and did not appear to be an indicator of biotransformation yield, nor was the total uptake of substrate, which was also similar between substrates. The affinity of substrates to the P450 enzyme, as evaluated by molecular docking and apparent KM, could be an indicator of whole-cell biotransformation performance. Molecular docking was also applied to assess potential substrate targets, which may be experimentally validated in future experimental work. The insights gained here will allow biotransformation to be further optimised and there is potential to further improve process performance towards industrial viability using fed-batch and continuous processes at larger scale.
