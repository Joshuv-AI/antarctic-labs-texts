Does stringent cytoreduction improve survival in advanced proliferative chronic myelomonocytic leukemia?
Authors: Dorothée Selimoglu-Buet, Sylvie Chevret, Valeria Santini, Sylvain Thépot, Margot Morabito, Lionel Adès, Lucie Laplane, Aristoteles Giagounidis, Nathalie Droin, Michael Lübbert, Rosa Sapena, Stanislas Nimubona, Jose Miguel Torregrosa-Diaz, Ulrich Germing, Anna Maria Pelizzari, Sophie Park, Nadja Jaekel, Georgia Metzgeroth, Francesco Onida, Franciane Paul, Andrea Patriarca, Aspasia Stamatoullas, Katharina Götze, Fatiha Chermat, Uwe Platzbecker, Pierre Fenaux, Eric Solary, Raphael Itzykson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether mitigation of myeloproliferation improves prognosis of CMML independently of bone marrow response is unknown. Flow-defined classical monocytes (cMo) and immature granulocytes (iGRAN) have not yet been studied as biomarkers of response. We inspected the prognostic value of WBC, circulating monocytes, cMo and iGRANs in the 120 DACOTA (NCT02214407) patients randomized to decitabine (n = 63) or hydroxyurea (n = 57) evaluated after 3 cycles with BM aspiration and complete blood count. Across arms, 59% and 56% patients had monocytes > 1 × 109/L or WBC > 10 × 109/L at the 3- and 6-cycle evaluation respectively. After 6 cycles, persistence of monocytes > 1 × 109/L or WBC > 10 × 109/L increased the hazard of death (HR = 5.38, p = 0.0003) irrespective of treatment, baseline CPSS and persistence of BM blast excess. After 3 cycles, both higher absolute cMo and iGRAN counts independently predicted poorer OS, without significant interaction with treatment arm. Median OS from landmark was 35.1 months in the 28% patients with cMo ≤ 0.94 ×109/L AND iGRAN ≤ 0.40 ×109/L versus 15.3 months in others (p = 0.013). Biomarkers integrating blood counts and flow cytometry may predict CMML prognosis irrespective of treatment.Subject terms: Myelodysplastic syndrome, Prognosis

Introduction

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a neoplasm with overlapping myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative (MDS/MPN) features of the elderly [1]. CMML is defined by monocytosis but also a variable accumulation of immature myeloid cells (IMC) in the peripheral blood (PB). Myeloproliferation, often defined by a WBC count > 13 × 109/L, is a robust adverse prognostic factor at CMML diagnosis [2–5], with some studies stressing the specific prognostic value of IMC [6, 7]. In recent years, systematic assessment of peripheral blood by multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) has allowed detailed assessment of circulating granulomonocytic cells in CMML, pointing to the aberrant distribution of monocyte subsets and the variable but pervasive accumulation of immature granulocytes (iGRAN) [8, 9]. Recent pre-clinical data suggest that iGRANs could contribute to disease progression through feedback interaction with the stem cell compartment of CMML [8]. Some retrospective studies also pointed to the specific prognostic contribution of flow-defined monocyte and immature granulocyte subpopulations [8, 10].

Despite the adverse prognostic value of elevated WBC counts at diagnosis, mitigation of WBC counts with low-dose oral ‘cytoreductive’ cytotoxic agents is widely regarded as a palliative strategy devoid of disease-modifying potential [1, 11–13]. Normalization of WBC (≤ 10 × 109/L), monocytes (≤ 1.0 × 109/L) and IMC (≤ 2%) is embedded in the definition of response per international working group (IWG) criteria in as much as it reflects normalized mature cell production by CMML stem and progenitor cells [14]. Indeed, a retrospective study inspecting the prognostic value of response achievement in CMML patients treated with hypomethylating agents failed to identify survival improvement in patients achieving ‘clinical benefit’ alone, a response category that encompasses, but is not restricted to, WBC and monocyte normalization [15].

Two randomized trials have been conducted in proliferative CMML patients presenting features of advanced disease. In the first one, patients randomized to the hydroxyurea (HY) arm had more sustained cytoreduction and significantly improved overall survival compared to oral etoposide [16]. The second one, the recently completed DACOTA trial, failed to identify in the same patient population a survival benefit of the hypomethylating agent decitabine (DAC) over HY, despite higher complete response rates in the DAC arm [17]. These findings led us to raise the hypothesis that normalization of WBC and monocyte counts could improve CMML outcomes regardless of the therapeutic strategy used, including cytoreduction by HY alone. Here, we leverage the peripheral blood smear and MFC data at response assessment visits in the DACOTA trial to address this hypothesis.

Patients and methods

Patients

The DACOTA trial (NCT02214407) is a randomized multicenter phase III study comparing decitabine (DAC) with or without hydroxyurea (HY) versus HY alone as frontline therapy for proliferative CMML with advanced disease. The study was approved by the ethics committee of each participating institution. All patients provided written consent. Patient characteristics and treatment disposition have previously been reported [17]. Patients were grouped by randomization arm in an intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis. Both treatments were administered in 28-day cycles. Disease evaluation visits including complete blood count (CBC) and bone marrow aspirate assessment were planned after 3 and 6 cycles of their assigned treatment before study exit and potential treatment switch. All patients from the DACOTA ITT population having received ≥3 treatment cycles and with CBC available at the 3-cycle evaluation visit were included in the present study (n = 120). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the guidelines for Good Clinical Practice of the International Conference on Harmonization, European directives and national legislations on clinical trials.

Multiparameter flow cytometry

Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Appendix. Gating strategies and quantification were performed as described [8, 9, 18] and reported in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

The absolute concentration of monocyte subsets was obtained by multiplying the percentage of monocyte subsets within the consolidated monocyte gate (Supplementary Fig. 1I) with the absolute monocyte count from the CBC. The absolute quantification of iGRANs is obtained as follows: (number of iGRAN events by MFC)* (absolute monocyte count from the CBC) / (number of monocyte events by MFC). The detailed protocol is reported in [19].

Definitions

Overall survival (OS) was defined as time from randomization to death, whatever the cause. AML transformation-free survival (AMLFS) was defined as time from randomization to acute myelomonocytic leukemia (AML) transformation, progression, or death, whichever occurred first.

Statistical methods

Categorical and continuous variables are reported as numbers and percentages or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), respectively. Due to right-skewed distributions, monocyte and WBC counts were log10-transformed for all analyses. To determine the prognostic value of monocyte or WBC count at specific time points, landmark analyses were done at 3, 6, and 12 months after randomization. Survival was estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and groups compared with the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were fitted. The proportional hazard assumption was validated by visual inspection and testing of Schöenfeld residuals. All analyses were performed using the R 4.1.1 software (https://www.R-project.org/). Data will be made available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

Patient population

Of 170 patients enrolled in the DACOTA trial, 130 (76%) received at least 3 cycles of treatment and 120 (70%) had at least a monocyte count measurement after randomization and were included in the present study (Supplementary Fig. 3). Of those, 63 and 57 patients were enrolled in the DAC and HY arm, respectively. Median age of the study population was 73 years (IQR, 68.75-77). Median monocyte and WBC count following randomization was 4.5 (IQR, 2.1-9.6) x109/L and 27.1 (14.2-45) x109/L in the DAC arm, and 5.1 (IQR, 2.6-9.9) x109/L and 22.7 (IQR, 15.2-40.4) x109/L in the HY arm. Their baseline characteristics (Table 1) are comparable to those of the 50 randomized patients not included in the present study (Supplementary Table 1).

In those 120 patients, median follow-up from the 3-cycle evaluation was 19 months (IQR, 9.4–30.4, range 0–76.4 months). At 3 and 6 cycles, 120 and 80 patients were alive and free of AML respectively. Median OS from the 3-cycle evaluation landmark was 22 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 13.2-33.3) and 22.3 months (95%CI, 18.4-29.4) in the DAC and HY arm respectively (HR = 1.14, 95%CI 0.74-1.74, p = 0.56). Median AMLFS from the 3-cycle landmark was 16.2 months in the DAC arm (95%CI, 8.54-26.0) and 19.7 months in the HY arm (95%CI, 14.28-29.4, HR = 1.07, 95%CI, 0.71-1.61, p = 0.75). No evidence of nonproportional hazards was found (p = 0.45 and p = 0.92, respectively). Median OS at the 6-cycle landmark according to treatment arm were 21.8 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 10.4-34.6) and 26.4 months (95%CI, 19.3-NA) in the DAC and HY arm respectively (HR = 1.50, 95%CI 0.87-2.56, p = 0.14). Median AMLFS from the 6-cycle landmark was 15.7 months in the DAC arm (95%CI, 7.9-29.3) and 19.5 months in the HY arm (95%CI, 15.4-35.6, HR = 1.24, 95%CI, 0.75-2.07, p = 0.40) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Landmark analyses of WBC and monocyte counts

We performed landmark analyses of continuous and dichotomized monocyte and WBC counts after 3 and 6 cycles. We first inspected monocyte and WBC counts as continuous variables or dichotomized at 1 ×109/L and 10 ×109/L respectively, corresponding to current thresholds defining response according to international criteria [14]. Across randomization arms, 70 (58%) and 41 (34%) had monocytes > 1 ×109/L and WBC > 10 ×109/L at the 3-cycle evaluation, while 42 (52%) and 25 (31%) still maintained monocytes > 1 ×109/L and WBC > 10 ×109/L at the 6-cycle evaluation respectively. There were marked differences between DAC- and HY- treated patients with 28 (76%) HY with persistent monocytes > 1 ×109/L after 6 cycles, compared to 14 (33%) DAC patients, and 19 (51%) HY patients with WBC > 10 ×109/L compared to 6 (14%) DAC patients (Supplementary Table 2).

In univariable analyses, while differences were not significant after 3 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 5), both a monocyte count > 1 ×109/L and a WBC > 10 ×109/L predicted both shorter OS and AMLFS after 6 cycles (Fig. 1). Specifically, median OS from landmark was 19.7 months (95% CI, 15.3-27.4) in patients with a monocyte count > 1 × 109/L compared to 26.0 months (95% CI, 17.9-42.5) in those with a monocyte count ≤ 1 × 109/L (p = 0.017) and 18.9 months (95% CI, 14.3-28.1) in patients with a WBC > 10 × 109/L compared to 25.3 months (95% CI, 17.9-36.8) in those with a WBC ≤ 10 × 109/L (p = 0.00021).

We then performed similar analyses focusing on the HY and DAC arms respectively, focusing on the 6-cycle evaluation. In univariable analyses, a high WBC count was predictive of poorer OS in both arms, while a high monocyte count only impacted OS in DAC-treated patients (Fig. 2). In multivariable analyses, persistence of a WBC count > 10 × 109/L after 6 cycles of treatment was independently predictive of poorer OS in the HY arm (HR = 7.00, 95% CI 2.17-22.6, p = 0.001) but not the DAC arm (HR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.09-8.23, p = 0.91; Supplementary Table 4), while the opposite was true for an absolute monocyte count > 1×109/L (HY arm: HR = 2.18, 95% CI 0.58-8.23, p = 0.25; DAC arm: HR = 4.50, 95% CI 1.36-14.9, p = 0.014). Specifically, the median OS from the 6-cycle landmark in HY-treated patients was 13.9 (95% CI 11.1-NA) in the 19 (51%) of patients with WBC > 10 × 109/L compared to 45.3 (95% CI 27.6-NA) in the 18 (49%) of patients with WBC ≤ 10 × 109/L at landmark (Fig. 2).

To derive a simple, treatment-agnostic, biomarker accounting for monocyte and WBC counts, we integrated these two criteria with the Boolean rule of monocytes > 1 ×109/L OR WBC > 10 × 109/L. This led to a marginal increase in the proportion of DAC-treated patients assigned to the higher count category, while most (84%) informative HY-treated patients fell in this category (Supplementary Table 2). Across treatment arms, 45 (56%) of patients had monocytes > 1 × 109/L OR WBC > 10 × 109/L after 6 cycles (including 35 who already met these criteria at the 3-cycle visit, and 10 who converted from adequate to inadequate cytoreduction between the two visits). These patients had poorer overall (HR = 5.38, 95% CI 2.14-13.5, p = 0.0003) and AML-free (HR = 3.44, 95% CI 1.51-7.85, p = 0.003) survival, irrespective of treatment, CPSS risk at baseline and persistence of bone marrow blast excess at the 6-cycle evaluation. The predictive impact of this biomarker on the hazard of death was significant in both patients treated with HY (p = 0.049) and with DAC (p = 0.014, Table 3) and there was no significant interaction between this biomarker and treatment arm for either OS (p = 0.86) or AMLFS (p = 0.70). Across treatment arms, patients with monocytes > 1 × 109/L OR WBC > 10 × 109/L after 6 cycles had a median OS from landmark of 13.9 months (95%CI 10.9-23.2) compared to 34.6 months (95%CI 28.0-50.0) for those with both monocytes ≤ 1 × 109/L AND WBC ≤ 10 × 109/L at the 6-cycle evaluation (Fig. 3). Except for their more frequent allocation to the DAC arm, patients failing to achieve monocytes ≤ 1 ×109/L AND WBC ≤ 10 × 109/L at the 6-cycle visit had higher baseline WBC counts (Supplementary Table 5) and were less frequently TET2-mutated (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, the higher hazard of death of these patients (HR = 5.67, 95% CI 2.47-13.05, p = 0.00004) was retained in a multivariable Cox model stratified per treatment arm and accounting for TET2 mutations (HR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.53-1.93, p = 0.98) and baseline (log10-transformed) WBC count (HR = 5.64, 95% CI 1.83-17.33, p = 0.003). Collectively, these results suggest that persistence of an excess of monocytes or total WBCs after 6 treatment cycles portends poor prognosis in CMML.

Multiparameter flow cytometry

These results also led us to hypothesize that DAC and HY may have different impact on WBC subpopulations such as classical (cMO, also known as MO1), CD14 + CD16- monocytes and CD45 + CD66b + CD15 + CD14- immature granulocytes (iGRAN) endowed with specific prognostic potential at diagnosis [8, 10] and that studying those could point to earlier biomarkers after 3 instead of 6 cycles. To test this hypothesis, we leveraged multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) performed in 97 patients at 3 cycles and/or 6 cycles (both timepoints n = 39, 3 cycles only n = 40, 6 cycles only, n = 18).

We first integrated MFC profiles to bin samples into CMML-like, normal-like, and inflammatory-like profiles, as previously published (Supplementary Fig. 7) [9, 20]. At 3 cycles, 12 of 79 assessable patients (15%) had a normal-like profile, including 2 with a normal-like profile at baseline, and 10 who converted from a CMML-like (n = 9) or an inflammatory-like (n = 1) phenotype, all of whom were randomized to the DAC arm. At 6 cycles, 21 of 57 assessed patients (37%) had a normal-like CMML phenotype, including 2 with a baseline normal phenotype and 19 (14 and 5 randomized to the DAC and HY arm respectively) with a baseline CMML-like phenotype. Thus, of 52 patients with non-normal baseline phenotypes assessed at 6 cycles (24 and 28 assigned to the DAC and HY arm, respectively), 14/24 (58%) and 5/28 (18%) converted to normal-like phenotypes (Fisher exact test p = 0.0038; Supplementary Fig. 8). In a landmark analysis, when adjusting for treatment arm, baseline CPSS, and bone marrow blast excess at evaluation, normal-like phenotype at 3 or 6 cycles had no impact on OS and AMLFS (Supplementary Table 6).

Combining MFC and CBC data, absolute cMo and iGRANs at 3 cycles could be computed in 73 and 64 patients respectively, and in 51 and 47 patients respectively after 6 cycles (Supplementary Table 7). Absolute cMo (p < 0.0001), and to a lesser extent iGRAN (p = 0.09) counts decreased faster in DAC treated patients (Supplementary Fig. 9). We inspected the prognostic value of absolute cMo and iGRAN counts at the 3 and 6 cycles evaluations as continuous (log-transformed) variables, accounting for treatment arm, baseline CPSS (high versus else) and BM blast excess at each evaluation. This analysis revealed that as continuous variables, both higher cMo and iGRAN at the 3-cycle evaluation visit were independently associated with poorer AMLFS, while only higher iGRAN impacted overall survival at this landmark, and no significant prognostic impact was notable at the 6-cycle evaluation visit (Supplementary Table 8). Of note, there was no significant interaction between the impact of treatment arm and absolute cMo (p = 0.55) and iGRAN (p = 0.84) counts on overall survival.

We next defined cutoffs for absolute cMo and iGRAN counts based on previous literature. Specifically, the absolute count cutoff for cMo at 0.94 ×109/L combines the diagnostic threshold of 94% MO1 amongst monocytes with the 1.0 ×109/L cutoff for absolute monocyte count, while the absolute count cutoff for iGRAN at 0.40 ×109/L derives from maximally ranked statistics for prognostic impact at diagnosis [8, 9]. Supplementary Table 7 reports the proportion of patients in each category.

At the 3-cycle evaluation, both an absolute cMo count > 0.94 ×109/L and an iGRAN count > 0.4 ×109/L predicted poorer OS and AMLFS independent of treatment arm, CPSS risk at baseline, and persistence of a bone marrow blast excess at evaluation (Supplementary Table 8). Of note, there was no significant interaction between treatment arm and cMo excess (p = 1.0) or iGRAN excess (p = 0.42) in these models, suggesting that the prognostic impact of these biomarkers is agnostic of therapy. At the 6-cycle visit, the impact of absolute cMo count > 0.94 ×109/L was non-significant (OS, HR = 2.36, 95% CI 0.81-6.92, p = 0.12; AMLFS, HR = 2.03, 95% CI 0.73-5.63, p = 0.17) while that of iGRAN > 0.4 ×109/L retained independent prognostic value (Supplementary Table 8).

We next built a Boolean variable accounting for dichotomized cMo and iGRAN as done supra for WBC and monocyte counts. At 3 cycles, persistence of cMo > 0.94 ×109/L OR iGRAN > 0.40 ×109/L was found in 49 (72%) evaluable patients (clinical characteristics in Supplementary Table 9). There was no genetic footprint predictive of persistence of cMo > 0.94 ×109/L OR iGRAN > 0.40 ×109/L (Supplementary Fig. 10). Such persistence was associated with both poorer OS (HR = 2.55, 95% CI 1.17-5.53, p = 0.018) and AMLFS (HR = 3.26, 95% CI 1.46-7.24, p = 0.004) independent of treatment arm, baseline CPSS risk and persistence of bone marrow blast excess at 3 cycles. This poor prognostic value was still notable after 6 cycles on OS (HR = 2.41, 95% CI 1.02-5.67, p = 0.044) and to a lesser extent AMLFS (HR = 2.07, 95% CI 0.92-4.64, p = 0.077; Supplementary Table 8). Specifically, the median OS from the 3-cycle landmark was 15.3 months (95% CI 11.1-24.6) in patients with cMo > 0.94 ×109/L OR iGRAN > 0.40 ×109/L compared to 35.1 months (95% CI 25.2-NA) in patients with cMo ≤ 0.94 ×109/L AND iGRAN ≤ 0.40 ×109/L, while median AMLFS were 11.9 (95% CI 7.56-19.7) and 27.4 (95% CI 21.07-NA) months respectively (Fig. 4). Of note, of 47 patients with available 3-cycle MFC and 6-cycle CBC data, the proposed MFC-based biomarker at 3 cycles was in agreement with the 6-cycle CBC biomarker in 39 (83%) patients (Cohen’s kappa 0.62, 95% CI 0.39-0.86), 6 of the 8 discrepant cases corresponding to patients with inadequate MFC-based cytoreduction at 3 cycles who achieved adequate CBC-based cytoreduction at 6 cycles.

Discussion

In this study, we leverage standard blood counts and flow-based assessment of monocyte subsets and immature granulocytes in the peripheral blood of advanced proliferative CMML patients at the evaluation visits planned in the DACOTA trial to determine simple biomarkers of overall and leukemia-free survival irrespective of treatment arm.

We identify that persistence of an excess of WBC ( > 10 ×109/L) and/or of absolute monocytes ( > 1.0 ×109/L) after 6 treatment cycles is endowed with poor prognosis independently of baseline CPSS risk, treatment arm (decitabine or hydroxyurea) and persistence of bone marrow blasts at the evaluation visit. Across treatment arms, patients with higher baseline WBC counts and lacking TET2 mutations are less likely to achieve such CBC-defined stringent cytoreduction. However, their poorer outcome does not merely reflect these baseline differences in initial disease presentation.

Combining CBC and MFC data, we then tailor a bespoke biomarker to identify patients with a persistent excess of classical (cMo) CD14 + CD16- monocytes and/or CD45 + CD66b + CD15 + CD14- immature granulocytes (iGRAN). This MFC-informed biomarker is in good agreement with the cytoreduction status determined by CBC only and is predictive of shorter overall and leukemia-free survival across treatment arms at both disease evaluation visits, irrespective of baseline CPSS risk and bone marrow blast excess. The more reliable prognostic value of this MFC-informed criterion may derive from its focus on cell populations with a known impact on CMML pathophysiology [8, 21].

These results, though they warrant independent validation, accounting for additional therapies beyond decitabine or hydroxyurea, have three broad lines of implications for future clinical and translational research in CMML. First, the proposal for a simple flow-based, peripheral blood biomarker allowing early ( ~ 3 months of treatment) prediction of survival could, if confirmed, be used as a surrogate endpoint in early phase CMML trials not powered to detect survival benefits. Next, our results raise hypotheses on the mechanisms through which therapeutic interventions could improve the survival of CMML patients, a myeloid neoplasm where no agent has so far been demonstrated to do so in a prospective, randomized setting. We demonstrate that reducing the absolute number of classical monocytes and immature granulocytes improves patient’s outcomes, while the impact of restoration of a normal balance in terms of relative proportions of monocyte subsets, a phenomenon previously reported in patients treated with hypomethylating agents [21], is less prominent. Our results raise the possibility that the observed improved survival of these patients results from the dampened production of inflammatory cytokines, and not from a reversion of the aberrant myeloid differentiation of early CMML stem and progenitor cells. This hypothesis is in line with translational findings on the role of CXCL8 secreted by iGRANs in the competition between normal and CMML hematopoietic stem cells [8], the impact of other inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 on CMML prognosis [22], and the lack of impact of hypomethylating agents on the clonal architecture of CMML stem/progenitor cells [23, 24]. A correlative study on longitudinal plasma samples from the DACOTA trial is ongoing to explore this hypothesis.

We identified a faster decline of cMo (and to a lesser extent iGRANs) with decitabine compared to hydroxyurea, supporting the disease-modifying potential of hypomethylating agents in at least a fraction of CMML patients [25]. However, the survival benefit conferred by cytoreduction was similar across arms. Our results thus also invite to reassess the role of hydroxyurea in the management of CMML patients. Hydroxyurea is widely considered a palliative option in CMML devoid of disease-modifying potential [1, 11–13], except in patients with very high WBC counts (typically > 30 ×109/L) where end-organ-damage may be prevented by cytoreduction [26]. Our results showing that patients treated with HY alone and achieving a WBC count < 10×109/L and a monocyte count < 1 ×109/L at the 6-month landmark, or normal absolute cMo and iGRAN counts at the earlier 3-month landmark, have improved overall survival, raise the hypothesis that stringent cytoreduction could provide long-term benefits to CMML patients with even mildly proliferative disease. The risk/benefit ratio of such intervention should be carefully investigated in terms of patients reported outcomes, which were not assessed in the DACOTA trial. Stringent control of mature myeloid populations, even in the context of persistent clonal disease [23] could reduce the levels of cytokines regulating the stem cell compartment such as CXCL8 [8], in turn slowing long-term disease progression. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, our findings shed a new light on the results of the first CMML randomized trial where randomization to hydroxyurea was associated with both more efficient cytoreduction and improved overall survival compared to oral etoposide [16]. We can now speculate that such a difference in cytoreduction efficiency (likely resulting from the better safety profile of hydroxyurea over oral etoposide) could have played a causal role on survival prolongation.

Our results, along with the translational and clinical results of JAK inhibition in CMML [27], suggest that in addition of reduction of bone marrow blasts and clonal eradication, mitigation of myeloproliferation could potentially bear disease modifying potential. While developing rational CMML interventions with epigenetic modulators, cytokine or kinase inhibitors remains of prime importance [28], our results suggest that a randomized study would be relevant to determine whether assigning patients with proliferative but less advanced CMML to a stringent versus a conventional ‘liberal’ cytoreduction with hydroxyurea can improve long-term outcomes with manageable hematological cytotoxicity. Until then, the liberal recommendations on cytoreduction from available guidelines should be enforced [12, 29].

As a post-hoc, correlative study, our study has several limitations. It excludes higher-risk patients that fail to achieve 3 treatment cycles, and patient data is incomplete (of 120 patients and 80 with CBCs after 3 and 6 cycles, 96 [80%] and 60 [75%] had bone marrow evaluation and baseline CPSS information). Centralized MFC at response evaluation was also available in a subset of patients only (n = 97 [81%]), though these patients did not differ from those not assessed by MFC at their evaluation visits. MFC was focused on monocytes and immature granulocytes, while other cell populations such as plasmacytoid dendritic cells or helper T cells, which have previously been shown to contribute CMML pathogenesis were not analyzed [30–32]. A significant proportion of patients may drop out of any therapy before the proposed 6-month landmark to study a CBC-based biomarker, an interval that can however be shortened to ~3-months by resorting to an MFC-based biomarker. We also only inspected CBC and MFC-based biomarkers at specific timepoints. Prognostic assessment of a longitudinal variable such as the cumulative time spent under the proposed CBC/AMC cutoff values would likely require a larger dataset and ad hoc methodological approaches to avoid an immortal time bias. Most importantly, our study was exploratory, and our proposed MFC-based biomarker must be explored in forthcoming clinical trials before being considering as decisional in routine practice.
