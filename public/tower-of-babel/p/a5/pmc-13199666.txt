Electrochemical liquid biopsy across cancer care pathway
Authors: Sima Singh, Ada Raucci, Antonella Miglione, Panagiota M Kalligosfyri, Gabriella Misso, Michele Caraglia, Stefania Cocco, Michelino De Laurentiis, Antonio Giordano, Stefano Cinti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiquid biopsy has emerged as a powerful approach for tracking tumor dynamics through circulating biomarkers such as cell-free tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), microRNAs (miRNAs) and soluble proteins. Unlike tissue biopsy, which captures only a single tumor region at one moment, liquid biopsy enables repeated, minimally invasive sampling that reflects the molecular state of disease across all lesions. However, current detection methods based on next-generation sequencing and digital PCR require centralized laboratories, specialized personnel, and turnaround times of days to weeks. Electrochemical biosensors offer a fundamentally different path: compact, low-cost platforms that convert biorecognition events into electrical signals within minutes. This review examines the major electrochemical transduction modes and their compatibility with circulating biomarker classes, maps existing sensor platforms to the phases of cancer care from early detection through treatment monitoring and recurrence surveillance, and addresses the practical requirements for clinical implementation. Despite impressive analytical performance under controlled conditions, most electrochemical sensors have not progressed beyond proof-of-concept. Bridging this translational gap will require robust function in complex biological matrices, rigorous analytical and clinical validation, regulatory approval, and reproducible manufacturing. Meeting these challenges could position electrochemical biosensing as an accessible complement to existing molecular diagnostics in oncology.Keywords: Electrochemical biosensors, Liquid biopsy, Circulating tumor DNA, Circulating tumor cells, Extracellular vesicles, Point-of-care diagnostics, Cancer monitoring, Clinical translation

Introduction

Cancer mortality persists due to failure of diagnostic techniques to keep pace with tumor evolution. Tissue biopsy exemplifies this lag: a single invasive procedure captures one tumor region at one moment, but tumors are genetically diverse across space and resistant clones emerge under treatment pressure [[1], [2], [3]]. Cross-sectional imaging provides high spatial and temporal resolution but limited molecular specificity; for example, Erdi and colleagues report that clinical nuclear medicine investigations demonstrate minimum lesion detectability of about 1.5 cm in diameter (corresponding to 1.77 mL), and that modern PET scanners with a resolution limit of 4 mm can detect tumors with a volume of approximately 0.2 mL (7 mm diameter) at a tumor-to-background ratio of 5:1, implying that smaller foci of disease remain below the threshold of radiologic visibility [4].

Tracking tumor dynamics in real time would require repeated and high-frequency molecular sampling. This cannot be provided by conventional tissue biopsy as the procedure is invasive, is associated with morbidity risk, accumulation of costs with repetition and serial sampling is not feasible in the everyday clinical environment [5]. Such a time lag poses a clinical blind spot: even a single tissue biopsy cannot reflect the process of continuous clonal selection, resistance mutations, and pathway changes that arise during treatment [2,6,7].

Liquid biopsy overcomes tissue accessibility limitations by continuously sampling tumor-derived analytes from body fluids, simultaneously capturing the genotypic and phenotypic landscape across all lesions [8,9]. There are now several types of circulating biomarkers that have clinically actionable information: circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), circulating microRNAs (miRNAs), and soluble protein markers [10,11]. All these analytes encode a dynamic molecular portrait of tumor genotype, epigenetic condition and phenotype in all lesions and can be sampled repeatedly from a single blood draw with negligible risk, inverting the sampling constraint [12]. The different types of biomarkers present different information: ctDNA identifies genetic changes; CTCs still have the morphology and functioning of cells; EVs carry proteins and nucleic acids that mediate cell-to-cell communication; circulating miRNAs reflect post-transcriptional gene regulation and tumour-specific expression patterns; soluble proteins indicate tumour burden and immune response [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Large clinical studies validate multiple biomarker classes for tracking clonal dynamics, emerging resistance, and minimal residual disease (MRD) in solid and hematologic malignancies [15].

Liquid biopsy is now recommended for use in non-invasive genotyping due to inadequacy of tissue, choice-based targeted therapy, identification of resistance mutations [16,17]. Simultaneously, multi-analyte, multi-cancer early detection (MCED) approaches that combine ctDNA with circulating biomarkers and machine-learning (ML) classifiers go beyond liquid biopsy as a treatment monitoring tool and offer it as a screening tool and interception of disease at its initial stages [18]. Despite its promise, liquid biopsy remains tethered to centralised facilities by its technological requirements. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR) require costly equipment, specialized laboratories, complicated bioinformatics, and specialized personnel [19,20]. This causes a mismatch in clinical results: early relapse or therapy response measurements need near-real-time results but since the sample is batched, shipped, and placed on a queue, there is a delay, rendering the data clinically obsolete [21,22]. The cost of infrastructure is an additional constraint of repeated sampling in resource-constrained environments, which poses a global disparity [23].

Electrochemical biosensing offers a fundamentally different approach. Biorecognition processes are transformed into electronic signals that can be measured by miniaturized and low-power detectors [24,25]. The intrinsic miniaturizability and power efficiency of electrochemical transducers makes them useable in point-of-care (PoC) applications in lab-on-chip (LoC), bedside readers and wearable formats where standard NGS facilities are inoperable [26,27]. This translates to rapid assay times in minutes, microliter sample volumes, and per-test costs potentially orders of magnitude lower than sequencing [28,29].

The potential of the technology is in line with clinical requirements: decentralized sampling at the bedside would provide actionable data on a clinically relevant timescale. However, there is an endemic translational gap in electrochemical sensors. Proof-of-concept devices show extraordinary sensitivity under idealized conditions but performance degrades due to serum matrix effects (non-specific binding, protein fouling, ionic strength variation), pre-analytical variability (collection tube, sample stability, operator technique), and the demands of stringent clinical diagnostic standards [[30], [31], [32]]. This gap is not incidental; it reflects a fundamental mismatch between sensor-centric optimization and system-level requirements like specificity and sensitivity across patient populations and clinical workflows.

This Review critically examines electrochemical biosensing for liquid biopsy in oncology, focusing on what is needed to bridge the translational gap between laboratory performance and routine clinical utility. We first examine the principal electrochemical transduction modes and how they pair with circulating biomarker classes including ctDNA, CTCs, extracellular vesicles, circulating miRNAs, and soluble proteins. We then map reported sensor platforms onto the phases of cancer care, from screening and early detection through treatment monitoring and post-therapy surveillance, asking which clinical decisions each approach can realistically support. The final sections address what clinical implementation demands: rigorous analytical validation, method comparison against established assays, regulatory approval, and reproducible manufacturing at scale. Our emphasis throughout is on sensors evaluated in real biological matrices rather than idealized buffer conditions, and on identifying where electrochemical approaches must mature before they can complement existing molecular diagnostics in routine oncology. Electrochemical detection principles and the biomarker landscape

Electrochemical biosensors transduce molecular recognition events into measurable electrical signals by exploiting the redox chemistry that occurs at a functionalized electrode-solution interface. A conventional electrochemical cell consists of a three-electrode configuration comprising a working electrode, a counter electrode, and a reference electrode, wherein the working electrode serves as the transduction surface on which biorecognition reactions take place [33,34].

Electrochemical liquid biopsy platforms generate electrical signals upon analyte binding, recorded through several distinct modalities: amperometry, voltammetry, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and electrochemiluminescence (ECL). Each of these methods provides its own unique benefits for different types of biomarkers and applications. These modalities are applied across a spectrum of circulating tumour biomarkers, each exhibiting distinct biological and analytical constraints [35,36].

Electrochemical transduction modes in liquid biopsy

Amperometric and chronoamperometric detection

Amperometric and chronoamperometric sensors measure current at a fixed potential (or after a potential step) generated by a redox process. In most cancer applications this current is produced by enzyme labels or nanozymes attached to antibodies or nucleic-acid probes in sandwich-type assays [35,37,38]. Amperometry applies a constant potential and measures the resulting oxidation or reduction current over time. Amperometric readouts have also been used for nucleic-acid-based assays. In the electric field-induced release and measurement (EFIRM) platform, Wei et al., employed an electric field to promote hybridization of mutation-specific probes with EGFR-mutant DNA in saliva and plasma from NSCLC patients, followed by amperometric current measurement at an arrayed electrode [39]. In cancer diagnostics, amperometric immunosensors and aptasensors have been widely deployed for the detection of protein biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) [40], prostate-specific antigen (PSA) [41], and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [42], with reported low detection limits. More recently, single-atom nanozyme labels (e.g., Fe single-atom carbon-dot nanozymes) have been coupled with DNA walker amplification for ultrasensitive ctDNA detection, highlighting the continued evolution of amperometric signal carriers [43]. The key strengths of amperometric modes are high sensitivity via catalytic turnover and relatively straightforward adaptation from existing immunoassay workflows. Limitations include background currents from matrix redox species and oxygen, diffusion limitations that can constrain linear range and temporal resolution, and the need to control enzyme stability and batch-to-batch variability for clinical translation [37,44].

Voltammetric methods

Voltammetric techniques, especially differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), square-wave voltammetry (SWV), and anodic stripping voltammetry (ASV), are the most widely used readouts in electrochemical liquid biopsy devices. By superimposing potential pulses on a base potential and sampling current at carefully chosen times, DPV and SWV strongly suppress capacitive background, allowing detection of small Faradaic signals associated with hybridization or binding events [45,46]. For ctDNA and other nucleic acids, redox-tagged probes such as methylene blue (MB) or ferrocene are frequently employed. In the network sensor developed by Chen et al., MB-labeled thiolated DNA probes immobilized on gold-coated magnetic nanoparticles were hybridized with target ctDNA in suspension and then assembled onto an electrode; DPV of MB enabled dynamic ranges from 2 aM to 20 nM for short targets and 200 aM to 20 nM for longer fragments, with LoDs as low as 3.3 aM, and detection was demonstrated in whole blood [47]. Dual-amplification ctDNA platforms exploit metal-loaded nanocarriers; Cai et al., used lead-phosphate apoferritin on AuNPs as a tag, with SWV of released Pb2+ providing a 10 fM LoD for mutation plus methylation analysis in patient serum [48]. More complex architectures integrate CRISPR/dCas9 or Cas12a, EXPAR, DNA walkers, or DNAzymes, all typically read by DPV/SWV and claiming fM-aM sensitivities [43,[49], [50], [51]]. Voltammetry offers very low LoDs and facile multiplexing via potential-encoded labels, at the expense of increasing assay complexity and sensitivity to electrode kinetics and surface fouling.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) occupies a distinctive niche among electrochemical methods because it is inherently label-free, probing changes in the interfacial charge-transfer resistance and double-layer capacitance that arise when an analyte binds to the electrode surface [52,53]. Many ctDNA genosensors immobilise capture probes and monitor increases in charge-transfer resistance (Rct) upon hybridisation in plasma or spiked serum [54,55]. EIS is attractive for simple architectures and real-time monitoring, but in complex matrices small analyte-induced changes can be obscured by drift and nonspecific adsorption, and oversimplified equivalent-circuit fits can misassign noise as signal [56,57]. These issues have limited the number of EIS-based devices that progress beyond proof-of-concept.

Electrochemiluminescence

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) combines electrochemical excitation of luminophores with photon detection. Ru(bpy)32+-based ECL immunoarrays, in particular, have achieved pg mL−1 sensitivities for prostate specific antigen (PSA) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [58], CEA [59], and other cancer proteins in serum. Arrays of capture spots are incubated with sample and ECL-labeled detection antibodies; upon application of a potential waveform, light intensity at each spot is proportional to analyte concentration. ECL offers negligible optical background, high sensitivity, and natural compatibility with multiplex microarrays. Its main drawbacks are the need for optical components and sensitivity to electrode fouling and luminophore stability. At present, ECL is most mature for serum protein markers; its application to ctDNA, CTCs, and EVs is emerging but less clinically advanced [35,37,60].

Biomarker classes for electrochemical liquid biopsy

Electrochemical liquid biopsy platforms can, in principle, read out any circulating species that (i) can be selectively captured (or enriched) from blood-derived matrices and (ii) produces a reproducible interfacial change or label-derived signal at an electrode. ctDNA, CTCs, EVs, circulating miRNA, and soluble proteins are currently the five biomarkers in practice. They represent clinical questions of genotype, prognosis, treatment monitoring, and heterogeneity profiling that can be at least partially captured using affinity-based capture and electrochemical readout [26,29,35,61].

Circulating tumor DNA

ctDNA, the tumor-derived component of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), underpins liquid biopsy approaches that enable non-invasive, serial molecular profiling for genotyping and treatment monitoring of actionable alterations. Key to the development and application of tests for detection and measurement of ctDNA is the recognition of its extremely low fractional abundance relative to the dominant component of wild-type (wt) cfDNA, necessitating assays that incorporate superior amplification and robust suppression of wild-type background signal to accurately measure these low mutant allele fractions [62,63]. Electrochemical ctDNA biosensors employ a strategy that involves sequence-selective capture and voltammetric transduction; amplification is often achieved via a multi-step process. Recent primary studies in this field include magnetic nanoparticle network-based approaches for highly sensitive hybridization readout in complex matrices [47] and CRISPR/dCas9-based electrochemical strategies for single-nucleotide discrimination and genotyping of patient blood samples [49]. A clinically framed early example is Electric Field-Induced Release and Measurement (EFIRM), which reports mutation detection in patient saliva/plasma with comparison to tissue genotyping in NSCLC [39].

Circulating tumor cells

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) are rare, intact cancer cells in the blood that preserve essential phenotypic and molecular context for prognosis and treatment monitoring. However, their extreme rarity and high heterogeneity limit capture efficiency, as single-epitope assays frequently miss critical subpopulations that lack the targeted marker [[64], [65], [66]]. Electrochemical CTC platforms typically combine affinity capture (antibody or aptamer) with or without magnetic enrichment and voltammetric or amperometric detection by attached redox labels or catalytic tags. A recent example of such an approach is the aptamer-based capture of rare target cells by aptamer-functionalized magnetic graphene/AuNPs by Dou et al. who achieved direct electrochemical detection of target cells in whole blood with multiplexed voltammetric signals [67]. Broader CTC-focused reviews emphasize that, beyond analytical sensitivity, clinical credibility hinges on standardization and benchmarking against established enumeration paradigms [68].

Extracellular vesicles

EVs are membrane-bound particles released by both healthy and malignant cells that carry a rich cargo of proteins, mRNAs, and microRNAs. Based on biogenesis and size, EVs encompass small EVs (sEV, typically <200 nm) and large EVs (lEV, typically >200 nm), as defined by the updated MISEV2023 nomenclature [69,70]. Because they actively mediate intercellular communication and reflect the phenotypic state of their cell of origin, tumour-derived EVs offer a valuable, complementary window into tumour biology and spatial heterogeneity [[71], [72], [73]]. The key barrier to clinical use is analytical specificity: tumour-derived EVs must be distinguished from abundant normal EVs in blood. Diagnostic accuracy therefore depends on strict pre-analytical control and well-designed multiplex marker panels to detect the rare tumor fraction [74,75].

Electrochemical EV assays commonly use immunocapture/aptamer capture of surface markers followed by enzyme/redox labeling or (less often) label-free interfacial readouts. Dezhakam et al. summarise electrochemical approaches for EV analysis and highlight the specificity and standardization issues that dominate translation [76].

Circulating microRNAs

Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) are short, non-coding RNA molecules (typically 22 nucleotides) that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. They are detectable in blood in a remarkably stable extracellular form, a property that helped catalyze their early adoption as liquid-biopsy candidates. In seminal work, Mitchell et al. demonstrated that miRNAs persist in human plasma in a form protected from endogenous RNase activity and that tumor-derived miRNAs can be measured in circulation, including the use of serum miR-141 to distinguish prostate cancer patients from controls [77]. Mechanistically, extracellular miRNA stability and partitioning reflect multiple carrier states rather than a single dominant packaging mode. Arroyo et al. provided evidence that a substantial fraction of circulating miRNAs in human plasma co-fractionate with Argonaute2 (Ago2) ribonucleoprotein complexes (i.e., non-vesicle-associated), while a subset associates predominantly with vesicles, supporting a mixed population model [78]. In parallel, Vickers et al. showed that high-density lipoproteins (HDL) can transport endogenous miRNAs in plasma and deliver them to recipient cells with functional consequences, supporting lipoprotein association as an additional extracellular carrier mechanism [79]. From an oncology standpoint, the promise of circulating miRNAs is that tumors often exhibit dysregulated miRNA expression programs and that at least some of these alterations can be reflected in plasma/serum measurements (sometimes years prior to clinical diagnosis in specific cohorts/settings). For example, Boeri et al. reported miRNA signatures in tissues and plasma linked to development/prognosis of CT-detected lung cancer in heavy smokers, illustrating the broader “preclinical window” motivation for miRNA-based screening/risk stratification (noting that performance depends strongly on cohort design and pre-analytics) [80].

Soluble protein biomarkers

Soluble protein biomarkers are among the most clinically established circulating indicators in oncology, offering straightforward integration with multiplex arrays and sandwich electrochemical immunoassays. However, their primary limitation is biological specificity; because many of these proteins are also produced by non-malignant tissues, distinguishing tumor-derived signals from background noise typically requires multi-marker panels or longitudinal monitoring rather than relying on single-analyte thresholds [37,[81], [82], [83]]. Electrochemical protein assays frequently mirror ELISA architectures but replace optical readout with amperometric or ECL signals. Multiplex electrochemical/ECL immunoarrays reviewed by Munge et al. include examples of serum testing and correlation to reference immunoassays-an important benchmark for translational credibility [37]. From a device perspective, proteins are often the most practical near-term targets for scalable electrochemical panels, provided that matrix effects, calibration, and assay standardization are handled to clinical-laboratory expectations [35,37].

Mapping electrochemical sensors to cancer-care phases

Electrochemical liquid-biopsy sensors span a wide range of maturity, from analytically impressive “signal-amplified” assays to more workflow-integrated sample-to-answer devices. Mapping them to cancer-care phases is best done by asking (i) what clinical decision is being supported [84], (ii) what analytical regime that decision requires (absolute concentration vs variant allele fraction (VAF) vs cell counts) [82,85], and (iii) ensuring that workflow, quality control, and performance relative to clinical standards are compatible with either centralized laboratories or point-of-care settings.

Phase 1: screening and early detection

The screening phase presents the most demanding analytical challenge: detecting tumor-derived analytes at their lowest concentrations, in asymptomatic individuals, with acceptable specificity to avoid false positives in large populations. The CancerSEEK multi-analyte blood test, which integrated genetic and proteomic profiles, achieved a median sensitivity of 70% and specificity of >99%, while successfully localising the primary tumour to one or two anatomic sites in 83% of positive cases, demonstrating the clinical potential of combining orthogonal biomarkers for early-stage cancer screening [86]. While next-generation sequencing (NGS) has established the clinical viability of multi-cancer early detection (MCED), its heavy reliance on centralised laboratory infrastructure, high per-test costs, and complex bioinformatics limits widespread accessibility [87,88]. Electrochemical platforms offer a rapid, decentralized alternative. By leveraging multiplexed sensor arrays to simultaneously profile diverse circulating biomarkers, these devices enable low-cost, point-of-care cancer screening without the need for off-site processing [89].

Electrochemical sensors excel in this screening phase by achieving femtomolar or picomolar detection limits through the specific binding of target analytes to engineered electrode interfaces. Recent innovations have achieved the sensitivity required for early detection. The gold-coated magnetic nanoparticle network platform detects ctDNA at 3.3 aM within 20 min [47], approaching single-molecule sensitivity. A ratiometric electrochemical switch using recycling activation of blocked DNAzymes reports an LoD of 25 aM, using two electroactive species to improve robustness against drift as shown in Fig. 2 A [51].

Another group developed an aptasensor that enabled sensitive detection of C-reactive protein, an early marker of risk for acute coronary events, with an LOD of 0.44 pg/mL and rapid readable results within 5 min [92]. In addition, an ultrasensitive dual-signal amplification biosensor was developed for the analysis of mixtures of two EV-associated circRNAs to identify early gastric cancer patients from healthy donors with an extraordinary LOD of 153.1 aM [93]. The circulating miRNAs seem particularly promising, as they are stable in plasma and sensitive to amplify electrochemical detection methods, to develop biomarkers for early cancer detection and diagnosis, such as the cancer-specific upregulated miR-21 [94] and miR-2115-3p [95].

These studies demonstrate that electrochemical sensors are ultrasensitive, enable rapid determination of biomarkers from very small samples and are appropriate for screening scenarios where the low levels of the biomarker of interest still exceed the LoD of the sensor.

Phase 2: Diagnostic profiling and staging

Following initial detection, precise molecular profiling is essential to determine the cancer subtype, cellular origin, and disease stage. Miniaturized, multiplexed sensor designs allow for the simultaneous parallel detection of multiple diagnostic biomarkers without the extended processing times of traditional biopsies [5,96].

An electrochemical CRISPR/dCas9 assay reports an LoD of 2.86 fM with a 6-order dynamic range, and demonstrates strong discrimination of mutant targets in a large wild-type background (reported as 0.001% mutant fraction capability), alongside application to NSCLC patient blood samples [49]. Another advanced multiplexed amperometric platform specifically targeted the aggressive breast cancer marker HER2 on screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs), achieving a highly sensitive LOD of 0.5 ng/mL using purely electrochemical signal transduction [96]. A voltammetric ctDNA platform combines a PNA capture probe (mutation recognition) with an anti-5-methylcytosine antibody (methylation recognition), using square-wave voltammetry (SWV) of Pb2+ tags released from lead-phosphate apoferritin. It reports an LoD of 10 fM (linear range 50-10,000 fM) and includes testing in cancer patient serum as shown in Fig. 2 B [48]. This is profiling-oriented because it reads two molecular features rather than a single sequence target. An aptamer-functionalized magnetic graphene/AuNP-array approach enables magnetic enrichment and voltammetric detection with two distinct peaks for multiplex cell-type discrimination. It reports detection limits of 4 cells/mL and 3 cells/mL (two model cell types) in whole blood, aligning with staging logic where rare-cell burden can act as a dissemination proxy [67]. A liquid-biopsy biochip integrates electrical lysis/release, nanofluidic acceleration of solid-phase isothermal amplification, and nanozyme-mediated electrochemical detection, reporting a 50-copy detection limit within 30 min and demonstrating prostate-cancer liquid-biopsy applications including risk prediction and relapse monitoring-use-cases that overlap with baseline staging/stratification workflows [97]. Circulating miRNAs contribute an orthogonal layer of molecular information at this phase: electrochemical assays targeting miRNA-652 on screen-printed electrodes have demonstrated subtype discrimination in triple-negative breast cancer [98]. Overall, electrochemical platforms support fast, multiplex tumor-marker profiling, improving molecular characterization that can inform subtype definition and staging decisions.

Phase 3: Therapeutic monitoring and treatment evaluation

Once the pharmacological treatment has been initiated, electrochemical sensors provide real-time information to assess the efficacy of drugs and the levels of therapeutically active compounds in the biological matrix. Real-time therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) performed using versatile direct electrical sensors enables the detection of drugs and their metabolites based on oxidation and reduction peak currents specific for each analyte, which guides clinicians in tailoring dosages to optimize treatment efficacy and prevent adverse effects [99]. Electrochemical methods for the determination of chemotherapeutic compounds have been rapidly evolving. A novel electrochemical sensor for the determination of chemotherapeutic drugs was developed by modifying a glassy carbon electrode with single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and double-stranded DNA (ds-DNA). The resulting differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) sensor detected the anthracycline chemotherapy drug doxorubicin by intercalating into DNA; it provided accurate measurement of the compound at sub-nanomolar concentrations in both model and biological samples, with an LOD of 0.6 nM [100]. An ultrahigh-sensitivity electrochemical doxorubicin sensor was developed based on a VMSF/p-GCE modified substrate. The anti-fouling properties of VMSF and electrostatic preconcentration through nanochannel walls enabled ultrahigh-sensitivity analysis of doxorubicin in unprocessed human whole blood samples without pretreatment. An ultra-low LOD of 0.2 nM and a broad linear range from 0.5 nM to 23 μM were achieved, as shown in Fig. 2C [90]. For anti-metabolite drugs, a highly sensitive electrochemical sensor modified with NiMn2O4/multi-walled carbon nanotube nanocomposite was fabricated for the real-time determination of methotrexate in aqueous solution using voltammetric techniques. The modified sensor showed a very low LOD of 0.627 nM for methotrexate [101]. Electrochemical sensors can be employed in the treatment phase for sensitive real-time analysis of drugs/biomarkers to aid in dose finding and treatment response assessment.

Phase 4: Post-treatment surveillance and recurrence monitoring

Monitoring recurrence of cancer requires long-term surveillance to detect molecular relapse before clinical or imaging recurrence. Electrochemical circulating tumor cell (CTC) sensors allow sensitive tracking of CTCs through selective capture at functionalized electrodes and subsequent impedance or voltammetry readout. Zhou et al. developed a dual-recognition cytosensor strategy incorporating mucin-1 (MUC1) aptamer–functionalized PdPtCuRu mesoporous nanospheres and differential pulse voltammetry, enabling capture and detection of less than 10 cancer cells/mL directly in undiluted and unprocessed human serum [102]. A self-assembled DNA tetrahedron-based electrochemical aptasensor for HepG2 liver tumour cell detection, combined with dendritic nanoprobes, achieved detection down to a single cell (5 cells/mL) using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) [103]. Furthermore, an advanced anodic stripping voltammetry/cyclic voltammetry (ASV/CV) dual-signal electrochemical biosensor was constructed by utilizing quantum dots to not only facilitate the specific capturing and accumulation of CTCs but also monitor the process for metastasis tracking; intriguingly, the biosensor displayed an ultra-low LOD of 3 cells/mL, as depicted in Fig. 2D [91]. For monitoring of post-treatment relapse, highly sensitive electrochemical sensing platforms can be used for detection and characterization of rare circulating tumor cells that are associated with relapse and metastasis.

Technical validation and the path to clinical implementation

Pure electrochemical biosensors can achieve striking analytical sensitivity in controlled experiments, but translation into routine oncology testing requires more than low limits of detection. The dominant barriers are usually system-level: robustness in real clinical matrices, reproducibility across lots and sites, validated clinical decision thresholds, and a regulatory-quality manufacturing workflow. A recent perspective on translation emphasizes that limited commercial uptake is often driven by these practical constraints rather than by analytical performance ceilings [117].

Recent innovations and analytical validation

Bridging laboratory prototypes to point-of-care deployment increasingly depends on interface and workflow engineering, not only on improving nominal limits of detection. A major advance is the integration of microfluidic sample handling with electrochemical transducers, which enables on-chip handling of whole blood through modules such as plasma filtration or separation upstream of an electrochemical immunoassay, thereby reducing manual preprocessing and operator dependence [28]. In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are being applied to electrochemical signal processing to improve classification and quantification from complex voltammetric and impedimetric datasets, particularly under conditions affected by fouling, matrix effects, and poor signal-to-noise ratio. In practice, these methods can reduce reliance on manual feature selection and ad hoc calibration heuristics by learning relationships directly from multivariate electrochemical data [118]. Finally, anti-biofouling surface chemistries are becoming central to clinical readiness because they preserve electron-transfer kinetics and stabilize baselines in undiluted biofluids. Vertically ordered mesoporous silica films (VMSF) have been used to enable direct voltammetric measurements in unprocessed whole blood, illustrating a concrete route to matrix-tolerant sensing [90]. More broadly, chemical antifouling strategies used in electrochemical sensors include PEG-based coatings and zwitterionic polymers, and zwitterionic copolymer coatings have been reported as thin anti-fouling layers for electrochemical biosensing in biological fluids [119].

Clinical validation requirements

Before clinical deployment, electrochemical sensors must undergo rigorous, multi-tiered validation. Analytical validation follows Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, which specify clinical-laboratory conventions for detection capability rather than simply reporting the lowest tested concentration as the “LoD.” CLSI EP17 provides the standard framework for estimating and verifying limit of blank (LoB), limit of detection (LoD), and limit of quantitation (LoQ), and it is explicitly intended for both commercial IVDs and laboratory-developed tests [120]. Following analytical verification, clinical validation necessitates comprehensive method-comparison studies against gold-standard techniques such as ELISA, liquid chromatography, or quantitative PCR. These evaluations must utilize large, diverse patient cohorts to account for intra- and inter-patient biomarker variability, matrix effects, and disease co-morbidities [121].

Regulatory pathway for oncology diagnostics

In addition to technical challenges, commercializing electrochemical cancer diagnostics involves navigating a strict, risk-based regulatory environment. For products intending to be marketed in the United States, electrochemical oncology assays are regulated as in vitro diagnostic (IVD) devices under a risk-based framework. Potential submission pathways for these products include 510(k), De Novo, or PMA depending on intended use and availability of predicate devices [122]. For oncology clinical trials of investigational IVDs codeveloped with cancer therapies, FDA guidance provides for a more simplified determination of study risk than typically applies [123]. For products being introduced to the market in Europe, the commercialisation regulations are governed by Regulation (EU) 2017/746 (IVDR), focusing on the performance evaluation, risk-based categorisation and generation of evidence across the product lifecycle [124]. Across jurisdictions, scalable manufacturing and design control typically require an ISO 13485 quality management system to support traceability, supplier controls, and controlled change management [125].

Implementation barriers and scalability

A central barrier is lot-to-lot control of the sensing interface. Electrochemical sensors can be mass-produced at the electrode level, but reproducibly functionalizing the surface with bioreceptors and maintaining consistent electrochemical baselines remains difficult, especially for nanostructured interfaces [117]. Highly amplified ctDNA assays can be analytically impressive but operationally fragile. Integrated sample-to-answer architectures reduce transfers, simplify contamination control, and are more consistent with routine clinical operation [97]. Implementation also requires software that converts raw voltammograms or impedance spectra into stable, interpretable clinical results with QC flags, since clinical users cannot manually interpret Nyquist plots or peak assignments at scale. Finally, for Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) laboratory deployment, performance specifications must be verified before patient reporting, including accuracy, precision, and reportable range [126].

Barriers to clinical implementation, future perspectives, and conclusion

Electrochemical biosensor development has multiple obstacles that need to be overcome before they can transition from a lab environment to a regulatory clinical setting. The first obstacle is matrix complexity; this includes protein, lipid and nucleic acid components within whole blood or plasma which adhere to electrodes, degrades recognition elements and shifts baseline values. In addition, most of these sensors have been characterized with buffer solutions such as saline versus clinical samples. Furthermore, there are few studies comparing them directly to other well-established diagnostic platforms within actual patient populations [117,127].

Standardization is also required. A number of pre-analytic parameters that include tube types for collecting blood, speed at which centrifuged, and whether or how many times thawed/frozen will significantly affect the concentration values of analyzed substances. Therefore, laboratory to laboratory comparisons cannot be accurately made until standardized protocols have been adopted [128,129]. Regulatory approval adds further weight: assays must satisfy in-vitro diagnostic (IVD) requirements or Premarket Approval pathways in the United States) [130], while in Europe the IVDR (EU 2017/746) imposes risk-based classification, notified-body review, and post-market surveillance [131]. Both are based on the requirement that all manufactured products meet the ISO 13485 standard; however, there is limited evidence at scales greater than pilot scale demonstrating the consistent preparation of nano-structure electrode interfaces.

In addition to those, there are practical barriers in terms of data integration. A sensor that lacks the appropriate interface to connect to either laboratory information management systems (LIMS) or electronic health records (EHR), lack a definition of measurement uncertainty, and does not provide quality control flags can never become part of standard operating procedures for the workflow regardless of its analytical value. Ultimately, economic evidence is becoming increasingly required by both payers and Health Technology Assessment (HTA) agencies prior to providing reimbursement; therefore, it is just as critical to model the costs associated with each technology as soon as possible as it is to validate its clinical effectiveness [[132], [133], [134]].

The future of electrochemical biosensing in oncology will be shaped less by incremental reductions in nominal limits of detection and more by the development of clinically integrated platforms that provide reproducible, decision-relevant outputs from real patient samples. Across recent reviews, a consistent theme is that many electrochemical liquid-biopsy demonstrations remain proof-of-concept: performance is often established in buffer or simplified matrices, while robustness in minimally processed plasma/serum/whole blood, long-term stability, and cross-batch reproducibility are underdeveloped [26,29,35,135,136]. As a result, the next stage of progress hinges on demonstrating reliable function in complex clinical matrices and generating outputs that clinicians can interpret with confidence and compare against existing standards of care.

A major direction is the convergence of electrochemical sensing with microfluidic sample handling and automated preprocessing, which can reduce operator-dependent variability and enable more realistic “sample-to-answer” workflows. Translation-focused discussions emphasize that automation and integration are central to point-of-care feasibility, particularly when the target is extremely low abundance and when pre-analytical variation (collection, storage, processing delays) can dominate error budgets [29,135]. Representative integrated architectures include nanofluidic manipulation and solid-phase isothermal amplification coupled to electrochemical detection for ctDNA analysis in prostate-cancer liquid biopsies, illustrating how workflow integration can enable rapid turnaround (tens of minutes) with clinically framed applications such as relapse monitoring [97]. Similar integration themes appear for multiplex protein immunoarrays, where microfluidic automation is positioned as a prerequisite for reproducible multi-marker measurements rather than a convenience feature [37]. Point-of-care-oriented evaluations also increasingly use explicit deployment criteria (e.g., REASSURED framing) to assess whether electrochemical devices are realistic in clinics rather than only analytically impressive in the lab [137].

Another important direction is the expansion of multiplexed electrochemical platforms that can quantify panels spanning proteins, nucleic acids, extracellular vesicles, and circulating tumor cells. Tumor heterogeneity-both biological and technical-is widely recognized as a driver of false negatives and ambiguous interpretation in single-marker assays, motivating multi-target approaches that better reflect disease biology [5]. Multiplex breast-cancer biomarker discussions similarly emphasize that electrochemical readouts are inherently amenable to miniaturization and parallelization (arrays, multi-electrode formats), supporting low-cost, potentially disposable devices, though they also highlight practical challenges such as cross-talk, differential fouling, and calibration/normalization across channels [5,37,96].

AI and ML are expected to become increasingly relevant as multiplexing and longitudinal measurements expand [138]. Point-of-care biosensing perspectives anticipate ML-driven signal processing and classification to improve robustness against drift, matrix effects, and overlapping biomarker signatures, particularly when repeated measurements generate high-dimensional datasets that exceed simple threshold-based interpretation [139]. Clinical adoption will additionally require appropriately designed clinical studies and method-comparison evidence against gold standards, since many reports still lack robust head-to-head datasets and clinically meaningful validation endpoints.

Electrochemical biosensors have demonstrated genuine analytical capability across the liquid biopsy landscape. Translating that capability into clinical impact will require standardised workflows, rigorous validation against established assays, regulatory-grade manufacturing, and integration into real clinical environments. Where these conditions are met, electrochemical biosensing is well placed to make cancer diagnostics faster, less invasive, and more accessible.
