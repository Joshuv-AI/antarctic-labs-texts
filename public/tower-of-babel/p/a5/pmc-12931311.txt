Disseminated cutaneous sarcoidosis triggered by eyebrow tattoo: a case report highlighting lesions beyond tattooed sites
Authors: Lara Saeid, Moatasem Ali Hussein Al-janabi, Fouz Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcoidosis is a multisystem granulomatous disease commonly involving the lungs and lymph nodes, but it may also affect the skin. Tattoos have been recognized as potential triggers for cutaneous sarcoid lesions. We report a case of a 46-year-old female who developed sarcoidosis presenting as asymptomatic papules and plaques initially confined to her tattooed eyebrows, followed by the appearance of similar lesions at distant non-tattooed sites, including the upper back and elbows. Histopathology confirmed cutaneous sarcoidosis, and imaging revealed bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, indicating systemic involvement. The patient responded well to systemic corticosteroid therapy after topical treatment failed. This case highlights the unique phenomenon of sarcoid skin lesions occurring beyond tattoo sites, emphasizing the systemic nature of the disease and the importance of thorough clinical evaluation and management.Keywords: sarcoidosis, cutaneous sarcoidosis, tattoo, granulomatous inflammation, systemic sarcoidosis, skin lesions, corticosteroids

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a chronic inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology characterized by the formation of non-caseating granulomas in various organs. These granulomas predominantly affect the lungs and mediastinal lymph nodes, but may also involve the skin, eyes, heart, and other tissues [1]. Since the first documented case of sarcoidosis developing in tattooed skin in 1939 [2], it has become increasingly recognized that tattoos may trigger localized inflammatory reactions, including cutaneous sarcoid lesions, in susceptible individuals [3].

What makes this case particularly notable is the development of sarcoid skin lesions not only on the tattooed areas but also at distant, non-tattooed sites. This phenomenon highlights the systemic nature of sarcoidosis and suggests that tattoo-induced inflammation can precipitate a more generalized cutaneous sarcoid reaction.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old woman, a non-smoker, presented to the dermatology department with a 6-month history of asymptomatic skin eruptions. The lesions initially appeared on her tattooed eyebrows and rapidly extended over several days to involve the upper back and elbows, areas that were not tattooed.

The patient denied any prior medical history or systemic symptoms. Physical examination revealed erythematous, infiltrated, non-pruritic plaques and papules localized on the tattooed eyebrows as well as on distant sites, including the upper back and elbows (Fig. 1). No other abnormalities were noted on systemic examination.

Clinical examination of the patient showing infiltrated plaques on the eyebrows, elbows, and upper back.

She reported undergoing eyebrow tattooing approximately 15 months before presentation.

A skin biopsy obtained from a representative non-tattooed lesion on the upper back revealed non-caseating granulomatous inflammation consistent with cutaneous sarcoidosis (Fig. 2).

Skin biopsy revealing non-caseating granulomas.

Routine laboratory investigations were within normal limits. Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) level was not assessed due to unavailability of the test at our institution. Chest X-ray and computed tomography revealed bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, supporting the diagnosis of systemic sarcoidosis.

Initial treatment with topical clobetasol propionate 0.05% resulted in minimal clinical improvement and was limited by facial involvement and extensive body surface area. Systemic corticosteroid therapy was therefore initiated with prednisolone at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day. Noticeable clinical improvement was observed within one week, involving both facial and truncal lesions. The prednisolone dose was tapered by 5 mg weekly, followed by a maintenance dose of 5 mg daily for three months before discontinuation, with sustained clinical remission.

Discussion

Sarcoidosis is a chronic granulomatous disorder with an unclear etiology, thought to result from an exaggerated immune response to unidentified antigens in genetically predisposed individuals [1]. The disease most commonly involves the lungs and intrathoracic lymph nodes, but cutaneous manifestations are seen in approximately 25% of cases, representing a readily accessible marker of systemic disease activity [4, 5].

Tattooing introduces exogenous pigments and metallic compounds into the dermis, which can serve as persistent antigenic stimuli. These materials—particularly black carbon, titanium dioxide, and metal oxides—are phagocytosed by dermal macrophages and may trigger a Th1/Th17-dominant immune response, leading to granuloma formation [6]. The local inflammatory microenvironment in tattooed skin may therefore act as a nidus for sarcoidal granuloma development in susceptible hosts.

While sarcoid reactions confined to tattooed sites are well documented, the occurrence of lesions at distant, non-tattooed sites—as observed in our case— supports the concept that an initial localized granulomatous response may evolve into a systemic immune process in predisposed individuals. Dissemination of cutaneous lesions beyond tattooed sites is hypothesized to result from systemic Th1/Th17-mediated immune activation rather than a purely localized foreign body response [1, 5].

Clinically, this pattern suggests that tattoo-induced sarcoidosis may represent the first manifestation of systemic disease rather than an isolated cutaneous reaction. Indeed, in our patient, chest imaging revealed bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy consistent with pulmonary involvement. Similar findings have been described by Antonovich and Callen [3], who reported systemic sarcoidosis triggered by cosmetic tattooing.

The differential diagnosis of tattoo-related granulomatous reactions includes foreign body granulomas, infectious granulomatous diseases (such as mycobacterial or fungal infections), and allergic contact dermatitis to tattoo pigments. Histologically, sarcoidosis is characterized by well-formed non-caseating ‘naked’ granulomas composed of epithelioid histiocytes, often separated by fibrous stroma and showing minimal surrounding lymphocytic inflammation. This pattern helps distinguish sarcoidosis from foreign body granulomas, which typically demonstrate identifiable exogenous material and a more prominent inflammatory response. However, the presence of non-caseating granulomas without necrosis and the absence of infectious organisms on special stains favor the diagnosis of sarcoidosis [4].

Management should be individualized according to disease extent. Topical corticosteroids may provide symptomatic relief in localized cases, but systemic corticosteroids remain the mainstay of therapy for disseminated cutaneous or systemic disease, as illustrated in our patient. Alternative immunomodulatory agents such as methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, or azathioprine can be considered for refractory cases or for steroid-sparing purposes [5].

In summary, this case highlights that sarcoidosis triggered by tattooing can extend beyond tattooed sites, reflecting the systemic and immunologically mediated nature of the disease. Clinicians should therefore perform thorough systemic evaluation of any patient presenting with sarcoid-like reactions at tattoo sites, including chest imaging and laboratory testing for extrapulmonary involvement. Early recognition of disseminated disease is crucial to initiate appropriate therapy and prevent chronic complications.

Conclusion

This case underscores the potential for tattoo-induced sarcoidosis to manifest not only as a localized cutaneous reaction but also as part of a disseminated, systemic disease process. The appearance of sarcoid lesions beyond the tattooed area highlights the capacity of tattoo pigments to trigger widespread immune activation in predisposed individuals. Therefore, any patient presenting with granulomatous inflammation within tattooed skin should undergo comprehensive systemic evaluation, including imaging and laboratory assessment, to rule out multisystem involvement. Recognition of this pattern is crucial for timely diagnosis and appropriate management. While topical corticosteroids may be adequate for localized lesions, systemic therapy is often required when dissemination or systemic organ involvement occurs. Early intervention generally leads to excellent clinical outcomes and may prevent chronic or fibrotic complications of sarcoidosis.

Clinical significance

This case emphasizes the importance of considering systemic sarcoidosis in patients who develop granulomatous lesions confined to or extending beyond tattooed areas. Early recognition of extra-tattoo sarcoid lesions can prevent delays in diagnosis of systemic involvement, particularly pulmonary disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, as tattoo-related cutaneous sarcoidosis may represent the first visible manifestation of a multisystem granulomatous disorder. Comprehensive assessment and prompt initiation of systemic therapy can significantly improve prognosis.
