A Test of the Cognitive Benefits of Bilingualism With the Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task
Authors: Alexandra Piuze, Sayde Jennings, Diane Poulin‐Dubois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is some evidence suggesting that bilingual children differ from monolingual children in cognitive processing due to their exposure to a more complex environment. While several studies have supported this hypothesis, most research has focused on school‐age children. The present study examined whether differences in inhibitory control between monolinguals and bilinguals are present early in life with the Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task (ECITT). This task provides a reliable measure of inhibition control in infants, requiring them to shift their responses from a previously rewarded location to a new one. It minimizes language and memory demands while engaging infants through animations, making it an ideal measure for very young children. A sample of 60 infants (mean age 20.21 months) was tested, with language exposure measured on a continuum using the Language Exposure Assessment Tool (LEAT). Vocabulary was assessed using the McArthur‐Bates Communicative Development Inventory (MCDI), and inhibitory control was assessed, as well as a range of executive functions, with the Early Executive Functions Questionnaire (EEFQ). The ECITT data replicate previous research, showing that overall performance on this task improves with age. However, inhibitory control skills did not vary as a function of the amount of second language exposure. The current results suggest that inhibitory control skills do not differ as a function of language exposure diversity in toddlers younger than 24 months.

Introduction

Over the past decades, a large body of work has examined whether bilingualism is associated with a range of cognitive benefits, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the bilingual cognitive advantage hypothesis (BCA; Bialystok 2017). One of the most frequently examined cognitive domains underlying this advantage is executive functioning (EF), a set of higher‐order cognitive processes that support goal‐directed behavior, self‐regulation, and flexible thinking, particularly in nonroutine or complex situations (Miyake et al. 2000; Arizmendi et al. 2018). The constant management of two or more active language systems, which requires individuals to selectively attend to the target language while suppressing interference from the non‐target language, has been proposed as a means to strengthen these control processes (Green and Abutalebi 2013; Bialystok 2017).

Miyake and colleagues' (2000) framework, refined by Friedman and Miyake (2004), identifies three core components of executive function (EF): updating, shifting, and inhibition. Updating involves managing and manipulating information in working memory (Arizmendi et al. 2018; Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022), shifting enables transitions between tasks or mental sets (Calcott and Berkman 2015), and inhibition involves suppressing automatic responses to favor goal‐relevant behavior (Kang et al. 2022). EF can also be divided into hot and cold categories. Hot EF relates to emotional and motivational processing, while cold EF involves neutral, cognitive processing (Salehinejad et al. 2021). Inhibition is divided into hot inhibition, such as response suppression in delay‐of‐gratification tasks (Arizmendi et al. 2018; Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022), and cold inhibition, which is measured in tasks like the Stroop or Flanker, which develops from around 9 months onward (Hendry et al. 2016; Lowe et al. 2021).

Bilingual Cognition in Children

As previously mentioned, one of the primary variables of interest in studying the Bilingual Cognitive Advantage (BCA) is the effect of bilingualism on executive function (EF) skills. According to Green's (1998) inhibitory control model, the bilingual advantage in EF is thought to stem from the continual need to manage competition between two simultaneously active linguistic systems. When bilinguals produce or comprehend speech, both language systems are activated, requiring executive control processes to selectively attend to the target language while suppressing interference from the non‐target language. This process relies on a supervisory attentional system that regulates mental frameworks, or task schemas, governing language use by inhibiting those associated with the irrelevant language. Over time, this constant practice in goal‐directed selection and inhibition is believed to strengthen domain‐specific inhibitory control (Bialystok 2017). Supporting this theory, recent research on infants suggests that bilingual experience can enhance attentional orienting and alter cortical organization, which are foundational to cognitive control mechanisms (Arredondo et al. 2021; Arredondo et al. 2022). These early developments in attentional orienting may lay the groundwork for the later emergence of cold inhibition, enabling bilinguals to suppress distractions and focus more effectively on cognitive tasks.

The BCA transfer hypothesis proposes that bilingualism enhances executive functioning (EF) through “far transfer,” in which cognitive skills used in language control extend to non‐linguistic tasks. Because bilinguals frequently suppress one language while activating another, this repeated practice is thought to strengthen inhibitory control mechanisms (Arizmendi et al. 2018). More potent inhibition may support performance on interference‐heavy tasks, such as the Stroop, where suppressing competing information is essential. From this perspective, the simultaneous activation of multiple language systems creates constant demands for linguistic inhibition, which may generalize to more efficient EF skills (Noble et al. 2012).

More recently, however, the Adaptation Model has been proposed as an alternative account, arguing that differences between bilinguals and monolinguals do not emerge from the transfer of inhibitory skills across domains. Instead, bilinguals adapt their cognitive system to the ongoing demands of managing two languages, resulting in broader changes in cognitive flexibility and EF (Bialystok and Craik 2022; Bialystok 2024). This framework emphasizes systemic reorganization driven by the dynamic need to select, monitor, and coordinate languages rather than inhibition alone. For example, Bialystok (2025a) argues that the impact of bilingualism should not be reduced to EF advantages but rather understood as a set of context‐dependent, experience‐driven adaptations across multiple cognitive domains. Consistent with this perspective, work on selective attention suggests that attention operates as a flexible, context‐sensitive system that dynamically allocates resources rather than simply inhibiting irrelevant information (Bialystok 2025b), further supporting a shift away from inhibition‐based accounts. Importantly, several studies provide some evidence that inhibitory control might not be the primary mechanism underlying bilingual cognitive differences. For example, the transfer of inhibitory control skills cannot account for findings of a bilingual advantage in preverbal infants (e.g., Bialystok 2015), who have not yet developed language‐based inhibition skills. Such evidence suggests that bilingual experience may enhance attention switching rather than inhibitory control, even early in development (D’Souza and D’Souza 2016; D’Souza et al. 2020). Whereas inhibitory control involves suppressing irrelevant or competing information, attention switching refers to the ability to shift focus between stimuli or tasks flexibly. Bilingual infants' early exposure to variable linguistic input may therefore strengthen their capacity to reorient attention efficiently, a mechanism that better explains EF differences observed before language production and inhibition skills fully emerge.

Results from several meta‐analyses (Gunnerud et al. 2020; Lowe et al. 2021) suggest that any bilingual advantage in EF skills is likely domain‐specific, rather than domain‐general. This difference means that bilinguals may show advantages in specific EF components, such as managing lexical interference and inhibiting conflicting information, rather than across all EF tasks. Studies have found bilingual advantages in monitoring both languages and suppressing competing linguistic inputs (e.g., Colomé 2001; Kaushanskaya and Marian 2007). As a result, the current study focuses on specific components of executive functions, inhibitory control, given the lack of evidence for a broad cognitive advantage in bilinguals. Within this framework, research has examined potential bilingual advantages across different components of executive functioning (EF) and across developmental stages, with findings varying across both domains and age groups.

One domain that has received considerable attention is working memory, where some evidence suggests a potential bilingual advantage. For instance, Morales et al. (2012) found that bilingual children aged 5–7 outperformed monolingual peers on working memory and visuospatial tasks. However, this apparent advantage may reflect the involvement of other EF components rather than working memory per se. Indeed, several studies, including those by Bialystok (2017) and Poulin‐Dubois et al. (2022), have failed to replicate these findings, instead reporting no significant differences between bilingual and monolingual children.

Beyond working memory, attentional flexibility, which is often linked to managing multiple languages, has also been proposed as a domain in which bilinguals might show an advantage. However, evidence in this area remains inconsistent. While some individual studies suggest benefits, meta‐analyses have found no reliable advantage in attentional flexibility across childhood (Gunnerud et al. 2020; Lowe et al. 2021). A similar pattern emerges in the domain of emotion regulation. Although Melo et al. (2024) reported that bilingual preschoolers demonstrated better self‐regulation than monolinguals, this effect was contingent on high‐quality teacher–child interactions. Moreover, this finding has not been replicated in younger populations; for example, Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois (2022) found no differences in 23‐month‐olds using parent‐report measures, suggesting that any potential advantages may depend on both contextual and developmental factors.

Hot and Cold Inhibition and Multilingualism

Inhibition can be divided into three categories: attention inhibition, cold inhibition, and hot inhibition. Hot inhibition involves suppressing a response to obtain a reward, as seen in gift‐delay tasks. Cold inhibition requires the ability to inhibit an automatic or prepotent response to a neutral stimulus, as in the Stroop or go/no‐go tasks. Attention inhibition is the ability to focus on a target and suppress attention to distractor stimuli, such as in the Flanker task. When examining inhibitory control in bilinguals, findings differ between hot and cold inhibition. In studies of cold inhibition, most research has shown that it may be more potent in bilingual children. For example, Poulin‐Dubois et al. (2011) found that 24‐month‐old bilingual infants performed better on an age‐appropriate Stroop task than their monolingual counterparts. Bilingual preschool children outperform monolinguals on other conflict tasks, such as the Flanker task (Carlson and Meltzoff 2008). Additionally, a recent meta‐analysis by Yurtsever et al. (2023) reported that bilingual children outperform their monolingual counterparts on cold inhibition, monitoring, and shifting even when controlling for task, publication bias, and sample size (see also Gunnerud et al. 2020).

However, few studies on very young children have examined cold and hot inhibition, as most of the tasks measuring these skills can be challenging for young children due to their complexity (Bialystok and Craik 2022; Carlson 2005; Londono et al. 2025). A bilingual advantage in cold inhibition was observed at 24 months on the Baby Stroop task (Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2011), but not at earlier ages, as evidenced by null findings on the detour‐reaching task at 17 months (Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2022). Crivello et al. (2016) found an advantage in task‐specific inhibitory control in a longitudinal study of children aged 24–31 months. This developmental pattern might be due to the challenges of testing infants. Many tasks used to test cold inhibition often have language and working memory demands that are too high for younger children to handle. For example, the Go/No‐Go task, a common task, is too complex for children younger than 3 years of age (Holmboe et al. 2021). There is also a need for more reliable tasks that researchers can use across the lifespan to increase the clarity, replicability, and generalizability of previous findings on a bilingual cognitive advantage in cold inhibition.

Regarding hot inhibition, a meta‐analysis by Lowe et al. (2021) reported greater hot inhibition in children aged 3–17 years. However, this result could be partially explained by the small effect of confounding variables, such as socioeconomic status (SES). Indeed, as Naeem et al. (2018) point out, SES is a vital mediator and must be considered when studying the BCA. This is further supported by evidence that SES independently predicts both EF performance and associated neural measures in young children, even when accounting for bilingual experience (N. H. Brito and Noble 2018). In very young children, Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois (2022) found that increased exposure to a second language was associated with greater response inhibition (hot inhibition) as measured by the Early Executive Functions Questionnaire (EEFQ). The present study builds on Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois’ (2022) work by using the EEFQ with a younger population.

Traditional measures of inhibitory control are often not developmentally appropriate for infants and toddlers, making it difficult to reliably assess early executive function. Holmboe et al. (2021) recently introduced the Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task (ECITT) to address a longstanding gap in the measurement of cold inhibition in very young children. The ECITT was designed as an age‐appropriate inhibition control task administered via touchscreen and has demonstrated applicability across an extensive age range from as young as 10 months to as old as 84 years (Fiske et al. 2022, 2025; Hendry et al. 2021a; Holmboe et al. 2021; Lui et al. 2021). In this new task, participants have to overcome a tendency to respond to a frequently rewarded location on a touchscreen and instead make an alternative response. This innovation provides researchers with a valuable tool for investigating cognitive processes during early developmental stages.

Present Study

The recent introduction of the ECITT presents a unique opportunity to investigate the impact of bilingual exposure on cold inhibition during infancy. The current study leverages this measure to examine potential cognitive differences between monolingual and bilingual children aged 16–24 months. This age range was chosen because previous studies have shown an advantage on the Stroop task at 24 months, and the current study examines whether this advantage can be replicated in a younger population using a well‐validated task. Additionally, this study employs the EEFQ to explore the impact of bilingualism on a broader range of executive functions, including working memory, emotional regulation, and attentional flexibility. The EEFQ was also included as a measure of hot inhibition to determine whether a bilingual advantage reported in toddlers could be replicated (Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022). We hypothesized that greater exposure to a second language would be associated with enhanced cold inhibition. Given past findings, we hypothesized that increased multilingual exposure is unlikely to impact cognitive flexibility, working memory, or regulatory abilities. Furthermore, we predicted that infants categorized as multilingual would exhibit better attentional control than their monolingual peers. This multidimensional approach aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on early bilingualism and executive function.

Methods

The target sample size, included variables, hypotheses, and planned analyses were preregistered on Open Science Framework (registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/6ANC9) before any data were collected.

Participants

Participants were recruited across a large Canadian city through a laboratory database of past participants and advertising in local daycares and libraries. Parents received a recruitment email for a study on the impact of multilingualism on cognition, which involved one online interview and one in‐person session. To be eligible to participate in this study, participants had to be between 16 and 24 months old. They had to have been born full‐term without visual, auditory, or developmental delays. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the American Psychological Association, and the data were collected with approval from the institution's Human Research Ethics committee.

A total of 64 children were tested, of whom four were excluded in full because they were too fussy to participate in any of the tasks (N = 4). Regarding the ECITT analyses, other participants were partially excluded from the ECITT analyses. This exclusion was due to having fewer than 60% of prepotent trials correct (N = 5), resulting in complete exclusion from the ECITT analyses. If participants had fewer than 60% correct prepotent trials, it indicates they did not develop a prepotent response, suggesting their performance is unlikely to reflect response inhibition (Holmboe et al. 2021). Participants who improperly executed the ECITT task (N = 3) (i.e., failing all inhibition trials) were excluded from the reaction time analyses but included in the accuracy analyses. Regarding the EEFQ, one participant was excluded due to experimenter error (N = 1). No participant was excluded for other reasons.

Our final sample consisted of 60 participants (30 females, 28 males, 2 prefer not to say) with a mean age of 20.21 months and a range of 16.30–24.96 months (see Table 1). Responses from the demographic questionnaire indicated that a majority had an annual net income of more than $150,000 (N = 25). Parents predominantly reported their child's ethnic background as European (61.67%), with 40% of participants reporting a mix of ethnic backgrounds. Four parents did not complete the demographics questionnaire, so their demographic data could not be included. Our sample was relatively balanced between monolingual (N = 35; 58.33%) and multilingual (N = 25; 41.67%) infants, with 25% exposure to a second (or multiple) language(s) used as a cutoff, as typically used in early bilingualism research (Byers‐Heinlein et al. 2019; Place and Hoff 2011). Of the participants, 36.67% were children whose first language was English, 40% had French as their first language, and the remaining children spoke either Spanish, Portuguese, Vietnamese, Italian, or Armenian as their first language. Furthermore, 46.67% of participants had English as one of their non‐dominant languages, and 53.33% had French as one of their non‐dominant languages. 60% of children had exposure to more than one non‐dominant language. The mean amount of exposure to a second language across all participants was 24.27%. The mean exposure to a second language among multilingual participants was 43.48%. Additional information on the distribution of language and ethnicity across participants is provided in Supporting Information S1: Tables S1 and S2.

Measures

Demographics Questionnaire: This questionnaire was used to collect basic demographic information, including the child's gender, health history, parents' and child's ethnic backgrounds, household income, occupation, and civil status. This questionnaire also inquired about the parents' and children's language status and the time spent with screens.

Language Exposure Assessment Tool (LEAT): The Language Exposure Assessment Tool (LEAT; DeAnda et al. 2016), a validated measure of early language exposure, was used to assess language input. This Excel‐based tool has been used with children aged 4 months to 5 years old (Kalashnikova et al. 2023; Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022; Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2022). By interviewing parents on who interacts with the child at least once a week, how much time each person spends with the child, at what age they began to interact with the child, and which languages each individual speaks, the LEAT tool calculates each participant's time of exposure to one or more languages per day, week, and throughout their life (DeAnda et al. 2016). The LEAT was used in this study to determine participants' overall exposure to their dominant and non‐dominant languages and to classify participants as monolingual or multilingual. The LEAT interview was conducted with one or both participants' parents via the Zoom videoconferencing platform.

MacArthur‐Bates Communicative Development Inventories: Words and Sentences (MCDI: WS): The MacArthur‐Bates Communicative Development Inventories: Words and Sentences (MCDI: WS is a parent‐report checklist that measures expressive vocabulary and productive grammatical complexity in children aged 16–30 months (Fenson et al. 1993). For this study, participants were assigned to the Canadian‐English and the French‐Canadian long form (Trudeau et al. 1999) based on their exposure to English or French, as determined by the Web CDI (Frank et al. 2017), following the administration of the LEAT interview. Those considered bilingual in English and French were assigned the MCDI: WS in both languages. Participants were assigned the English version, the French‐Canadian version, or both, depending on their reported exposure to the languages. The results of this inventory were used to assess participants' vocabulary size at the time of testing.

Early Executive Functions Questionnaire (EEFQ): The Early Executive Functions Questionnaire (EEFQ) is a 31‐item parent‐report questionnaire used to assess executive functions in children aged 9–30 months (Hendry and Holmboe 2021b). This tool was used to assess inhibitory control (IC), working memory (WM), flexibility (FX), regulation (RG), and cognitive executive function (CEF), which comprises IC, WM, FX, and RG. The first three items of the assessment include games that the parents completed during the in‐person session. The Waiting Game measures IC and requires the parent to place a snack the child enjoys in front of them and to ask the child to wait for up to 30 seconds, with latency to reach the snack serving as the outcome. The Finding Game assesses WM by asking the child to locate a toy hidden under one of two opaque containers across four trials, with correct responses summed. The Sorting Game measures FX by requiring the child to sort small and large spoons into matching bowls, then switch the sorting rules. All games were videotaped for scoring, and parents received both written and video instructions before each task. Once the games were completed, the parents answered 28 questions assessing the EF domains. These questions ask about the child's behavior over the past 2 weeks, and parents respond using a 7‐point Likert scale from “Never” to “Always.” The questionnaire section of the EEFQ was administered via SurveyMonkey in English or Quebec‐French, depending on the parents' preferred language. Each subscale consisted of 7 or 8 items, and each game was treated as a single item.

Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task (ECITT): The Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task (ECITT) measures the ability to inhibit a prepotent response. This task has multiple versions suitable for individuals aged 10 months to 84 years (Holmboe et al. 2021). It is administered as an iPad game for children whose goal is to press one of two buttons, depending on which one has a “happy face” on it. Instructions are straightforward and require little verbal ability and working memory; the only instruction is to press the smiley face. A short animation plays once the correct button is pressed to maintain motivation and attention and to promote understanding of the task requirements. The stimuli were presented on an iPad with an 11‐inch screen. The experimenter controlled the ECITT stimuli via their smartphone.

The iPad was in landscape orientation, with two 24 × 17 mm blue rectangular touchscreen buttons 81 mm apart horizontally. A 14 mm simple “smiley face” icon was presented on either the left or right button, and the stimuli were presented against a dark gray background (see Figure 1 for a visual representation). During one demonstration and practice trial, a single blue button with the smiley face icon, in the exact dimensions as previously described, was displayed in the center of the screen. The touch‐sensitive area included the rectangular buttons and a small area around each one, totaling 44 × 44 mm on each side. This slightly larger response area was designed to accommodate the motor skills of young children.

ECITT schematic structure. Source: Fiske et al. 2022.

The ECITT involved three sets of trials: a demonstration trial in which the experimenter pressed a blue smiley‐face button; a practice trial repeated as needed until the child pressed independently; and a block of 32 experimental trials. Two buttons were shown, and the child was asked to press the smiley‐face button. Correct responses triggered a 3.75–4‐s animation, after which the subsequent trial began automatically. Incorrect responses left the screen unchanged until the child or experimenter pressed the correct button.

To build a prepotent response, the smiley‐face appeared on the same side for 75% of trials (24 prepotent trials), with the remaining 25% (8 trials) on the opposite side serving as inhibition trials requiring children to override the prepotent response. Each block began with at least three prepotent trials. The trial order was automatically randomized but constrained to a maximum of five prepotent or two inhibition trials in sequence. The side associated with the prepotent response (left vs. right) was counterbalanced across participants.

The participant's behavior was recorded using a video camera placed on a tripod behind the participant. This was done to allow for manual coding after the session. After each correct response, a short cartoon animation combined with sound effects was played. The iPad recorded the accuracy and reaction times of each response. These recorded responses were later manually checked and corrected using the recording. Corrections included overriding the response time if the iPad did not detect the first touch, accounting for experimenter error, and accounting for caregiver interference. Software access was granted by the team that designed the test at the University of Oxford, and data was saved on their server.

Procedure

Testing involved two steps: an online call and an in‐person session. After signing up, families received an email with demographic and consent forms and a Zoom link. All materials were available in English or French. During the online meeting, the experimenter completed the LEAT with the parent(s) and scheduled the in‐person session; meetings were recorded for scoring. The LEAT was scored manually, and second‐language exposure was calculated. Children were classified as monolingual (< 10% exposure to a non‐dominant language) or multilingual (> 25% exposure; Hoff et al. 2012), with trilingual/multilingual children included in the bilingual group. Caregivers were sent one or two WebCDI‐WS links to complete in the language(s) relevant to the child.

At the in‐person session, participants completed the ECITT and the EEFQ games in counterbalanced order. For the ECITT, children sat on a caregiver's lap, in a highchair, or in a child‐sized chair. Caregivers wore blackout glasses when holding the child. They were instructed not to point or label the buttons in any seating arrangement. For the EEFQ, the experimenter first showed each instructional video to the caregiver, who then played each game with their child in a comfortable location (on the floor or at a child‐sized table). The experimenter remained present to answer questions. After the games, caregivers completed the EEFQ questionnaire. Upon completing the study, families received a Certificate of Merit and a $25 bookstore gift card.

Power and Sensitivity Analyses

An a priori G*Power analysis (Faul et al. 2007) was conducted to determine the required sample size for linear multiple regression, assuming 80% power, an effect size of 0.15, and an α level of 0.05. A medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15) was selected based on Cohen's (1988) conventions for multiple regression and common practice in G*Power analyses. This approach is recommended when prior studies do not provide consistent regression‐based estimates (Durlak 2009). Although meta‐analytic evidence suggests that bilingual advantages in executive functioning may be small, prior studies have varied widely in design and effect‐size reporting, making a medium effect size a reasonable and conservative estimate for a priori power analysis. In the power analysis, the primary predictor tested was exposure to a second language, with age and socioeconomic status (SES) included as covariates. This analysis yielded a required sample size of 55 participants. However, the primary analytic approach for the ECITT data was modified from a series of hierarchical linear regressions to linear mixed‐effects models as suggested by the reviewers. The planned analysis required summarizing trial‐wise data using central tendency measures, which ignore trial‐wise variability within each individual and reduce statistical power (Lo and Andrews 2015). As a result, we decided to deviate from the planned analysis after data collection and reviewer feedback, and to conduct mixed models to account for the non‐independence of individual trials on the ECITT and to better model the meaningful variability in accuracy and RT across ECITT trials within individual children. To explore how this modeling changes affected study power, we conducted simulation‐based sensitivity analyses to assess the power of the accuracy and RT mixed‐effects models to detect effects of plausible magnitude for each fixed predictor, given the observed sample size and variance structure. For child‐level predictors (age, gender, income, and non‐dominant language exposure), power remained below conventional thresholds even for effects substantially larger than those observed (See Supporting Information S1: Table S3 for detailed sensitivity analysis results). In contrast, both mixed models showed greater power to detect trial‐level effects (e.g., trial number and trial type), exceeding 80% power at comparatively smaller effect sizes. Overall, these findings indicate heterogeneity in sensitivity across predictors, with higher power for within‐child manipulations than for between‐child demographic variables. This pattern was expected given the hierarchical data structure, as child‐level predictors were estimated based on approximately 50 participants, whereas trial‐level predictors were estimated from over 1000 observations.

Results

Data Processing

Correlational and EEFQ analyses were conducted in SPSS 28 (IBM Corp. Released 2021). Assumptions for all primary analyses were checked before running them. No major violations were detected. Sixty participants met the inclusion criteria for the final sample. An alpha level of p < 0.05 (two‐tailed) was used.

Coding

ECITT responses were coded by comparing video footage with iPad‐recorded scores to ensure accuracy. Across all participants, 1682 trials were recorded. The iPad failed to detect a touch in 25 trials (1.49%), requiring coders to manually time responses. Trials were excluded if parents provided answers (0.06%), if reaction times were < 300 ms (4.4%), or if parents/experimenters touched the screen during disengagement (38 trials; 2.26%). Accidental touches, which happened when a child brushed the incorrect button while clearly aiming for the other, were also excluded (6 trials; 0.36%). At least 60% accuracy on prepotent trials was required to confirm a prepotent response tendency.

Intercoder reliability, calculated using scores from a second coder blind to children's language status, was excellent (ECITT prepotent accuracy κ = 0.735; inhibition accuracy κ = 0.872; prepotent RT κ = 0.738; inhibition RT κ = 0.868).

Participants who completed fewer than 20 trials were excluded (N = 4; same participants excluded for fussiness). All remaining participants had at least two valid inhibitory trials. Trials with reaction times > 5000 ms were excluded from RT analyses but retained for accuracy analyses. Accuracy was calculated as the percentage correct for prepotent and inhibition trials separately. Reaction time analyses used median RTs for correct responses (Holmboe et al. 2021), given their robustness to outliers. The ECITT descriptive statistics are shown in Table 2.

Vocabulary size was measured using total words produced from the Web‐CDI. Only monolingual English, monolingual French, or bilingual English–French CDI forms were scored. Of the 60 participants, 34 provided CDIs: English monolingual (N = 9), French monolingual (N = 11), English‐first multilingual (N = 7), and French‐first multilingual (N = 7). Only six of the fourteen multilingual participants completed both English and French forms. Of the other 26 participants, twelve could not complete either form in their child's first language. An additional fourteen eligible participants did not provide usable CDI data (no forms or only the second‐language form, despite reminders). Due to the small number of complete CDI responses, vocabulary size was not included in the final analyses.

Data Cleaning

Participants' raw scores were examined for univariate outliers. Although one score, ± 3 standard deviations from the mean (Raykov and Marcoulides 2008), was identified for the variable Prepotent Reaction Time, the score was included because it was not considered sufficient cause for deletion. No multivariate outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distance. Finally, all variables were assessed for normality in the sample. All skewness values were within the recommended range of ± 3, and all kurtosis values were below ± 10 (Kline 2011). We applied case‐wise deletion for observations with missing scores when possible; list‐wise deletion was used for analyses requiring complete cases.

Correlational Analyses

Given that the key variables were either ordinal or continuous, the first analyses used bivariate Spearman correlations to examine the relationship between non‐dominant language exposure and all executive functioning variables. These studies were conducted to test the first and second hypotheses. Before conducting the correlational analyses, a series of independent‐samples t‐tests was conducted to ensure that demographic variables, such as age and annual income, did not differ between the monolingual and multilingual groups. Groups did not differ in age, t (58) = −0.097, p = 0.923, Cohen's d = −0.025, nor annual income, t (54) = −0.186, p = 0.853, Cohen's d = −0.05.

No statistically significant correlations were found between amount of non‐dominant language exposure and accuracy on ECITT Inhibitory trials, r (53) = −0.12, p = 0.38, ECITT Prepotent trials, r (53) = −0.02, p = 0.89, reaction time on ECITT Inhibitory trials, r (50) = −0.04, p = 0.77, or ECITT Prepotent trials r (50) = 0.11, p = 0.44. Similarly, no statistically significant correlations emerged between non‐dominant language exposure and all the EEFQ measures: IC, r (57) = 0.14, p = 0.29, FX, r (57) = 0.06, p = 0.64, REG, r (57) = −0.022, p = 0.87, WM, r (57) = −0.01, p = 0.94, or CEF, r (57) = 0.123, p = 0.355.

When conducting correlational analyses between age and those same executive function outcomes, we found a statistically significant positive correlation between age and ECITT INH accuracy, r (53) = 0.35 p = 0.01, as well as for age and ECITT PRPT accuracy, r (53) = 0.28, p = 0.04. This is consistent with our developmental expectations (Kang et al. 2022). Otherwise, no statistically significant correlations were found between age and other executive functioning variables.

Finally, correlational analyses were conducted to examine the relation between annual income and executive function outcomes. Annual revenue was included in the analyses because it serves as a moderator of the Bilingual Cognitive Advantage (Naeem et al. 2018). The results showed a statistically significant negative correlation between annual income and EEFQ FX, r (54) = −0.30, p = 0.02. They also showed a statistically significant positive correlation with accuracy on ECITT Prepotent trials, r (53) = −0.32, p = 0.02. All correlations are shown in Table 4.

ECITT Accuracy: The accuracy model included 1556 trials from 53 children. On average, children were more accurate on prepotent than on inhibition trials, and accuracy increased with age when averaged across all trials. The fixed effects accounted for a meaningful amount of variance in children's accuracy on the ECITT (see Table 5). The odds of a correct response were substantially higher on prepotent trials than on inhibition trials, OR = 7.50, 95% CI [5.60, 10.05]. The model predicted that the average child would have 90% accuracy on prepotent trials and 57% accuracy on inhibitory trials, when averaged across all other predictors, indicating that once a prepotent response was established, children were much less likely to override it on inhibitory trials. Age was also positively associated with accuracy, OR = 1.17 per additional month, 95% CI [1.07, 1.29]. The model predicted that each additional month of age would result in a 1.98% increase in trial accuracy when averaging across all other predictors, consistent with age‐related improvements in cold inhibitory control across the second year of life. The effects of non‐dominant language exposure, gender, income, and trial number were negligible and imprecise, providing no evidence that these factors were associated with children's accuracy on the ECITT after controlling for trial type and age.

EEFQ Analyses

To test the second hypothesis, a series of hierarchical linear regressions was conducted to further analyze the effects of non‐dominant‐language exposure, scored on a continuum, on executive functioning skills, with EEFQ scores for Inhibition Control, Working Memory, Attentional Flexibility, Emotional Regulation, and Cognitive Executive Functioning as dependent variables. Analyses were conducted in SPSS 28 (IBM Corp. Released 2021). The predictors included age, non‐dominant language exposure, and annual income. Model 1 included age; Model 2 included non‐dominant language exposure and age; and Model 3 included age, non‐dominant language exposure, and yearly income. No significant models were observed when conducting the regressions with EEFQ Inhibition Control, Working Memory, Attentional Flexibility, Emotional Regulation, or Cognitive Executive Functioning as the outcome variable.

Bayesian Statistics

Given the null results of the analyses, we also performed Bayesian Independent Samples T‐Tests. Overall, the results show that both monolingual and multilingual participants performed equally well in terms of accuracy and reaction time for both inhibition and prepotent trials. To further investigate this null effect, four Bayesian Independent Samples Normal tests were run. The tests showed evidence for the null hypothesis on accuracy in prepotent trials (BF10 = 0.307, error% = 0.007), accuracy in inhibition trials (BF10 = 0.741, error% = 0.007), reaction time in prepotent trials (BF10 = 0.293, error% = 0.007), and reaction time in inhibition trials (BF10 = 0.334, error% = 0.007). According to the Bayes Factors, all of which are below 1, there is evidence in favor of the null hypothesis that there is no difference between monolingual and multilingual participants in accuracy or reaction time on both prepotent and inhibition trials (Wagenmakers et al. 2017).

Discussion

The present study investigated whether hot and cold inhibitory control, previously reported to be stronger in bilingual children, can also be observed in toddlers with measures suitable for that age group. The primary aim was to investigate whether exposure to one or more languages benefits inhibitory control skills in toddlers aged 16–24 months. This question was addressed with a newly developed behavioral measure of inhibitory control (the ECITT) and a parent‐report questionnaire (the EEFQ) to assess multiple aspects of executive functioning.

Grounded in studies demonstrating superior cold inhibitory control among bilingual adults, school‐aged children and toddlers (Arizmendi et al. 2018; Carlson and Meltzoff 2008; Lowe et al. 2021; Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2011), it was first hypothesized that toddlers with greater exposure to more than one language would show better inhibitory control than those with no or less exposure. Secondly, consistent with prior findings indicating no significant bilingual advantage in regulation and cognitive flexibility in young children (Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022; Arizmendi et al. 2018; Gunnerud et al. 2020; Lowe et al. 2021), it was hypothesized that multilingual exposure would not provide a benefit in these executive functions. A third hypothesis posited that toddlers classified as multilingual, defined as having at least 25% exposure to non‐dominant languages, would demonstrate better cold and hot inhibitory control than their monolingual peers. To test these predictions, multilingualism was operationalized both continuously (to test Hypotheses 1 and 2) and categorically (to test Hypothesis 3), enabling a more nuanced assessment of whether cognitive effects are directly related to language exposure or emerge solely from dual‐language experience. The ECITT and EEFQ were used to assess hot and cold inhibition, while flexibility and regulation were evaluated through the EEFQ alone.

Contrary to our predictions, the results revealed no significant relation between the degree of language exposure and cold inhibitory control as measured by the ECITT, thus failing to support the first hypothesis. Similarly, no significant differences in inhibitory control were observed between categorically defined multilingual and monolingual toddlers, failing to support the third hypothesis. However, as predicted by the second hypothesis, the data confirmed that multilingual exposure did not have a significant effect on toddlers' regulation or flexibility skills. Finally, no link was observed between hot inhibitory control, as measured by the EEFQ parental questionnaire, and the degree of non‐dominant language exposure.

Although the main hypotheses of the present study were not supported, the present findings nonetheless offer important contributions to the literature on executive functions in infancy, as well as about the validity and replicability of the ECITT as a measure of cold inhibitory control in early development. Notably, the results closely mirror those reported by the research team that initially developed the ECITT to capture the emergence of inhibitory skills in toddlers (Hendry et al. 2021a; Holmboe et al. 2021). Specifically, our data replicated two key findings from these studies. Firstly, older toddlers consistently outperformed their younger peers for accuracy in both prepotent and inhibition trials, reflecting age‐related improvements even within a relatively narrow developmental window (Kang et al. 2022). Secondly, performance on prepotent trials consistently exceeded that on inhibitory trials for both accuracy and reaction time, reflecting the cognitive challenge of suppressing a dominant motor response at this age. The similarities of results across studies confirm the ECITT's sensitivity to developmental change and its construct validity as an age‐appropriate behavioral measure of inhibitory control.

In addition to its methodological contributions, the current findings add to the growing body of work on the developmental timing of the impact of bilingual experience on the mind. Using a task designed explicitly for toddlers, such as the ECITT, enhances the interpretation of these null findings. Rather than a consequence of methodological limitations, as the ECITT has been shown to be reliable and valid (Holmboe et al. 2021), the current lack of observed bilingual advantages likely reflects an absence of executive functioning benefits associated with multilingualism during the second year of life. Such an advantage in cold inhibition was observed at 24 months on the baby Stroop task (Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2011), but not at earlier ages, as evidenced by null findings on the detour‐reaching task at 17 months (Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2022). Regarding the null effects of second‐language exposure on hot inhibitory control, as measured by the EEFQ and a parental report, these findings conflict with those of a recent study (Beaudin and Poulin‐Dubois 2022). This could be explained by higher language exposure in their sample (40%) and by children being slightly older (23 months). Future studies will be needed to clarify this issue.

Although the present study contributes to the growing literature on bilingualism and executive functioning, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample is not fully representative, particularly with respect to socioeconomic status (SES). Most participants were from upper‐income households (i.e., annual income > $150,000), highly educated, and identified as of European descent. Perhaps due to this limited variability, SES was not a significant predictor in the analyses, which diverged from prior findings identifying SES as a potential mediator of the bilingual cognitive advantage (Naeem et al. 2018). The only exceptions were the negative correlations between SES and flexibility on the EEFQ and between SES and accuracy on prepotent trials. Future studies should prioritize recruiting participants across a broader range of socioeconomic backgrounds to ensure more generalizable and inclusive findings.

Secondly, unlike studies with older children that define multilingualism in terms of usage, bilingualism was defined in terms of exposure to more than one language. This approach is in line with all studies testing the bilingual cognitive advantage in preverbal infants or in children with limited language skills (N. Brito and Barr 2012; Comishen et al. 2019; Dal Ben et al. 2022; D’Souza et al. 2020; Kovács and Mehler 2009; Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2011, 2022). However, a measure of usage in multiple languages might be a more reliable way to assess early stages of bilingualism as it better reflects the bilingual experience. To our knowledge, the few studies that have identified bilingualism in very young children, in terms of use, have reported an advantage in cognitive control (Crivello et al. 2016; Poulin‐Dubois et al. 2011; Verhagen et al. 2020). In contrast, studies based on language exposure have yielded mixed findings.

A third limitation was the incomplete data from the MacArthur‐Bates Communicative Development Inventory: Words and Sentences (MCDI: WS). Due to insufficient data, analyses of the impact of vocabulary development on executive functions were not performed. Specifically, analyses of translation equivalents and total conceptual vocabulary in multilingual children could not be conducted on the limited sample of French–English bilinguals. The wide variety of languages participants were exposed to often made it challenging to administer a language‐appropriate MCDI. This limitation is notable given prior evidence that vocabulary size, the number of translation equivalents, and the efficiency of word recognition in very young bilinguals provide important insights into early bilingual language development (Legacy et al. 2016). To address these constraints, future studies could ask caregivers of multilingual participants to complete the same form in all languages the child is exposed to, thereby enabling more comprehensive assessments of conceptual vocabulary and translation equivalents across language environments. These metrics could have provided additional insight into how language development interacts with executive functioning, as more translation equivalents increase the frequency of code‐switching. Future studies should conduct a more detailed analysis of vocabulary data to explore the relationship between bilingual language exposure and cognitive development at a finer‐grained level.

Finally, sensitivity analyses indicated that the mixed models analyses of ECITT accuracy and reaction time were underpowered to detect small effects of between‐participant predictors. Accordingly, while the absence of a large effect of non‐dominant language exposure on accuracy or reaction time can be inferred, the presence of a small effect cannot be ruled out. The null findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future studies seeking to replicate the current study and its analytic approach are encouraged to conduct an a priori power analysis for mixed‐effects models to enhance the validity of the results.

The findings of this study open several promising directions for future research. One potential avenue is to examine whether a domain‐specific effect emerges later in development by administering the ECITT task, which has been validated for older toddlers, to children aged 24–30 months. Inhibitory control is mainly recruited during language production, especially when bilingual speakers switch between languages and inhibit the language that is not currently in use (Arredondo and Castro 2026). Thus, the current sample might not have a large enough productive vocabulary to allow sufficient code‐switching experience. Secondly, future studies could diversify the multilingual population by using the MCDI‐WS across a broader range of languages beyond English and French. Expanding the sample size would also enable the analysis of doublets, providing deeper insight into multilingual development. Finally, as noted in the introduction, the Adaptation Model proposes that bilinguals continuously adjust their cognitive systems to the demands of managing two languages, leading to changes in attentional control, rather than to domain‐general executive functioning (Bialystok and Craik 2022; Bialystok 2024, 2025a, 2025b). Within this framework, bilingual experience may enhance attention switching rather than inhibitory control. Future research should therefore develop age‐appropriate tasks to directly assess attentional switching in very young children and further test the Bilingual Adaptation Model's predictions. There is some evidence that bilingual‐ and monolingual‐raised infants show differences in performance for attentional orienting, a cognitive mechanism likely supporting cognitive control (i.e., attentional and inhibitory control) and especially relevant to bilingual language acquisition (Arredondo et al. 2022).

In conclusion, these findings indicate no evidence for a bilingual effect in inhibitory control, regulation, or flexibility at this early developmental stage. These findings support the view that a potential bilingual advantage may emerge only after children have acquired more extensive, structured experience managing two languages. This study makes a unique contribution to the literature by employing a novel, age‐appropriate touchscreen task to compare inhibition control between multilingual and monolingual 16–to 24‐month‐olds. It also serves as a proper replication of previous findings for the ECITT, further validating the task.
