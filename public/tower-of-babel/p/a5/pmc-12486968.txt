Introduction

Mitochondria, the powerhouse of eukaryotic cells, are essential for energy production, redox balance and biosynthetic pathways. Their function relies on the transport of solutes across the inner mitochondrial membrane, a process mediated by the mitochondrial carrier family (MCF) members. This family comprises 53 members in humans, characterized by a conserved tripartite structure of three tandem repeats, each containing two transmembrane α-helices, resulting in six transmembrane helices (H1-H6) [1–3]. These helices enclose a similarly located common substrate-binding site, localized near the center of the membrane between the three repeat domains [4, 5], and characterized by three conserved contact points that are responsible for the substrate binding by conferring to the different mitochondrial carriers the ability to recognize and translocate amino acids, organic acids or nucleotides. These contact points in mitochondrial carriers are situated between two regions enriched in proline and glycine residues, which are conserved across all members of the mitochondrial carrier family. These regions are referred to as proline-glycine (PG) levels due to their ideally planar distribution above and below the substrate-binding site [1, 4, 5] (Figure S1). More in detail, PG-level 2 consists of the conserved proline and glycine residues of the MCF-specific sequence motifs (e.g., PX[D/E]XX[K/R] and [D/E]-GXXXX[Y/W/F]-[K/R]-G) [1–3], conserved at the end of odd helices (Pro) or at the beginning of even helices (Gly), located one turn of helix below the contact points of the similarly located mitochondrial carrier substrate binding site and slightly above the matrix-gate residues (Figure S1). Conversely, residues of PG-level 1 consists of similarly conserved prolines and glycine residues conserved on the even helices (Pro) or on the odd helices (Gly) one turn of helix above the substrate binding site contact points and two turns of helices below the cytosolic gate residues [1, 4, 5] (Figure S1).

It is believed that the prolines and glycines in the PG levels are responsible for the main conformational changes enabling substrate translocation, as the tilt and kink movements of the transmembrane helices of mitochondrial carriers are hinged on these conserved Pro and Gly residues. Prolines in the sequence motifs of odd-numbered helices (H1, H3, and H5) induce kinks that enable the formation of the matrix gate. This gate is stabilized by inter-helix ionic pairs formed between charged residues of the sequence motifs on the odd transmembrane helices [1, 4, 5] (Figure S1). Substrate binding at the conserved contact points disrupts these ion pairs, initiating a sequence of structural changes. The tilting and kinking movements at the PG levels, combined with interactions at the contact points, drive the carrier's transition from the cytosolic conformation (c-state) to the matrix-conformation (m-state) [1, 4, 5] (Figure S1).

The transport mechanism elucidated for the majority of the characterized mitochondrial carriers follows a “ping-pong” model, alternating between the c-state and the m-state via an intermediate state that is partially closed to both the intermembrane space and the mitochondrial matrix [1, 2, 4–6]. This alternating access mechanism ensures that the same substrate-binding site is sequentially exposed to either the intermembrane space or the mitochondrial matrix.

Among mitochondrial carriers, the Aspartate/Glutamate Carrier paralogs AGC1 and AGC2 are vital for the exchange of aspartate and glutamate, supporting the malate-aspartate shuttle and other essential metabolic processes [7–9]. AGC1 and AGC2, encoded by the SLC25A12 and SLC25A13 genes, respectively, facilitate NADH oxidation and integrate nitrogen and carbon metabolism. Remarkably, AGC1 (NP_003696.2) and AGC2 (NP_055066.1) share the 82% of identical residues, comparing their human full-length sequences (see [7] and the corresponding supplementary material for more details on their sequence comparison). AGC2 is highly expressed in liver, kidney, pancreas, and intestinal cells [7, 8, 10]. Dysfunction of AGC2 due to mutations in SLC25A13 gene is associated with citrin deficiency, a rare metabolic disorder characterized by impaired urea cycle function, hyperammonemia and liver dysfunction [7, 8]. Conversely, AGC1 is predominantly expressed in neurons and muscle, where it plays a critical role in energy metabolism and neurotransmitter balance. AGC1 deficiency, caused by mutations in SLC25A12 gene, results in abolished carrier activity and has been linked to a neurodevelopmental syndrome featuring global hypomyelination, arrested psychomotor development, hypotonia, and seizures, likely due to impaired aspartate efflux and disrupted myelin formation [7, 9]. Notably, AGC1 overexpression has also been implicated in tumorigenesis, where it promotes metabolic reprogramming, pyrimidine biosynthesis, and rapid cell proliferation [7].

Despite AGC2’s critical role, structural and functional characterization remains incomplete due to challenges in studying membrane proteins [1, 2, 4–6]. Traditional radiolabeled transport assays have been instrumental in elucidating AGC2 function, but they are technically demanding and limited by the availability of labeled substrates [7]. Advances in biophysical methods, such as thermal shift assays (TSA, [11]) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC, [12]), offer complementary tools to investigate AGC2-ligand interactions. TSA is particularly valuable for high-throughput screening [11], while ITC provides precise binding affinity and thermodynamic parameters [12].

In this study, for the first time we employ TSA and ITC to investigate AGC2-ligand interactions, and compare the obtained results with those coming from traditional transport assays [7]. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, also conducted for the first time on the reconstituted AGC2, confirms the structural integrity and localization of the transporter in proteoliposomes and n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (DDM)-based vesicles. These experimental methods are integrated with computational approaches, such as docking-based virtual screening of chemical libraries [13, 14], enabling the identification of high-affinity AGC2 modulators. These complementary techniques provide a comprehensive pipeline for studying AGC2 function, structure and therapeutic potential, but also substrate specificity and transport activity modulation of other members of the MCF.

Results

Bacterial expression, purification and reconstitution of the human AGC2

The AGC2 protein (AC: NM_014251.3; NP_055066.1) was expressed in bacteria using the E. coli BL21 (DE3) cell system after codon optimization (Figure S2). The expressed protein contains an extra six His-tag amino acids at its C-terminus encoded by the vector, resulting in a final apparent molecular weight of about 65 kDa, in good agreement with its theoretical molecular mass (Fig. 1A, lane 2). The yield of the purified protein was approximately 15 mg per liter of culture, as evaluated by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure S3). The recombinant protein was not detected in bacteria harvested immediately before the induction with IPTG (Fig. 1A, lane 1). After expression, the IBs were purified as reported in Materials and Methods (MM) section (Fig. 1B, lane 3) with a yield of 75 mg per liter. Next, the protocol for the solubilization and reconstitution of AGC2 IBs has been developed and optimized, as reported in the MM section. Subsequently a further step of purification using FPLC chromatography was done (Figure S4), starting from the reconstituited AGC2 protein, in order to remove the excess of detergent, which might interfere with the calorimetry assays. An overview of the methodology employed for recombinant AGC2 expression, purification, and reconstitution is shown in the workflow of Figure S2.

Functional characterization of the human recombinant AGC2 in proteoliposomes

First of all, we started by measuring transport activity of the reconstituted AGC2 in proteoliposomes (PL-AGC2) by using radiolabeled substrates ([14C]Asp), to compare results from the standardized transport assays in proteoliposomes [7, 13] with those obtained through thermostability assays (TSA) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). In this way, we also checked the integrity of our PL-AGC2.

The antiport of the external [14C]Aspartate (Asp) in exchange for Glutamate (Glu) of the human recombinant AGC2 was tested in hetero-exchange experiments (i.e. with Glu preloaded in proteoliposomes and the radiolabelled [14C]Asp added externally). Using internal and external substrate concentrations of Glu 20 mM and [14C]Asp 0.1 mM, respectively, the reconstituted proteins catalyzed active [14C]Asp/Glu exchange that was inhibited completely by a mixture of 10 mM pyridoxal 5’-phosphate, 10 mM bathophenanthroline (as previously described [7, 13]).

The time-courses of 0.1 mM [14C]Asp uptake into liposomes reconstituted with AGC2 were compared either as uniport (in the absence of internal substrate) or as exchange (in the presence of 20 mM Glu) (Fig. 2).

Kinetics of [14C]Asp transport in proteoliposomes reconstituted with AGC2 (PL-AGC2). Uptake of [14C]Asp. 0.1 mM [14C]Asp was added to proteoliposomes containing 20 mM Glu (heteroexchange, ●) or 10 mM NaCl and no substrate (uniport, ♦). Indicated values are the means of at least three independent experiments

The Asp uptake increased linearly up to approximately 1 min, after which the curve starts to level off. For the following kinetics analysis, the uptake of [14C]Asp, stopped at 60 s would allow measurements during the linear phase of the initial rate.

The [14C]Asp/Glu exchange follows first-order kinetics with rate constants 0.023 min−1 and initial rates 57.2 μmol/(min × g protein). Indeed, the curve's rise to a plateau is indicative of exponential kinetics, consistent with a first-order exchange mechanism driven by a concentration gradient, with isotopic equilibrium being approached exponentially (Fig. 2).

Conversely, the flat line for the [14C] Asp /NaCl (diamonds) assay indicated that the uniport uptake was negligible under these conditions. There was no significant change in [14C]Asp uptake over time, confirming that the Asp uptake occurred predominantly through the exchange mechanism (Asp/Glu) rather than uniport. Notably, the absence of [14C]Asp uptake in the assay with the NaCl provided evidences about the integrity of our proteoliposomes. In addition. PL-AGC2 preloaded with radioactive ligands did not catalyze any export or did not show any leakage of radioactive compound, demonstrating the integrity of PL-AGC2.

Importantly, no [14C]Asp/Glu exchange activities were detected when AGC2 has been boiled before incorporation into liposomes or when proteoliposomes were reconstituted with sarkosyl-solubilized material from bacterial cells either lacking the expression vector for AGC2 or harvested immediately before induction of expression. In addition, the above-mentioned heteroexchanges were null using liposomes without incorporated protein.

3D modelling and virtual screening of chemical libraries

Thanks to the in-house built 3D comparative model of AGC2 (Fig. 6) it was possible to observe that the N-terminal region (residues 1–324) of the obtained 3D comparative model consists of 8 EF-hands (EF1-EF8) each formed by a helix pair. Two supplementary little helices in the N-terminal region do not participate to EF-hands. The C-terminal domain is shown in cartoon representation and contains the transmembrane transport domain (residues 325–605) consisting of six transmembrane helices (H1-H6) and three small helices parallel to the membrane plane (h12, h34, h56), as observed for other MCs [1, 13, 15–20]. Between the end of the three odd helices (H1, H3, H5) and the beginning of the three even helices (H2, H4, H6) the two-parts characteristic sequence motif of MCF can be observed: Px[D/E]xx[K/R]x[R/K] is the first part, far about 30 residues from the second part EGxxxx[W/F/Y][R/K]G [1, 13, 15–20] (Figure S1).

In order to shed light on possible new modulators of AGC2 transport activity catalyzed by the recombinant AGC2, we firstly defined a binding region to be explored through docking-based virtual screening of an in-house developed chemical library. Then, we performed docking experiments using aspartate as ligand, before running the virtual screening of our chemical library. A gridbox involving residues of the predicted binding region between the PG-level 1 and the PGlevel 2 [18, 19] was built in order to investigate the binding of aspartate at this region. Autodock suggested that the aspartate substrate binds AGC2 3D model by interacting with residues of the proposed common binding site (e.g., R492, D493, R585 and R588, see Fig. 6).

Then, by using the defined gridbox, we performed the virtual screening of our in-house developed chemical library. The list sorted by energy of the 9669 successfully screened ligands is available upon request. Notably, suramin, whose ability in inhibiting AGC1 was previously verified [13], was the best binder according to the virtual screening performed on AGC2, with a binding energy of -10.75 kcal/mol (Figs. 6, 7). In addition, among the first 8 best binders, we chose to test taurolithocholic acid 3-suflate, because it contains a cis-decalinic moiety, a sulfate group at the 3-position of lithocholic acid, and a sulfonic group as part of its taurine moiety, structural features already observed in carboxyatractyloside that is the strongest known inhibitor of a mitochondrial carrier (namely, the ADP/ATP carrier). The binding energy estimated for interactions between taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate and AGC2 explored binding region was − 9.05 kcal/mol. Mersalyl, among the best know inhibitors of AGC2, was considered as a reference inhibitor, and in our virtual screening its docking produced a binding energy of − 6.05 kcal/mol (Figs. 6, 7). Thus, we proceed in testing the three mentioned molecules in our in vitro transport and binding assays. Kinetic characteristics of the human recombinant AGC2

Our results about substrate specificity are consistent with previously reported data about the human AGC2 confirming the known AGC2 substrate preferences [7, 22].

The Michaelis–Menten (half-saturation) constant (Km) of the human reconstituted AGC2 (PL-AGC2) was determined by measuring the initial substrate transport rate by varying the external [14C]Asp concentrations in the presence of a fixed internal saturating concentration (20 mM) of Glu. Aspartate Km for AGC2 was 47.6 ± 12.3 μM, with a Vmax of 165.7 ± 15.3 μmol/min/g protein. The IC50 values calculated for Mersalyl, Suramin, and Taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate were 0.30 ± 0.05 μM, 1.37 ± 0.11 μM, and 117.3 ± 14.5 μM (Fig. 8).

Thermostability assays

To assess the stability and folding state of AGC2 following its reconstitution, thermostability data were collected using the thiol-reactive fluorescent dye N-[4-(7-diethylamino-4-methyl-3-coumarinyl)phenyl] maleimide (CPM), which increases in fluorescence upon binding to accessible cysteine residues. Since recombinant AGC2 was expressed in E. coli as inclusion bodies, the protein was first solubilized in 0.8% sarkosyl and then reconstituted in 0.04% n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (DDM) supplemented with cardiolipin, conditions designed to promote proper folding and membrane insertion. In its native folded state, cysteine residues within AGC2 are largely buried and inaccessible to the solvent; upon thermal unfolding, these residues become exposed, allowing reaction with CPM and resulting in a fluorescence increase. This thermal denaturation profile provides insight into the structural integrity of the reconstituted protein. Additionally, ligand-induced shifts in thermal stability (e.g., in the presence of substrates or inhibitors) help to confirm functional folding, as these shifts reflect specific ligand–protein interactions that stabilize or destabilize the carrier.

Specifically, we evaluated the binding affinity for the three modulators/ligands/inhibitor (suramin, taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate and mersalyl) that gave the best result from the virtual screening previously carried out (Fig. 9), at concentrations of 0.1, 1 and 3 mM. The binding affinity using the substrates (aspartate and glutamate) was also evaluated (Fig. 9A). Only for the substrate aspartate at 0.1 and 1 mM it was observed a statistically significant stabilization of the AGC2 protein (ΔTm = around 3 ± 1.1) (Fig. 9A, B).

Suramin showed no significant effect on thermal stability at 0.1 mM and 1 mM, but caused a statistically significant destabilization at 3 mM (ΔTm = − 2.3 ± 0.5 °C) (Fig. 9C and panel E). Similarly, taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate had no significant impact at 0.1 mM and 1 mM, but induced a significant destabilization at 3 mM (ΔTm = − 3.0 ± 0.4 °C) (Fig. 9C and panel F).

In contrast, the addition of mersalyl resulted in a strong stabilizing effect at 1 mM (ΔTm = 29 ± 0.3 °C; Fig. 9B and panel G) and 3 mM (ΔTm = 25 ± 0.4 °C; Fig. 9C and panel G), while at 0.1 mM, it caused a statistically non-significant and slightly destabilizing effect (ΔTm < 0; Fig. 9A and panel G). The reduced ΔTm at 3 mM compared to 1 mM may reflect quenching of the fluorescent signal, as reported previously [23, 24].

Isothermal titration calorimetry assays

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was used to determine the thermodynamic parameters of AGC2 reconstituted protein with the molecules suramin, mersalyl and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate (Fig. 10) previously screened to confirm their binding affinity by thermostability assays. ITC experiments were performed using the FPLC-AGC2 protein. Moreover, all the ITC measurements were carried out in the same buffer solution containing 10 mM PIPES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 6.5 in the ITC cell and syringe, which contained the FPLC-AGC2 protein and the specific molecule, respectively. As negative controls, both buffer injection from the syringe into the calorimeter cell containing the protein and substrate injection from the syringe into the calorimeter cell containing the buffer were carried out. As already observed in the thermalshift experiments, the mersalyl showed a strong binding affinity to the AGC2 protein (KD = 11.85 ± 4.62 nM, as a mean value of three replicates, Table 1 and Figure S10) compared to suramin (KD = 7.57 ± 3.17 µM, as a mean value of three replicates, Table 1 and Figure S11) and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate (KD = 100.73 ± 8.97 µM, as a mean value of three replicates, Table 1 and Figure S12) (Fig. 10). Remarkably, ITC assays show that suramin has more than ten-fold higher binding affinity for AGC2 protein compared to taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate, being both estimations consistent with those obtained from transport assays.

Discussion

Integrated pipeline for AGC2 modulator identification

This study establishes an integrative pipeline for the identification and characterization of small molecules targeting the mitochondrial Aspartate/Glutamate Carrier paralog 2 (AGC2). The results also highlight the importance to balance potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic profiles in developing therapeutics targeting mitochondrial carriers. Indeed, while radiolabeled transport assays although limited by the availability of radiolabelled substrates and their suitability for high-throughput applications, remain crucial for evaluating functional activity in near-native environments, the integration of docking-based virtual screening of chemical libraries, thermal shift assays (TSA) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) addresses these limitations, enabling the rapid identification of promising candidates, and providing scalable and precise validation platforms for identifying candidate ligands and elucidating their thermodynamic binding parameters. Docking simulations, in particular, serve as a powerful filtering tool to prioritize molecules predicted to have high binding affinity, significantly streamlining the experimental phase. By narrowing down vast chemical libraries to a manageable number of candidates, docking analysis reduces the need for exhaustive high-throughput in vitro screening, a process that would otherwise require years of labor-intensive work. However, it is important to emphasize that docking scores and predicted binding affinities (such as Ki or ΔG) should not be interpreted as absolute values. Discrepancies between computational predictions and experimental results often arise from the inherent variability in ligand properties, such as molecular size, shape, flexibility, and selectivity, as well as limitations in the docking environment, which typically involves rigid receptor models and simplified, solvent-free (in vacuo) conditions. These factors highlight the necessity of experimental validation to accurately assess ligand behavior under physiologically relevant conditions. The insights gained from this study provide a foundation for rational drug design, emphasizing the need to balance potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic profiles in developing therapeutics targeting mitochondrial carriers.

In the present study, the recombinant AGC2 protein was expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3), purified as inclusion bodies, solubilized and reconstituted in either proteoliposomes or DDM vesicles to enable a comparison between traditional transport assays of radiolabeled substrates in proteoliposomes, with biophysical methods such as thermal shift assays (TSA) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) performed on the recombinant AGC2 reconstituted in DDM vesicles.

Transport assays provided essential functional data but are technically challenging, as they rely on substrates that are often unavailable in radiolabeled forms or prohibitively expensive, limiting their applicability in large-scale studies. By contrast, TSA proved to be a practical and alternative high-throughput screening, leveraging specific substrate-protein interactions that induced stabilization or destabilization of AGC2, leading to measurable changes in the thermal shift (ΔTm). Ligands producing higher ΔTm can then be tested by ITC binding affinity assays for estimating precise kinetics/thermodynamic parameters.

It should be noted that the possibility of using thermostability assays to check the stability of the ligand-bound form relative to the unbound form, looking at the shift in the apparent melting curve of a membrane protein sample, and ITC for determining KD were already tested with the ADP/ATP carrier and with the Uncoupling Protein 1 [23, 25, 26].

In our case study, thermal shift assays highlighted distinct stabilization effects induced by the candidate molecules, supporting their specific interactions with AGC2. Among the tested molecules, mersalyl showed the highest ΔTm, aligning with its known strong inhibitory effect on AGC2 [7]. This effect is particularly notable given that both CPM and mersalyl compete for cysteine residues, suggesting that mersalyl’s higher affinity causes the formation of a very stable AGC2-mesalyl protein–ligand complex, requiring higher temperatures to induce protein destabilization and making accessible AGC2 cysteine residues to CPM. However, the observed high ΔTm could also be ascribed to the ability of mersalyl to reach different cysteine residues, including those located in allosteric regions most likely involved in conformational changes, as those observed in AGC2 and more in general in mitochondrial carrier matrix loops [1]. Notably, the ITC experiments provided additional thermodynamic insights, demonstrating a KD lower than 1 µM for mersalyl, compared to weaker binding affinities observed for suramin and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate (KD values in the micromolar range).

These results underscore the complementarity of TSA and ITC in evaluating protein–ligand interactions. While TSA offers a rapid screening platform, ITC provides precise thermodynamic parameters, including binding stoichiometry, enthalpy (ΔH), and entropy (ΔS). The results from ITC on molecules selected in silico by virtual screening and in vitro by TSA, are consistent with transport assays, validating the use of TSA as a reliable high-throughput method for identifying AGC2 modulators. Conversely, both the TSA and ITC based approaches do not discriminate at the moment between translocated substrates or stabilizing inhibitors or activators, highlighting the need for further analysis to clarify their respective capacities in this regard. Remarkably, a targeted set of radiolabeled transport assays can still be employed to assess the abilities of selected molecules to modulate transport activity, while competitive ITC assays will be developed to extract kinetic parameters that may offer insights into transport versus binding-only behavior.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first successful application of an integrated docking-TSA–ITC workflow combined with transport assays for the investigation of AGC. Although similar strategies have been sporadically applied to other SLC25A transporters. such as AAC and UCP, with debated results about poor reproducibility, our approach yields robust and reproducible data, underscoring its potential as a reliable platform for characterizing AGC (SLC25A members) modulators.

TEM analysis, uniformity and unicompartment vesicles

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of PL-AGC2 and DDM-AGC2-based vesicles (Amb-AGC2 and FPLC-AGC2) revealed a population of frequent smaller spherical vesicles (diameter of 25–50 nm), containing one or two protein units, with exceptional larger vesicles (150–200 nm) often hosting more than three protein units. This heterogeneity is not unique to AGC2 but has been observed in other studies on membrane transporters, such as proteoliposomes used to study channel proteins and transporters under buffer gradients [27, 28]. Importantly, the control assays demonstrated that PL exhibited no leakage of radioactive ligands or uptake of radiolabeled substrates in the absence of active transporters, whereas DDM based vesicles showed with thermalshift assays the profiles expected with vesicle systems with a good thermostability.

In addition, the coherence between binding affinity estimated through ITC on FPLC-AGC2 vesicles and transport assays on PL-AGC2 preparations (according to [7]) provides a piece of evidence about the possibility to use FPLC-AGC2-like systems for investigating AGC2 (and other mitochondrial carriers) high affinity molecules under controlled conditions. Notably, the shape, size, and protein content of FPLC-AGC2 and PL-AGC2 prepared for this analysis were also comparable. The presence of multicompartment vesicles or vesicles with asymmetrically distributed protein monomers, as described in [27] for synthetic compartmentalized systems, appears less prominent in AGC2 preparations. The absence of multicompartmentalization could indicate a relatively straightforward assembly process, enhancing the reliability of kinetic and thermodynamic analyses performed using these systems. Remarkably, the insertion of AGC2 in proteoliposomes and DDM-based vesicles observed by TEM is consistent with the expected “right-side-out” orientation [29–32] of AGC2 in the prepared vesicles, namely with the C-terminal and N-terminal portions protruding out of the vesicles, as supported by the employed antibody targeting the first 300 resiedues (the N-terminal) of human AGC2.

Oligomerization and functional implications

The presence of both monomeric and dimeric protein-vesicles observed by TEM (Figure S7 and Figure S9) is consistent with previous findings [33] and supports the coexistence of multiple oligomeric states of AGC2. Notably, the apparent divergence from the main dimeric state observed by [33] may stem from differences in expression and purification strategies. Indeed, while their yeast-based system allows AGC2 to fold directly within the mitochondrial membrane mainly as a protein dimer, our E. coli-based protocol involves solubilization of inclusion bodies using sarkosyl and reconstitution into artificial membranes (POPC or DDM), conditions that are slightly harsher and may also favor monomeric or oligomeric forms, as already proposed for other carriers [34]. The observation that larger vesicles host more than one AGC2 protein unit raises intriguing questions or speculations about the potential formation of "opportunistic" oligomers. While purification of AGC2 expressed in yeast suggests a dimeric state as the functional unit of AGC2 [33], our TEM results, supported by previous findings for the carnitine carrier [34] or for the ADP/ATP carrier [35], hint at the possibility of transient higher-order oligomers forming under specific conditions [34–36], also observed in crystallographic dimers of the ADP/ATP carrier [17]. Such oligomers may arise opportunistically within the lipid bilayer or vesicle environment, potentially influencing the efficiency of substrate exchange between mitochondria and the cytosol, without necessarily affecting the intrinsic kinetics parameters of the transporter. This hypothesis aligns with evidence from other transporters, where oligomerization states beyond the crystallographic dimers have been implicated in substrate specificity, transport kinetics, or regulatory mechanisms. Moreover, the possibility of opportunistic oligomerization offers a compelling avenue for further investigation, shedding light on the dynamic behaviour of AGC2 beyond its canonical dimeric state, supposed based on previous observations with other mitochondrial carriers and/or membrane proteins [34–37].

Structure–property relationships of the investigated ligands within AGC2 and other SLC25A transporters

Larger ligands (like carboxyatractyloside or suramin in the AAC, or suramin and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate in AGC2) tend to penetrate less deeply into the substrate-binding cavity of mitochondrial carriers compared to smaller molecules (like aspartate or mersalyl in AGC2). However, the investigated larger molecules are able to establish interactions with residues close both to the matrix gate and to the cytosolic gate, in contrast to smaller ligands, which tend to interact mainly with residues located between the contact points of the similarly located substrate-binding site of mitochondrial carriers and the matrix gate, when mitochondrial carriers are in the cytosolic conformation. More in detail, beyond the interactions formed between all the investigated ligands and the residues of the substrate-binding site or the residues of the m-gate, the sulfate group of carboxyatractyloside, the sulfate and sulfonate groups of taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate, and the sulfonate groups of suramin can form electrostatic interactions with basic residues situated between the contact points of the substrate-binding site and the cytosolic gate (see interactions between the crystallized carboxyatractyloside or the docked suramin and residues K91 and R187 within AAC2, or see interactions between the docked suramin and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate and residues K405 and K408 in AGC2; Figure S1). This interaction pattern, observed in both the crystallized AAC and the modelled AGC2, likely contributes to the affinity of these ligands for the substrate cavity across various members of the SLC25A family that display characterizing triplets of residues contributing to the substrate specificity of mitochondrial carrier subfamilies located between the substrate binding area and the c-gate [1, 4, 6]. In terms of selectivity, suramin’s high molecular flexibility allows it to adapt and bind to carrier proteins that display different amino acid compositions in their substrate-binding regions (i.e., AAC and AGC). In contrast, carboxyatractyloside, with its rigid cis-decalin ring, lacks such adaptability, which may explain its low affinity for AGC-like carriers.

Concerning mersalyl, a small organomercurial inhibitor, it also interacts with residues located between the AGC2 binding site and its matrix gate, similarly to what is observed with the substrate aspartate, enabling efficient inhibition with an IC₅₀ in the submicromolar concentration range. In addition to its inhibitory potency, mersalyl caused pronounced destabilization of AGC2, as evidenced by a marked decrease in thermal stability and significant quenching of the fluorescence signal in our assays. These effects likely reflect extensive ligand–protein interactions that destabilize the conformational integrity of the carrier, most likely due to the presence of Hg2⁺ disrupting the cysteine-based interaction network within AGC2. However, mersalyl’s high affinity is due not only to the strong coordination of Hg2⁺ ions with thiol groups on cysteine residues, but also to its organic scaffold, which includes a phenyl ring and a carboxylate group, likely enhancing its ability to fit into and engage the substrate-binding cavity of mitochondrial carriers. These structural features resemble those of other known inhibitors of SLC25A family members, such as phenylsuccinate (which inhibits dicarboxylate carriers [38, 39]) and benzene-1,2,3-tricarboxylate (which targets the mitochondrial citrate carrier [40–43]). Despite mersalyl’s low selectivity, primarily due to the reactivity of Hg2⁺ with cysteine residues across various proteins, its submicromolar IC₅₀ for AGC2 highlights its potential as a valuable scaffold for the rational design of more selective small-molecule inhibitors targeting AGC paralogs.

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics considerations about the investigated small molecules

Mersalyl, a thiol-reactive organomercury compound, likely interacts with cysteine residues near the substrate-binding site, stabilizing AGC2 in a specific conformation. This observation aligns with studies demonstrating that SH-reagents, like mersalyl, significantly alter AGC2 transport activity [44, 45].

Remarkably, mersalyl was historically used as a diuretic [46]. Due to its poor oral bioavailability, mersalyl was administered intravenously [47]. Its pharmacokinetics is characterized by rapid systemic clearance, with a plasma half-life of less than 1 h [48]. The mersalyl forms covalent bonds with cysteine residues in target proteins, irreversibly inhibiting their function [49]. While this property underlies its potent inhibitory effect on AGC2, it also contributes to off-target interactions and systemic toxicity. These issues led to its discontinuation in clinical use. Despite these limitations, mersalyl remains a valuable tool compound for studying AGC2’s functional and structural properties.

Notably, suramin, with its polyanionic structure, interacts with positively charged residues in the AGC2 binding cavity, mimicking endogenous substrates such as aspartate. Suramin has a long history of use in treating neglected tropical diseases, such as African trypanosomiasis and onchocerciasis [50]. It has also been investigated for its anticancer potential, particularly in breast cancer and prostate cancer [50]. Suramin is typically administered intravenously, as its high molecular weight (1297 Da) and polyanionic structure result in poor oral bioavailability [51].

Pharmacokinetic studies reveal that suramin has a remarkably long plasma half-life, ranging from 41 to 78 days, depending on the dosage and patient variability [51]. Its slow clearance is attributed to its high protein binding and extensive distribution in tissues. While suramin’s long half-life facilitates prolonged therapeutic effects, it also raises concerns about cumulative toxicity and off-target interactions [50]. Suramin targets a wide range of proteins, including mitochondrial carriers (e.g., ADP/ATP carrier, or AGC1 [13]), metabolic enzymes (e.g., succinate dehydrogenase and CRAT), and purinergic receptors [13, 52–54]. Notably, concerning its ability to inhibit mitochondrial carrier substrate translocation, it is known that suramin weakly inhibit ATP-Mg/Pi carrier, and does not inhibit at all ornithine carrier [13]. It needs to be mentioned that suramin is not indicated in any PAINS register [55]. Conversely, due to its broad target profile, it necessitates further optimization to improve selectivity for AGC2 and/or other mitochondrial carriers.

Taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate is observed to contain a cis-decalinic moiety, a sulfate group at the 3-position of lithocholic acid, and a sulfonic group as part of its taurine moiety [56]. Notably, ligands containing sulfate and sulfonic groups have demonstrated strong inhibitory effects on mitochondrial carriers. For example, 4,4'-(1,10-phenanthroline-4,7-diyl)dibenzenesulfonate is a well-known stop-inhibitor used in transport assays [7, 13]. Similarly, the sulfonic groups of carboxyatractyloside have been shown to interact with key basic residues within the cavity of the crystallized ADP/ATP carrier [16, 57]. Furthermore, the strong inhibitory effects of suramin on the ADP/ATP carrier, AGC1 [13], and now AGC2 can be attributed to its sulfate and sulfonic groups, which target essential basic residues in the cavities of the mentioned mitochondrial carriers. Collectively, these findings suggest that the sulfate and sulfonic groups, along with the lithocholic portion of taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate, are likely the key functional groups responsible for AGC2 inhibition through the targeting of basic residues protruding towards the AGC2 carrier cavity between the c-gate and the m-gate regions, as supported by the provided 3D comparative models. These interactions align with the predicted structure of AGC2, which features conserved motifs (Figure S1) and alternating states (c-state and m-state) as part of the “ping-pong” transport model [1, 2, 4].

Remarkably, taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate is a bile salt derivative with amphiphilic properties that influence its pharmacokinetics. It is typically metabolized via enterohepatic circulation, with the liver playing a central role in its processing and recycling. Taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate sulfonate group enhances its solubility and might facilitate intravenous administration [58] in potential experimental and therapeutic settings for the targeting of AGC2.

While specific pharmacokinetic data related to the possible administration of taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate are limited, studies on related bile salts suggest a plasma half-life lower than 30 min, depending on species and experimental conditions [59]. Taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate has been shown to induce apoptosis in hepatocytes by activating caspase pathways and to alter protein expression in AR42J cells, affecting cellular metabolism, calcium regulation, and cytoskeletal dynamics [60]. Although Taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate weaker binding affinity for AGC2 (KD values in the low 100 micromolar range) limits its direct application as a selective AGC2 modulator, its structural properties offer a basis for thinking about rational drug-design of taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate derivatives, with higher affinity for AGC2, with improved pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles.

Conclusion remarks

The presented pipeline is not only robust for investigating AGC2 but also adaptable for other mitochondrial carrier family (MCF) members, starting from AGC1. The structural and functional similarities between AGC1 and AGC2 (82% of identical residues comparing their full-length sequences, and more than 96% of identical residues facing the substrate translocation cavities of the two carriers) highlight the potential for a unified strategy in the identification of new modulators of both AGC1 and AGC2. Both paralogs play pivotal roles in mitochondrial metabolism: AGC2 supports the malate-aspartate shuttle, while AGC1 is essential for brain energy metabolism and myelination processes. Dysregulation of each paralog contributes to metabolic disorders and neurological diseases, underscoring their therapeutic relevance. Several avenues for future research emerge from this work. First, TEM analysis provides valuable insights into the structural and functional organization of AGC2 vesicles, revealing systems consisting mainly of 40–50 nm vesicles, containining one or two monomers, and exceptionally 150–200 nm vesicles containing several protein monomers , similarly to what observed with other well-studied membrane transporters [38]. While the presence of exceptional larger vesicles may raise concerns regarding challenges in kinetic analyses, the consistency between KD determined through ITC-based binding affinity assays performed on FPLC-AGC2, and IC50 determined through transport assays conducted on PL-AGC2, suggests that AGC2 DDM-based vesicles (i.e., FPLC-AGC2) could be suitable for high-throughput screening of drug libraries. This could be achieved through thermal shift assays (that can be performed on 96 multiwell plates) for highlighting molecules able to produce higher ΔTm, and ITC to determine the KD of the selected high-affinity AGC2-binding molecules. Finally, the same molecules can then be validated in the more expensive, radiolabeled transport assays using PL-AGC2, for verifying their abilities in modulating AGC2 transport properties. In addition, structural studies using cryo-electron microscopy or X-ray crystallography are essential to obtain high-resolution insights into AGC2’s binding pockets and conformational transitions, similarly to what performed with other members of the mitochondrial carrier family [16, 18]. Remarkably, recent algorithms employed for cryo-EM are able to perform structure refinement starting from non-uniform samples by using cross-validation optimization approaches, which automatically regularize 3D density maps during refinement to account for spatial variability [61], making quicker/possible to obtain structural information about protein membranes also starting from non-uniform samples. If such structural information would be obtained, it would allow to refine virtual screening workflows and facilitate the design of selective modulators. Second, further optimization of lead compounds, starting from those described in this work, is warranted to enhance their binding affinities and pharmacokinetic properties. Chemical modifications that exploit AGC2’s conserved substrate-binding features while minimizing off-target interactions will be critical for developing clinically viable therapies. It should be stressed that in in vivo validation of AGC2 and AGC1 modulators in preclinical models is necessary to assess therapeutic potential for conditions such as citrin deficiency (in case of activators), cancer (in case of activators or inhibitors, depending on the specific cancer), and other described disorders [7]. Evaluations should focus on efficacy, safety, and tissue-specific distribution, particularly for broad-spectrum compounds like suramin. By elucidating the mechanisms of AGC2 modulation and establishing a versatile screening platform, this study provides a roadmap for targeting mitochondrial carriers in therapeutic contexts, starting from the closest paralog, AGC1, playing a crucial role in brain myelination [9]. The integration of computational and experimental approaches not only have the potential to enhance the efficiency of drug discovery but also deepens our understanding of the complex roles played by mitochondrial proteins in health and disease.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of the human recombinant AGC2 protein

The wild-type AGC2 mRNA sequence (NM_014251.3) and a 5′ 6 × His-tag were assembled into a pET-21b(+) plasmid purchased from Life Technologies Europe BV (Segrate-MI). The plasmid containing the sequence coding for AGC2 was transformed into E. coli XL-1 blue competent cells. Transformants were selected on LB plates containing ampicillin (100 mg/ml) and screened by direct colony PCR and by restriction digestion of purified plasmids. The sequence of the insert in the pET-21b (+) was verified by DNA sequencing by using the Eurofins Genomic Support. The expression of the recombinant AGC2 was obtained after 5 h induction with isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at a final concentration of 0.7 mM (cell O.D. = 0.4–0.6), at 37 °C in E. coli BL21(DE3) strain following the protocol reported for AAC2 and AGC1 in [13]. Control cultures with the empty vector were processed in parallel. Inclusion bodies (IBs) were purified on a sucrose density gradient [13, 62] and washed at 4 °C first with TE buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.0), then with a TNS buffer (Triton X-114 (3% w/v), 20 mM sodium sulfate, 1 mM EDTA and 10 mM PIPES/NaOH (pH 7.0)) as previously described [13]. Finally, the obtained inclusion bodies were washed again using TE buffer. The obtained IBs were finally stored in TE buffer (10 mM Tris/HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.0). Protein concentration was calculated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), using the ImageLab software tools. Standard curve was obtained using known amounts of bovine serum albumin standard (BSA) from 1 to 5 μg (Figure S3).

Solubilization and reconstitution of AGC2 into liposomes

The AGC2 recombinant protein was solubilized in 8% sarkosyl (N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt) and reconstituted into liposomes by cyclic removal of the detergent with a hydrophobic column of Amberlite XAD4 resin beads (Fluka), as previously described [13, 63–65] with some modifications. More in detail, the excess of sarkosyl was removed from the solubilized protein mixture by repeated passage (10–15 times) over Amberlite XAD-4 resin until the disappearance of foam, indicating effective detergent removal (according to [13, 63–65]). From these operations we obtained the so called Amb-AGC2. The composition of the initial mixture used for reconstitution was 50 μL of solubilized AGC2 (ca. 20 μg of protein), 70 μL of 10% Triton X-114, 100 μL of 10% phospholipids (l-α-phosphatidylcholine from egg yolk from Sigma) in the form of sonicated liposomes, 70 mM glutamate substrate, 50 μg/μL of cardiolipin in TNS 0.6%, 10 mM PIPES–NaOH (pH 6.5), and water to a final volume of 700 μl. After vortexing, this mixture was recycled 15-times through an Amberlite column (3.2 × 0.5 cm) pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 10 mM PIPES–NaOH (pH 6.5). All operations were performed at 4 °C except for the passages through Amberlite, which were carried out at room temperature. From the performed operations we obtained the so called PL-AGC2 (Figure S2).

Transport measurements in AGC2 proteoliposomes

The obtained AGC2 proteoliposomes (PL-AGC2) were subsequently eluted on Sephadex G-75 columns pre-equilibrated with 50 mM NaCl and 10 mM PIPES–NaOH at pH 6.5 (buffer A) to remove the external substrate not entered in the proteolipsomes. Thus, the eluted proteoliposomes were distributed in reaction vessels, then a control transport assay was started at 25 °C by adding externally [14C] Asp to substrate-loaded proteoliposomes (exchange). A stop inhibitor was used to stop the transport assay, by adding 30 mM pyridoxal-5' -phosphate and 20 mM bathophenanthroline, which in combination and at high concentrations inhibit the activity of several mitochondrial carriers rapidly and completely. In controls, the inhibitors were added at the beginning with the radioactive substrate. Finally, the external radiolabeled substrate was removed from each sample of proteoliposomes by a Sephadex G-75 column pre-equilibrated with buffer A and the radioactivity entrapped within proteoliposomes was measured. The experimental values were corrected by subtracting control values. The initial transport rate was calculated from the radioactivity taken up by proteoliposomes after 1 min, in the initial linear range of substrate uptake.

Solubilization and reconstitution of the recombinant AGC2 protein in DDM-based vesicles

The AGC2 protein was first solubilized in buffer A containing 0.8% sarkosyl (N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt), 0.03 mM DTT (Dithiothreitol), PIPES/EDTA buffer (15 mM PIPES, 1 mM EDTA, pH 6.5) and TE buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7). Before reconstitution, non solubilized protein was removed by centrifugation at 9,000 rpm, 4 °C, for 12 min. For the AGC2 reconstitution, 100 µl of solubilized protein was resuspended with 3.3 volumes of buffer B (0.01 mM DTT, 0.04% n-dodecyl β-d-maltoside, 0.014% DPG (1,3-bis(sn-3′-phosphatidyl)-sn-glycerol and PIPES/NaCl buffer (10 mM PIPES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 6.5). Also in this case, this mixture was recycled 13-times through an Amberlite column (3.5 × 0.5 cm) preequilibrated with a buffer containing 10 mM PIPES–NaOH (pH 6.5) for removing the excess of detergent. From these operations we obtained the so called DDM-AGC2 vesicles (Figure S2). All operations were performed at 4 °C except for the passages through Amberlite, which were carried out at room temperature.

Size exclusion chromatography for detergent removal in AGC2 reconstituted in DDM based vesicles

The excess of sarkosyl was removed from the solubilized protein mixture by repeated passage (10–15 times) over Amberlite XAD-4 resin until the disappearance of foam, indicating effective detergent removal (according to [13, 63–65]) for obtaining the so called Amb-AGC2 (Figure S2). Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) was employed as an extra-step of protein purification and detergent removal. Indeed, it is reported that to remove the excess of detergent from the reconstituted membrane proteins, Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) is considered a valuable approach due to SEC capacity to separate molecules based on their hydrodynamic radius [66]. Here, a 10 × 300 mm Enrich SEC 650 column was used equipped on NGC Quest 10 Plus FPLC system. The protein buffer was composed of 10 mM PIPES, 50 mM NaCl, 0,1 mg/mL DPG (in TNS0.6%), 0.1% dodecil-β-maltoside, pH 6.5, whereas the elution buffer consisted of 10 mM PIPES and 50 mM NaCl, pH 6.5. The flow was set at 1 ml/min. The purity of the AGC2 protein, eluted by the column in 1 ml fractions, was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1, Figure S3). From these operations we obtained the so called FPLC-AGC2 vesicles (Figure S2).

Transmission electron microscopy

In order to identify the localizations of AGC2 monomers, samples of empty liposome (LPs), AGC2 proteoliposomes (PL-AGC2), AGC2 reconstituted DDM-based vesicles (DDM-AGC2), after purification of DDM-AGC2 on amberlite (Amb-AGC2), and DDM-AGC2 after FPLC purification (FPLC-AGC2), were processed for immunogold labelling. Essentially carbonated grids were floated for a few minutes on a drop of diluted suspensions of liposomes, twice rinsed on 0.05 M phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.01% Tween 20 (PBST) for 5 min each and incubated for 30 min on a drop of primary antibodies to SLC25A13 raised in rabbit (Recombinant Human Calcium-binding mitochondrial carrier protein Citrin/AGC2, Immunogen residues 1–300, CSB-PA892444ESR2HU, Cusabio Technology LLC Houston USA) diluted 1: 20 with PBST. After floating twice on drop of PBST for 5 min each, the grids were exposed for 15 min to a preparation of colloidal gold 5 nm in diameter conjugated with anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Anti-Rabbit IgG—Gold antibody produced in goat, G7277, Sigma Aldrich- Merck KGaA, Germany) diluted 1: 100 with PBST. Grids were rinsed twice with PBS and twice with glass-distilled water for 5 min each before negative staining by 1% w/v uranyl acetate solution-UA ZERO (AGR1000—UA-Zero® EM Stain, Agar Scientific Ltd Essex UK). After draining off the excess of staining solution by means of a filter paper, the samples were transferred to the electron microscope for examination, using a Philips Morgagni 282D transmission electron microscope, operating at 80 kV. Electron micrographs of negatively stained samples were photographed on Kodak electron microscope film 4489 (kodak Company, New York, USA). For controls, one batch of the same samples was processed without the primary antibodies step and another without negative staining.

Dataset from micrographs

ImageJ (Rasband, W.S) software was used to extrapolate the measurements from TEM images. The straight-line tool was used to calculate the liposome/vesicle diameter as the average of 8 orthogonal measurements across the vesicle, and to determine the monomer–monomer distance. Excel software (Microsoft Excel-2021) was used to calculate the mean.

3D Comparative modeling

Structure determination of membrane proteins has proven to be far from trivial [67], in particular for mitochondrial carriers of the SLC25A family. The absence in the database RCSB Protein Data Bank of the crystallized structure of AGC made it necessary the construction of three-dimensional atomic models by comparative modeling. Considering that the N-terminal of AGC1 (apo and calcium bound states) and AGC2 (apo state) [36] together with the transmembrane domains of several ADP/ATP carriers [16, 17] and UCPs [18, 19] are available on the PDB, it was possible to build reliable multitemplate comparative 3Dmodels of the human AGC2 (as already done for other protein investigated in our laboratories [1, 5, 13, 14, 20, 34, 68]). Our 3D multitemplate comparative models are based on the sequence/structure alignment consisting of the sequence to be modelled and crystallized protein structures used as a template to drive the 3D protein modeling of the AGC N-terminal domain (for the modelling of residues 1–324) and of the bovine ADP/ATP carrier (for the modelling of residues 325–605). The accuracy of the so-generated 3D models is based on the multiple sequence alignment strengthened by PhyloPscores previously described [1].

Virtual screening of a chemical library

The chemical library used for our docking-based virtual screening was created by taking all the ligands greater than 10 atoms and lighter than 1200 g/mol from the KEGG ligand database and other molecules from KEGG drugs databas, as previously described [13, 52, 69]. All the ligands were in “.mol2” format [13]. We added to this library the known AGC1 inhibitors such as suramin (identified as an AGC1 inhibitor in our previous characterization assays [13]), bathophenanthroline, pyridoxal phosphate, N-amino-l-aspartate, 1,2,3-benzenetricarboxylate, N-ethylaniline, 3-Hydroxy-l-glutamate, p-chloridemercuripheylsulphate [7]. All the ligand structures within our library were energetically optimized (2D ligands were converted in 3D ligands, missing hydrogen atoms and charges were added where necessary) by using OpenBabel (http://openbabel.org/wiki/Main_Page) and were converted in “.mol2” files.

Before proceeding with the docking runs, the AGC2 3D model was prepared for the docking simulation by converting the AGC2 “. pdb” file in a “.pdbqt” file by using the MGLTools of Autodock4.2. The “.pdbqt” file contained gasteiger charges for every atom present in the original AGC2 “.pdb” file. Similarly, all the ligands (.mol2″ files) contained in our chemical library (energetically minimized) were converted in “.pdbqt” files for the virtual screening by using the Autodock scripts. In order to identify the ideal gridbox to be screened with ligands of our library, searching for new putative AGC2 high affinity ligands, firstly we performed single-docking simulations with aspartate, one of the preferred AGC2 substrates, by using Autodock 4.2, starting from the gridbox defined for investigating the ADP/ATP carrier interactions with its inhibitors [13], based on the assumption that mitochondrial carriers share a similarly located substrate binding site [1, 4]. Single docking simulations with aspartate were performed by varying gridbox size and center until having a pose of aspartate interacting with charged residues of the contact points of AGC2 binding site (with reference to R492, D493, R585, R588). The gridbox that was sufficiently large to contain the biggest molecules of our library and that better reproduced the expected interactions between aspartate and AGC2 mentioned binding site residues consists of 96 × 64 × 76 gridpoints. The spacing between gridpoints was 0.281 Å, and the gridcenter was in the point of coordinates 91.742 (x) 73.241 (y) 97.87 (z), according to the orientation of our 3D model in the x, y, z space.

Inhibition (IC50) assays

Transport measurements were performed on PL-AGC2 in presence of different concentrations of the investigated inhibitors, added simultaneously with the radiolabeled substrates. The percentage of residual transport activity was graphed against the corresponding log10 inhibitor concentration in order to determine IC50 values of the tested inhibitors, namely the inhibitor concentration that caused a 50% reduction of the maximum transport activity (namely the estimated transport activity in absence of the tested inhibitors). Although IC50 is not a direct estimation of inhibitor affinity for AGC2, IC50 and inhibitor affinity can be related through the following Cheng–Prusoff Eqs. (1) and (2) (according to [13, 70]).

Thermostability analysis of the AGC2 reconstituted protein in presence of selected ligands

Thermostability data were obtained by using the sulfhydryl-binding dye N-[4-(7-diethylamino-4-methyl-3-coumarinyl)phenyl] maleimide (CPM), which develops an increase in fluorescence emission following the reaction with cysteine residues. Thermostability assays were performed on DDM-AGC2. A modified procedure using a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, United States) was used. A 5 mg/mL CPM stock was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL into the same buffer B used for the reconstitution, vortexed and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Reactions were prepared mixing about 30 μg of reconstituted AGC2 protein with different concentrations of compounds: aspartate and glutamate as AGC2 substrates; suramin, taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate and mersalyl as AGC2 inhibitors. The suramin and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate were solubilized at 100 mM in ultrapure water; the mersalyl at 200 mM in 250 mM NaOH. The fluorescent intensity was measured at 497 nm (excitation) and 520 nm (emission) from 25 °C to 95 °C with a ramp of 1 °C every 15 s, with an initial pre-incubation step of 90 s at 18 °C. Data analysis and calculation of the apparent melting temperature (Tm) was performed using the GraphPad Prism software.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

The heat changes associated with the interaction of the reconstituted FPLC-AGC2 and its inhibitors/substrates were recorded with a MicroCal isothermal titration calorimeter (MicroCal PEAQ-ITC by Malvern Panalitical Ltd., United Kingdom) at 18 °C. As described above, excess detergent was removed using Amberlite treatment followed by size exclusion chromatography. To ensure that any residual detergent did not interfere with the calorimetric assays, control ITC experiments were performed using only the reconstitution buffer without protein (i.e., empty vesicles, also observed in TEM assays). These controls showed no interfering heat signals, confirming that any remaining detergent did not significantly affect the measurements. Additionally, any minor background signals were subtracted during baseline correction to ensure accurate interpretation of protein–ligand interaction data.

The investigated inhibitors/substrates were diluted in the same elution buffer. Suramin, mersalyl and taurolithocholic acid 3-sulfate, at the indicated concentrations were titrated with the reconstituted FPLC-AGC2 (see Table 1 in the Results section, for details about protein and ligand concentration) in 2 µl injections at 2.5 min intervals for all the tested ligands with a stirrer speed of 750 rpm. ITC experiments were performed in triplicate and the data were analyzed using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software (v. 1.41) and fitted to a One Set of Sites binding model [71–74].

Quantification and statistical analyses

For CPM-based thermostability analysis, the apparent melting temperature (Tm) was calculated from the first derivative of the thermal denaturation profile. The average apparent Tm of control samples (without addition of inhibitors/substrates) was subtracted from the apparent Tm measured for each compound addition in the same run, which provides the ΔTm. Statistical significance between groups was assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc analysis to correct for multiple comparisons test were performed using in Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Substrate binding curves related to IC50 estimations obtained by transport assays on PL-AGC2 were fitted with a one-phase association curve and dose–response curves using a sigmoidal dose–response regression curve-fitting. All values are shown as mean ± SEM, unless indicated otherwise.
