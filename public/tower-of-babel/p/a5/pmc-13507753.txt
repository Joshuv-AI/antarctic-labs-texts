Ultrahypofractionated Partial Breast Reirradiation for Ipsilateral Breast Tumor Recurrence
Authors: Jacob F Oyeniyi, Tracy Nassif, Taylor Abraham, Oyinlola Thomas, Ronald Levitin, Gregory Gustafson, Maha S Jawad, Nayana Dekhne, Peter Y Chen, Sayee H Kiran, Carolyn A Schnurr, Joshua T Dilworth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSalvage mastectomy has traditionally been the standard treatment for ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) following breast-conserving therapy (BCT). Repeat lumpectomy followed by brachytherapy-based or hyperfractionated external beam partial breast reirradiation (PBrI) has emerged as an alternative in select patients. Ultrahypofractionated PBrI offers a potentially more convenient approach, but its use remains under investigation. This study evaluates the safety and feasibility of ultrahypofractionated PBrI following repeat lumpectomy.Methods and MaterialsTwenty-five consecutively treated women with IBTR after prior BCT, including whole-breast irradiation, who underwent repeat lumpectomy followed by ultrahypofractionated PBrI between 2021 and 2025, were identified from a prospectively maintained, multicenter, single-institution database. Treatment consisted of 26 Gy in 5 daily fractions using volumetric modulated arc therapy; 2 patients received a simultaneous integrated boost to 30 Gy. Acute (<3 months) and chronic (>3 months) side effects (National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0), cosmetic outcome (Harvard scale), IBTR, regional and distant failure, and overall survival were assessed. Kaplan–Meier estimates analyzed time-to-event outcomes.ResultsMedian age at recurrence was 70 years, with a median interval of 16.6 years from initial treatment. At recurrence, most tumors were stage 0 to I (96%), grade 1 to 2 (88%), estrogen receptor-positive (92%), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative, with a median size of 1.2 cm; 56% recurred in the same quadrant. Following PBrI, 1 patient experienced an IBTR, yielding a crude local control rate of 96%. No regional failure, distant failure, or death occurred. Acute side effects were grade 1 only, including dermatitis (44%), breast edema (12%), breast pain (8%), and fatigue (8%). Late side effects included grade 1/2 breast shrinkage (50%), fibrosis/induration (48%), edema (8%), and telangiectasia (4%). Cosmetic outcome was rated as excellent/good in 71%, and 13% experienced a decline to poor/fair after repeat BCT.ConclusionsUltrahypofractionated PBrI following repeat lumpectomy appears to be well tolerated, with acceptable cosmetic outcomes and short-term cancer control in appropriately selected patients. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Introduction

Breast-conserving therapy (BCT), which combines lumpectomy with adjuvant whole-breast irradiation, is a standard treatment for early-stage breast cancer. Approximately 5% to 20% of patients who initially undergo BCT will experience an ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence (IBTR) during long-term follow-up.1,2 Historically, salvage mastectomy has been the standard management for IBTR after prior whole breast irradiation, achieving local control rates of approximately 90% to 95% at 5 years. Despite its oncologic effectiveness, mastectomy is associated with significant psychosocial consequences. Studies have shown that patients undergoing mastectomy, despite reconstruction, report worse outcomes in body image, sexual well-being, and overall quality of life compared to those undergoing BCT.3,4 Patients who continue to desire breast preservation may undergo repeat lumpectomy. For patients treated with excisional surgery alone, however, IBTR rates approach 20% at 5 years, and breast cancer-specific mortality is increased 1.5-fold, compared to those receiving salvage mastectomy or repeat lumpectomy with adjuvant breast irradiation.5,6

The addition of partial breast reirradiation (PBrI) following repeat lumpectomy offers the potential to preserve the breast, while reducing a second IBTR and maintaining superior cosmetic outcome and quality of life. Evidence from retrospective studies and a single-arm prospective study supports the feasibility and safety of a second course of BCT in carefully selected patients.7, 8, 9, 10 The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group 1014 phase 2, prospective clinical trial investigated the effectiveness and tolerability of PBrI in patients with an IBTR treated with a second lumpectomy. The radiation regimen was hyperfractionated external beam irradiation, involving 30 treatments (administered twice daily and separated by at least 6 hours) that spanned 3 weeks. The study reported an acceptable side effect profile, a 5-year IBTR rate of ∼5%, and an overall survival of 95%.11 Other external beam radiation therapy (EBRT)-based series have reported similar findings.8,12, 13, 14 Likewise, large multi-institutional cohorts using brachytherapy-based PBrI have shown 6-year second recurrence-free survival of 97.4% and overall survival of 91.2%.15 These outcomes are comparable to those achieved with salvage mastectomy, further supporting repeat BCT in appropriately selected cases. Notably, a recent matched analysis from the GEC-ESTRO group found no significant difference in 5-year disease-free or overall survival between repeat BCT and mastectomy, with similar ipsilateral tumor control (∼94%-95%) across both strategies.10 Collectively, these findings support repeat BCT with PBrI as a safe, effective, and patient-centered alternative to mastectomy in select individuals with IBTR, particularly those who develop low-risk, recurrent disease following a long disease-free interval.

Ultrahypofractionated radiation therapy, which delivers higher doses per fraction over a shorter overall treatment duration, has transformed the landscape of primary breast cancer management. The FAST-Forward phase 3 trial demonstrated that 26 Gy in 5 daily fractions over 1 week is noninferior to the standard 40 Gy in 15 fractions for adjuvant whole-breast irradiation, with a 10-year ipsilateral breast tumor relapse rate of approximately 2% and similar rates of late normal tissue toxicity.16,17 Complementing this, the Florence Trial, a randomized phase 3 study evaluating intensity-modulated partial breast irradiation (PBI), found that delivering 30 Gy in 5 fractions to the tumor bed yielded comparable 10-year IBTR rates (1.5%) to 50 Gy in 25 fractions of whole-breast irradiation followed by a surgical bed boost of 10 Gy in 5 fractions, with significantly reduced late toxicity and better cosmetic outcomes.18 These studies affirm the safety of ultrahypofractionated regimens in the primary setting, and importantly, their efficacy in addressing microscopic disease in the breast after lumpectomy with clear surgical margins. Building on this evidence, ultrahypofractionated PBrI presents a potentially more convenient strategy for managing ipsilateral recurrence; however, its safety and feasibility remain under investigation.

Here, we report treatment outcomes, including acute and chronic side effects, cosmetic outcome, and cancer control in patients with IBTR treated with a second lumpectomy followed by ultrahypofractionated PBrI. We hypothesized that this short-course, reirradiation regimen would achieve favorable local control with minimal toxicity, offering a practical and well-tolerated alternative to more protracted reirradiation schedules or salvage mastectomy. These findings aim to contribute to the evolving paradigm of breast conservation in the recurrent setting and support further investigation of ultrahypofractionated PBrI as a viable salvage option.

Methods and Materials

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained, multicenter, single-institution (multiple campuses of a single integrated hospital system) database to identify all women who received ultrahypofractionated PBrI for IBTR. All identified patients meeting eligibility criteria were treated between January 2021 and May 2025, yielding a total of 25 cases. Eligible patients had a history of early-stage breast cancer initially managed with BCT, defined as lumpectomy followed by adjuvant radiation therapy, and subsequently developed a pathologically confirmed IBTR. For initial radiation therapy, all patients received whole-breast irradiation (88% conventionally fractionated), and 92% had received a sequential cavity boost (median total dose: 61 Gy).

At recurrence, all patients declined mastectomy and underwent salvage lumpectomy (without oncoplastic reduction of the involved breast) followed by ultrahypofractionated PBrI. All patients were offered a protracted hyperfractionation course of PBrI but declined. Patients typically had a unifocal recurrence ≤2 cm, histologic grade 1 or 2, hormone receptor-positive disease, clinically and/or pathologically negative axillary nodes, and clear surgical margins on second lumpectomy. All patients had no clinical or radiographic evidence of distant metastases at the time of recurrence. The study was approved by the institutional review board.

Reirradiation treatment details

All patients were treated with ultrahypofractionated PBrI using volumetric modulated arc therapy. All patients underwent computed tomography imaging as part of their radiation therapy planning simulation and had repeat lumpectomy cavities that were well defined and distinguishable from the first lumpectomy cavity (either because of distinct distances between the 2 cavities on imaging or clinically; or because of the use of radiopaque suture in 1 cavity and surgical clips in the other, shown in Fig. 1). The clinical target volume was defined as the postoperative lumpectomy cavity (including clips/sutures, seroma, and architectural distortion) plus a 1.5 cm margin, confined to the ipsilateral breast and adjusted to exclude skin-5 mm and the true chest wall. A planning target volume (PTV) expansion of 5 mm was applied, then cropped 5 mm from the skin surface and from the true chest wall. The use of deep inspiration breath hold was at the discretion of the treating physician. Organs-at-risk contoured included lungs, heart, contralateral breast, and left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) for left-sided cases.

Abbreviation: CT = computed tomography.

The prescription dose was 26 Gy in 5 daily fractions of 5.2 Gy, delivered on consecutive weekdays. Two patients (8%) received a simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) to 30 Gy (6 Gy × 5) to the surgical cavity with no margin (Fig. 2). Receipt of SIB was based on the treating physician’s discretion, and the patients had unfavorable risk factors, including hormone receptor negativity and/or grade 3 disease. Treatment planning aimed to achieve uniform PTV coverage with at least 95% of the PTV_EVAL receiving at least 95% of the prescription dose, while meeting normal tissue constraints. The volume of the ipsilateral lung receiving at least 3 Gy, 5 Gy, and 8 Gy was limited to 50%, 35%, and 15%, respectively. The volume of the contralateral lung receiving at least 2.4 Gy was limited to 10%. The mean heart dose was ideally 0.3 Gy and 0.6 Gy for right-sided and left-sided cases, respectively. Mean and maximum doses to the left anterior descending artery were ideally 1.2 Gy and 5 Gy, respectively, for left-sided cases. The maximum dose to the contralateral breast was limited to 1 Gy (planning constraint not selected for tabular reporting). The volume of a reference ipsilateral breast receiving the prescription dose was considered ideal if less than 25% and acceptable if less than 30%. All treatment plans underwent prospective peer review.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

Patients were assessed once by a physician during the 5-day radiation treatment course and 2 weeks posttreatment by a radiation oncology registered nurse for a skin check. They were then seen in follow-up by a physician at 3 months and 1 year post-treatment, then yearly. At each visit, history and physical exam were performed, including breast exam and assessment of toxicity and cosmetic outcome. Annual mammographic surveillance of the treated breast was done per institutional protocol. Acute side effects were defined as events occurring from the start of reirradiation to <3 months posttreatment, and late side effects as those occurring ≥3 months posttreatment, graded per National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0. Physician-graded cosmesis was assessed by the Harvard 4-point Cosmesis Scale (Excellent, Good, Fair, and Poor) at baseline (postlumpectomy, pre-PBrI) and at follow-up based on breast size/shape symmetry, scarring, and fibrosis compared to the untreated breast.

Tumor control outcomes assessed included rates of IBTR, distant metastasis, and overall survival after retreatment. For patients who developed a second IBTR, subsequent management and outcomes were noted.

Statistical analysis

This study employed descriptive statistics given the relatively small sample size and short follow-up duration. Patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics were summarized with medians and inter-quartile range (IQR)/range or proportions as appropriate. Time-to-event outcomes, including IBTR, distant metastasis, and overall survival, were planned to be estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Toxicity and cosmesis outcomes were summarized as incidence proportions. Data analysis was performed with R version 4.4.2.

Results

Patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics

Twenty-five female patients met the inclusion criteria. Table 1 details patient and tumor characteristics at recurrence. Briefly, median age at recurrence was 70 years (Range, 61-86) with a median interval from initial BCT to IBTR of 16.6 years. At recurrence, almost all tumors (96%) were early stage: 17% were pure ductal carcinoma in situ, and 79% were invasive stage I. Of note, 1 patient classified as pT4 had a clinically T1 lesion that was upstaged due to focal microscopic skin involvement identified following excision, without gross or extensive skin involvement. The median recurrent tumor size was 1.2 cm (Range, 0.1-2.0 cm) following repeat lumpectomy, and 88% were low-to-intermediate grade (grade 1-2). All patients were clinically node negative at the time of IBTR. All patients received standard diagnostic breast workup, including mammography and breast ultrasonography; 20% underwent breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as part of their workup, 44% had axillary ultrasound, and 36% underwent positron emission tomography/computed tomography. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) was performed in 44% of patients at the time of repeat lumpectomy, of which 72% had successful mapping. All SLNB procedures in this cohort yielded pathologically negative nodes. The majority of recurrent tumors (92%) were estrogen receptor (ER) positive, and none was human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positive. The majority (56%) of recurrences occurred in the same quadrant as the first cancer, and the remaining arose in a different quadrant, suggesting a mix of true recurrences and new primaries. Only 2 patients (8%) received adjuvant chemotherapy after the salvage lumpectomy, and 56% of patients with hormone receptor-positive tumors received endocrine therapy.

Treatment delivery and dosimetry

All 25 patients completed the ultrahypofractionated PBrI regimen as planned. Three patients with left-sided tumors used deep inspiration breath hold. Treatment breaks or significant deviations were not observed. The median PTV was 191 cm3 (IQR, 142-234), with a breast volume of 1127 cm3 (IQR, 739-1320) and a PTV-to-breast volume ratio of 18% (IQR, 15%-20%). All planning constraints were adhered to, and dosimetric parameters are detailed in Table 2. The median breast volume subtracted from PTV V50% (breast-PTV V50%) was 32.1% (IQR, 25.5-36.1); though this parameter is reported by other authors, we did not routinely optimize for it in our treatment planning.19 The median volume of the PTV receiving at least 95% of the prescribed dose (PTV V95%) was 99.86% (IQR, 98.97-99.95) with a median PTV V105% of 0% (IQR, 0-0.01). Of note, in patients who received SIB, the SIB dose of 30 Gy was used to determine PTV V95% and PTV V105%. The median mean heart dose was 0.44 Gy (IQR, 0.34-0.65) and the median mean LAD dose (for left-sided cases) of 1.18 Gy (IQR, 0.76-1.89).

Toxicity and cosmesis outcomes

All observed acute side effects were grade 1 (mild) and are listed in Table 3. The most common acute effect was radiation dermatitis, seen in 11 patients (44%), characterized by faint erythema or hyperpigmentation. Breast edema was noted in 3 patients (13%), 2 patients (8%) had transient breast pain, and 2 (8%) reported fatigue. There was no dry or moist desquamation, grade 2+ pain, or any grade pneumonitis.

All late side effects were grade 1 or 2 with no grade 3 or higher toxicity observed in patients with >3 months of follow-up. The most common late effect was breast shrinkage/volume loss, observed in 50% of patients, and breast fibrosis in 48%. Breast edema was present in 2 patients (8%), and telangiectasia was seen in 1 patient (4%). Importantly, no patient developed chronic breast pain, rib fracture, or skin ulceration.

Oncologic outcomes

At a median follow-up of 13 months (IQR, 7-24, Range, 4-49) after reirradiation, 1 patient (4%) experienced a second IBTR. This patient had a prior history of right upper outer quadrant ductal carcinoma in situ treated with lumpectomy and whole-breast irradiation, followed by a recurrence 17 years later of ER/progesterone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative invasive ductal carcinoma. She underwent repeat lumpectomy demonstrating a 1.7 cm grade 3 tumor with clear (>2 mm) surgical margins, followed by PBrI (PTV V95 = 98%), but declined endocrine therapy. At approximately 18 months postreirradiation, imaging revealed a 1.7 cm ipsilateral breast mass, biopsy-confirmed as ER/progesterone receptor-positive grade 2 invasive ductal carcinoma. She again declined a mastectomy and underwent a third lumpectomy.

In the overall cohort, the Kaplan–Meier estimate of IBTR-free survival was 100% (95% CI, 100%-100%) at 12 months, and 90% (95% CI, 71%-100%) at 36 months, although only 2 patients remained at risk at the latter timepoint (Fig. 3). There were no regional nodal recurrences, distant metastases, or deaths.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first series evaluating ultrahypofractionated PBrI following salvage lumpectomy for IBTR. We observed encouraging short-term local control with no observed regional or distant failures, only low-grade acute side effects, and acceptable late side effects and cosmetic outcomes. These results support the feasibility of ultrahypofractionated PBrI as a patient-centered and logistically convenient alternative to hyperfractionated reirradiation or salvage mastectomy.

Patient selection and oncologic outcomes

The success of re-BCT is contingent on careful patient selection. In our study, 96% of patients met the ASTRO 2024 eligibility criteria for PBI.20 Median tumor size at recurrence was 1.2 cm, 92% were ER positive, 88% were grade 1 to 2, and all margins were clear. These features are well recognized as criteria for optimal selection of repeat BCT and are similar to the patient populations in brachytherapy PBrI series, as well as Radiation Therapy Oncology Group 1014, which also included higher-risk patients such as those with triple-negative or grade 3 disease.11,21

In our cohort, only 1 patient (4%) experienced a second IBTR, yielding estimated IBTR-free survival of 100% at 12 months and 90% at 36 months. There were no regional nodal or distant recurrences, and no deaths. These outcomes align with prior EBRT and brachytherapy reirradiation studies, which reported 5-year IBTR rates of ∼5% to 7% and survival comparable to salvage mastectomy.11,15,21 Similarly, our results support the principle that repeat BCT is most appropriate for patients with small, low-risk recurrences occurring after a long disease-free interval (median 16.6 years in our cohort).

Of note, only 56% of patients in our study received endocrine therapy despite 92% having hormone receptor-positive disease. The reasons why many eligible patients did not receive endocrine therapy were not captured; however, plausible explanations include patient refusal, clinical contraindications, or prior intolerance and adverse effects. The impact of this underutilization on long-term outcomes remains unclear. Nonetheless, current standards for recurrent hormone receptor-positive breast cancer generally include endocrine therapy as a key component of management.

Toxicity and cosmetic outcome

Ultrahypofractionated PBrI was very well tolerated. No grade ≥2 acute side effects were observed, and late side effects were limited to grade 1 to 2 effects, primarily breast shrinkage and fibrosis. While longer follow-up is required to fully assess late fibrosis and any further cosmetic decline, our data show that late effects were mild and manageable and compare favorably to historical PBrI series, where grade ≥3 late toxicities have been reported in 4% to 10% of patients.9,11,14,21,22 We did not systematically collect baseline pre-PBrI toxicity data, which limits our ability to distinguish treatment-emergent effects—particularly fibrosis and breast shrinkage—from pre-existing changes related to prior surgery and irradiation; however, the absence of any grade ≥3 toxicities despite 2 courses of breast irradiation remains a reassuring indicator of the tolerability of this approach. Cosmetic outcomes in our study were also encouraging, with 71% of patients maintaining excellent or good cosmesis, comparable to the 70% to 85% range reported in brachytherapy and other EBRT PBrI series.10,11,23 Notably, only 13% of patients experienced a posttreatment cosmetic downgrade to fair/poor, highlighting the potential for continued cosmetic preservation. Importantly, there were no cases of chronic pain, rib fractures, or severe adverse events in our cohort. These findings add to the growing evidence supporting the safety of breast preservation with high-dose-per-fraction schedules, as demonstrated in brachytherapy studies, and further show that this approach can be safely implemented using EBRT, which offers the advantage of being noninvasive.

In our study, the median PTV-to-whole-breast volume ratio was also low at 18%, which likely contributed to the favorable cosmetic outcomes observed. Appropriate patient selection is important, and expectations regarding cosmetic results should be discussed carefully. Some patients may not have sufficient residual breast volume to accommodate a second lumpectomy without compromising cosmetic outcome, and some may have a large lumpectomy cavity relative to the residual breast volume, further increasing the risk of suboptimal cosmetic outcome. Nevertheless, many patients may still prefer breast conservation despite these potential aesthetic trade-offs, underscoring the importance of individualized counseling and shared decision-making. These considerations highlight the need to incorporate patient-reported outcomes alongside physician-assessed data. Patient-reported measures would provide valuable insights into quality of life, and patient satisfaction with treatment.

Imaging and axillary evaluation

Modern imaging played a role in recurrence workup: 20% of patients underwent breast MRI, 44% axillary ultrasound, and 36% positron emission tomography/computed tomography. While MRI was not universally used, its ability to detect multifocal or multicentric disease makes it a valuable adjunct, consistent with its requirement in RTOG 1014. Axillary evaluation also warrants discussion. In our cohort, 44% underwent SLNB, the majority (72%) of which were successful, with others staged with imaging and physical examination alone. This reflects evolving practice patterns: the Sentinal Node vs Observation After Axillary Ultrasound (SOUND) and Intergroup Sentinel Mamma (INSEMA) trials demonstrated that axillary surgery can be safely omitted in select clinically node-negative patients, provided careful imaging is performed.24,25 Importantly, eligibility in both SOUND and INSEMA required patients to consent to receive radiation therapy. However, the applicability of these findings to breast cancer recurrences or PBI is uncertain as both trials accrued patients with primary breast cancer not those with recurrent disease, and approximately 10% of patients treated on the SOUND trial and none from INSEMA received PBI. Given that SLNB success decreases after prior breast and axillary surgery, axillary imaging remains essential for confirming a negative axilla in this setting.26 In the event of pathologically node-positive disease at repeat SLNB, PBrI would not be ideal and regional nodal irradiation would require consideration. The UK FAST-FORWARD nodal trial, delivering 26 Gy in 5 fractions to the whole breast and regional nodes, offers a conceptual framework for ultrahypofractionated nodal irradiation and could be investigated in future reirradiation studies. Importantly, longer follow-up is needed to confirm low rates of regional and distant recurrence in those who do not have repeat SLNB.

Technical considerations: cavity delineation, dose and dosimetry

One unique challenge in reirradiation is distinguishing between the original and recurrent lumpectomy cavities, particularly when tumors arise in the same quadrant (observed in 56% of our patients). Accurate localization is essential to avoid geographic miss. Practical strategies include the placement of radiopaque sutures in 1 cavity and clips in the other. MRI correlation may also be helpful.

Dosimetrically, our series demonstrated excellent plan quality. The median PTV-to-breast volume ratio of 18% shows significant sparing of the majority of the ipsilateral breast. Nearly all patients achieved >99% PTV V95% coverage, and hotspots (V105%) were minimal. Doses to organs at risk were low, with a median mean heart dose of 0.44 Gy (IQR, 0.34-0.65) and a median mean LAD dose (for left-sided cases) of 1.18 Gy (IQR, 0.76-1.89). These metrics demonstrate the advantages of volumetric modulated arc therapy planning to minimize dose to organs at risk and untreated ipsilateral breast tissue, which is especially important to minimize toxicities and optimize cosmesis in this reirradiation setting.

We prescribed 26 Gy in 5 fractions, consistent with the FAST-Forward regimen in the primary setting. For select patients with higher-risk features, escalation or SIB to 30 Gy in 5 fractions, biologically akin to a boost as used in the Florence Trial, may be reasonable.16,18 Importantly, ongoing prospective trials (eg, NCT05592938, NCT04371913) are evaluating ultrahypofractionated PBI regimens of 26 or 30 Gy in 5 fractions in the reirradiation setting, and our data provide early real-world support for investigating this approach. More broadly, our findings contribute to the growing body of literature on hypofractionated reirradiation strategies, and further investigations are warranted.

Limitations and future directions

This study is limited primarily by its small sample size and relatively short follow-up, precluding definitive conclusions about long-term local control, late toxicity, and cosmesis. Of note, more than half of our patients have at least 12 months of follow-up, a timepoint commonly used to assess for adverse effects in prospective clinical trials of PBrI, including RTOG 1014.11 Also, given the retrospective nature and treatment-selected design of the study, selection bias is inherent, and the findings may not be generalizable to the broader population of patients with IBTR. Rather, our results reflect outcomes in a highly selected group of patients motivated to pursue breast conservation and willing to accept the uncertainties associated with reirradiation. Further, the lack of a comparative cohort treated with hyperfractionated PBrI or mastectomy limits direct outcome comparisons. Finally, we did not include patient-reported outcomes or formal quality-of-life assessments, which are important in evaluating the full impact of breast conservation strategies and the advantages of a shorter course of treatment.

Despite these limitations, our data highlight the promise of ultrahypofractionated PBrI as a practical alternative to salvage mastectomy, invasive brachytherapy, or protracted hyperfractionated reirradiation courses. Given the increasing use of hypofractionation in the primary setting, its extension into the retreatment paradigm is logical and may increase accessibility and patient convenience. Prospective studies with larger cohorts, standardized imaging and axillary staging protocols, as well as longer follow-up are needed to confirm its long-term efficacy, safety, and patient-reported quality-of-life outcomes. Incorporating genomic biomarkers may further help to refine patient selection.

Conclusion

Ultrahypofractionated PBrI following salvage lumpectomy offers a promising, convenient, and well-tolerated strategy for managing IBTR following initial BCT. Careful patient selection and accurate cavity delineation remain critical. Our early findings support ultrahypofractionation as a practical extension of the hypofractionation paradigm into the reirradiation setting. Prospective studies with longer follow-up will be essential to validate efficacy, durability, and cosmetic outcomes, as well as assess patient-reported outcomes.
