Cardiac Manifestations of KCNK17 Mutations and/or Polymorphisms: A Systematic Review
Authors: Amir Askarinejad, Mahshid Malakootian, Dorsa Shekouh, Amirreza Sabahizadeh, Majid Haghjoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe KCNK17 gene encodes k2p17.1 channels (TASK‐4 or TALK‐2) with dominant expressions in the atria and the Purkinje fibers. Emerging studies have suggested possible associations between KCNK17 variants and cardiovascular as well as cerebrovascular diseases. This review aimed to systematically evaluate the evidence on KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms in cardiovascular diseases (primary outcome) and cerebrovascular diseases (secondary outcome). Given the fragmented and limited evidence, a systematic review is warranted to summarize current knowledge and clarify potential clinical implications.MethodsPubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched for studies evaluating any cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases associated with KCNK17 published up to August 1, 2023. Inclusion criteria were original studies reporting KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms in relation to cardiovascular diseases (primary outcome) or cerebrovascular diseases (secondary outcome). Exclusion criteria were studies evaluating pharmacological interventions on the K2P17.1 (TALK‐2) channel, as well as reviews, editorials, and non‐original articles.ResultsNine studies concerning KCNK17 were finally included in the systematic review. Six studies evaluated cardiovascular diseases, including atrial fibrillation, cancer‐induced cardiac dysfunction, long QT syndrome, and progressive cardiac conduction defects, and 3 studies evaluated cerebrovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke and cerebral hemorrhage.ConclusionsRecent genetic discoveries in the aforementioned diseases warrant further research to develop innovative cardiovascular treatments based on KCNK17.

Introduction

Two‐pore‐domain potassium (k2p) channels are described as potassium (K + ) leak channels that regulate cellular excitability in almost all tissues, dissimilar to the tetramer formation of typical K+ channels concerning subunit and dimer formation [1]. (Figure 1.) The k2P family in humans is coded by 15 genes [2], of which 9 are expressed in the heart.

The image depicts the 2‐pore‐domain potassium channel, consisting of 4 transmembrane subunits (M1–M4) and 2 loops (P1 and P2).

Two k2P channels, k2p2.1 (TREK‐1) and k2p6.1 (TWIK‐2), are expressed in the cardiac chambers of human, predominantly in the ventricles [1, 3]. In human, the KCNK2 gene encodes k2p2.1 (TREK‐1), and KCNK2 mutations are linked to right ventricular outflow tract tachycardia [4]. Additionally, k2p6.1 (TWIK‐2) knockout in mice is associated with elevated right ventricular systolic pressure and pulmonary hypertension [5].

In human heart, K2p1.1 (TWIK‐1), k2p3.1 (TASK‐1), k2p5.1 (TASK‐2), k2p7.1 (TWIK‐3), k2p9.1 (TASK‐3), k2p13.1 (THIK‐1), and k2p17.1 (TALK‐2) channels are expressed in the ventricles and, especially, in the atria [1, 6, 7, 8]. In porcine models, the upregulation of k2p3.1 (TASK‐1) is associated with atrial fibrillation (AF) [9]. Notably, the k2P13.1 channel (THIK‐1) is expressed in the human heart, and its level in the atria declines in patients with AF or heart failure [10]. The k2p17.1 channel (TALK‐2) is encoded by the KCNK17 gene in humans, with dominant expressions in the atria and the Purkinje fibers of the ventricles [11, 12] (Figure 1). Mutations and/or polymorphisms of KCNK17 result in cardiovascular diseases, such as arrhythmias, heart failure, and cancer‐induced cardiac dysfunction, and cerebrovascular diseases, such as ischemic stroke and cerebral hemorrhage [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21] (Figure 2).

The image presents diseases associated with KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms.

The current study is our systematic review of the cardiac and cerebrovascular manifestations of KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms. Given the emerging but fragmented evidence linking KCNK17 variants to arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation, and cerebrovascular events, a comprehensive synthesis of these findings is warranted to clarify their potential clinical significance. To our knowledge, we herein present the first comprehensive review of the cardiac manifestations of KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms.

Methods

The present study was conducted in accordance with the standard methodology of the Cochrane Handbook [22], and the results are reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyzes (PRISMA) 2020 statement [23].

Search Strategy

PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched for studies on cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases associated with the KCNK17 gene published up to August 1, 2023. The following MeSH terms were used: the KCNK17 gene, TALK‐2, TASK‐4, and k2p channels (Supporting Information Table S1).

Study Selection

Titles, abstracts, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and full texts were reviewed independently by 2 reviewers (AA and AS) as a part of the eligibility assessment. In the event of expected disagreements, a panel discussion settled the dispute, and unresolved issues were referred to a third‐party reviewer (MH). Any studies on cardiovascular diseases associated with KCNK17 were included initially. Studies evaluating pharmacological interventions on the k2p17.1 channel (TALK‐2) were excluded, as were editorials, reviews, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms associated with cardiovascular diseases were considered the primary outcome, while KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms associated with cerebrovascular diseases were regarded as the secondary outcome.

Data Extraction

A data extraction form was designed by AA and AS, who independently extracted data from studies that met the eligibility criteria and resolved any disagreements through consensus. The following information was extracted: the name of the first author, the year of publication, the study design, the name of the country, the main finding, the disease, and KCNK17 mutations and/or polymorphisms.

Results

The flow diagram of study selection is illustrated in Figure 3. A total of 3269 papers were identified using systematic searches of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. After the removal of duplicate records, 2028 articles were subjected to further screening. Totally, 2008 records were excluded based on an assessment of their title and abstract since their topics of study or findings were considered unrelated to our research. Subsequently, an in‐depth assessment of 20 papers was performed through the examination of their full‐text content. Nine studies with a total population of 8159 individuals were ultimately included [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Cardiovascular Diseases

Six studies evaluated cardiovascular diseases, consisting of AF [16, 21], ventricular fibrillation (VF) [15], long QT syndrome [19], and cardiac dysfunction [16, 18], in association with the KCNK17 gene. (Figure 2.).

Atrial Fibrillation

In the analysis of messenger RNA (mRNA) from the left atrial free wall taken during mitral valve surgery on 62 Caucasian individuals, KCNK17 was one of the genes with a significantly reduced expression in the atrial tissue of patients who had postoperative AF [21]. Schmidt et al. [16] analyzed the right atrial tissue of 135 patients with paroxysmal AF, chronic AF, and sinus rhythm. Chronic AF was associated with an elevated expression level of k2p3.1 but decreased mRNA and protein abundance of k2p17.1. In patients with sinus rhythm, a severely reduced left ventricular function was associated with decreased expression levels of k2p3.1 and k2p17.1.

Ventricular Fibrillation

The whole‐exome sequencing (WES) analysis of a patient with a progressive cardiac conduction defect (PCCD) and idiopathic VF demonstrated a nucleotide change in KCNK17, c.262 G > A, leading to an amino acid substitution (p.G88R) in k2p17.1. Further, the KCNK17 gene was introduced as a novel gene contributing to significant cardiac conduction disorders, primarily VF [15].

Long QT Syndrome

Chai et al. [19] concluded that the S21G variant of KCNK17 was a protective modifier for long QT syndrome. This variant might promote enhanced repolarization reserve and protect individuals from the severely affected phenotypes of long QT syndrome.

Progressive Cardiac Conduction Defect

Mutations in the KCNK17 gene (p.Gly88Arg) were reported as a novel arrhythmia gene in a patient with PCCD [15]. The results of WES in a patient with severe PCCD and idiopathic VF revealed that the G88R mutation was a disease‐modifying mutation for PCCD [15].

Cardiac Dysfunction

Chai et al. [14] found that k2p17.1 was significantly lower in nonischemic heart failure tissues than in control normal heart ventricles.

In 2022, Sapkota et al. [18] reported the results of the whole‐genome sequencing of 1870 survivors of European ancestry. Their study revealed a novel locus in the 6p21.2 region, rs2815063, associated with doxorubicin‐induced cardiac dysfunction. The rs2815063 variant in the KCNK17 gene was associated with a 1.80‐fold (p = 0.008) risk of severe, disabling, or life‐threatening PCCD in childhood cancer survivors treated with doxorubicin.

Cerebrovascular Diseases

Three of the studies included in the present study evaluated the association between KCNK17 variants and cerebrovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke and cerebral hemorrhage [13, 17, 19, 20].

He et al. [24] pinpointed specific single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the KCNK17 gene in association with cerebral hemorrhage and transient ischemic attack in the Chinese population. Moreover, the rs12214600 (C/T) SNP was significantly associated with cerebral hemorrhage (OR, 0.55; 95% CI, 5.35–0.86; p = 0.0082, q = 0.0328), and rs2758912 (A/G) and rs10807204 (C/G) SNPs were associated with transient ischemic attack (OR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.09–2.53; p = 0.0174, q = 0.0348).

An analysis of 271 new ischemic stroke cases indicated that the A allele of rs10947803 was associated with ischemic stroke (OR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.14–1.91; p = 0.003). Interestingly, the gene expression analysis in the same investigation revealed that KCNK17 mRNA levels were higher in the ischemic stroke group in the acute phase than in the control group (14 ± 78% vs 91 ± 41; p = 0.002) but not in the chronic phase (56 ± 57%; p = 0.230) [17].

Ma et al. [20] indicated that the rs10947803 SNP (A allele) of KCNK17 was significantly associated with cerebral hemorrhage (adjusted OR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.04–2.62; p = 0.033) but not with ischemic stroke (adjusted OR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.72–1.05; p = 0.143) in the Chinese population.

Discussion

AF is the most common rhythm disorder and a major public health concern since it elevates the risk of arterial thromboembolism and ischemic stroke [25]. The development of various scoring systems over the years has simplified the evaluation process, with the CHA2DS2VASc scoring system being the most extensively utilized [26]. Previous studies have provided evidence indicating that elevated levels of C‐reactive protein and interleukin‐6, along with the existence of G20210A factor II gene polymorphism and hyperhomocysteinemia, are independent risk factors for ischemic complications in individuals with AF [27].

Genetic factors play a prominent role in the pathogenesis of AF [28, 29, 30]. Most genes involved in AF pathogenesis encode ion channels, distinct cardiac‐enriched transcription factors, and other regulatory genes [31]. In human, the expressions or function alterations of specific k2p channels, namely k2p3.1 (TASK‐1), k2p1.1 (TWIK‐1), and k2p17.1 (TASK‐4 or TALK‐2), are associated with AF [6, 32]. A prior investigation reported that the current density of TASK‐1 in isolated human atrial cardiomyocytes was considerably higher in patients with paroxysmal and chronic AF than in those with normal sinus rhythm [16]. Intriguingly, an increased expression of the KCNK17 gene induces action potential duration (APD) prolongation, whereas a decreased expression leads to APD shortening [3, 15]. Shortening of APD reduces reentry wavelength and promotes AF initiation, development, and persistence [33, 34], which may constitute an underlying mechanism linking the KCNK17 gene to AF. To sum up, the KCNK17 gene significantly influences AF pathogenesis. The current dearth of data on this topic, nonetheless, warrants further research into the underlying mechanisms.

Among the genes investigated by Sigurdsson et al. [21], KCNK17 exhibited a notable decrease in expression within the atrial tissue of patients who developed postoperative AF. A study by Schmidt et al. [16] comprised a collection of right atrial tissue from 135 patients diagnosed with paroxysmal AF, chronic AF, and sinus rhythm. Chronic AF was associated with the elevated expression of the k 2p 3.1 gene, while there were lower k 2P 17.1 mRNA levels and protein concentrations. In addition, patients with sinus rhythm exhibited a significant decrease in the left ventricular function, correlated with diminished expression levels of k2p3.1 and k2p17.1.

AF treatment continues to pose a formidable challenge in cardiovascular medicine [35]. Recent years have, however, witnessed the emergence of k2p channels as novel pharmacological targets for AF treatment [36]. The classical mechanism of atrial arrhythmogenesis in AF involves reduced electrical conduction velocity, shortened APD, and effective refractory periods, promoting electrical reentry and inducing AF. As mentioned, a clear link has been established between k2p17.1 downregulation and AF and APD shortening [1, 16, 21]. In their analysis of the electropharmacology of channels, Staudacher et al. [12] found that in human TASK‐4 and TALK‐2 were significantly activated by propafenone, quinidine, mexiletine, propranolol, and metoprolol. (Table 3.) Recent data on the arrhythmogenic effects of KCNK17 gene variants on the cardiovascular system need further investigations to demonstrate this gene as a therapeutic target for arrhythmias.

VF is a common life‐threatening arrhythmia with significant mortality and morbidity [38]. Genetics, epigenetics, lifestyle, electrocardiographic markers, socioeconomic and environmental factors, cardiac symptoms, and public education can aid VF risk assessment [39]. Monogenic factors, including mutations in SCN5A, KCNQ1, KCNH2, and RYR2, and polygenetic factors are associated with ventricular arrhythmias in non‐structural heart diseases. Moreover, epigenetics with noncoding RNA, DNA methylation, histone modifications, and genomic imprinting can play a significant role in VF [40]. A WES analysis of 76 patients with idiopathic VF demonstrated some clinically relevant gene variants in association with cardiac channelopathies and cardiomyopathies [41]. Nucleotide alterations in K + ‐ATP and voltage‐gated sodium channels are associated with idiopathic VF [42]. Genetic factors seem to affect VF significantly, an issue in need of further elucidation.

The WES analysis of a patient diagnosed with PCCD and idiopathic VF revealed a specific nucleotide alteration in the KCNK17 gene, c.262 G > A, leading to an amino acid substitution (p.G88R) within the k2p channel. The results also presented the KCNK17 gene as a newly identified gene linked to the pathogenesis of significant cardiac conduction abnormalities, including VF [15].

The cardiac mRNA of KCNK17 is vastly expressed in the Purkinje fibers [10, 29]. Strong evidence also indicates the vital role of the Purkinje fibers in AF induction [43]. According to a previous investigation, in human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes, the suppression of the KCNK17 gene was associated with APD shortening, whereas the overexpression of this gene led to APD prolongation [3, 15]. Jeong et al. [44] concluded that APD shortening might augment the sensitivity of heart tissue to VF. Collectively, compelling evidence suggests that KCNK17 expression alterations may significantly impact VF genesis.

Long QT syndrome is a cardiac electrophysiological disorder associated with cardiac arrhythmias, syncope, and rarely sudden cardiac death [45]. In human, mutations in KCNQ1 and KCNH2 cause voltage‐gated K+ channels to malfunction and lead to diminished repolarizing K+ currents or persistent inward sodium currents, resulting in prolonged depolarization [46]. Remarkably, k2p channels control resting membrane potential and action potential repolarization [47]. Therefore, alterations in the function of k2p channels may be associated with long QT syndrome. Decher et al. [48] reported that blocking the TASK‐1 channel encoded by the KCNK3 gene in mice caused QT prolongation and QRS complex disturbance.

Strikingly, TALK‐2 channel subunits encoded by the KCNK17 gene heterodimerized with the TASK‐1 channel encoded by the KCNK3 gene in a prior study [49]. In humans, the heteromeric form of k2p channels, such as TALK‐2 and TASK‐1, may have distinctive in vitro and in vivo effects compared with their homomeric form. It is noteworthy that heteromerization between TASK‐1 and TALK‐2 subunits requires clarification [50].

Chai et al. [19] found in vitro that the S21G variant of the KCNK17 gene served as a protective modifier in cases with long QT syndrome. This particular genetic variation possesses the potential capacity to promote an increased repolarization reserve and confer protection against the severe manifestations of long QT syndrome. Although data on the specific role of the KCNK17 gene in long QT syndrome are very limited, preliminary findings suggest that it may influence susceptibility to QT interval alterations. Indeed, an in‐depth knowledge of the KCNK17 gene is needed to determine details of its effects on long QT syndrome.

PCCD is one of the most common heart conduction disorders, and it usually manifests itself in the fifth decade of life [11, 51]. Several genes are involved in the pathogenesis of PCCD. These genes predominantly encode specific cardiac channels such as the sodium channel (SCN5), the calcium ion (Ca2 + )‐activated nonspecific channel (TRPM4), and voltage‐gated K+ channels (KCNH2, KCNJ2, and KCNQ1) [52]. In PCCD, the involvement of the His‐Purkinje system with the right or left bundle branch block causes QRS complex prolongation eventually. The Purkinje fibers are essential in forming inheritable conduction disorders [53, 54]. In human, the KCNK17 gene is expressed dominantly in the Purkinje fibers, the atrioventricular node, and the sinoatrial node, respectively [12, 15].

Friedrich et al. [15] reported a mutation in the KCNK17 gene (p.Gly88Arg) as a novel arrhythmia gene in a patient with PCCD. The results of WES in a patient with severe PCCD and idiopathic VF revealed that the G88R mutation was a disease‐modifying mutation for PCCD. This finding has led to the conclusion that the KCNK17 gene has an undeniable role in the cardiac conduction system and its severe inherited diseases, such as PCCD. Increased expression of β2‐adrenergic receptors and implantation of vectors transforming the HCN2 gene into an atrium or bundle branch for pacemaking are some gene‐based therapy mechanisms explored recently [55]. Gene‐based therapies, including cardiac‐specific gene transfer technologies, have progressed over the past few years [56, 57]. More investigations into the association between the KCNK17 gene and PCCD may improve these therapeutic approaches.

Stroke is the second most common cause of death and disability worldwide [58]. Ischemic stroke accounts for almost 80% of strokes [59]. A combination of cardiovascular risk factors, environment, and genetic factors may lead to ischemic stroke [60]. Specific genome‐wide risk loci have been linked to ischemic stroke [61]. Further knowledge regarding genetic risk factors for ischemic stroke can be beneficial in designing therapeutic targets and prevention strategies [61].

Four studies in the present systematic review assessed the association between KCNK17 variants and cerebrovascular diseases, such as ischemic stroke and cerebral hemorrhage, in humans [13, 17, 19, 20]. Specific SNPs in the KCNK17 gene are associated with cerebral hemorrhage and transient ischemic attack in the Chinese population, as determined by He et al. [24]. The KCNK17 rs10947803 SNP was linked to ischemic stroke by Domingues‐Montanari et al. [17], who reported that the KCNK17 mRNA level was higher in ischemic stroke cases in the acute phase than in controls, but not in the chronic phase. Ma et al. [20] found that the rs10947803 SNP (A allele) of KCNK17 was associated with cerebral bleeding but not with ischemic stroke in the Chinese population.

In physiological mechanisms, K+ channel activation hyperpolarizes membranes, lowering neuronal activity and cell death. K+ channels, such as large conductance Ca2 + ‐activated and ATP‐sensitive ones, may also protect neurons from prolonged harmful Ca2+ influx via pathways like Na + /Ca2+ antiporter reversal and voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels [62]. Interestingly, mice with a k2p2.1 gene knockout exhibited markedly reduced neuronal survival in a cerebral ischemia condition [63]. As discussed, some KCNK17 gene mutations are associated with AF, known as a major risk factor for ischemic stroke (20‐25 [64], Moreover, in human, mutations in KCNK17 may increase the extracellular K+ concentration, leading to the dilation of cerebral blood vessels [24]. Changes in the function and expression of k2p channels can result in cerebrovascular diseases, such as ischemic ischemia and cerebral hemorrhage, principally due to arrhythmogenic events and diminished neuroprotective effects.

Chemotherapy‐related cardiac dysfunction is one of the most frequent and hazardous complications of chemotherapeutic agents [65], while improved diagnosis and novel treatment strategies have enhanced the life expectancy of patients with cancer [66]. Still, cardiovascular complications caused by chemotherapeutic agents warrant due heed [67] given patients' lifetime struggle concerning the quality of life, survival, and morbidity [68].

Doxorubicin is an anthracycline used to treat many cancers and is associated with irreversible dose‐dependent cardiac dysfunction [69]. Recent human studies have indicated the likelihood of a genetic basis for anthracycline‐based cardiotoxicity [70] and underscored the need for improved patient risk stratification before chemotherapy [71].

In addition, childhood cancer survivors are at considerable risk for cardiomyopathies as long‐term chemotherapy complications [72]. Early detection of childhood cancer survivors at risk of cardiomyopathies may improve outcomes and decrease mortality and morbidity in these patients [73].

Two studies in the current systematic review reported an association between nucleotide variations of the KCNK17 gene and decreased heart systolic function [14, 18]. Chai et al. [14] found that nonischemic heart failure tissues had significantly less k2p17.1 (KCNK17 gene‐expressed channel) than control normal heart ventricles. Sapkota et al. [18] reported the results of the whole‐genome sequencing of 1870 European survivors in 2022 and introduced a new locus on 6p21.2, rs2815063, linked to doxorubicin‐induced cardiac failure. In their investigation, the KCNK17 gene variant, rs2815063, increased the incidence of severe, debilitating, or life‐threatening PCCD in childhood cancer survivors treated with doxorubicin by 1.80‐fold (p = 0.008).

K+ channels are widely acknowledged as the primary ion channels that contribute significantly to the repolarization process in cardiomyocytes. The results of an experimental model of heart failure revealed that K+ current downregulation contributed to the greater liability of the repolarization process in heart failure significantly [74]. Alterations in APD and the QT interval could induce electromechanical impairment, giving rise to channelopathies and subsequent cardiac insufficiencies [75]. It is worthy of note that the downregulation of the TALK‐2 channel is associated with decreased APD [1]. These are probable mechanisms linking k2p17.1 nucleotide variations to decreased cardiac systolic function.

Future in‐depth studies are required to elucidate the impact of the KCNK17 gene on PCCD and facilitate the detection of susceptibility to cardiomyopathies.

Conclusions

Specific nucleotide variations harbored in the KCNK17 gene are associated with numerous diseases, including cerebrovascular diseases (e.g, ischemic stroke and cerebral hemorrhage) and cardiovascular diseases (eg, ventricular and atrial arrhythmias, heart failure, and cancer‐induced cardiomyopathies). The significant association between KCNK17 and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases warrants further studies to establish this gene as a novel therapeutic target.
