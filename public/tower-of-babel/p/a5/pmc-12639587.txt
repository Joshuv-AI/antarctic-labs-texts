Development of a cost-effective LAMP method for screening and monitoring enteropathogenic Yersinia in environmental matrices
Authors: Hamadou Oumarou Hama, Idir Bitam, Michel Drancourt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClosely related bacterial pathogens, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, Yersinia pestis (the plague agent), and Yersinia enterocolitica, reside in environmental ecosystems that may be the source of infection for animals and humans. Reliable field-deployable detection methods are essential for monitoring these pathogens in natural ecosystems.MethodsA colorimetric loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assay targeting the mglB gene (shared by Y. pseudotuberculosis and Y. pestis) and the chbG gene (specific to Y. enterocolitica) was developed. Specificity was evaluated using six Yersinia isolates, and sensitivity was determined from serial dilutions of Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis cultures. The optimized LAMP assays were then applied to 28 environmental samples collected from two plague-endemic regions and one plague-free region in Algeria, and results were compared with those obtained using conventional PCR.ResultsNo Yersinia species were detected in any environmental samples by either LAMP or PCR. However, both assays successfully detected 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL of Y. pseudotuberculosis or Y. enterocolitica when samples were artificially spiked, confirming that no environmental inhibitors interfered with detection.ConclusionThe reported LAMP assays show characteristics that make them well suited for field deployment in the environmental monitoring of Yersinia species of medical and veterinary importance.Keywords: Yersinia, Environmental surveillance, Loop-mediated isothermal amplification, Pathogen detection

Introduction

A minority of Yersinia species are responsible for acute enteritis following the ingestion of contaminated foodstuffs, including rarely invasive Yersinia enterocolitica and moderately invasive Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, which is also responsible for intestinal lymphadenitis [1]. Its 3800-year-old derivative, Yersinia pestis, causes rarely recognized enteritis [2] but is nevertheless fully invasive after ingestion and mainly after transcutaneous inoculation through ectoparasite bites, and is responsible for deadly plague [3]. Accordingly, Y. pseudotuberculosis is routinely used as a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) bacterium proxy for the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) bacterium Y. pestis, as in the present study [4].

These pathogenic Yersinia species have been detected in various environmental matrices, some of which are representative of reservoirs, others are sources of infection, and yet others are simply markers of environmental contamination. This indicates the interest of tracing Yersinia in any of these situations in such environmental matrices [3,[5], [6], [7]]. In particular, Y. pseudotuberculosis has been detected in water, soil, and iceberg lettuce [7,8]. These environmental detections commonly relied on the manipulation of environmental samples in a microbiology laboratory, where Yersinia were detected by isolation in culture, and the colonies were characterized and identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS), immuno-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (immuno-LC-MS/MS), pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), lateral flow immunoassay (LFA), and molecular detection methods [5,6,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. While a few reports have suggested the potential of loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) for field detection of the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex at points of interest (POI), these studies did not demonstrate direct on-site applications [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. In contrast, LFA has already been successfully implemented in the field, with one study specifically confirming the detection of the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex, and more precisely Y. pestis, at POI [11]. Given that rapid and efficient DNA extraction is critical for molecular diagnostics, alkaline lysis represents a simple and cost-effective alternative to conventional methods [17,18], and when coupled with LAMP, it provides a rapid, sensitive, and field-adapted detection strategy.

Here, we developed an integrated protocol for one-shot field detection of environmental Y. pseudotuberculosis complex and Y. enterocolitica at the POI, relying on an original extraction and LAMP protocol. We also present the proof of concept for subsequent field deployment of this approach.

Materials and methods

Yersinia whole genome sequence database

We created a Yersinia whole genome sequence (WGS) database incorporating all Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis WGS retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (November 2023), using Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis as keyword entries. In order to enrich this database, we also whole-genome-sequenced two Y. enterocolitica isolates (Q8179 strain and Q7480 strain) and one Y. pseudotuberculosis isolate (P6349 strain) recovered from the Collection de Souches de l’Unité des Rickettsies (CSUR, WDCM 875, Marseille, France). These three isolates were cultured for 24 h on Columbia Sheep Blood Agar (COS, bioMérieux, Craponne, France) at 37 °C, and their identity was confirmed using MALDI-TOF-MS with highly significant score (≥2.13), as previously described [19]. Total DNA was extracted from identified colonies using the EZ1 Advanced XL extraction kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) in line with the reference extraction method. Alternatively, total DNA was extracted from identified colonies by using the alkaline lysis protocol. Briefly, 100 μL of inoculum (0.5 McFarland) was mixed with 100 μL of 50 mmol/L NaOH by vortexing before the addition of 100 μL of 0.1 mol/L Tris–HCl, in a 1.5 mL-Eppendorf tube. An extraction blank was used as a negative control. Sequencing was then performed using the MiSeq sequencer (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) as described elsewhere [20]. Reads were assembled using Spades software (version 3.11.1) and annotated using Prokka software (version 1.14.6). The resulting genomic sequences were compared against the complete reference genomes of Y. enterocolitica or Y. pseudotuberculosis available in the NCBI database in November 2023 using Pyani software (version 0.2.12).

Conserved genes

A Y. pseudotuberculosis and Y. enterocolitica pangenome was constructed by incorporating 26 Y. pseudotuberculosis WGS (NCBI accession number: CP033717.1, CP033707.1, CP033713.1, CP009792.1, CP130901.1, CP031780.1, CP033711.1, CP000720.1, CP009786.1, LR134373.1, CP032566.1, LT596221.1, LR134306.1, CP044064.1, CP009759.1, LR134160.1, CP033715.1, CP033709.1, CP009780.1, AP024605.1, CP010067.1, CP009757.1, CP009712.1, CP008943.1, BX936398.1, and LR134163.1) and 23 Y. enterocolitica WGS (NCBI accession number: CP016937.1, CP022337.1, CP011286.1, CP016940.1, CP009838.1, FR729477.2, LR134335.1, CP022338.1, AM286415.1, CP011118.1, HF571988.1, CP009367.1, CP002246.1, CP068146.1, CP016945.1, CP009456.1, LR134161.1, CP016944.1, LR590469.1, CP068147.1, CP016935.1, CP030980.1, and CP016931.1) available in the NCBI database in November 2023. A search for conserved genes was then performed using Prokka software (version 1.14.6) and Roary software (version 3.13.0).

Designing LAMP primers

LAMP primer sequences were designed using the NEB LAMP Primer Design Tool (version 1.4.1, available at: https://lamp.neb.com/#!/).

LAMP amplification

A LAMP assay was conducted in a final 50 μL volume, including 16 μmol/L of forward inner primer (FIB) and backward inner primer (BIP), 2 μmol/L of forward outer primer (F3) and backward outer primer (B3), 4 μmol/L of loop forward primer (LP or LF) and loop backward primer (LB), 44 μL of water resuspended in RT-LAMP colorimetric Master mix (Biotechnology Co, Wuhan, Hubei, China), and 1 μL of DNA, incorporated into a 30–45 min incubation at 65 °C. A DNA-free mix was used as a negative control, and the previously published Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis LAMP primers [13,14] were used as reference controls. The specificity of the primers was tested using four additional Yersinia strains available at the CSUR, including Yersinia bercovieri (Q3908), Y. enterocolitica subsp. palearctica (Q3790), Yersinia intermedia (Q7478), and Yersinia timonensis (Q5920). The sensitivity of the LAMP amplification of the ChbG and mglB genes was assessed using 1.5 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL to 1.5 × 10° CFU/mL serial dilutions prepared from pure cultures of Y. enterocolitica (Q7480) and Y. pseudotuberculosis (P6349) isolates, respectively; and the test was performed in duplicate. Amplification was evidenced by a color change in the reaction mixture, with yellow indicating a positive result and pink indicating a negative result. In these experiments, standard PCR was used as reference, as specified below. Accordingly, we designed outer LAMP primers F3 and B3, as well as two previously reported primer pairs targeting Y. pseudotuberculosis and Y. enterocolitica (used as controls) which were incorporated into standard PCR amplification [13,14]. PCR amplifications conducted in a 25 μL final volume including 20 μL of mix (12.5 μL AmpliTaq Gold Master Mix [Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania], 6 μL of RNase/DNase-free water and 0.75 μL of each primer at 20 μmol/L) and 5 μL of DNA (pure and or diluted at 1:50), were incorporated into one cycle at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension cycle at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were analyzed using 1.5 % agarose gel electrophoresis (135 V for 20 min) and visualized using Gel Doc™ XR+ with Image Lab™ software version 4.0 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

Applying LAMP detection to environmental matrices

In February 2024 (winter season), 28 environmental samples comprising 13 soil samples taken from land bordering saline lakes, seven Chenopodiaceae roots and the aerial parts of two of them, and six rodent droppings were collected in Chott El Hodna and Oued El Ham, where Y. pestis had previously been detected by isolation by culture [21] after reports of plague cases in that region of Algeria [22] (21 samples). Samples were also taken from a distant, negative control region in Algeria (Boughezoul) devoid of any evidence of plague (seven samples, Fig. 1). Soil and fecal samples were collected in 50 mL Falcon tubes, and plant material in bags. All samples were transported and stored at room temperature, without pretreatment prior to DNA extraction. These environmental samples were transported from Algeria to France without restriction, as at the time of the study (2023–2024), Algeria had not yet signed the Nagoya protocol. The samples were further manipulated in the BSL-3 laboratory in the Institut Hospitalo-Universitaire Méditerranée Infection, Marseille. A negative control (DNA-free mix) and a positive control (reference strains) were systematically included in each tested series of the amplification assay.

Results

Yersinia WGS-based primer design

Comparative genomic analysis of the Q8179 strain (BioProject: PRJNA1244305, BioSample: SAMN48514825), the Q7480 strain (BioProject: PRJNA1244305, BioSample: SAMN48514746), and the P6349 strain (BioProject: PRJNA1244305, NCBI accession number: JBNXQF000000000) indicated between 96 % and 100 % sequence identity with the reference genomes of Y. enterocolitica for the Q8179 and Q7480 strains, and with the reference genome of Y. pseudotuberculosis for the P6349 strain. More precisely, these sequences matched with the reference genome of Y. enterocolitica (type O:5) YE53/03 (NCBI accession number: HF571988.1), the Y. enterocolitica strain FDAARGOS_1090 chromosome (NCBI accession number: CP068147.1) and the Y. pseudotuberculosis IP 32953 (NCBI accession number: CP009712.1 and BX936398.1), respectively (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). A total of 2880 conserved genes were identified among the 23 Y. enterocolitica reference genomes and 3183 conserved genes among the 26 Y. pseudotuberculosis reference genomes. From this large set of conserved genes, we worked further with two genes which were selected based on an online blastn of the gene sequences in the NCBI database: the Y. pseudotuberculosis/Y. pestis mglB gene encoding a d-galactose-binding periplasmic protein and the Y. enterocolitica chbG gene for a chitooligosaccharide deacetylase. Primers F3, B3, FIP, BIP, LF and LB (Table 1, Fig. S1 and Fig. S2) were then designed based on the mglB gene sequence, selected for Y. pseudotuberculosis/Y. pestis (Table S1), and the chbG gene sequence, selected for Y. enterocolitica (Table S1).

LAMP amplification

When incorporated into standard PCR as in the reference amplification method, these primers yielded no amplification in the negative controls, while the Y. enterocolitica Q7480 strain and the Y. pseudotuberculosis P6349 strain DNA yielded positive amplification as expected, with the primers designed in this study (targeting the ChbG and mglB genes, respectively) and with primers retrieved from the literature (targeting the hypothetical protein and inv genes, respectively) (Fig. 4). With the exception of the Y. enterocolitica Q8179 strain, which was negative following PCR amplification of the ChbG gene, LAMP amplification revealed that all three strains were positive for the designed primers and the primers in the literature, with negative controls that remained negative (Fig. 5).

The LAMP amplification results for the Y. bercovieri, Y. intermedia and Y. timonensis strains were negative for the mglB and ChbG genes, unlike the Y. enterocolitica subsp. palearctica strain, which was positive for the ChbG gene. The detection limit of LAMP amplification of the targeted genes was 103 CFU for both strains.

LAMP detection in environmental matrices

In the presence of negative controls that consistently remained negative, seven samples collected from the negative control region were negative by PCR and LAMP amplification, as were the 21 other samples collected in the endemic area. Inoculation of 100 μL of 3 × 108 CFU of Y. pseudotuberculosis or Y. enterocolitica into 100 μL of each of these 28 environmental samples all resulted in positive detection by LAMP amplification of the mglB and ChbG genes (Fig. S3 and S4).

Discussion

We report on the proof-of-concept that an original integrated protocol for the LAMP detection of the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex in environmental matrices, warrants further field deployment in geographic areas known to harbor Y. pestis in soil [21]. The data reported in this study were validated by the negativity of negative controls included in every experiment.

Previous reports described LAMP amplification of Y. pestis, Y. pseudotuberculosis and Y. enterocolitica without application to environmental samples [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. The integrated protocol in this study reported is deemed suitable for a field application at POI to monitor the spatial and temporal dynamics of Y. enterocolitica and the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex, including Y. pestis, in the environment. We successfully designed two LAMP tests targeting a conserved gene of Y. enterocolitica (chbG) and another conserved gene of the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex (mglB). These conserved genes were selected following a bioinformatics analysis of all complete genomes available in the NCBI database, in contrast to previous limited studies which primarily targeted virulence genes for the molecular detection of Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis [23]. The results showed that the ChbG and mglB genes are conserved in all complete genomes of Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis submitted to GenBank, respectively. Additionally, the primers designed have 100 % similarity with representative strains of Y. enterocolitica for the ChbG gene and of Y. pseudotuberculosis, including Y. pestis, for the mglB gene, but no other Yersinia species and any other bacterium. Further, we applied our LAMP tests to 28 environmental samples taken from endemic and non-endemic areas in Algeria. The results for all samples tested were negative (0/28), which was consistent with the results of conventional PCR. The absence of inhibitors for our LAMP method in environmental samples was demonstrated by the positivity of these samples inoculated with Y. enterocolitica and Y. pseudotuberculosis strains. Altogether, these observations suggest the absence of any cross-reactivity with non-target species present in environmental samples and a predicted specificity for targeted Yersinia, founding the proof-of-concept. However, these observations obviously do not guarantee that un-mastered field conditions including parameters such as physical and chemical characteristics of environmental samples such as temperature, acidity, potential inhibitors would not influence the limit of detection.

The detection of the Y. pseudotuberculosis complex in environmental matrices is of interest for the chain of transmission of these pathogens. In the case of zoonotic plague, source animals may acquire Y. pestis by grazing in these environmental sources, as is the case of camelids [24]. Animals have also been source of deadly plague after human populations skinned dead animals and ate uncooked meat and liver, which led to clusters of acute enteritis and deadly plague in several Maghreb countries [[25], [26], [27]].

Compared with PCR and lateral-flow immunochromatographic assays [11], the LAMP assay we reported offers a rapid and cost-effective test. Table 2 presents the comparison in terms of estimated costs including time for realization of the test. Therefore, this LAMP test is appropriate for deployment in the field due to the simplicity and speed of DNA extraction and with no need to use a thermocycler. This LAMP method is, therefore, a powerful tool for screening and monitoring environmental matrices for yersiniosis and plague in endemic countries.
