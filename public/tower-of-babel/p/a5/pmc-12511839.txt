Liver Stiffness Measurements After Oral Antivirals Effectively Predict the Risk of HCC in Patients With Chronic Hepatitis C
Authors: Yu Rim Lee, Hyun Young Woo, Young Oh Kweon, Won Young Tak, Se Young Jang, Jung Gil Park, Min Kyu Kang, Jeong Eun Song, Byoung Kuk Jang, Changhyeong Lee, Byung Seok Kim, Jae Seok Hwang, Woo Jin Chung, Jeong Heo, Nae‐Yun Heo, Seung Ha Park, Jun Sik Yoon, Joonho Jeong, Sang Gyu Kwak, Yeowool Kang, Yang Hyun Baek, Soo Young Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) can still occur in patients with chronic hepatitis C after achieving a sustained virologic response (SVR) with direct‐acting antiviral (DAA) therapy. Therefore, we aimed to identify and validate predictors and HCC risk models using longitudinal data.MethodThis retrospective cohort study included patients who achieved SVR after DAA therapy. Model performance was assessed using Harrell's C‐index and the area under the time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).ResultsAfter excluding patients who developed HCC or died within 1 year after achieving SVR, a total of 2778 patients were finally included in this study. 154 (5.5%) patients developed HCC at a median of 3.3 years. LSM value showed significant improvement after antiviral therapy (all p < 0.05). In the multivariable analysis, LSM at baseline and 1 year after SVR were significantly associated with the occurrence of HCC (all p < 0.05). LSM at 1 year after SVR could better predict HCC risk than baseline LSM (C‐index: 0.760, 0.788 at baseline, 1 year after SVR, respectively). Low‐risk patients (LSM < 10 kPa) using LSM values at 1 year after SVR (76.4%) showed an incidence risk of 0.482 incidence rate/100 patient‐years. In patients with advanced chronic liver disease, LSM at 1 year after SVR demonstrated superior predictive performance for HCC development compared to baseline LSM and other prediction models assessed at various time points (C‐index: 0.744, 95% CI: 0.660–0.829; 3‐year follow‐up AUROC: 0.794, 95% CI: 0.754–0.831).ConclusionsLSM after achieving SVR effectively predicts HCC risk, especially in patients with advanced chronic liver disease.Keywords: hepatitis C virus, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver stiffness, risk score, sustained virological response (SVR)

Introduction

With the remarkable development of direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs), most patients with chronic hepatitis C achieve a sustained virologic response (SVR), regardless of the presence of liver fibrosis or genotype [1]. However, these patients still have a risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2, 3]. Thus, it is necessary to identify high‐risk patients who are likely to develop HCC. Current guidelines recommend HCC surveillance for all chronic hepatitis C patients with cirrhosis before antiviral treatment [3, 4, 5, 6]. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis without cirrhosis also remain at a considerable risk for HCC, with pooled incidence rates of approximately 0.5 per 100 person‐years after successful cure of HCV infection [6]. However, as liver damage ameliorates after treatment, potentially decreasing the risk of HCC [7], further tailored surveillance should be established.

Currently, fibrosis stage is commonly assessed by noninvasive methods, such as liver stiffness measurement (LSM) or reliable serum biomarkers, including aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐platelet ratio index (APRI) and fibrosis index based on 4 factors (FIB‐4) scores, rather than invasive liver biopsy. Factors reflecting liver fibrosis were found to be related to HCC development [8, 9, 10]. In addition, HCC prediction models were developed to evaluate HCC risk in patients with chronic hepatitis. However, there are limited studies on the validation of risk factors and prediction models using longitudinal data, especially on the comparison of the prognostic accuracy of these parameters and HCC risk scores in patients with chronic hepatitis C [11]. Therefore, studies on the risk factors for HCC development are urgently needed as they can help make decisions regarding surveillance recommendations according to the risk of chronic hepatitis C after achieving SVR.

In this study, we identified the factors related to HCC development in patients with chronic hepatitis C after SVR. We then validated the performance of these parameters and HCC risk scores using data obtained after treatment and determined their prognostic accuracy in predicting HCC occurrence.

Methods

Patients

From January 2015 to June 2021, patients with chronic hepatitis C from 10 community and academic hospitals in Daegu, Busan, and Ulsan, South Korea, who were treated with oral direct antiviral agents and achieved SVR were included in this retrospective cohort study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) failure to achieve SVR; (3) concomitant coinfection with HBV and/or HIV; (4) history of liver decompensation, HCC, or liver transplantation; and (5) development of HCC or death within 1 year after achieving SVR, or a follow‐up duration of less than 1 year.

This study was performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of each participating institution, including Kyungpook National University Hospital (IRB No. KNUH‐2017‐11‐011). The requirement for informed consent from the study participants was waived by the ethics committee because of the retrospective nature of this study.

Data Collection and Study Definition

We obtained data from patients' medical records, including demographic variables, hepatitis C virus (HCV) genotype, type of DAA therapy, laboratory tests, and radiologic imaging studies before DAA therapy. Laboratory tests included platelet count and liver and renal function tests. Harmful alcohol intake was defined as an alcohol intake of ≥ 30 g/day in male and ≥ 20 g/day in female populations [12]. Liver cirrhosis (LC) was defined as a liver biopsy specimen showing evidence of cirrhosis (Metavir stage F4). If a histological assessment was not available, cirrhosis was diagnosed based on laboratory findings of a platelet count of less than 100 000/μL, radiologic findings including a surface nodularity, the presence of varices, and clinical findings of complications of LC such as ascites and hepatic encephalopathy [13]. Fatty liver was evaluated using abdominal ultrasonography based on the following standard criteria: increased echogenicity of liver parenchyma relative to that of the cortex of the kidneys, unclear visualization of the intrahepatic vessels, and/or deep beam attenuation [14]. LSM was performed by a single highly trained technician at each institute using transient elastography (TE) (FibroScan, EchoSens, Paris, France) at baseline and after DAA therapy. Details of the technique and examination procedure have been reported previously [15]. Serum noninvasive fibrosis scores including APRI and FIB‐4 scores were also calculated using the previously presented formulas [16, 17]. Advanced chronic liver disease was defined as LSM ≥ 10 kPa, FIB‐4 > 3.25, or APRI ≥ 1.5 at baseline [18, 19, 20].

HCC Risk Prediction Models

We examined aMAP score [21], an etiology‐independent HCC risk score, and several commonly used risk scores that do not include virologic parameters in antiviral‐treated HBV patients: PAGE‐B [22], mPAGE‐B [1], REAL‐B [23], APA‐B [24], HCC‐RESCUE [25], AASL‐HCC [26], CAMD [27], and RWS‐HCC [28] scores. Information on the components of the risk scores and previously reported cutoffs in each model are shown in Table S1.

Study Outcomes and Follow‐Up

SVR was defined as undetectable serum HCV RNA at 12 weeks after treatment completion using Cobas AmpliPrep/Cobas TaqMan (Roche Molecular Diagnostics, Pleasanton, CA; lower limit of detection, 15 IU/mL). The index date for outcome analyses was defined as the time of SVR confirmation. Blood tests and ultrasound follow‐up were performed every 6 months after SVR, and computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed if required. During follow‐up, the occurrence of HCC was recorded. HCC was diagnosed according to the EASL guideline using pathology or imaging studies such as multiphasic CT or dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI [29]. Patients were followed up until HCC development, death, or May 2023, when the final data were updated. For patients who were lost during the follow‐up period, the last confirmed date of being alive was documented. The primary endpoint was the development of HCC in patients with chronic hepatitis C.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean (SD), median (IQR), or number (%), as appropriate. No imputation was conducted for missing data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test (or Fisher's exact test), whereas Student's t‐test (or Mann–Whitney's test) after Shapiro–Wilk normality testing was used to compare continuous variables. Paired data were compared using the paired t‐test. We used the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model to identify factors associated with HCC development while accounting for competing non‐HCC related deaths. The subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were determined. We assessed the performance of the HCC risk predictors and prediction models and compared the predictive ability of each model using the C‐index and the area under the curve receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve at 3 and 5 years after SVR, along with sensitivity and specificity. The AUROCs of the two models were compared using DeLong's test. The cumulative incidence of HCC among groups of patients with different risk scores was examined using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared using the log‐rank test. Data were censored when any of the following events occurred first: HCC development, death, loss to follow‐up, or end of follow‐up. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows Version 25.0 (Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp.), R software (Version 3.0, http://cran.r‐project.org/,install.packages (“devtools”)), and MedCalc Statistical Software (Version 20.115, Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Clinical Outcomes After SVR

From January 2015 to June 2021, a total of 3489 patients from 10 centers were treated with DAA therapy. Among them, 3357 patients (96.2%) achieved SVR. After excluding patients who met the exclusion criteria, a total of 2778 patients were finally included in this study. The baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 60.4 years, and 1271 (45.8%) patients were male. Advanced chronic liver disease was observed in 1217 (44.4%) patients. The most common genotype was Type 1 (n = 1476, 53.1%), followed by Type 2 (n = 1243, 44.7%), which accounted for the majority of patients. The mean baseline APRI score, FIB‐4 score, and LSM value were 1.1 (1.3), 3.9 (4.1), and 11.5 (9.9), respectively.

During the study period (median 3.9 years), HCC occurred in 154 (5.5%) patients at a median of 3.3 years (incident rate 1.459/100 patient‐years), and 63 patients (2.3%) died at a median of 3.4 years.

When the patients were divided into HCC (n = 154, 5.5%) and non‐HCC (n = 2624, 94.5%) groups, patients who developed HCC during follow‐up were significantly older, had higher bilirubin and international normalized ratio (INR), and had lower platelet counts and albumin levels before antiviral treatment (Table 1). In addition, patients who developed HCC had higher LSM, APRI, and FIB‐4 scores (all p < 0.05). Comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension were more common in patients with HCC than in those without HCC (p < 0.05).

Changes in Biochemical Profile and Liver Fibrosis Parameters After DAA Therapy

There were significant changes in noninvasive liver fibrosis markers and liver and kidney functions at baseline, SVR, and 1 year after SVR (Table 2). At baseline, SVR, and 1 year after SVR, the mean LSM values were 11.5, 9.1, and 8.3, respectively, and the LSM value showed significant improvement after antiviral therapy (p < 0.05 for all comparisons), with a mean decrease of 28% from baseline. This trend was maintained among patients with advanced chronic liver disease (Table S2). Total bilirubin level and INR gradually decreased from baseline to SVR and 1 year after SVR (p < 0.05). The platelet count and albumin level gradually increased from baseline to SVR and 1 year after SVR (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Independent Predictors of HCC Occurrence in Patients With Chronic Hepatitis C Achieving SVR

In the multivariable analysis, older age (SHR 1.046, 95% CI: 1.028–1.064, p < 0.001), platelet count at baseline (SHR 0.989, 95% CI: 0.986–0.993, p < 0.001), albumin level at baseline (SHR 0.370, 95% CI: 0.250–0.548, p < 0.001), platelet count at 1 year after SVR (SHR 0.989, 95% CI: 0.985–0.992, p < 0.001), alanine aminotransferase at 1 year after SVR (SHR 1.020, 95% CI: 1.007–1.033, p = 0.002), and albumin level at 1 year after SVR (SHR 0.535, 95% CI: 0.334–0.856, p = 0.009) were identified as independent predictors of HCC (Table 3). When LSM value was included in the multivariable analysis, older age (SHR 1.036, 95% CI: 1.004–1.068, p = 0.026), platelet count at baseline (SHR 0.991, 95% CI: 0.984–0.997, p = 0.005), aspartate aminotransferase at baseline (SHR 0.994, 95% CI: 0.987–1.000, p = 0.047), albumin level at baseline (SHR 0.153, 95% CI: 0.065–0.359, p < 0.001), LSM at baseline (SHR 1.022, 95% CI: 1.003–1.042, p = 0.025), platelet count at 1 year after SVR (SHR 0.990, 95% CI: 0.982–0.999, p = 0.027), and LSM at 1 year after SVR (SHR 1.060, 95% CI: 1.015–1.108, p = 0.009) were identified as independent predictors of HCC. The presence of LC and advanced liver disease was identified as a significant risk factor for HCC development in the univariable analysis. However, these variables were excluded from the multivariable analysis due to strong collinearity with other fibrosis‐related factors, including LSM, platelet count, and serum albumin levels. In patients with advanced chronic liver disease, older age, platelet count and albumin level at baseline and 1 year after SVR, as well as LSM values at both time points, were also identified as independent predictors of HCC (all p < 0.05) (Table S3).

Prognostic Accuracy of Parameters to Predict the Occurrence of HCC

We validated noninvasive fibrosis markers and previously reported HCC prediction models for chronic hepatitis. Table 4 shows the C‐index and AUROCs at 3 and 5 years after SVR of the noninvasive fibrosis markers and HCC risk scores for predicting HCC development. LSM at 1 year after SVR demonstrated better predictive performance for HCC risk compared to baseline LSM (C‐index: 0.760, 0.756, and 0.788 at baseline, at SVR, and 1 year post‐SVR, respectively), with the highest C‐index was observed at 1 year of follow‐up (Table 4 and Table S4). We additionally compared AUROC values at different time points. LSM at 1 year after SVR showed statistically significant differences compared with baseline LSM for AUROCs at both 3 and 5 years (p = 0.013 and 0.021, respectively). Low‐risk patients (LSM < 10 kPa) using LSM value at 1 year after SVR (76.4%) showed an incidence risk of 0.482 incidence rate/100 patient‐years (Table 5). The Kaplan–Meier curves for LSM at 1 year after SVR showed consistent results, with good differentiation according to the risk of HCC (Figure 1). Among the various HCC risk scores other than LSM value, such as aMAP and mPAGE‐B at baseline, also demonstrated predictive accuracy comparable to that of LSM alone and showed good discriminatory ability for HCC (Table 4).

Discussion

With the development of DAA therapy, liver‐related complications and mortality rates have greatly improved [8]. However, patients with chronic hepatitis C after SVR are still at a risk of HCC. After the use of potent antiviral agents, factors that reflect the degree of liver fibrosis, rather than virological factors, have been identified as important risk factors of HCC development [8, 11]. However, the most predictive parameters and risk scores using data obtained after treatment have not been sufficiently validated in patients with chronic hepatitis C. Therefore, identifying the most useful parameters or prediction models using longitudinal data in patients with chronic hepatitis C for risk stratification are required in the current DAA era. In the present study, we assessed the factors related to HCC occurrence in patients with chronic hepatitis C who achieved SVR and identified the most useful factors or risk scores in a large cohort of patients.

In our study, HCC occurred in 154 (5.5%) patients at a median of 3.3 years, which is similar to the incidence in other studies [8, 30, 31]. Liver function tests and noninvasive fibrosis markers including LSM showed marked improvement over time in patients with chronic hepatitis C who achieved SVR after DAA therapy. In the multivariable analysis, old age, platelet count, albumin level, and LSM value after SVR were significantly associated with the occurrence of HCC. LSM at 1 year after SVR demonstrated better predictive performance for HCC risk compared to baseline LSM, and the highest C‐index and AUROCs were observed at 1 year of follow‐up. LSM assessed at SVR did not show a significant improvement in predictive accuracy over baseline LSM, with no significant difference in C‐index and AUROC values. Due to the limited availability of LSM data at SVR in our cohort, further studies are needed to confirm the predictive value of LSM assessed at SVR. Low‐risk patients (LSM < 10 kPa) using LSM values at 1 year after SVR (76.4%) showed an incidence risk of 0.482 incidence rate/100 patient‐years. Among the various HCC risk scores other than LSM value, such as aMAP and mPAGE‐B at baseline, also showed good discriminatory ability for HCC. When only patients with advanced chronic liver disease were analyzed, who are at higher risk of HCC, LSM at 1 year after SVR was the strongest predictor of HCC occurrence among LSM and other risk models assessed at various time points. The findings of our study are consistent with previous studies identifying LSM, particularly post‐SVR LSM, as the most significant clinical predictor of HCC development [32]. When classifying the low‐risk group using a cutoff value of 8 or 10 kPa in LSM at 1 year after SVR, even patients with advanced chronic liver disease before treatment showed an HCC risk of less than 1 incidence rate/100 patient‐years. LSM after treatment can serve as a simple and promising tool for risk stratification of HCC development, particularly among patients with advanced chronic liver disease who are at high risk for HCC.

This study provides important findings. Current guidelines recommend surveillance for HCC in chronic hepatitis C patients with cirrhosis before antiviral therapy [18, 19, 20]. However, in patients with elevated transaminase levels or TE values because of necroinflammatory activity, baseline noninvasive fibrosis markers may overestimate the fibrosis stage, making it difficult to predict HCC development accurately using baseline values. Therefore, simple criteria for baseline liver fibrosis may not be appropriate for HCC surveillance. In our study, LSM was performed at baseline, at the time of SVR and 1 year after SVR; therefore, we suggested information about dynamics after treatment. LSM improved significantly over time after antiviral therapy, LSM at 1 year after SVR could predict HCC development better than baseline LSM. In patients with advanced chronic liver disease, LSM at 1 year after SVR also showed the highest predictive power among HCC prediction models. Regardless of the presence of initial advanced chronic liver disease, the risk of HCC was significantly low when follow‐up LSM after treatment was 10 kPa or less. Therefore, surveillance for HCC can be more effectively recommended using LSM, particularly after 1 year after SVR, for patients at a high risk of developing HCC. Given these findings, rather than targeting patients with cirrhosis before treatment, as suggested by current guidelines, surveillance may be performed more efficiently by using LSM after achieving SVR. Moreover, in patients with pretreatment advanced chronic liver disease who are currently recommended for HCC surveillance, follow‐up LSM may help further stratify their risk of HCC. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Various HCC prediction models easily and accurately predict the occurrence of HCC in patients with chronic hepatitis C. In this study, we validated HCC risk prediction models, which can be calculated using simple demographic data and laboratory tests, with a large number of patients in multiple centers. Several baseline HCC risk scores, including aMAP and mPAGE‐B, also exhibited comparable or superior predictive performance to LSM alone, indicating their feasibility for risk stratification in clinical settings. The aMAP score is a model for HCC prediction in chronic hepatitis of various etiologies, which can be calculated using age, male sex, platelet count, albumin, and bilirubin level [21]. Given the potential limited availability of TE in primary care centers, these models can also be applicable to patients for whom obtaining LSM is not possible.

This study has some limitations. First, some cases had incomplete records regarding some factors of metabolic syndrome, harmful alcohol intake, laboratory tests, and LSM value because of the retrospective nature of the study. Several previous studies have reported that some metabolic factors, such as the presence of diabetes, dyslipidemia, body mass index, or harmful alcohol intake can influence the occurrence of HCC after SVR [33, 34], but these factors were not related to the occurrence of HCC in our study. Further studies are required to investigate the effects of these factors on HCC development in patients with chronic hepatitis C after SVR. Second, it remains unclear whether the changes in biochemical profile and liver fibrosis parameters indicate actual liver fibrosis regression, because our data lacked liver biopsies. Third, our study was conducted with only Asian patients, and most patients had genotypes 1 and 2. Therefore, validation in other ethnic groups and genotypes is required. Fourth, patients who experienced HCC development or death within the first year after SVR, as well as those with follow‐up of less than 1 year, were excluded from the analysis. Therefore, LSM assessed at 1 year after SVR is not suitable for predicting HCC development within the first year post‐SVR, as it is measured after the potential onset of the event. Finally, statistical analyses, including the C‐index and AUROC, have limitations as they did not account for competing risks.

Conclusion

LSM and simple HCC risk models based on noninvasive parameters after treatment can effectively identify patients at a risk of developing HCC. LSM measured after antiviral therapy represents a valuable tool for stratifying HCC risk, especially in patients with advanced chronic liver disease who are at high risk of HCC.
