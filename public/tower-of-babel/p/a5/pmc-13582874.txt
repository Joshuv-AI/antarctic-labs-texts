Genomic investigations of Plocamiomonas psychrophila and Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098 reveal organellar genome complexity in Arctic Stramenopiles
Authors: Romain Gastineau, Nastasia J. Freyria, Claude Lemieux, Monique Turmel, Christian Otis, Connie Lovejoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stramenopiles (Heterokonta) comprise a diverse assemblage of mostly microbial eukaryotes, yet their organellar genomes remain poorly characterized. Here, we present complete mitochondrial and plastid genomes for two Arctic stramenopile flagellates, Plocamiomonas psychrophila and Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, representing the Pelagophyceae and Dictyochophyceae, respectively. The mitochondrial genome of Pl. psychrophila contains numerous group II introns and uses genetic code 4, a rare feature among Stramenopiles. In Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, the plastome exhibits an unusual architecture characterized by divergent inverted-repeat (IR) regions, duplicated rps16, and multiple copies of ycf66. Comparative analyses indicated that the plastid ycf60 and the mitochondrial rps1, may be more widespread in these two algal classes than currently recognized. Together, our results highlight the structural complexity and evolutionary divergence of organellar genomes in Arctic Stramenopiles and underscore the need to reassess assumptions regarding organellar gene content and genetic code usage in this group. These newly sequenced genomes provide valuable resources for understanding the evolution of cold-adapted photosynthetic lineages within the Stramenopiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58736-z.

Introduction

Mitochondria and plastids are vertically inherited organelles whose evolutionary trajectories often diverge from those of nuclear genomes. This feature is useful for separating closely related species of animals and higher plants. For example, the mitochondrial cox1 is widely used for species-level identification in animals. In higher plants and other photosynthetic taxa, the plastid rbcL encoding for RuBisCO is preferred for DNA barcoding. While chloroplast and nuclear phylogenies tend to be somewhat congruent among green plants and green algae, there is greater divergence within microbial eukaryotes that acquired plastids through secondary or tertiary endosymbiosis, consistent with large evolutionary distances1,2. Mitochondrial evolution in microbial eukaryotes remains even less understood, largely due to limited taxonomic sampling of most groups. As basic knowledge of the structure and diversity of the organelles is lacking, organellar gene phylogenies have not been attempted for most microbial eukaryotes.

Among the less studied major groups are Stramenopiles (heterokonts), which are mostly single cell eukaryotes within the Telonemia, Stramenopiles, Alveolata, Rhizaria (TSAR) supergroup3. Photosynthetic Stramenopiles acquired their plastids via endosymbiosis, with many taxa maintaining the capacity for heterotrophy2. It is currently debated as to whether plastids in Stramenopiles originated via secondary or via tertiary endosymbiosis involving cryptomonad chloroplasts2, and comparative plastid genomics could contribute to this ongoing debate.

Organellar genome features in Stramenopiles are poorly studied compared to animals and plants. As of May 2026, there were 244 mitochondrial and 206 chloroplast/plastid genomes for Stramenopiles, compared with more than 15,000 plastomes (plastid genomes) and over 18,000 mitogenomes (mitochondrial genomes) for all eukaryotes (GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/organelle/). Most of the stramenopile organellar genomes to date belong to diatoms, followed by kelps (brown algae) and oomycetes. This is largely due to the relative ease of obtaining and characterizing these three groups. Oomycetes are economically important, and kelps easily collected from coastal regions. Although diatoms are also unicellular, their silica frustules are used for taxonomic characterization by way of light microscopy or scanning electron microscopy4. The ease of identification of fossil and dead diatoms facilitates paleoecology studies and reconstruction of past climates5, hence the greater general interest in diatoms. Diatoms are also relatively easy to cultivate, at least for short periods of time, as enrichment cultures or from single cell picking and isolation. These cultures facilitate higher level genomic studies. In contrast, full genomes and organellar genomes from smaller flagellated Stramenopiles are rare. Among the 39 validly described pelagophyte species, only three have annotated organellar genomes, while another two species have non-annotated mitochondrial genomes and there is one non-annotated plastid genome available. Within the ca. 250 validly described Dictyochophyceae we located only two unvalidated, non-annotated mitogenomes (accessed February 2026).

Molecular phylogenies support a sister relationship between the two classes (Pelagophyceae and Dictyochophyceae) within Stramenopiles6,7, but global species diversity and evolutionary ecology of these two classes remain poorly resolved. Small flagellates usually need to be observed live for valid taxonomic descriptions, and cultures are required to obtain sufficient DNA for sequencing or material for Transmission electron microscopy, which is needed for characterization of cellular traits, including cell wall structures and flagellar insertion architecture. The limited number of pelagophyte and dictyochophyte cultures hinders broader understanding of their evolution and inference of ecological adaptation to specific environments. These challenges are amplified for high-latitude environments such as the Arctic Ocean. Sampling in these regions is logistically demanding, culturing Arctic microalgae requires cold-adapted equipment, and their slow growth rates make it difficult to obtain sufficient biomass for sequencing.

Despite these obstacles, five Arctic flagellate species isolated from Northern Baffin Bay have been sequenced by the Department of Energy Joint Genome Institute (DOE JGI) including the pelagophyte recently described as Plocamiomonas psychrophila Daugbjerg & Lovejoy, and Dictyochophyceae CCMP2098. Plocamiomonas psychrophila is the designated type species for the order Plocamiomonadales8. This ice-adapted flagellate9,10 has been the subject of genomic and transcriptomic studies7,10,11. In contrast, Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, remains undescribed and has received less attention due to difficulties annotating its large nuclear genome. For both species, organellar genomes have not previously been assembled or annotated, prompting the present study. Initial short-read assemblies suggested unexpectedly complex organellar architectures, motivating further investigation using long-read sequencing.

Results and discussion

Mitogenome of Plocamiomonas psychrophila

Initial short-read sequencing produced two contigs of 78,232 bp and 72,054 bp that could not be merged. Long-read sequencing resolved a complete circular mitochondrial genome of 175,983 bp. The mitochondrial genome contains 34 protein-coding genes (PCGs), three ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, and 20 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, distributed along both strands (Fig. 1). The overall GC content is 30.59%. Qualimap reports for short- and long-read mappings are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2. Genome annotation was particularly challenging due to the extensive presence of group II introns (Table 1). Translation of PCGs required the use of genetic code 4 (Mold, Protozoan, and Coelenterate Mitochondrial Code) because numerous UGA codons occur within exonic regions and would otherwise yield severely truncated proteins under genetic code 1. For example, the short, intron-less rps3, when translated using code 1 from its first reading frame, would be split into five short amino-acid fragments, only one of which would qualify as an open reading frame. The highest number of introns was found in nad7 (Table 1), with eight introns spanning a predicted gene length of 15,169 bp.

Notably, only a small fraction of group II introns retained the conserved 5′-GTGCGA-3’ motif. Of the 49 introns identified across all PCGs and rRNA genes, only three exhibited this sequence. This unusual feature was independently confirmed by MFannot, which failed to identify clear splice boundaries for three introns. Despite the abundance of introns, relatively few intronic ORFs encoding putative reverse transcriptases were detected. Only four such ORFs could be annotated with reasonable confidence, located in nad1, nad4, and cox1 (the latter harboring two ORFs). No genes encoding adenine or cytosine methyltransferases (dam/dcm), which are present in the pelagophyte Aureococcus anophagefferens, were detected (Fig. 1). No heteroplasmy was identified using VarScan (Supplementary Data 3).

Comparative analysis with the five available annotated pelagophyte mitogenomes (as of February 2026), based on the framework of Sibbald et al.12 and expanded here (Table 2), revealed both shared and unique features. The mitochondrial rps1, previously considered rare among Stramenopiles13 but characteristic of pelagophytes, was present (Table 1), whereas rps10, reported in all other sequenced pelagophytes, was absent. As in other Pelagophyceae, rrn5 encoding 5S rRNA was located between the large and small rRNA genes. However, a notable difference was observed; while other species contain a tRNA-Ala gene between rns and rnl, Pl. psychrophila instead possesses a tRNA-Gly gene between rrn5 and rns, a configuration not previously reported (Fig. 1).

In total, 20 tRNA genes were annotated (Table 1), with several deviations from known pelagophyte mitogenomes (Supplementary Table 1). The tRNA-Ala was absent, and only single copies of tRNA-Ser and tRNA-Leu were present. Three copies of tRNA-Met were detected, compared to two in the other species, along with a single tRNA-Ile bearing an unusual UAU anticodon. Of particular importance was the presence of a tRNA-Trp gene with a UCA anticodon (Supplementary Table 1), required for decoding TGA codons under genetic code 4. Codon usage analysis of all PCGs showed TGA occurring 88 times compared to 36 instances of TGG (Supplementary Table 2). For cox1, all 11 tryptophan residues were encoded exclusively using TGA.

A large non-coding region of 26,030 bp was identified between rps13 and nad9 (Fig. 1). A 181 bp repeat occurred approximately 60 times across two intergenic regions (tRNA-Gln/cox1 and nad9/rps13). In the latter region, this repeat was interspersed with additional repetitive elements (Supplementary Data 4). Secondary structure prediction of the 181 bp repeat at − 2 °C, representative of Arctic surface seawater, revealed six loop structures (Supplementary Fig. 1). Several microsatellites composed almost exclusively of A and T residues were also identified (Supplementary Data 4).

Plastome of Plocamiomonas psychrophila

Short-read sequencing initially assembled the plastome as a single contig of 88,922 bp with very high coverage (~ 16,401×) and redundant terminal regions (Fig. 2). Long-read sequencing confirmed the plastome structure (Qualimap reports provided in Supplementary Data 5 and 6 respectively). The plastome is a circular 88,751 bp molecule with a GC content of 31.94%. We found no inverted repeats (IR) regions and a single copy of the rRNA operon (three rRNA genes). A total of 98 PCGs, two non-conserved ORFs (putative orf127 and orf274), and 26 tRNA genes were present (Fig. 2). BLASTp analyses of these two ORFs are summarized in Table 3. Gene-prediction software and MFannot both indicated that orf274 is homologous to the conserved hypothetical chloroplast gene ycf60, and it was annotated accordingly. Our designated orf127 had best matches to ORFs previously found between psbH and psbX in other pelagophyte mitogenomes (Table 3)14,15. The A. anophagefferens ORF is proposed to encode a putative integral membrane protein. However, in Pl. psychrophila, orf127 lacked conserved domains, only partially overlapped with the homologous protein in A. anophagefferens and lacked the conserved YGGT motif. No heteroplasmy was detected in the plastome (VarScan report, Supplementary Data 7).

Mitogenome of Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098

The complete mitogenome of Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098 is 42,796 bp. Qualimap reports for short- and long-read data are provided as Supplementary Data 8 and 9. Translation was performed using genetic code 1. The genome encodes 34 PCGs, 23 tRNA genes, and two rRNA genes (rns and rnl), which were nearly opposite of each other on the circular genome (Fig. 3). The genome GC content was 30.75%.

Circular map of the mitochondrial genome of Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098. The inner grey circle represents GC content. Genes represented by different colored blocks on the outer and inner rings are transcribed from the positive and negative strands, respectively.

A 5,967 bp non-coding region was present between cox2 and nad6. We did not detect any microsatellites or tandem repeats. Ribosomal protein genes rps1 and rps2 were difficult to identify due to low sequence conservation relative to other Stramenopiles. To further validate genes, raw fasta files of Florenciella parvula (MK546607) and Pseudopedinella elastica (MK546609) mitogenomes were downloaded and annotated using the same pipeline used for our analysis. Both species possessed the same PCG content as our dictyochophyte. Genes included rps1, which was consistently between rpl16 / atp9, and rps2, which was between rps4 / rpl6. No heteroplasmy was detected in CCMP2098 (Supplementary Data 10).

Plastome of Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098

Short-read data failed to assemble a complete plastome, yielding fragmented contigs, which did not cover full large or small single-copy regions, nor any doubled-coverage IR signatures. Long-read sequencing resolved a circular plastome of 175,816 bp with an atypical organization (Fig. 4). Qualimap reports are provided as Supplementary Data 11 and 12. The plastome has a GC content of 30.76% and encodes 114 PCGs, including several with multiple copies. A group II intron was detected within petD, containing an ORF encoding a putative reverse transcriptase homologous to those found in the green alga Sykidion marinum (Shin Watanabe & al.) Darienko & al. (GenBank: YP_009367524) and the diatom Halamphora calidilacuna. J.G. Štěpánek & Kociolek (GenBank: YP_009686222). In total, 31 tRNA genes were annotated. Two divergent copies of rps16 were found on opposite strands, one between psaD and psbD and another within a 6,488 bp non-coding region between psbH and rps4. Although both copies were 240 bp long, they shared only 94.58% nucleotide identity, corresponding to nine amino acid substitutions.

Eight adjacent copies of ycf66 were identified. One copy (ycf66_4) was truncated due to a premature stop codon and is likely non-functional, while the remaining copies were 561 bp long, sharing 98.40–100% nucleotide identity. Nine non-conserved ORFs within an 18,457 bp region between secA and tRNA-Met aligned with one another as illustrated using a LOGO alignment (Fig. 5). BLASTp searches returned no significant matches, but InterProScan predicted N-terminal signal peptides and non-cytoplasmic domains. A putative ycf60 gene, not previously reported in dictyochophyte plastomes, was independently identified by findORF and MFannot. BLASTx searches using the putative ycf60 sequence retrieved homologs in all available dictyochophyte plastomes, corresponding to previously unannotated ORFs (Supplementary Data 13) and InterProScan revealed conserved domain architecture across these ORFs (Table 4) suggesting ycf60 is common in Dictyochophytes. Notably, an ORF from the chrysophyte Rhizochromulina marina was classified as a TIC20-II plastid protein, consistent with being a divergent ycf60 homolog. A BLASTp query of the ORF from R. marina returned two results; the putative Ycf60 from Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098 and Ycf60 in the rhodophyte Polysiphonia sertularioides (Grateloup) J.Agardh (GenBank: ARW64907).

Multigene phylogeny

Concatenated multigene phylogenetic analyses (Fig. 6) were consistent with previous studies of Pelagophyceae and Dictyochophyceae16, which diverged over 600 Mya2. Plocamiomonas psychrophila was recovered as sister to a clade comprising Ankylochrysis lutea (van der Veer) Billard, 1995 (CCMP3068), Wyeophycus julieharrissiae Wetherbee, 2022, Chromopallida australis Wetherbee, 2022 and an unidentified species labelled Veerela sp. (RCC6043). Although the name A. lutea is retained here following the GenBank entry, we note that the name is not considered validly published and CCMP3068 is currently recognized as Veerella venusta R.A. Andersen & Wetherbee17. CCMP3068 was isolated in France, as was RCC6043. Wyeophycus julieharrissiae and C. australis both originate from Australia. In the 18S rRNA gene phylogeny presented by Daugbjerg et al.8, three of these taxa (V. venusta, W. julieharrissiae and C. australis) form a sister clade to one containing Aureococcus anophagefferens and Pelagomonas calceolata R.A. Andersen & G. Saunders, 1993. Plocamiomonas psychrophila forms a distinct clade with sequences from uncultured high-latitude pelagophytes sampled in Baffin Bay, Svalbard, the Beaufort Sea, and the Gulf of Finland8.

Reevaluating the distribution of organellar genes among stramenopiles

Our findings suggest that the perceived rarity or absence of various organellar genes among Stramenopiles should be reconsidered. For instance, the plastid ycf60 has previously been reported primarily in Phaeophyceae19,20, Eustigmatophyceae21–23, and Olisthodiscophyceae24. Interestingly, kleptoplastidic sequences from the recently reclassified centrohelid genus Meringosphaera25 also contains this putative ycf60, with the enslaved chloroplast originating from a dictyochophyte26. These observations, combined with our results and a re-examination of available references, indicate that ycf60 is more widespread among Pelagophyceae and Dictyochophyceae than previously recognized. Although the length differences of ycf60 in dictyochophytes may seem surprising, similar or even greater variability has been documented for other Ycf genes, such as ycf1 in Chlorophyta27–31. A similar re-evaluation applies to the distribution of the mitochondrial gene rps1, which appears consistently in sequenced Dictyochophyceae and Pelagophyceae.

Code 4 and intron-rich mitogenomes

To our knowledge, this study presents the first report of a genetic code 4 mitogenome in Pelagophyceae. Code 4 mitogenomes have been identified among other Stramenopiles such as in the family Cafeteriaceae32,33 and occasionally in diatoms that more usually have code 1 mitogenomes. The diatom exceptions suggest variability in code 4 usage. All sequenced Thalassiosira spp. use code 4, including the model species Thalassiosira pseudonana34–36. The Thalassiosira spp. possess a gene encoding tRNA-Trp(UCA) and show widespread use of the TGA codon across PCGs. In contrast, Amicula micronesica, whose mitogenome is highly intron-rich (57 introns) contains only four PCGs with TGA codons37. Notably, no tRNA-Trp(UCA) was detected in the Amicula mitogenome.

A preliminary annotation of Veerella sp. RCC6043 was done for comparison using the same protocol as for Pl. psychrophila and we verified the use of code 1 (Supplementary Data 15). There were similarities between the two pelagophytes, such as a large size (133,198 bp) and the presence of 15 introns distributed among four PCGs (cob, cox1, cox3, nad5) and in rnl. However, differences were also evident, as RCC6043 does not have tRNA-Trp(UCA) but contains instead a single tRNA-Trp(UGG). This last result suggests that the adoption of code 4 in Pl. psychrophila is specific and could reflect the relative isolation of the genus that is only known from the Arctic Ocean7.

Divergent copies of the inverted repeat in plastomes

Plastomes within Dictyochophyceae exhibit a wide range of modifications to their IR regions38. Some possess canonical IRs, others display direct repeats on the same strand, and some lack repeats entirely, resulting in a single rRNA copy, a feature also observed in all available plastomes of Pelagophyceae. By way of contrast, massive IR expansions in diatoms have been documented in the genus Climaconeis, with the expansion occurring at the expense of the small single copy gene region, resulting in a segment devoid of genes39.

Our study found the presence of varied IR copies. While complete IR loss has been known in land plant plastomes since the early 1990s40, divergence between IR copies is less commonly reported. Cases have been described in Ulvophyceae41 and Haptophyta42,43, where alternative IRs led to the loss of tRNAs typically found between rrs and rrl. In contrast, Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098 retains a gene for tRNA-Thr in this region, with a fully conserved cloverleaf structure. The failure to recover different isoforms of the plastome suggests that recombination might not be possible, as suggested for Pseudoneochloris marina Shin Watanabe & al.41.

In dictyochophytes, rps16 is typically located between psbD and psaD (seen in Rhizochromulina marina, MK561360; Florenciella parvula, MK518352; Pseudopedinella elastica, MK518353), or between psbH and psaD (seen in Octactis speculum, MK561359) and the duplication of rps16 in the plastome of CCMP2098 is anomalous. While gene duplication in stramenopile plastomes is rare, one known case involves acpP in diatoms44. The two rps16 copies in CCMP2098 are more similar to each other than the diatom acpP duplicates, suggesting a more recent duplication event in the Dictyochophyte.

Materials and methods

DNA sequencing

For Pl. psychrophila, short-read sequence data generated by Department of Energy Joint Genome Institute (DOE JGI) were retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject PRJNA210205 (accession SRX875206). To complement these data, additional sequencing was performed on cultures grown as described in Freyria et al.11. The harvested biomass was collected on 0.22 μm pore-size Sterivex filter units (Millipore) and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction and library preparation at Institute de biologie integrative et des systèmes (IBIS) Université Laval, Québec, Canada). DNA was extracted from 30 mg of frozen biomass transferred to a pre-chilled plastic tube containing magnetic beads. Samples were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a Retsch MM 400 grinder (Verder Scientific, Haan, Germany). Cell lysis was performed by adding 1.0 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 2.0% SDS, and 20 mg ml⁻¹ proteinase K) followed by incubation at 65 °C for 30 min. An equal volume of CTAB buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1.4 M NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 2.0% CTAB, 1.0% polyvinylpyrrolidone 40,000) was then added, and the mixture was incubated at 65 °C for an additional 60 min. RNA was digested by adding 5 µl of RNase A (100 mg ml⁻¹) and incubating at room temperature for 20 min.

DNA was extracted twice with an equal volume of chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1) and precipitated with two volumes of cold absolute ethanol. The dried DNA pellet was resuspended in 100 µl of TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA). DNA quality and integrity were assessed using a Femto Pulse System with the Genomic DNA 165-kb kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Removal of short DNA fragments was performed on 3 µg of DNA using the Short Read Eliminator XS kit (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA).

A long-read sequencing library was prepared from 2 µg of size-selected DNA using the LSK-114 kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Littlemore, UK). A total of 700 ng of the resulting library was loaded onto an R10 flow cell and sequenced on a PromethION 2 Solo device (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Littlemore, UK) for 72 h, yielding 4,638,946 reads corresponding to 22.5 Gb of sequence data.

For Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, two datasets generated by the DOE JGI were downloaded from the SRA: SRX7118918 (PacBio HiFi long reads) and SRX7118919 (Illumina NovaSeq 6000 short reads)45.

Assembly, data mining, and annotation

Illumina reads were quality-trimmed using fastp v0.23.446, with a minimum retained read length of 125 bp. De novo assemblies were generated with SPAdes v4.047 with a k-mer size of 125. For Pl. psychrophila, resulting contigs were screened using standalone BLASTn queries48 against organellar reference sequences from Aureoumbra lagunensis (GenBank MW438350 and GQ231542)12,14. For Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, organellar references from Pseudopedinella elastica (GenBank MK518353 and MK546609)38 were used. Long reads were filtered with Filtlong v0.2.1 (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong), retaining reads ≥ 20,000 bp. Filtered reads were assembled using Flye v2.9.549 with the –meta option and a minimum overlap of 5,000 bp. Assemblies for Pl. psychrophila were generated using the –nano-raw option, whereas the –pacbio-hifi option was used for Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098. Assemblies were polished through three iterative rounds of Pilon v1.2450 using the corresponding Illumina reads.

Organellar genomes were annotated following the protocol of Prasetiya et al.51, using the findORF script52. ORFs not automatically identified as conserved PCGs were queried using BLASTp against the NCBI database (accessed April 2025). When required, additional functional annotation was performed using InterProScan53, with default parameters. Ribosomal RNA genes were identified by alignment with reference sequences, except for rrn5, which was detected using MFannot54. Transfer RNA genes were predicted with the standalone version of ARWEN v1.2.355, with appropriate genetic codes: -gcbact (code 11) for plastomes and -gcprot (code 4) for the Pl. psychrophila mitogenome.

Short and long reads were mapped to assembled genomes using BWA v0.7.17 and LRA v1.3.7.156,57, respectively, with coverage visualization performed using Qualimap v2.358. Potential heteroplasmy was assessed using VarScan v2.4.359. To test for the existence of alternative haplotypes in the plastome of Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098, the Cp-hap pipeline was run twice60, with each divergent inverted-repeat region as a reference. Organellar genome maps were generated with OGDRAW61. Microsatellites and tandem repeats were identified using the standard tools described previously62. LOGO plots were generated with WebLogo63 from alignments produced using MAFFT v7.50564. Tandem repeats were detected using the Tandem Repeats Finder portal65.

Multigene phylogenetic analysis of 56 pelagophytes and dictyochophytes was carried out based on six-genes, comprising 18S rRNA, rbcL, psaA, psaB, psbA, and psbC as in Wetherbee et al.66. The 56 taxa included the five plastid genes and the 18S rRNA gene sequences for Dictyochophyceae sp. CCMP2098 and Pl. psychrophile (CCMP 2097). The 18S rRNA gene sequences for our two species were retrieved from GenBank (EU247836 and EU247837). Individual genes were aligned with MAFFT v7.50564, then trimmed using trimAl v1.4.rev15 with the -automated1 option67. The best-fitting evolutionary models were selected using ModelTest-NG v0.1.768 on the trimmed alignments before concatenation with Phyutility 2.7.169 prior to ML analysis using IQ-TREE v2.4.070 with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and a dataset partitioned according to the results returned by ModelTest-NG v0.1.7. The concatenated alignment and partition files are provided as Supplementary Data 16 and 17.
