Aerobic Exercise Plus Hypocaloric Diet on Serum Leptin Concentrations in Adults With Primary Hypertension and Overweight/Obesity: Results From the EXERDIET‐HTA Trial
Authors: Virginia A. Aparicio, Pablo Corres, Ilargi Gorostegi‐Anduaga, Javier Margareto, Sara Maldonado‐Martín
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHyperleptinaemia is an emerging mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of obesity‐related primary hypertension (HTN). This substudy from a large clinical trial aimed to determine differences in [leptin] in physically inactive adults (n = 69, 46.4% women) with HTN and living with overweight or obesity following a 16‐week supervised aerobic exercise training (EX group, 2 days/week) intervention with an attention control (AC, only physical advice) group, both combined with a hypocaloric diet; to analyse whether the changes observed after the intervention were maintained after 6 months (6M); and to explore differences and potential mechanisms affected by sex. Cardiorespiratory fitness and body composition evaluation, and biochemical measurements were conducted. After the intervention [leptin] decreased in both groups (EX, 36%, p = 0.003; AC, 23%, p = 0.06), returning to baseline concentrations after 6M of follow‐up in both groups. Changes over time were similar in both sexes, with no significant time × group interaction (p > 0.05). For all participants, body mass index was the only factor independently associated with [leptin] (β = 0.339, B = 2.11, SE = 0.53), explaining 12% of the variability (p < 0.001). Among women, waist‐to‐hip ratio (12%, β = 0.365, B = 135.8, SE = 59.4) and fat‐free mass (11%, β = −0.443, B = −2.33, SE = −2.27) additionally and independently explained leptin variability (p < 0.05). A hypocaloric diet combined with supervised exercise appears to be an effective strategy for regulating [leptin] and improving metabolic health in individuals with HTN and overweight or obesity. The influence of fat distribution on leptin dynamics in women calls for research to consider sexual dimorphism.

Introduction

Obesity represents a significant global health challenge, with its prevalence reaching epidemic levels in recent decades [1]. Characterised by an excessive accumulation of adipose tissue, obesity is a substantial risk factor for numerous chronic diseases, including primary hypertension (HTN) and cardiovascular disease [2, 3]. The relationship between obesity and HTN underscores the importance of effective interventions targeting body mass management and blood pressure control [4].

Leptin, a 16‐kDa adipocyte‐derived hormone whose circulating concentrations are proportional to fat mass, is pivotal in regulating energy balance (i.e., a negative regulator of energy homeostasis), appetite, metabolism and total body mass [5, 6]. Hyperleptinaemia is an emerging mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of obesity‐related HTN. It is related to the development of ‘selective’ leptin resistance, which is associated with attenuated anorexic and metabolic effects as well as mitochondrial dysfunction [7].

Given its central role in metabolic regulation, leptin represents a promising target for interventions aimed at mitigating the cardiometabolic consequences of obesity [8]. It is important to note that although both men and women produce leptin, their secretion patterns and responses to physiological stimuli differ [9]. Overall, women exhibit higher circulating leptin concentrations than men because of differences in adipose tissue distribution and hormonal influences [10]. Therefore, understanding these sex‐related differences in leptin regulation is crucial for tailoring personalised approaches to managing obesity and reducing cardiovascular risk [10, 11].

Physical inactivity, along with poor dietary habits, is a leading cause of the obesity epidemic [12]. Conversely, regular physical activity is crucial in preventing and managing obesity‐related complications because it not only aids total body mass loss but also improves cardiovascular health, insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism and blood pressure regulation [13, 14, 15, 16]. Therefore, in pursuing effective interventions to address these interconnected health issues, supervised aerobic exercise training emerges as a promising approach, especially when combined with dietary interventions [13, 17]. However, understanding the precise impact of such interventions on leptin concentrations among individuals with overweight and obesity, and diagnosis of HTN remains an area of active research. Similarly, it would be interesting to elucidate what occurs after the interventions have ended and supervision is no longer present. Moreover, given the established sexual dimorphism in adipose tissue distribution and metabolic regulation [10], understanding sex disparities in leptin dynamics may inform personalised approaches to obesity and HTN management.

Consequently, the present study had three aims: (1) to determine differences in leptin concentrations in physically inactive adults with HTN and overweight or obesity following a 16‐week supervised aerobic exercise training intervention (EX group) compared with an attention control (AC) group, both combined with a hypocaloric diet; (2) to analyse whether the changes observed during supervision were maintained after 6 months without supervision; and (3) to determine sex‐related differences and potential mechanisms affected by sex.

Methods

Study Design

The present paper was a sub‐study of the EXERDIET‐HTA study, a randomised controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT02283047) comparing the baseline pre‐intervention status (PRE) with the immediate (POST, after the 16‐week intervention period) and 6‐month (6M) follow‐up effects of different 16‐week supervised aerobic exercise training programmes (performed 2 days per week) combined with dietary intervention in physically inactive adults (18–69 years of age) with HTN and overweight or obesity. The study design, protocols and informed consent for the EXERDIET‐HTA study were approved by the Clinical Investigation of Araba University Hospital (2015‐030) and the Ethics Committee of the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU, CEISH/279/2014). Further details regarding the design, selection criteria and procedures of the EXERDIET‐HTA study have been described in previous publications [15, 16, 18].

Participants

This sub‐study was conducted in a sub‐sample of 69 participants from the EXERDIET‐HTA study, who had measurable leptin concentrations. Of these 69 participants, 47 completed the intervention and provided valid assessment data (Figure 1). The reasons for people withdrawing at POST and 6M were related to difficulty integrating exercise sessions into daily routines, acute illness or medical complications, changes in personal circumstances and/or loss of commitment to the study in the AC group. Detailed information regarding the aims, inclusion and exclusion criteria and methodology of the EXERDIET‐HTA study can be found in the published protocol [18]. In brief, all participants were diagnosed with overweight or obesity, were physically inactive, and had stage 1 or 2 HTN.

Measurements

Trained investigators and specialists conducted all measurements at PRE, POST and 6M. All the details have been previously published [18]. In brief, the assessment included anthropometry and body composition measurements. Thus, body mass index (BMI), waist and hip circumferences were measured, and the waist‐to‐hip ratio was defined as the waist circumference divided by the hip circumference, both in centimetres. Estimation of fat‐free mass (FFM) and fat mass (FM) was performed using bioelectrical impedance (Tanita, BF 350, Arlington Heights, IL, USA) with standardised recommendations (i.e., avoiding fluid and food intake for at least 3 h before, no alcohol for 48 h, and no caffeine for 12 h) [19]. Further 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring was conducted using an oscillometric device (Welch Allyn 6100, New York City, NY, USA). Objective peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) to evaluate cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed through a cardiopulmonary (Ergo CardMedi‐soft S.S, Belgium Ref. USM001 V1.0) exercise test on an electronically braked cycle ergometer (Lode Excalibur, Groningen, The Netherlands).

Fasting blood samples (12.5 mL) were collected and serum separated by centrifugation and stored at −80°C until analysis. The Human Leptin ELISA Kit was used to determine insulin and leptin levels, an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay designed to measure the amount of the target bound to a matched antibody pair. A target‐specific antibody was pre‐coated in the wells of the supplied microplate. Samples, standards, or controls were then added to these wells and bound to the immobilised (capture) antibody. The sandwich was formed by the addition of the second (detector) antibody. A substrate solution was then added, which reacted with the enzyme‐antibody‐target complex to produce a measurable signal. The intensity of this signal was directly proportional to the concentration of the target present in the original sample (serum). The assay's analytical sensitivity was 3.5 pg/mL. Total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides (TG) and glucose were measured using enzymatic colourimetric methods on an automated analyser (Roche cobas series). Insulin and leptin concentrations were determined using commercially available immunoassay kits (Human Leptin ELISA kit, Catalogue #KAC2281; Invitrogen, ThermoFisher). C‐reactive protein (CRP) was measured using a high‐sensitivity immunoturbidimetric assay (CardioPhase hsCRP on a Siemens automated clinical chemistry analyser). According to the manufacturers, the intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variation were < 3.6% and < 4.6% for leptin.

Intervention and 6M Post‐Intervention Follow‐Up

Following baseline measurements, participants were randomly assigned to one of the intervention groups and stratified by sex, systolic blood pressure (SBP), BMI and age using a time‐blocked computerised randomisation program. All participants followed a hypocaloric Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet [20], were weighed every 2 weeks, and received encouragement and nutritional counselling to aid compliance. The study included four intervention groups: the AC group, which received only physical activity advice, and three EX groups: (1) high‐volume, moderate‐intensity continuous aerobic training (45 min), (2) high‐volume, high‐intensity aerobic interval training (45 min of total volume, with more than 10 min at high intensity) and (3) low‐volume, high‐intensity aerobic interval training (20 min of total volume with less than 10 min at high intensity). Participants in the EX groups underwent supervised training with exercise specialists for 16 weeks, consisting of two non‐consecutive training sessions per week. The AC group was advised to follow the World Health Organisation's physical activity recommendations. All details of the exercise protocols for each group, including procedures and dietary intervention, have been previously published [15, 16, 18].

In a preliminary analysis, differences in leptin concentrations across time (PRE, POST and 6M) between the EX groups were examined using a repeated‐measures analysis of covariance, including the time × group interaction and adjusting for sex, age and BMI. No significant interaction effects were observed (p > 0.05), indicating similar leptin response patterns across EX groups over time. These findings, reflected in Table S1, support the decision to pool the three EX groups into a single group for subsequent comparative analyses with the AC group.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Independent t‐tests were used to compare men and women at baseline.

Linear regression analysis was employed to assess the association of different components of body composition, energy expenditure (metabolic equivalents [METs]) and cardiometabolic markers (independent variables in separate models) with the leptin concentration (dependent variable). The model was controlled for sex because age was not significantly associated with the leptin concentration. Variables significantly associated with leptin were subsequently used to identify modifiable factors independently associated with leptin through forward stepwise regression analysis. Leptin was included as the dependent variable in the forward stepwise regression analysis. In step one, the potential confounder (sex) was included and kept fixed in the model. In step two, variables previously identified as significantly associated with leptin were simultaneously introduced into the model using a stepwise procedure. This approach introduced the studied components one at a time (when p < 0.05) based on the strength of their association with the outcome. The model was reassessed after each addition, and variables were excluded if p > 0.10. To further explore sex‐specific differences in the independent association of these markers with leptin concentrations, the analyses were repeated after stratifying by sex (i.e., separate analyses for men and women).

A repeated‐measures analysis of covariance was used to assess changes in the variables over time. In addition, a two‐way repeated‐measures analysis of covariance was performed to examine time × group interaction effects. Analyses of total body mass, BMI, waist circumference and FM were adjusted for sex and age, whereas all other variables were adjusted for sex, age and BMI. Finally, pre‐post differences in BMI, FM and plasma leptin concentrations were calculated to assess Spearman's rank‐order correlations between changes in BMI and FM and changes in plasma leptin. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05.

After the intervention (POST), both the AC and EX groups showed decreases (p < 0.05) in total body mass, BMI, waist circumference, FM percentage, diastolic blood pressure and TC. Additionally, the EX group showed decreases (p < 0.05) in SBP, leptin, LDL‐C, TG and CRP. From POST to 6M, both groups experienced increases (p < 0.05) in total body mass, BMI, waist circumference, FM percentage, leptin, TC and LDL‐C. Although the EX group demonstrated changes in more variables than the AC group, there was no significant time × group interaction for any outcome (Table S3).

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to determine differences in leptin concentrations in physically inactive individuals with HTN and overweight/obesity following a 16‐week supervised aerobic exercise training intervention with an AC group, both combined with a hypocaloric diet. Additionally, the study aimed to determine whether any observed changes were sustained 6 months after supervision ended and to explore potential differences between sexes. Finally, we examined the direct and independent associations of body composition, energy expenditure (METs) and cardiometabolic markers with leptin, also considering potential sex differences. Briefly, our findings suggest that supervised aerobic exercise combined with dietary intervention (EX group) led to a greater reduction in leptin concentrations than in the counselling group (AC) on a hypocaloric diet, irrespective of sex. As expected, women exhibited higher leptin concentrations than men at all time points.

Moreover, changes in leptin concentrations over time were similar in men and women, with no significant time × group interaction. However, BMI was the only factor independently associated with leptin concentrations in men, explaining 15% of its variability. By contrast, for women, BMI accounted for 24% of the variability, while waist‐to‐hip ratio and FFM additionally and independently explained 12% and 11%, respectively, of the leptin concentration variability.

Our group previously reported that this combined supervised aerobic exercise and hypocaloric diet intervention improved body composition, glucose and lipid profile [16, 21]. Adding to these results, in the present sample of participants, the supervised aerobic exercise training intervention reduced leptin concentrations by 36% (p = 0.003) from PRE to POST intervention, whereas in the AC group (i.e., hypocaloric diet and physical activity counselling), leptin concentrations decreased by 23% (p = 0.06). This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating the beneficial effects of aerobic exercise on leptin regulation in individuals with obesity [5, 22, 23, 24]. Recognising that exercise and diet are two essential interventions for total body mass control, some studies have compared the influence of diet alone, exercise alone, or their combination (i.e., diet + exercise) on leptin regulation in people with overweight and obesity [25]. This study suggested that exercise alone was less effective than diet or diet + exercise in decreasing leptin, indicating that diet is critical in beneficially altering leptin concentrations [25]. Similarly, the present study found greater reductions in the diet + EX group. The different exercise protocols reviewed in these studies (moderate‐intensity and continuous aerobic exercise, combined exercise, soccer playing, resistance exercise, yoga, or a shorter HIIT program) may explain the lack of greater improvements in their combined diet and exercise groups. Another review concluded that the minimum weekly exercise required to significantly reduce plasma leptin was 180 min of moderate‐intensity exercise or 120 min of high‐intensity exercise [22] and most of the studies in the aforementioned review did not meet these standards [25]. Additionally, in the present study, the AC group was advised to engage in at least 30 min of moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise 5–7 days per week, combined with dynamic resistance exercises, which may also have contributed to leptin reductions. Notably, the present study only included aerobic exercise protocols and did not incorporate resistance training. However, systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have failed to find a significant effect of combined exercise training on leptin reductions [26]. Future studies should assess the impact of incorporating resistance exercises within the training protocol. Indeed, in a study conducted on men with overweight or obesity, the authors compared aerobic and resistance training, both combined with a ketogenic diet and found that although both interventions had favourable effects on body mass loss and body fat reduction, resistance training had a more pronounced effect on maintaining lean body mass [27].

Two potential mechanisms that could explain exercise‐induced reductions in leptin are as follows. The first is improvements in body composition (i.e., decreased FM percentage and increased muscle mass). Leptin is primarily synthesised by adipose tissue and serves as a critical signalling molecule in regulating energy balance and body mass [5], as suggested by the observed decreases in leptin concentrations during the supervised aerobic exercise training intervention. The second mechanism is enhanced leptin sensitivity, mediated by favourable metabolic adaptations associated with regular aerobic exercise [22]. Notably, during the 6M follow‐up period without supervision, both the EX and AC groups experienced increases in leptin concentrations, indicating a potential reversal of the initial improvements observed post‐intervention. Further, in the present study, the significant association between changes in FM and changes in leptin concentrations from post‐intervention to 6M follow‐up and the increase in leptin resistance (i.e., leptin/FM % ratio) might indicate greater leptin resistance (Table S3). These findings support the notion that, although changes in FM partly mediate changes in leptin, the supervised exercise intervention induced additional regulatory effects on leptin that were not fully maintained after 6M without supervision, which could have been mediated through improvements in FFM. Hence, when comparing values from baseline to 6M, only the EX group maintained reduced FM, concomitant with reduced SBP, TG and CRP and increased VO2peak, indicating an apparent metabolic and anti‐inflammatory effect coordinated by the exercise and the muscle mass itself. In this sense, a recent meta‐analytic compendium has shown that aerobic exercise training is the most effective intervention for metabolic and anti‐inflammatory adaptations [28], confirming the close and indispensable inverse relationship between the CRF (via mitochondrial biogenesis) and inflammatory processes [29]. This finding highlights the importance of long‐term adherence to exercise and dietary interventions for sustaining metabolic benefits and preventing body mass regain in individuals with HTN and obesity.

Due to the influence of sexual dimorphism in adipose tissue distribution and metabolic regulation [10], understanding sex disparities in leptin dynamics (i.e., higher concentrations in women compared to men) is of clinical interest. As expected, BMI was strongly and independently associated with leptin concentrations in our sample population. However, after stratifying the stepwise regression by sex, waist‐to‐hip ratio and FFM were independently associated with leptin concentrations only in women. This finding could be partially explained by the greater importance of fat distribution or enhanced leptin sensitivity induced by muscle mass (via myokines) in women [30, 31]. We found a baseline inverse association (β = −0.279, p = 0.030) between CRP and leptin concentrations, particularly among women (r = 0.404, β = 0.353, p = 0.027; data not shown), further suggesting that myokines/exerkines may play a relevant systemic anti‐inflammatory role [32]. Notably, hypothalamic inflammation has been linked to diet‐induced obesity, impairing local leptin signalling and contributing to dysfunction in energy balance regulation and subsequent body mass gain [33]. Understanding these sex‐specific differences is crucial for designing personalised approaches to managing obesity and HTN and for reducing cardiovascular risk based on sex [10, 11]. For instance, leptin concentrations have been associated with HOMA‐IR only in men [34], whereas significant leptin/adiponectin ratio correlations with HDL‐C and CRP have been observed exclusively in women [24]. Nevertheless, the effects of the supervised aerobic exercise training intervention on leptin regulation were consistent across sexes, underscoring the efficacy of aerobic exercise in modulating leptin concentrations irrespective of sex.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations that warrant highlighting. Firstly, within the EX group, three different training protocols were implemented, although no significant differences in leptin concentrations over time were found. Secondly, information on food intake and appetite, which could have provided a better context for interpreting the present results, was lacking. However, a recent study concluded that 12‐week diet and exercise interventions were equally effective in maintaining body mass loss in women, with no differences in leptin or other measures of appetite regulation and food intake [23]. Furthermore, body mass loss has been associated with changes in concentrations of long‐term adiposity‐related hormones toward healthier levels but not with orexigenic changes in most short‐term appetite signals [35]. Thirdly, we did not record the menopausal status or menstrual cycle phase of our female participants, despite higher leptin concentrations being observed in the luteal than follicular phase [36] and in eumenorrheic than postmenopausal women [9, 11]. Fourthly, the lack of physical activity data for the AC group after counselling makes it challenging to assess the influence of this variable. Fifthly, the analysis was conducted using a completer‐only approach because some participants did not attend the post‐intervention assessment, resulting in missing follow‐up data. Consequently, an intention‐to‐treat analysis was not performed. This approach may introduce attrition bias, as participants who completed the intervention may differ from those who withdrew. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as the findings may overestimate the intervention's true effect. Finally, the cross‐sectional design precludes the determination of causality regarding factors independently associated with leptin concentrations. Despite these limitations, this study also has strengths worth highlighting. It comprehensively examines several potentially modifiable factors inconsistently associated with leptin concentrations in this population. Additionally, all analyses and associations were stratified by sex, offering valuable insights into sex‐specific differences.

Conclusion

A hypocaloric diet combined with supervised aerobic exercise training appears to be an effective strategy for regulating leptin and improving metabolic health in individuals with HTN and overweight or obesity. However, sustained lifestyle modifications are essential to maintain these long‐term benefits. The influence of FM distribution on leptin dynamics in women warrants further research to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving these changes and to develop personalised interventions tailored to individual needs, considering the influence of sexual dimorphism.
