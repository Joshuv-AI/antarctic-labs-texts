Breastfeeding self-efficacy, milk output, and influencing factors in mothers of preterm infants: a prospective cohort study in the NICU
Authors: Yanan Liu, Haixia Shan, Yan Chen, Yuan Zhuang, Jingxin Zhou, Liwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding self-efficacy (BSE) is a critical predictor of lactation success, yet its role among mothers of preterm infants remains understudied, especially during the early postpartum period. This study aimed to (1) examine the association between BSE, feeding choices, and milk output in preterm mothers within 3–14 days postpartum, and (2) identify factors influencing BSE.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted with 200 mothers of preterm infants in a Chinese NICU. Data on demographics, social support, breastfeeding behaviors, and BSE (measured via the BSES-SF scale) were collected longitudinally at 3, 7, and 14 days postpartum. Multivariate logistic and linear regression analyses were used.FindingsHigher BSE correlated with increased milk output (6.28–6.85 mL per 1-point BSE increase) and preference for exclusive breastfeeding. Key positive influencers included social support (OR: 0.2–0.3), prenatal breastfeeding plans (OR: 0.24), and reliable lactation knowledge (OR: 0.26). Negative factors were pregnancy complications (OR: 2.83) and breastfeeding difficulties (OR: 2.73).ConclusionEarly BSE dynamically shapes lactation outcomes, yet Chinese preterm mothers exhibited suboptimal BSE scores (50/70), signaling urgent needs for targeted interventions. Social support and prenatal education emerged as modifiable drivers, aligning with global evidence but highlighting regional gaps in clinical practice. Enhancing BSE through structured support and education during the first two weeks postpartum may improve breastfeeding rates in preterm populations.

Introduction and background

A preterm infant is defined as a newborn whose gestational age at birth is less than 37 weeks, a condition that poses a significant global health challenge. In 2020, an estimated 13.4 million premature babies were born worldwide, with a global preterm prevalence of 9.9%. China is among the regions with the highest preterm birth rates [1]. Since the implementation of the universal two-child policy in July 2016, the preterm birth rate in China has been rising, increasing from 5.9% in 2012 to 6.4% in 2018 [2]. Preterm birth is a global burden considered to be one of the major risk factors for mortality in children aged under 5 years old [3]. The most important complications of prematurity include long-term neurodevelopmental issues, such as cerebral palsy (CP), motor and cognitive impairment, visual and auditory deficits, and behavioral problems [4]. These infants often require care in a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), which provides professional care after birth including respiratory support, feeding, infection control and neurodevelopmental support.

Breastfeeding is the most economical, scientific, and effective feeding pattern [5]. Its benefits are well-established, with substantial evidence showing its critical role in promoting child survival, health, and growth [6]. Human milk supplies essential macronutrients and micronutrients, along with numerous bioactive components, such as immunoglobulins, cytokines, growth factors, hormones. In addition, its microbiome is abundant in probiotic bacteria, further contributing to infant health. Human milk is especially critical for premature infants, as it has been associated with reduced risks of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), late-onset sepsis, chronic lung disease, retinopathy of prematurity, and neurodevelopmental impairment [7–10]. Recent studies also shown that some components of breast milk, such as miRNA and lactoferrin, have a positive effect on the neurodevelopment of newborns, particularly premature infants [11, 12]. These facts underscore the importance of breastfeeding and the need for medical staff and parents to prioritize it.

To promote child health, the Chinese government aims for more than 50% of infants aged 0–6 months to be exclusively breastfed, as outlined in the China Children’s Development Program (2021–2030) [13]. However, among preterm infants in China, the exclusive breastfeeding rate is only 41% during hospitalization and drops to 19% after discharge, with a median duration of four months [14–16], far below the government’s target. This decline may result from maternal and family behaviors (such as formula feeding), the level of family support after discharge, maternal factors such as pregnancy planning and psychological state [15]. Against this backdrop, short-term follow-up studies are particularly important for identifying factors influencing breastfeeding after discharge, thereby providing timely evidence to support mothers of preterm infants.

Different from healthy babies, premature infants living in NICU face many difficulties and challenges with breastfeeding, including physical and emotional trauma from premature birth, mother-infant separation, insufficient milk production, delayed lactation, breast milk storage, delivery, and cold chain management [17, 18]. The lack of maternal breast milk is one of the most important factors [19], which can impact a mother’s confidence in and the duration of breastfeeding. Actually in NICU, the actual volume of maternal milk is equally critical, as preterm infants often rely on expressed breast milk. Inadequate supply may not only restrict infants’ nutritional intake and growth but also further undermine maternal confidence and shorten breastfeeding continuation.

Breastfeeding self-efficacy (BSE) theory is a social cognitive theory that has been widely applied in breastfeeding research and practice. It refers to a mother’s perceived ability to successfully breastfeed the infant, emphasizing the mother’s confidence in breastfeeding [20, 21]. BSE theory has been widely used to build strategies for promoting breastfeeding among full-term mothers, with higher breastfeeding self-efficacy being associated with longer duration of both any and exclusive breastfeeding [22, 23]. However, the effect of breastfeeding self-efficacy on breastfeeding and lactation in mothers of preterm infants has not been fully clarified. A qualitative study finding the four sources of information outlined in BSE theory—verbal persuasion, performance accomplishment, and physiologic/affective responses—highlighted their relevance and applicability to mothers of preterm infants [24]. Breastfeeding self-efficacy has been identified as a predictor of breastfeeding at hospital discharge and is associated with improved state regulation in infants [25, 26]. In NICU, where mothers often face separation from their infants and heightened stress, BSE theory can guide interventions aimed at enhancing maternal confidence and coping skills, potentially improving both breastfeeding outcomes and milk output. Nevertheless, the relationship between breastfeeding self-efficacy and milk output in preterm mothers, as well as the factors influencing this relationship, requires further investigation.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the changes in breastfeeding self-efficacy, milk production, and feeding pattern choice among preterm mothers within the first half-month postpartum, and to identify the factors influencing these outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study utilized a prospective cohort design, conducted within 3–14 days postpartum.

Participant recruitment and eligibility criteria

Participants were recruited from mothers of newborns hospitalized in the NICU. The research was conducted using convenience sampling. Eligibility criteria included: being at least 18 years old, having no contraindications to breastfeeding, possessing normal understanding and communication skills, having no history of mental illness, and being capable of providing informed consent. Participants were required to agree to participate and complete the study in the hospital, including regular follow-ups and adherence to the study’s integrity and ethical standards. Mothers hospitalized due to illness or undergoing psychological treatment were excluded.

Data collection process

Data collection spanned over twelve months, from January 2024 to December 2024. The process was divided into three phases. In the first phase, mothers of newborns admitted to NICU were asked to complete several questionnaires. These included assessments of socio-demographic details, pregnancy and delivery characteristics, breastfeeding behavior and milk production, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and social support. In the second and third phases, mothers continued to assess their breastfeeding behavior and milk production, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and social support situation. All assessments were collected through the Chinese online questionnaire collection platform Wenjuanxing. In order to ensure the integrity of the data, a trained nurse assisted in all aspects of questionnaire distribution, collection and patient communication. The data were collected at 3,7 and 14 days postpartum.

Ethical considerations

This research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring rigorous ethical standards in its execution. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee.

Informed consent process

Before any data collection, a detailed meeting was conducted between the researcher and the participants. During the meeting, participants were informed about the research’s objectives, methodologies, potential risks, and benefits associated. An informed consent form was provided, emphasizing that participation was voluntary and that participants would be able to withdraw from the study at any time. The form also outlined strict measures to ensure data confidentiality, with participants’ data anonymized and access restricted to the research team directly involved in the study to safeguard privacy.

Research instruments

In the pursuit of a comprehensive understanding of maternal breastfeeding behavior and milk production, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and social support situation, the study incorporated psychometric instruments. The questionnaire had 5 parts, socio-demographic, pregnancy and delivery characteristics, breastfeeding behavior, and milk production and social support situation designed by researchers themselves besides the psychometric scales used for breastfeeding self-efficacy. (1) Socio-demographic data included maternal age, education level, ethnicity, monthly household income, etc. (2) Pregnancy and delivery characteristics collection included birth history, number of pregnancies, pregnancy complications, mode of delivery, gestational age of the baby, birth weight, reasons for discharge, etc. (3) Breastfeeding behavior and milk production collection included mother’s feeding method, milk pumping method, milk production, whether there are feeding difficulties, etc. (4) Social support situation was assessed using a set of study-specific items developed for this research, rather than a standardized social support scale. These items captured practical aspects of support relevant to breastfeeding, including maternal plans for breastfeeding and its intended duration, participation of the mother and her spouse in breastfeeding-related training, the level of support provided by the spouse, sources of maternal breastfeeding knowledge, and other sources of social support. As these items were designed to reflect real-world support behaviors rather than measure a single latent construct, internal consistency (e.g., Cronbach’s α) was not calculated. (5) Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale-Short Form (BSES-SF): The BSES-SF was originally developed by Dennis and Faux to evaluate the self-efficacy levels of breastfeeding mothers and then the short form was created by Dennis [27]. The scale was translated into Chinese by Yang, and measured in mothers of premature infants, achieving a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.973 [28], indicating high reliability. Scores range from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The scale helps identify high-risk mothers, evaluate breastfeeding behaviors, tailor confidence-building strategies, assess intervention effectiveness, and guide future programs.

Statistical analysis strategy

Data sorting and statistical analysis were conducted using R version 4.2.2. Continuous data following a normal distribution were expressed as mean (standard deviation) and compared using an independent sample T-test. The measurement data of skewness distribution were described by the median (quartile), and the rank sum test was used for comparison between groups. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. A potential category mixed model was used to assess changes in breastfeeding self-efficacy over time, dividing the population into a limited number of potential categories and exploring the influencing factors through logistic regression analysis. Multinomial Logistic Regression was used to analyze the relationship between self-efficacy and feeding style, while linear regression was used to analyze the relationship between self-efficacy and milk output. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 263 participants met the inclusion criteria and were recruited during the study period, of whom 28 were subsequently excluded. Among the remaining 234 participants, 200 completed the full 14 days postpartum follow-up, leaving a final sample size of 200 participants included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Changes in early postpartum lactation volume and breastfeeding self-efficacy

We conducted a latent category analysis on the change in breastfeeding self-efficacy over time and observed an overall increase. The breastfeeding self-efficacy could be divided into two groups: low self-efficacy (class 1) and high self-efficacy (class 2), as shown in Fig. 2. In a single-factor analysis based on self-efficacy grouping, several factors were identified as potential influencers. These included family income (p < 0.001), pregnancy complications(p = 0.024), plan the duration of exclusive breastfeeding(p < 0.001), can get support or help from medical staff(p < 0.001), family members (p < 0.001), friends(p < 0.001) during feeding, have reliable sources of breastfeeding knowledge (p < 0.001), breastfeeding related difficulties (p < 0.001). These factors, with a significance level of p < 0.05, were considered in the multi-factor analysis, as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

This study explored the influencing factors of breastfeeding self-efficacy and examined its relationship with milk output and maternal feeding practices in mothers of preterm infants. Our findings highlight the importance of social support, breastfeeding planning, and breastfeeding knowledge in shaping maternal confidence during breastfeeding.

We found that higher BSE in the early postpartum period was associated with a stronger inclination toward breastfeeding, consistent with findings among mothers of full-term infants [29]. However, self-efficacy levels among mothers of preterm infants remained below the suggested threshold for sustaining exclusive breastfeeding (BSES-SF score >50), which may place them at risk of premature discontinuation [23, 30]. These results suggest that targeted measures are urgently needed to enhance BSE among preterm mothers. Evidence from meta-analyses has confirmed that interventions to improve BSE are effective in extending breastfeeding duration and exclusivity [31]. Therefore, it is recommended for healthcare providers to implement interventions to increase the self-efficacy of breastfeeding in the early postpartum period, especially for preterm mothers, thereby promoting breastfeeding and extending its duration.

Insufficient milk supply is a leading cause for discontinuing breastfeeding among premature infants [32]. In our study, mothers with higher self-efficacy consistently demonstrated greater milk output across the first two weeks postpartum, supporting the view that maternal confidence not only influences feeding decisions but also contributes to physiological outcomes such as lactation [33]. This underscores the importance of strengthening BSE early after delivery to support both maternal and infant health.

The study also shows that family support, absence of pregnancy complications, planned breastfeeding and anticipated duration of breastfeeding are important factors influencing self-efficacy of breastfeeding, which is consistent with previous research results [15, 34, 35]. We also found that higher family income and support from friends and health professionals were also important factors for breastfeeding self-efficacy, which further complements previous studies. In China, family members-particularly spouses and grandparents play a decisive role in feeding decisions, and their support and knowledge are often as critical as maternal intention. It is suggested that the medical staff should not only target mothers but also engage partners and extended family members, consider increasing the knowledge learning and support of the maternal spouse before birth and enhance the awareness of communication between the medical staff and the maternal family when formulating intervention measures. In addition, healthcare providers should pay attention to mothers with complications during pregnancy and help these mothers prevent in advance to avoid adverse feeding experiences such as nipple breakage and mastitis, which may undermine maternal confidence.

The above results suggest the need to develop appropriate intervention strategies to enhance maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy. Evidence indicates that multicomponent, theory-based interventions combining face-to-face instruction with at least three telephone follow-up before and after delivery can effectively improve exclusive breastfeeding self-efficacy for over 6 months and partial breastfeeding for more than 2 months, while also supporting maternal mental health [36]. These findings confirm that targeted prenatal education can improve maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy, however their applicability to preterm mothers remains uncertain. Researchers in China designed an online WeChat mini-program for mothers of premature babies, which provided breastfeeding courses, consultations, peer support, and follow-up; although this three-month program improved maternal knowledge, it did not increase self-efficacy [37]. Similarly, an Iranian study guided by the theory of planned behavior implemented a four-day on-site intervention-including lectures, educational videos, Q&A sessions, group discussions, individual counseling, and role play—which improved maternal attitudes and perceived behavioral control but did not directly assess self-efficacy [38]. Therefore, these findings highlight the urgent need for well-designed, culturally appropriate interventions specifically targeting the breastfeeding self-efficacy of mothers with preterm infants.

This study provides the first longitudinal evidence of BSE dynamics in Chinese preterm mothers, highlighting the critical role of social support and early intervention. But it also has several limitations. First, the sample was predominantly recruited from economically advantaged urban areas in China, which may limit the generalizability of findings to rural populations where access to healthcare and breastfeeding resources differs. Second, potential confounding factors such as maternal mental health status (e.g., postpartum depression) were not systematically controlled, which could influence breastfeeding self-efficacy and milk production. Future research should expand to diverse geographical settings and incorporate comprehensive assessments of maternal psychological well-being. In addition, more studies should continue to explore interventions that focus on improving breastfeeding self-efficacy in Chinese mothers of preterm infants, ultimately enhancing maternal and infant health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the influence of breastfeeding self-efficacy in the early postpartum period for preterm mothers, revealing its impact on feeding pattern choice and milk production. The results suggest that higher levels of breastfeeding self-efficacy lead to stronger preference for breastfeeding and increased milk production. Factors such as higher household income, better social support system (from family, friends, or health professionals), effective access to breastfeeding information, and the presence of a prenatal breastfeeding plan all play a crucial role in fostering breastfeeding self-efficacy. The study underscores the need for early postpartum interventions aimed at improving self-efficacy, emphasizing the importance of social support and effective communication between medical staff and the mother’s family. Future studies should continue to explore possible influencing factors of breastfeeding self-efficacy and develop interventions to support preterm mothers, ultimately improving the outcomes for both mothers and infants.
