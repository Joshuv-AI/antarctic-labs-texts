A Personalized, 3D Printed Polymeric Device for the Prevention of Post‐Myocardial Infarction Cardiac Remodeling
Authors: Nicola Mansour, Daniel Cohn, Ishita Sarkar, Raja Alamore, Kareem Abd Rbo, Offer Amir, Dean Nachman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMyocardial remodeling and dilatation following myocardial infarction initiates a life‐threatening process leading to heart failure. As every patient's anatomy and pathophysiology are unique, developing a custom‐made cardiac restraint device tailored for each patient is crucial. This study introduces a personalized, 3D printed cardiac restraint device (CARD) aimed at restoring cardiac physiology and preventing cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction. Personalization is achieved by 3D printing the CARD based on precise anatomical and physiological data from the CT and Echocardiography scan. The rationally designed printable molecules (“inks”) key to this research are developed using a modular synthetic strategy. The inks consist of a triblock structure consisting of a central polypropylene oxide (PPO) segment and two side polycaprolactone (PCL) blocks, end‐capped with the C═C double bonds required for printing. Studies conducted on rat and swine myocardial infarction models demonstrated the capability of the integrated use of imaging, designing and 3D printing, to fabricate a CARD that accommodates to the cardiac anatomy without compromising the diastolic function. Improved hemodynamic profiles following CARD implantation suggest enhanced left ventricular function and better fluid dynamics within both cardiac and pulmonary systems. No device‐related mortality occurred, and the CARD remained securely in place for 28 days post‐implantation.Keywords: 3D printing, cardiac remodeling, device, personalization, polymer

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a serious pathology of cardiac dysfunction that leads to severe symptoms, holds a high mortality rate and constitutes one of the most devastating contemporary health problems, especially in developed countries [1, 2].

Myocardial remodeling following a causing insult, such as myocardial infarction (MI) initiates a vicious cycle leading to further remodeling and HF. This versatile condition materializes with different presentations, etiology, and pathophysiology [3, 4]. The main differentiation of HF types according to the ejection fraction status is between HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and ventricular dilatation, and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). This study focuses on HFrEF, a pathology that accounts for approximately half of all HF cases, with pathophysiological remodeling characterized by progressive ventricular dilatation. The leading cause of this widespread life‐threatening pathology is ischemia due to coronary artery disease, even though additional causes such as inflammatory, infectious, genetic, and toxic etiologies also often lead to HF [4, 5].

In the US alone, nearly 6.5 million patients suffer from HF, and 500,000 new patients are diagnosed with HF annually. [6, 7, 8, 9] Despite the development of novel drugs and devices, the mortality and morbidity burdens of HF remain extremely high, with 1 in 3 patients dying within one year of hospitalization for HF, and 50%–75% within five years of diagnosis. [6, 10] This important unmet need led to attempts to develop elastic support devices encasing the ventricles, preventing further cardiac dilatation and remodeling [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Despite promising preliminary results, large clinical trials failed to demonstrate substantial improvement following implantation of these devices [12].

Due to the complex, multi‐factorial nature of the HF pathologic process, every patient's heart exerts a unique injury pattern, both spatially as well as over time. Since the previously developed elastic support devices were based on an off‐the‐shelf, one‐size‐fits‐all design paradigm, they had fundamental shortcomings [16]. It is apparent, therefore, that there is a pressing need for a personalized cardiac restraint device for the relief of HF morbidity and mortality.

The last two decades have witnessed the emergence of innovative digital design and manufacturing technologies, collectively known as Additive Manufacturing, which allow the rapid and accurate production of complex structures which were difficult and often even impossible to create in the past. [17, 18, 19, 20] Much work has been devoted in recent years in the clinical arena to manufacture personalized medical devices. [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27] Novel high‐speed, high‐resolution modalities such as Computed Tomography (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) allow precise imaging to enable personalized device fabrication.

The objective of this research is to develop a 3D printed, personalized cardiac restraint device (labeled CARD) for the prevention and treatment of cardiac remodeling leading to heart failure. The digital information obtained from the patient's heart, the cornerstone of the printing of the device, pertains not only to the size and shape of the heart but also to its physiology. The precise spatial and hemodynamic characterization of the failed heart, translated into digital information, is fed into the 3D printer, so the device engineered displays the optimized set of properties.

It is important to stress that existing printable molecules, named “inks” hereby, are typically stiff materials, clearly inappropriate for soft tissue applications, such as a CARD.

This study introduces a rich and versatile arsenal of flexible inks, cross linkable via photo‐polymerization, that enabled the 3D printing of the flexible and elastic CARD. Due to the modular synthetic methodology implemented when developing these inks, which constitute the basic building blocks of the CARD, they cover a broad range of compositions, morphological features, and mechanical properties. Further to the optimization of the inks and the geometrical parameters of the device printed, this study investigated the short‐term performance of the CARD in small and large animal models using echocardiography and CT imaging, respectively.

The combination of the tailored inks, the printing capabilities developed and the imaging‐based personalization, allowed the custom‐made CARD to achieve superior properties and display enhanced in vivo performance.

In a previous conference abstract, limited echocardiography results from a rat myocardial ischemia model were reported [28]. This manuscript provides a detailed evaluation of the development and properties of the CARD and examines its effects on rat and swine models of myocardial infarction.

Materials and Methods

Ink Synthesis and Characterization

Synthesis of the Dimethacrylate of the PCL‐b‐PPG‐b‐PCL (PPCAs) Triblock

Short polypropylene glycol (PPG) chains having a molecular weight of 2K, 3 and 4K were supplied by Thermo Scientific Chemicals. Longer PPG chains were synthesized by reacting short PPG chains using hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI) (Sigma‐Aldrich) as the coupling molecule. The second step involved synthesizing the polycaprolactone‐b‐polypropylene glycol‐b‐ polycaprolactone (PCL‐b‐PPG‐b‐PCL) (PPCA) triblock via the ring‐opening polymerization (ROP) of ε‐caprolactone (Thermo Scientific Chemicals. Finally, the hydroxyl‐terminated PPCA triblock was end‐capped with C═C double bonds by its reaction with 2‐isocyanatoethyl methacrylate (IEMA) (Tokyo Chemical Industry), yielding the final ink. These PPG/PCL inks are named PPCA, and they will be denominated by relating first to the length of the PPG segment, followed by the number of CL units on each side of the central PPG chain. For example, PPCA2K‐8 refers to an ink comprising a PPG2000 mid‐segment and two lateral blocks, each consisting of eight CL units. The corresponding dimethacrylate will, therefore, be labelled PPCA2K‐8‐dMA.

The synthesis is illustrated here for an ink consisting of a PPG12K central segment, synthesized as described below, and two PCL side blocks, each comprising twelve CL units. This ink is designated PPCA12K‐12‐dMA. In detail, PPG4K was dried under vacuum in a three‐necked flask at 120°C for 2 h. Subsequently, 0.67 moles of HDI (relative to 1 mole of PPG) was added dropwise over 1 h at 85°C under a nitrogen atmosphere and reacted with Tin(II) 2‐ethylhexanoate (Sn(Oct)2) (Sigma–Aldrich) catalyst for 1.5 h. The chain extended PPG chains were cleaned by dissolving them in Chloroform (Bio‐Lab), followed by their precipitation in an excess of petroleum ether (Bio‐Lab). This purification process was repeated three times, and the chain‐extended PPG polymer was then dried in a fume hood. A 93%–95% yield was obtained for these coupling reactions. This coupling reaction was not needed for inks comprising short PPG 2K, 3 and 4K chains. Next, the required amount of ε‐caprolactone was added under nitrogen to dried HO‐PPG4K‐HDI‐PPG4K‐HDI‐PPG4K‐OH at 145°C to form the two PCL side blocks, each consisting of twelve CL units. This reaction proceeded for 3 h under ROP conditions with Sn(Oct)2 as the catalyst. In the final step, the triblock was reacted with IEMA in a 1:2 molar ratio under nitrogen, by adding IEMA and Sn(Oct)2 to a solution of the synthesized triblock in dry dioxane = (Bio‐Lab. This reaction was conducted for 2 h at 85°C. The resulting PPCA12K‐12‐dMA ink was dissolved in Chloroform (Bio‐Lab,) and precipitated in an excess of petroleum ether (Bio‐Lab). This precipitation process was repeated three times, and the polymer was dried in a fume hood.

The thermal analysis of the inks was performed using a Mettler‐Toledo TA‐400 DSC. The heating and cooling rates were 10 and 30 K min−1, respectively

Characterization of the Printed Devices

As described by Lendlein et al. (2005), the printed thermoset networks were characterized by their gel content and water absorption [29].

X‐Ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis

XRD analysis was performed to determine the degree of crystallinity and phase composition of the printed models. The measurements were performed on the D8 Advance diffractometer with LYNXEYE‐XE‐T detector (Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany) operating in 1D mode with CuKα (1.5418 Å) as the X‐ray source with the following measurement conditions: tube voltage of 40 kV, tube current of 40 mA. Data acquisition was conducted over a 2θ range of range 5°–55°, with a step size of step size 0.02° and a counting time of 0.5 sec/step.

Ink Preparation

The ink was formulated by mixing 3 wt.% of 2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoyl diphenylphosphine oxide (TPO) (BASF, Germany) as photoinitiator and 0.1 wt.% of vitamin E (Ahava) as a premature crosslinking inhibitor. A Micro 15 Twin Screw compounder (DSM Research, Netherlands) was used to mix the material at 75°C and 60 rpm.

3D printing Process

The inks were prepared for printing by first heating them above their melting temperature (typically in the 55°C–60°C range, depending on the ink's composition). As soon as the melt was homogeneous, it was poured into the pre‐heated custom‐made monomer bath, which kept the ink at 85°C throughout the printing process.

The digital information of the anatomy of the rat's heart was obtained by echocardiography scanning, while the anatomy of the pig's heart was obtained by computed tomography (CT) imaging. This information was then converted into volumetric parameters manually, at a 1 mm resolution. The digital model was supplied in an OBJ format and was transferred into CAD software. More precisely, it was processed using the following software packages: FreeCad, Blender, Autocad Inventor (Autodesk) and Mesh‐mixer (Autodesk), so to design the CARD model for each rat. 3D CARDs were printed in a DLP 3D printer (MAX X, Asiga, Australia) equipped with a 385 nm UV‐LED source and a custom‐made temperature‐controlled resin bath. For the printing, bath temperature was maintained at 85°C, layer thickness was 100 µm, burn‐in‐time was 6s, and exposure time was 4s.

Mechanical Properties Measurements

The mechanical tensile tests were conducted on printed dog‐bone samples using an Instron 4500 Universal Testing Machine equipped with a 10N (Newton) mesh load. The crosshead speeds were 10 mm/min.

The terms “oxide” and “glycol” in polypropylene oxide (PPO) and polypropylene glycol (PPG), respectively, will be used throughout this study interchangeably

Animal Model Study

Study Design

The small animal model study included twelve male Sprague Dawley rats (330–350 g) that were procured from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem (HUJI) Animal House Facility. All animals were housed under standard laboratory condition (23°C and 12‐hour light/dark cycle) with food and water in ad libitum.

Myocardial infarction was induced in all animals by surgically ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. The animals were then randomly divided into two groups: treatment group (LAD+CARD) implanted with a prefabricated personalized CARD, and control group (LAD). Two animals succumbed during the surgical LAD ligation. Of the ten remaining animals, six were implanted with the CARD. For the large animals’ study, twelve female house pigs (22–25 kg) (Laboratory Farm, Lahav, Israel) were randomly assigned to the LAD ligation only group (LAD), or LAD ligation and CARD implantation (LAD+CARD). Two animals succumbed shortly after the surgical procedure, one in each group, leaving five in the LAD+CARD and five in the LAD groups.

The cardiac dimensions for designing the CARD were measured pre‐surgery in rats (day ‐2) via echocardiography (Figure 1A) and in swine (day ‐7) via computed tomography (CT) (Figure 1B). Baseline echocardiography was performed on surgery day, before LAD ligation in swine (Figure 1B). The CARD was implanted in LAD+CARD animals for 28 days. Post‐surgery echocardiography was conducted in rats on days 7, 14, and 28 using a Vevo 3100 ultrasound machine (FujiFilm, Visualsonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) (Figure 1A) and in swine on day 28 (Vivid I, GE Health Care, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) (Figure 1B). Animals were humanely sacrificed at the end of the experiment. Echocardiographic measurements included: Left ventricular end‐diastolic volume (LVEDD) and ejection fraction (EF). Using pulsed‐wave Doppler, peak early diastolic transmitral flow velocity (Ewave), early diastolic velocity of the mitral annulus (E’ wave) and their ratio E/E’ ratio, which reflects left ventricular diastolic function were assessed. A higher E/E’ ratio suggests impaired LV relaxation or elevated filling pressure, which are key features of diastolic dysfunction.

Study design in rat (A) and swine (B) HFrEF models. Cardiac volumes were assessed by echocardiography (rat) or CT (swine), followed by personalized CARD design/printing, LAD ligation for MI induction, along with CARD implantation, echocardiographic follow‐up until sacrifice and tissue collection to measure fibrosis.

Volumetric Analysis of Rat and Swine Heart

A key part of the personalized design of the CARD is the ability for precise anatomic‐volumetric, hemodynamic, and physiologic assessment of cardiac function. Echocardiographic evaluation for rats was performed using a Vevo 3100 ultrasound machine with 40 MHz and 0.01 mm resolution. Animals were anesthetized in a chamber (3% isoflurane‐95% oxygen at 2 L/min) and afterward supinely placed on a heating pad at 37°C on maintenance anesthesia (0.5%–2% isoflurane). Anesthesia was titrated to maintain a heart rate of 300–400 beats per minute. The images were converted to DICOM format and processed with commercial 3D reconstruction software (Meshmixer, Autodesk) to create a 3D‐printable model.

For the swine model study, animals were anesthetized by (Ketamine HCl 20 mg/kg + Xylazine 2 mg/kg) intramuscular (IM) and maintained by Isoflurane 1%–3% via positive pressure ventilation utilizing 100% oxygen (2 L/min). Volumetric cardiac assessment was performed using a cardiac CT scan. The Cardiac CT was ECG‐synchronized and reconstructed only at mid‐diastole (70% R‐R) and breath‐hold in inspiration, heart rate was kept at ≈60–70 per min by adjusting the Isoflurane dosing. Iodinated contrast (70 mL) was injected at 6 mL/s with a 50 mL saline chaser, using bolus tracking in the ascending aorta. The CT images were used for cardiac modeling using a 3D slicer (3D Slicer 5.6.2) software, allowing personalized CARD design.

Surgical Procedure

Distal LAD ligation and CARD implantation were performed via midline sternotomy on day 0. Anesthesia was induced (rat: 3% isoflurane; swine: Ketamine HCl 20 mg/kg + Xylazine 2 mg/kg IM) and maintained (rat: 2–2.5% isoflurane; swine: Isoflurane 1‐ 3% via positive pressure ventilation utilizing 100% oxygen (2 L/in). After iodine sterilization, a midline sternotomy was performed to expose the heart. The pericardium was excised, and the LAD below the first diagonal branch was permanently ligated with 5‐0 silk sutures, confirmed by myocardial discoloration. The CARD was positioned over the ventricles, aligned with the atrioventricular groove. The heart was repositioned into the ribcage, hemostasis ensured, and the sternotomy closed withsutures. Pain management included Tramadol (5 mg/kg), every 8 h IM or subcutaneously (SC) for rats pre‐ and post‐surgery, and for swine, Dipyrone (30 mg/kg), twice daily (BID), IM or SC and Buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg), BID, SC.

Tissue Sectioning and Staining

At the end of the experiment, rat hearts were excised, sectioned into base, middle, and apical transverse portions, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. After ethanol infusion, tissues were paraffin‐embedded, longitudinally sectioned into 5‐µm slices, and stained with Masson's trichrome at the Hadassah Medical Center pathology lab. Images were captured at 1400 × 1400 dpi resolution using a Nikon TL microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Swine hearts were excised at the experimental end point, the heart was sliced and stained with Tetrazolium Chloride (TTC).

Ethical Considerations

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) of HUJI for the rodent model study (MD‐21‐16604‐4) and Asaf Harofeh Medical Center for the swine model study (11‐2024).

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using the GraphPad Prism 8 software. A two‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was applied for repeated comparisons across multiple groups over time. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was used to assess normality, and either a student's t‐test or a Mann‐Whitney U test was employed for comparing two groups.

Results and Discussion

The Inks

It is the interplay between three levels of personalization that will enable obtaining the optimal clinical performance of the device. The first is derived from the anatomical and physiological digital data of the heart of the patient, obtained in this research via echocardiography and CT imaging. The second and third personalization levels stem from the optimization of the ink and the geometrical parameters of the architecture printed both dictated by the specific clinical requirements posed by each patient.

The first chapter of this study focuses, therefore, on the development of the inks that constitute the basic building blocks of the CARDs and largely determine the properties of the 3D printed devices.

The inks developed comprise a triblock consisting of a central polypropylene glycol (PPG) segment and two side polycaprolactone (PCL) blocks end‐capped with C═C double bonds. This modular synthetic approach enables each of the components to perform a specific chemical or mechanical task. While the PPG chains allow to fine tune the flexibility of the ink, the PCL blocks contribute to its mechanical properties and allow to optimize its crosslink density by controlling the average molecular weight between crosslink junctions. Although PCL is known for its biodegradability, it degrades extremely slowly, over several years [30, 31], and, hence, its degradability is immaterial to this 28 day implantation study.

Figure 2 presents the two‐step synthesis of the inks. In the first step of the synthesis, the ring opening polymerization of ε‐caprolactone rings is performed, initiated by PPG's hydroxyl terminal groups. In the second step, the PCL‐PPG‐PCL triblock is end‐capped with C═C double bonds by reacting its hydroxyl terminal groups with isocyanate ethyl methacrylate (IEMA) in a PCL‐PPG‐PCL:IEMA 1:2 molar ratio.

Synthesis of 3D printable PPG/PCL dimethacrylate (PPCA‐dMA) inks.

Initially, the PPG segments had a 2K, 3K or 4K molecular weight. Above 4K, no PPG chains are commercially available. Since the length of the PPG segment plays a key role in determining the printability of the ink and its mechanical properties, longer PPG chains were synthesized. This was achieved by reacting hydroxyl‐capped PPG3000 and PPG4000 with hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI) in PPG:HDI molar ratios so that PPG diols as long as 16K were produced.

The composition of the chain‐extended PPG molecules was determined by H‐NMR analysis. The molecular weight of the PPG12K molecule, for example, was evaluated by comparing HDI's C3/C4 methylene protons at 1.4 ppm with PPO's methine proton at 3.4 ppm. The integration showed 70% HDI incorporation relative to the PPG4K precursor. This value closely matches the theoretical requirement of 67% (2/3 equivalent) HDI needed for reacting three PPG4Ks with two HDI molecules, to generate the desired hydroxyl‐terminated PPG12K molecule. The composition of the PCL‐PPG‐PCL triblocks and their degree of methacrylation, meaning the number of C═C double bonds present per triblock molecule, was determined using NMR spectroscopy. The NMR spectrum of PPCA12K‐12‐dMA, a flexible elastomeric ink, is shown in Figure 3, with the peaks properly assigned.

The peaks in the NMR spectrum and the ratio of their integration values demonstrated that both the ε‐CL ring opening polymerization and the end‐capping reaction were performed successfully, and the degree of methacrylation attained was approximately 2.

The composition of the PCL‐PPG‐PCL triblocks was optimized by fine‐tuning the molecular weight of each of its two components as well as the ratio between them. A rich and versatile battery of PPCA inks was synthesized, with the molecular weight of the PPG segments spanning from 2 to 16K and the number of CL repeating units covering the 4–40 range. As a result, inks displaying different viscosity values and printability levels prior to and during printing, and constructs exhibiting a wide range of mechanical properties once printed, were developed.

PPCA inks have a melting point of around 57°C, as determined by DSC studies (not shown), performed satisfactorily during printing.

The mechanical properties of the inks and, therefore, those of the 3D printed CARDs, markedly affect the clinical performance of the device. In accordance with theoretical considerations, the longer the PCL block, for the same PPO segment, the stronger and stiffer the inks, while their respective elongation at break values increased as well. For example, the stress at break values of the inks increased from 0.7 MPa, all the way up to 15.7 MPa, for inks comprising 12 and 26 CL repeating units, respectively. The same applied to their stiffness, with Young's modulus values increasing from 3.9 to 93.0 MPa, accordingly. Finally, their elongation at break values spanned from 23% for the ink containing a short PCL block (12 units), reaching 720% when the PCL block contained 26 CL repeating units. The same pattern of behavior was encountered when the inks comprised PPG3K and PPG4K segments. It is apparent from the data reported that the strength, stiffness and extensibility of the inks decreased, as the length of the PPG segment increased, since the PCL content decreased concomitantly. Additionally, it is not only that the relative CL content diminished but also that its degree of crystallinity decreased, the longer the PPG segments.

Moving one step further, when comparing two inks that contained equally long PCL blocks, for example, consisting of 23 CL units, but comprising PPG 2 and 3K segments, the former was stronger (10.0 vs. 7.7 MPa), stiffer (26.3 v 21.7 MPa), and they elongated more (311% vs. 250%), as expected.

In light of the significant rigidity of inks comprising PPG segments shorter than 6K, inks consisting of longer segments (up to 16K) were synthesized and analyzed. Inks comprising PPG chains longer than 16K could not be printed.

To illustrate how effective long PPG chains are in increasing the flexibility of the ink, suffices to compare PPCA2K‐26‐dMA and PPCA8K‐26‐dMA, with the former exhibiting a Young's modulus more than eight times higher than the latter (93 MPa vs. 11 MPa).

Aiming at tailoring inks that can successfully generate highly flexible and elastic devices approaching isocompliance with the heart, additional fine‐tuning of the inks was conducted. Following this line of thought, in one of the strategies pursued, the length of the PPG segment was maximized, while the number of the CL units minimized, so long as the required mechanical properties were obtained. PPCA12K‐12‐dMA, consisting of a central PPG12K flexible segment and two lateral PCL blocks, each consisting of 12 CL repeating units, and end‐capped with methacrylate moieties, exhibited excellent mechanical properties. PPCA12K‐12‐dMA displayed a suitably low Young's modulus of 0.47 MPa and a 60% elongation at break value. Additionally, this ink showed a stress at break value of 0.4 MPa, far beyond what is physiologically required.

The XRD spectra shown in Figure 4 shed light on the morphology of the inks, focusing primarily on the crystallinity of the PCL blocks, since all PPG chains are amorphous. That said, PPG segments may affect the crystalizability of the PCLblocks, as shown below. The PCL80K spectrum, which was added as a control, shows the two sharp peaks at 21.3° and 23.6°, assigned to PCL's (110) and (200) crystallographic planes. The spectra presented illustrate the delicate interplay existing between the length of the two components, affecting especially PCL's ability to crystallize. While PCL chains in the PPCA8K‐26‐dMA ink were able to crystallize to a considerable extent, this ability decreased sharply in PPCA12K‐26‐dMA, comprising longer PPG chains. The same effect can be seen when keeping the length of the PPG segments constant (12K) and enlarging the PCL blocks from 12 to 26K.

In light of its mechanical properties and morphology, PPCA12K‐12‐dMA was chosen as the optimal ink for the continuation of this research.

3D Printing the CARD

Moving one step forward and seeking to print personalized devices, custom‐made CARDs were engineered by feeding the printer with the precise digital information of the heart of each animal. This was achieved by conducting real imaging of the heart of the rat and swine using echocardiography and CT, respectively. The data were processed on CAD software to obtain the accurate heart shape, with special attention being given to the left ventricle region, to print a CARD that conformed optimally to the heart of that animal.

The required set of mechanical properties was achieved by the judicious design of both the ink and the structural parameters of the printed device. To this end, the flexible PPCA12K‐12‐dMA ink was used to print honeycomb (HC) architectures, where their different structural parameters, such as the thickness of their struts and the size of their empty cells, were tightly controlled and fine‐tuned. The printing was conducted at 85 degrees to achieve the optimal ≈1–2 Pa·s viscosity, which allowed optimal printing conditions. While below 85 degrees, the viscosity of the melt was too high to allow satisfactory printing; already at 90 degrees, premature crosslinking occurred, resulting in an unacceptable sharp increase in viscosity. After screening several layer thickness values, a 100 µm layer thickness was selected since it provided the optimal balance between resolution and structural integrity.

The effect of the shape, density and size of the HC structure on the behavior of the CARD was investigated initially in dog‐bone samples. Three sizes of HC architectures were analyzed, having 800, 1200, and 1600 µm cell diameters. As derived from their respective cell diameter, the HC dog‐bone contained 29, 14 and 10 cells, as the diameter increased from 800 to 1200 µm and finally to 1600 µm, correspondingly. The different HC structures were labelled “HC followed by a number that described its cell diameter”. Hence, an HC800 structure, for example, refers to an HC architecture having an 800 µm cell diameter.

Table 2 shows dog‐bone samples consisting of HC structures in their mid‐section, printed using PPCA12K‐12‐dMA inks, and presents their respective mechanical properties. While the solid dog‐bone displayed a 470 kPa Young's modulus, it drastically decreased as the size of the cells became larger, with the HC having a 1600 µm cell diameter exhibiting a very low 7 kPa modulus.

Evaluation of CARD in Animal Model of HFrEF

Personalized 3D‐Printed CARDs Achieve Optimal Fit and Anatomical Integration

3D‐printed CARDs fabricated using the flexible PPCA12K‐12‐dA ink with an 800 µm cell diameter were selected, as they provided the best fit. The personalized CARDs demonstrated seamless anatomical alignment and orientation with the native cardiac muscle upon implantation in both rats (Figure 6A) and a swine model of HFrEF (Figure 6B).

Implantation of the CARD and anatomical conformability for the rat model (A) and swine model of HFrEF (B).

Echocardiographic Assessment of CARD Effects on Cardiac Function and Remodeling in Hfref Models

Ejection fraction (EF) is a measure of systolic function; a reduced EF, indicative of impaired contractility, is characteristic of HFrEF. In the rat study, two‐way ANOVA repeated measures showed a significant main effect of time on EF. As expected in HFrEF model, EF dropped significantly seven days after LAD ligation in both groups (LAD: 76.24 ± 7.43% to 45.71 ± 7.19%, p = 0.05; LAD+CARD: 74.21 ± 5.77% to 48.09 ± 5.8%, p <0.01), followed by gradual improvement, returning to baseline by day 28 (LAD: 68.62 ± 16.05%; LAD+CARD: 67.73 ± 6.86%) (Figure 7A and Table 3). This gradual recovery in EF is likely attributable to the distal LAD ligation performed in the rats, which is less severe than proximal ligation and was chosen to minimize mortality.

Ejection fraction (EF) in rats, fractional shortening (FS%) in swine, LV end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDD), change in LVEDD (ΔLVEDD), and E/E′ values were assessed for the LAD and LAD+CARD groups in both rat and swine studies across multiple time points. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using two‐way repeated measures ANOVA, mixed two‐way ANOVA, or unpaired t‐test, as appropriate.

In the swine study, mixed‐effects two‐way ANOVA for fractional shortening (FS%) revealed a significant main effect of time (Table 3). FS % (a surrogate for EF) decreased significantly from baseline to 28 days post‐LAD surgery in both groups (LAD: 32.97 ± 5.81% to 20.60 ± 7.18%, p <0.001; LAD+CARD: 33.91 ± 5.36% to 23.10 ± 4.42, p < 0.01), confirming the development of an MI model progressing to HFrEF (Figure 7B and Table 3).

E/E’ is the ratio of early mitral inflow velocity (E) to early diastolic mitral annular velocity (E’). It is used to estimate left ventricular filling pressure, with higher values indicating elevated filling pressures and impaired diastolic relaxation of the heart. In the rat study, E/E' remained more stable in the LAD+CARD group compared to the LAD group (Figure 7C). While two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA showed no significant main effects of treatment or time, there was a significant interaction between the two (Table 3). Bonferroni post‐hoc analysis revealed a strong trend on Day 7, with the LAD group showing higher E/E' compared to the LAD+CARD group (LAD: 25.81 ± 8.27; LAD+CARD: 17.26 ± 6.59, p = 0.07). These findings suggest that CARD treatment substantially prevented diastolic dysfunction, as indicated by the elevated E/E' in the LAD group, which was markedly mitigated in the LAD+CARD group at Day 7. In the swine model, no significant differences were observed across the groups over the study period, indicating that the CARD had no deleterious effects on diastolic function (Figure 7D and Table 3).

LVEDD (left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter) is a key indicator of cardiac remodeling. In HFrEF, the heart undergoes pathological cardiac remodeling that leads to ventricular dilatation, so an increased LVEDD reflects worsening cardiac structure and function. In rats, two‐way repeated‐measures analysis revealed a significant main effect of time and a strong trend toward an interaction between time and treatment (p = 0.07), indicating that while LVEDD increased in the LAD group, it remained stable in the CARD‐treated group, suggesting prevention of cardiac dilation. Over 28 days, LVEDD increased significantly in the LAD group (6.77 ± 0.48 cm at baseline to 8.02 ±0.63 cm on day 7, p = 0.03 to 8.38 ± 0.59 cm 28‐days post‐surgery, p <0.01) but remained relatively stable in the LAD+CARD group (7.16 ± 0.47 cm at baseline to 7.37 ± 0.73 cm 28 days post‐surgery), indicating restraining effect of the CARD (Figure 7E and Table 3). In swine, LVEDD analysis revealed both a significant interaction between time and treatment and a main effect of time. Post‐hoc testing showed a strong trend toward reduced LVEDD progression in the LAD+CARD group (3.12 ± 0.31 cm at baseline to 3.53 ± 0.12 cm 28 days post‐surgery) compared to the LAD group (2.87 ± 0.31 cm at baseline increasing to 3.970 ± 0.737 cm 28 days post‐surgery, p < 0.001). These findings support the potential of CARDs in mitigating cardiac remodeling over time (Figure 7F and Table 3). In rats, the LVEDD delta (the change in LVEDD from baseline) appeared lower in the LAD+CARD group throughout the study. Two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of treatment, with the LVEDD delta being significantly reduced in the LAD+CARD group by day 28 (1.61 ± 1.05 cm in LAD vs. 0.22 ± 0.84 cm in LAD+CARD, p < 0.05). This result highlights the long‐term efficacy of the intervention in mitigating adverse ventricular remodeling (Figure 7G and Table 3). In swine, an unpaired t‐test for LVEDD delta on day 28 showed a significant reduction in LVEDD delta in the LAD+CARD group compared to the LAD group (1.1 ± 0.41 cm in LAD vs. 0.42 ± 0.37 cm in LAD+CARD, p = 0.025) (Figure 7H and Table 3). These findings indicate that LV size remained largely unchanged in the LAD+CARD group, further supporting the CARD's protective effect against cardiac dilatation in HFrEF.

Histological and Macroscopic Assessment of CARD

Macroscopic tissue analysis revealed significant adhesions in the LAD+CARD group, as observed in Masson's trichrome‐stained sections from rats (Figure 8B, indicated by the white rim surrounding the tissue) and in TTC‐stained sections from pigs (Figure 8D). This fibrotic response to the CARD device was anticipated and is consistent with reactions observed in other elastic cardiac support systems. The research team is actively investigating strategies to prevent or minimize this response.

Conclusion

Treating advanced HF remains challenging despite the recent advancements in drug and device‐based therapies. [32]Advanced heart failure treatments such as ventricular assist devices and transplantation are effective in end‐stage HF, yet they suffer from high complication rates such as infection, thrombosis, and device malfunction, low availability and high cost. [33, 34, 35]In addition, a major limitation of many cardiac support devices developed for HFrEF is their lack of personalization to the patient's specific cardiac geometry and cardiac deformity. HFrEF manifests with variable patterns of left ventricular (LV) dilation, wall thinning, and regional deformation, which are not uniform across all patients. Consequently, devices designed without accounting for this heterogeneity have shown limited efficacy—often demonstrating only moderate improvement in preclinical studies, and in some cases, failing to translate success into clinical trials. [16, 36]This lack of treatment options triggers much interest in novel HF treatments.

In this study, we present a significant advancement, showcasing the successful 3D printing of a personalized polymeric device (CARD) that fits seamlessly to the hearts of both rat and swine models. The versatility of the molecular design of inks displaying the appropriate set of mechanical properties and of the 3D printing technology allowing the production of devices that can be scaled to fit different heart sizes, from small rat hearts to large swine hearts, offering isocompliance, precision and conformability. Key innovations of this device pertain to the custom‐made inks printed and to its personalized design based on the digital information of the cardiac anatomy of each patient using imaging techniques such as echocardiography and CT scans. This level of personalization has a critical beneficial effect on the preserved E/E′ ratio, indicating stable diastolic function. This represents a promising outcome, as it demonstrates that the device can provide mechanical support without causing further harm to the heart's function.

The most striking finding of this study, however, lies in the CARD's ability to be particularly beneficial for patients with HFrEF, where the heart's structure and function vary significantly from patient to patient due to disease progression and individual anatomical differences.

Our results showed that the CARD achieved precise anatomical fitting in both rat and swine models, with no device‐related mortality. Earlier devices, like soft robotic sleeves, caused substantial problems, most importantly, high mortality rates in mice within 7 days after MI, likely due to the strain imposed by the device mounted on the damaged heart, leading to ruptures [37].

A noteworthy potential drawback of cardiac restraint devices previously developed is diastolic dysfunction. Substantial engineering and design efforts were made in this research to prevent such consequences. Diastolic function in both rat and swine models was maintained with prevented increases in LVEDD, effectively halting left ventricular dilatation—a hallmark of HFrEF progression. This suggests that the device not only supports the failing myocardium mechanically but potentially also interrupts the vicious cycle of cardiac dilatation and dysfunction. By attenuating ventricular wall stress, the CARD acts as a stabilizing force, mitigating adverse remodeling and preserving cardiac integrity. The dual benefit of maintaining diastolic function while reversing pathological geometric changes paves the way for patient‐specific therapies.

This proof‐of‐concept study introduced 3D‐printed personalized CARDs, which represent a paradigm shift in cardiac repair devices, that provide a custom‐made solution for failing hearts. Already ongoing studies, to be published separately, focus on understanding the effect induced by the CARD on the heart at the cell and molecular biology levels. Furthermore, efforts are being devoted to optimizing the inks printed and extending implantation periods to better capture their therapeutic effects, paving the way for their clinical applications. This study focused on the events taking place during the acute post‐infarction 28‐day period. Future research will investigate long‐term processes and evaluate the potential effects of the CARD on diastolic function as well as improvements in systolic performance.
