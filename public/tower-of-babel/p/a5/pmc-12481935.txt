RAMM: A Robotic, Autonomous Magnetic field Mapper
Authors: Shinjer Li, Samuel J Rubin, Tyler Meldrum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate spatial mapping of magnetic fields is crucial for a range of scientific, industrial, and medical magnetic devices. Here, we present RAMM: a Robotic, Autonomous Magnetic field Mapper. RAMM consists of a delta-style 3D robot coupled with a three-axis Hall sensor that is able to measure magnetic fields accurately and at relatively low cost. In addition, RAMM is programmatically controlled via a Python interface, facilitating volumetric measurement of x, y, and z-components of magnetic fields ranging from the millitesla to single-digit Tesla range. We demonstrate the performance of RAMM, via detailed 3D-maps of the magnetic fields of several different sizes and arrangements of permanent magnets, and demonstrate agreement between measured and manufacturer-reported field gradient values. RAMM is easy to build, affordable, and suitable for teaching and research applications.Keywords: Magnetic field, Spatial mapping, Robot, Open-source

Hardware in context

High-precision magnetic instruments rely on carefully engineered magnetic fields to function effectively. Technologies such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) [1], [2] and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [3] require well defined magnetic fields to obtain detailed molecular and anatomical information. Even slight field aberrations can degrade performance. Achieving a precise target field can be done using supplemental correction or “shim” fields, but their correct implementation requires an accurate map of the primary magnetic field. Developing efficient, cost-effective, and flexible methods to map and characterize magnetic fields is essential to ensure that these instruments operate at peak performance.

There are several instruments available for measuring magnetic fields such as fluxgate magnetometers [4], SQUIDs (Superconducting Quantum Interference Devices) [5], magneto-optical sensors [6], [7], [8], and search coil magnetometers [9]. While these tools can offer high precision and sensitivity, they come with trade-offs. SQUIDs require cryogenic cooling and are expensive for most non-specialized applications. Fluxgate magnetometers, though more affordable than SQUIDs, are relatively bulky and require more sophisticated circuitry. In contrast, magneto-optical and search coil-based methods can be highly sensitive but are often complex to set up and less suitable for compact or low-budget projects.

Another device that can measure magnetic fields is a Hall sensor. Hall sensors function by leveraging the Hall effect [10], a phenomenon in which a magnetic field exerts a force on charge carriers, typically electrons or holes, within a current-carrying conductor. This force, known as the Lorentz force, causes the charge carriers to accumulate on one side of the material and creates a voltage difference perpendicular to both the current and the magnetic field. This voltage, called the Hall voltage, is directly proportional to the magnetic field strength and the applied current; it is trivial to quantify the strength of the magnetic field from the Hall voltage.

Hall sensors excel in situations where size, cost, and accessibility are important [10]. These sensors are compact, low-power, and relatively simple to integrate into systems. They provide real-time, direct measurements of magnetic field strengths and are widely available through commercial electronics suppliers. Hall sensors can reach sensitivities in the microtesla range, depending on the sensor design and operational conditions [11]. This sensitivity level of Hall sensors makes them ideal for applications where detecting general field strength or imbalances is sufficient, such as diagnosing coil asymmetries in electric motors or verifying magnetic shielding performance [12], [13]. When spatial resolution is important, such as in electrical transformers, these sensors help localize sources of electromagnetic interference and map magnetic field variations [14]. Spatial mapping is also valuable in applications like designing shim coils for open-geometry or benchtop NMR systems, where understanding field uniformity is critical. For these and other projects that do not require ultra-high sensitivity, Hall sensors provide a balance between low-cost and ease of use.

By way of comparison, a minimum field resolution on a high-field NMR is ∼0.1 ppm on a 400 MHz instrument, or approximately 1 µT; clinical imaging instruments can have even more stringent demands on field uniformity. These instruments require more sensitive field measurements than Hall sensors can provide. For example, fluxgate and magnetoresistive sensors can achieve sensitivities in the nanotesla range [15], while SQUIDs can reach even lower levels, down to 100pT [16].

We describe a method to spatially map magnetic fields using a Hall sensor mounted on a robotic system we have named RAMM: a Robotic, Autonomous Magnetic field Mapper. This cost-effective and versatile setup enables accurate magnetic field measurements and provides detailed spatial information with every measurement. Although other instruments offer higher-resolution and/or faster magnetic-field mapping, they tend to be more expensive [17], [18]. RAMM is a lower cost alternative that maintains good spatial accuracy, making it a versatile option for magnetic field analysis. Similar home-built instruments have been previously described; [19] however, we find that the ease of construction and calibration, the programmatic approach to collecting data, and the cost of materials are particularly well matched in RAMM. This instrument is also adaptable for users who may want to replicate, modify, or build on this work without needing heavily specialized equipment or training. This device would be well suited for an undergraduate laboratory or a robotics program, for example.

Our specific use case, NMR relaxometry, is an illustrative example of an application for which RAMM is well suited. Our instrument probes a region of space that is approximately 25 mm × 25 mm in area and 350 µm in height. Within this spatial region, the magnetic field produced is approximately 456.7 mT, causing hydrogen nuclei at the center of the target measurement region to precess at a frequency of 19.44 MHz. However, our magnetic field, produced by permanent magnets, exhibits an intentional and large field gradient that causes the range of magnetic field strengths within the target measurement range to vary by up to 10 mT (or, equivalently, 425 kHz proton frequency). These specifications are well matched to RAMM’s ∼150 µm and ∼1.5 mT resolution. Other component-level systems may also be well suited to RAMM’s field mapping specifications.

Hardware description

RAMM, depicted in Fig. 1, integrates a delta robot (Delta X 2) with a three-axis Hall sensor (MagVector MV2), operated via a microcontroller board (Arduino Uno). This design enhances flexibility by incorporating a detachable sensor head, extended via a ribbon cable, and securely mounted onto a 3D-printed support structure.

Complete RAMM setup: (a) the Delta X 2 robot, (b) the Hall sensor MV2 chip, (c) the robot’s frame, (d) the robot arm, and (e) the ribbon cable that connects the MV2 chip to the main Arduino board (located above the Delta X 2 robot and behind the frame). The two main components of the setup, the Delta X 2 robot and the MV2 chip, are outlined in yellow. The entire setup is secured onto a wooden panel (f).

This configuration is compact and precise. The MV2 chip, measuring 3 mm × 3 mm × 0.9 mm, incorporates three orthogonal Hall sensors (Bx, By, Bz) with a microscopic measurement volume of 200 µm × 200 µm × 5 µm and a mass of ∼25 mg.1 The chip supports measurements up to 30 T and supports both analog and digital interfaces to enable data acquisition with integrated analog-to-digital converters. In this work, we describe only the use of the digital interface.

The delta robot has a working space diameter of 320 mm with a working height that ranges from 118 mm to 220 mm, depending on the angular displacement from the central axis. The positioning is nominally accurate to approximately 0.15 mm. It operates at speeds of up to 0.8 m s-1 and can handle payloads up to 700 g, making it suitable for precise and dynamic applications. Additionally, the robot operates through a USB connection and requires no specialized computer interface or external power supply.

RAMM is significantly less costly than commercial spatial magnetic field sensors—these can cost tens of thousands of US dollars. In contrast, the Hall sensor used in this report is priced at approximately $900 USD per unit. While more expensive than other Hall sensors, the spatial colocalization of the Bx, By, and Bz sensors, along with a prefabricated circuit board to facilitate integration with a microcontroller, makes the MV2 sensor an attractive option for rapid fabrication. By integrating the Hall sensor with a robotic system that manually positions the sensor, researchers can substantially reduce costs associated with more expensive sensors and complex positioning mechanisms. The cost is sufficiently low and construction sufficiently straightforward that designing and constructing this instrument could be a teaching project for undergraduate students.

RAMM is highly customizable and adaptable. Its flexible architecture facilitates a simple integration process while also supporting future hardware upgrades. These can allow for automation, precision measurement, and complex magnetic instrument geometries where interchangeable components may be needed. Furthermore, since both components are controlled via an open-source software (Python), the entire data processing workflow from the Hall sensor remains customizable and adaptable. Build instructions

Increase separation between microcontroller board and Hall sensor

To increase flexibility and optimize the movement of the robotic system, the circuit board to which the Hall sensor was mounted at the neck was cut into two pieces; the kit from the manufacturer identifies the appropriate cut-line. This modification allows the Hall sensor to be mounted independently on the robot, eliminating the need to move the entire circuit board and the microcontroller board along with the Hall sensor, bringing them into proximity of the magnet.

The separation process was initiated by carefully cutting the circuit board using a rotary tool equipped with a circular blade. This procedure isolated the smaller section of the board that housed the Hall sensor chip. Each piece of the circuit board was fitted with a 16-pin mount/receptacle, and the two pieces of the circuit board were then connected using an approximately 30 cm ribbon cable. A 16-pin ribbon cable was chosen to match the digital outputs of the sensor and because it does not tangle easily. The ribbon cable was held in place with electrical tape to the nearest side arm of the robot (see Fig. 2).

The Hall sensor chip (a) was detached at (b) using a rotary tool and then reconnected to the larger board containing the Arduino (c) via a ribbon cable, allowing for more flexible positioning.

Calibration of the Hall sensor

To ensure accurate magnetic field measurements, the Hall sensor was calibrated against a magnetic field produced by a pair of Helmholtz coils. The purpose of this calibration was to establish a direct correlation between the raw sensor output (in arbitrary units) from the Hall sensor and the actual magnetic field strength measured in Tesla (T).

The calibration was performed at three different sensitivities: 100 mT, 300 mT and 1000 mT. These sensitivities were pre-programmed in the sensor’s settings; the sensitivity setting used for a given experiment was based on common field strengths encountered in our laboratory. The Hall sensor also includes a 3000 mT setting; this setting was not used. Firstly, by editing the manufacturer-provided *.xml files, the Hall sensor was configured to average 100 individual measurements into a single output value—this minimized variance in field strength measurements over time. Details on how these edits were made are available, see Table 1.

A pair of Helmholtz coils driven by a benchtop power supply generated a well defined magnetic field for calibration. A high-precision Gaussmeter was used to measure the actual field strength at the center of the coils, where the Hall sensor was also positioned. The Hall sensor and the Gaussmeter outputs were recorded at different field strengths, achieved by incrementally increasing the current through the coils. The current was adjusted in 0.05 A steps from -0.75 A to +0.75 A, producing fields from -3 mT to +3 mT.

Calibration curves were then generated for each sensitivity setting and for each axis of the Hall sensor. A least-squares regression analysis was used to calibrate sensor output to magnetic field strength. The final calibration conversions were implemented in the Python script to enable automatic conversion of Hall sensor readings into mT. An example calibration curve is provided as Fig. 3; detailed calibration values are provided in Table 3.

Securing robot

To improve positioning accuracy during magnetic field measurements, the Delta X 2 robot was constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and its frame was mounted to a flat wooden panel. A 1 in-spaced grid (2.54 cm) was then traced across the entire surface of the panel, and the center point for the robot’s “home” position was marked. This allowed the panel to serve as a stable base and alignment tool. While this step is not necessary for operation, it is helpful when positioning and aligning the magnetic field of interest, especially when working with smaller magnetic field sources (see Fig. 4).

The robot’s frame was secured to the wooden panel with wood screws to improve stability and reproducibility. Grid lines were drawn on the wooden panel at 1 in spacings.

Another practical advantage of using the gridded wooden panel is the ability to mark specific magnetic field positions based on previous experiments. By recording and labeling past measurement points directly on the panel, we can quickly and reliably replicate prior experimental conditions. This reduces setup time, increases consistency between trials, and supports more accurate comparisons between data sets.

Mounting the Hall sensor on the robot

With the Hall sensor properly calibrated, it was then mounted to the robotic system. The arms of the Delta X 2 robot are configured to accommodate mounts for different objects, such as a pencil or a 3D printer extruder nozzle—we designed and produced a two-part mount to accommodate the Hall sensor instead. (See Table 1). The smaller part of the circuit board (the portion containing the Hall sensor) was secured to the bottom of the robot’s arms using this 3D-printed mount. The two parts of the mount were secured around the ribbon cable connector using M4 machine screws, and the entire assembly was secured to the robot arms.

We mounted the Hall sensor and its accompanying circuit board in a “portrait” orientation. This allowed the Hall sensor itself to get as close to the magnet in the z-dimension as possible. It also mitigates drift of the sensor from the robot’s nominal z-position due to a long extension. Other usages may vary and the user should check that the Hall sensor’s Bx, By, and Bz output match the expected dimensions. A photograph of this setup is shown in Fig. 5.

The Hall sensor (a) is attached to the robot’s mount (b) by positioning the ribbon cable’s connection point (c) between the two red 3D-printed casings (d). It is fastened in place with machine screws (f). The ribbon cable (e) is secured to the robot’s arms with tape.

The Arduino and the large portion of the circuit board for the Hall sensor were mounted on top of the delta robot’s housing, while a ribbon cable connected the smaller portion of the board and the Hall sensor. The ribbon cable was routed carefully along the robot’s hanging arms and secured using electrical tape.

Interfacing the Hall sensor and robot with the computer

The final step in the integration process was ensuring that the robot and Hall sensor functioned properly with the software. A custom Python script was developed to interface with both the Arduino-controlled robot and the Hall sensor system. This facilitated the synchronization of the robot (spatial coordinates) and the Hall sensor (magnetic field components).

Communication between the computer and the Arduino board was established via serial connection over USB using the PySerial library. The robot was controlled by sending G-code commands through the serial interface to define precise 3D movements within a user-defined grid. The scan area and step size (resolution) in all three axes are adjustable in the script to allow flexibility for different experimental setups.

Before running a full measurement, the script executes a preliminary test path to verify the input boundaries and motion parameters. It prompts the user for confirmation before proceeding with the full scan. To help the user visualize the scan layout and expected duration, the script generates a 3D scatter plot of all planned measurement points and provides an estimated time for the entire procedure.

The Hall sensor, connected to a PC running Windows 11 via a COM port, is activated at each spatial coordinate by running an external executable (MV2Host.exe, provided by the MV2 manufacturer) with a specified configuration file (*.xml). The program returns raw Hall sensor data, which are then converted to field strength in millitesla using the calibration curves obtained in Step 5.2.

For each specified spatial coordinate, the robot moves to the location defined by the G-code and pauses briefly. During this pause, the Hall sensor measures the magnetic field. An output *.txt file is generated that records the robot’s spatial location (x, y, z) and all three magnetic field components (Bx, By, Bz). The sensor additionally records a raw-format for the temperature, though that was not used in our study.

Operation instructions

Positioning the sample of interest

Before initiating any scans, accurately position and center the sample/magnet of interest beneath the mounted Hall sensor. Depending on how the wooden panel is marked, the grid lines from Step 5.3 may help with alignment. Exact positioning of the magnet is not critical at this stage because adjustments can be made later in Step 6.3 and during data processing.

Configuring device connections

Access the Device Manager on the Windows-based PC to verify that both the Delta X 2 robot and the Arduino are properly connected: each device should be assigned to a different port. Ensure that the port assignments are correctly inputted in the main.py script before proceeding. Incorrect port settings will prevent communication between the computer and the devices and lead to operational failures.

Dimension measurement

Launch the Delta X software provided with the Delta X 2 robot. Follow the instructions provided with the purchase of the robot. Use this program to lower the robot arms and manually adjust the magnet’s position if necessary from Step 6.1. Precise centering is not critical at this stage since adjustments can be made during data processing. However, ensuring a reasonable initial alignment will streamline subsequent steps.

Once the field of interest is properly centered, determine the dimensions of the scanning area. It is advised to perform a quick sweep across the magnetic field region of interest to confirm there is sufficient clearance between the Hall sensor chip’s board and the magnet. Any potential collision may damage the sensor and impact measurements. Importantly, after noting the dimensions, disconnect the robot from the Delta X program using the appropriate software button before proceeding.

Validation and characterization

To validate our magnetic field mapping methodology, we applied it to three configurations of increasing field strength, shown in Fig. 6: (1) a single NdFeB magnet, (2) an array of eight small NdFeB magnets, and (3) the PM5 single-sided NMR magnet (an array of four large NdFeB magnets). These configurations, labeled single_magnet, square_array, and PM5, respectively, allowed us to assess how field and spatial sensitivity evolves across different magnet assemblies. Identical visualization techniques were used to facilitate direct comparison between these systems with slightly varying scan parameters.

Images of magnets: (a) a single 0.5 in × 0.5 in × 0.25 in (1.3 cm × 1.3 cm × 0.64 cm) NdFeB magnet, (b) an array of eight NdFeB magnets from (a), arranged pairwise in a 2 × 2 array, and (c) the PM5 single-sided NMR magnet, consisting of four large NdFeB magnets.

In the single_magnet configuration, we scanned a single NdFeB magnet that is 0.5 in × 0.5 in × 0.25 in (1.3 cm × 1.3 cm × 0.64 cm). The scan range extended from −7mm to +7mm in both the x and y directions and measurements were sampled at 0.5 mm intervals. Along the z-axis, measurements were taken from −395.5mm to −385.5mm, also at 0.5 mm resolution. With each measurement taking ∼2 s, the full scan required approximately 10 h to complete.

The square_array setup consisted of eight of NdFeB magnets in single_magnet, but now arranged pairwise in a 2 × 2 grid housed in a 3D-printed frame (see Fig. 6b). The scan covered a larger area: from -20 mm to +20 mm in x and y, sampled at 1 mm steps, and from -389.0 mm to -384.0 mm in z, also at 1 mm intervals. This led to a total scan time of approximately 6 h.

Additionally, a detailed 3D map of the magnetic field was measured across the perimeter of the PM5. This provided the scan boundaries of the x-axis from -63.0 mm to +61.5 mm, and the y-axis from -52.0 mm to +51.5 mm, both with a step size of 1.5 mm. Typical usage of the PM5 magnet in our lab requires that our sample is located several millimeters above the magnet surface. Consequently, we measured the magnetic field of this magnet array with an emphasis on our particular region of interest. Along the z-axis, we collected data across seven layers ranging from -322.5 mm to -319.5 mm with a finer 0.5 mm step size. These step sizes in the xy-plane and z-axis were chosen to obtain a balance between spatial resolution and total scan duration, which was constrained to approximately 24 h for the full scan.

The analysis of the magnetic field measurements was done using NumPy for numerical operations and Matplotlib for data visualization. For three-dimensional plotting and spatial representation of the field data, the Axes3D module from the mpl_toolkits. mplot3d package within Matplotlib was employed to visualize the magnetic field across all scanned layers.

The resulting field data were visualized as heat maps representing the three orthogonal magnetic field components (Bx, By, and Bz) at each z-layer. Conclusion

RAMM, a delta robot with an integrated Hall sensor, was successfully developed and is able to generate spatial mappings of various magnetic fields. Each map was further decomposed into its individual components, Bx, By, and Bz components, in addition to the magnetic field magnitude, Bmagnitude. This experimental setup serves as a robust foundation for enhancing field homogeneity through targeted shimming or for advancing future studies into magnetic field behavior. Notably, RAMM is significantly lower in cost than commercial alternatives.

This approach has several limitations affecting measurement efficiency and accuracy. The main bottleneck is time: each measurement takes approximately 2 s. The time to average 100 readings for a one measurement is a small fraction of that time; most of it comes from the time to initiate a connection with the computer. Robot movement between measurement points is also negligible in comparison. To improve efficiency, implementing a finer step size in the central 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm sensitive region and a coarser step size in the surrounding regions would reduce the scan time.

Measurement uncertainty also stems from the Delta X 2’s positioning resolution (∼0.15 mm), Hall sensor calibration accuracy (∼1.5 mT per digital point), and residual noise persisting despite scan averaging. Hardware upgrades, such as newer delta-style robots with finer resolution (∼0.05 mm [22]) or higher-precision Hall sensors, could attenuate these errors. The modular architecture of the system facilitates easy hardware replacement, if needed. We also caution users in terms of thermal stability: the MV2 chip is rated for a temperature range of -40 °C to +125 °C, with a manufacturer-reported temperature drift of 0.15% of the full field range per degree Celsius; calibrated temperature compensation, though not used in this study, is a feature of the MV2 chip. Users should consider whether their use case necessitates such temperature correction. Furthermore, changes in the magnetic susceptibility near the measurement region, potentially caused by large and moving metal objects (e.g., construction vehicles, elevators) may impact the measured fields. Again, our laboratory environment was spatially isolated from these effects, but the user should exercise caution.

Geometric constraints currently limit RAMM’s versatility. The current G-code for the robot uses Cartesian trajectories to measure fields, which makes it less suitable for circular or spherical fields and objects. Additionally, measurements beyond or above the frame of the robot are not feasible. However, the system described here is remarkably flexible: a robot with greater spatial range and/or dimensional flexibility, such as a multi-axis arm or mobile platform, could overcome these limitations.
