Divalent aptamer-mediated clustering for extracellular vesicle separation
Authors: Xiaoling Liu, Taiyi Zhang, Qi Zhang, Biying Yan, Bin Liu, Yaxuan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are important mediators of intercellular communication and promising sources of diagnostic and therapeutic biomarkers, yet effective EV isolation remains challenging due to trade-offs among yield, purity, and adaptability across biofluids. Here, we introduce divalent aptamer-mediated clustering (DAC), a streamlined affinity-based EV isolation strategy that induces controllable vesicle clustering and enables recovery by standard filtration. By exploiting multivalent aptamer binding to EV surface markers, DAC converts nanoscale vesicles into micron-scale clusters while preserving EV integrity and biological activity. We demonstrate robust EV isolation from plasma, urine, and cell culture media, and benchmark DAC against ultracentrifugation, density gradient ultracentrifugation, and size-exclusion chromatography. DAC achieves comparable or improved EV yield and purity with reduced processing time, cost, and operational complexity. Proteomic and metabolomic analyses show that DAC isolates affinity-defined EV subpopulations with cargo profiles distinct from those obtained using conventional methods. Moreover, DAC is readily adapted to alternative EV targets, exemplified by enrichment of EpCAM-positive EVs. Together, DAC provides a versatile and accessible platform for studying EV heterogeneity, function, and molecular composition.Subject terms: Nanoparticles, Biochemistry

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound structures naturally released by all known cell types. EVs carry a variety of molecular cargoes and facilitate intercellular communication. Advancing EV research depends on improvements in isolation methods; however, due to their nanoscale size and the lack of universal surface markers, an ideal method that achieves optimal recovery, purity, adaptability, and accessibility has yet to be established1,2. Differential ultracentrifugation (UC) is widely used in laboratories to separate EVs based on size and density, with large EVs and small EVs collected at differing spin rates. While effective for handling bulky samples such as cell culture medium, UC may be less efficient in recovering EVs from more complex biofluids. Density gradient (DG) ultracentrifugation offers notably improved EV purity but depends on hands-on experience and often suffers from limited reproducibility due to the cumbersome gradient setup and collection. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) provides a satisfactory yield and accessibility when processing plasma or other biofluids with small volumes. However, the dilution of collected EV fractions can limit continuous downstream analysis in certain cases.

Apart from physical characteristic-dependent isolation methods, affinity-based approaches have been widely adopted, where EVs are captured by a coated matrix and then recovered through elution. These methods purify subgroups of EVs that share common surface molecules, such as tetraspanins (e.g., CD63 and CD81), phosphatidylserine, or more tissue-specific markers like EpCAM and L1CAM3,4. Antibodies are commonly used for EV recognition and capture, but high material cost and lot-to-lot variation can hinder their up-scaling and potential clinical applications. Aptamers are single-stranded oligonucleotides that are artificially developed to bind target molecules through their three-dimensional folding conformation. Aptamer sequences are mainly identified using a procedure known as systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX), which involves multiple rounds of target-binding selection5,6. The process begins with the construction of a random oligonucleotide library, followed by iterative cycles of target binding, separation, and amplification of the bound candidates. While the original SELEX technique provides a foundational approach for screening aptamers with high affinity toward specific targets, several enhanced SELEX variants, such as capillary electrophoresis SELEX (CE-SELEX)7, cell-based SELEX (cell-SELEX)8, and capture-SELEX9, have been developed to improve screening efficiency and aptamer performance. Candidate sequences identified through SELEX are typically subjected to post-selection validation, including evaluation of binding affinity (Kd), sensitivity, specificity, and predicted secondary structure10.

As alternatives to antibodies, aptamers are easily modifiable, stable through freeze-thaw cycles, and relatively low in cost, making them increasingly favored for biosensor design and imaging applications in clinical settings11–13. Additional features such as non-immunogenicity and low toxicity further support their use in drug development and therapeutic applications14,15. Recently, several aptamer sequences with high affinity and specificity for EV-associated membrane proteins have been identified, contributing to rapid advances in EV research. For example, modified aptamers conjugated with functional modules can bind to EVs and initiate signal transduction and amplification, thereby enabling sensitive EV detection and quantification. Such applications have been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere16. In contrast to the rapid progress in EV detection technologies, the development of innovative EV isolation strategies has been comparatively limited and remains an ongoing challenge. Recently, Tang et al. introduced an approach utilizing a polyvalent DNA chain containing repeated CD63-binding aptamer sequences for EV capture. This aptamer chain self-assembled into a hydrogel sediment, from which EVs could be effectively retrieved17. However, the lattice-like DNA hydrogel structure likely co-precipitated a substantial amount of non-EV contaminants.

In this study, we developed a highly efficient and streamlined affinity-based method for EV separation, introducing a concept in which EVs can be clustered into oligomers of significantly increased size by divalent aptamers, thereby enabling their recovery through standard filtration techniques. Di- and multivalent aptamers have been previously explored in biosensor and therapeutic designs due to their enhanced avidity and elevated local concentration at the target site18. Here, we report the design of divalent aptamers specifically applied to EV isolation, termed Divalent Aptamer-mediated Clustering (DAC). We demonstrate that this method, with its simplified protocol, is effective for isolating EVs from various biofluids and cell culture media. By integrating experimental measurements with a diffusion-limited clustering model, we elucidate the key parameters governing EV clustering behavior and establish practical guidelines for aptamer usage across sample types. We further benchmark DAC against widely used methods, including UC, DG, and SEC, and show that DAC achieves superior or comparable performance in EV recovery and purity while offering advantages in simplicity, reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness. Importantly, we show that DAC preserves EV structural integrity and biological activity, supporting its use in downstream functional studies. Finally, by replacing the CD63-binding sequence with an EpCAM-targeting aptamer, we demonstrate the adaptability of the DAC platform for isolating distinct EV subpopulations, highlighting its potential utility for EV subtyping and biomarker-oriented investigations. Together, these results establish DAC as a versatile and accessible EV isolation strategy that complements existing methodologies and expands the toolkit available for EV research.

Results

Divalent aptamer pairs build EV clusters with enlarged size

Aptamers targeting several EV-associated surface proteins, such as CD63, EpCAM, MUC-1, CEA, PDGF, and HER2, have been identified through the SELEX approach, and their binding affinities have been validated across various EV studies19–22. Among these, CD63 is broadly and stably expressed on the surface of EVs across diverse cell types and physiological conditions; thus, in this proof-of-concept study, we use a CD63-binding sequence to isolate CD63+ EVs as a representative general EV population. A pair of modified aptamers, aptX and aptY, was designed with each comprising a CD63-binding module and a complementary spacer sequence. The CD63 binding sequence (aptCD63) was adapted from multiple published studies21,23, and a spacer sequence of 10 nucleotides was conjugated to the 3’ end of aptCD63 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1, sequences provided in Table S1). Upon spacer hybridization, the paired construct with dual functional binding sites (divalency) independently binds to EV surface CD63 epitopes, forming enlarged oligomeric assemblies. Based on this design, the DAC method is implemented as follows (Fig. 1B). In Step I, the sample is pretreated with 0.22 μm filtration, and the flow-through is divided into two parts, with each part incubated separately with either aptX or aptY. The aptamers bind to EV surface epitopes via the CD63-binding module with their spacer sequences extending outward as sticky ends. In Step II, aptX-EVs and aptY-EVs are mixed, and the hybridization of the spacer sequences initiates the assembly of EV clusters. In Step III, the clustered EVs are recovered through 200 nm filtration.

Considering that sequence modifications could disrupt the secondary structure of aptCD63 and compromise its binding affinity, we first validated the CD63-binding functionality of aptX and aptY through fluorescence co-localization (Fig. 1C). Then, to ensure that spacer motifs were appropriately exposed for hybridization, we conducted an isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay on aptX/Y and control sequences aptScr1/aptScr2, where the spacers of the same length were scrambled and non-complementary (Supplementary Fig. 1). A significant enthalpy change (ΔH) indicated the correct orientation of aptX/Y spacers for interaction (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2). Further validation of aptX/Y hybridization was achieved via agarose gel electrophoresis, where the paired aptX-aptY, compared to individual species, displayed a single band with doubled intensity and altered running distance (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Next, we examined the capacity of aptX/Y to catalyze EV clustering. Purified EVs were incubated with an excess of aptX or aptY to saturate the CD63 binding sites, and aptamer-bound EVs were recovered from 100,000×g pellet to remove extra free oligonucleotides that could interfere with the following clustering process. We employed dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis to monitor the cluster formation during titration of aptX-EVs (1E9 total particles) against aptY-EVs (at an equal concentration) (Fig. 1E). Starting amount of aptY-EVs (1.7E8 and 3.3E8) did not alter the particle size distribution, but the addition of more than 5E8 aptY-EVs (X:Y = 2:1) substantially increased the particle sizes to a range of 300-800 nm (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 4). The polydispersity index (PDI) declined along with aptY-EV addition, indicating a transition from a highly heterogeneous population of single vesicles to more uniformly sized EV clusters (Fig. 1G). This EV clustering and size alteration was absent in the scrambled controls (Supplementary Fig. 5). The EV clustering process was reproducible under varying EV concentrations, incubation times, or with different aptX/Y spacer sequences (Figs. S6 and 7). DAC-induced EV clustering was further evidenced by fluorescence and electron microscopy imaging (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, when aptX-EVs and aptY-EVs were dual-stained with distinct dyes, the resulting clusters exhibited blended spectra, confirming that the clustering depends on the pairing of aptX with aptY (Fig. 1J). These results demonstrate that the divalent aptamer pairs were effectively implemented to build EV clusters, through which the significantly increased sizes were able to enhance the feasibility of EV separation.

Modeling and experiments suggest aptX/Y dosage for efficient EV recovery

It is important to note that the quantity of aptX/Y is critical in regulating cluster formation. EV linkage depends on the sticky spacer sequences, but excess free aptX/Ys in the reaction can hybridize with EV-bound counterparts, reducing the number of available sticky ends for clustering. An EV with fewer than two available sticky ends cannot initiate cluster growth, while an increase in the number of sticky ends may affect the final cluster size distribution. This, in turn, influences the overall EV recovery. As mentioned, the number of sticky ends is a regulatory determinant in tuning cluster formation and the eventual EV recovery. To clarify their relationship, we established a modified binary diffusion-limited cluster aggregation (DLCA) model to simulate the DAC clustering process. The DLCA model describes aggregation processes in which particles undergo Brownian diffusion and irreversibly adhere upon contact. Under diffusion-limited conditions, the aggregation rate is governed primarily by particle diffusion rather than by reaction kinetics, leading to the formation of fractal-like clusters. As clusters grow, their diffusion coefficients decrease, which progressively slows further aggregation and naturally limits cluster size24,25. This model aligns well with our clustering system, where EVs diffuse freely and attach through sticky end hybridization upon encountering each other. In the simulation, particles of unlike types (i.e., aptX-EVs and aptY-EVs) attached, forming oligomer clusters over time (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 9, see Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 10 for simulation details). The observed time evolution of the cluster-size distribution was consistent with the analytical solutions of the Smoluchowski coagulation equation (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 11, and Note 2). We ran DAC simulations for various numbers of sticky ends (S∈[1,8]) and plotted the total particle number for monomers, dimers, trimers, or higher-order oligomers (≥4-mers) as a function of time (Fig. 2D). Particles forming high-order oligomers are considered the recovered population in DAC separation, and the time required to achieve a 75% recovery rate was used to evaluate efficiency at discrete S values (Fig. 2E). As expected, a system with one sticky site per particle (S=1) produced predominantly dimers. With two sticky ends per particle (S=2), linear oligomers formed at a slow rate, with recovery not reaching 75% by the end of the simulation. As S increased beyond three (S≥3), branched high-order oligomers formed more efficiently, and the influence of sticky end number on recovery efficiency tended to diminish. Based on these findings, we propose a criterion of S≥3 for effective DAC separation, which, according to the piecewise function (1), corresponds to an aptX/Y quantity within the range 3N0,(2m−3)N0.

To test the modeling results, we conducted DAC EV separation using a range of aptX/Y input quantities across various sample types (see Note 3 for the estimation of N0 and m, along with corresponding aptamer calculations). Urine EV isolated under different aptX/Y inputs was analyzed for several EV protein biomarkers via Western blots. By examining CD63 expression from equal volumes of EV isolates, we observed the highest EV recovery with the addition of 2E10 aptX/Ys, which aligns well with the modeling prediction (Fig. 2F, top panel). Reaction with more aptamers (2E11 or 2E12) still yielded EV recovery, yet with a decline that was anticipated based on the calculation. In contrast, the reaction with 2E9 aptX/Ys failed to retrieve relevant results, likely due to an insufficient number of sticky ends as suggested by the model. Other EV markers, ALIX and flotillin-1, followed a similar trend with the exception of CD9, which may reflect known heterogeneity in tetraspanin expression across EV subfamilies. EVs isolated using 2E10 aptX/Ys were analyzed for size distribution by NTA and for morphology by TEM (Fig. 2F, lower panel). To obtain a single-vesicle suspension for NTA, DNase I treatment was applied to disrupt aptamer-mediated EV clusters. Without DNase I, thorough pipetting alone was insufficient to fully dissociate the clusters, resulting in a broader size distribution extending into the 200–400 nm range (Supplementary Fig. 12). Plasma EV isolated with dilutions of aptamer dosage was evaluated for CD63 and flotillin-1 levels. By loading equal volumes, the most abundant expression of the two marker proteins was observed at a dose of 2E10 molecules, within the estimated range considering the m variance (Fig. 2G, top panel). Higher or lower aptX/Ys resulted in reduced EV recovery efficiency, likely because the quantity of aptX/Y fell outside the optimal range, producing an average of fewer than three sticky ends per vesicle. The size profile and morphology of EVs isolated under the optimal aptamer dosage were confirmed accordingly (Fig. 2G, lower panel). EVs isolated from serum-free cell culture medium (SF-CM) under different aptX/Y input quantities were analyzed by Western blots. Using equal volumes, lower dosages (2E9 or 2E10) demonstrated a better performance in SF-CM, whereas higher doses (2E12 or 2E11) led to decreased retrievals, as evidenced by CD63 expression and the consistent profile of flotillin-1 (Fig. 2H, top panel). These results may be ascribed to the lower EV abundance in the SF-CM compared to the urine of the same volume. We further validated the size distribution and morphology of SF-CM EVs via NTA and TEM, respectively (Fig. 2H, lower panel). From the above results, we determined the optimal aptX/Y quantities to achieve the maximized DAC efficiency for specific volumes of urine, plasma, and SF-CM. Thus, in practice, DAC dosage selection does not require direct measurement of N0 and m for each individual sample, but can be guided by sample type–adapted reference ranges validated experimentally.

To substantiate the DAC efficacy, we performed EV separation at the optimized aptX/Y dosage on an expanded sample set. Midstream urine was collected from 9 healthy volunteers, and using DAC, 4.5 ml of urine yielded 2.20 ± 0.23E9 particles of EV with a mode size of 128 nm as determined by NTA analysis (Fig. 3A, top panel; representative TEM image shown in inset). Western blot analysis with equal protein amount confirmed the presence of CD63, ALIX, flotillin-1, and CD81 in EV isolates from six donors (Fig. 3A, lower panel). Blood was collected from 8 volunteers without recognized diseases, and plasma was separated through standard protocol. A portion of 20 µl plasma paired with 2E10 molecules of aptX/Y finally yielded a total of 1.17 ± 0.12E9 vesicles with an average mode size of 81 nm (Fig. 3B, top panel; representative TEM image shown in inset). Western blot analysis loaded by equal protein level confirmed the efficacy of EV isolation across individuals (Fig. 3B, lower panel). SF-CM from six independent HEK293T cultures were processed with DAC for EV isolation. On average, we recovered 8.22 ± 1.80E8 particles of EV from 4.5 ml of SF-CM with the particle mode size of 123 nm (Fig. 3C, top panel; representative TEM image shown in inset). The presence of EV markers (ALIX, CD63, flotillin-1, and CD81) was validated across all replicates by Western blot with equal protein loading (Fig. 3C, lower panel). In addition, we evaluated the EV isolation efficacy of DAC on tear fluid, and confirmed the recovered particle counts along with their presence of EV biomarkers (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, the DAC method was optimized for varying spacer lengths of aptX/Y and reaction temperatures (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). Based on these experiments, we summarized EV estimates and key measurements and defined optimal aptX/Y quantities for different sample types. These recommendations are consolidated in Table 1 and provide practical guidance for aptamer usage across diverse biological samples.

Beyond dosage optimization, we further assessed EV structural integrity using in situ SEM imaging. Following 200 nm filtration and buffer washing, the EV cluster-retained membrane was collected, lyophilized, and directly visualized. SEM images revealed intact, efficiently clustered vesicles attached to the polycarbonate membrane, whereas only minimal EV retention was observed in the absence of aptX/Y, supporting the effectiveness and specificity of DAC-mediated clustering (Fig. 3D).

In summary, pairing aptX and aptY enables EVs to be efficiently clustered into substantially larger assemblies, thereby facilitating effective recovery from diverse sample types while preserving vesicular structure and morphology. When combined with sample type-adapted aptX/Y dosages, DAC provides a streamlined EV isolation strategy that expands the available methodological toolkit.

DAC outperforms major existing methods of EV isolation

We next conducted a comprehensive evaluation of DAC performance against UC, DG, and SEC, which are all widely recognized in the field. Protocols for UC, DG, and SEC were adapted from the MISEV2013 guidelines and are illustrated in the flowchart (Fig. 4A)26. The comparison of EV yield across methods was first performed using identical volumes of plasma (20 µl). According to NTA analysis, DAC produced a comparable plasma EV yield to SEC in terms of total particle number (9.08 ± 2.66E8 versus 11.74 ± 4.82E8), with both far surpassing the yields from UC and DG (Fig. 4B). Plasma EVs isolated by SEC showed variability, probably due to dilution in flow-through fractions. The vesicle size distributions across all methods were consistent, with mode values between 70 and 130 nm as the characteristic of small EV populations (Supplementary Fig. 16A). These results were further validated by Western blot using equal volume loading, where the expression levels of EV biomarkers including ALIX, CD63, flotillin-1, and CD81 were consistent with the NTA measurements (Fig. 4C; quantification shown in bar graphs). The EV yield comparison was then extended to equal volume of SF-CM (4.5 ml), where DAC recovered 9.52 ± 0.90E8 EVs, making it the most effective isolation method based on NTA analysis (Fig. 4D). UC recovered minimal EVs from 4.5 ml of medium, and DG yielded undetectable material according to NTA analysis. Vesicle sizes in SF-CM-EV samples were similar across methods, with the exception of DG showing under-detection (Supplementary Fig. 16B). Western blot analysis by equal volume revealed the highest expression of CD63, flotillin-1, CD81, and ALIX in DAC-isolated EVs (Fig. 4E; quantification shown in bar graphs). SEC and UC produced comparable or less EV yields relative to DAC, while DG showed the least efficiency in EV recovery, as expected. Together, EV isolation from both plasma and SF-CM demonstrated DAC’s superior yield and the capability to process minimal sample volumes effectively.

EV purity across different methods was first assessed using plasma, a particularly challenging biological sample for EV purification. Following the described protocols, EVs were separated using UC, DG, SEC, and DAC, and analyzed by Western blot with equal loading of total protein (15 µg) (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 18). Higher expression levels of EV markers (CD63, flotillin-1, CD81, and ALIX) were observed in EVs isolated by DAC and DG, indicating the high purity achieved by these methods. In contrast, SEC consistently showed low expression of these markers under equal protein amount, highlighting potential purity issues associated with SEC-based plasma EV isolation. To assess contamination, ApoA1, a major component of high-density lipoproteins, and albumin, the most abundant plasma protein, were probed, and both showed lower presence in DAC- and DG-isolated EVs, whereas higher levels were observed in SEC- or UC-derived EVs. EV purity across methods was subsequently evaluated using SF-CM. Similarly, EVs isolated from HEK293T culture supernatant by each method were analyzed by Western blot with equal protein loading, and probed for EV markers, as well as the potential contaminant protein, calnexin (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. 18). Consistent with the results from plasma samples, DAC and DG demonstrated higher EV purity as indicated by stronger bands for EV markers including CD63, flotillin-1, CD81, and ALIX. Calnexin was undetectable in EVs isolated by all methods. EV purity was further evaluated using the particle-to-protein mass ratio27. Using a normal plasma sample, EVs isolated by DAC exhibited higher ratios than those recovered by other methods, consistent with the higher purity indicated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 4H). Together, these findings support the high purity and low contamination of EVs recovered using DAC compared to other widely used isolation techniques.

We generated a radar chart to visualize key features of DAC and the other methods (Fig. 4I). Yield scores were calculated based on both NTA data and CD63 band intensities from equal volumes of plasma (Fig. 4B, C) and SF-CM (Fig. 4D, E), with each contributing 25% to the final score. Specifically, the highest value, whether particle count or CD63 intensity, was set to 25 points, and the others were normalized accordingly. The resulting yield scores followed the order: DAC > SEC > UC > DG. Purity was assessed by Western blot with equal protein loading (Fig. 4F, G). The ratio of CD63 to ApoA1 in plasma EVs accounted for 50% of the final score, while the CD63 intensity in SF-CM EVs (in the absence of detectable calnexin) contributed the remaining 50%. Purity scores followed the order: DG ≥ DAC > UC > SEC. Repeatability scores were based on standard deviations (SD) from three biological replicates of NTA measurements for plasma EVs (Fig. 4B). These SDs were converted to reciprocals and normalized to yield final scores in the order: DG > UC > DAC > SEC. Time efficiency was scored based on estimated processing durations for each method, including centrifugation, gradient preparation, incubation, and elution steps. These durations were converted to reciprocal values, normalized, and ranked as: DAC > SEC > UC > DG. Cost efficiency scores were calculated based on the estimated expenses for instruments and consumables, and followed the order: DAC > SEC > UC > DG. Numerical data are provided in the Methods section and Supplementary Data 2. In summary, DAC offers a strong balance of high yield and purity, combined with a simplified protocol that requires no specialized equipment or costly materials. These advantages position DAC as a highly competitive and promising method for EV isolation.

DAC-isolated EVs represent a distinct subpopulation from UC- and SEC-isolated EVs

Next, we investigated the cargo profiles of DAC-derived EVs, hypothesizing that affinity-based DAC isolation would enrich an EV subpopulation distinct from those obtained using conventional size- or density-based methods. Proteomic profiling was performed on EVs isolated from the same healthy plasma sample using DAC, UC, and SEC. EVs normalized to equal protein input were analyzed by ion mobility-enhanced mass spectrometry combined with a data-independent acquisition (DIA) strategy. Among the 629 proteins identified in DAC-isolated EVs, 65 proteins were uniquely detected compared with UC- and SEC-derived EVs (Fig. 5A). Remarkably, these DAC-unique proteins were strongly enriched in EV-relevant Gene Ontology (GO) categories, including “extracellular exosome”, “extracellular space”, “extracellular region”, and “vesicle”. In contrast, such enrichment was not observed among proteins uniquely detected in UC- or SEC-isolated EVs (Fig. 5B).

For proteins commonly identified across methods, DAC-derived EVs exhibited substantial quantitative differences. Specifically, when compared with UC-derived EVs, 343 proteins were differentially expressed while 206 showed similar abundance levels; similarly, relative to SEC-derived EVs, 248 proteins were differentially expressed and 258 exhibited comparable expression levels (Fig. 5C). Together, these findings indicate that DAC enriches an EV subpopulation with a proteomic profile distinct from those isolated by UC or SEC.

We further examined whether such distinctions extended to small-molecule cargo by performing metabolomic analysis on EVs isolated in parallel from a healthy urine sample using DAC, UC, and SEC. EVs normalized by particle number were subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) mass spectrometry (UHPLC–Q-TOF MS). Integrated analysis across both positive and negative ion modes identified 248 compounds from DAC-derived EVs and a comparable number of features from UC or SEC-derived EVs (Fig. 5D). Although the majority of identified molecules (209 out of 248) were common across all methods, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation of the DAC EV metabolome from those obtained by UC and SEC (Fig. 5E). Volcano plots highlighted the compounds most responsible for distinguishing DAC from UC and SEC, with pathway enrichment analysis linking these distinctions to fatty acid oxidation and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 19).

In summary, combined quantitative proteomic and metabolomic analyses demonstrate that DAC selectively enriches CD63-positive EVs with cargo compositions distinct from the size- or density-defined EV populations isolated by UC or SEC. Given the increasing recognition of EV heterogeneity and subpopulation-specific functions, the ability of DAC to isolate affinity-defined EV subsets provides a powerful approach for precise EV characterization, functional studies, and biomarker discovery.

DAC-isolated EVs preserve biological functions

We next evaluated whether EVs isolated by DAC retain biological activity after clustering and subsequent dissociation. DAC-isolated EVs derived from HEK293T cell culture medium were first treated with DNase I to dissociate EV clusters and then labeled with the lipophilic fluorescent dye DiO. Excess free dye was removed by an additional ultracentrifugation step. In parallel, a dye-only control underwent the same ultracentrifugation procedure to exclude the possibility of free dye sedimentation. DiO-labeled EVs or dye-only controls were subsequently added to recipient cells. Cellular membranes were counterstained with DiI, and nuclei were labeled with Hoechst. Live-cell confocal fluorescence imaging performed 5 h after incubation revealed clear intracellular DiO signals in EV-treated cells, whereas no comparable signal was observed in the dye-only control (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate efficient cellular uptake of DAC-isolated EVs and confirm that the observed fluorescence originates from EV internalization rather than nonspecific dye carryover.

Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs are well documented to promote cell migration and tissue repair through delivery of bioactive cargo28. To determine whether these functions are preserved following DAC isolation, EVs were collected from MSC-conditioned medium and isolated by DAC, followed by DNase I treatment to generate a single-vesicle suspension. EVs isolated from the same medium by UC were included as a positive control, and PBS-treated cells served as a negative control. NTA showed that DAC- and UC-isolated EVs exhibited comparable size distributions, both peaking at approximately 100 nm (Fig. 6B). Functional activity was assessed using a scratch wound–healing assay in mouse fibroblast (3T3) cells. Cells treated with either DAC- or UC-isolated EVs at equal protein level exhibited significantly enhanced wound closure compared with PBS-treated controls over a 48 h period (Fig. 6C). Quantitative analysis of the normalized wound area confirmed that DAC-derived EVs promoted cell migration to a similar extent as UC-derived EVs (Fig. 6D).

Together, these data demonstrate that DAC isolation preserves EV structural integrity and biological activity. The retained capacity for cellular uptake and functional modulation supports the suitability of DAC-isolated EVs for downstream applications involving EV biology, functional studies, and cargo delivery.

DAC separates an EpCAM + EV subpopulation

Finally, we extended the adaptability of the DAC platform by replacing the CD63-binding sequence with an EpCAM-binding aptamer, thereby enabling the isolation of a distinct EV subpopulation in a disease- and tumor-relevant context. EpCAM is a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed in epithelial cells and is markedly upregulated in many carcinomas29,30. It has been widely reported to be present on tumor-derived EVs and is commonly used as a surface marker for cancer-associated EV populations31–33. To reconfigure DAC for EpCAM+ EV capture, the CD63-binding sequence in the aptX/Y construct was replaced with a reported EpCAM-binding aptamer34 (sequence listed in Table S1). Using this aptX/YEpCAM pair, EVs were isolated from MCF-7 breast cancer cell culture medium, a model system known to express EpCAM on both cells and secreted EVs35. As expected, the isolated vesicles were positive for canonical EV markers, as well as EpCAM by Western blot analysis (Fig. 7A, left). NTA further confirmed that the recovered particles exhibited a size distribution consistent with EVs, predominantly ranging from ~70 to 200 nm (Fig. 7A, right). These results demonstrate that DAC can be readily adapted to isolate EpCAM+ EVs by simply substituting the affinity sequence.

EpCAM overexpression has also been reported in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and EpCAM+ EVs have been detected in the circulation of NSCLC patients, where they have been explored as potential biomarkers for disease progression and outcome prediction36. Based on this rationale, plasma samples from two late-stage squamous NSCLC patients were collected and pooled for EV analysis. Using the same aptX/YEpCAM construct, EpCAM+ EVs were successfully isolated from plasma and validated by the presence of CD63, flotillin-1, and EpCAM, together with an EV-consistent size distribution (Fig. 7B).

To further investigate whether EpCAM-based DAC enriches an EV subpopulation distinct from CD63-based DAC, we isolated CD63+ EVs and EpCAM+ EVs in parallel from the same pooled NSCLC plasma sample using aptX/YCD63 and aptX/YEpCAM pairs, respectively. Equal protein amounts of each EV subpopulation from three independent biological replicates were subjected to quantitative proteomic analysis by mass spectrometry. A total of 631 proteins were commonly detected in both EV subsets, while 66 proteins were uniquely identified in CD63+ EVs and 103 proteins were uniquely identified in EpCAM+ EVs (Fig. 7C). PCA based on all detected proteins clearly separated the two EV populations, indicating systematic differences in their cargo composition despite their common plasma origin (Fig. 7D).

GO cellular component (CC) analysis of all detected proteins showed that proteins from both CD63+ and EpCAM+ EVs were predominantly associated with canonical EV-related terms, including “vesicle lumen”, “extracellular exosome”, “extracellular vesicle”, “extracellular region”, and “extracellular space” (Supplementary Fig. 20), supporting the effective enrichment of bona fide EVs by both DAC configurations. We then focused on the 103 proteins uniquely detected in the EpCAM+ EV subpopulation. GO CC enrichment analysis of these proteins further confirmed strong EV association, with top-enriched terms again mapping to EV-relevant CCs (Fig. 7E). To gain insight into the functional characteristics of this EpCAM+ EV subset, we performed GO biological process and molecular function enrichment analyses on the uniquely detected proteins. These analyses highlighted pathways related to cytoskeletal remodeling and cell–cell interactions, including “actin filament–based process”, “cell junction organization”, and “integrin-mediated signaling”, as well as molecular functions associated with cell adhesion and calcium-dependent interactions (Fig. 7F, G). In addition, enriched biological processes such as “aldehyde metabolic process” and “blood vessel morphogenesis” were observed, which have been widely implicated in tumor progression, metabolic reprogramming, and angiogenesis. Together, these enrichment patterns indicate that EpCAM-based DAC preferentially enriches an EV subpopulation with cargo features distinct from those isolated by CD63-based affinity capture.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that by simply substituting the aptamer binding sequence, the DAC platform can be readily adapted to isolate distinct EV subpopulations based on surface affinity. The ability to selectively enrich tumor-associated EV subsets, such as EpCAM+ EVs, directly from plasma underscores the versatility of DAC and supports its utility for EV subtyping, tumor biology studies, and biomarker discovery.

Discussion

EVs have emerged as critical mediators of intercellular communication and as promising sources of biomarkers and therapeutic carriers. Despite rapid advances in EV characterization technologies, the intrinsic heterogeneity of EV populations remains a fundamental challenge. Even within a single preparation, EVs vary widely in size, molecular composition, cellular origin, and biological function. This heterogeneity is evident not only in complex biofluids but also in conditioned media from defined cell cultures. Addressing this variability is essential for improving reproducibility, enabling meaningful biological interpretation, and supporting the translation of EV-based technologies into clinical and industrial settings37. A growing body of work has focused on single-vesicle characterization platforms, including nanoscale flow cytometry38, single-particle spectroscopy39, hybrid interferometric reflectance imaging-fluorescence microscopy (i.e., ExoView)40, and super-resolution microscopy41,42. These technologies have substantially improved our ability to interrogate EV heterogeneity at the individual particle level. However, EV enrichment and isolation strategies capable of matching this level of molecular resolution remain comparatively underdeveloped43. Widely used physical separation methods—such as ultracentrifugation, DGs, and SEC—operate primarily on size or density, parameters that do not necessarily correlate with EV molecular identity or biological function. As a result, EV populations isolated by different physical methods often represent overlapping yet distinct subsets, complicating downstream analyses and cross-study comparisons.

Affinity-based isolation strategies enable the enrichment of EV subpopulations defined by surface markers that more closely reflect vesicle cargo and biological function44. However, most existing approaches rely on antibodies and are therefore limited by high cost, batch-to-batch variability, restricted chemical modifiability, and challenges in scalability. Here, we introduce DAC as an alternative affinity-based strategy that overcomes several of these constraints. By exploiting multivalent aptamer binding, DAC induces controlled EV clustering, converting nanoscale vesicles into micron-scale aggregates that can be recovered by simple filtration, thereby decoupling molecular recognition from physical separation. Importantly, the extent of clustering—and thus isolation efficiency—can be tuned by adjusting aptamer dosage and sample composition, providing flexibility across cell culture media and complex biofluids. Compared with UC, DG, and SEC, DAC achieves competitive or superior EV recovery and purity while offering advantages in simplicity and reproducibility. In this workflow, free proteins and soluble protein complexes are efficiently removed during filtration and washing, reducing non-vesicular protein background, while EV-associated surface proteins, including reported protein corona components, are preserved as integral features of circulating EVs45,46. Together, these characteristics position DAC as a practical and accessible platform for affinity-defined EV isolation.

Beyond isolation performance, preserving EV integrity and biological activity is essential for downstream functional studies. In situ SEM imaging shows that DAC-clustered EVs retained on filtration membranes maintain intact morphology without evidence of rupture or deformation. Consistently, DAC-isolated EVs are efficiently internalized by recipient cells and retain biological activity comparable to EVs isolated by ultracentrifugation. In the DAC workflow, DNase I treatment is expected to primarily remove extracellular or loosely associated DNA, while being unlikely to penetrate intact EVs and affect luminal nucleic acids, as supported by preserved morphology and uptake behavior. Nevertheless, because DNA species associated with the EV surface have been reported, the potential functional impact of DNase treatment should be evaluated in a context-dependent manner for studies focused on EV biological activity, whereas such treatment may not be strictly required for EV isolation and molecular profiling applications.

The adaptability of the DAC platform is further demonstrated by replacing the CD63-binding aptamer with an EpCAM-targeting sequence. Using this modification, we successfully isolated EpCAM+ EVs from cancer cell culture media and plasma samples. Comparative proteomic analysis between CD63+ and EpCAM+ EVs isolated from the same plasma sample revealed distinct protein signatures, highlighting the molecular divergence between EV subpopulations defined by different surface markers. These results emphasize the potential of DAC as a modular platform for EV subtyping and for interrogating EV populations associated with specific tissues or disease states. While comprehensive biomarker validation is beyond the scope of this study, the ability to selectively isolate tumor-associated EV subsets illustrates how DAC could facilitate more targeted and informative EV analyses in future investigations.

From a translational perspective, DAC offers several practical advantages. The method relies on chemically synthesized aptamers and standard filtration materials, both of which are amenable to scalable manufacturing and quality control. Unlike many EV isolation approaches that depend on complex centrifugation protocols or microfabricated devices, DAC can be implemented with minimal infrastructure, supporting reproducibility across laboratories and potential adaptation into kit-based formats. These features align with key requirements for the eventual development of EV-based diagnostic or research tools.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we employed previously reported aptamer sequences, the detailed structural basis of aptamer–target interactions remains unresolved. Improved structural understanding could enable rational optimization of aptamer affinity and specificity. Second, the expansion of DAC to additional EV subpopulations depends on the availability of validated aptamers targeting relevant surface markers. For targets lacking established aptamers, de novo selection and validation will be required. Third, the current implementation of DAC relies on dead-end filtration, which may be subject to membrane fouling and limited scalability. Future integration with microfluidic or tangential-flow designs could enable more continuous and high-throughput operation. Finally, while we demonstrate the applicability of DAC to EV subpopulation profiling, large-scale clinical validation of DAC-enabled biomarker discovery remains an important direction for future work. In conclusion, DAC represents a versatile and accessible affinity-based EV isolation strategy that complements existing methodologies. By enabling controllable clustering, preserving EV integrity and function, and allowing modular targeting of EV subpopulations, DAC expands the experimental toolkit available for studying EV heterogeneity and function. We anticipate that this platform will facilitate more precise EV analyses and support the continued advancement of EV research across basic, translational, and applied contexts.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HEK293T and NIH-3T3 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Cat.No.: CRL-3216 and CRL-1658, respectively, ATCC, VA). Cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Cat.No.: SH30243, Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat.No.: 40130ES76, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Cat.No.: V900929, Sigma-Merck, US). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For EV isolation, the culture medium was replaced with FBS-free DMEM 48 h prior to harvest. FBS-free conditioned media was subsequently collected for EV purification, and cell pellets were lysed for downstream analyses.

Human MSCs used in this study were isolated from human umbilical cord tissue following protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital (approval no. BJXK-2020-KY-09), and written informed consent was obtained from the donor(s) prior to tissue collection, in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. MSCs were cultured in low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Cells between passages P3 and P6 were used for experiments. For EV collection, MSCs were washed with PBS and cultured in serum-free medium for 48 h. The resulting serum-free conditioned medium (SF-CM) was collected and subjected to EV isolation by UC or DAC, and the isolated EVs were subsequently used in scratch assays.

Sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were drawn from healthy donors or NSCLC patients. Five to 10 ml of whole blood was collected in EDTA-treated tubes and immediately centrifuged at 1800×g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate blood cells. Plasma was collected from the supernatant, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until further use. Urine samples (20–50 ml) were collected from 9 healthy volunteers for DAC optimization and method comparisons. Urine samples were transported in sealed containers on ice, and immediately pre-processed by centrifuging at 2000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Tear fluid samples from 9 healthy donors were collected using Schirmer’s test paper (Jingming Bio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tear-moistened paper strips were dissolved in PBS and stored at −80 °C. Donor age and gender information is summarized in Table S2. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital (approval no. BJXK-2020-KY-09), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients and healthy volunteers for participation in the study and for publication of de-identified information including age and gender. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Preparation of divalent aptamer pairs

Single-stranded DNA aptamers (sequences provided in Table S1) were synthesized, with or without fluorophore labels, using an Dr. Oligo 192XLc DNA synthesizer (Biolytic, US) and purified by reversed-phase HPLC (BGI Genomics, Beijing, China). The lyophilized DNA samples were dissolved in deionized PBS and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by rapid cooling on ice to facilitate self-annealing and proper secondary structure formation.

EV isolation by DAC

The DAC method was employed to isolate EVs from SF-CM, plasma, urine, and tear fluid. For SF-CM, urine, and tear fluid, samples were processed through differential centrifugation at 300×g and 2000×g. The supernatant was then filtrated through a 0.22 μm filter to remove large debris and ready for aptamer treatment. For plasma, samples were diluted 150 times with PBS and centrifuged sequentially at 300×g, 2000×g, and 10,000×g before passing through a 0.22 μm filter. The filtered samples were divided into two parts, each incubated with either aptX or aptY for 15 min at room temperature. Following this, the two parts were gently mixed and incubated for an additional 15 min to allow for EV clustering. The final reaction was passed through a 200 nm polycarbonate filter membrane (Cat.No.:10417004, Whatman, Cytiva Life Science), and EVs were recovered by resuspension in PBS. PBS used for EV resuspension was pre-filtered with a 0.1 μm syringe filter (Cat.No.: FPV103013, BioFil, China). The protein concentration and particle size distribution were measured using the Qubit protein assay kit (Cat.No.: Q33211, Invitrogen, US) and NTA, respectively. The separated EVs were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, or used immediately in subsequent experiments.

EV isolation by UC

EVs were isolated by UC following previously established protocols47. In brief, collected SF-CM or diluted plasma samples underwent differential centrifugation to remove cell debris and large vesicles (300×g for 15 min, 2000×g for 20 min, and 10,000×g for 30 min; all centrifugation steps performed at 4 °C). The clarified supernatant was then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g using a Type 70 Ti fixed-angle rotor (Beckman Coulter, US) at 4 °C for 70 min to obtain an EV pellet. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and centrifuged again at 100,000×g for 70 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was then resuspended in 0.1 μm-filtered PBS, and the protein concentration was determined using the Qubit protein assay kit (Cat.No.: Q33211, Invitrogen, US). The separated EVs were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, or used immediately in downstream analysis.

EV isolation by DG

DG ultracentrifugation for EV separation was performed as previously described47. For SF-CM, samples were sequentially centrifuged at 300×g, 2000×g, 10,000×g, and 100,000×g at 4 °C to obtain a crude EV pellet. The resulting EV suspension from SF-CM, or diluted plasma, was loaded on top of a three-step iodixanol gradient (OptiPrep, Cat.No.: D1556, Sigma-Aldrich) comprising 5 ml of 15%, 10 ml of 25%, and 5 ml of 35% iodixanol solution. The gradient was centrifuged at 250,000×g for 2 h using polycarbonate bottles (Cat.No.: 355618, Beckman Coulter, US) and a Type 70 Ti fixed-angle rotor (Beckman Coulter, US). Fractions of 500 μl were collected from the top to the bottom of the gradient. Fractions 22–25 that contained EVs were pooled, diluted in PBS, and centrifuged at 100,000×g for 1.5 h. The EV pellet was resuspended in 0.1 μm-filtered PBS for downstream experiments. EV protein concentration was determined using Qubit protein assay kit (Cat.No.: Q33211, Invitrogen, US). The separated EVs were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C, or used immediately in downstream characterization.

EV isolation by SEC

EVs from SF-CM and plasma were isolated using a SEC column (qEVoriginal, Cat.No.: ICO-70, IZON) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. To accommodate the column’s volume capacity, SF-CM was centrifuged at 300×g and 2000×g, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm filter. The filtered supernatant was preconcentrated to 500 μl using a 100 kDa concentrator (Vivaspin 20, Cat.No.: VS2042, Sartorius). The preconcentrated SF-CM, or diluted plasma, was then loaded onto the qEV column and flushed with 0.1 μm-filtered PBS. Fractions of 500 μl were collected. Fractions 7, 8, and 9 containing enriched EVs were pooled and concentrated to 200 μl using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration concentrator (Vivaspin 2, Cat.No.: VS0291, Sartorius). The final EV isolates were analyzed for protein concentration using the Qubit protein assay kit (Cat.No.: Q33211, Invitrogen, US). The separated EVs were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C, or used immediately in subsequent experiments.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

EV size distribution and particle concentration were analyzed using the NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK) equipped with a 405 nm laser and a high-sensitivity sCMOS camera. Purified EV samples were serially diluted in PBS to achieve optimal concentrations for the instrument (1E8-1E10 particles/ml). A syringe pump set at a flow rate of 30 μl/min was used to assist in sample loading. Data acquisition followed the manufacturer’s protocol with a set of parameters standardized throughout the experiments: temperature at 25 °C, screen gain at 1.0, camera level at 15, camera shutter at 1200, and 25 frames per second. Post-acquisition analysis was performed with a detection threshold of 5 and automatic mode for blur size, maximum jump distance, and minimum track length. For each sample, three consecutive videos were recorded. The data, including nanoparticle size distribution and concentration (particles/ml) were processed using NTA 3.4 software package and further analyzed and graphically displayed in GraphPad Prism 9 software.

DLS

The size alterations of EVs during the DAC clustering were monitored using DLS analysis. UC-purified EVs were incubated with an excess of either aptX or aptY, followed by centrifugation at 100,000×g to remove unbound aptamers. The recovered aptX-EVs and aptY-EVs were then resuspended in PBS at equal particle concentrations. Baseline DLS measurements were taken for aptX-EVs, after which incremental volumes of aptY-EVs were added to induce EV clustering. A scrambled aptamer pair (aptScr1/2) served as a control. DLS measurements were conducted on a Zetasizer Pro instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK) using a 633 nm light source and a detector angle of 173°. Each sample was analyzed in three consecutive runs under the following conditions: refractive index of 1.331, viscosity of 0.89, and temperature maintained at 25 °C. The measurements were considered valid when the average count rate ranged between 300 and 500 kcps. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using ZS XPLORER software (Malvern Panalytical, UK), and the resulting data were visualized in GraphPad Prism 9 software.

TEM analysis

EV isolates or EV clusters were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, adsorbed onto a Formvar/carbon-coated copper grid for 30 min at room temperature. After wash, the grids were incubated in 1% glutaraldehyde for 5 min at room temperature, followed by thoroughly washed with deionized water. Subsequently, 2% uranyl acetate was applied to the grid for negative staining of the EV samples. The grids were scanned using a JEM-2100F transmission electron microscope with a working voltage of 200 kV.

SEM analysis

After the filtration step, polycarbonate (PC) membranes (200 nm pore size) from the “With DAC” and “Without DAC” groups were carefully retrieved from the filter holder and transferred to a sterile cell culture dish, taking care to minimize residual fluid loss from the membrane surface. The dish was placed with the lid partially ajar in a vacuum drying oven and dried overnight at 35 °C under low-vacuum conditions. PC membranes from the “Blank control” group were not subjected to any EV treatment and were processed in parallel. After drying, all membranes were mounted onto SEM stubs using conductive carbon tape and coated with a thin conductive layer of gold using a sputter coater (MSP-1S, SHINKKU VD, Japan) to minimize charging effects. SEM imaging was performed using a HITACHI Regulus 8100 instrument operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV under high-vacuum conditions. Images were acquired at multiple magnifications to visualize EV retention, clustering morphology, and membrane surface features.

Western blot analysis

EV samples with equal volumes or equivalent protein amounts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in PBS (pH 7.4, containing 0.1% Tween 20) and then probed for CD63 (1:2000, ABclonal, clone ARC51703, monoclonal rabbit IgG; 1:1000, Santa Cruz, clone MX-49.129.5, monoclonal mouse IgG1κ), CD81 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, clone B-11, monoclonal mouse IgG), flotillin-1 (1:2000, ABclonal, clone ARC0765, monoclonal rabbit IgG), ALIX (1:1000, Santa Cruz, clone 1A12, monoclonal mouse IgG), CD9 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, clone C-4, monoclonal mouse IgG), THP (1:1000, Santa Cruz, clone B-2, monoclonal mouse IgG), ApoA1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, clone 5F4, monoclonal mouse IgG), Albumin (1:2000, ABclonal, polyclonal rabbit IgG), calnexin (1:1000, ABclonal, clone ARC0648, monoclonal rabbit IgG), or EpCAM (1:1000, ABclonal, clone ARC65853, monoclonal rabbit IgG). All primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer. Following incubation, membranes were washed three times with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20) and subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000, goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit, ABclonal) diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBST, blots were visualized with the ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, US). For antibody stripping, membranes were treated with stripping buffer (Cat.No.: C520012, Sangon, China) for 20 min, followed by washing with PBS and re-blocking for 40 min. The membranes were then re-probed with a different primary antibody. For experiments performed with equal protein loading, EV input was normalized according to total protein concentration measured using the Qubit protein assay kit prior to electrophoresis.

For experiments performed with equal protein loading, EV input was normalized according to total protein concentration measured using the Qubit protein assay kit prior to electrophoresis. Western blot images were converted to 8-bit grayscale and analyzed using ImageJ. For each target protein, band intensity was measured from the selected band area after subtraction of local background. Relative protein intensity (%) was calculated by normalizing each band to the mean intensity of the same target protein across all lanes on the same blot. Quantification was performed independently for each biological replicate, and normalized values were used to generate bar graphs.

Fluorescence microscopy

For the affinity assays, HEK293T cells were plated on sterile 35 mm glass-bottom plates. When grown to approximately 60% confluency, cells were transfected with a CD63-EGFP construct using linear polyethylenimine reagent (MW 25,000, Cat.No.: MX2202, Shanghai Maokang Bio.). After 24 h of culture, the transfected cells were washed with HBSS (Cat.No.: C14175500BT, Gibco). Cy5-conjugated aptX or aptY, or buffer alone, was incubated with live cells for 30 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Cat.No.: C1022, Beyotime, China) before imaging. Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope with a 40× oil immersion lens, controlled by Zeiss ZEN software (Blue edition). Fluorescence images were adjusted to enhance contrast using ZEN software.

For EV cluster imaging, UC-purified EVs were stained with fluorescent dye DiI or DiO (Cat.No.: 40792ES50 / 40725ES10, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) and ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g to remove excess dye molecules. The stained EVs were incubated with an excess of either aptX or aptY, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g to exclude unbound aptamers. The resulting aptX-EVs and aptY-EVs were mixed to promote cluster formation. The reaction was then transferred onto a glass slide and imaged using the Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective lens and Airyscan 2 detector. Images were processed with ZEN software for contrast enhancement.

ITC

The heat release from the hybridization of aptX and aptY was measured using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC system (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Briefly, aptX or the control sequence (aptScr1) was diluted to 8 μM in PBS and then loaded into the calorimetric cell. The solution of aptY or the control sequence (aptScr2) at 100 μM was loaded in the syringe. The titration involved 20 consecutive injections of aptY or aptScr2 into the cell, with the first injection being 0.2 μl, followed by 19 injections of 2 μl each. Each injection was spaced by 300 s to allow the thermal power baseline to stabilize. The stirring speed was set at 750 rpm, and the experiment was conducted at a constant temperature of 25 °C. Deionized water was used to fill the reference cell. Data analysis was performed using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software (Malvern, UK), applying a two-site binding model.

Estimation of EV recovery efficiency in simulated urine

To estimate EV recovery efficiency, UC-purified HEK293T-derived EVs were quantified by NTA. In parallel, a midstream urine sample collected from a healthy donor was depleted of endogenous EVs by overnight UC. The resulting EV-depleted urine was analyzed by NTA to determine residual particle background before use. For each isolation method, 6 ml of EV-depleted urine was supplemented with 2.66E9 purified EV particles to generate a simulated urine sample. The particle concentration of the spiked sample was verified by NTA prior to EV isolation. Simulated urine samples were then subjected to either UC or DAC using the same procedures described above. Recovered EVs were quantified by NTA, and recovery efficiency was calculated as recovered particle number divided by particle number of the spiked urine after correction for residual background. All numerical values associated with this experiment are summarized in Table 2.

Silver staining

Silver staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, EV samples isolated using UC, DG, SEC, and DAC were quantified for total protein mass using the Qubit protein assay kit (Cat. No.: Q33211, Invitrogen, US). EVs with equal protein amounts (15 μg) were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and stained using the Protein Stains Q kit (Cat. No.: C500029, Sangon, China). The gel was then imaged using the ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, US).

EV uptake assay

EVs were isolated from HEK293T SF-CM collected after 48 h using the DAC method. Isolated EVs were treated with DNase I (Cat. No.: 14550ES, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) to dissociate clusters. For fluorescent labeling, EVs (2E9 particles) were incubated with 1 μM DiO (Cat. No.: 40725ES10, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) at room temperature for 30 min. Excess free dye was removed by PBS washing and UC sedimentation. As a negative control, an equal volume of PBS (corresponding to the EV volume) was subjected to the same DiO labeling and washing procedure. When cell confluency reached approximately 70%, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with HBSS (Cat. No.: C14175500BT, Gibco). DiO-labeled EVs or dye-only control were then added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 5 h. After incubation, cells were washed twice with HBSS to remove unbound EVs. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst dye at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by two additional HBSS washes. Cell membranes were subsequently labeled by incubation with 1 μM DiI (Cat.No.: 40792ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) for 10 min, followed by three HBSS washes. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope with a 40× oil immersion lens, controlled by Zeiss ZEN software (Blue edition). Fluorescence images were adjusted to enhance contrast using ZEN software.

Scratch (wound-healing) assay

NIH-3T3 fibroblasts were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured in complete high-glucose DMEM (10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin) until reaching full confluency. A linear scratch was generated in each well using a sterile 200 μl pipette tip, followed by gentle washing with PBS to remove detached cells. Cells were then incubated with MSC-derived EVs isolated either by UC or by DAC. For DAC-isolated EVs, clustered vesicles were dissociated by DNase I (Cat.No.: 14550ES, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) treatment prior to use. EV treatments were normalized by total EV protein amount (20 μg per well). PBS alone served as a negative control. Wound closure was monitored by phase-contrast microscopy at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h post-scratching. The scratched area was quantified using ImageJ and normalized to the initial wound area at 0 h. All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates.

Mass spectrometry analysis for EV metabolomics

Urine-derived EVs isolated using UC, SEC, or DAC, with equal particle numbers (1E9 particles), were diluted to 100 μl in PBS and mixed with 400 μl of extraction buffer (50% acetonitrile and 50% methanol). The samples were vortexed for 30 s and sonicated for 30 min at 4 °C. Then the samples were incubated at −20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 13,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and dried under vacuum. The dried samples were reconstituted in 100 μl of buffer containing 50% acetonitrile and 50% water and sonicated for 5 min at 4 °C. Afterward, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove fine particulates. The supernatant was transferred to a glass vial for ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography-coupled Q-TOF mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-TOF MS) analysis.

Each sample (5 μl) was injected onto a reverse-phase Kinetex core-shell C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.4 × 100 mm, Phenomenex, Switzerland) using a 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system (Agilent, US). The gradient mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (Solvent A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (Solvent B). Each sample was resolved at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min for a total run time of 14 min. The UHPLC gradient was as follows: 95% A and 5% B for 0.5 min, ramping to 10% A and 90% B from 0.5 to 12.0 min, and then returning to 95% A and 5% B from 12.0 to 14.0 min. The column effluent was introduced directly into the mass spectrometer via electrospray ionization (AJS ESI, Agilent, US). Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q-TOF instrument (6546 LC/Q-TOF, Agilent, US) operating in either negative (ESI−) or positive (ESI+) electrospray ionization mode with a capillary voltage of 3500 V in positive mode and 2,800 V in negative mode. Data were acquired in centroid MS mode over a mass range of 100–1200 m/z, with a single injection per sample. The batch acquisition was repeated to ensure experimental reproducibility, and the sample queue was randomized to minimize bias. Raw data were processed using MassHunter Profinder (v10.0, Agilent, US) and Mass Profiler Professional (v15.1, Agilent, US) for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Metabolites were annotated by using the METLIN database (https://metlin.scripps.edu). The quantification of identified metabolites was based on the primary ion heights. Result files were analyzed for differential expressions, fold changes, PCA, and KEGG/GO enrichment using MetaboAnalyst software (v6.0).

Mass spectrometry analysis for EV proteomics

DAC-separated EVs from the plasma of NSCLC patients or healthy controls were suspended in triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB, 100 mM in water). Each sample was reduced with tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP, 10 mM), alkylated with iodoacetamide (40 mM), and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min. The pellets were resuspended in TEAB and then digested with proteomics-grade trypsin (trypsin:protein mass = 1:50) at 37 °C overnight. The resulting digested peptides were dried under vacuum and reconstituted in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid buffer (TFA). Acidified peptides were desalted using an Oasis HLB μElution Plate (Waters, US) and quantified using the Pierce Quantitative kit (Cat.No.: 23275, Thermo Scientific, US). Equal amounts of EV peptides (200 ng by protein mass) were loaded onto a C18 column (1.6 μm, 75 μm × 250 mm, Ionopticks, US) using an Evosep One LC system (Evosep, Denmark). Separation of peptides was performed at 45 °C and 500 nl/min using a linear acetonitrile gradient of buffer A (water with 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) and buffer B (water with 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid). The gradient was as follows: from 3% buffer B to 28% B over 33 min, followed by an increase to 44% B over 4 min, a ramp to 90% B in 3 min, and 4 min at 90% B. The column was then equilibrated with water containing 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile. Data were collected on a timsTOF Pro2 MS (Bruker, Germany) in DIA-PASEF mode (DIA Parallel Accumulation Serial Fragmentation) with a scan range of 1/K0 m/z 100-1,700. For ion mobility settings, inverse mobilities from 0.6 to 1.6 Vs/cm2 were analyzed with both the ion accumulation time and ramp time set as 100 ms. One survey TMS-MS scan was followed by ten PASEF ramps for MS/MS acquisition, resulting in a 1.9-s cycle time. Singly charged ions were excluded using a polygon filter mask, and isolation windows for MS/MS were set to m/z 2.0 for precursor ions below m/z 700, and m/z 3.0 for ions above. The chromatographic retention of peptides was calibrated using iTR peptide standards (Cat.No.: 1816351, Biognosys, Switzerland).

Data were searched against the UniProt Taxonomy (Taxon ID: 9606) using Spectronaut (Biognosys AG, Switzerland) with standard parameters and modifications, including a protein false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤1%, peptide FDR of ≤1%, peptide confidence of ≥99%, and an XIC width of ≤75 ppm. Only proteins with at least one unique peptide were included, and shared or modified peptides were excluded from protein identification. Protein quantification was based on the summarized peak areas of six peptides and three daughter ions per peptide. Result files were uploaded into the online platform of NovoMagic for further analysis, including PCA, differential analysis, GO annotation, and data visualization.

Statistics and reproducibility

To assess the significance of differences between groups, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first conducted to evaluate whether the data followed a normal distribution. For normally distributed data, equality of variances was tested using the F-test (for two groups) or the Brown–Forsythe test (for three or more groups). Normally distributed groups with equal variances were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test (for three or more groups). For normally distributed groups without equal variances, significance was assessed using the unpaired Welch’s t-test (for two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA (for three or more groups). Non-normally distributed groups were analyzed using either the Mann–Whitney U-test (for two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA (for three or more groups). Data are presented as mean ± SEM for normally distributed data. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 20). All graphs were generated using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, version 8.0).

Scoring for the performance of EV isolation methods

The EV separation of DAC, UC, DG, and SEC was evaluated and scored in aspects of yield, purity, repeatability, processing time, and cost efficiency according to their performance on plasma and SF-CM EV isolation. Yield included the measurement of recovered particle numbers using NTA and the expression levels of CD63 via Western blot from equal volumes of the sample. The recovered particle numbers from plasma-EV and SF-CM-EV each contributed 25% to the final yield score, with the maximum across four methods converted to 25 and others normalized to the maximum. The CD63 intensity from plasma-EV and SF-CM each accounted for 25%, with the maximum intensity value of CD63 across four methods converted to 25, and others normalized to the maximum. Purity scoring considered the expression intensities of CD63 and ApoA1 (Western blot) under equal total protein mass conditions. CD63 levels indicated EV abundance, while ApoA1 levels reflected contaminant presence. The ratio of CD63 to ApoA1 from plasma-EV, with the maximum normalized to 50, contributed to 50% of the final score. As calnexin was not detected from SF-CM-EVs across methods, the CD63 intensities were used to account for the remaining 50%. Repeatability was assessed by calculating the SD of total particle counts from three biological replicates of NTA measurements for plasma-EVs. As DG-isolated EVs were below the detection limit by NTA, SF-CM EV isolations were not included in this analysis. For scoring, the reciprocal of the smallest SD was set to 100, and the other values were normalized accordingly. Time efficiency concerns the time duration of major steps in each method, including ultracentrifugation, gradient establishment, column elution, and incubations, concluding as 180 min for UC, 270 min for DG, 60 min for SEC, and 45 min for DAC. These times were converted to efficiency scores, with the reciprocal of the minimum time (45 min) set to 100, and the others normalized accordingly. Cost efficiency was considered the requirement of key instruments and major materials and ranked as follows: DG (instrument and reagents like iodixanol), UC (instrument), SEC (commercial columns), and DAC (materials like aptamers and filters), resulting in cost efficiency scores of 40, 60, 80, and 100, respectively. The source data is available in Supplementary Data 2.

Simulation and algorithm

To initialize the binary DLCA simulation, a total of N0=1000 spherical particles, each with a diameter d=0.1, are randomly distributed within an off-lattice cubic box with side length L=10, mimicking a system containing particles of 100 nm in diameter at a concentration of 1E12 particles/ml. All particles are assigned labels, either X or Y, corresponding to aptX-bound EVs or aptY-bound EVs, with a ratio set to 1:1. The simulation begins with each particle performing a size-dependent walk in a random direction. The step distance correlates to the diffusion coefficient D, which is related to the cluster’s radius of gyration Rg through Stokes’ law (Note 1). After each step, an aggregation test is imposed: if the center-to-center distance between two unlike particles is less than or equal to d, the two are bonded. A particle stops forming new bonds when the number of existing bonds reaches S, the number of sticky ends predefined as an integer from 1 to 8. Following the test, particles or clusters with updated Rg continue the random walk. The primary algorithm is outlined in the flowchart (Supplementary Fig. 10). The simulation does not account for the possible rotation of clusters. Further details on this type of simulation can be found in references48,49.
