Emerging Design Principles at the Forefront of Mitochondrial Transplantation Using Engineering Methodologies: Current Achievements and Future Directions
Authors: Naoto Yoshinaga, Keiji Numata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitochondrial transplantation has gathered much attention as therapeutics to improve multiple mitochondrial functions simultaneously. While the administration of naked mitochondria into the target tissue has demonstrated therapeutic outcomes sufficient to advance to clinical trials, there remain many limitations, including a low cellular uptake efficiency in the target tissue and dysfunction of the isolated mitochondria. To address these issues, engineering approaches have been developed to functionalize the isolated mitochondria. In this review, we focus on the three critical topics for efficient mitochondrial transplantation and outline emerging design rules and their limitations for each purpose: (i) tissue targeting, (ii) protection of mitochondria from external stresses, and (iii) improvement of cellular uptake efficiency. From these achievements, we also discuss the current limitations of mitochondrial transplantation and propose the future direction of the attractive therapeutic methodology.Keywords: lipid, metal–organic framework, mitochondria transplantation, mitochondrial coating, peptide, polymer

Introduction

Mitochondria, which originated from an endosymbiotic ancestor, are essential organelles that execute a wide range of indispensable cellular functions (Roger et al. 2017; Monzel et al. 2023). During evolution, much of the genomic information of the ancestral endosymbiont was either lost or transferred to the host nuclear genome. In humans, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) encodes genetic information for 13 protein‐coding genes related to oxidative phosphorylation, 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and two ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) (Anderson et al. 1981). The mtDNA regulates multiple mitochondrial functions, such as oxidative phosphorylation and signal transduction, together with nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial genes and mtDNA‐encoded genome (Spinelli and Haigis 2018). The mitochondrial dysfunction has been linked to diverse diseases, including metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiac diseases (Zong et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2025). Therefore, maintaining mitochondrial integrity is a crucial condition for maintaining health.

Mitochondrial dysfunctions arise from genetic mutations and environmental causes, such as ischemic injury (Zorov et al. 2000; Butterfield and Halliwell 2019). Therapeutic strategies for these abnormalities have mainly focused on (i) supplementation of metabolites and (ii) gene therapies that repair pathogenic mutations to improve mitochondria‐related metabolisms (Schapira 2012; Rahman and Rahman 2018; Falabella et al. 2022; Soldatov et al. 2022; Yoshinaga and Numata 2022; Yamada et al. 2020). Coenzyme Q10 and vitamin B supplementations are commonly used medications (Avula et al. 2014), and recent clinical studies have explored the delivery of mtDNA‐encoded genes using adeno‐associated virus (AAV) vectors (Ball et al. 2025). More recently, there has been active development of mitochondrial transplantation therapy, which can, in principle, address both metabolic and genetic defects of mitochondria simultaneously (Kim, Lee, et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2025; Li, Wu, et al. 2025; Gollihue and Rabchevsky 2017; McCully et al. 2017; Perrier et al. 2025; Brestoff et al. 2025).

Mitochondrial transplantation refers to the administration of healthy autologous or allogenic mitochondria into target sites that harbor dysfunctional mitochondria. Mitochondrial transplantation therapy has been developed as two related but independent approaches: one involves the delivery of mitochondria to somatic tissues with mitochondrial dysfunction, such as ischemia–reperfusion, and the other is an assisted reproductive technology that supplements healthy mitochondria to oocytes to prevent the inheritance of pathogenic mtDNA mutations. The former therapeutic approach has been referred to as “mitochondrial transplantation,” and the latter as “mitochondrial replacement therapy.” Some clinical trials of mitochondrial transplantation have been conducted to treat acquired mitochondrial dysfunction, including postoperative cardiac dysfunction in pediatric heart surgery (Emani et al. 2017; Guariento et al. 2021). To prevent maternal transmission of pathogenic mtDNA mutation, after careful preclinical testing of mitochondrial donation using pronuclear transfer, mitochondrial replacement therapy has been achieved by transferring the nucleus of a patient's oocyte or zygote into an enucleated donor cell (Hyslop et al. 2016, 2025; McFarland et al. 2025). In addition, ex vivo mitochondrial replacement has been attempted in CD34+ hematopoietic cells for patients with large‐scale mtDNA deletion syndromes (Jacoby et al. 2022). In this review, unless otherwise specified, we use the term “mitochondrial transplantation” collectively to include both mitochondrial transplantation and mitochondrial replacement therapies.

Exogenous mitochondria enter the cells by actin‐dependent macropinocytosis or endocytosis (Islam et al. 2012; Hayakawa et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024). While a portion of the internalized mitochondria is degraded by lysosomes, others can reach the cytoplasm, despite the unknown mechanism of endosomal escape, followed by fusion with the endogenous mitochondrial network and then normalizing inter‐organelle crosstalk (Li et al. 2024; Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al. 2021). The settled mitochondria in the recipient cells can rapidly increase ATP production and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, leading to the recovery of cellular functions. Improving Ca2+ ion levels and transcriptomic/proteomic profiles has also been reported (Görlach et al. 2015; Pacak et al. 2015; Doulamis et al. 2024, 2022). These multiple effects of mitochondrial transplantation increase tolerance to cell‐death pathways and inflammatory responses. Furthermore, mitochondrial transplantation holds potential as a gene therapy because it can shift the heteroplasmy rate toward a wild‐type mtDNA‐rich condition (Jacoby et al. 2022; Lin et al. 2019). Many studies have reported mitochondrial transplantation as a promising approach, but there are several issues for improvement. One of the most serious problems is the transient improvement of mitochondrial transplantation, which is likely due to the insufficient transplantation efficiency. Simply administering naked mitochondria often results in transient improvements in mitochondrial function, probably because the transplanted mitochondria may become injured due to exposure to a hostile environment, leading to insufficient cellular uptake of intact mitochondria to reprogram cellular homeostasis in the target (Kim, Lee, et al. 2023; Li, Zheng, et al. 2025; Ali Pour et al. 2020). Therefore, “mitochondrial engineering,” which involves applying various modifications to the surface of isolated mitochondria, has been developed to enhance the efficiency of mitochondrial transplantation. While previous reviews comprehensively categorized mitochondrial transplantation studies based on the using materials (Kim, Lee, et al. 2023; Miao et al. 2025; Li, Wu, et al. 2025; Gollihue and Rabchevsky 2017; McCully et al. 2017; Perrier et al. 2025; Brestoff et al. 2025), in this review, we discuss operational strategies according to the purpose from three angles: (i) tissue targeting strategy, (ii) protection of mitochondria, and (iii) improvement of cellular uptake efficiency, and highlight emerging rational design guidelines while outlining future perspectives (Figure 1).

Mitochondrial Modification for Efficient Transplantation

The earliest findings on intercellular mitochondrial transfer were discovered in the 1970s, showing that incorporated mitochondria were maintained when mtDNA‐deficient ρ0 cells were fused with normal cells or enucleated cells, which are referred to as cybrid cells (Ferenczy and Maráz 1977; Gunge and Sakaguchi 1979; Yoshida 1979; Fukuda and Kimura 1980). The concept derived from cell–cell fusion was later extended by the finding that cancer cell lines can take up isolated mitochondria alone in the 1980s (Clark and Shay 1982; King and Attardi 1988). Mitochondrial transplantation technology initially contributed to fundamental research on mtDNA mutations. However, the discovery of in vivo intercellular mitochondrial transport between normal cells opened a new window for therapeutic approaches. At the animal experiment level, supplementation of mitochondria into rabbit myocardium after ischemia–reperfusion was reported in 2009 (McCully et al. 2009). Subsequent clinical trials have suggested efficacy in humans, with authorization in the UK in 2015 (Emani et al. 2017; Guariento et al. 2021; Hyslop et al. 2016, 2025; McFarland et al. 2025). However, (i) tissue targeting strategy, (ii) protection of mitochondria from environmental stress, and (iii) enhancement of cellular uptake efficiency remain key bottlenecks for broader implementation of mitochondrial transplantation. Controlling Biodistribution of Transplanted Mitochondria and Target Tissue

To deliver healthy mitochondria to specific tissues, two approaches have been developed: one involves optimizing the administration route, and the other involves chemical modification based on accumulated knowledge of drug delivery systems. Even with naked mitochondria, an appropriate administration route enabled the accumulation in specific sites, exemplifying the direct and near‐direct injection into various organs with enhanced vascular permeability after ischemia–reperfusion injury (Emani et al. 2017; McCully et al. 2009; Cowan et al. 2016; Masuzawa et al. 2013; Cloer et al. 2023; Norat et al. 2023; Chou et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2024; Rossi et al. 2023). Meanwhile, local transplantation of mitochondria to the target tissue often requires invasive surgical procedures, which limit its use as clinical applications. Intravenous administration, as a relatively non‐invasive method, has also been attempted, but phagocytosis by macrophages in the liver and spleen entraps the administered mitochondria. As a simple intravenous injection of the isolated mitochondria is insufficient to obtain sufficient therapeutic effects, active targeting strategies for mitochondrial transplantation have been developed.

The first study to attribute tissue specificity to mitochondria involved surface modification by asialoorosomucoid (AsOR) to target AsOR receptors on hepatocytes (Liu et al. 2020). AsOR was conjugated with poly‐L‐lysine (PLL) or listeriolysin O (LLO), a pore‐forming toxin known to exhibit endosomal escape ability (Birmingham et al. 2008), and the prepared AsOR‐PLL and AsOR‐LLO were attached to the isolated mitochondria surface via electrostatic interaction. Intravenous administration of both AsOR‐modified mitochondria to rats accomplished an efficient accumulation in hepatocytes even at a low dose of mitochondria. Remarkably, approximately 25% of the administered AsOR‐LLO mitochondria accumulated in the hepatic parenchyma after 1 h of administration, whose accumulation efficiency in the liver was 10‐fold higher than that of naked mitochondria. The transplanted AsOR‐LLO mitochondria remained in the hepatocytes after 24 h, especially localized in the portal area. These results confirmed that, similar to drug and gene delivery systems, ligand installation on the surface of mitochondria can provide target specificity.

It is not necessary to use a full‐length protein to target a specific tissue. Peptides are sufficient to add targeting ability to mitochondria. An ischemia‐homing peptide (PEP: CSTSMLKAC) discovered by in vivo phage display screening (Kanki et al. 2011) was conjugated with triphenylphosphonium (TPP), a lipophilic cation strongly interacting with mitochondrial membrane, and then introduced to the surface of the isolated mitochondria (PEP‐TPP‐Mito) (Sun, Chen, et al. 2023). PEP‐TPP‐Mito demonstrated specific internalization into cultured cardiomyocytes exposed to hypoxia, mimicking an ischemia–reperfusion condition, resulting in a significant improvement in oxygen consumption rate compared with naked mitochondria transplantation. Intravenous injection of PEP‐TPP‐Mito in a myocardial ischemia–reperfusion mouse model also showed approximately 30% increase in accumulation efficiency at the disease site, suppressing the expression of apoptotic and inflammatory factors, and ultimately improving cardiac function. The therapeutic effect continued for 4 weeks after administration.

The availability of peptides for mitochondrial surface modification enabled targeting of other tissues. Mann et al. (2016) developed surface‐modified mitochondria with a TPP‐conjugated chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan‐binding tetrapeptide (CAQK, CAQK‐TPP‐Mito) (Xu et al. 2024), which was identified via in vivo phage display screening in an acute brain injury mouse model (Mann et al. 2016). The CAQK peptide can interact with chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, which increases in the extracellular matrix (ECM) in the injured central nervous system. The Mito‐TPP‐CAQK was selectively accumulated in the injured site after intravenous injection into a spinal cord injury mouse model through interactions between CAQK and over‐expressed chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan in the ECM, followed by internalization into macrophages. The CAQK modification of the mitochondria induced rapid accumulation in the spinal cord and showed approximately threefold higher accumulation after 12 h of intravenous transplantation compared with the naked mitochondria. The transplanted Mito‐TPP‐CAQK improved ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential, and respiration in macrophages, leading to the attenuation of inflammatory factor expression and an enhancement of phagocytic capacity for myelin remnants, which cause chronic inflammation in the central nervous system. The reprogramming of macrophage functions by mitochondrial transplantation ultimately preserved axons and myelin, resulting in a significant recovery of motor function in the mouse model.

A similar strategy to the Mito‐TPP‐CAQK was employed, although the results are limited to in vitro studies, by using a homing peptide for VCAM‐1 in chronic vascular disease targeting (Figure 2) (Applewhite et al. 2025). VCAM‐1 is an inflammatory adhesion molecule whose expression increases in chronic vascular diseases, and type IV collagen is a subendothelial matrix protein exposed during endothelial injury. VCAM‐1 binding peptide (VBP: VHPKQHRGGSKGC) and collagen type IV binding one (CBP: CQDSETRTFY) were conjugated with DSPE‐PEG lipid, and then incorporated into the isolated mitochondria. The peptide‐modified mitochondria were selectively transferred into inflammatory and injury‐induced endothelial model cells. The mitochondria encapsulated in the VBP‐conjugated liposome especially performed approximately fivefold higher cellular uptake efficiency in the model cells after 96 h of transplantation.

The tissue‐targeting strategy, of course, is not limited to proteins and peptides. Tissue‐selective mitochondrial transplantation can be achieved using synthetic polymers. Wu et al. (2024) designed triple‐coated mitochondria using (i) a pH‐responsive enteric capsule, (ii) cardiomyocyte membrane (CM) for oral mitochondrial transplantation, and (iii) a nitric oxide (NO)‐driven zwitterionic polymer‐based nanomotor (NM) for oral mitochondrial transplantation (Wu et al. 2024). An enteric capsule can protect the loaded mitochondria from the highly acidic conditions in the stomach and release the mitochondria in the small intestine in response to pH. The second coating material, CM, allowed the released mitochondria from the enteric capsule to exhibit transcytosis into the bloodstream after cellular uptake by the intestinal epithelium. The NM finally guided mitochondria in the bloodstream toward damaged cardiac tissue via the NO‐generating chemotactic property, in response to elevated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and ROS (Chen et al. 2023). The NM was synthesized by a radical polymerization reaction of methacrylamidated arginine monomer and diselenide crosslinker on the mitochondria modified with N‐acryloyloxysuccinimide to prepare NM coating mitochondria (NM/Mito). After surface charge conversion of NM/Mito using triphenylphosphonium‐amine, the fragmented CM was added to the NM/Mito via electrostatic interaction for preparation of NM/CM/Mito. Finally, the NM/CM/Mito was encapsulated in an enteric capsule (NM/CM/Mito@Cap). Orally administering the CM/NM/Mito@Cap performed expected sequential behavior: (i) degradation of the capsule in the small intestine, (ii) transcytosis into the bloodstream after enhanced cellular uptake by the intestinal epithelium through CM function, and (iii) automatic accumulation toward ischemic heart disease lesion site driven by the chemotactic property of NM. Triple‐coated CM/NM/Mito@Cap only demonstrated internalization and a high retention ratio in cardiomyocytes of the ischemic heart disease mouse model. Although NM coating alone did not improve transplantation efficiency in the heart in an acute ischemic heart disease model, CM and NM dual coating of mitochondria resulted in approximately eightfold higher accumulation in the heart compared with the uncoated mitochondria. The efficient transplantation induced improved cardiac function and suppression of infarct progression. These results suggested that mitochondrial transplantation was possible even via oral administration, which is an exceptionally non‐invasive approach.

Inorganic materials are also effective for guiding transplanted mitochondria to target sites. Magnetic iron oxide microparticles escorted the encapsulated mitochondria to a target tissue using an external magnetic field (Zhou, Zang, et al. 2025). Using an external magnetic field, the authors demonstrated the successful accumulation of mitochondria encapsulated in iron oxide microparticles within microglia in the central nervous system of intracerebral hemorrhage model mice, which possess a leaky blood–brain barrier structure (Keep et al. 2014). The fluorescence intensity of the labeled mitochondria encapsulated in the magnetic nanoparticles was 3.8‐fold higher than that of the naked mitochondria in the brain slice after 24 h of transplantation. The incorporated mitochondria restored mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production, and reduced the ROS level, apoptosis, and inflammatory factors. Furthermore, mitochondrial transplantation improved mouse behavior after brain injury following intracerebral hemorrhage, suggesting that selectively transplanting mitochondria into the brain can suppress inflammation and improve neurological function.

Protection of the Mitochondria From Stress

Mitochondria need to withstand a severe microenvironment and maintain their functions, such as membrane potential and oxygen consumption rate, to be used as therapeutics. Even during the brief interval between isolation and transplantation, numerous factors can trigger the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, ultimately resulting in a loss of function (Bernardi et al. 2015). For example, Ca2+ or inorganic phosphate in buffers, mismatch of osmotic pressure and pH, shear stress from contact with storage surfaces, and exposure to ROS cause dysfunction of the isolated mitochondria. After administration, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis occur at higher levels than in healthy tissue, and these processes also severely damage the transplanted mitochondria. To protect against these insults, several coating strategies have been explored (Patel et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2023; Picone et al. 2024; Nakano et al. 2022; Wang, Liu, Ma, et al. 2024; Zhou, Liu, et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2018). Notably, none of these approaches has been reported to compromise the post‐transplant functional rescue in the recipient cells.

As an early example, Patel et al. (2022) embedded mitochondria in hydrogels (Patel et al. 2022). Mitochondria in a hyaluronic acid and methylcellulose gel maintained mitochondrial membrane potential for a few hours at 4°C and showed higher oxygen consumption immediately after release than uncoated mitochondria. Similar findings were obtained with encapsulation of the isolated mitochondria in a Pluronic F‐127 hydrogel (Huang et al. 2023). Encapsulation in a plant polysaccharide‐based degalactosylated xyloglucan hydrogel likewise relieved oxidative stress under H2O2 exposure and attenuated the drop in mitochondrial membrane potential (Picone et al. 2024). These results indicated efficient preservation of mitochondrial function using hydrogels.

In a different approach, encapsulating the isolated mitochondria in 1,2‐dioleoyl‐3‐trimethylammonium propane (DOTAP)/1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine (DOPE) liposomes helped maintain a significantly higher mitochondrial membrane potential than uncoated controls for a few hours, suggesting that lipid layer coating can mitigate the functional decline between harvest and administration (Nakano et al. 2022).

Whereas hydrogel or lipid coatings provide protection for a few hours, metal–organic framework (MOF) shells enable much longer stabilization in saline. Wang, Liu, Ma, et al. (2024) developed mitochondria coated with Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework‐8 (ZIF‐8) that retained the mitochondrial membrane potential and oxidative phosphorylation capacity for at least 48 h and improved recipient mitochondrial function after transplantation, even with a long storage time (Wang, Liu, Ma, et al. 2024). The same group further optimized the ZIF‐8 coating, achieving a further longer preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential for up to 4 weeks at room temperature in saline (Figure 3) (Zhou, Liu, et al. 2025). Possibly, while polymer, lipid‐bilayer, or hydrogel coatings cannot exclude external adverse species, including Ca2+ at the Å scale, ZIF‐8 has a pore aperture of ~3.4 Å (Lu et al. 2022), which is smaller than the hydrated radius of Ca2+ (~4.1 Å) (Richards et al. 2011). Therefore, ZIF‐8 coating can kinetically limit the entry of molecules and ions that would otherwise impair mitochondrial function. However, the intramitochondrial ATP level still declined to roughly half by 48 h even after ZIF‐8 coating, indicating that additional optimization and mechanistic studies are needed.

Enhancement of Uptake Efficiency of Mitochondria

Coating mitochondria with various materials that add targeting or protective functions can indirectly enhance cellular uptake by partially neutralizing the negatively charged mitochondrial surface, thereby increasing contact frequency with the negatively charged cell surface. As surface modification using cationic materials can further enhance the cellular uptake efficiency of the mitochondria, charge conversion of mitochondrial surface is a particularly effective approach for enhanced uptake. Encapsulation of mitochondria in liposomes is a useful method for regulating the surface potential without compromising mitochondrial integrity. Naked mitochondria typically show a ζ‐potential around −50 mV. Nakano et al. (2022) reported that DOTAP/DOPE coating partially neutralized the ζ‐potential to about −16.6 mV and roughly doubled the uptake efficiency of mitochondria into cultured primary neurons (Figure 4) (Nakano et al. 2022). Moreover, intravenous administration of the DOTAP/DOPE‐coated mitochondria in mice after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion also decreased lesion volume and produced robust neuroprotection. Lipid formulations for mitochondrial transplantation have been studied in more detail. By changing the mixing ratio of cationic DOTAP and neutral phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), the ζ‐potential was pushed into the positive range (up to +53 mV), which yielded approximately fourfold higher uptake efficiency of mitochondria into the cultured human chondrocyte cells compared with PE‐only coating ones (Kim, Cho, et al. 2023). Local administration of these surface‐cationized mitochondria into joints of an osteoarthritis mouse model promoted cartilage regeneration.

A more elaborate strategy of mitochondrial surface cationization was reported by Wang, Liu, Hu, et al. (2024), who used Parkin‐encoding messenger RNA (mRNA) lipoplex to coat the mitochondrial surface through electrostatic interaction for the co‐delivery of mRNA and mitochondria (Wang, Liu, Hu, et al. 2024). As Parkin promotes mitophagy of damaged mitochondria, the co‐delivery can remove defective endogenous mitochondria while supplying healthy exogenous mitochondria by transplantation. In the Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) model cells, co‐delivery of mRNA and mitochondria increased the number of intact mitochondria with a proper mitochondrial membrane potential, resulting in improved ATP production compared to the transplantation of mitochondria alone. The strategy also achieved a significant improvement in mitochondria in the LHON model mice that had lost their eyesight, resulting in a recovery of retinal layer thickness and visual function.

Surface modification of the mitochondria using cell‐penetrating peptides is also examined to enhance transplantation efficiency. Mixing a cell‐penetrating peptide Pep‐1 (KETWWETWWTEWSQPKKKRKV) and the isolated mitochondria significantly enhances cellular internalization in various cells (Chang et al. 2013, 2017, 2021). The transplanted mitochondria improved oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ATP production efficiency while lowering ROS levels in mitochondrial disease patients' derived cells (myoclonus epilepsy associated with ragged‐red fibers: MERRF, and mitochondrial myopathy encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke‐like episodes: MELAS). Finally, intranasal administration of Pep‐1 modified mitochondria in a Parkinson's disease rodent model resulted in successful transplantation into the brain along olfactory pathways, improved motor behavior, preserved nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons, and reduced systemic inflammatory markers. Meanwhile, the authors mentioned remaining needs including optimizing dosing and formulations, conducting long‐term safety and immunogenicity assessments, and quantifying the integration and durability of the graft.

Current Limitations

The evidence described above indicates that targeted mitochondrial transplantation is emerging as a promising potential therapy for multiple diseases by chemically modifying mitochondria. However, these findings are largely sporadic, and establishing comprehensive insights, especially on safety issues, is still in progress. Although mitochondrial transport, which encourages the development of mitochondrial transplantation therapy, is indeed observed in our bodies and contributes to maintaining tissue homeostasis (Brestoff et al. 2025; Borcherding and Brestoff 2023), it is not frequent, nor does it occur in every tissue, which raises concerns about the side effects of mitochondrial transplantation. Thus, it is quite important to examine the potential adverse effects, such as off‐target effects and immunogenicity, of transplanted mitochondria. As extracellular mtDNA, cytochrome c, and the mitochondrial enzyme carbamoyl phosphate synthase are well‐studied mitochondrial‐derived damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (Banoth and Cassel 2018; Zhang et al. 2010; Lin et al. 2022; West et al. 2015), the loss of integrity in transplanted mitochondria can cause the release of DAMPs and provoke inflammation through TLR9, NLRP3, and cGAS‐STING recognition (Aloraini 2025). In oncology, mitochondrial transfer has been shown to promote tumor genesis, drug resistance, and altered chemotherapy sensitivity (Ikeda et al. 2025; Hoover et al. 2025; Berridge et al. 2025). Moreover, the differences in molecular mechanisms between mitochondrial transfer and transplantation could lead to a distinct fate for the exogenous mitochondria. During naturally occurring mitochondrial transfer from mesenchymal stem cells to macrophages in mice, microRNA‐containing exosomes released together with the mitochondria dampen macrophage activation by negatively regulating Toll‐like receptor signaling (Phinney et al. 2015). Current mitochondria transplantation methods do not control macrophage activity in this way. To mimic naturally occurring mitochondrial transfer, an alternative approach of transplanting mitochondria donor cells (bone marrow) was also evaluated (Nakai et al. 2024). In this manner, researchers must not only implement designs to improve transplantation efficiency but also honestly confront the negative aspects of mitochondrial transplantation, thereby developing safer methodologies.

Another current problem with mitochondrial transplantation as therapeutic agents is their poor storability. As described in Section 2.2, mitochondria rapidly lose their function unless protected. Simple options, such as storage at −80°C in 10% DMSO (Nukala et al. 2006) or a trehalose solution (Yamaguchi et al. 2007), have been reported, but the protection efficiency was significantly limited in these techniques. Although the abovementioned ZIF‐8 approach suggests a path to long‐term preservation, ZIF‐8 degrades in acidic environments and releases Zn2+, which induces cytotoxicity at high concentrations. Thus, concentration, residence time, and degradation kinetics of ZIF‐8 coating must be tightly managed (Guo et al. 2025; Mu et al. 2025). Another strategy of freezing whole organelle fractions containing mitochondria has been reported as an effective method to improve mitochondrial function even after thawing (Liu, Shimizu, et al. 2025). However, the presence of diverse molecules and organelles complicates the attribution of effects and safety assessment. That is, the lack of established preservation methods undermines the potential of mitochondrial transplantation, making the development of preservation techniques that maintain mitochondrial stability and functionality critically important. Establishing solid preservation methods is also necessary for developing quality control standards for transplantable mitochondria. The cell type and age (passage number) of donor cells affect mitochondrial function, and it has been demonstrated that respiratory phenotypes can vary even within the same recipient cell (Swerdlow 2007; Li et al. 2017; Paliwal et al. 2018). Therefore, it is necessary to define the quality criteria for mitochondria suitable for transplantation, and to understand the parameters critical for each target organ, such as oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and Ca2+ buffering capacity. These accurate and strict definitions enable the provision of donor selection and lot evaluation criteria. For this issue, comprehensive studies are necessary to evaluate the effects of mitochondrial transplantation by varying cell types and donor cell culture conditions.

Future Perspectives: Biologically Engineered Mitochondria for Advanced Transplantation Therapy

Many challenges remain from a viewpoint of biomaterials, yet the potential of mitochondria as therapeutics extends beyond simply improving mitochondrial function. Combining mitochondrial transplantation with intramitochondrial cargo delivery should be more promising for effective therapeutics (Figure 5). The transplantation of genetically engineered mitochondria represents an attractive strategy for personalized medicine. As a proof‐of‐concept study, mitochondria with edited mtDNA using a cytidine deaminase that accumulates in the mitochondria (Mok et al. 2022; Sun, Wang, et al. 2023; Mi et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2024) were transplanted into cultured cells to regulate heteroplasmy rate (Liu, Chen, et al. 2025). The strategy can be applied to mitochondrial transplantation therapy for inherited mitochondrial disorders caused by maternal mtDNA mutations. Transplanting mitochondria carrying wild‐type mtDNA has been explored, but in the current approved therapy, it raises ethical concerns reminiscent of the “three‐parent” debate and therefore requires careful, rigorous oversight. Transplantation of mitochondria from maternal cells after mtDNA editing could be a solution to mitigate many of the risks associated with non‐autologous transfer. Other technologies controlling mtDNA heteroplasmy may also contribute to advanced mitochondrial transplantation to improve maternal mtDNA heteroplasmy. Mitochondrial gene delivery systems (Yoshinaga and Numata 2022, 2024; Yamada et al. 2020; Yoshinaga et al. 2024) might be promising for the biological engineering of mitochondria. For example, we reported a peptide‐based mitochondrial gene delivery system that enables delivery of the full‐length mtDNA to cultured cells (Yoshinaga et al. 2024). The direct exogenous introduction of mtDNA into mitochondria allows control of heteroplasmy rate without genome editing. Furthermore, very recently, mtDNA editing using the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) of the clustered, regularly interspaced, short palindromic repeats/Cas9 (CRISPR/Cas9) system became available for isolated mitochondria (Norota et al. 2025). The in vitro mtDNA editing could be a potent methodology for engineering mitochondria for transplantation.

Future perspective of mitochondrial transplantation with biological engineering, including transplantation after mtDNA editing and introduction of exogenous biomacromolecules. Not only the surface modification of mitochondria but also the engineering of mitochondria itself using biomacromolecules should be promising for enhancing mitochondrial transplantation efficiency.

mtDNA editing is not the only approach for advanced mitochondrial transplantation. Transient modification of mitochondria with exogenous biomacromolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, may enhance the therapeutic effects of transplantation. Co‐delivery of mRNA‐loading polyplexes to mitochondria was reported as described above (Wang, Liu, Hu, et al. 2024), but preliminary modification of mitochondria with proteins or nucleic acids also extends the possibilities for transplantation. Exogenous proteins and nucleic acids, which could enable mitochondria to produce energy more efficiently or to avoid mitophagy for effective integration into the mitochondrial network, can provide therapeutic benefits through enhanced mitochondrial transplantation efficiency. Several studies have already reported the use of nuclear‐expressed mitochondrial‐targeting sequence (MTS)‐fused fluorescent reporter proteins for tracking exogenous mitochondria during transplantation (Chang et al. 2021, 2019; Kitani et al. 2014; Gäbelein et al. 2022; Alway et al. 2023). This strategy can be extended beyond reporters to therapeutic genes improving mitochondrial activity and cellular function, expected to broaden applications and therapeutic impact. Modification of mitochondria with nucleic acids expressing therapeutic genes may provide a prolonged effect compared with protein modification, although technological advancement is required to develop a much more efficient mitochondrial nucleic acid delivery system.

Small‐molecule payloads differ fundamentally from nucleic acids and proteins, as they can rapidly modulate the function of grafted mitochondria or the host microenvironment, such as by alleviating acute inflammatory stress, immediately upon delivery. Coenzyme Q, which is one of the most well‐studied small molecules for improvement of mitochondrial function (Wang, Lilienfeldt, and Hekimi 2024), could be considered a highly promising first‐line option. For combination use, the engineering bottleneck is typically retention and release kinetics. The payload must remain with the mitochondria throughout the critical vulnerability window but not persist so long as to cause unintended side effects from prolonged, non‐specific exposure. While this principle aligns with general mitochondrial drug‐delivery strategies, its combination with transplantation makes timing requirements more precise and experimentally measurable.

Across all cargo classes, combinatorial strategies require expanded quality attributes beyond transplantation‐only paradigms. At minimum, combination products should report mitochondrial membrane potential, membrane integrity, and respiratory competence after cargo loading/formulation, because cargo association procedures that compromise these properties can increase the likelihood of releasing mitochondrial‐derived DAMPs and provoking inflammation (Bernardi et al. 2015; Banoth and Cassel 2018; Zhang et al. 2010; Lin et al. 2022; West et al. 2015; Aloraini 2025). In addition, class‐specific metrics are necessary. Nucleic acids and small molecules require quantitative payload‐per‐mitochondrion and retention/release kinetics, proteins require activity‐per‐mitochondrion and localization competence, and mtDNA‐level interventions require quantitative editing and heteroplasmy characterization alongside functional profiling. Establishing standardized characterization workflows will allow the rational selection of the appropriate cargo class and timescale for each disease mechanism and facilitate cross‐study comparisons as the field moves toward clinically relevant manufacturing, storage, and safety assessment frameworks.

In conclusion, mitochondrial transplantation is transitioning from descriptive biology toward more rationally designed therapeutics. Previously, the focus has been on improving transplantation efficiency, with functional enhancements made to three key factors: (i) targeting, (ii) protection, and (iii) cellular uptake. Advancement in this field requires accumulating foundational knowledge to standardize manufacturing, storage, and quality control processes that balance freshness and preservability, as well as to quantify safety aspects, including biodistribution and off‐target effects. To enhance technological convenience, contributions from material engineering are indispensable. This includes establishing platform technologies enabling autologous mitochondrial transplantation based on mtDNA editing of patient‐derived cells and the direct delivery of biomolecules into mitochondria. Achieving these goals will make the widespread use of mitochondrial transplantation and the creation of customized transplant mitochondria tailored to specific target diseases a realistic development objective.
