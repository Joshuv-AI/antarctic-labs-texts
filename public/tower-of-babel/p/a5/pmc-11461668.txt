The comparative effects of probiotics on growth, antioxidant indices and intestinal histomorphology of broilers under heat stress condition
Authors: Seyed Hamidreza Hashemitabar, Seyedeh Alemeh Hosseinian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Heat stress adversely affects both the productivity and well-being of chickens. Probiotics offer beneficial impacts on the health and growth performance of broilers. The current study investigates the influence of administering of Bacillus (including B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, B. coagulans, and B. indicus) and Lactobacillus (consisting of L. acidophilus, L. plantarum, L. buchneri, and L. rhamnosus) probiotics via drinking water, either singular or combined, on various aspects including growth performance, oxidative stress markers, carcass characteristics, fecal microbial composition, intestinal structure, and intestinal pH in broilers exposed to chronic heat stress. A total of 150 one-day-old broiler chicks were divided into 5 groups: (1) NC, negative control; (2) HS, birds exposed to chronic heat stress; (3) HSpBacil, exposed to chronic heat stress and received Bacillus probiotic; (4) HSpLAB, subjected to chronic heat stress and provided with Lactobacillus probiotic; (5) HSpMix, subjected to chronic heat stress and administered a combined probiotic from Bacillus and Lactobacillus. The HS group exhibited significantly reduced levels of growth performance, carcass traits, and notably affected oxidative stress indices, as well as intestinal pH and histomorphology in the birds. Additionally, the administered probiotics led to increased weight of lymphoid organs, enhanced body weight gain, and improved intestinal histomorphology. Furthermore, the probiotics decreased malondialdehyde and increased total antioxidant capacity in broilers. In conclusion, Bacillus and Lactobacillus probiotics, as single or multi-species, particularly Lactobacillus and combined probiotic, demonstrated potential in alleviating the adverse effects of heat stress in broiler chickens. They could serve as beneficial feed additives and growth enhancers.

Introduction

The escalation of global ambient temperatures has emerged as a significant hazard to poultry in recent years, leading to heat stress and disturbing the development of the poultry industry1. Heat stress adversely affects various aspects of broilers, including their health status, well-being, and growth performance of broilers, resulting in substantial economic losses for the poultry sector globally2. Heat stress negatively affects broilers’ behavior, immune system, and body oxidant status and metabolism. Additionally, it decreases feed intake, weight gain, and carcass characteristics of broilers3. Furthermore, heat stress caused intestinal epithelium injury, physiological instability in the gut, and microbial dysbiosis, all of which reduced nutritional absorption and utilization4. Consequently, researchers have explored a range of dietary strategies aimed at increasing the welfare, health condition, and growth performance of broilers under heat stress5,6. Numerous studies have evaluated different feed additives to mitigate the detrimental effects of heat stress on intestinal health and microbiota balance of broiler chickens7,8. Probiotics, with their beneficial effects on gut health, stand out as valuable feed additives in alleviating the adverse impacts of heat stress on broiler chickens9.

Probiotics serve as natural microbial therapies that enhanced gut health by regulating gut microbiota, preserving intestinal integrity, improving villi structure, and boosting immunity10–12. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated their positive effects on growth performance, carcass yield, immune responses, and meat quality of chickens9. However, the effectiveness of probiotics can vary depending on factors such as type, strain, administration method, dosage, and duration of use13. Among probiotic bacteria, Lactobacillus and Bacillus are the most widely used probiotics in broilers9. The positive effects of Bacillus sp. and Lactobacillus sp. probiotics in broilers have been documented previously14–16. Probiotics as single- or multi-species can elicit different responses in birds17,18. Probiotics come in several forms for poultry use, including drops, sprays, granules, tablets, powders, and sachets18. In experimental studies, probiotics can be administered to the feed or drinking water of broiler chickens; and the diet is most frequently used19,20. While probiotics are typically added to feed in experimental settings, administering them through drinking water is advantageous, particularly to ensure viability in heat-sensitive strains and achieve uniform distribution probiotic21,22. Furthermore, another important benefit of adding probiotics to drinking water is reducing feed consumption and increasing water intake in birds under heat stress23. For this reasons, investigating the effects of different probiotic cocktails delivered via drinking water to heat-stressed birds can provide valuable insights.

Hence, the hypothesis posits that administering probiotics can improve the health and productivity of broiler chickens subjected to chronic heat stress. However, there is limited research comparing the effects of administering probiotic mixtures containing of Bacillus and Lactobacillus either individually or in combination, via drinking water in broilers under chronic heat stress. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of multi-strain Bacillus and Lactobacillus probiotics, administered singly or in combination, on growth performance, carcass traits, oxidative stress indices, intestinal morphology, fecal microflora, and pH of intestinal contents in broiler chickens subjected to chronic heat stress. In the current study, the overarching objective was to determine the most effective probiotic combination for improving the health condition of broiler chicks under heat stress conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethics declaration

The current experiment adhered to animal welfare guidelines and experimental protocols established by the Research Ethics Committee of Shiraz University. Approval was granted by the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of Shiraz University (Approval No. 9730100/400). During the experiment, every effort was made to minimize bird suffering. All procedures were conducted in c accordance with the standards issued by ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.

Broiler chickens and experimental design

The present study was performed at the Avian Diseases Research Center within the School of Veterinary Medicine. A total of 150 one-day-old broiler chicks (Cobb-500) were provided from a commercial hatchery poultry corporation (Shiraz, Fars Province, Iran), and then the chicks were randomly allocated into five groups, each comprising three replicates with 10 birds per replicate. Each group was housed in a separate temperature-controlled room, and each replicate was placed in a distinct wire cage (150 cm length × 100 cm width × 80 cm height). This experiment persisted for 42 days with a lightening program lasting 23 h per day throughout the study. The treatment groups were designated as follows: (1) negative control (NC): Chicks kept at the thermoneutral zone without probiotic administration; (2) heat-stressed group (HS): Chicks subjected to chronic heat stress without probiotic administration; (3) HSpBacil: Chicks subjected to chronic heat stress and received probiotic type 1, consisting of four strains of Bacillus probiotic (listed the following); (4) HSpLAB: Chicks subjected to chronic heat stress and received probiotic type 2, comprising four strains of Lactobacillus probiotic (listed the following); (5) HSpMix: Broilers subjected to chronic heat stress and received probiotic type 3, a combination of probiotic types 1 and 2. Throughout this study, the basal diets were based mainly on maize and soybean meal at three distinct feeding phases, including the starter diet (1 to 10 days of age), the grower diet (11 to 24 days of age), and the finisher diet (25 to 42 days of age) (Table 1). These diets were formulated to meet the nutrient requirements recommended by the National Research Council24. Chronic heat stress induction

In this experiment, broiler chickens were initially raised at a temperature of 32–34 ℃ for the first three days. Subsequently, the temperature was gradually reduced to a constant temperature of around 24 ± 1 ℃, maintained until the birds reached 21 days of age. From day 21 to day 42, the NC group was reared at a normal temperature (24 ± 1 ℃ for 24 h per day), while the HS, HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups were exposed to cyclic chronic heat stress. The heat-stressed groups were subjected to cyclic heat stress at 38 ± 1 ℃ for 8 h (from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.) followed by rearing at an ambient temperature of 24 ± 1 ℃ for 16 h (from 5:00 p.m. to 9:00 a.m.)25.

Compound probiotic preparation

In this study, from 21 days until 42 days of age, the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups received three different probiotic cocktails via drinking water. The HSpBacil group received probiotic type 1, containing four strains of Bacillus (namely B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, B. coagulans, and B. indicus) in a 1:1:1:1 ratio, with a concentration of 4 × 109 colony-forming unit (CFU) per gram. Similarly, the HSpLAB group received probiotic type 2, containing four probiotic strains of Lactobacillus (consisting of L. acidophilus, L. plantarum, L. buchneri, and L. rhamnosus) in a 1:1:1:1 ratio, also at a concentration of 4 × 109 CFU/g. The HSpMix group received a combined probiotic cocktail (probiotic type 3), comprising eight various strains of Bacillus and Lactobacillus, as mentioned earlier, also at a concentration of 4 × 109 CFU/g. These probiotic bacteria were obtained from Pardis Roshd Mehregan Company (Shiraz, Fars Province, Iran) and were in lyophilized forms. During the entire experimental period, the probiotic powders were suspended daily in drinking water (at a rate of 1 gr per liter) to obtain the compound probiotics at a total daily dosage of 4 × 109 CFU/bird in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups. During the experiment, the NC and HS groups received fresh drinking water without probiotics.

Growth performance assay

During the experimental trial, all broilers were individually weighed every week following a 12 h feed deprivation. The body weight and daily feed consumption are recorded per replicate to calculate the average body weight (BW), average daily body weight gain (BWG), average daily feed intake (DFI), and average feed conversion ratio (FCR). These terms were recorded at three different phases, including starter (0–21 days), grower (22–42 days), and overall (1–42 days) phases, on a replicate basis. Additionally, health status and mortality were monitored and recorded daily throughout the entire experiment.

Carcass characteristics assay

On days 21, 32, and 42 of the experiment, four broilers from each treatment group (one bird per replicate) were randomly chosen, weighed, and humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation to determine carcass traits. Following euthanasia, the birds were de-feathered, and various organs including breast muscle, bursa of Fabricius, spleen, liver, and intestine were carefully removed and weighed using a digital scale (± 0.01). In addition, the intestinal length was measured and documented for each bird.

Intestinal histomorphological and goblet cell number assay

On days 21, 32, and 42 of the experiment, the ileum and jejunum from four birds per treatment group (one bird per replicate) were used for histological analysis. Sections of approximately 2 cm from of the jejunum (defined as the gut length from the end of the duodenal loop to the origin of Meckel's diverticulum) and ileum (from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction) were collected. Then, the collected samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and subjected to histological processing using standard techniques involving hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)26 staining and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)27 staining. On H&E-stained slides, various morphological variables of the ileum and jejunum were assessed using a light microscope with image analysis software (Dino Capture 2.0). The measurements, including villus height (VH), villus width (VW), and crypt depth (CD), were conducted on the five villi per bird28,29. The VH was defined as the distance from the base of the villi to the crypt mouth. The CD was measured from the distance from the base of the villus to the muscularis mucosa28,29 (Fig. 1B). The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was also calculated. Furthermore, the thickness of different layers, including the mucosa, submucosa, and muscular layers in the intestine samples was evaluated on the five villi per bird as described previously30. Blood sampling and serum antioxidant activity assay

At ages 21, 32, and 42 days, blood samples (3 ml each) were taken from the wing veins of eight birds per each treatment group (two birds per replicate). The collected samples were transferred to collection tubes without any anticoagulant and left at room temperature approximately 2 h to allow clotting. Following clotting, the blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm at room temperature for 10 min to separate the serum. The serum samples were collected and stored at − 20 ℃ until further laboratory analysis. Serum levels of total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and malondialdehyde (MDA), as oxidative stress indices, were analyzed using a commercial ELISA kit (ZellBio GmbH, Lonsee, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions14.

Fecal microbial population assay

At the end of the experiment (d 42), adequate amounts of pooled and fresh feces (50 g) were collected from one bird in each replicate to perform the intestinal microbial population assay. The collected samples were weighted, homogenized with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then subjected to a tenfold serial dilution in PBS. The total bacterial count was measured by culturing on plate count agar (Merck, Germany) and incubating aerobically at 37 ℃ for 48 h. Coliform bacteria were determined on VRBL (Violet Red Bile Lactose) agar (Merck, Germany) under similar incubation condition. Lactic acid bacteria were assessed on the MRS (de Man, Rogosa, Sharpe) agar (Merck, Germany) and incubated aerobically at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The microbial count was presented as log10 CFU/g of feces. The count of microflora was expressed as log10 CFU/g of feces. To count bacterial spores, homogenized fecal samples in PBS were put in an 80 ℃ water bath for 10 min, followed by rapid chilling to stimulate spore germination. These samples were then serially diluted in PBS before plating on plate count agar (Merck, Germany) and incubating aerobically at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The spore count was expressed as spores/gram32,33.

Intestinal digesta pH assay

On days 21, 32, and 42 of the study, the adequate digest contents of ileum and jejunum from slaughtered birds were collected and transferred into clean and pre-weighted 50 mL centrifuge tubes. Distilled deionized water was added to intestinal digesta samples at a ratio of 1:10, and the mixture was homogenized thoroughly. The pH level of the homogenized samples was measured using a suitable pH meter (Basic 20, Crison, Spain) as established previously34,35.

Statistical analysis

The statistical design used for the present study was completely randomized. Replicate present as the experimental unit, resulting in three replications per treatment, for statistical analysis of feed intake, weight gain, and FCR. Live body weight, carcass characteristics, serum oxidative stress indices, fecal microbial population assay, intestinal histomorphology, and intestinal pH were all analyzed on an individual bird basis (with individual birds as the experimental unit). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data within each parameter. The obtained results from Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that all data exhibited a normal distribution with similar variances. Consequently, to evaluate the effects of various treatments, all data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS) for Windows version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Subsequently, the Bonferroni post hoc test was utilized to compare the differences in mean across different parameters to evaluate pairwise differences. A significance level of P < 0.05 was applied. Results were presented as mean values with pooled standard error of the mean (SEM) for each treatment group. All figures were drawn using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (Graph Pad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Growth performance

The influence of three distinct probiotic types in drinking water on the growth performance of broilers under chronic heat stress is summarized in Table 2. In the starter phase (1–21 days), there were no statistically significant differences in daily BWG, FI, and FCR among the treatment groups (P > 0.05). At 42 days of age, the broilers in the HS group exhibited a significant lower (approximately 20%) body weight compared to the NC group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the HS group exhibited significantly lower BWG compared to the NC group during the grower and overall phases, approximately 30 and 21%, respectively (Table 2, P < 0.05). At the end of the experiment (day 42), final body weights in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups were notably higher than those in the HS group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the BWG of the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups was substantially higher compared to HS group during the grower and overall periods (P < 0.05). As seen in Table 2, the HSpLAB and HSpMix groups showed the highest BW and BWG on days 21 and 42, among the probiotic-receiving groups (P < 0.05). Throughout the growth phase, there were no significant changes (P > 0.05) in BW and BWG between the NC group and the probiotic-receiving groups (HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix). All heat-stressed groups (HS, HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix) demonstrated considerably lower DFI during the grower and overall periods compared to the NC group. There were no significant differences in the DFI among the HS, HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups during the grower and overall phases. During the growing phase, the HS group had significantly a higher FCR than the NC, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups. Similarly, during the overall phase, the HS group had significantly higher FCR compared to the other treatment groups. Carcass characteristics

Table 3 demonstrates the effect of three distinct probiotic compounds added to drinking water on carcass traits. On day 21, there were no notable differences in carcass traits among the various treatments (P > 0.05). As depicted in Table 3, chronic heat stress notably decreased the spleen weight (by 36% and 50%) and the bursa of Fabricius weight (by 30 and 43%) in the HS group on days 32 and 42, respectively (P < 0.05). At day 42, probiotic administration notably increased the weights of the spleen and bursa of Fabricius in birds of the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups compared to those in the HS group (P < 0.05). There were no noticeable differences in spleen and bursa of Fabricius weights across different study days among the groups receiving various probiotics. During study, liver and the abdominal fat weights did not remarkably differ between the various treatment groups (P > 0.05). On days 32 and 42, intestinal weight was substantially lower in the HS group compared to the NC group, by approximately 19 and 37%, respectively. Additionally, the intestinal length of birds in the HS group was significantly shorter, by around 26%, than that of the birds in the NC group on day 42. The probiotic usage in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups increased the intestinal length and weight, though only the difference in intestinal length was statistically significant (P < 0.05, Table 3). Compared to the NC group, breast muscle weight significantly decreased in all heat-stressed groups on day 42. Probiotics increased the breast meat weight in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups compared to the HS group at days 32 and 42, though this difference was not statistically significant (Table 3). Discussion

Various researchers have shown that administering probiotic can be a beneficial nutritional approach to reduce the harmful effects of heat stress23. However, a limited studies have been performed to compare the impacts of Lactobacillus and Bacillus probiotics in broilers exposed to chronic heat stress. In this study, multi-strain Lactobacillus and Bacillus probiotics, either singular or combined, were delivered via drinking water. The obtained results indicate that all probiotics used improved growth performance, carcass traits, antioxidant status, and the health of the ileum and jejunum in broilers exposed to chronic heat stress. Additionally, the findings suggest that the positive effects of Lactobacillus, either alone (probiotic type 2) or in combination with Bacillus (probiotic type 3) had a greater effect on the growth performance compared to Bacillus probiotic alone (probiotic type 1).

The results of this study reveal that broilers exposed to heat stress (HS group) had noticeably higher levels of MDA and lower TAC compared to broilers kept at normal room temperature (NC group), indicating that heat stress induced oxidative stress in the birds (Fig. 3A,B). Previous studies have documented the link between heat stress and oxidative stress in broilers4,11. The findings obtained in the current study are consistent with previous studies, which state that heat stress in broilers can cause an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation and MDA production1,36,37. In this regard, elevating TAC levels in birds can help alleviate the deleterious effects of heat stress by suppressing ROS production36. In the present experiment, probiotics in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups led to a notable reduction in circulating MDA levels and a significant increase in TAC levels compared to the HS group (Fig. 3A). These results indicate that all the probiotic mixtures studied exhibited antioxidant activity and could protect broilers from chronic heat stress by increasing total antioxidant activity in the body and reducing MDA levels. In agreement with these findings, previous literature has shown that probiotics can enhance levels of antioxidant activity and decreased MDA production in stressful conditions38. The findings from the present study imply the use of probiotic mixtures containing Lactobacillus and Bacillus bacteria through drinking water is beneficial in enhancing the oxidant status of broilers under stressful conditions.

In this study, heat stress significantly decreased the final body weight of the birds in the HS group on day 42 (− 20%) compared to those in the NC group (Table 2). The results indicate that heat stress decreased BWG in birds in the HS group during the grower and overall phases by nearly 30 and 21%, respectively. Furthermore, heat stress substantially lowered DFI during the grower and overall periods. As shown in Table 2, the FCR in the HS group significantly increased compared to the NC, HSpBasil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups during the grower and overall phases. These findings stated that chronic heat stress is a considerable challenge for broilers, resulting in a reduced growth rate, as documented in relevant studies and literature3,39. Heat stress negatively affected growth performance due to digestive enzymes disruption, intestinal microbiota imbalance, decreased feed digestibility, and impaired nutrient absorption in the gastrointestinal tract40–42. Moreover, the decrease in the thyroid hormone levels in the heat-stressed broilers could contribute to reduced growth performance23. In the present study, the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups had higher levels of BW and BWG compared to the HS group at 21 and 42 days of age (Table 2). Consistent with the findings of this study, Sugiharto et al.43 and Mullur et al.44 reported that probiotics increased plasma levels of thyroid hormones (T3 and T4), enhanced activity of digestive enzymes, and improved nutrient digestibility in broilers exposed to heat stress, leading to improved growth performance. They also demonstrated that enhanced levels of T3 and T4 led to an increase in the length of the intestine villi and their absorption capacity, thereby controlling the metabolic processes of the bird’s body’s and enhancing growth performance43,44. In this study, birds receiving Lactobacillus probiotic (HSpLAB group) and mixed probiotic (HSpMix group) had significantly higher final BW and BWG at the end of the experiment compared to those in the HSpBacil group receiving Bacillus probiotic (Table 2). Based on the results of the current study, it can be inferred that probiotics, particularly the Lactobacillus probiotic alone and combined probiotic (containing both Lactobacillus and Bacillus), had greater effect on growth rate compared to the Bacillus probiotic alone in broilers under chronic heat stress.

On days 32 and 42, the HS group exhibited lower spleen weights (− 36 and − 50%) and bursa of Fabricius weights (− 30 and − 43%) compared to the NC group, respectively (Table 3). Thus, it can be suggested that chronic heat stress adversely affected the weight of immune organs (spleen and bursa of Fabricius) in birds, consistent with previous research45,46. As presented in Table 3, probiotics added to drinking water significantly increased the weight of immune organs in birds in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups compared to the HS group. Based on these findings, it can be suggested that Bacillus and Lactobacillus probiotics mitigate the negative effects of heat stress on immune organs health. These results agree well with previous studies investigating the impacts of probiotics on the immune system47,48. The weight of the immune organs can be considered a valuable indicator to evaluate the health status of the immune system in birds38. Therefore, the elevation in spleen and bursa of Fabricius weight in the present study is a meaningful and valuable finding that may indicate enhanced immunity in heat-stressed broilers that received probiotic. According to the results by Chen et al.49 and Sugiharto et al.50, probiotics lead to an increase in the weight of lymphoid organs by enhancing the numbers of lymphocyte cells in lymphoid organs, maintaining intestinal balance, and showing antioxidant properties. As observed in Table 3, heat stress significantly decreased breast meat weight and intestinal weight and length in the HS group compared to the NC group at 42 days. In the current study, neither heat stress nor probiotics had significant impacts on liver or abdominal fat weight, in line with the previous study51. At 42 days, probiotic-receiving birds had greater breast weights, intestinal weights, and intestinal lengths than the HS group; however, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 3). Although these findings were not statistically significant, they are valuable and notable because they indicate the positive effects of probiotics on bird carcasses under stress. Previous studies have also shown that probiotics had beneficial impacts on carcass traits in birds exposed to heat stress52,53. Probiotics have been shown to increase carcass characteristics in broilers, linked to reduced blood cortisol levels, improved diet nutrient utilization, and improved gut microbiota43,54,55. The outcomes of this investigation suggest that the Bacillus and Lactobacillus probiotics, either alone or in combination, have beneficial effects in reducing the detrimental effects of heat stress on carcass characteristics of broilers under chronic heat stress. Based on these findings, can infer that probiotics serve as valuable supplement for stressed broilers.

The health status and arrangement of intestinal epithelial cells play a vital role in nutrient absorption and the protection of the body from harmful microorganisms invading the bloodstream56,57. In the current investigation, on day 42, birds in the HS group had significantly lower ileal and jejunal crypt depth, mucosal layer depth, villus height, and villus depth compared to those in the NC group (Tables 4, 5). Environmental factors, particularly chronic heat stress, are one of the critical causes that influence gut health and disrupt intestinal structure23. Heat stress damaged the intestinal villi structure, resulting in a reduction in villus height and crypt depth, extensive desquamation of epithelial cells in the tips of villi, and a negative impact on nutrient absorption and digestion58. Similar to the obtained results, Mazzoni et al.59 and Santos et al.60 reported that heat stress decreased intestinal wall thickness, mucous layer height, villous height, and crypt depth in broilers. In this study, probiotics significantly enhanced the mucosal layer depth, crypt depth, villus height, and villus depth in the ileum and jejunum of broilers in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups on day 42 compared to the HS group. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have shown that Bacillus and Lactobacillus probiotics have positive impacts on intestinal histomorphological measurements61,62. Moreover, in the current study, the GC number in the ileum and jejunum was significantly lower in the HS group than in the NC group. These findings are in line with previous study that showed the goblet cell numbers in the ileal and jejunal villi reduced in chickens under heat stress23. Goblet cells play a main role in forming the mucus layer in the intestine, which is an important barrier for intestinal epithelium tissue63. Additionally, the number of GC in ileal villi in the studied broilers receiving probiotics was substantially higher than that in the HS group. Several studies have showed that Bacillus-based and Lactobacillus-based probiotics increase the goblet cell population in various parts of the intestine in birds exposed to heat stress64–66. On day 42, the jejunal VH:CD ratio increased in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups compared to the NC and HS groups. It has been claimed that probiotics improve the structure of intestinal villi and crypts in heat-stressed broilers40. Therefore, the results of the present study suggest that probiotic administration through drinking water plays a beneficial role in the ileal and jejunal morphology of broilers exposed to chronic heat stress, possibly by reducing the excessive release of proinflammatory agents and decreasing corticosterone production23,64. The outcomes of this study indicate that all probiotic mixtures used, especially multi strains Bacillus probiotic, can be utilized as promoters of intestinal structure in broiler chickens to increase intestinal health status during chronic heat stress.

As demonstrated in Fig. 5, the ileal pH increased significantly in the HS group compared to the NC group on day 42, in agreement with prior study findings4. The adverse effects of chronic heat stress on intestinal health and disruption of the intestinal barrier have been documented previously67. The elevation in intestinal pH in heat-subjected broilers may be a result from an imbalance in the intestinal microbiota and an increase in pathogen microorganisms following heat stress4,68. In the present investigation, the pH of both ileal and jejunal contents decreased in the HSpBacil, HSpLAB, and HSpMix groups on days 32 and 42 compared to the HS and NC groups (Fig. 5A,B). In agreement with these findings, Sugiharto and Ranjitkar69 also noted that probiotics decreased the gut pH in chicken due to the production of organic acids and short-chain fatty acids. Decreasing the gut pH in heat-stressed birds can be beneficial in preventing pathogen colonization in the gut tissue and improving the gut barrier function against pathogens70,71. The findings of the current study suggest that probiotics serve as a valuable microbial intervention for broilers, helping to regulate the gut pH and enhance intestinal health.

In the present study, neither heat stress nor probiotic usage had no significant impact on the population of fecal bacteria in the studied broilers (Fig. 4). These findings are contrast with the reports by Song et al.72 who stated that birds exposed to heat stress exhibited a reduction in the number of beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, and an increase in harmful bacteria number like coliforms and Clostridium in the small intestine. While most studies have demonstrated the beneficial impacts of probiotics on the microbial population in the gut in both healthy and stressed birds70, similar to our findings, Sohail et al.52,73 have also reported no significant effects of probiotic on the intestinal microbial population. However, the current study showed that the addition of Bacillus probiotic to drinking water significantly increased the number of spores in the intestinal contents in broilers (Fig. 4C). These results may be explained by variations in different number, types, and doses of probiotic bacteria used across different studies43.

The authors recommend that this study be performed in poultry farms and the effects of probiotic administration were investigated in broilers under high ambient temperature conditions. In the next study, the authors try to investigate the effect of these probiotic mixtures in broilers under different stressful conditions to choose the best probiotic combination for use in poultry industry.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study found that chronic heat stress induced oxidative stress in broilers, reflecting by increased serum levels of MDA and decreased levels of TAC. Furthermore, the broilers exposed to heat stress showed reduced growth performance and lymphoid organ weights. This study also revealed alteration in the pH and histomorphological indices of the ileum and jejunum due to chronic heat stress. Additionally, it was showed that administration of studied Lactobacillus probiotic or Bacillus probiotic, either as a single species or a combined probiotic compound, via drinking water can improve growth performance, carcass traits, and intestinal morphology in broiler chickens under heat stress. These positive effects on growth performance were particularly pronounced with the Lactobacillus probiotic and the combined probiotic mixture. In conclusion, all studied multi-strain probiotics were effective in attenuating the negative effects of heat stress in broiler chickens, but Lactobacillus probiotic (including L. acidophilus, L. plantarum, L. buchneri, and L. rhamnosus) both alone and in combination with Bacillus probiotic (including B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, B. coagulans, and B. indicus) had stronger effects on growth performance compared to Bacillus probiotic alone.
