Endogenous metabolite signaling orchestrates tumor immune evasion
Authors: Xiaorui Liu, Congqing Jiang, Youjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndogenous metabolites are a class of bioactive metabolic molecules synthesized endogenously within the organism that mediate core physiological processes, spanning bioactive small-molecule compounds, carbohydrates, nucleotides, and gut microbial metabolites. Serving as the fundamental basis for sustaining normal physiological homeostasis, these metabolites also exert pivotal regulatory effects within the tumor microenvironment, especially during the progression of cancer immune evasion. This review encapsulates the major classifications, biological functions, and underlying mechanisms of endogenous metabolites, with a dedicated focus on dissecting the molecular pathways through which these metabolites either facilitate or suppress tumor immune evasion. Additionally, it delves into the interplay between endogenous metabolites and immune checkpoint blockade therapy, with the ultimate goal of offering innovative research insights and promising therapeutic targets for the advancement of cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: Tumor metabolic reprogramming, Endogenous metabolites, Immune evasion, Immune checkpoint blockade therapy

Introduction

Under pressure from genetic mutations and metabolic stress, tumor cells adapt to their nutritional environment by remodeling core metabolic pathways, enabling sustained growth. Tumors from different tissues often display distinct metabolic profiles, yet most share common features—most notably the Warburg effect: increased glucose uptake and lactate production (Pavlova & Thompson, 2016). Disruptions in the TCA cycle and mitochondrial respiration not only disturb energy balance but also impair immune recognition and promote tumor progression. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), immune cells also undergo metabolic changes to support their functions. However, competition for nutrients like glucose and amino acids often leads to immune dysfunction, weakening antitumor responses (Mao et al., 2026). Nutrient availability in the TME shapes immune cell metabolism and function, while stromal cells may support tumor growth through metabolite exchange.

Tumor immune evasion is a key mechanism by which cancer cells escape immune destruction. Recent studies highlight endogenous metabolites as crucial regulators of immune cell behavior and TME remodeling. These metabolites influence not only metabolic adaptation but also gene expression and signaling through interactions with proteins such as histones and transcriptional regulators. Tumors exploit these metabolites to manipulate immune cells and establish an immunosuppressive environment.

The role of endogenous metabolites in tumor immune evasion is an emerging focus in immunology. This review summarizes how endogenous metabolites reshape antitumor immunity in the TME and interact with ICB therapy, offering insights for metabolism-based immunotherapies.

Types and functions of endogenous metabolites

Endogenous metabolites are small molecules and their derivatives produced via metabolic pathways under normal or pathological conditions. They participate in cellular energy metabolism, biosynthesis, and signaling and play a central role in the tumor immune microenvironment. Based on chemical structure and metabolic origins, they can be classified into the following categories.

Organic acids

Organic acids are fundamental products of cellular energy metabolism, including lactate, succinate, and fumarate. Among these, lactate, the end product of aerobic glycolysis, accumulates at high concentrations in the TME and serves as a key immunosuppressive signaling molecule (Jin et al., 2025). Succinate and fumarate, when accumulated due to mitochondrial dysfunction, act as oncometabolites that reshape the immune landscape via epigenetic reprogramming (Ryan et al., 2019).

Amino acids and their derivatives

Amino acids serve not only as fundamental building blocks for protein synthesis but also as key regulators of metabolic reprogramming in immune cells, thereby playing a critical role in modulating immune cell function. For instance, arginine enhances antitumor immune responses by supporting T cell metabolism and functional activation (Geiger et al., 2016). The tryptophan metabolite kynurenine can activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) pathway, leading to T cell apoptosis and the establishment of an immune-tolerant phenotype (Stone & Williams, 2023). Glutamine supplies carbon and nitrogen sources for lymphocyte activation, fulfilling the nutritional and energetic requirements for rapid lymphocyte proliferation (Newsholme, 2001).

Carbohydrates and their derivatives

Glucose is the main energy source for both immune cells and tumor cells. In the TME, high glycolytic activity in tumor cells rapidly depletes glucose, limiting glycolysis and effector function in tumor-infiltrating T cells. Additionally, carbohydrate derivatives like glycosaminoglycans contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling and cell recognition, influencing immune cell infiltration and activation (Li et al., 2025).

Lipids and their metabolites

Lipid metabolites, including fatty acids, prostaglandins, sphingolipids, cholesterol and its derivatives, play critical roles in regulating systemic inflammatory and immune responses. Among them, prostaglandin E2 exerts immunosuppressive effects by inhibiting dendritic cell (DC) function and recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Obermajer et al., 2011). Meanwhile, sphingolipid metabolites participate in the regulation of biological processes such as T cell receptor signaling and apoptosis (Nakamura et al., 1996).

Nucleotides and their derivatives

Nucleotide molecules such as ATP and adenosine not only serve as the cellular energy currency and fundamental building blocks for nucleic acid synthesis but also act as extracellular signaling molecules regulating immune responses (Kepp et al., 2017). ATP released during cellular damage or stress conditions activates purinergic receptor pathways, promoting inflammatory responses (Martinon et al., 2002). In contrast, adenosine suppresses effector T-cell functions through A2A receptor pathways, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion (Zarek et al., 2008).

Polyamines and their derivatives

Polyamines are small, positively charged alkylamines, primarily including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine. In the TME, polyamine levels are significantly elevated, directly promoting tumor cell proliferation and modulating immune cell functions as signaling molecules. They induce the expansion of MDSCs and promote M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), thereby suppressing antitumor immunity. Moreover, polyamines are directly taken up by CD8+ T cells, inhibiting their proliferation and effector functions, as reflected by reduced secretion of interferon-gamma and granzyme B. Additionally, polyamine metabolism affects histone methylation, regulating the expression of immunosuppressive genes (Bundgen et al., 2025).

Neurotransmitters

Current research indicates that both tumor cells and immune cells express neurotransmitter receptors, and certain tumor cells can autonomously synthesize and secrete neurotransmitters. Elevated levels of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and its metabolite, 4-acetamidobutanoic acid (4-Ac-GABA), in various tumor tissues suppress CD8+ T-cell infiltration and functional activation by activating GABA receptor pathways, thereby promoting immune evasion. Histamine modulates DC function and T-cell polarization through histamine receptors (Zhou et al., 2024a). Neurotransmitters serve as critical molecular bridges connecting the nervous system and the TME via neuro-immune-tumor signaling crosstalk (Xu et al., 2025).

Gut microbiota metabolites

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by gut microbiota, such as butyrate and propionate, regulate immune cell functions in distal TME through the “gut-tumor axis”. Studies have confirmed that butyrate effectively enhances the effector functions of CD8+ T cells, improves the therapeutic efficacy of ICB, and alleviates tumor immunosuppression (Bachem et al., 2025; Chen & Fang, 2025).

Modes of action of endogenous metabolites

Endogenous metabolites are not only energy sources and biosynthetic precursors but also regulators of protein function and gene expression, influencing cellular behavior. In the TME, they mediate tumor–immune cell signaling and promote immune escape. Accumulating evidence points to three primary modes of action: covalent protein modification, direct binding to functional proteins, and competitive protein binding (Fig. 1) (Wang & Lei, 2018).

Action modes and mechanisms of endogenous metabolites

Endogenous metabolites regulate tumor immune evasion via three main mechanisms.

(a) Covalent modification of proteins. Metabolites act as donors for post-translational modifications (PTMs), altering protein function and immune outcomes.

Acetylation (Acetyl-CoA): Acetylation of SEPP1 reduces MDSC viability and activates CD8+ T cell immunity.

Methylation (SAM): Methylation of METTL3 suppresses type I interferon responses, promoting immune evasion.

Lactylation (Lactate): Lactate drives M2 macrophage polarization, impairs p53 function, and inhibits ferroptosis.

Succination (Fumarate): Fumarate modifies MAVS and KEAP1, enhancing antiviral signaling or supporting tumor cell survival.

Glutamylation (ATP): Glutamylation of YAP1 increases PD-L1 expression and facilitates immune evasion.

(b) Direct interaction with functional proteins. Metabolites directly bind membrane receptors, metabolic enzymes, transcription factors, or immune checkpoints.

Membrane receptors: ATP binds P2X7, activating inflammasome and pro-inflammatory immunity; lactate binds GPR81, mediating immunosuppression; PGE2 binds EP2/EP4, inhibiting T and NK cells while promoting MDSC expansion, Treg function, and M2 polarization of TAMs.

Metabolic enzymes & transcription factors: Fumarate binds GAPDH, regulating glycolysis; succinate stabilizes HIF-1α, driving pro-inflammatory gene expression; 2-HG inhibits (α-ketoglutarate) α-KG-dependent dioxygenases, causing hypermethylation and silencing of immune-related genes.

Immune checkpoints: Cholesterol binds PD-L1, stabilizing its expression and enhancing immunosuppression.

(c) Competitive binding. Metabolites compete with natural ligands for binding sites on enzymes, receptors, or transporters. Citrate competes for phosphofructokinase (PFK), providing feedback inhibition of glycolysis. Kynurenine competes for the AHR, activating immunosuppressive gene transcription. Lactate competes with pyruvate for monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), impairing mitochondrial metabolism.

Covalent modification of proteins

Endogenous metabolites can modify target proteins through covalent binding, altering their spatial structure and biological functions, thereby regulating cellular signaling and gene expression (Fig. 1(a)). Among these, the covalent modification of histones by metabolites mediates epigenetic reprogramming and represents the most representative mode of action. Specific types are detailed as follows.

Acetylation

Acetyl-CoA serves as an acetyl donor for protein acetylation, regulating enzymatic activity, stability, and interaction networks by altering the charge distribution and spatial conformation of target proteins. For instance, KAT8-mediated acetylation of SEPP1 at residues K247/K249 impairs the viability of MDSCs via the LRP8 axis, thereby activating the antitumor immune response of CD8+ T cells (Zhu et al., 2025c).

Methylation

S-adenosylmethionine acts as a methyl donor for protein methylation, catalyzed by methyltransferases to broadly regulate protein function. For example, SETD1A-mediated dimethylation of METTL3 at lysine 513 enhances its enzymatic activity, leading to increased RNA m6A modification. This modification suppresses endogenous retroviral element expression and impairs type I interferon responses, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion (She et al., 2026).

Lactylation

Lactate serves as a precursor for histone lactylation, a newly identified epigenetic modification that regulates gene transcription and influences macrophage polarization and T cell function. In the TME, elevated lactate levels drive M2 macrophage polarization via histone lactylation, contributing to immunosuppression (Jin et al., 2025). Lactylation of non-histone proteins also plays key roles: it impairs p53 transcriptional activity to promote tumorigenesis (Zong et al., 2024); enhances homologous recombination repair through lactylation of MRE11, BRCA1, and NBS1, leading to chemoresistance (Shou et al., 2025); and stabilizes GCLC mRNA via NSUN2 lactylation to boost glutathione synthesis and inhibit ferroptosis (Niu et al., 2025).

Succination and succinylation

Succination refers to the non-enzymatic reaction of fumarate with cysteine thiols on proteins, forming S-(2-succinyl)cysteine. This reaction is particularly prominent in renal cancer cells deficient in fumarate hydratase. Succination-modified KEAP1 can stabilize the NRF2 protein, upregulating the antioxidant factor HMOX1 and enhancing tumor cell survival (Kinch et al., 2011). In virus-stimulated macrophages, the aspartate-arginosuccinate shunt can promote cytosolic fumarate accumulation, driving MAVS protein succination and enhancing RLR-mediated antiviral signaling (Xia et al., 2025).

Succinylation involves the covalent attachment of a succinyl group to lysine residues in proteins, and can occur through either enzymatic or non-enzymatic mechanisms. Desuccinylation is primarily mediated by SIRT5 and SIRT7, whereas lysine succinyltransferases such as KAT2A and CPT1A facilitate succinylation. In macrophages, LPS stimulation elevates succinate levels and downregulates SIRT5 expression, resulting in enhanced succinylation of glycolytic enzymes. For example, succinylation of pyruvate kinase suppresses its enzymatic activity and promotes its nuclear translocation, where it interacts with HIF1-α to upregulate IL-1β expression (Liu et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2017a). Additionally, succinylation has been shown to enhance the antigen-presenting function of DCs (Zhang et al., 2023b).

Glutamylation

Glutamylation is an ATP-dependent post-translational modification (PTM) carried out by tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL) enzymes, which add glutamate chains to specific glutamate residues on target proteins. This modification is reversed by cytosolic carboxypeptidases. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, TTLL1-mediated glutamylation of YAP1 at the E100 site promotes its nuclear translocation, leading to increased PD-L1 expression and facilitating tumor immune evasion (Chen et al., 2025). Glutamylation may influence group 3 innate lymphoid cell development via IL-7R, but its precise role in the TME remains unclear (Liu et al., 2017).

Other covalent modifications

Beyond the types mentioned above, metabolites can also mediate various other PTMs, including palmitoylation, β-hydroxybutyrylation, crotonylation, pyruvylation and vitcylation. These covalent modification mechanisms directly translate changes in metabolite abundance into regulatory signals for protein function and gene expression, playing a critical role in the interaction between tumor cells and immune cells.

Different modification types have distinct spatiotemporal patterns and mechanisms. Lactylation is prominent in hypoxic environments (Li et al., 2025), acetylation reflects cellular energy status (Webster et al., 2014), and methylation contributes to long-term epigenetic programming. A deeper understanding of these modifications and their regulatory networks may offer new strategies for tumor immunotherapy targeting metabolic pathways.

Direct Interaction with functional proteins

Some endogenous metabolites can directly bind to functional proteins, altering their activity through allosteric regulation, active site competition, or other mechanisms, thereby modulating cellular signaling pathways and biological functions (Fig. 1(b)). Specific modes of action include the following.

Metabolites as signaling molecules binding to membrane receptors

Extracellular metabolites can bind to cell surface membrane receptors, activating downstream signaling pathways. For example, extracellular ATP can bind to purinergic receptors P2X7, activating the inflammasome and pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Kvist et al., 2014). These metabolite-receptor interactions represent an important mechanism of metabolic-immune crosstalk within the TME.

Metabolites directly regulating metabolic enzymes and transcription factors

Intracellular metabolites can directly bind to metabolic enzymes or transcription factors, altering their biological activity. For instance, succinate can stabilize hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α under inflammatory conditions, promoting pro-inflammatory gene expression (Tannahill et al., 2013). Fumarate can bind to and inhibit GAPDH enzyme activity, regulating glycolytic flux (Blatnik et al., 2008). The oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate, produced by mutant IDH enzymes, can competitively inhibit multiple α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)-dependent dioxygenases, leading to histone and DNA hypermethylation (Xu et al., 2011).

Metabolites regulating immune checkpoint molecule function

Recent studies have confirmed that certain metabolites can directly interact with immune checkpoint molecules, modulating their function. For example, cholesterol can bind to the PD-L1 protein, stabilizing its expression on tumor cells and thereby potentiating immunosuppressive signaling (Wang et al., 2022).

Competitive binding to proteins

Endogenous metabolites can interfere with normal signaling and metabolic regulation by competing with natural ligands for binding sites on proteins (Fig. 1(c)). Specific mechanisms include the following.

Competition for enzyme active sites

Metabolic intermediates can act as competitive inhibitors, regulating the activity of key metabolic enzymes. For instance, citrate can competitively inhibit phosphofructokinase (PFK) activity, providing feedback regulation of glycolytic flux (Garland et al., 1963). Changes in the ATP/ADP ratio can competitively bind to AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), regulating cellular energy homeostasis (Hardie et al., 2012).

Competition for receptor binding sites

Some metabolites can mimic the structure of natural ligands and competitively bind to cell membrane receptors. For example, the tryptophan metabolite kynurenine can competitively bind to the AHR, activating the transcription of immunosuppressive genes (Campesato et al., 2020). Lactate can competitively bind to the GPR81 receptor, regulating adipocyte function and immune cell activity (Liu et al., 2009).

Competition for nutrient transporters

Metabolites can also compete for transporters, affecting nutrient uptake and signaling. For example, lactate and pyruvate share monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) (Halestrap & Price, 1999). High concentrations of lactate can competitively inhibit pyruvate uptake, interfering with mitochondrial metabolism and cellular redox balance (Corbet et al., 2018).

TME and tumor immune evasion

TME

The TME is a dynamic ecosystem that supports tumor survival, comprising cellular (tumor, immune, stromal, and vascular endothelial cells) and non-cellular components (extracellular matrix and soluble mediators). It is typically marked by hypoxia, low pH, high interstitial fluid pressure, and nutrient deprivation. Although immune surveillance may occur early in tumorigenesis, the TME progressively becomes immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting as the disease advances. (Yu et al., 2025b).

Unique physicochemical characteristics of the TME

Hypoxia

The rapid growth of solid tumors impairs local blood flow and leads to abnormal vascular structure, causing widespread intratumoral hypoxia. Under low-oxygen conditions, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are stabilized, transcriptionally reprogramming hundreds of downstream genes. HIFs upregulate glycolysis enzymes, promoting aerobic glycolysis (Kumar & Gabrilovich, 2014). Hypoxia also modulates immune cell function. HIFs promote Treg differentiation and immunosuppressive activity, suppress CD8+ T cell function and promote exhaustion, enhance MDSC expansion and suppression, and drive macrophage polarization toward the tumor-promoting M2 phenotype (Mortezaee & Majidpoor, 2021; Xiao et al., 2025).

Acidic environment and lactate accumulation

Tumor cells produce lactate via aerobic glycolysis, leading to its accumulation and an acidic TME under hypoxia. Lactate is not just a waste product but also an immunoregulatory signal. The acidic condition suppresses T cell function and promotes MDSC and Treg expansion.

Nutrient deprivation

The high metabolic demands of tumor cells deplete key nutrients—such as glucose, amino acids, and lipids—in the TME, creating intense nutritional competition for infiltrating immune cells.

Glucose competition is the best-characterized form. Tumor cells consume large amounts of glucose via aerobic glycolysis, depriving T cells of this nutrient. Glucose shortage inhibits mTOR signaling and glycolysis in T cells, impairing their activation, effector function and promoting exhaustion (Lim et al., 2020).

Amino acid competition involves arginine, tryptophan, and glutamine. Tumor cells outcompete immune cells by upregulating amino acid transporters. Arginine depletion impairs T cell proliferation and reduces CD3ζ chain expression (Rodriguez et al., 2003). Tryptophan catabolism via IDO/TDO generates kynurenine, which activates the AHR, driving immunosuppression (Campesato et al., 2020). Glutamine deprivation limits T cell activation and differentiation (Klysz et al., 2015).

Lipid metabolism contributes as well. Lipids accumulated in the TME are taken up by TAMs and MDSCs, enhancing their immunosuppressive function (Liu et al., 2025a). Free fatty acids trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammasome activation in macrophages, promoting IL-1β secretion and shaping the inflammatory landscape (Kim et al., 2014).

Core mechanisms of tumor immune evasion

Recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells

The TME contains diverse immunosuppressive cells that help tumors evade immune responses (Fig. 2(a)). Key populations include:

Mechanisms of tumor immune evasion

(a) Recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells. M2-type TAMs secrete TGF-β and IL-10, promoting CD4+ T cell differentiation into Tregs. Tregs then release IL-35 to expand MDSCs. Together with M2 macrophages, these cells suppress CD8+ T cell function. Concurrently, M2 macrophages produce VEGF to drive tumor angiogenesis. This coordinated network amplifies local immunosuppression and physically excludes cytotoxic T cells from the tumor core.

(b) Regulation of immune checkpoint molecules. CTLA-4 outcompetes CD28 for CD80/CD86 binding, reducing T cell co-stimulation. Additional checkpoints—LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT—are upregulated on tumor-infiltrating T cells. LAG-3 inhibits activation via MHC-II binding; TIM-3 promotes exhaustion through galectin-9 engagement; and TIGIT suppresses proliferation via CD155 interaction. Together, these pathways form a multilayered barrier to antitumor immunity.

(c) Secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. Tumor cells, M2 TAMs, and Tregs secrete IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35, which inhibit MHC-II expression and CD8+ T cell function. Notably, TGF-β not only directly suppresses T cell cytotoxicity but also drives differentiation of Tregs and pro-tumorigenic CAFs, reshaping the tumor microenvironment.

Treg cells – CD4+ T cells marked by FOXP3 and CTLA-4. They are recruited via CCL22–CCR4 or induced locally by TGF-β and IL-10 (Gobert et al., 2009). Tregs suppress effector T cells by releasing inhibitory cytokines, consuming IL-2 via CD25, and blocking CD80/CD86 co-stimulation through CTLA-4.

TAMs–The most abundant immune cells in tumors, highly plastic, and skewed toward an M2-like phenotype. M2 TAMs inhibit T cells via IL-10, arginase 1, and TGF-β; promote angiogenesis and metastasis through VEGF and MMPs; and deplete arginine to impair T cell proliferation.

MDSCs–A heterogeneous immature myeloid population, divided into monocytic and granulocytic subsets. They suppress T cells by depleting amino acids, producing reactive species, secreting IL-10/TGF-β to boost Tregs, and expressing PD-L1.

These cells form a reinforcing network: Tregs promote MDSCs via IL-35; MDSCs drive M2 polarization and Treg induction; M2 TAMs recruit more Tregs via CCL22—amplifying immunosuppression.

Regulation of immune checkpoint molecule expression

Immune checkpoints regulate immune tolerance and prevent excessive responses. Tumors exploit these pathways to evade immunity by upregulating checkpoint ligands and inducing receptor expression on immune cells (Fig. 2(b)).

The PD-1/PD-L1 axis is the most studied. IFN-γ and other inflammatory signals in the TME drive PD-L1 expression on tumor and myeloid cells (Abiko et al., 2023). PD-L1 binding to PD-1 on T cells recruits SHP-1/2 phosphatases, which dephosphorylate TCR signaling molecules and suppress T cell activation, proliferation, and effector functions (Yokosuka et al., 2012).

The CTLA-4/CD80–CD86 pathway regulates early T cell activation. CTLA-4 outcompetes CD28 for CD80/CD86 binding due to higher affinity, reducing co-stimulation (Greene et al., 1996). Tregs, which constitutively express high CTLA-4, can capture and degrade CD80/CD86 from antigen-presenting cells via trans-endocytosis, indirectly dampening effector T cell responses (Qureshi et al., 2011).

Other checkpoints—LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT—are upregulated on tumor-infiltrating T cells. LAG-3 binds MHC-II to inhibit activation (Wakamatsu et al., 2024); TIM-3 engages galectin-9, promoting exhaustion (Hill et al., 2025); TIGIT binds CD155, suppressing proliferation and function (Yu et al., 2025a). These molecules cooperate with PD-1 to form a multilayered immunosuppressive network.

Secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines

The TME is enriched with various cytokines with immunosuppressive functions (Fig. 2(c)). The key factors and their roles are as follows.

IL-10 is a central immunosuppressive cytokine, mainly secreted by Treg cells, M2-type TAMs, and some tumor cells. It inhibits MHC II and co-stimulatory molecule expression in antigen-presenting cells via the STAT3 signaling pathway (Frei et al., 1994), impairing their ability to activate T cells, while directly suppressing CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity (Groux et al., 1998).

TGF-β has pleiotropic immunoregulatory functions. In the TME, it inhibits granzyme and perforin expression in CD8+ T cells (Thomas & Massagué, 2005), promotes Treg differentiation and maintenance (Sanjabi et al., 2017), induces macrophage polarization toward M2 (Jing et al., 2024), and drives the conversion of fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), remodeling the extracellular matrix (Hirota et al., 2025).

Impaired antigen presentation

Tumor cells evade T cell recognition through several mechanisms. The most common is downregulation of MHC class I expression via genetic or epigenetic alterations, which prevents CD8+ T cell recognition (Huang et al., 2026). Some tumors retain MHC I but show defective antigen processing, such as reduced TAP or proteasome subunit expression.

Antigen loss results from immunoediting: immunogenic tumor cells are eliminated, allowing low-immunogenicity variants to expand (Vesely & Schreiber, 2013). DC dysfunction also contributes—tumor-derived factors impair DC maturation and antigen presentation (Zhu et al., 2020).

Immunosuppression mediated by metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells supports their proliferation and promotes immune evasion by competing for nutrients and secreting metabolites. In glucose metabolism, aerobic glycolysis depletes glucose in the TME, limiting T cell mTOR signaling, glycolysis, and effector function (Chang et al., 2015). Lactate accumulation acidifies the microenvironment, suppressing T cells and expanding MDSCs and Tregs (Ji & Xia, 2025). In amino acid metabolism, tumor cells upregulate transporters to compete for arginine, tryptophan, and glutamine. Arginine loss reduces CD3ζ expression and T cell proliferation (Rodriguez et al., 2003); tryptophan-derived kynurenine activates the AHR, driving immunosuppression (Campesato et al., 2020); glutamine deprivation impairs T cell activation and Th1/Th17 differentiation (Johnson et al., 2018). In lipid metabolism, accumulated lipids are taken up by TAMs and MDSCs, enhancing their suppressive activity (Liu et al., 2025a). Free fatty acids trigger ER stress and inflammasome activation in macrophages, while altered cholesterol metabolism disrupts T cell receptor signaling and membrane fluidity (Anderson et al., 2012).

TME heterogeneity and immune evasion

The TME is highly heterogeneous in both space and time. Spatially, different tumor regions show distinct features: the central area is often hypoxic, acidic, and nutrient-poor, with high infiltration of immunosuppressive cells; the peritumoral region has better oxygenation and more effector T cells; the invasive front displays active tumor–stroma interactions and enhanced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasion signaling. Temporally, the TME evolves during progression—early stages retain partial immune surveillance, advanced stages shift toward immunosuppression, and metastatic tumors adapt to diverse organ environments with complex immune evasion mechanisms.

This heterogeneity has key clinical implications. High intratumoral heterogeneity is linked to lower response rates to ICB, as uneven immune pressure allows resistant tumor cells to survive and drive recurrence. Advances like spatial transcriptomics and single-cell sequencing are helping to dissect this complexity, offering insights for more precise immunotherapy approaches.

TME and immunotherapy resistance

The TME mediates immunotherapy resistance through three mechanisms. Primary resistance: no initial response; driven by immunosuppressive cells, low PD-L1/TMB, and defective antigen presentation. Adaptive resistance: immune evasion under pressure; involves PD-L1 upregulation, antigen loss, and alternative checkpoints (LAG-3, TIM-3). Acquired resistance: relapse after response, linked to JAK1/JAK2 mutations causing defective IFN-γ signaling, leading to loss of MHC I expression, and fixed T cell exhaustion.

Overcoming resistance requires combination strategies: anti-angiogenics to enhance T cell infiltration, metabolic modulators to reverse exhaustion, and Treg/MDSC-targeted agents to relieve immunosuppression.

Endogenous metabolites and tumor immune evasion

Endogenous metabolites act as signaling molecules, epigenetic modifiers, and modulators of immune activity, playing key roles in tumor immune evasion. Based on their chemical nature and functional impact in the TME, these metabolites can be grouped into immune-evasion promoters and suppressors.

Immune-evasion promoters

Metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells and stromal cells within the TME leads to abnormal accumulation or depletion of diverse metabolites. These metabolites act as critical signaling molecules that reshape the functional phenotypes of immune cells and construct a metabolic network favoring tumor immune evasion (Table 1). Organic acids

Organic acids are highly abundant in the TME and represent core components that establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Lactate

Beyond its role as an energy source for neighboring cells, lactate acts as a signaling molecule that reshapes immune cell function and promotes tumor immune evasion (Bell & Zou, 2024). Tumor-derived lactate disrupts metabolism in naïve T cells by reducing NAD+ levels and suppressing FAK family-interacting protein expression (Fong & Takeda, 2008). This leads to defective autophagy, excessive ROS production, and apoptosis, blocking the initiation of anti-tumor immunity (Guillaumond et al., 2013).

Lactate also enhances the immunosuppressive activity of Tregs, which take it up via MCT1 to sustain their suppressive function. It further promotes Treg stability by inducing deacetylase 1-mediated degradation of T-bet, increasing Treg frequency and tumor invasiveness (Kaymak et al., 2021).

In CD8+ T cells, lactate inhibits pyruvate carboxylase activity, impairs TCA cycle replenishment, and suppresses NFAT transcriptional activity—reducing IFN-γ production and weakening T and NK cell-mediated immune surveillance (Klimova & Chandel, 2008).

Lactate is traditionally viewed as an immunosuppressive metabolite in the TME, where it promotes immune evasion by acidifying the milieu, impairing CD8+ T cell function, and enhancing Treg activity. However, recent evidence reveals a concentration- and cell type-dependent role: high levels (>20 mM) suppress immunity, whereas physiological concentrations (2–10 mM) can enhance CD8+ T cell effector function and metabolic fitness. Additionally, as a substrate for lactylation, lactate drives M2-type macrophage polarization, though its effects on T cells remain debated. Thus, lactate is not simply a “waste product” of immunosuppression but a conditional immunomodulator whose function depends on concentration, cell type, and microenvironmental context (Wang et al., 2026).

Succinate

Succinate reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating immune cell differentiation and function (Baryla et al., 2022). In SDHB-deficient tumors, succinate accumulation promotes the survival of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and supports the generation of stem-like subsets, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy (Dalla Pozza et al., 2020). Succinate improves mitochondrial function in T cells by upregulating BNIP3-mediated mitophagy to eliminate damaged mitochondria, enhancing their survival under persistent antigen stimulation (Gudgeon et al., 2022). It also inhibits histone demethylase activity by lowering the α-KG/succinate ratio, regulating histone modifications of stemness-related genes, and driving CD8+ T cells toward a stem-like phenotype (Hirschey & Zhao, 2015).

Fumarate

Fumarate, a normal TCA cycle intermediate, accumulates as an oncometabolite due to loss-of-function mutations in fumarate hydratase. Structurally similar to α-KG, it competitively inhibits α-KG-dependent dioxygenases, causing global DNA and histone hypermethylation. These silence genes involved in tumor immunogenicity and immune function, reducing immunogenicity, promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and enabling immune evasion (Cheng et al., 2023; Ghoreschi et al., 2011). Fumarate also succinates protein cysteine residues, inhibiting Gasdermin D and blocking IL-1 family cytokine release, thereby dampening inflammation and anti-tumor immunity. Additionally, fumarate and its derivatives impair DC maturation and effector T-cell function (Humphries et al., 2020).

Itaconate

Itaconate, a metabolite generated from cis-aconitate by immune-responsive gene 1 (IRG1) in the TCA cycle, is produced by tumor-associated myeloid cells in the TME and promotes immune evasion through multiple mechanisms. It suppresses ZAP70 signaling and glycolysis in T cells, reducing interferon-gamma and granzyme B secretion and impairing CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. In macrophages, itaconate drives M2-like polarization via Nrf2 activation, increasing interleukin-10 (IL-10) and arginase-1 production (Nan et al., 2025). Additionally, it compromises DC antigen cross-presentation and downregulates MHC-I expression, limiting CD8+ T cell activation. Collectively, these effects facilitate tumor immune evasion and confer resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy (Chen & Li, 2025; Yang et al., 2025).

Amino acids and their derivatives

Amino acids sensed by tumors and immune cells modulate anti-tumor immunity. Dysregulated metabolism, especially tryptophan and glutamine catabolism, generates immunosuppressive metabolites and drives immune evasion.

Tryptophan and kynurenine

Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, is primarily metabolized via the kynurenine pathway and plays a key role in tumor immune regulation. In tumors, overexpression of IDO1 leads to tryptophan depletion and kynurenine accumulation (Qiu et al., 2023). Kynurenine activates the AHR in immune cells, promoting Treg expansion, M2 TAM polarization, and tolerogenic DCs (Wang & Lei, 2018).

IFN-γ from CD8+ T cells induces FLII, CBP, and STAT1 in tumor cells, upregulating IDO1 and forming a negative feedback loop. Kynurenine enters CD8+ T cells via transporters, activates AHR, increases PD-1 expression, and drives T cell exhaustion (Campesato et al., 2020). As an endogenous AHR ligand, kynurenine shifts CD4+ T cells from Th17 to Treg phenotype, suppressing anti-tumor immunity.

In DCs, kynurenine inhibits NF-κB through AHR, reducing antigen presentation (D'Amato et al., 2015). Tryptophan starvation activates GCN2, inducing T cell cycle arrest and TCR ζ-chain downregulation (Mezrich et al., 2010). The pathway also promotes M2-like macrophage polarization, and metabolite 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid further inhibits NF-κB in macrophages (Nishi et al., 2018). Together, these mechanisms link tumor metabolism to immune evasion.

Glutamine

Glutamine, the most abundant amino acid in the body, enters T cells via ASCT2 and promotes immune evasion (Gu et al., 2026). Tumor cells highly express glutamine transporters (e.g., ASCT2, SLC38A1, and SLC38A2), competing with T cells for glutamine and causing its deprivation. This inhibits mTORC1 signaling in T cells, reducing their activation and anti-tumor function (Nakaya et al., 2014). In TAMs, glutamine is metabolized into α-KG, which supports M2 polarization and reinforces immunosuppression (Recouvreux et al., 2024). Glutamine also fuels oxidative metabolism in MDSCs, sustaining their suppressive activity (Reinfeld et al., 2021).

Arginine

Arginine, a semi-essential amino acid derived from diet or citrulline, supports protein synthesis, the urea cycle, polyamine metabolism, and nitric oxide production. In the TME, it modulates immune and tumor cell functions, contributing to immune evasion.

In breast cancer, tumor cells overexpress ASS1, becoming a major arginine source. Arginine is taken up by TAMs and converted to spermine via polyamine synthesis. Spermine activates p53, upregulating TDG, which promotes DNA demethylation at the PPARG promoter and increases PPARG transcription (Zhu et al., 2025b). As a key regulator of M2 polarization, PPARG drives immunosuppressive factors, reinforcing a pro-tumor macrophage phenotype (Ino et al., 2013). These TAMs suppress CD8+ T cell activation—reducing effector markers and increasing exhaustion markers—thereby strengthening an immunosuppressive TME. Targeting ASS1, polyamine synthesis, or TDG may reverse this process and restore anti-tumor immunity (Carpentier et al., 2022).

Asparagine

Asparagine, a non-essential amino acid essential for nucleotide synthesis and protein translation, has dual effects on T cells: it promotes early activation but limits later effector functions. In the TME, asparagine predominantly supports immune evasion (Chang et al., 2025). It binds RIG-I, promoting CBL-mediated degradation of RIG-I and suppressing type I interferon signaling and anti-tumor immunity (Gnanaprakasam et al., 2023). Additionally, PTTG1 enhances DNA repair in tumor cells and impairs CD8+ T-cell function by reprogramming asparagine metabolism. Tumor cells also outcompete T cells for asparagine via high ASNS expression or enhanced uptake, contributing to T-cell dysfunction (Wei et al., 2025).

Taurine

Taurine, a sulfur-containing β-amino acid abundant in mammalian tissues, plays diverse biological roles, including osmoregulation, membrane stabilization, calcium homeostasis, and antioxidant activity. In tumor immune regulation, taurine modulates immune cell activity through several mechanisms. It enhances mitochondrial function, boosting energy metabolism and cell survival. In the nutrient-deprived TME, this may support immunosuppressive cells like Tregs or TAMs (Beyer et al., 2018). Taurine also increases intracellular serine, aiding glycosphingolipid synthesis and potentially promoting immunosuppressive phenotypes. Additionally, its regulation of calcium homeostasis may interfere with effector T cell receptor signaling, reducing antitumor activity. Thus, taurine contributes to tumor immune evasion by supporting immunosuppressive cell survival, remodeling immune metabolism, and disrupting effector T cell signaling (Cao et al., 2024).

Lipids and their derivatives

Lipid metabolites contribute to energy supply and membrane structure, and their derivatives serve as immunoregulatory signals critical for tumor immune escape.

Prostaglandin E2

Prostaglandin E2, a lipid mediator derived from arachidonic acid via COX enzymes, is elevated in many cancers and correlates with poor prognosis (Alcolea et al., 2012). Produced by both tumor cells and CAFs, it accumulates in the TME. PGE2 suppresses adaptive immunity by activating EP2/EP4 receptors on T cells, inhibiting TCR signaling, reducing IL-2 production, and dampening CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity (de Groot et al., 2007). It also impairs NK cell function via CAF-derived PGE2, weakening innate immune surveillance (Finetti et al., 2020). Additionally, PGE2 promotes pro-tumor macrophage recruitment and activation through EP4 and ERK1/2 signaling, and enhances neutrophil expression of TNFα, IL-6, CXCL1, and COX2 via EP2, fueling inflammation and tumor progression (Guo et al., 2008).

Palmitic acid

Palmitic acid, a 16-carbon saturated fatty acid abundant in human plasma, is released in the TME mainly from lipolysis of cancer-associated adipocytes. It serves both as a core membrane component and a signaling lipid that promotes tumor immune escape. Uptake via CD36 activates the Akt/mTOR pathway, impairing T cell function—reducing calcium flux, mitochondrial activity, and oxidative phosphorylation—while upregulating exhaustion markers and decreasing IFN-γ and granzyme B secretion (Zhang et al., 2024). It also expands Tregs in a CD36-dependent manner, reinforcing immunosuppression. These effects are counteracted by oleic acid, which competes for uptake, restores T cell metabolism and function, reduces Tregs, and enhances anti-tumor immunity (Sun et al., 2024).

Cholesterol and its derivatives

Cholesterol is a key structural component of membranes and lipid rafts, and together with its oxidized derivatives, acts as a signaling molecule and immunomodulator (Lagunas-Rangel, 2025). These molecules promote tumor progression by driving metabolic reprogramming and suppressing anti-tumor immunity, ultimately enabling immune evasion (Fig. 3) (Lin & Gustafsson, 2015).

Cholesterol and its metabolites as key regulators of tumor immune evasion

Cholesterol and its derivatives drive tumor immune evasion via multiple mechanisms. 7-DHC inhibits phospholipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in tumor cells, reducing damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) release and DC cross-presentation to limit CD8+ T cell immunity. CH25H-mediated hydroxylation generates 25-HC, which promotes M2 polarization of TAMs via mTORC1 inhibition and AMPK-STAT6 activation, while directly suppressing CD8+ T cells. CYP27A1-derived 27-HC recruits neutrophils through CXCR2, increases γδ T cells, and stimulates IL-17 secretion, collectively inhibiting CD8+ T cell function.

(1) 25-Hydroxycholesterol

25-hydroxycholesterol is an oxidized cholesterol derivative produced by cholesterol-25-hydroxylase. In the TME, it is abundantly secreted by TAMs and promotes immune escape. IL-4 and IL-13 upregulate macrophage CH25H expression via STAT6, increasing 25-hydroxycholesterol levels. Within lysosomes, it binds GPR155, suppressing mTORC1 and activating AMPKα. Activated AMPKα phosphorylates STAT6 at Ser564, enhancing ARG1 expression and driving immunosuppressive macrophage polarization. High CH25H expression in macrophages correlates with poor prognosis in multiple cancers. 25-hydroxycholesterol also activates liver X receptors (LXRs), upregulating fatty acid synthase and SREBPs to promote lipid synthesis and tumor growth (Cao et al., 2020). Concurrently, it suppresses CD8+ T cell function—reducing IFN-γ and granzyme B—while expanding Tregs, thereby reinforcing local immunosuppression (Xiao et al., 2024).

(2) 27-Hydroxycholesterol

27-hydroxycholesterol is a major oxysterol generated from cholesterol by mitochondrial CYP27A1 (Norlin et al., 2003). It has been shown to drive immune escape and metastasis in breast cancer. The metabolite acts primarily on myeloid cells, increasing polymorphonuclear neutrophils and γδ-T cells within metastases while reducing CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity. Mechanistically, 27-hydroxycholesterol exerts pro-metastatic effects via tumor-extrinsic pathways, relying on phagocytic myeloid cells and promoting neutrophil recruitment through CXCR2 signaling. It also enhances γδ-T cell and neutrophil colocalization; γδ-T cells secrete IL-17, driving neutrophil polarization and establishing an immunosuppressive niche that inhibits CD8+ T cell function. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of CYP27A1 reduces lung metastasis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target (Baek et al., 2017).

(3) 7-Dehydrocholesterol

7-dehydrocholesterol is an intermediate in cholesterol biosynthesis, produced by sterol C5-desaturase and converted to cholesterol by DHCR7. While it can suppress tumor growth by inhibiting the Akt/NF-κB pathway, its key role in immune evasion involves ferroptosis regulation. The 5,7-unsaturated double bonds in its B-ring inhibit phospholipid peroxidation, protecting tumor cells from ferroptosis. Ferroptotic tumor cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit DCs and enhance CD8+ T cell cross-priming. DHCR7 is pro-ferroptotic; its loss leads to 7-dehydrocholesterol accumulation, suppressing ferroptosis and weakening antitumor immunity, thereby promoting immune escape (Dong et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2024).

(4) Lanosterol

Lanosterol, a key intermediate in cholesterol synthesis produced by lanosterol synthase, normally supports lipid homeostasis. In cancers like colorectal cancer, however, its role shifts fundamentally: tumor cells upregulate cholesterol synthesis via the DUSP18-USF1-SREBF2 axis, causing lanosterol to accumulate and be released into the TME. Once taken up by CD8+ T cells, lanosterol suppresses immunity by promoting HMG-CoA reductase degradation, blocking the mevalonate pathway. This impairs KRAS prenylation and inactivates downstream ERK and AKT signaling, inhibiting T cell proliferation and activation, reducing effector molecule secretion, and upregulating immune checkpoints—driving T cell exhaustion and enabling immune evasion (Zhou et al., 2024b).

(5) Secondary bile acids (Deoxycholic acid)

Secondary bile acids are gut microbiota-derived metabolites formed from primary bile acids via dehydroxylation and deconjugation, primarily by bacteria like Clostridium. In tumor immunology, secondary bile acids—especially deoxycholic acid—mediate immune escape. Deoxycholic acid binds to plasma membrane calcium ATPase on CD8+ T cells, enhancing calcium extrusion and reducing intracellular calcium. This inhibits calcineurin activity and blocks NFAT2 nuclear translocation, impairing effector molecule expression and CD8+ T cell killing function (Ma et al., 2025a; Sinha et al., 2020). This establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment in colorectal cancer and accelerates tumor progression.

ATP and adenosine

ATP, normally intracellular, is released into the TME upon cell damage, acting as a danger signal. Its immunomodulatory effects are dual and context-dependent: it can promote inflammation or, via conversion to adenosine, drive immunosuppression (Kaplinsky et al., 2024).

Extracellular ATP directly activates purinergic receptors on immune cells, promoting the expansion of immunosuppressive populations like MDSCs and M2 macrophages. More critically, ATP is rapidly hydrolyzed by CD39 and CD73—enzymes highly expressed in the TME—into adenosine. This metabolic conversion is key to establishing an immunosuppressive barrier (de Andrade Mello et al., 2017).

Adenosine, through A2A/A2B receptors on T cells, potently suppresses their anti-tumor functions(Buisseret et al., 2018). It is produced by multiple cell types in the TME (e.g., Tregs, tumor cells), reinforces M2 macrophage polarization, and is further enhanced by hypoxia via HIF-1α (Hansen et al., 2022). Targeting this pathway—by antagonizing A2A receptors, combining with CAR-T therapy, or alleviating hypoxia—can reduce adenosine-mediated immunosuppression and restore T cell function (Henrich et al., 2016).

Gut microbiota metabolite- butyrate

Butyrate, a gut microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acid, fuels intestinal epithelial cells and supports barrier function and immune balance. It enters the TME via circulation and acts as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, modulating anti-tumor immunity (Son & Cho, 2023). In the TME, butyrate impairs DC maturation and antigen presentation, dampening T cell activation. It also promotes Treg expansion, reinforces immunosuppression, and disrupts effector T cell metabolism and function—reducing IFN-γ secretion and weakening tumor immune evasion (Han et al., 2025).

Polyamine metabolites

Polyamine metabolites—primarily putrescine, spermidine, and spermine—are small, positively charged alkylamines. Their synthesis begins with ornithine decarboxylase converting ornithine to putrescine, which is then converted to spermidine and spermine. These metabolites are highly elevated in tumors and contribute to immune evasion through multiple mechanisms (Fig. 4) (Bundgen et al., 2025).

Polyamines and their derivatives modulate tumor immune evasion

Polyamines promote tumor immune evasion via multiple mechanisms. In the TME, tumor cells, TAMs, and MDSCs overexpress polyamine synthesis enzymes to support immunosuppression. Polyamines taken up by CD8+ T cells inhibit their proliferation and effector function, reducing IFN-γ and granzyme secretion. Spermidine acts as a methyl donor precursor and drives eIF5A hypusination, sustaining the immunosuppressive phenotype of MDSCs and Tregs. Polyamines also upregulate PD-L1 on tumor cells or modulate PD-1 on T cells, reinforcing checkpoint-mediated suppression.

In the TME, polyamines are taken up by CD8+ T cells, suppressing their proliferation and function—marked by reduced IFN-γ and granzyme B production. They also promote the expansion of MDSCs and M2 polarization of TAMs, further dampening anti-tumor immunity. MDSCs highly express ODC and spermine synthase, which are critical for their suppressive activity (Lian et al., 2022).

Spermine can serve as a methyl donor precursor, influencing histone methylation. In TAMs, polyamine metabolism modulates M2 gene expression via H3K4me3 and H3K27me3. Additionally, polyamines upregulate PD-L1 on tumor cells, directly inhibiting T cells (Xu et al., 2024).

Spermidine is essential for hypusination of eIF5A, a modification that supports protein synthesis in MDSCs and Tregs, sustaining their immunosuppressive phenotype. Blocking polyamine synthesis or hypusination can reverse immune suppression and boost anti-tumor responses (Ma et al., 2020).

Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are small molecules that transmit signals in the nervous system. Recent studies show that both tumor and immune cells in the TME express neurotransmitter receptors, and some tumor cells can even produce neurotransmitters themselves. Acting as molecular bridges, neurotransmitters connect the nervous system and TME through neuro-immune-tumor interactions, playing a key role in regulating tumor immune escape (Zhang et al., 2025a).

GABA

GABA, a non-protein amino acid synthesized from glutamate by glutamate decarboxylase, is traditionally known as the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Emerging evidence, however, highlights its role in modulating anti-tumor immunity within the TME through distinct molecular mechanisms (Lloyd et al., 1978).

GABA is produced and released by activated B cells and plasma cells. In the TME, GABA binds to GABA-A receptors on monocytes and macrophages, driving their differentiation toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype and promoting IL-10 secretion, which suppresses CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses (Zhang et al., 2021).

Paracrine GABA signaling also dampens the effector functions of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, impairing T cell activation by modulating calcium flux and TCR signaling, which reduces CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity (Huang et al., 2022). Furthermore, GABA contributes to the polarization of TAMs toward an M2 immunosuppressive phenotype, reinforcing immune evasion (Dong et al., 2024a).

4-Ac-GABA

4-Ac-GABA, a GABA metabolite upregulated in cancers like HCC, is synthesized via a mechanism involving PMVK. PMVK stabilizes GAD1 to enhance glutamine-to-GABA conversion and promotes GABA acetylation to 4-Ac-GABA. Secreted 4-Ac-GABA binds the GABA-A receptor on CD8+ T cells, suppressing AKT1 signaling and reducing IFN-γ and granzyme B expression, thereby impairing anti-tumor immunity. Inhibiting PMVK or GABA-A receptor in HCC models restores CD8+ T cell function, suppresses tumor growth, and overcomes anti-PD-1 resistance (Zhou et al., 2024a).

Histamine

Histamine, derived from histidine via histidine decarboxylase, is mainly produced by mast cells, basophils, and some tumor cells in the TME. It binds to four G protein-coupled receptors, exerting dual effects on tumor immunity. In promoting immune escape, histamine enhances MDSC expansion and function via the H1 receptor, and suppresses T cell activity while supporting Treg differentiation through the H2 receptor (Martin et al., 2014). Elevated histamine in tumors like melanoma and breast cancer correlates with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and poorer prognosis (Li et al., 2022).

Immune-evasion suppressors

Certain endogenous metabolites can enhance anti-tumor immunity. They act by boosting immune cell function, limiting immunosuppressive cell differentiation, and improving metabolic conditions within the TME. Their key features and mechanisms are outlined in Table 2.

Organic acids

α-KG

α-KG is a key TCA cycle intermediate, derived from glutamate via deamination or transamination. It functions as an essential co-substrate for dioxygenases such as histone demethylases and TET DNA hydroxylases, linking cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation (Asadi Shahmirzadi et al., 2020). In tumor immunity, α-KG plays a central positive role by promoting T cell activation and proliferation (Matias et al., 2021). It fuels the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation to meet the energy demands of effector T cells, while sustaining dioxygenase activity to maintain a hypomethylated epigenetic state. This supports effector T cell gene expression and suppresses Tregs differentiation. In contrast, oncometabolites accumulated in the tumor microenvironment, including 2-hydroxyglutarate, succinate, and fumarate, competitively inhibit α-KG-dependent dioxygenases. This inhibition induces global hypermethylation, remodels the immune landscape, and facilitates immune escape—highlighting the immunoprotective function of α-KG (Sarkar et al., 2025).

Creatine

Creatine, a nitrogen-containing organic acid obtained from diet or endogenous synthesis, is transported into cells via SLC6A8. Once inside tumor cells, creatine binds to and inhibits ERK2 activation, blocking ERK2-mediated phosphorylation and stabilization of FSP1. This accelerates FSP1 degradation, relieving its suppression of ferroptosis and inducing immunogenic ferroptosis in tumor cells. Consequently, CD8+ T cell infiltration is enhanced, boosting the anti-tumor immune response (Zhou et al., 2025).

β-Hydroxybutyrate

β-Hydroxybutyrate, a ketone body produced by the liver during caloric restriction, serves both as an energy source and a signaling molecule that modulates immune cell function (Oswald et al., 2025). Elevated during caloric restriction, it is preferentially oxidized by T cells, boosting acetyl-CoA production, mitochondrial efficiency, and ATP synthesis (Pelletier et al., 2007). This metabolic shift promotes the differentiation of CD8+ T cells into effector-like progenitor exhausted cells, reducing the buildup of terminally exhausted T cells and preserving cytotoxic function (Rojas-Morales et al., 2016). Such metabolic remodeling enhances the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, and combining both approaches further suppresses tumor growth (Wang et al., 2017b).

Vitamin C

Vitamin C, an essential water-soluble nutrient that humans cannot synthesize, plays a key role in regulating tumor immunity. Proteomic studies have identified a novel PTM called lysine vitcylation, where vitamin C directly modifies lysine residues on proteins. In tumor cells, vitamin C specifically targets the K298 residue of STAT1, disrupting its interaction with the phosphatase TCPTP. This prevents dephosphorylation at Y701, thereby enhancing STAT1-mediated activation of the interferon signaling pathway (Kouakanou et al., 2021). Activated STAT1 upregulates MHC/HLA class I molecules on tumor cells, improving antigen processing and presentation, which in turn boosts CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses both in vitro and in vivo. The STAT1 K298R mutation abolishes the effects of vitamin C on STAT1 phosphorylation, MHC-I expression, and anti-tumor immunity, underscoring the specificity and functional importance of this modification (He et al., 2025).

Amino acids and their derivatives

Aspartate

Aspartate, a non-essential amino acid, supports anti-tumor immunity through multiple mechanisms: it provides precursors for nucleotide synthesis to fuel T and B cell proliferation; sustains glycolytic energy via the malate-aspartate shuttle to maintain NAD+/NADH balance; activates mTOR signaling to promote CD8+ T cell differentiation and effector functions such as interferon-gamma and granzyme B secretion; enhances DC antigen presentation; and helps immune cells outcompete tumor cells for nutrients in the TME (Ali & Ben-Sahra, 2023; Liu et al., 2025c).

Methionine

Methionine is an essential sulfur-containing amino acid and a precursor for S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), playing a key role in epigenetic regulation and anti-tumor immunity. In T cells, methionine uptake via SLC43A2 supports SAM production, enabling histone H3K79 dimethylation, which sustains STAT5 expression and IL-2 signaling to promote T cell survival and effector function (Zou & Green, 2023). Tumor cells can outcompete T cells for methionine by upregulating SLC43A2, leading to SAM depletion, loss of H3K79me2, and T cell dysfunction. Restoring methionine or SAM levels reverses these effects, enhancing T cell activity and improving ICB efficacy. Thus, methionine critically regulates anti-tumor immunity through epigenetic control of T cell function (Bian et al., 2020).

Lipids and their derivatives

Certain specific lipids can directly antagonize the immunosuppressive effects, thus exerting positive effects in anti-tumor immune regulation.

Oleic acid

Oleic acid, the most abundant free fatty acid in plasma, is derived mainly from adipocyte lipolysis and elevated in obesity and the TME. It exerts anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, supporting tumor immunity by antagonizing palmitic acid-induced T cell exhaustion and Treg expansion. In obese mouse tumor models, oleic acid reduces T cell exhaustion and Tregs, enhances cytokine secretion, and inhibits tumor growth. Mechanistically, it acts in a CD36-dependent manner to restore Akt/mTOR signaling and calcium flux, optimize mitochondrial function, and reverse metabolic reprogramming in T cells (Sun et al., 2024).

Nucleotide cofactor: tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)

BH4 is a key cofactor for nitric oxide synthase. It inhibits tumor immune escape through multiple mechanisms: Firstly, it maintains appropriate nitric oxide synthase activity, regulating NO generation. Appropriate concentrations of NO can potentiate anti-tumor immunity, whereas excessive NO mediates immunosuppression (Clark et al., 2023). Secondly, it is an essential cofactor for T cell activation, supporting T cell proliferation (Cronin et al., 2018). Thirdly, as a potent antioxidant, it protects immune cells from oxidative stress damage within the TME. Therefore, maintaining a high BH4/BH2 ratio is crucial for sustaining robust anti-tumor immunity (Liu et al., 2024a).

Gut microbiota metabolite: indole-3-lactic acid

Indole-3-lactic acid, a tryptophan metabolite produced by gut bacteria like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, exerts dual anti-tumor effects in colorectal cancer by remodeling the immune microenvironment. It boosts anti-tumor immunity by: promoting IL-12 secretion in DCs via enhanced H3K27ac at the IL12a enhancer to activate CD8+ T cells; improving CD8+ T cell function via transcriptional repression of Saa3 through chromatin remodeling; activating the AHR to modulate TAM immunosuppression; and inhibiting RORγt to reduce Th17 differentiation (Zhang et al., 2023a).

Endogenous metabolites and tumor ICB therapy

ICB therapy restores T cell anti-tumor function by blocking negative immune regulators like CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1. Since its first approval in 2011, it has shown significant clinical efficacy in various solid tumors—including melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer—providing long-term survival benefits for some patients. However, objective response rates remain modest, and both primary and acquired resistance are common, limiting broader clinical application (Korman et al., 2022).

Emerging evidence links tumor metabolic reprogramming to immune evasion. In the TME, cancer and stromal cells undergo metabolic changes that not only support tumor growth but also alter the metabolic landscape and generate bioactive metabolites. These metabolites influence immune cell function through effects on receptor signaling, epigenetic regulation, and metabolic adaptation (Zhang et al., 2025c).

The interplay between endogenous metabolites and ICB therapy has become a key focus in tumor immunology. Metabolites can enhance or suppress ICB responses. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms will help clarify immune resistance pathways, identify predictive biomarkers, and support the development of metabolism-targeted combination therapies (Zhu et al., 2025a).

Endogenous metabolites boost ICB response

A subset of endogenous metabolites can augment the therapeutic efficacy of ICB and surmount immune resistance via multifaceted mechanisms, including bolstering effector T cell function, reversing T cell exhaustion, optimizing the metabolic fitness of immune cells, improving antigen presentation efficiency, and inducing immunogenic cell death.

α-KG preserves T cell oxidative phosphorylation and effector activity while suppressing Treg differentiation, furnishing robust metabolic support for T cells activated by ICB (Matias et al., 2021). Beta-hydroxybutyrate optimizes mitochondrial function in T cells and enhances ATP generation, driving the differentiation of CD8+ T cells toward effector-like progenitor exhausted subsets, reducing the accumulation of terminally exhausted T cells, and thereby potentiating the anti-tumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy (Luda et al., 2023). Methionine, a critical precursor of S-adenosylmethionine, sustains H3K79me2 epigenetic modification, upregulates STAT5 expression, and strengthens IL-2 signaling cascades, ultimately restoring T cell responsiveness to ICB therapy (Bian et al., 2020). Oleic acid reverses palmitic acid-induced T cell exhaustion and Treg expansion by restoring Akt/mTOR signaling and mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby mitigating T cell dysfunction (Sun et al., 2024). Furthermore, aspartate supports de novo nucleotide synthesis and activates mTOR signaling, enhancing the effector functions of T cells. Linolenic acid suppresses prostaglandin E2 production and promotes the polarization of macrophages toward an anti-tumor M1 phenotype (Sharma et al., 2024). Creatine triggers immunogenic ferroptosis in tumor cells, facilitating increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the TME (Zhou et al., 2025). Together, these metabolites synergize with ICB therapy via distinct pathways to enhance systemic anti-tumor immunity (Table 3).

Endogenous metabolites antagonizing ICB therapeutic sensitivity

Certain endogenous metabolites undermine ICB efficacy by directly suppressing effector T cells, promoting immunosuppressive subsets, competing for metabolic resources, and remodeling epigenetics. Lactate induces naïve T cell apoptosis, impairs CD8+ T cell function, and enhances Treg activity (Leu et al., 2021). Kynurenine activates AHR, converting Th17 cells to Tregs and suppressing DC antigen presentation. PGE2 inhibits T cell proliferation via EP2/EP4 and promotes pro-tumor macrophage responses (Li et al., 2013). Adenosine from CD39/CD73 engages A2A/A2B receptors to suppress T and NK cell function (Limm et al., 2014). Succinate stabilizes HIF-1α, reshaping the TME (Inamdar et al., 2023). Palmitate causes T cell mitochondrial dysfunction and exhaustion. 25-hydroxycholesterol drives M2-like TAM polarization. Lanosterol blocks T cell activation and upregulates immune checkpoints. These metabolites form a network antagonizing ICB (Table 4). Future strategies—dietary modulation, metabolite supplementation, or enzyme targeting—could remodel the TME to support immunity and improve clinical outcomes.

Perspectives and unresolved issues

Beyond their canonical metabolic roles, endogenous metabolites actively regulate tumor immune evasion and modulate the efficacy of ICB via protein modification and direct target binding. Deciphering how metabolites are sensed, dynamically altered, and regulated within the TME can substantially advance fundamental and translational cancer immunology research. Technological breakthroughs in metabolite detection, spatial metabolomics, and single-cell profiling have now enabled high-resolution mapping of the TME metabolic landscape, greatly accelerating the identification of functionally critical metabolites and actionable therapeutic targets. Future translational strategies—including the targeting of metabolite-sensing pathways, the combination of metabolic modulation with immunotherapy, and the application of metabolites as predictive biomarkers—offer promising avenues for developing precise immuno-metabolic therapies and improving clinical outcomes of cancer patients.

Despite these encouraging advances, multiple core challenges and unresolved bottlenecks still hinder the progress of this field. First, the majority of the “dark metabolome” remains underexplored. Over 80% of metabolic features detected in untargeted metabolomics assays lack accurate functional and structural annotation. Although these uncharacterized metabolites are presumed to mediate key immunoregulatory events, their identification and functional validation are severely constrained by structural complexity, isomer interference, and the scarcity of reference standards and comprehensive metabolite databases (Jones, 2018). Second, the context-dependent and species-specific activities of metabolites remain poorly defined. Even a single metabolite (e.g., lactate) can exert consistent or opposing immunomodulatory effects across distinct cell types, tumor subtypes, and microenvironmental contexts, highlighting the urgent need for systematic, comparative functional studies (Wang et al., 2026). Third, establishing definitive causality remains a major methodological hurdle. It remains challenging to distinguish whether observed metabolic alterations drive tumor immune evasion or merely arise as secondary consequences, a problem that requires dynamic metabolic flux analysis to resolve. Fourth, technical limitations continue to impede the dissection of spatiotemporal dynamics and tissue heterogeneity of metabolic networks, restricting the discovery of precise and effective intervention targets (Sears et al., 2024). Fifth, robust systems-level integration of multi-omics datasets—such as the combination of spatial metabolomics and single-cell transcriptomics—for mechanistic modeling and rational therapeutic design has not yet been achieved. Finally, clinical translation faces substantial practical barriers, including inherent tumor heterogeneity, interindividual patient variability, the shortage of validated reliable biomarkers, and the potential toxicities of metabolic inhibitors and combination treatment regimens. Collectively, these unresolved issues require in-depth and systematic investigation in future studies.
