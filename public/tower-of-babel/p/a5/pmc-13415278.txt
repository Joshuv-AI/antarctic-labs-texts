Post Hoc Analysis of Recombinant C1 Inhibitor Clinical Data Using Contemporary Endpoints for Hereditary Angioedema
Authors: Marc A Riedl, Nihal Narsipur, Douglas Jones, Raffi Tachdjian, Anurag Relan, Hannah Kilvert, Emily Aiello, Ketsia Habimana, Neil Roskell, Adam Gough, Joseph R Harper, H Henry Li, Amanda Harrington

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContemporary trials of medications to treat hereditary angioedema (HAE) attacks define efficacy endpoints using Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI-S) and Patient Global Impression of Change (PGI-C) scales; these differ from the visual analog scale (VAS) and treatment effect questionnaire (TEQ) used in legacy recombinant human C1 esterase inhibitor (rhC1-INH) studies. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Contemporary trials of medications for treating acute hereditary angioedema (HAE) attacks use efficacy endpoints that differ from the efficacy endpoints used in legacy recombinant human C1 esterase inhibitor (rhC1-INH) studies.This study reported the results of a post hoc analysis that pooled and assessed outcomes from three clinical trials of rhC1-INH for the on-demand treatment of HAE attacks using endpoints that align with contemporary HAE clinical trials. What was learned from this study? Compared with placebo, rhC1-INH had significantly shorter median [95% confidence interval (CI)] time to onset of symptom relief [0.75 h, (95% CI, 0.55–1.50) vs. 8.00 h, (95% CI, 3.00–8.05); P < 0.001) and significantly shorter median time to complete attack resolution, a meaningful outcome for patients [4.50 h, (95% CI, 3.55–5.50) vs. 24.00 h, (95% CI, 16.00–>24.00); P < 0.001).In patients receiving rhC1-INH treatment, 75% of attacks were resolved within 6 h and 90% were resolved by 24 h.Building on the data from previous clinical trials, rhC1-INH demonstrates significantly faster onset of symptom relief and resolution of HAE attacks compared with placebo, regardless of the efficacy outcome definition used.

Introduction

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by recurrent swelling episodes—or attacks—that can affect the abdomen, extremities, pharynx, oropharynx, and any skin surface [1, 2]. These attacks are associated with substantial burden of illness and impairment of normal life for patients [1]. HAE attacks can negatively impact a patient’s ability to work, pursue an education, and participate in leisure activities, thereby decreasing quality of life [3]. HAE symptoms may also provoke anxiety and depression [4]. Notably, oropharyngeal–laryngeal swelling can be life-threatening due to the risk of asphyxiation [1].

Guidelines for the management of HAE recommend that patients have access to on-demand therapies for treating HAE attacks [1]. Several therapies have been approved for the treatment of acute HAE attacks, including ecallantide, icatibant, plasma-derived C1 esterase inhibitor (pdC1-INH), recombinant human C1-INH (rhC1-INH), and sebetralstat [5–9].

Rigorous, controlled phase 2/3 or phase 3 studies of on-demand treatments for HAE, conducted in the 2000s and 2010s, demonstrated the efficacy and safety of ecallantide, icatibant, pdC1-INH, and rhC1-INH [10–16]. Specific patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures accepted by regulatory bodies were used in these studies, including the visual analog scale (VAS; 0- to 100-mm scale), treatment effect questionnaire (TEQ; 7-point categorical scale), mean symptom complex severity (MSCS; 4-point scale), and treatment outcome score (TOS; – 100 to + 100 scale) [10, 11, 13–16]. In addition, HAE on-demand treatment study designs during this period required patients to reach prespecified symptom severity scores to be eligible for treatment, to ensure a treatment effect could be adequately measured [10, 12, 15, 16]. Previous studies of on-demand treatments for HAE also required patients to travel to a clinic and undergo evaluation before treatment administration [10–16]. Contemporary on-demand HAE treatment studies have utilized different PRO measures [e.g., the 5-point Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI-S) and the 7-point Patient Global Impression of Change (PGI-C) scales] and endpoint definitions based on the evolution of regulatory requirements [17, 18]. Study designs have also changed to emulate evidence-based HAE management guidelines, which now recommend early treatment of all HAE attacks [1]. Innovations, including the use of electronic clinical outcome assessments, especially in decentralized clinical trials, have also allowed for the collection of trial data outside of a clinic setting [19].

The efficacy and safety of rhC1-INH for treating acute HAE attacks have been demonstrated in phase 2/3 [C1 1205 (NCT00225147)] and phase 3 [C1 1310 (NCT01188564) and C1 1304 (NCT00262301)] randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blinded clinical trials [15, 16]. These legacy trials of rhC1-INH used VAS and TEQ PRO measures to assess time-to-event efficacy outcomes [15, 16]. Patients with HAE who were treated with rhC1-INH 50 U/kg had a significantly shorter median time to onset of symptom relief (TOSR) that was sustained without worsening [C1 1310 results: (TEQ: 90 vs. 152 min; P = 0.031; VAS ≥ 20 mm decrease: 75 vs. 303 min; P = 0.003); C1 1205 results: (VAS ≥ 20 mm decrease: 122 vs. 495 min; P = 0.013)] and median time to minimal symptoms [C1 1310 results: (VAS < 20 mm: 240 vs. 362 min; P = 0.005); C1 1205 results: (VAS < 20 mm: 247 vs. 1210 min; P = 0.001)] compared with those who received placebo [15, 16]. These trials only enrolled patients with an overall severity score of ≥ 50 mm on a VAS (0–100 mm) at the time of dosing, with no evidence of symptom regression. Furthermore, patients were required to travel to a study center for eligibility evaluation before receiving trial intervention.

Contemporary HAE clinical trials, including the KONFIDENT (sebetralstat) and RAPIDe-3 (deucrictibant) trials, initiated in 2022 and 2024, respectively, used the 5-point PGI-S and the 7-point PGI-C scales to assess efficacy outcomes [17, 18]. In these trials, TOSR was defined as achieving a PGI-C score of at least “a little better” at 2 or more consecutive time points within 12 h after initial administration of the intervention; time to complete resolution (TTCR) was defined as a PGI-S score of “none” within 24 h (KONFIDENT) or 48 h (RAPIDe-3). Furthermore, patients were enrolled regardless of baseline severity level, with no minimum severity threshold, and were able to initiate treatment at home with self-administration of medication early in the course of symptoms [17, 18].

The goal of this post hoc analysis was to pool and evaluate TOSR and TTCR from three rhC1-INH trials using endpoint definitions that align with contemporary HAE clinical trials.

Methods

Data Pooling

Data were pooled from the three randomized, placebo-controlled studies of rhC1-INH: C1 1205, C1 1310, C1 1304 (placebo arm only). These studies were similar in design and patient recruitment; full study details have been previously published [15, 16, 20, 21]. Patients included in this analysis received the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved dosage of rhC1-INH for an acute attack (i.e., 50 U/kg for patients weighing < 84 kg and 4200 U for patients weighing ≥ 84 kg) or placebo. Patients in the intervention arm of the C1 1304 trial received rhC1-INH 100 U/kg and were excluded from the active treatment arm of this analysis to maintain consistency with the dosing used in the other two trials.

Assessments

TOSR

To improve the interpretability of legacy HAE clinical trial data, a PRO bookmarking study was completed to establish location-based VAS thresholds for PGI-S and to map VAS scores to PGI-C categories [22]. Results from this PRO mapping study, also known as the “bookmarking” study, evaluated responses from patients with HAE and clinicians treating HAE to map between PROs of HAE trials [22]. These results were used to convert TEQ and VAS scores to PGI-C and PGI-S categories [22]. Mapping algorithms are provided in Table S1 and Table S2 in the Supplementary Material. The TEQ included three questions and was used in the C1 1310 rhC1-INH study to assess HAE symptoms at a relevant attack location over time [15]. TEQ question 1 was as follows: “To what extent has the overall severity of your HAE attack changed since you received the infusion?” Symptom severity was assessed using a 7-point categorical scale ranging from “much worse” to “much better.” TEQ questions 2 and 3, respectively, were as follows: “Overall, has the intensity of your HAE attack symptoms begun to decrease noticeably since you received the infusion, yes or no?” and “At this moment, are your HAE attack symptoms minimal (barely noticeable), yes or no?” PGI-C consisted of one question assessing a patient’s impression of change using a 7-point categorial scale ranging from “much worse” to “much better”; the question was identical to that of TEQ question 1 [15, 23]. Therefore, analyses of TOSR data in the C1 1310 rhC1-INH study isolated TEQ question 1 responses to more closely align with PGI-C.

VAS scores were used to assess onset of symptom relief in the C1 1310, C1 1205, and C1 1304 rhC1-INH trials [15, 16]. VAS scores were mapped to PGI-C categories based on the percentage change in the VAS scores. Using results from the PRO bookmarking study [22], a PGI-C response of “better” was defined as a ≥ 50% decrease from baseline in VAS (i.e., a ≥ 50% improvement in composite VAS), and “a little better” was defined as a ≥ 30% decrease in VAS (i.e., a ≥ 30% improvement in composite VAS). Analyses of TOSR data in the C1 1205 and C1 1304 (placebo only) rhC1-INH studies mapped VAS measurements to more closely align with PGI-C [16].

In the original rhC1-INH trials, TOSR endpoint definitions required sustained response without worsening [15, 16]. In this analysis, patient-level data were used to redefine TOSR outcomes across rhC1-INH trials with a new endpoint definition: the time to reach a PGI-C category of “a little better,” “better,” or “much better” for two consecutive time points within 12 h of administration of rhC1-INH.

TTCR

Results from the PRO bookmarking study were also used to convert VAS scores to PGI-S categories [22]. PGI-S consists of one question that assesses a patient’s impression of severity using a 5-point categorial scale ranging from “none” to “very severe” [23]. For each PGI-S category (i.e., none, mild, moderate, severe, and very severe), a corresponding lower and upper limit VAS score was mapped for abdominal, peripheral, and head and neck attack locations. A PGI-S category of “none” during the falling phase of an HAE attack was mapped to VAS scores ≤ 10 mm, ≤ 15 mm, and ≤ 10 mm for abdominal, peripheral, and head and neck locations, respectively (Table S3 in the Supplementary Material). VAS scores from the C1 1310, C1 1205, and C1 1304 (placebo only) rhC1-INH trials were mapped to a corresponding PGI-S category.

In this analysis, patient-level data were used to redefine TTCR outcomes with a new endpoint definition: the time to reach a PGI-S category of “none” within 24 h of rhC1-INH administration.

Statistical Analysis

To address differences in redosing and align censoring definitions between the rhC1-INH trials and contemporary trials [17, 18], data in the rhC1-INH studies were censored at time of redosing for patients who received an agent other than the originally assigned intervention. Data were right censored at 12 h for TOSR and 24 h for TTCR for patients who did not achieve the endpoint by the end of the analysis window or who lacked post-baseline assessments. This approach could underestimate the treatment benefit. Kaplan–Meier estimates were calculated for TTCR and TOSR; medians and associated interquartile ranges (IQRs) and confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from these estimates. Landmark estimates for the proportion of patients with complete resolution at 6-, 12-, and 24-h time points were also derived. Log-rank tests were used to determine differences between treatment groups.

Subgroup and Supportive Analyses

Analyses were conducted in both the overall and earlier treating populations. The earlier treating population was defined as patients who received treatment at or before the median time to treatment observed in the overall population.

A supportive analysis of TOSR based on mapping TEQ only to PGI-C was conducted using patient data from the C1 1310 study alone; the C1 1205 and C1 1304 studies did not include the TEQ. This supportive analysis explored the TOSR by eliminating uncertainty due to mapping a continuous scale (VAS) to a categorical scale (PGI-C).

An additional supportive analysis of TTCR explored the upper and lower ranges of VAS scores that could correspond to “none” on PGI-C based on patient- and clinician-recommended values. These were termed as ceiling and floor thresholds. Given that patients may have a baseline level of symptoms unrelated to an HAE attack [4], a supportive analysis was conducted to explore the ceiling thresholds of patient-determined complete resolution. The ceiling threshold was defined as 15 mm for abdominal attacks, 25 mm for peripheral attacks, and 25 mm for head and neck attacks [22]. TTCR was additionally analyzed using a floor threshold of 5 mm for all attack locations to account for potential residual pain after symptom resolution.

Ethical Approval

This study is a post hoc analysis of previously conducted studies that were performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments and does not contain any new studies with human participants performed by any of the authors, therefore ethics committee approval was not required.

Results

Overall Pooled Analyses

The overall population included 48 patients (C1 1310, 43 patients; C1 1205, 5 patients) in the pooled rhC1-INH arm and 60 patients (C1 1310, 31 patients; C1 1304, 16 patients; C1 1205, 13 patients) in the pooled placebo arm. Most patients were white (95.8% and 95.0%, respectively) and female (68.8% and 66.7%, respectively), with a median age of 39 years across rhC1-INH and placebo groups (Table 1). The most common attack locations were peripheral (47.9% and 41.7%, respectively) and abdominal (35.4% and 35.0%, respectively), followed by head and neck (16.7% and 23.3%, respectively); 100% of patients experienced a severe or very severe HAE attack at baseline. The median (IQR) time from attack onset to treatment was 4.0 h (3.1–4.8) in the rhC1-INH arm and 4.4 h (3.5–5.3) in the placebo arm.

Subgroup Analyses: Earlier Treating Population

The median time to treatment observed in the overall population was 4.2 h. A total of 54 patients (rhC1-INH, n = 22; placebo, n = 32) initiated treatment after the median time to treatment in the overall population. The remaining 54 patients (rhC1-INH, n = 26; placebo, n = 28) initiated treatment at or within the median time to treatment observed in the overall population, and were defined as the earlier treating population. In the earlier treating population, patients received their assigned treatment at a median (IQR) of 3.2 (2.8–3.7) h in the rhC1-INH group and 3.4 (2.9–3.8) h in the placebo group.

Baseline demographics and attack characteristics of the earlier treating population were generally similar to those in the overall population (Table S4 in the Supplementary Material). In the pooled rhC1-INH arm and the pooled placebo arm, all patients were white, and the majority were female (65.4% and 60.7%, respectively), with a median age of 39.0 and 36.5 years, respectively. The most common attack locations were peripheral (57.7% and 46.4%, respectively) and abdominal (34.6% and 28.6%, respectively), followed by head and neck (7.7% and 25.0%, respectively). Baseline attack severity was predominantly very severe (73.1% and 82.1%, respectively).

Based on mapping TEQ and VAS to PGI-C, rhC1-INH significantly reduced the TOSR compared with placebo in the earlier treating population (Fig. 4; Table 3). The median TOSR was 0.75 h (95% CI, 0.53–1.23) for rhC1-INH and 8.18 h (95% CI, 2.00–12.00) for placebo (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This post hoc analysis demonstrates that treatment with rhC1-INH provides rapid, significant onset of symptom relief and complete symptom resolution of HAE attacks relative to placebo when evaluated with contemporary time-to-event efficacy endpoints defined by PRO measures.

By applying mapping algorithms to convert legacy VAS and TEQ scores to PGI-S and PGI-C categories, we assessed rhC1-INH data with contemporary efficacy endpoints to enhance interpretability among the broader HAE clinical evidence base. In the pooled data from three clinical trials evaluating treatment with the FDA-approved dose of rhC1-INH (50 U/kg for patients < 84 kg or 4200 U for patients ≥ 84 kg) for acute HAE attacks, the median TOSR was 0.75 h (95% CI, 0.55–1.50), and the median TTCR was 4.50 h (95% CI, 3.55–5.50); 75% of attacks were resolved within 6 h, and 90% of attacks were resolved within 24 h. In the 2024 phase 3 KONFIDENT trial, patients receiving the FDA-approved 600-mg dose of sebetralstat had a median TOSR of 2.0 h (95% CI, 1.5–2.8). Considering right censoring of patients who (1) did not achieve TTCR, (2) received an alternative on-demand therapy, or (3) lacked post-baseline assessments, a median TTCR of > 24.00 h was observed; less than 50% of patients receiving sebetralstat 600 mg and placebo had attack resolution within 24 h [6, 18]. Data from the recent deucrictibant RAPIDe-3 trial demonstrated that deucrictibant 20 mg has a median TOSR of 1.28 h (95% CI, 1.05–1.52) and a median TTCR of 11.95 h (95% CI, 8.61–21.79) [24]. Future research can build on these results in the form of indirect treatment comparisons that adjust for differences in study populations or designs between studies [25].

The study design of the rhC1-INH trials only permitted enrollment of patients with VAS scores ≥ 50 mm. Baseline mean VAS scores in the rhC1-INH trials ranged from 73.5 mm to 82.5 mm [15, 16, 26]. VAS scores ≥ 50 mm are considered to be severe; in peripheral attacks, patients have reported an inability to write with an affected hand or walk on an affected foot, along with swelling and pain [27]. Notably, in the pooled rhC1-INH data, more than 75% of patients reported very severe attacks based on PGI-S. The pooled results reported here demonstrate that, even in patients with severe and very severe HAE attacks, rhC1-INH delivers both rapid onset of symptom relief and resolution of HAE attacks. Although it may be easier to detect changes in patients with severe attacks compared with those experiencing mild attacks, severe attacks require a greater reduction in severity to achieve resolution (PGI-S of “none”) than milder attacks. Future research is needed to validate the PRO mapping and evaluate the treatment effects of rhC1-INH in patients with less severe attacks (e.g., VAS scores < 50 mm).

In the pooled rhC1-INH trials, the median time to treatment was 4.0 h for rhC1-INH and 4.4 h for placebo, largely due to study design requirements involving in-clinic assessment and treatment, necessitating patient travel and evaluation before treatment administration [15, 16]. The results from the earlier treating population analysis reported here were consistent with the overall results (earlier treating vs. overall population: median TOSR, 0.75 vs. 0.75 h; median TTCR, 3.78 vs. 4.50 h).

Limitations of this study include the post hoc nature of the analysis; the lack of symptom-level granularity captured in the VAS, PGI-S, and PGI-C instruments; the pooling of data across three clinical trials; and the inclusion of only patients with severe and very severe attacks who traveled to a treatment center within 5 h of symptom onset as designated by the study designs [15, 16]. The three rhC1-INH trials included in this analysis were designed to address the unmet need for rescue therapies at a time when limited treatment options existed and predated the current guidelines that recommend early treatment of HAE attacks of all severities and locations [1]. Given the rarity of HAE, data from the three clinical trials were pooled to attain a meaningful sample size for this analysis. Although HAE affects both sexes, the predominance of female patients enrolled in the three rhC1-INH trials [15, 16] and subsequently included in this analysis may be clinically relevant. Given the role of hormonal factors in HAE [28] and the increased disease severity observed in women [29], it is possible that the requirements for enrollment of only severe or very severe attacks in these studies may have resulted in predominantly female study populations.

Because PRO assessments were collected at discrete, prespecified time points in the original rhC1-INH trials, time-to-event endpoints derived from mapped PGI-C and PGI-S categories are inherently subject to interval-censoring and assessment-schedule bias. No imputation was performed for missing PRO assessments: analyses were conducted using observed data only, with patients censored at their last evaluable assessment or at the predefined analysis window if the mapped endpoint was not met. Consequently, missing or intermittently unavailable assessments may have delayed attainment of mapped thresholds for symptom relief or resolution at scheduled assessments, such that the true onset of response could have occurred earlier than recorded. Accordingly, the time-to-event estimates of TOSR and TTCR should be interpreted as approximations rather than exact measures of symptom change. Importantly, PRO assessment-schedule and censoring rules were applied uniformly across treatment arms, and missing assessments were not systematically associated with treatment assignment. As a result, any bias introduced by assessment timing or missing data is expected to be nondifferential and likely conservative and does not materially alter the observed treatment effect of rhC1-INH compared with placebo.

A major component of the study is the reliance on mapped rather than directly measured PRO data. Although mapping is inherently subjective and may introduce some level of uncertainty, the algorithms used in this study were derived from patient and clinician bookmarking studies that validated the relationship between VAS and PGI-C and PGI-S categories [22, 30]. However, the convergence of findings across multiple analyses, including supportive and subgroup analyses, provides confirmation of the efficacy of rhC1-INH in reducing the time to onset of symptom relief and complete resolution of acute HAE attacks. First, the supportive analysis using TEQ-only mapping adds validity to the TOSR results, providing a median TOSR of 0.75 h (95% CI, 0.55–1.25) for rhC1-INH and confirming the findings based on TEQ and VAS mapping to PGI-C. In this scenario, we reduced uncertainty by removing the mapping between the instruments, which produced results similar to those of the analysis mapping VAS and TEQ to PGI-S. Second, the threshold analyses add validity to the TTCR results. Based on the range for “none” using PGI-S in the bookmarking analysis by Manning et al. [22], the median TTCR did not change considerably, ranging from 4.00 h (95% CI, 2.95–4.50) using the ceiling threshold to 6.00 h (95% CI, 4.55–16.25) using the floor threshold. The small deviation of 0.5–1.0 h from the overall pooled analysis TTCR hazard ratio of 4.50 h (95% CI, 3.55–5.50) supports the robustness of the mapping algorithm.

Conclusions

Despite restriction of baseline enrollment to patients with severe and very severe HAE attacks as well as longer time to treatment initiation (as a result of legacy requirements for in-clinic assessment and administration), patients treated with rhC1-INH reached onset of symptom relief within a median of 0.75 h. Median time to complete resolution was 4.5 h, a meaningful outcome for patients; 75% of attacks were resolved within 6 h, reaching 90% by 24 h following rhC1-INH treatment. Mapping to more recently developed HAE study outcome measures, this post hoc analysis of legacy phase 2/3 and phase 3 trials reinforces the efficacy of rhC1-INH, which delivered rapid and significantly faster onset of symptom relief and resolution of HAE attacks compared with placebo, regardless of efficacy outcome definition [15, 16].
