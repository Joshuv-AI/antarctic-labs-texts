Cardiovascular risk scores, non-invasive vascular measures and myositis damage index reveal increased cardiovascular risk in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies: a case-control study
Authors: Gabriela Paola Garcia-Ordoñez, Carlos Gerardo Ramos-Becerra, Fernanda-Isadora Corona-Meraz, Mario Salazar-Paramo, Felipe Perez-Vazquez, Edgar-Federico Quirarte-Tovar, Christian Juarez-Gomez, Andrea Aguilar-Vazquez, Monica Vazquez-Del Mercado

ABSTRACT

Background

Patients suffering from autoimmune diseases are particularly vulnerable to developing cardiovascular complications due to persistent systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and metabolic disturbances [1]. Among these conditions, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are characterized by chronic skeletal muscle inflammation and a wide spectrum of extra-muscular manifestations, including cardiovascular involvement [2].

IIM poses increased cardiovascular risk (CVR), similar to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [3]. Cardiovascular disease has been documented as a leading cause of mortality in IIM [3]. A population-based cohort study found that individuals with dermatomyositis (DM) or polymyositis (PM) had more than two-fold higher risk of cardiovascular events, and up to four times increased all-cause mortality compared to matched general population controls [4].

Synergistic effects of conventional CVR factors, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and long-term glucocorticoid (GCC) use, an interchange that accelerates the development of atherosclerosis. Moreover, evidence from imaging and biomarker studies has demonstrated that subclinical vascular damage is already present in many patients at diagnosis [5].

Despite this recognized CVR burden in IIM remains inadequately captured in routine care. Moreover, few studies have directly compared conventional risk calculators with functional vascular assessments such as pulse wave velocity (PWV) and flow mediated dilation (FMD) in this population [6–8] In practice, CVR is still commonly estimated using traditional lipid and blood pressure–centric tools, most notably the Framingham Risk Score (FRS), which may underestimate risk in systemic inflammatory diseases [6, 9]. By contrast, more contemporary approaches that incorporate inflammatory contexts or broaden predictor sets may capture otherwise missed risk. In SLE, for example, QRISK3 (each letter does not correspond to a specific word, but rather the name of an algorithm, created by the QResearch group) which explicitly includes inflammatory disease and chronic GCC use, consistently identifies a larger proportion of high-risk patients than Framingham or American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association, Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)] (ACC/AHA ASCVD) Eq. (9).

Given these limitations, the identification of accurate tools to assess CVR in IIM should be worldwide included in the approach of these patients, either as a clinical and research priority. A deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of cardiovascular involvement in IIM is essential not only for developing tailored risk stratification strategies but also for refining preventive interventions and treatment targets. Thus, this study aims to evaluate CVD risk in IIM patients using both traditional FRS with more contemporary [QRISK3; Globorisk; ACC/AHA ASCVD risk calculators and non-invasive vascular tools to test whether more contemporary scores disclose clinically relevant risk not recorded by traditional calculators.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a cross-sectional study in 24 patients with a definitive diagnosis of IIM who were enrolled at the Nuevo Hospital Civil “Dr. Juan I. Menchaca”, part of the Universidad de Guadalajara in Guadalajara, Jalisco, Mexico, and 38 community volunteer age and sex matched healthy controls (HC) from February 2023 until November 2024.

All subjects gave their written consent before enrolling in the study. This protocol received approved from the Institutional Ethics and Research committees of the Nuevo Hospital Civil “Dr. Juan I. Menchaca” (HCJIM/0637/2023). Research conducted in accordance with the Helsinki criteria, as updated in 2024, Helsinki, Finland.

Cases were classified according to Bohan and Peter [10, 11] the 2017 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology and the American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification criteria [12], and the 2011 European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) International Workshop [13], resulting in 13 DM (54.2%), 5 PM (20.8%), 4 overlap myositis (OM) (16.6%), 1 antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS) and 1 inclusion body myositis (IBM) patients (4.2% respectively). Eligible patients included male and female participants who were 18 years of age or older. Exclusion criteria were current pregnancy and history of major CVD (myocardial infarction, peripheral artery disease, cerebrovascular disease).

For HC, the inclusion criteria were males and females aged ≥ 18 years with no history of rheumatic, autoimmune, inflammatory, or infectious diseases; no history of cardiovascular or metabolic diseases; and no history of chronic pharmacological therapy. Controls serving as a low-risk reference group, were selected as apparently healthy individuals to minimize confounding by cardiometabolic comorbidities and chronic therapies.

From the clinical chart, we obtained clinical history of the patients included the presence of comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, congestive heart failure, arrhythmia, RA, osteoporosis, hypothyroidism, interstitial lung disease, gout, cataracts, cancer). In addition, IIM treatments were extracted.

Myositis-specific (MSA) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAA) were registered [14]. Disease activity was measured using the validated Myositis Disease Activity Assessment Tool (MDAAT) score [15], which is part of the Core Set Measures proposed by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) [15]. To quantify cumulative organ damage, we used the Myositis Damage Index (MDI) severity score from IMACS, a validated instrument composed by eleven clinical domains, captures established, persistent damage attributable to myositis and its complications across multiple organ systems (including cardiopulmonary involvement). Within the cardiovascular domain, the MDI includes items such as hypertension requiring treatment for > 6 months, ventricular dysfunction/cardiomyopathy, angina or coronary artery bypass, and myocardial infarction [16]. To evaluate muscle strength, we used the manual muscle test 8 (MMT8) score [17]. Disease duration was defined as the time from the first typical disease manifestation (e.g., proximal muscle weakness, characteristic skin involvement, arthritis, lung involvement) to the date of inclusion in this study.

Metabolic and inflammation biomarkers

For blood samples, individuals included in this study were fasted for 12 h prior to the collection. These samples were left to clot at room temperature before being centrifuged at 1500 RPM (Rotanta 460R; Andreas Hettich GmbH & Co. KG., Germany) for 10 min at 20 °C erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was determined according to Wintrobe’s technique [18]. The separated serum was preserved at − 20 °C until further biochemical analyses were performed. We quantified the serum lipid profile (mg/dL) including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), HDL-c, LDL-c and VLDL-c with the Friedewald et al. formula [19].

Conventional cardiovascular risk assessment

We assessed the risk scores: FRS [20], QRISK3 [21] and ACC/AHA ASCVD [22]. Also, Globorisk laboratory (Globorisk lab) risk calculator was included [23]. Relevant patient information, such as age, gender, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, smoking status, and other risk factors were recorded. We then use the appropriate online calculator for each score to estimate the individual’s 10-year CVR. To avoid extrapolation beyond validated ranges, the scores were applied according to their eligibility criteria. Participants outside of the age range were not assigned that score to both the IIM cases and HC. For categorical analyses, we applied the pre-established 10-year risk thresholds recommended/used for each tool in percentage: FRS low ≤ 10, intermediate 10–20, high ≥ 20; ACC/AHA ASCVD low < 5, borderline 5–7.4, intermediate 7.5–19.9, high ≥ 20; QRISK3 low < 10, high-risk threshold ≥ 10; and Globorisk lab low-to-moderate < 10, intermediate 10–<20, and high-to-very-high ≥ 20.

Non-invasive cardiovascular assessment

Arterial stiffness was determined by carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) using tonometry. This is the recommended method for assessing arterial stiffness [24] and was performed using the SphygmoCor® CP System (ArtCor, Sydney, Australia), which has been validated for measuring cfPWV [25]. We use ≥ 10 m/seconds as a cutoff point for CVD risk assessment, as it has been established and used by this research group [26].

For measuring flow-mediated dilation (FMD), a calibrated OMRON HEM 7320 digital electronic sphygmomanometer and a high-resolution UNEX-EF 38G ultrasound (UNEX Co. Ltd., Nagoya, Japan) with a 10 MHz linear transducer were used. We use < 7% as a cutoff point for CVD risk assessment, as previously established [27].

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS v23.0.0.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Measures of central tendency and variability were used to describe quantitative variables; qualitative variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups for non-parametric or categorical data were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as suitable. We computed Spearman correlations and fitted multiple linear regressions. Our modeling used a prespecified, clinically interpretable strategy to limit overfitting. Functional vascular biomarkers were modeled as primary outcomes (cfPWV; FMD). Age was forced into all models a priori, and each regression included only one additional clinically motivated covariate, resulting in parsimonious two-predictor models. Models were interpreted as exploratory/hypothesis-generating. A separate model relating the clinical accumulated damage in IIM or MDI, the non-invasive tool FMD and the ACC/AHA ASCVD risk estimate is provided in the Supplementary Material as an exploratory association. We verified regression assumptions and stability by inspecting linearity (partial regression plots; ZPRED–ZRESID), residual normality (histograms; normal P–P), homoscedasticity (ZPRED–ZRESID), independence (Durbin–Watson ≈ 2), multicollinearity (tolerance > 0.20, VIF < 5, condition index < 30), and influence (studentized deleted residuals |t|<3, leverage < 2[k + 1]/n, Cook’s D < 4/n), reporting for each model the F test (df, p), R²/adjusted R², and unstandardized coefficients (B) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value less than 0.05.

Exploratory associations between vascular measures and risk estimates in IIM

To explore potential associations between vascular function, CVR, and inflammation, Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted. A significant inverse correlation was found between ACC/AHA ASCVD risk scores and FMD percentage (ρ=–0.620, p = 0.042, n = 11), indicating that higher estimated CVR was associated with impaired endothelial function (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Furthermore, cfPWV demonstrated a significant positive correlation with both ESR (ρ = 0.564, p = 0.023, n = 16) (Supplementary Fig. 1b) and age (ρ = 0.554, p = 0.026, n = 16) (Supplementary Fig. 1c), indicating that cfPWV was associated with age and, in unadjusted analyses, with ESR.

Discussion

In this study, we applied four widely used risk scores (Framingham, Globorisk lab, ACC/AHA ASCVD, and QRISK3) in a Mexican population and examined their relationship with non-invasive CVR tools: cfPWV and FMD. Importantly, when risk was analyzed as continuous estimates, differences between groups were minimal (Table 2). By contrast, threshold-based classification revealed a higher proportion of IIM patients exceeding moderate/high-risk cutoffs using QRISK3, ACC/AHA ASCVD, and Globorisk lab, whereas FRS did not identify this subset (Fig. 1), supporting risk heterogeneity within IIM. This pattern suggests that reliance on FRS alone may fail to identify a higher-risk subset in IIM, consistent with prior observations in systemic autoimmune disease [9, 28, 29].

One potential explanation is that FRS overlooks disease-specific drivers, such as chronic systemic inflammation and long-term GCC exposure, that accelerate atherosclerosis in IIM through endothelial dysfunction, lipid oxidation, and plaque instability [4, 30]; similar blind spots have been reported for FRS and the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE) in SLE and systemic sclerosis when detecting subclinical atherosclerosis [6].

In contrast, QRISK3, which was modestly higher in our cases, incorporates connective tissue diseases (RA, SLE) and GCC use, given us a more inflammation-aware assessment that is conceptually aligned with IIM pathobiology, even if not yet formally validated in IIM [18]. This is consistent with broader features commonly present in IIM, including elevated inflammatory markers [e.g., C reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6)], metabolic alterations, reduced physical activity due to muscle weakness, and variable myocardial involvement on advanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [31–37]. Together, these systemic and myocardial manifestations underscore the importance of cardiovascular surveillance, in concordance with recent recommendations by EULAR and the British Society for Rheumatology, advocating regular CVR assessment in patients with IIM [38, 39].

Physiological measures provided complementary information alongside risk estimates. In exploratory analyses, FMD showed an inverse association with the ACC/AHA ASCVD estimated risk, and this relationship persisted after accounting for MDI (Supplementary Table 1). Given our small sample size, and the limitations of risk equations in IIM, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Nonetheless, they support further prospective study of endothelial dysfunction as a potential early vascular feature in IIM [40, 41]. cfPWV showed the expected association with age-related vascular remodeling, reflecting reduced arterial compliance due to elastin fragmentation, collagen accumulation, medial calcification, and endothelial dysfunction, patterns well described in the general populations [42, 43].

cfPWV and FMD did not differ significantly between IIM patients and healthy controls in our cohort (Table 2), consistent with prior reports in IIM and related inflammatory populations [44]. This negative finding should be interpreted in the context of limited sample size and the marked heterogeneity of risk within IIM, where only a subset of patients exceeded high-risk thresholds using non-Framingham equations (Fig. 1). In addition, the vascular aging process is itself a clinical factor that increase the cfPWV. In this context, we cannot underestimate this biological process, in such way that in our IIM cohort diluted differences between-groups [43]. FMD also has substantial physiological and technical variability, and cross-sectional measurements may be insufficient to detect subtle or early endothelial changes [27].

We also observed an exploratory association between anti-SAE1 positivity with lower cfPWV; while a small sample size, this could reflect distinct inflammatory ways (e.g., prominent cutaneous disease with milder myonecrosis) compared with anti-SRP positive, which are linked to complement-mediated vasculopathy [45–47].

Because the MDI measures only established, cumulative damage may be less sensitive to earlier subclinical changes, underscoring the possibility that risk-related vascular changes may evolve before injury is reflected in damage indices. Additionally, both GCC exposure and prolonged disease duration, key contributors to damage increase, are also well-recognized drivers of accelerated atherosclerosis and CVD events across immune-mediated diseases [4, 48].

Consistent with this concept, in our exploratory analysis restricted to IIM patients we observed that those classified as high CVR exhibited a higher damage burden as assessed by the MDI severity score (Table 3). Our findings suggest that indices reflecting cumulative disease burden may provide clinically relevant context when interpreting risk estimates in this population. Importantly, our data are descriptive, does not evaluate cardiovascular outcomes; therefore, we cannot determine the prognostic accuracy of any risk equation in IIM.

Given these limitations, we propose conceptual framework for CVR characterization in IIM in which contemporary risk equations (e.g., QRISK3) are interpreted alongside functional non-invasive tools (FMD, cfPWV) and clinical disease burden (MDI). This approach may provide complementary information for identifying higher risk subsets but requires prospective outcome-based validation.

While cfPWV and FMD are well recognized non-invasive tools for CVR, complementary local and regional parameters such as carotid intima–media thickness, arterial distension/distensibility, and myocardial perfusion–demand balance metrics (e.g., subendocardial viability ratio) may add prognostic information and help refine risk characterization in future studies [49–51].

The main limitation of this study is its descriptive nature, which prevents causal inference or temporal sequencing among vascular measures, disease burden, and risk estimates. The small sample size (n = 24), typical of rare diseases, limits power and raises the risk of false negatives. Findings such as the anti-SAE1–cfPWV association should be viewed as exploratory. In addition, we did not include a disease-control group (e.g., SLE or RA). Thus, we cannot determine whether the observed risk-score discordance and vascular patterns are specific to IIM or reflect features shared across systemic inflammatory diseases; future studies incorporating inflammatory disease comparator cohorts are warranted. The control group was defined as HC (excluding history of cardiovascular and metabolic disease, and chronic pharmacological therapy); consequently, baseline CVR in controls is expected to be low, which may limit generalizability to population-based control samples. Finally, clinical heterogeneity across IIM subtypes, such as DM, PM, IBM may attenuate subtype clinical differences in CVR.

Conclusions

Our results show that QRISK3, ACC/AHA ASCVD, and Globorisk lab (but not Framingham) identified a higher-risk subset of IIM patients, which also exhibited higher cumulative damage (MDI severity). However, there are no overall differences in FMD or cfPWV between IIM patients and HC. A multimodal approach integrating risk scores, vascular assessment, autoantibodies, and organ damage may better contextualize cardiovascular risk in IIM, but longitudinal validation is needed.
