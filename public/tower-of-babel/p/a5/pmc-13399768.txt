Double‐Row Rotator Cuff Repair With Biological Augmentation Using Fibrin Clot‐Loaded Tendon Remnants
Authors: Chenyang Meng, Xinghao Deng, Changxu Han, Xinhao Li, Rui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRotator cuff tears are a common cause of shoulder dysfunction, and the management of remnant tendon during repair remains a clinical challenge. This article describes a technique that preserves tendon remnants by wrapping them in a fibrin clot, thereby enhancing the biological environment at the tendon‐bone interface. The fibrin clot, prepared from the patient's venous blood using a simple and cost‐effective method, provides a mechanical scaffold and promotes healing by delivering growth factors to the repair site. By preserving remnant tendon function and promoting biological healing, this fibrin clot‐loaded technique offers a promising, practical solution for rotator cuff repair, which improves outcomes in both standard and challenging cases.

Rotator cuff tears are a prevalent cause of shoulder joint dysfunction, with a particularly high incidence in the middle‐aged and elderly populations. 1 Surgical intervention, particularly arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, has been well‐documented for its ability to significantly improve shoulder function, alleviate pain, and restore normal activities of daily living. 2 , 3 However, despite the established benefits of surgical treatment, rotator cuff repair continues to face several challenges, including tendon healing, retear of the tendon, and postoperative pain. 4 , 5 Emerging evidence suggests that preserving the remnant tendon may promote healing after repair and enhance functional outcomes. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Fibrin clots, as natural biomaterials, have been shown to play a crucial role in facilitating wound healing. 10 When fibrin clots are used to load the remnant tendon, they not only provide a mechanical scaffold that stabilizes the repair site and improves structural integrity but also create a healing environment conducive to tendon regeneration through their inherent growth factor and cellular properties. Consequently, the incorporation of fibrin clot‐loaded remnant tendon technique represents a promising approach for improving the outcomes of rotator cuff repair.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning, Surgical Portal, and Arthroscopic Examination

The patient is positioned in the 50° lateral decubitus position with the shoulder joint in 60° abduction. Arthroscopy surgery is performed using a 30° scope (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN). The standard posterior portal is initially established as the viewing portal, followed by the creation of the standard anterior portal as the working portal. The glenohumeral joint structures are then examined, including the subscapularis tendon, humeral head and glenoid cartilage, biceps tendon, labrum, and the inferior surface of the rotator cuff. Next, through the posterior portal, the subacromial space is accessed. Under the guidance of an epidural needle (Qionghua, Jiangsu, China), the anterolateral portal is established as the working portal. The synovium is debrided by a synovium shaver (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN), and the acromion is exposed, followed by a cutting bolt procedure. Then, under the guidance of the epidural needle, a lateral portal is established as the viewing portal, with the anterolateral portal maintained as the working portal. The subacromial synovium is further cleared, and the torn rotator cuff tissue is exposed (Video 1).

Anchor Placement

Anchors are placed at the anterior and posterior edges of the torn rotator cuff, closely adjacent to the cartilage margin. The position of the medial‐row anchors is determined based on the anterior and posterior edges of the tear (Figure 1). The location for the lateral‐row anchor is marked with radiofrequency (Johnson & Johnson, NJ) 1.5 cm below the footprint (Figure 2).

Fibrin Clot‐Loaded Tendon Remnant Complex Preparation

Twenty milliliters of venous blood is drawn and placed into a metal graduated cup. The blood is then subjected to clockwise grinding for approximately 8 minutes using a 20 mL syringe (Qionghua, Jiangsu, China) with a frosted exterior (Figure 3, Table 1). After grinding, the fibrin clot adheres to the syringe plunger, and the clot is carefully removed for later use. The tendon remnant tissue from the rotator cuff and the footprint area is collected (Figure 4A) using a nucleus pulposus forceps (Wuyang, Guangdong, China) and synovial shaver (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN), wrapped in the fibrin clot, and then circumferentially sutured with 4‐0 absorbable sutures (Assut Medical Sarl, Vaud, Switzerland) to secure the clot around the tendon remnant (Figure 4B, Table 1).

Anchor Insertion, Suturing, and Complex Implantation

Two medial‐row anchors (double) are inserted at the radiofrequency‐marked points. The torn rotator cuff is divided into 4 equal segments to suture. A suture lasso (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is used to carry public distribution system (PDS) sutures loop (Ethicon; Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) through the tendon, starting from the posterior to the anterior, following the 4 equal division points (Figure 5). The sutures are then pulled through the tendon from the anchor with different colored suture tails. In the anterior‐most segment, an additional PDS suture is passed. A cannula (8.5 mm × 72 mm, Double, Fujian, China) is inserted via the anterolateral portal, and the PDS suture is pulled through the cannula. The tail end of the fibrin clot complex is tied to this suture. The fibrin clot‐loaded tendon remnant complex is then drawn under the rotator cuff with the PDS suture (Figure 6). Using the posterior portal, a suture lasso is employed to carry the PDS suture through the tendon, with the second suture of the complex being pulled to the upper surface of the tendon (Figure 7). Finally, the 2 sutures of the complex are tied together onto the rotator cuff to secure the complex to the articular surface of the tendon (Figure 8, Table 1).

Suture Bridge Fixation

The anchor suture tails are pulled from the anterior to the posterior through the cannula, with one of the adjacent suture tails being grasped. This suture is fixed anteriorly with the lateral‐row anchor (Double, Fujian, China). The remaining suture tails are pulled, and with the assistance of a probe, the suture is fixed posteriorly through the lateral‐row anchor (Figure 9).

The patient is positioned in the 50° lateral decubitus position with the right shoulder joint in 60° abduction. The lateral portal serves as the observation route, while the anterior‐lateral portal is the operational route. The torn rotator cuff is fixed using the knotless suture bridge method.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The affected arm is immobilized in a brace at 45° abduction and neutral external rotation for 4 weeks. Symptomatic supportive treatment includes Nonsteroidal Anti‐Inflammatory Drugs and ice therapy for significant pain relief in the early postoperative phase. Early passive range of motion exercises are initiated within 4 weeks, including finger flexion, scapular mobilization, and elbow flexion‐extension exercises. After 4 weeks, active assisted range of motion exercises are introduced within a safe range, along with training for activities of daily living.

DISCUSSION

The management of tendon remnants in rotator cuff repair has long been a topic of debate. Some scholars believe that degenerative tendon remnants may hinder healing. 11 However, recent studies have confirmed the efficacy of remnant‐preserving repair, showing that it can improve early postoperative outcomes and result in better tendon signal integrity after repair. 7 , 8 Animal studies have also shown that preserving the tendon remnant can achieve a superior tendon‐bone healing interface and enhanced mechanical strength, thus supporting the necessity of remnant‐preserving repair. 12 , 13 Furthermore, regarding the significance of degenerative rotator cuff tear remnants, research has found that even in degenerative tendon tissue, viable tendon stem cells remain active, potentially contributing to enhanced healing following repair. 14 In the technique of preserving the remnant tendon, a reported method is to preserve the lateral remnant tendon and integrate it into the “pants‐over‐vest” structure, which is then fixed to the natural insertion point of the rotator cuff by lateral suture bridge compression. This strategy of preserving the remaining tendon can not only maintain local blood supply but also deliver progenitor cells to the healing area, thereby optimizing the biological environment for tendon‐bone interface repair. 6 However, in clinical practice, tendon remnants often complicate the procedure, particularly during the insertion of the anchor. On the other hand, some surgeons attempt to freshen the footprint area to promote healing, but the presence of the remnant can interfere with this step. Therefore, a technique that preserves the remnant while not impeding other procedures is necessary. We have employed the technique of wrapping the remnant tendon with a fibrin clot, which preserves the function of the remnant tendon while also facilitating the surgical procedure. In this technique, the footprint area and the tendon attachment to the greater tuberosity are grasped using a bone curette, and any remnants that cannot be grasped are collected with a shaver to maximize the collection of tendon remnant tissue.

It has been reported that arthroscopic rotator cuff repair combined with the application of platelet‐rich plasma yields optimal clinical outcomes. 15 , 16 Fibrin clots, similar to platelet‐rich plasma, contain active components that promote tissue healing 17 (Table 2). Traditional fibrin clot preparation requires specialized equipment, but we have developed a simple method for clot preparation. 18 The fibrin clot produced is sufficiently strong for suturing and tensioning, and it is necessary to rotate the clot for at least 8 minutes, after which it can be collected from the syringe plunger (Table 1). The design of the tendon remnant complex involves wrapping the remnant in fibrin clot, which allows the remnant to attach to the tendon‐bone interface during the clot's absorption. Additionally, the complex is sutured in a circular fashion, leaving 2 suture tails, and we recommend using absorbable sutures to minimize suture interference. For fixation of the complex, it can be secured with a knot above the tendon or through an external fixation bridge. Knotting above the tendon is simpler, so this technique was used in our study (Table 1).

In our approach, the medial‐row anchor suture tails were not tied, as knotting may reduce tension and prevent cutting, whereas a knotless technique reduces tendon compression but may slightly decrease mechanical stability, particularly in terms of initial rigidity and compressive forces 19 , 20 (Table 1). Knotting in the medial row could potentially restrict tendon blood supply due to suture compression, thus increasing the risk of rupture in the medial tendon. 21 Clinical studies have found minimal differences in clinical outcomes between knot and knotless techniques, 22 with the knotless technique being faster and less risk of retear. 23 Therefore, we utilized a knotless suture bridge technique for fixation. However, in patients with osteoporosis, external fixation anchors may fail, and medial‐row knotting is necessary. During surgery, it is essential to assess the patient's bone quality. If the patient has osteoporosis, knotting of the same‐colored suture tails from the anchor should be performed first, followed by using lateral‐row anchor fixation. Thus, for rotator cuff tears with good tendon quality and an ideal tendon‐bone interface, the knotless medial‐row suture bridge is a reasonable and safe option. This technique offers advantages such as reduced tendon compression, potentially better blood supply restoration, easier manipulation, and shorter operative time. For high‐risk patients with poor tendon quality, osteoporosis, suboptimal footprint bone bed, the need for high initial fixation rigidity, larger tears, or high activity demands, the knotted medial‐row technique may still be valuable due to its superior mechanical stability, footprint compression, initial fixation strength, and reduced displacement (Table 1).

The limitations of this study include the need for further clinical evaluation, particularly regarding whether degenerative tendon remnants are suitable for fibrin clot wrapping. This approach requires additional clinical validation to confirm its efficacy and safety in such cases.
