Waste Valorization of Passion Fruit Peel Hydrolysate for Bacterial Cellulose Production: Influence of Nitrogen Source on Yield and Functional Properties for Food Packaging
Authors: Aida Aguilera Infante-Neta, Alan Portal D’Almeida, Raissa Saraiva Lima, Juan Antonio Cecília, Ivanildo José da Silva Junior, Luciana Barros Gonçalves, Tiago Lima de Albuquerque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The valorization of agro-industrial residues represents a strategic approach to advancing sustainability and circular bioeconomy principles in the food sector. Although bacterial cellulose (BC) production from waste substrates has been widely explored, limited attention has been given to the role of nitrogen source modulation in complex fermentation systems. This study evaluated passion fruit peel hydrolysate (PFPH), a cellulose- and hemicellulose-rich by-product, as an alternative carbon source for BC production using a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY) under static conditions. Acid hydrolysis and detoxification were performed to obtain fermentable sugars while minimizing inhibitory compounds. Different nitrogen sources and purification strategies were comparatively assessed. The highest purified BC yield (81 g L−1 of culture medium) was obtained using ammonium sulfate, whereas sodium nitrate promoted greater impurity removal (77.51% mass reduction). Structural and chemical analyses (FTIR, XPS, and XRD) confirmed effective delignification, enhanced surface purity, and increased crystallinity. SEM revealed a homogeneous nanofibrillar network, and thermogravimetric analysis indicated thermal stability up to approximately 300 °C. Soil burial assays showed 26% mass loss after 42 days, demonstrating controlled biodegradation consistent with food packaging requirements. Overall, PFPH proved to be an efficient and sustainable substrate for BC biosynthesis. The modulation of nitrogen source significantly influenced both production yield and structural properties, highlighting the potential of this system for developing environmentally responsible biopolymer materials for food packaging applications.

1. Introduction

The agro-industrial sector is widely recognized for its crucial role in the global economy, representing a significant share of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of many nations. Global agricultural production is estimated to reach a total annual value of USD 4.82 trillion in 2025, with a projected growth rate of 3.44% between 2025 and 2029 [1], reinforcing its economic importance. Recently, global agricultural exports have exceeded USD 1.5 trillion, underscoring the financial and commercial relevance of this sector. However, the expansion of the agro-industry faces significant challenges, particularly regarding the sustainable management of the diverse residues it generates [2,3].

Countries such as China, India, and Brazil play key roles in the production and export of various agricultural commodities, including corn, soybeans, and wheat [4]. Among the globally relevant tropical fruit crops, passion fruit (Passiflora edulis) stands out, with Brazil being the world’s largest producer, accounting for approximately 90% of total production [5]. The processing of this fruit for pulp and juice production generates a considerable amount of waste, primarily composed of peel, which can account for 50 to 60% of the total fruit weight. This residue is rich in pectin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, making it a promising raw material for various industrial applications [6,7,8].

Physical, chemical, and enzymatic pretreatments of the cellulose and hemicellulose fractions of the passion fruit peel release significant amounts of fermentable sugars, such as glucose and xylose [9,10]. Pretreatment with dilute acids, for instance, reduces cellulose crystallinity and hemicellulose recalcitrance, thereby releasing these sugars, which can serve as a carbon source for the production of bioproducts through fermentative processes [6,7,8]. There are reports of lignocellulosic hydrolysates being used in the production of renewable fuels [11], pharmaceuticals [12], biomaterials [2] and fine chemicals [13,14]. In this context, implementing an agricultural waste biorefinery can optimize the utilization of these resources, leading to a sustainable and integrated approach to fully valorize the by-products of passion fruit and other agricultural crops, thereby contributing to a more circular economy.

Bacterial cellulose (BC) can serve as an alternative to plant-derived cellulose. Establishing this process may reduce production costs while providing an environmentally friendly destination for agricultural residues by converting them into a new raw material. Compared to plant cellulose, BC exhibits significant advantages, including high purity, a highly crystalline structure, excellent mechanical strength, high porosity, and biocompatibility [15,16,17], making it suitable for both sensitive and demanding applications [18,19]. Despite its superior mechanical and functional properties compared to plant cellulose, large-scale production of BC still faces challenges, particularly due to the high cost of traditional substrates used as fermentable carbon sources, such as glucose and sucrose [6].

Traditionally, bacterial cellulose (BC) is produced by microorganisms such as Komagataeibacter, which synthesize cellulose from various carbon sources, including glucose, sucrose, and fructose [5]. Passion fruit peel, rich in cellulose and hemicellulose, can be hydrolyzed to release fermentable sugars suitable for BC-producing microorganisms. Microorganisms present in the SCOBY (symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast), used in kombucha production, are capable of metabolizing these sugars to synthesize BC [6,20]. Therefore, passion fruit peel hydrolysate can serve as an abundant substrate for these microorganisms, promoting sustainable practices and contributing to the circular economy. The use of SCOBY was selected due to its metabolic versatility and resilience in complex lignocellulosic substrates. Passion fruit peel hydrolysate may contain residual inhibitory compounds and mixed sugar profiles, conditions under which microbial consortia can exhibit enhanced tolerance and complementary metabolic activity compared to defined strains [21,22]. Such interspecies interactions may influence substrate utilization efficiency and cellulose biosynthesis dynamics. In addition, nitrogen availability is a key regulator of microbial growth and polymer production. In hydrolysate-based systems, where carbon composition and residual compounds vary, nutrient balance may significantly affect BC productivity and structural organization [23]. Therefore, comparing different nitrogen sources allows us to assess how metabolic regulation impacts both yield and material characteristics.

Beyond the substitution of conventional carbon sources with agro-industrial residues, advancing BC production requires an understanding of how nutrient composition influences microbial metabolism and cellulose assembly. Although the feasibility of producing BC from lignocellulosic substrates has been widely demonstrated, most studies primarily focus on carbon source replacement, paying limited attention to nitrogen source modulation as a regulatory factor in cellulose biosynthesis [24,25]. This gap is particularly relevant in lignocellulosic hydrolysate systems, where residual compounds and nutrient balance may significantly influence microbial metabolism and polymer formation. Furthermore, investigations commonly employ defined bacterial strains under controlled conditions, whereas the behavior of complex microbial consortia such as SCOBY in these substrates remains insufficiently characterized. Despite the recognized potential of passion fruit peel as a lignocellulosic resource, its application in SCOBY-mediated BC production under different nitrogen regimes has not been systematically evaluated.

In this context, it is hypothesized that nitrogen source selection influences both BC productivity and the structural and physicochemical characteristics of the material obtained from passion fruit peel hydrolysate. Therefore, the present study investigates BC production using this agro-industrial by-product as a carbon source in static SCOBY fermentation, emphasizing the comparative effects of distinct nitrogen sources and purification strategies. The resulting cellulose was characterized in terms of structural organization, thermal stability, and biodegradation behavior to elucidate the relationship between nutrient modulation and material performance, contributing to waste valorization and sustainable biomaterial development. Future studies may explore the incorporation of natural antimicrobial agents into BC-PFPH matrices to enhance active food packaging functionality.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Preparation and Characterization of the Passion Fruit Peel

Passion fruit peel (Passiflora edulis) (PFP) was obtained from a local juice production market in Fortaleza, Brazil. The peels were washed three times with tap water, cut into slices no larger than 3 cm, dried at 60 °C for 24 h, ground, and sieved through 20–80 mesh (0.841–0.177 mm) screens to ensure uniformity. The resulting flour was then stored at 25 °C for subsequent experiments. PFP was characterized for its cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and ash content following validated methodologies [6,26] and according to the NREL protocols NREL/TP-510-42618 [27], NREL/TP-510-42619 [28], and NREL/TP-510-42621 [29].

The ash content was determined by heating the PFP at 800 °C for 2 h in a muffle furnace. The extractive content of the dried PFP was analyzed using a Soxhlet extractor, in which 3 g of sample was subjected to extraction with 190 mL of ethanol (95%) for 8 h. After the reflux process, the material was dried at 40 °C for 48 h, and the extractive content was calculated gravimetrically. For the determination of cellulose and hemicellulose contents, 2 g of lipid-free PFP was placed in 100 mL beakers and treated with 10 mL of H2SO4 (72% v/v) at 45 °C for 7 min. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 275 mL of deionized water and subjected to thermal treatment in an autoclave at 121 °C for 30 min. After this process, the solid and liquid fractions were separated.

The solid fraction was dried at 100 °C until a constant weight was reached, and the insoluble lignin content was determined as the difference between the solid fraction mass and the previously calculated ash content [6]. The liquid fraction was analyzed for the quantification of sugars, organic acids, furfural, and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), as described by Albuquerque et al. [10]. Finally, the cellulose, hemicellulose, and soluble lignin contents (measured at 280 nm) were determined using the equations described by Infante-Neta et al. [6].

2.2. Preparation of the Inoculum

The SCOBY used in this study belonged to the Research and Development Group of Biotechnological Processes (GPBio) and was maintained under controlled laboratory conditions prior to use. The culture was preserved as an active kombucha consortium and periodically propagated in tea infusion to ensure metabolic stability and viability.

The selection of SCOBY as inoculum was based on its well-documented microbial composition and functional performance in cellulose-producing fermentations. Kombucha consortia typically comprise acetic acid bacteria (notably Komagataeibacter spp.) embedded within a cellulose matrix, along with yeasts that contribute to sugar metabolism and metabolite production essential for bacterial growth [30]. The use of this intact symbiotic consortium preserves native interspecies interactions that may enhance substrate utilization efficiency in lignocellulosic hydrolysate-based systems.

The culture was incubated in tea infusion at 25–28 °C for 3–5 days, and the fermented broth was used as a pre-culture for BC production in PFPH medium. This pre-culture was inoculated into tubes containing 9 mL of HS medium [31] (20 g/L glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L peptone, 2.7 g/L disodium phosphate, and 1.15 g/L citric acid), supplemented with 1 mL of the initial culture. The tubes were incubated at 30 °C for 3–5 days, and the resulting culture was used as the inoculum for the experiments in PFPH medium. The present study focused on evaluating fermentation performance and material properties; therefore, detailed microbial community profiling of the SCOBY was not included within the experimental scope.

2.3. Hydrolysis and Detoxification of PFP

Based on previous optimization studies [6], the hydrolysis of passion fruit peel was carried out using H2SO4 at a concentration of 0.71 M at 121 °C for 22 min, with a ratio of 10% (w/v) PFP:acid. The hydrolysate obtained under these conditions was then subjected to a detoxification treatment. Initially, the pH of the PFP hydrolysate was adjusted to 10 by adding Ca(OH)2, followed by the addition of H2SO4 until a pH of 5.0 ± 0.2 was reached. After this step, the resulting precipitate was separated by filtration, and the liquid phase was treated with activated charcoal. A proportion of 1% (w/v) activated charcoal was added to the hydrolysate, and the mixture was maintained under agitation at 200 rpm and 35 °C for 1 h. Finally, the PFP hydrolysate was sterilized at 110 °C for 10 min in an autoclave and used as the fermentation medium.

2.4. Bacterial Cellulose Production in PFPH

Fermentation conditions were defined according to previously reported protocols for bacterial cellulose production using kombucha-derived strains under static cultivation systems [32]. The incubation temperature (28–30 °C) and inoculum proportion (10% v/v) were selected within the optimal range described for acetic acid bacteria to ensure adequate metabolic activity and sufficient oxygen availability at the air–liquid interface, which is essential for pellicle formation in static systems. For BC production, 100 mL of PFPH was transferred into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and inoculated with 10 mL of a pre-culture grown in HS medium. Cultivation was carried out under static conditions at 28 °C for 14 days, a duration consistent with previously reported cultivation periods required for stable BC membrane development and structural consolidation under static fermentation. Sugars, pH, and product formation were monitored throughout the fermentation process. At the end of cultivation, the cellulose pellicle was carefully separated from the liquid medium, washed with distilled water to remove soluble residues, and dried at 60 °C until constant weight. The dry mass was recorded and used to calculate BC yield, expressed as grams of dry cellulose per liter of initial culture medium (g/L) [33]. Equation (1) was used to quantify the yield:

(1)Yield gL=Dry mass of BC (g)Initial volume of culture medium (L) where:

Dry mass of BC (g): refers to the dry weight of the bacterial cellulose after oven-drying to constant weight.

Initial volume of the culture medium (L): corresponds to the initial volume of the cultivation medium used in the fermentation process.

Additionally, the thickness of the bacterial cellulose samples was measured using a caliper. Measurements were taken at five different points on each sample (center and edges) to ensure uniformity of thickness. The average of these measurements was used for subsequent analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean values.

2.5. Influence of Nitrogen Source Addition on BC Production from PFPH

Once the PFPH-based medium was established, different nitrogen sources (peptone, yeast extract, sodium nitrate—NaNO3, and ammonium sulfate—(NH4)2SO4) were evaluated for their influence on BC production. Each compound was added to the PFPH medium at a concentration of 0.5% (w/v) prior to sterilization. Fermentation was carried out as described in Section 2.4. At the end of the fermentation period, the produced BC was separated, washed with distilled water to remove residual impurities, and dried in an oven at 60 °C until it reached a constant weight. The dry weight was then used to calculate the BC yield for each experimental condition.

2.6. Purification of BC Produced from PFPH

BC was separated from the culture medium by filtration and washed with distilled water. It was then subjected to a purification process using a 0.5 M NaOH solution at 90 °C for 1 h, following a methodology adapted from Liu et al. [34]. In addition to the conventional alkaline treatment, a second purification procedure was evaluated, combining NaOH treatment with a subsequent application of hydrogen peroxide (3% v/v), aiming to increase the purity of the bacterial cellulose by more efficiently removing residual impurities such as lignin, proteins, and pigments. After purification, the BC was dried at 60 °C until it was constantly weighted. For clarity, the term initial BC yield refers to the dry mass obtained immediately after fermentation and washing with distilled water, prior to chemical purification. The term final BC mass corresponds to the dry mass measured after alkaline and/or oxidative purification steps.

2.7. Analytical Methods and Characterization of the Obtained Cellulose

2.7.1. Analysis of Substrate Consumption and Product Formation

The concentrations of sugars, organic acids, and ethanol in the PFPH during fermentation were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). For this analysis, a Thermo HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Supelco 610-H column 30 cm × 7.8 mm (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (v/v) H3PO4, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and an operating temperature of 30 °C.

2.7.2. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The FTIR spectra of the cellulose samples were obtained using a Cary 600 Series spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. Measurements were performed on a Cary 630 spectrometer (Agilent Technologies) over the spectral range of 650 to 4000 cm−1, with a resolution of 1 cm−1 and a total of 32 scans.

2.7.3. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The morphology and structural conformations of the BC, as well as the evaluation of its porosity profile, were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy using a FEG Quanta 450 microscope (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at 10,000× and 50,000× magnification. The samples were lyophilized, mounted on carbon tape, and subsequently coated with silver using a Quorum QT150ES sputter coater (Quorum Technologies Ltd., Lewes, UK). The SEM chamber was maintained at a pressure of 10 Pa, with an incident electron beam of 20 kV.

2.7.4. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

The crystallinity of the samples was analyzed using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical B.V., Almelo, The Netherlands) equipped with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54059 Å), operating over a scanning range of 5° to 50° (2θ) with a step size of 0.05°.

2.7.5. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis was obtained in a thermal analyzer SKIMMER (NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Selb, Germany) using a 20 mL·min−1 flow rate of nitrogen. The analysis was conducted at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 600 °C following a heating ramp of 20 °C min−1.

2.7.6. X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were recorded using a Physical Electronics PHI 5700 spectrometer (Physical Electronics, Chanhassen, MN, USA) equipped with a Mg Kα X-ray excitation source (15 kV, 300 W, hν = 1253.6 eV) and a multichannel hemispherical analyzer (Physical Electronics, Chanhassen, MN, USA). All analyses were performed using a concentric hemispherical analyzer in constant-energy mode at 29.35 eV, with an analysis area of 720 μm in diameter. The pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained below 5 × 10−6 Pa. Binding energy values were determined with a precision of ±0.1 eV, using the adventitious carbon C 1s peak at 284.8 eV as a reference to correct for charging effects. For spectral analysis, a Shirley-type background was subtracted, and the peaks were fitted using Gauss–Lorentz functions to ensure accurate determination of the binding energies corresponding to the atomic levels of the different elements.

2.7.9. Biodegradability of BC Produced Using PFPH

The biodegradability of BC was evaluated through a soil burial degradation test under humid conditions. The purified and dried BC samples, with uniform diameters of approximately 3 cm, were weighed, and their initial dry weight (Wᵢ) was recorded. 2.7.10. Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software (Version 7.0, StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Composition of Passion Fruit Peel and Its Potential for the Production of Fermentable Sugars

The compositional analysis of passion fruit peel biomass revealed a high cellulose content (above 48%) and hemicellulose content (approximately 21%), along with significant amounts of lignin (12.3%), extractives (17.5%), and ash (1.8%). The high combined content of cellulose and hemicellulose indicates that this biomass has strong potential for biotechnological conversion processes, particularly for the production of fermentable sugars (Figure 1).

Cellulose and hemicellulose can undergo acid hydrolysis using diluted acids to break down the polysaccharides into simple sugars such as glucose and xylose, which can then be assimilated by microorganisms to produce bioproducts, including biofuels and biomaterials. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficiency of this approach in converting residual biomass into high-value-added products [6].

A promising application for these sugars is the microbial synthesis of bacterial cellulose, which has numerous industrial applications, particularly in the development of sustainable biomaterials, which have been previously reported in the literature [20,36,37]. Studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using agro-industrial residues as carbon sources for bacterial cellulose production. For example, Yang et al. [38] developed a functional film for sustainable food packaging using agricultural by-products through a zero-waste refinery approach. In that study, BC was fermented from the hydrolyzed supernatant of distilled grains and subsequently modified with chitosan to enhance its mechanical and barrier properties, which have demonstrated considerable mechanical strength and antioxidant capacity, making the material a viable alternative for biodegradable packaging applications.

Wastes have also been used for BC production. Sabatini et al. [39] evaluated the use of agro-food residues, such as citrus fruit waste and coffee grounds, for the production of kombucha and bacterial cellulose. Over 21 days of fermentation, efficient bacterial cellulose production was observed, demonstrating the potential of these alternative substrates for biopolymer synthesis. Furthermore, Deng et al. [40] investigated the incorporation of bacterial cellulose into biodegradable films for food packaging using a by-product derived from purple passion fruit peel treated by hydrothermal carbonization. The resulting films exhibited excellent antibacterial and antioxidant properties, significantly extending the shelf life of the packaged foods.

Previous studies that employed PFPH for xylitol production [6] demonstrated the importance of detoxifying the hydrolysate obtained from the acid hydrolysis of the lignocellulosic structure. This process is essential for obtaining fermentable sugars—particularly glucose—in a purer form. During the acid hydrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass, structural polysaccharides, such as cellulose and hemicellulose, are degraded, releasing monosaccharides such as glucose and xylose. However, this treatment can also lead to the formation of inhibitory by-products, including acetic and formic acids, as well as furfural and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF). Acetic acid originates from the degradation of acetyl groups that are present in hemicellulose, while formic acid results from the decomposition of furfural. Furfural and HMF compounds are formed by the thermal degradation of xylose and glucose, respectively [41].

To enable bacterial cellulose production from PFPH, detoxification of the medium was also performed in a previous study to remove inhibitory compounds [6]. The applied treatment resulted in the complete elimination of acetic acid and reduced formic acid levels to below 0.7 g/L. Furthermore, furfural and HMF compounds were not detected after detoxification, and the concentration of phenolic compounds was reduced to less than 0.5 g/L. Thus, use of activated charcoal has contributed to this process by removing inhibitors compounds; thus, it may have contributed to bacterial cellulose production by the microorganisms present in the SCOBY by reducing the presence of the same inhibitors.

The suitability of PFPH as a substrate for BC production is closely associated with its carbohydrate composition and fermentable sugar profile. Acid hydrolysis of passion fruit peel releases significant amounts of glucose and other soluble sugars, which serve as direct precursors for the formation of UDP-glucose—the immediate substrate for cellulose synthase during BC biosynthesis. In addition, residual micronutrients and organic compounds present in the hydrolysate may support metabolic activity and cellular growth. The detoxification step further improves substrate performance by reducing inhibitory compounds such as phenolics and furfural derivatives. Together, these characteristics create a favorable biochemical environment for efficient cellulose polymerization and matrix development under static cultivation conditions.

3.2. Production of Bacterial Cellulose from PFPH

Table 1 shows that the PFPH medium supplemented with sodium nitrate (NaNO3), an inorganic nitrogen source, and subjected to NaOH treatment, exhibited the highest initial BC yield (59.8%), surpassing the other nitrogen sources evaluated. This behavior partially contrasts with some reports in the literature, which indicate that complex organic nitrogen sources such as yeast extract may enhance BC production under defined culture conditions. For instance, in a study using Rhizobium sp., yeast extract resulted in BC production of up to 2.2 g/L, whereas inorganic salts such as ammonium sulfate showed lower performance, with values ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 g/L [42]. Similar results were also reported for Acetobacter xylinum, where organic nitrogen sources proved to be the most effective, outperforming inorganic ones [43]. It is important to distinguish that the initial BC yield reflects the amount of cellulose synthesized during fermentation, whereas the final BC mass after purification represents the usable material that remains after the removal of residual impurities.

The usage of different nitrogen sources influenced the total BC production in comparison to that of the HS medium (without nitrogen supplementation), as all media (except for sodium nitrate) showed higher relative BC production in comparison to the HS medium, probably due to the higher bioavailability of nitrogenated compounds, which may be important as a cofactor for BC production, as the presence of some nitrogen sources may increase the BC production [44]. However, the combined NaOH and peroxide treatment resulted in mass loss due to the removal of residual contaminants. Sodium nitrate (77.51%) and HS medium (75.18%) exhibited the highest total mass reduction after purification. Thus, after purification, ammonium sulfate (0.081 g/mL of medium) and yeast extract (0.073 g/mL of medium) presented the highest final BC mass, respectively. This behavior suggests that, under certain cultivation conditions, inorganic nitrogen sources can demonstrate competitive performance (e.g., when background media supply growth factors from agro-residues) [45,46]. Previous studies have shown that inorganic salts such as ammonium sulfate generally result in low BC yields—or even no production—compared to organic nitrogen sources, due to the lack of growth factors and to potential ionic-strength toxicity at higher concentrations [43,47,48]

The superior efficiency observed for the organic nitrogen sources can be attributed to their composition, which provides amino acids, vitamins, and cofactors essential for bacterial metabolism. These compounds act directly on the activation of biosynthetic pathways responsible for the formation of UDP-glucose, the main precursor of bacterial cellulose. This sugar nucleotide is converted into β-(1→4) cellulose microfibrils by the enzyme cellulose synthase (EC 2.4.1.12), whose activity depends directly on the proper availability of these nutrients. Consequently, organic nitrogen sources enhance enzymatic efficiency and promote greater deposition of the fibrillar matrix, resulting in higher BC yields [49,50].

The Hestrin–Schramm (HS) medium was adopted as a reference, as it represents the most widely established experimental standard for bacterial cellulose production, allowing direct comparison with the literature and serving as a positive control in formulation and supplementation assays. Recent reviews and applied studies have consistently employed HS as the baseline medium for evaluating alternative conditions and agro-industrial residues [49,51].

Using HS as the reference (0.102 g·g−1; 100%), the passion fruit peel hydrolysate (PFPH) without supplementation yielded 0.115 g·g−1 (112.8% vs. HS), indicating that the residue provides sufficient carbon and micronutrients to sustain bacterial cellulose synthesis, thereby supporting its use as a renewable matrix in this process.

Nitrogen form modulation was significant. Supplementation with (NH4)2SO4 increased the yield to 0.223 g·g−1 (218.6% vs. HS), surpassing yeast extract (0.196 g·g−1; 192.2%) and peptone (0.109 g·g−1; 106.9%), while NaNO3 resulted in the lowest yield (0.061 g·g−1; 59.8%). Mechanistically, ammonium assimilation via the GS–GOGAT pathway may involve a lower reductive burden under certain conditions, potentially favoring anabolic processes related to cellulose biosynthesis. In contrast, nitrate assimilation requires sequential reductive steps (NO3− → NO2− → NH4+), involving additional consumption of reducing power, which may increase cellular reducing power demand and could be associated with the lower performance observed with NaNO3 [52,53].

Moreover, complex nitrogen sources such as yeast extract can enhance productivity by supplying organic nitrogen, vitamins, and cofactors—an effect often associated with higher BC yields across different strains and substrates. These observations align with literature reports on nitrogen assimilation in prokaryotes, the physiology of the nitrate/nitrite pathway, and bioprocess studies that document productivity gains with complex nitrogen sources. Given the superior performance observed, (NH4)2SO4 was selected for subsequent stages of this study as the nitrogen supplementation condition of choice. This decision is consistent with the established use of HS as a benchmark and with evidence that reduced nitrogen sources—and, to a lesser extent, complex organic ones—enhance bacterial cellulose productivity under the evaluated conditions.

To further contextualize the performance of passion fruit peel hydrolysate (PFPH) as a fermentation substrate, the maximum BC yield obtained in this study was compared with values reported for other fruit-derived residues. Andritsou et al. [54] reported BC concentrations of 6.7 g/L (grapefruit juice) and 6.1 g/L (orange juice), while citrus peel aqueous extracts yielded 5.2 g/L (lemon peel extract), 5.0 g/L (grapefruit peel extract), and 2.9 g/L (orange peel extract), depending on the substrate and cultivation time (up to 13 days). Similarly, for pineapple-derived substrates, Mardawati et al. [55] reported BC yields of up to 2.52 g/L after 15 days of static cultivation using pineapple core media. Overall, these findings indicate that fruit by-products such as citrus peels/juices and pineapple residues can support BC production within the ~2.5–6.7 g/L range. The yields obtained using PFPH in the present study fall within this reported range, reinforcing its potential as a competitive lignocellulosic substrate for bacterial cellulose biosynthesis.

The decrease in dry mass observed after purification does not indicate reduced cellulose production, but rather reflects the removal of residual impurities such as proteins, pigments, and lignin-derived compounds. The effective removal of impurities from bacterial cellulose is essential for its application across various fields. The purification process directly influences properties such as transparency, porosity, and biocompatibility, making the cellulose more suitable for use in biodegradable packaging, biomedicine, and the food industry. Figure 2 illustrates the visual differences among BC samples obtained at different purification stages evaluated in this study. Figure 2A shows the BC immediately after fermentation in PFPH, without any purification treatment. The coloration indicates the presence of residual compounds from the hydrolysate, such as polyphenols and lignocellulosic degradation products, which may be adsorbed into the bacterial cellulose matrix.

Figure 2B shows the cellulose after NaOH treatment, which results in a lighter coloration, although some residual pigmentation remains. NaOH acts by removing organic residues, proteins, and part of the phenolic compounds that may be retained within the BC matrix. This effect occurs because NaOH breaks the chemical bonds present in these compounds, solubilizing them and allowing their removal during the washing step. Additionally, NaOH helps degrade cellular residues and other biological polymers that may remain adhered to the bacterial cellulose structure. Figure 2C shows the bacterial cellulose after a more rigorous treatment combining 0.5 M NaOH and H2O2, resulting in a significantly whiter material. Hydrogen peroxide assists in the oxidation and degradation of remaining impurities, promoting a more efficient clarification of the bacterial cellulose.

The bleaching of bacterial cellulose has been extensively studied in various bioprocesses. Studies have reported that the dark coloration of raw BC is often associated with the retention of phenolic compounds and residual metabolites from the culture medium. One such study evaluated the effectiveness of unconventional and biodegradable surfactants in BC purification, highlighting that sodium cocoyl isethionate (SCI) was particularly effective in removing residual proteins and DNA. Furthermore, combining SCI with NaOH in a two-step protocol proved to be an efficient way of eliminating residual surfactants, providing an environmentally sustainable alternative for bacterial cellulose purification [56].

In addition to conventional alkaline and oxidative methods, recent research has also explored the purification of bacterial cellulose using supercritical media, which allow enhanced extraction efficiency and reduced chemical waste generation. Previous studies have applied these methods in BC treatment. For example, Pigaleva et al. [57] evaluated the purification and sterilization of bacterial cellulose using carbon dioxide under supercritical conditions. The results demonstrated that this technique enabled a significant reduction in endotoxin levels and ensured material sterilization while preserving the structural integrity of the cellulose membranes. However, despite the effective removal of microorganisms and surface impurities, the purity levels required for medical applications have not yet been fully achieved.

Figure 3 complements the analysis of BC produced in PFPH and purified with NaOH and H2O2. Figure 3A, obtained at 10,000× magnification, reveals the surface morphology of the bacterial cellulose, highlighting its porous structure. This structural pattern is characteristic of bacterial celluloses produced under optimized conditions, providing a high water-retention capacity and enhanced interaction with biomolecules.

In Figure 3B, the network of nanofibrils that forms the bacterial cellulose structure can be observed. A more homogeneous surface with fewer cellular residues is evident, reinforcing the effectiveness of the purification protocol. These results confirm that impurity removal not only improves the coloration of the cellulose but also enhances its structural properties, making it more suitable for industrial and biomedical applications.

Previous studies suggest that combining alkaline and oxidative treatments—such as the use of NaOH and H2O2—is an efficient strategy for removing these contaminants, improving both the transparency and overall quality of the cellulose. However, other approaches have also been considered, such as the use of supercritical fluids [57], which may represent a promising alternative for obtaining high-purity bacterial cellulose without the need for harsh chemical reagents.

3.3. Structural and Chemical Characterization of the Purified Bacterial Cellulose

3.3.1. Surface Characterization by X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

The chemical surface characterization of BC-PFPH at different purification stages was performed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), focusing on the analysis of the carbon region (C 1s). Figure 4 presents the spectra of the three analyzed samples: (A) cellulose grown in PFPH without purification, (B) cellulose purified with NaOH, and (C) cellulose purified with NaOH followed by 3% hydrogen peroxide treatment. The identification of the chemical components was based on the deconvolution of the high-resolution C 1s spectra, allowing the assignment of peaks corresponding to different carbon oxidation states.

Figure 4A displays a spectrum dominated by an intense peak at ~284.8 eV, characteristic of C–C and C–H bonds. This signal can be attributed to the presence of organic compounds remaining from the culture medium, such as residual sugars, organic acids, and secondary metabolites derived from PFPH. The occurrence of hydrophobic material adsorbed on the cellulose surface is expected in unpurified samples, since bacterial synthesis takes place in a medium rich in carbohydrates and phenolic compounds that can interact with the cellulosic matrix. Additionally, a significant peak at ~286.5 eV, corresponding to C–O–H and C–O–C bonds, confirms the presence of glycosidic units typical of bacterial cellulose.

Moreover, the presence of a peak around ~288.5 eV (C=O, COOH) suggests the existence of carbonyl and carboxylic groups, possibly originating from partial oxidation of BC or adsorption of organic acids from the culture medium. This phenomenon is consistent with previous studies reporting that plant hydrolysates contain significant amounts of phenolic acids and carbonyl compounds, which can interact with the cellulose surface through hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions. The high intensity of the C=O signal indicates a chemical modification of the surface, suggesting that medium residues may contribute to partial functionalization of the cellulose even during biosynthesis.

The spectrum in Figure 4B, obtained after NaOH purification, reveals significant changes in the chemical composition of the cellulose surface. The C–C peak (~284.8 eV) shows reduced intensity, indicating effective removal of organic contaminants such as phenolic compounds, lipids, and residual proteins. This result aligns with literature reports describing NaOH as an efficient agent for solubilizing adsorbed organic substances and eliminating residues from the culture medium.

A relative increase is also observed in the intensity of the C–O–H/C–O–C peak (~286.5 eV), indicating that impurity removal allowed greater exposure of the polymeric cellulose structure. This suggests that the cellulose chains, previously coated with organic compounds, became more accessible after alkaline purification. Additionally, the persistence of the C=O peak (~288.5 eV), despite its reduced relative intensity, may indicate partial removal of carbonyl compounds that do not belong to the primary cellulose structure.

In Figure 4C, corresponding to sequential purification with NaOH followed by 3% hydrogen peroxide, a more pronounced reduction in the intensity of the C–C peak (~284.8 eV) is observed, indicating that this treatment removed remaining organic residues and yielded a purer cellulose surface. Furthermore, the C–O–H/C–O–C peak (~286.5 eV) exhibited increased intensity, suggesting that the oxidative treatment enhanced impurity removal and promoted greater exposure of characteristic cellulose bonds.

The continued presence of the C=O peak (~288.5 eV) in Figure 4C, but with a relative increase compared to Figure 4B, suggests that hydrogen peroxide induced the oxidation of hydroxyl (-OH) groups into carbonyl (C=O) or carboxyl (-COOH) groups. This effect is expected, since H2O2 is an oxidizing agent capable of modifying the chemical structure of cellulose, introducing new functional groups on its surface. Indeed, studies indicate that mild oxidation induced by peroxide can increase cellulose functionalization, enhancing its reactivity and hydrophilicity—properties that may be advantageous for specific biotechnological applications [58].

The XPS results confirm that NaOH purification was effective in removing surface impurities, significantly reducing the presence of C–C/C–H bonds associated with organic contaminants. The exposure of the cellulosic matrix was evidenced by the increased intensity of the C–O–H/C–O–C peak, indicating that the alkaline treatment successfully revealed the primary structure of BC-PFPH. Furthermore, the additional H2O2 treatment promoted mild oxidation, increasing the amount of carbonyl groups on the surface and potentially introducing carboxylic groups, which could modify its chemical and rheological properties.

This behavior is consistent with the literature, which reports that mild cellulose oxidation can enhance its hydrophilicity and chemical reactivity [58], favoring interactions with polymers, proteins, and metals in biomedical and environmental applications. Additionally, mild oxidation of bacterial cellulose (BC) selectively converts primary hydroxyl groups into carboxylic (acidic) groups without significantly degrading its crystalline structure [59,60]. This process introduces hydrophilic carboxylate groups onto the surface of cellulose nanofibrils, increasing BC’s affinity for water and generating new chemical functionalities. The oxidation also partially disrupts the intramolecular hydrogen-bonding network of cellulose, exposing more reactive sites and enhancing its chemical reactivity [61].

Therefore, the results demonstrate that the adopted purification protocol was sufficient to remove impurities from BC-PFPH, altering its surface chemistry and enabling controlled adjustments to the material’s final properties. The impact of these modifications on cellulose structure can be further validated through complementary analyses, such as FTIR and XRD, to correlate the observed chemical changes with potential morphological and structural alterations.

3.3.2. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopic Analysis (FTIR)

The FTIR spectra obtained reveal clear differences among the bacterial cellulose samples before and after chemical treatments, confirming the progressive effectiveness of the purification process (Figure 5). The spectrum of the untreated sample (black line) displays a broad absorption band around 3600 cm−1, attributed to hydrogen bonds of hydroxyl (-OH) groups, indicating the presence of cellulose but also suggesting the coexistence of hemicellulose and lignin [62]. In addition, bands at approximately 990 cm−1 and 950 cm−1 can be associated with C–O–C bonds characteristic of these non-cellulosic components, indicating that the structure remains partially impure.

With the alkaline NaOH treatment (red line), a reduction in the intensity of these bands is observed, indicating partial removal of hemicellulose and lignin. The decrease in the band around 990 cm−1 suggests the degradation of alkali-soluble impurities, while the attenuation of other bands indicates a more purified structure. However, the persistence of some of these bands suggests that residual lignin may still be present in the sample.

Samples treated with NaOH and hydrogen peroxide (blue line) exhibit more refined spectra, with a more pronounced reduction in bands associated with impurities. The absence of characteristic peaks of lignin and hemicellulose confirms the effectiveness of the oxidative treatment in purifying the cellulose. The efficient removal of these compounds is consistent with previous studies, in which oxidizing agents such as hydrogen peroxide have been widely employed to eliminate residual lignin and obtain a purer, more organized cellulose structure.

Thus, the progression observed in the FTIR spectra reinforces the sequential efficiency of the applied treatments, evidencing the gradual removal of non-cellulosic components and the formation of a more homogeneous and purified structure. Therefore, FTIR confirms the advancement of cellulose purification according to the treatments applied, validating the sequential removal of non-cellulosic components and the attainment of a more defined and pure cellulose structure.

3.3.3. Crystallinity Analysis by X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

XRD analysis enabled the evaluation of the crystallinity and phase composition of the obtained cellulosic materials. The comparative XRD patterns (Figure 6) reveal the structural modifications induced by the applied treatments. The untreated bacterial cellulose (Figure 6A1) exhibits a broad diffraction pattern characteristic of amorphous cellulose with low crystallinity. The absence of well-defined diffraction peaks suggests the presence of non-cellulosic components such as hemicellulose, lignin, and residual organic matter, which obscure the characteristic crystalline peaks of cellulose I. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting the predominance of amorphous structures in unprocessed cellulose derived from plant or microbial sources [63,64].

After the NaOH treatment (Figure 6A2), a significant increase in crystallinity was observed. The more pronounced diffraction peaks indicate the removal of amorphous non-cellulosic components, allowing the intrinsic crystalline regions of cellulose to become more evident. Alkaline treatment has been widely reported to induce partial deacetylation and the dissolution of hemicelluloses and lignin, thereby improving structural order [65]. However, some residual impurities may persist, as evidenced by the presence of minor additional peaks that do not correspond to pure cellulose.

The combined NaOH and hydrogen peroxide treatment (Figure 6A3) resulted in further purification, as indicated by the sharper and more distinct diffraction peaks. The disappearance of certain peaks suggests effective delignification and removal of residual hemicellulose. Hydrogen peroxide, as a strong oxidizing agent, facilitated the degradation of chromophoric and phenolic structures, promoting impurity removal and yielding a highly purified cellulose product [66]. The XRD pattern of A3 resembles that of highly crystalline bacterial cellulose, indicating a transition toward a more ordered structure.

When compared with literature reports on bacterial cellulose purification, the trends observed in this study align with the findings of Chen et al. [67], which demonstrated that sequential alkaline and oxidative treatments significantly enhance cellulose crystallinity while minimizing degradation. However, excessive oxidation can lead to partial depolymerization, reducing mechanical integrity. In this study, the A3 pattern showed no evidence of excessive degradation, suggesting that the applied conditions were optimized to increase purity without compromising cellulose integrity.

The combined NaOH and hydrogen peroxide treatment (Figure 6A3) effectively removed amorphous impurities, resulting in a highly crystalline product that may be considered for advanced applications such as biomedical scaffolds and nanocomposites. Furthermore, this process increases the accessibility of hydroxyl groups, facilitating subsequent functionalization for specialized applications such as controlled drug release and wound healing. Improved crystallinity is also correlated with enhanced mechanical properties, greater thermal stability, and increased resistance to microbial degradation. Studies have shown that alkaline treatments with moderate NaOH concentrations (up to 5% w/v) preserve the type Iα crystalline structure of cellulose. Additionally, the modified fiber interactions contribute to adjustable viscoelastic behavior, enabling optimization for various biomedical applications. The greater permeability resulting from alkaline treatment also enhances liquid absorption and molecular diffusion—desirable characteristics in tissue engineering materials [67].

Overall, the results of this study confirm that the combined NaOH and hydrogen peroxide treatment is highly effective for purifying bacterial cellulose derived from PFP. The progressive increase in crystallinity from Figure 6A1–A3 demonstrates the efficiency of the treatment in removing amorphous contaminants and non-cellulosic materials.

3.3.4. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The thermogravimetric profiles of BC obtained from passion fruit peel hydrolysate (PFPH) (Figure 7) exhibit the three classical stages of mass loss typical of cellulosic materials: (i) initial dehydration below ~120 °C; (ii) the main degradation event between ~280 and 350 °C, attributed to the cleavage of glycosidic bonds and the formation of levoglucosan; and (iii) the gradual carbonization and stabilization of the carbonaceous residue up to 600 °C. The purification process affected both the definition of the main DTG peak and the residual fraction at 600 °C—parameters that are particularly sensitive for assessing sample purity and compositional heterogeneity.

The unpurified sample exhibited multi-stage decomposition with pronounced shoulders in the DTG curve (Figure 7A), behavior consistent with the likely presence of non-cellulosic constituents derived from the lignocellulosic medium, such as hemicellulosic fragments, lignin, proteins, and mineral salts. The residual mass at 600 °C was approximately 39%, confirming the presence of inorganic material and refractory compounds. The earlier onset of degradation (Tonset ~240 °C) and the main decomposition peak at ~305 °C reflect a more heterogeneous microstructure and lower thermal stability. Similar results have been reported by Campano et al. [68], who reported Tonset values between 230 and 250 °C and residual masses above 30% in BC samples containing lignocellulosic impurities, reinforcing that contaminants decrease thermal stability.

The alkaline treatment (NaOH) partially simplified the thermal profile (Figure 7B). The main DTG peak appeared at ~270 °C, with a Tonset of ~220 °C, while the final residue increased to approximately 49%. This behavior is likely associated with the retention or formation of sodium-containing inorganic species during the alkaline treatment. Incomplete removal of residual Na+ ions or the formation of thermally stable sodium salts (e.g., carbonates) may have contributed to the elevated ash content observed at 600 °C, resulting in a higher residual mass despite partial purification.

Sequential purification with NaOH and H2O2 produced the most stable and homogeneous material (Figure 7C). The DTG curve exhibited a single well-defined maximum in the characteristic region of cellulose pyrolysis, with a Tonset of approximately 260 °C and a Tmax of ~320 °C—the highest among all samples. The final residue was only ~19%, confirming the more effective removal of organic and mineral impurities.

The thermal stability observed for BC-PFPH after sequential purification is consistent with values reported for commercial bacterial cellulose, which typically exhibits main degradation peaks between 280 and 320 °C [69]. In comparison, plant-derived cellulose generally shows degradation temperatures in the range of approximately 300–350 °C, depending on crystallinity, degree of polymerization, and purity [70]. The Tmax of ~320 °C obtained in this study therefore falls within the expected range for cellulosic materials, indicating that the use of passion fruit peel hydrolysate as substrate does not compromise the intrinsic thermal stability of the resulting bacterial cellulose [71].

Consistently, the sample treated solely with NaOH showed the highest amount of residue at 600 °C, followed by the unpurified sample and, finally, the sample treated with NaOH and H2O2, which exhibited the lowest value. Conversely, the Tonset and Tmax parameters showed the opposite trend—lower in the NaOH-treated sample, intermediate in the unpurified sample, and higher in the sequentially purified sample with NaOH and H2O2.

These results indicate that purification primarily acts by removing catalytically active impurities and refractory components without altering the fundamental chemical structure of cellulose. The observed behavior is consistent with the XRD analyses (Figure 6), which showed an intensification of the characteristic peaks of cellulose I, associated with increased effective crystallinity and molecular order—factors directly related to greater thermal stability.

In practical terms, the lower ash content and the more uniform DTG profile observed in the sample treated with NaOH and H2O2 represent potential advantages for thermal processing steps such as drying, sterilization, and hot pressing, as well as for applications requiring high thermal stability and optical transparency, including films and wound dressings.

3.4. Biodegradability of the Cellulose Produced from PFPH

The biodegradability of BC obtained from PFPH was monitored over 42 days through morphological analyses and quantification of degradation. The results revealed a progressive breakdown of the material, evidenced by changes in color, texture, and structural integrity. Over time, the BC exhibited a gradual yellowing followed by darkening, along with increasing brittleness that was visually apparent.

These transformations can be attributed to oxidative processes and the activity of microorganisms present in the environment, which secrete cellulolytic enzymes such as cellulases and β-glucosidases that are responsible for hydrolyzing the polysaccharide structure of cellulose. In addition, the dark coloration may be associated with the partial degradation of cellulose polymers and the formation of oxidation by-products such as phenolic compounds and quinones, which are commonly observed during the biodegradation of cellulosic biomaterials [72]. The loss of structural cohesion and the increase in brittleness are attributed to the fragmentation of glucose chains caused by the hydrolytic action of microorganisms and environmental factors such as humidity and temperature, which favor the biological degradation of the material.

Quantitative analysis revealed that after 35 days, the cellulose lost approximately 20% of its initial structure, and this increased to 26% after 42 days (Figure 8). This behavior is consistent with previous studies investigating the biodegradation of bacterial cellulose under different environmental conditions. Quantitative analysis revealed that after 35 days, the cellulose lost approximately 20% of its initial mass, increasing to 26% after 42 days (Figure 8). This value falls within the range commonly reported for bacterial cellulose films derived from agro-industrial substrates, which typically present mass losses between approximately 20 and 40% over similar time frames. The literature indicates that biodegradation rates of BC are strongly influenced by structural organization, particularly crystallinity and matrix purity [73,74]. Materials with higher crystalline content generally display slower degradation due to reduced accessibility of tightly packed cellulose chains to microbial enzymes, whereas more amorphous regions are preferentially attacked. In the present study, XRD analysis confirmed the preservation and intensification of the cellulose I crystalline structure after purification, which may have contributed to the moderate degradation rate observed. Furthermore, sequential NaOH and H2O2 treatment likely removed non-cellulosic impurities and catalytically active residues that could accelerate degradation, resulting in a more homogeneous and structurally ordered material. These findings suggest that the controlled biodegradation behavior of BC-PFPH is consistent with its crystallinity profile and purification level.

Reports indicate that bacterial cellulose (BC) exhibits high biodegradability in soil, with a mass loss of approximately 75% after eight weeks, while nylon membranes showed no significant degradation [72]. Controlled degradation of BC can be exploited for the sustained release of enzymes over extended periods, preventing uncontrolled decomposition while maintaining good mechanical properties and biocompatibility. These findings suggest that BC with controlled biodegradation behavior has strong potential for biomedical applications such as drug delivery systems and tissue engineering [75].

BC tends to exhibit high biodegradability in various natural environments, with more pronounced degradation occurring in soil and estuarine settings, where the presence of microorganisms and elevated moisture levels can accelerate the process [76].

The degradation rate observed in this study is consistent with literature reports for bacterial cellulose but can be considered faster than that of the synthetic polymers that are widely used in industry. In comparison with conventional plastics such as polyethylene and polypropylene) which can take decades or even centuries to degrade, BC offers a significant advantage, as it can be rapidly assimilated by microorganisms under normal environmental conditions [35,76]. This characteristic makes the material highly promising for sustainable applications such as biodegradable packaging and biomaterials, contributing to the reduction in environmental impacts caused by persistent plastic waste. Furthermore, bacterial cellulose exhibits advantageous mechanical and thermal properties, including high tensile strength and remarkable water retention capacity, which are beneficial for both biomedical and industrial applications [18,77,78].

The use of alternative substrates for BC production has been widely explored in the literature, with studies demonstrating the feasibility of employing various lignocellulosic sources and agro-industrial residues to reduce production costs and enhance process sustainability [75]. The results obtained in this study reinforce the viability of BC-PFPH as a biodegradable and sustainable alternative, with potential applications in several fields, particularly in biomaterials and biodegradable packaging. From an application standpoint, the observed degradation rate can be considered advantageous for sustainable food packaging systems. While structural integrity during product storage is essential, biodegradability after disposal represents a key requirement for environmentally responsible materials. The 26% mass loss observed after 42 days under soil burial conditions indicates controlled biodegradation rather than rapid structural collapse. It is important to note that soil burial represents a highly favorable microbial environment, significantly more aggressive than typical food packaging storage conditions, which are generally dry and less biologically active. Therefore, the degradation behavior observed suggests an adequate balance between functional stability during use and environmental degradability after disposal.

Its degradation occurs over a significantly shorter period compared to synthetic polymers commonly used in industry—such as PET, PP, LDPE, PS, and PVC—which can persist in the environment for decades or even centuries (Table 2).

The rapid degradation of BC, occurring within 30 to 90 days, indicates its potential as a biodegradable alternative for reducing the accumulation of plastic waste in the environment. Moreover, its production from renewable sources, such as agro-industrial residues, makes its manufacturing process even more attractive from both economic and ecological perspectives—unlike petroleum-derived polymers, whose production and disposal have significant environmental impacts.

In comparison to PLA, a biopolymer that is marketed as biodegradable, BC exhibits an even more favorable degradation rate. Although PLA is considered compostable under industrial conditions, in aerobic aquatic environments, studies have shown that it presents a limited biodegradation rate, with mineralization values below 5% after 77 days of incubation. This indicates that under such conditions, PLA has considerable resistance to degradation [86]. Bacterial cellulose, on the other hand, demonstrates a much more advantageous degradation rate and is synthesized through low-toxicity biotechnological processes.

4. Conclusions

This study demonstrated the feasibility of producing bacterial cellulose (BC) from passion fruit peel hydrolysate (PFPH) using a SCOBY-based static fermentation system. Nitrogen source modulation significantly influenced BC productivity and material characteristics, with ammonium sulfate promoting the highest initial yield, while purification strategy strongly affected the final usable mass and surface chemistry. Sequential alkaline and oxidative treatments enhanced structural uniformity and reduced residual impurities, resulting in improved thermal stability and lower ash content without altering the fundamental cellulose I crystalline structure. The biodegradation profile (26% mass loss after 42 days) was consistent with values reported for bacterial cellulose derived from agro-industrial substrates, suggesting maintained structural integrity alongside environmentally responsive degradation behavior. Beyond demonstrating substrate valorization, this work contributes to understanding how nutrient regulation in complex microbial consortia affects BC biosynthesis and material performance. The results indicate potential for the development of sustainable cellulose-based materials derived from low-cost residues. Future studies should further evaluate mechanical and barrier properties, as well as application-specific performance, to fully assess suitability for food packaging and related uses.
