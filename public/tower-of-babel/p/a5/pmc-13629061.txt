MKRN1 suppresses cuproptosis in colorectal cancer through ubiquitin-dependent regulation of SLC31A1
Authors: Huali Luo, Jiaxin Hu, Xiaojing Lin, Shu Zhao, Mengxing Li, Qinshan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the emerging role of cuproptosis in cancer biology, the regulatory mechanism by which ubiquitination modulates this process through MKRN1 and SLC31A1 in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the functional relationship between MKRN1, SLC31A1, and cuproptosis in CRC.MethodsStable MKRN1-knockdown and MKRN1-overexpressing colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines were established. Cuproptosis was induced using elesclomol and copper sulfate, and assessed by cell viability assays,, measurement of intracellular copper levels, and western blot analysis of hallmark proteins (HSP70 and DLAT). The MKRN1-SLC31A1 interaction was examined by co-immunofluorescence, ubiquitination assays (employing MG132, CHX, and linkage-specific ubiquitin mutants), and quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR). Mitochondrial function was evaluated via JC-1 staining and transmission electron microscopy. Rescue experiments were conducted using dual genetic manipulation of MKRN1 and SLC31A1. Finally, in vivo validation was performed using a xenograft model in NOD-SCID mice.ResultsMKRN1 overexpression significantly attenuated cuproptosis agonist-induced cytotoxicity and reduced intracellular copper accumulation. Mechanistically, MKRN1 directly interacts with SLC31A1 and promotes its polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This degradation prevents copper overload, helping to maintain mitochondrial integrity.(as evidenced by maintained mitochondrial membrane potential and Tomm20 expression) and suppressing key cuproptosis hallmarks, including HSP70 induction and DLAT oligomerization. Genetic rescue experiments confirmed that SLC31A1 knockdown reversed the cuproptosis sensitivity conferred by MKRN1 depletion. Consistently, MKRN1 overexpression promoted tumor growth in vivo while concurrently suppressing cuproptosis.ConclusionIn summary, MKRN1 suppresses copper-induced cell death in colorectal cancer by promoting the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of the copper transporter SLC31A1, thus limiting copper accumulation and mitochondrial toxicity. This mechanism confers resistance to copper-induced cell death and facilitates tumor progression, establishing the MKRN1/SLC31A1 axis as a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States [1]. Globally, the incidence of CRC continues to rise [2], with a concerning trend toward younger patient populations, driven largely by dietary patterns, obesity, and lifestyle factors [3]. Epidemiological data project approximately 153,020 new CRC cases and 52,550 CRC-related deaths in 2023, including 19,550 cases and 3,750 deaths among individuals under 50 years of age [4]. Current therapeutic strategies for CRC encompass surgical resection combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [5, 6]. However, chemoresistance remains a major clinical challenge, significantly limiting treatment efficacy and worsening patient prognosis [7]. Given the frequent adverse effects and dynamic nature of drug resistance, the development of safer and more effective therapeutic agents for CRC is urgently needed [8].Growing evidence suggests that chemoresistance is closely linked to the suppression of regulated cell death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis. This highlights the critical importance of targeting these pathways as a promising strategy for improving CRC treatment outcomes.

Cuproptosis represents a distinct form of regulated cell death (RCD) that differs fundamentally from other RCD modalities, characterized by its copper dependence, accumulation of lipoylated proteins, and depletion of Fe-S cluster proteins [9]. As an essential trace element, copper serves as a cofactor for over thirty enzymes involved in diverse physiological processes [10]. Intracellular copper levels must be tightly regulated alongside other metals—deficiency impairs metal-dependent enzymatic functions, while excess leads to cytotoxic effects and cell death [11]. Analogous to other cell death pathways, cuproptosis has demonstrated significant potential for cancer therapeutics [12].

The SLC31A1 gene encodes a protein that functions as a homotrimer in dietary copper uptake [13], serving as the principal mediator of Cu2+ absorption in mammalian cells and tissues [14]. Emerging evidence reveals its dual role in tumor progression: As an oncogenic promoter, SLC31A1 facilitates copper transport in tumor cells and stimulates angiogenesis, thereby accelerating cancer progression [15]. The LNP-miR-155-cy5 inhibitor exerts antitumor effects by downregulating SLC31A1-mediated copper transport and disrupting intracellular copper homeostasis, subsequently modulating the β-catenin/TCF4 pathway [16]. Furthermore, SLC31A1 undergoes ubiquitination and degradation mediated by the E3 ligase NEDD4L, which suppresses CTR1-AKT signaling and tumor growth [17].

Conversely, tumor-suppressive functions have also been documented. Multiple studies demonstrate that SLC31A1 modulates cisplatin concentrations across various malignancies and significantly influences patient prognosis [18–20]. The conceptualization of cuproptosis has further illuminated its role in copper overload-induced cell death: Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) induced by chronic hyperglycemia upregulate SLC31A1 expression, elevating intracellular copper levels and disrupting copper homeostasis to promote apoptosis in murine cardiomyocytes [21].SLC31A1 overexpression induces cuproptosis in both 293 T cells and human lung adenocarcinoma cells [9], suggesting its potential as a tumor suppressor through copper-dependent cell death mechanisms.

Eukaryotic cells maintain protein homeostasis through two principal degradation pathways: (1) the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) and (2) the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) [22]. Ubiquitination, a critical post-translational modification, plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular protein turnover [23]. Emerging evidence highlights the involvement of E3 ubiquitin ligases in modulating diverse biological processes associated with cancer progression and cellular stress responses [24]. These enzymes are classified into three major families based on their structural domains: the RING (Really Interesting New Gene) family [25], HECT (Homologous to E6AP C-Terminus) domain family [26], and U-box family [24]. Notably, RING-type E3 ligases have been extensively characterized as key regulators of oncogenic processes, functioning either as tumor promoters or suppressors to control critical cellular behaviors including proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [27, 28]. The ubiquitination cascade involves coordinated action of three enzymes: E1 (ubiquitin-activating enzyme), E2 (ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme), and E3 (ubiquitin ligase). E3 ligases serve as the crucial determinant of substrate specificity by selectively recognizing target proteins and facilitating their transfer to the E2 ~ Ub complex [29]. This unique position in the ubiquitination machinery establishes E3 ligases as the most influential regulators of protein ubiquitination.

The MKRN1 gene, located on the long arm of chromosome 7 (7q), exhibits remarkable evolutionary conservation across species [30]. This gene encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase that critically regulates both metabolic disorders and malignant transformation through substrate-specific ubiquitination [31]. Emerging evidence demonstrates MKRN1's multifaceted role in oncogenesis via ubiquitin-mediated degradation of key regulatory proteins: Direct ubiquitination of APC promotes Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation and tumor progression. Post-translational modification of p14ARF accelerates gastric tumorigenesis. FADD degradation-mediated apoptosis suppression enhances melanoma growth. SNIP1 protein degradation activates TGF-β signaling to facilitate colorectal cancer metastasis [32–35]. While these findings establish MKRN1 as a crucial oncogenic regulator, its complete mechanistic repertoire in tumor progression remains to be fully elucidated. Our bioinformatic analysis of the UCLCAN database reveals significant upregulation of copper transporter proteins and copper homeostasis regulators in colorectal cancer (CRC), indicating heightened copper demand in malignant cells. Maintenance of copper homeostasis appears essential for CRC progression, though copper's dual nature renders it cytotoxic at elevated intracellular concentrations [36]. Notably, we observed: Elevated SLC31A1 mRNA expression in TCGA CRC datasets. Paradoxically low/undetectable SLC31A1 protein levels in clinical specimens (HPA database) [37]. This discordance suggests post-translational regulation of SLC31A1, likely through ubiquitin-mediated degradation, which would directly impact: Intracellular copper accumulation, Cellular sensitivity to cuproptosis inducers, Copper-dependent signaling pathways.We hypothesize that E3 ubiquitin ligase-mediated degradation of SLC31A1 (potentially by MKRN1) represents a novel mechanism for modulating copper homeostasis in CRC, offering new therapeutic opportunities for cuproptosis-based interventions [38].

The clinical application of copper ionophores remains suboptimal, likely due to limited pharmacological studies and the emergence of therapy resistance, which reduces tumor sensitivity to these agents. MKRN1 has been implicated in promoting tumor progression, with multiple studies linking its expression to chemoresistance in cancers such as cervical and breast cancer [39–41]. However, experimental evidence regarding the regulatory role of MKRN1 in copper-induced cell death (cuproptosis) remains lacking. Our preliminary studies have demonstrated that MKRN1 is overexpressed in colorectal cancer (CRC), where it drives tumor progression while suppressing key cuproptosis-related genes. This finding provides novel insights into how tumor cells evade cuproptosis. Building on this foundation, we further investigate the mechanistic link between MKRN1 and cuproptosis in CRC, as well as the potential therapeutic value of SLC31A1 (a critical copper transporter) in clinical CRC treatment.

Western Blot

The key steps of Western Blotting include: (1) Sample preparation (cell/tissue lysis, protein quantification using BCA/Bradford assay, and denaturation in Laemmli buffer at 95 °C ~ 100 °C); (2) SDS-PAGE electrophoresis (protein separation based on molecular weight using a discontinuous polyacrylamide gel); (3) Membrane transfer (electrophoretic transfer of proteins onto PVDF or nitrocellulose membranes via wet/tank blotting); (4) Blocking (incubation with 5% non-fat milk or BSA in TBST to minimize nonspecific binding); (5) Primary antibody incubation (overnight at 4 °C or 1–2 h at RT with target-specific antibodies); (6) Secondary antibody incubation (HRP-conjugated antibody, 1 h at RT); and (7) Detection (ECL-based chemiluminescent substrate exposure for signal visualization). Protein expression levels are quantified by densitometric analysis (e.g., ImageJ) and normalized to internal controls (e.g., β-actin or GAPDH).

Add 5 × Loading Buffer protein Loading Buffer to the supernatant, dilute the 5 × Loading Buffer to 1 × with NP40, and resuspend the precipitate with 1 × loading buffer. Then boil the two components for protein denaturation and store them at −80 ℃ for later use.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA is extracted from samples (e.g., cells/tissues) using TRIzol® or column-based kits, followed by DNase treatment to eliminate genomic DNA. RNA quality/purity is verified via spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio ≥ 1.8) and electrophoresis. cDNA is synthesized via reverse transcription (RT) with oligo(dT) primers or random hexamers. qPCR is performed using SYBR® Green or TaqMan® probes in a thermal cycler: denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), amplification (40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s), and melting curve analysis (SYBR® Green only) to confirm specificity. Each reaction includes technical triplicates, no-template controls (NTCs), and reference genes (e.g., GAPDH/β-actin) for normalization. Data are analyzed via the ΔΔCt method to quantify relative gene expression. Key parameters include amplification efficiency (90% ~ 110%) and R2 > 0.98 for standard curves. Immunofluorescence co-localization assay

Cells cultured on coverslips are fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100, 10 min), and blocked (5% BSA, 1 h). Primary antibodies targeting proteins of interest (raised in different species) are incubated overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated to spectrally distinct fluorophores (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488/594) are applied (1 h, dark). Nuclei are stained with DAPI (5 min). Samples are mounted with antifade reagent and imaged by confocal microscopy (sequential scanning to minimize bleed-through). Z-stack images (0.5 μm intervals) are acquired for 3D analysis. Co-localization is quantified using Pearson’s coefficient (PCC) or Manders’ overlap coefficient (MOC) in ImageJ (Coloc2 plugin). Controls include single-labeling, no-primary antibody, and unstained samples. Data represent mean ± SEM of ≥ 3 independent experiments.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed in 600 μL NP-40 buffer (1 mM PMSF) and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). For input controls, 100 μL supernatant was mixed with 5 × Laemmli buffer (1:4), boiled (100 °C, 10 min), and stored at −80 °C. Lysates were pre-cleared with 20 μL Protein A/G beads (500 μL lysis buffer, 4 °C, 30 min, rotation; 3,000 × g, 5 min). Immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating pre-cleared lysates with primary antibody (1 μg/mg protein, 2 h, RT) followed by 35 μL A/G beads (overnight, 4 °C). Beads were washed (3 × PBS), and bound proteins were eluted (70 μL 1 × Laemmli buffer, 100 °C, 10 min) for WB analysis of SLC31A1-MKRN1 interaction. Input (10% lysate) and IgG controls were included.

Vortex and mix well while boiling.

The subsequent treatment was the same as the co-immunoprecipitation experiment, and the ubiquitination level of SLC31A1 was analyzed by WB.

Results

Biological Validation of the Association Between Cuproptosis and MKRN1 in Colorectal Cancer

Cuproptosis, a form of cell death driven by copper ion overload, represents a potential therapeutic strategy for cancer. This study investigated whether MKRN1 regulates cuproptosis in colorectal cancer (CRC). Bioinformatics analysis revealed upregulation of copper transport and homeostasis-related genes in CRC (Fig. 1A), whereas genes encoding cuproptosis-inducing toxic proteins were downregulated. Conversely, negative regulators of cuproptosis, such as CDKN2A and GLS, showed reduced expression in CRC (Fig. 1B). MKRN1 is known to be highly expressed in CRC. Our bioinformatics analysis further indicated that in CRC cells, cuproptosis-promoting genes are expressed at low levels, whereas negative regulators are expressed at high levels. Although analysis of the GDSC database revealed a weak positive correlation between MKRN1 mRNA levels and sensitivity to the copper ionophore elesclomol (R = 0.15, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 1D), the precise role and direction of MKRN1 in cuproptosis remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to perform functional validation to elucidate the regulatory relationship between MKRN1 and cuproptosis and explore its potential therapeutic relevance.

Functional assessment using trypan blue and Hoechst 33,258 staining revealed that MKRN1 expression levels significantly influenced the cytotoxic effects of cuproptosis inducers. Compared with control cells, MKRN1-overexpressing CRC cells exhibited lower mortality following cuproptosis induction, whereas MKRN1-knockdown cells showed higher mortality under the same conditions (Fig. 2B–E).

Consistently, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that treatment with the cuproptosis inducer (elesclomol + Cu2⁺) substantially inhibited cell proliferation. Notably, MKRN1 overexpression conferred resistance to this growth inhibition, whereas MKRN1 knockdown enhanced sensitivity (Fig. 2F-H).

Collectively, these findings indicate that MKRN1 protects CRC cells from cuproptosis-induced cytotoxicity, thereby promoting tumor progression. Targeting the MKRN1–cuproptosis axis may thus represent a potential therapeutic strategy for CRC.

MKRN1 suppresses expression of cuproptosis markers

Notably, MKRN1 overexpression resulted in: Reduced expression of the cuproptotic effector HSP70; enhanced stability of iron-sulfur cluster protein FDX1; increased monomeric DLAT protein levels; decreased expression of high-affinity copper transporter SLC31A1. Emerging evidence indicates that cuproptosis is an oxidative phosphorylation-dependent process characterized by DLAT oligomerization, which induces mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cell death [42]. Recent methodological advances have enabled the detection of oligomerized DLAT through fractionation of soluble and insoluble protein fractions [43].

Our immunoblotting analysis of these fractions revealed that: MKRN1-overexpressing CRC cells exhibited significantly reduced insoluble DLAT oligomers; MKRN1-knockdown cells showed marked accumulation of insoluble DLAT aggregates (Fig. 3E-F).

Complementary in vivo studies using intestinal tissues from MKRN1-knockout mice demonstrated: Upregulation of cuproptosis effector proteins; increased expression of pro-cuptoptotic copper transporters (Fig. 3G).

These findings collectively demonstrate that MKRN1 functions as a critical regulator of DLAT oligomerization and cuproptosis progression through both cellular and organismal mechanisms.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that MKRN1 regulates copper homeostasis in colorectal cancer cells by mediating ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SLC31A1. This mechanism prevents copper overload-induced pathological effects, including iron-sulfur cluster depletion, DLAT oligomerization, HSP70 upregulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction characterized by loss of membrane potential. In colorectal cancer cells, high expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase MKRN1 promotes the polyubiquitination of SLC31A1 and subsequent proteasomal degradation of the copper influx transporter SLC31A1. This reduces SLC31A1 localization at the plasma membrane, limiting copper ion uptake and preventing intracellular copper overload. Consequently, key cuproptosis events are suppressed: mitochondrial damage (loss of ΔΨm and Tomm20 downregulation) is mitigated, iron-sulfur cluster proteins (e.g., FDX1) remain stable, and toxic protein aggregation (DLAT oligomerization and HSP70 induction) is inhibited. Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies of the digestive tract and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [44]. As the second deadliest cancer in the United States [45] CRC ranks third in incidence among males and second among females globally, accounting for approximately 900,000 annual deaths [46].Despite advances in screening and treatment, CRC continues to pose substantial clinical challenges due to its high incidence, mortality, and metastatic potential. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC pathogenesis is therefore essential for early detection, prognosis evaluation, and the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Recent progress in CRC biomarker discovery has revealed that dysregulation of E3 ubiquitin ligases can lead to aberrant degradation or functional alteration of key proteins involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, DNA repair, and metastatic signaling pathways [47]. These findings highlight the potential of targeting the ubiquitin–proteasome system for developing innovative anti-tumor strategies.

Copper homeostasis plays a dual role in cellular physiology and tumor biology. Although essential for normal cellular functions, excessive copper accumulation induces cytotoxicity through a recently identified cell death mechanism termed cuproptosis [36]. This process involves copper overload-mediated binding to lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, leading to protein aggregation, loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, and subsequent proteotoxic stress [11]. Notably, cancer cells reliant on mitochondrial respiration exhibit heightened sensitivity to copper-induced cytotoxicity; for instance, NCI-H2030 lung cancer cells display nearly 1,000-fold greater susceptibility to elesclomol-Cu2⁺ when cultured in galactose versus glucose [42]. This highlights the therapeutic potential of cuproptosis induction in malignancies characterized.

Our investigation revealed elevated copper levels in CRC cells compared to normal counterparts, with MKRN1 overexpression significantly reducing intracellular copper concentrations. This observation suggests that MKRN1 maintains copper homeostasis within a physiological range, thereby conferring resistance to cuproptosis inducers. Mechanistically, we identified SLC31A1, a high-affinity copper transporter [9], as a key substrate of MKRN1-mediated ubiquitination. Through comprehensive biochemical and functional assays, we demonstrated that MKRN1 regulates cellular sensitivity to elesclomol by controlling SLC31A1 protein stability. Genetic rescue experiments confirmed that SLC31A1 knockdown reverses the enhanced cuproptosis sensitivity induced by MKRN1 depletion, while SLC31A1 overexpression abolishes MKRN1-mediated resistance.

In the early phase of this study, analysis of the GDSC database revealed a weak positive correlation between MKRN1 mRNA expression and sensitivity to elesclomol (Fig. 1D), suggesting a potential role for MKRN1 in cuproptosis regulation. However, subsequent functional experiments demonstrated that MKRN1 overexpression inhibited elesclomol-induced cuproptosis, whereas MKRN1 knockdown enhanced it. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by several factors. First, the GDSC analysis was based on mRNA levels, whereas MKRN1 primarily regulates SLC31A1 at the protein level via ubiquitin-mediated degradation; thus, mRNA and protein expression may not be concordant. Second, as a copper ionophore, elesclomol exerts cytotoxicity through multiple mechanisms, with cuproptosis representing only one of them; consequently, cellular sensitivity to elesclomol may reflect the integration of cuproptosis and other pathways. Third, TCGA data revealed marked discordance between SLC31A1 mRNA and protein expression in colorectal cancer, supporting a predominant role for post-translational regulation in copper metabolism. Collectively, our mechanistic findings indicate that MKRN1 suppresses cuproptosis by promoting ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SLC31A1, thereby uncovering a complex regulatory network underlying the initial bioinformatic correlation.

Consistent with established cuproptosis mechanisms, MKRN1 overexpression protected mitochondrial integrity against copper-induced damage, as evidenced by preserved membrane potential and cristae morphology. Conversely, these protective effects were negated by SLC31A1 co-overexpression. In vivo studies further validated the tumor-promoting function of MKRN1, with xenograft models showing increased FDX1 expression in MKRN1-overexpressing tumors, indicative of preserved iron-sulfur cluster proteins and suppressed proteotoxic stress.

In conclusion, our study establishes MKRN1 as a critical regulator of copper homeostasis that promotes CRC progression through ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SLC31A1. This molecular axis maintains sub-toxic copper levels and confers resistance to cuproptosis inducers, suggesting that targeted disruption of the MKRN1/SLC31A1 interaction may represent a promising therapeutic strategy. Future investigations will explore combinatorial approaches integrating cuproptosis inducers with conventional CRC therapies, with the aim of enhancing treatment efficacy through MKRN1 modulation.

While the E3 ligase NEDD4L has been reported to ubiquitinate SLC31A1, our work uncovers a distinct regulatory axis mediated by MKRN1. Notably, we identify K63-linked polyubiquitination as the primary mechanism, which often directs substrates for lysosomal degradation or signal modulation rather than canonical proteasomal degradation, suggesting a novel layer of regulation in copper transporter turnover.

Our findings posit the MKRN1/SLC31A1 interface as a ‘druggable’ node. Therapeutic strategies could include the development of small-molecule inhibitors that disrupt the MKRN1-SLC31A1 interaction, or PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) designed to selectively degrade MKRN1 in CRC cells. Such approaches would be predicted to stabilize SLC31A1, sensitize tumors to existing cuproptosis inducers like elesclomol, and potentially reverse chemoresistance.

Limitations and future perspectives

Our study establishes MKRN1 as a critical regulator of copper homeostasis in CRC through ubiquitin-dependent degradation of SLC31A1, yet several aspects require further investigation. Although we demonstrated that MKRN1 overexpression promotes tumor progression in vivo, its role in conferring resistance to copper-mediated therapies remains to be validated in animal models. Additionally, the precise molecular mechanisms governing MKRN1's substrate specificity and ubiquitination of SLC31A1 warrant deeper exploration, potentially through structural studies to map critical interaction domains.

We acknowledge that our mitochondrial data are largely correlative; direct measurements of OCR, ATP, TCA activity, and ROS, as well as the functional role of TOMM20, await future investigation.

We acknowledge that the in vivo evidence presented here is limited. Although MKRN1 knockdown intestinal tissues and MKRN1-overexpressing xenografts consistently support the role of MKRN1 in suppressing cuproptosis via SLC31A1, future studies using conditional knockout mice in an APC-driven spontaneous colorectal cancer model will be necessary to fully address the physiological relevance of this axis in tumorigenesis. Technical difficulties in xenograft establishment also highlight the need for more robust models in follow-up investigations.

Future research should focus on translating these findings into therapeutic applications, including the development of MKRN1-targeted strategies to sensitize CRC cells to cuproptosis inducers. The clinical relevance of MKRN1 expression as a predictive biomarker for copper-based therapies also merits investigation. Furthermore, given the emerging role of copper metabolism in cancer biology, exploring whether this regulatory axis extends to other malignancies could broaden the potential therapeutic implications. Addressing these questions will advance our understanding of copper-dependent cell death mechanisms and may lead to novel treatment paradigms for CRC and beyond.

Although we have not performed in vivo combination therapy or clinical dataset analyses, our identification of the MKRN1/SLC31A1 axis as a cuproptosis regulator provides a rationale for future translational studies, including testing MKRN1 depletion with elesclomol in animal models and evaluating its prognostic value in CRC cohorts.
