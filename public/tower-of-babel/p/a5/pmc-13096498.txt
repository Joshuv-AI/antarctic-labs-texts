Combined effect of atopic dermatitis and chronic kidney disease on overall and cardiovascular disease mortality
Authors: Xiaoqiong Lin, Changchang Li, Fangfang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is growing evidence that inflammatory skin diseases, including atopic dermatitis (AD), are associated with an increased prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD). The current study aimed to explore whether AD and CKD comorbidity confers a greater mortality risk than either condition alone. A total of 10,462 adults from the 1999–2006 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: AD−/CKD− (reference) group (n = 7998), AD+/CKD− group (n = 946), AD−/CKD+ group (n = 1378), and AD+/CKD+ group (n = 140). All-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality were compared among the four groups using Cox proportional hazards models. Overall, 1715 (11.68%) participants died during a median follow-up period of 192 months, of which 601 were CVD deaths. All-cause mortality (32.35%; n = 51) and CVD mortality (16.91%; n = 27) were highest in the AD+/CKD+ group. The hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for the AD+/CKD− group, AD−/CKD+ group, and AD+/CKD+ group were 1.11 (0.91–1.36), 1.35 (1.12–1.63), and 2.87 (2.07–3.98), respectively, for all-cause mortality, and 1.06 (0.71–1.59), 1.59 (1.25–2.04), and 4.02 (2.46–6.58), respectively, for CVD mortality. Stratified analysis indicated that the association between AD and CKD status with all-cause and CVD mortality was more pronounced in participants aged under 65 years. Sensitivity analysis obtained similar result. Thus, the concomitant presence of AD and CKD was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, particularly in individuals under 65 years of age. Thus, appropriate screening for AD in CKD patients or CKD in AD patients may be of potential utility for risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42382-6.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD), also known as eczema, is a common chronic inflammatory skin disorder that reportedly affects approximately 16.5 million adults in the US, with 6.6 million meeting criteria for moderate-to-severe disease1. Although AD is well-documented to impair sleep quality and intimate relationships, imposing significant lifelong physical and psychological morbidity, emerging studies have also linked active or severe AD to an elevated risk of mortality. For instance, a nationwide Korean cohort study indicated that patients with moderate to severe AD had a 16.3% and 9.6% increased risk for myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality, respectively, compared to those without AD2. Similarly, US data indicate that adults hospitalized for AD face a 71% greater 10-year mortality risk than the general population3.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a major global public health concern among noncommunicable diseases. While traditional risk factors including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia, predominate4, growing epidemiological evidence suggests that AD may represent a novel, non-traditional risk factor for CKD occurrence. Notably, current research reveals a clinically important bidirectional relationship between AD and CKD. Population-based studies have indicated that AD patients exhibit significantly higher rates of renal comorbidities5, with each unit increase in AD prevalence associated with a 7% elevation in CKD risk. Conversely, AD represents one of the most frequent causes of pruritus in CKD populations6. The pathophysiological crosstalk between AD and CKD appears to involve multiple interconnected pathways, including sustained low-grade systemic inflammation, overlapping metabolic dysregulation, and common cardiovascular risk factors.

Extensive research has observed an elevated risk of all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in patients with CKD7. Given the high prevalence of AD and CKD in the US population, elucidating the joint impact of AD and CKD on mortality risk remains an important research gap. In this study, we leveraged data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to explore the individual and combined effects of AD and CKD on the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality. The findings of this study may enhance understanding of the interactions between AD and CKD on mortality and inform targeted screening protocols for high-risk populations.

Methods

Study design and data source

This observational study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guideline for reporting the results. The NHANES protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. All adult participants provided informed consent. The design and detailed methodology for NHANES have already been described previously8,9. In brief, this community-based study collects nationally representative data from approximately 5000 to 10,000 individuals every two years. Health-related information was obtained by interviews, physical measurements, and biological sample testing. For the current study, we selected 4 cycles of NHANES (1999–2006) based on the availability of AD data. Among the 20,311 participants aged 20 years and older initially included, we further applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) a history of cancer or being pregnant at the time of NHANES survey; (2) missing data of AD and/or CKD; 3) lost to follow-up; and 4) missing data of covariates. Ascertainment of AD and CKD

According to previous studies10, the presence of AD was determined based on the responses to the following two questions “During the past 12 months, have you had dermatitis, eczema, or any other type of red, inflamed skin rash?” and ”Has a doctor ever told you that you have eczema?”. CKD was diagnosed based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 and/or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio > 300 mg/g11. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated from serum creatinine level, age, sex and race according to the 2009 CKD-EPI formula12.

Mortality assessment

The mortality status and causes of the death of the included participants were sourced from the public access files of the National Death Index, with latest records updated through December 31, 2019. All-cause mortality included those who died, regardless of the underlying causes. CVD mortality was defined as those who died from cardiovascular or cerebrovascular conditions that can be identified with the International Classification of Disease codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51 and I60–I6913.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance or prior studies reporting an association with outcomes in patients with AD or CKD. We included the following covariates: (1) basic demographic and socioeconomic variables, including age (continuous in years), sex (men/women), race or ethnicity (non-Hispanic white/non-Hispanic black/Mexican Americans/other Hispanics/other), marital status (single/non-single), education level (less than high school/high school/higher than high school), and poverty-income ratio; (2) lifestyle factors, including body mass index, physical activity, smoking and drinking; (3) medical comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and CVD; (4) laboratory measurement of hemoglobin; and (5) medications (statin use). Hypertension was diagnosed if participants had a self-reported physician diagnosis, a measured blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg at the Mobile Examination Center, or were taking anti-hypertensive medications14. Similarly, diabetes was diagnosed if participants had a self-reported physician diagnosis, a fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a 2-h post-load glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, an HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or were taking hypoglycemic medications or insulin14. CVD was defined as a self-reported history of coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was diagnosed if participants had: (1) a self-reported physician diagnosis; (2) a pre-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity ratio < 0.70 on pulmonary function testing; or (3) were aged > 40 years with a history of smoking or chronic bronchitis and were using related medications (e.g., selective phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mast cell stabilizers, leukotriene modulators, or inhaled corticosteroids)15. Dyslipidemia was defined as meeting at least one of the following five criteria: (1) total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL; (2) triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL; (3) low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL; 4) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women16.

Statistical analysis

We categorized participants into four mutually exclusive groups based on AD and CKD status: 1) AD-/CKD- (reference) group, consisting of individuals without AD nor CKD; 2) AD+/CKD− group, denoting participants with AD but not CKD; 3) AD−/CKD+ , referring to those with CKD but not AD; and 4) AD+/CKD+ group, signifying those with both conditions. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES by incorporating sampling weights, as well as the clustering (SDMVPSU) and stratification (SDMVSTRA). Comparisons among the four groups were enabled by one-way analysis of variance or chi-squared test, as appropriate. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests for group comparisons. After verifying the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals, Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to examine associations between AD and CKD status with mortality outcomes by reporting the hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We constructed three Cox proportional hazards models: a crude (unadjusted) model, a partially adjusted model (Model 1) for demographic and socioeconomic factors, and a fully adjusted model (Model 2) incorporating all covariates detailed in “2.4 Covariates” Section. Subgroup analysis, stratified by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (men vs. women), smoking status (never vs. former vs. current), hypertension (yes vs. no), diabetes (yes vs. no), physical activity (no vs. moderate vs. vigorous), and body mass index (< 25 vs. 25–29.99 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m2) were performed. The unadjusted P values for interaction were derived from Wald tests on the coefficient of the interaction term between AD/CKD status and the subgroup variable in the Cox proportional hazards model. To examine the robustness of the findings, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses: 1) excluding participants who died within the first two years of follow-up to address potential reverse causation; 2) further adjusting for the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers (n = 139); and 3) performing a direct comparison between the AD−/CKD+ and AD+/CKD+ groups. As an exploratory analysis, causal mediation analysis was conducted to quantify the direct and indirect effects of AD/CKD status on mortality risk through the mediator C-reactive protein. The analysis employed a counterfactual framework, fitting a linear regression model for C-reactive protein and a Cox proportional hazards model for mortality. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 signified statistical significance.

Subgroup analysis

Stratified analysis (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3) indicated that the association between AD and CKD status with all-cause (P for interaction < 0.001) and CVD mortality was more pronounced in participants aged less than 65 years (P for interaction = 0.002). Sex, smoking, diabetes, physical activity, and body mass index did not significantly modify the relationship between AD and CKD status with mortality outcomes (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results. In analyses that excluded early deaths (Supplementary Table 4) and adjusted for angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker use (Supplementary Table 5), the AD+/CKD+ group remained at the highest risk. Furthermore, a direct comparison (Supplementary Table 6) revealed that CKD patients with concomitant AD had more than double the risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.52–2.69) and nearly 2.5 times the risk of CVD mortality (HR = 2.46, 95% CI 1.37–4.42) compared to those with CKD alone.

Mediation analysis

The exploratory mediation analysis revealed that a small proportion of the total association between AD/CKD status and mortality was mediated by C-reactive protein: 3.75% for all-cause mortality and 3.13% for CVD mortality (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that combined presence of comorbid AD and CKD increase mortality risk in US community-dwelling adults, with HRs substantially higher than those associated with either condition alone. Furthermore, the associations between AD and CKD status with mortality risk were more pronounced in individuals under 65 years. Our sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of these findings. Collectively, these results highlight the importance of routine dermatological evaluation in CKD patients and alternatively, regular renal function monitoring in AD patients. Future research should investigate whether targeted management of comorbid AD and CKD would improve long-term outcomes.

In this US nationwide survey, the prevalence of AD and CKD was 11.95% and 14.31%, respectively, which is broadly compatible with previous estimates from the NHANES population17,18. In addition, AD patients were more likely to have a higher educational attainment and more physical activity. These findings corroborate existing evidence that higher socioeconomic status correlates with increased AD prevalence but reduced disease severity19. This paradoxical association may reflect healthcare access disparities, as individuals with a lower socioeconomic status potentially experience underdiagnosis due to limited medical access. Furthermore, AD patients with a lower socioeconomic status also showed poorer medication adherence and higher treatment discontinuation rates20, potentially accounting for their more severe clinical presentations. Interestingly, contrary to previous reports of elevated cardiometabolic risk in AD patients21, our study found comparable prevalence of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia among those with and without AD. This observed discrepancy may be attributable to differences in population characteristics, as our community-based cohort likely represents a broader spectrum of disease severity compared to previous studies conducted in hospitalized patient populations, who typically present with more advanced disease22.

Our analysis revealed no significant increase in all-cause and CVD mortality risk among AD+/CKD− patients as compared to the reference group. This contrasts with earlier findings demonstrating significantly elevated risks of coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction in AD patients from both NHANES and National Health Interview Survey populations23. Subsequent large-scale studies have clarified that the association between AD and CVD mortality is largely dependent upon AD severity, with severe AD patients showing 20% and 70% increased risks of stroke and heart failure, respectively24. Notably, a Danish nationwide healthcare registry of 8686 AD and 86,860 matched controls reported a 27% (95% CI 1.11–1.45) and 45% (95% CI 1.07–1.96) increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, respectively, in AD patients25. Similarly, hospitalized AD patients demonstrated a 71% (95% CI 1.20–2.44) higher 10-year mortality risk versus the general population3. However, a recent UK population-based cohort study found minimal elevation of overall mortality risk (HR = 1.04, 95%CI 1.03–1.06) that escalated substantially in severe AD cases (HR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.54–1.71)26. Collectively, these findings emphasize that elevated mortality risk appears predominantly confined to severe AD cases.

Another important observation of this study is the combined effect of AD and CKD on mortality risk. Emerging studies have indicated inflammatory skin disorders, such as psoriasis, AD, and hidradenitis suppurativa, may predispose to the development of renal impairment27. Furthermore, Mendelian randomization analyses have demonstrated a bidirectional relationship between AD and CKD, with each unit increase in AD prevalence associated with 7% higher CKD odds, and each unit increase in CKD prevalence corresponded to 14% greater AD odds28. In addition, there are also studies showing that AD increase the risk of IgA nephropathy, a prototype of CKD, by 5.4%29. Although the precise mechanisms linking AD and CKD remains largely unknown, factors like chronic systemic inflammation and immune-mediated injury may all be implicated. Specifically, AD is immunologically characterized by persistent Th2-dominant inflammation with elevated levels of interleukin-4, interleukin-13, and interleukin-3130, all of which may incur renal endothelial dysfunction, tubulointerstitial inflammation or even progressive renal interstitial fibrosis31. The IgE auto-antibodies in AD may cause renal injury, such as IgA nephropathy, by cross-reacting with renal antigens through molecular mimicry32. For instance, AD-like model mice demonstrated remarkable increase in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level and histologically inflammatory cell infiltration and podocyte foot process effacement33.

Of note, stratified analysis revealed a more pronounced mortality risk between AD and CKD in participants under 65 years. This age-specific effect may be partially explained by competing mortality risks in older populations, where other age-related comorbidities (e.g., CVD, malignancies) may overshadow AD/CKD- specific mortality. Emerging evidence suggests elderly-onset AD represents a distinct clinical subtype frequently complicated by multiple chronic conditions and higher malignancy rates34. Consequently, older AD patients may experience mortality primarily from these competing causes, potentially attenuating the observed joint effect in this age group.

Although our preliminary analysis identified C-reactive protein-mediated inflammation as a pathway linking AD/CKD status to mortality, its effect was modest at best. This suggests a predominant role for alternative mechanisms, such as dysregulation of other cytokines, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction35,36. Further investigation using in vivo models is warranted to elucidate these underlying pathways.

Our study have several practical implications for clinical research and future investigations. From a clinical perspective, these results suggest the need for systematic renal function monitoring in AD patients, particularly for those with severe or active AD. Conversely, dermatological evaluation should be considered for CKD patients presenting with unexplained pruritus or skin lesions. Future CKD management guidelines may need to consider AD as a potential risk-enhancing comorbidity that warrants closer surveillance. From a research standpoint, mechanistic studies are required to elucidate the pathophysiological basis of the combine effect of AD and CKD on mortality risk. Additionally, well-designed clinical trials should investigate whether optimized management of either condition can decrease mortality risk.

Our study benefits from national representativeness, extended follow-up duration, coupled with sensitivity analysis that enhanced study robustness. This study is also unique in its endeavor to examine the joint impact of both conditions on mortality. Nonetheless, this study also suffers from the following limitations. First, an important contextual limitation is that the study period (1999–2006) occurred before the widespread use of modern biologic therapies for AD, which may limit the applicability of our results to contemporary clinical practice. We recommend that future studies seek to replicate these analyses in newer datasets to determine the persistence of these associations over time. Second, the diagnosis of AD was relied on self-reported data collected through standardized questionnaire, which may inevitably introduce recall bias and potential misclassification. Third, the lack of detailed information on disease severity and corresponding treatment regimens precluded examination of more nuanced relationship between AD, CKD and mortality. Given that severe AD confers the greatest CVD risk and that treatment could modify this risk37,38, our analysis likely provides a conservative assessment of the mortality risk attributable to AD in isolation. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of the data prevents definitive conclusions about the temporal relationship between AD and CKD onset. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporality and further investigate their joint effects on mortality risk. Finally, the wide 95%CI observed in some subgroups, particularly the AD+/CKD+ group, suggest limited sample size and the potential for residual confounding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this US population-based study demonstrated that AD alone was not significantly associated with increased mortality risk. However, we observed a joint effect between AD and CKD, with comorbid AD and CKD conferring substantially higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks compared to either condition alone. These findings highlight the need for future research to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this combined effect and evaluate the potential benefits of targeted screening strategies in high-risk populations.
