Neural effects of expectation violation generalise across sensory modalities
Authors: Dragan Rangelov, Edwina Shi, Jason B Mattingley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brain receives more sensory information than it can usefully employ to control behaviour. This sensory overload can be reduced by exploiting regularities in the environment to predict future events. Previous work on the role of prediction in perception has mostly focused on events within a single sensory modality. Here, we asked whether expectation violations in one sensory modality (audition) can affect neural representations of stimuli in a separate modality (vision). Human observers viewed rapid sequences of randomly-oriented visual gratings together with a task-irrelevant stream of alternating high- and low-pitched tones. Using electroencephalography (EEG) to measure functional brain activity, we verified a robust neural mismatch response to unexpected sounds, consistent with previous studies. Critically, using machine learning to extract neural orientation tuning to gratings, we observed stronger tuning following unexpected sounds, suggesting that unimodal mismatches trigger cross-modal updating. The findings suggest that predictive coding theories of perception should incorporate cross-modal influences on internal models of the sensory environment.Subject terms: Cognitive control, Human behaviour

Introduction

The sensory environment is overwhelmingly abundant, posing significant processing challenges for the brain1. At a busy farmer’s market, for example, the sounds and movements from different stalls all compete for processing resources. This abundance is often compensated by spatiotemporal regularities—the produce stalls, for example, occupy the same location every week—engendering expectations about the sensory input. Research has shown that the brain differentiates between expected and unexpected stimuli2, demonstrating an ability to track and exploit regularities to mitigate sensory overload. Violated expectations seem to be particularly salient, as exemplified by the classic mismatch or “oddball” response elicited in brain activity measured with electroencephalography (EEG) during sequences of auditory3 and visual stimuli4. These effects have been observed repeatedly in humans and other species5,6, and thus appear to be a ubiquitous aspect of neural functioning. The question of whether regularities in one sensory modality (e.g., audition) affect processing in another (e.g., vision) has received limited attention. This is surprising because such “cross-modal” interactions are a feature of many aspects of perception7, perhaps most notably in audio–visual speech integration (e.g., the ventriloquist effect8 and the McGurk effect9), but also in such perceptual phenomena as the stream-bounce effect10. To address this knowledge gap, here we investigated cross-modal effects of expectation violation by characterising feature-specific neural responses to streams of randomly oriented visual gratings presented concurrently with a sequence of auditory tones in which expectations were established and occasionally violated.

Expectation effects have been widely studied using sequences of two or more alternating stimuli, e.g., high- and low-pitched sounds, known as the “roving oddball” paradigm3,4. To engender expectation, one stimulus is presented repeatedly (i.e., the standard). At random intervals, the standard is swapped with an unexpected, oddball stimulus, which violates established expectations. The oddball repeats henceforth, thereby becoming the new “standard” and controlling for stimulus-specific confounds. EEG studies that have contrasted responses to standards and oddballs have typically reported three phasic effects of expectation violation observed over spatially-restricted scalp areas11,12. The mismatch negativity (or MMN) appears around 150 ms post-stimulus and is thought to reflect automatic detection of unexpected stimuli regardless of their task-relevance. At around 300 ms, the novelty-P3 (or P3a) is sometimes observed for unexpected, task-irrelevant stimuli, reflecting orienting toward salient events. Finally, the re-orienting negativity (RON) arises around 500 ms in response to expectation violation.

More recent work has characterised patterns of brain activity across the whole scalp using multivariate analyses and computational modelling of brain activity13,14. We recently applied such modelling approaches to estimate the strength of orientation-selective neural responses to visual gratings15, and found stronger tuning to unexpected stimuli, an effect which arose relatively early (100–200 ms post-stimulus), overlapping in time with the classical MMN component. Of note, the expectation effect was dissociable from effects of attention both by experimental design and with regard to tuning time-course, demonstrating distinct roles of expectation and attention in mediating sensory processing. In a subsequent study16, we employed two-photon calcium imaging to record single neurons in mouse primary visual cortex, and again found stronger orientation tuning for grating stimuli that violated expectations, suggesting that the mismatch effects are evident at the earliest stages of cortical sensory processing.

Here, we investigated cross-modal expectation violation by characterising feature-specific orientation tuning in evoked neural responses to visual gratings presented rapidly and centrally, at fixation. The sequence of orientations was random and therefore carried no predictive information within the visual modality. This visual stream was accompanied by a roving-oddball auditory stream comprising a series of high- or low-pitch sounds. Critically, the auditory stream was always task-irrelevant, and participants were told they could ignore the sound stimuli. The key question was whether tuning of neural responses to randomly-oriented visual gratings varied as a function of whether they appeared concurrently with an irrelevant standard or an oddball sound. We found a robust unimodal oddball effect for the auditory stimuli, consistent with previous work17. Critically, we also found a cross-modal effect of expectation on the fidelity of visual feature-tuning, which arose substantially later than the unimodal auditory effect.

Results

Randomly oriented gratings were shown for 50 ms at the monitor centre every 150 ms separated by a white-noise mask (Fig. 1A). Occasionally, a target grating with a higher spatial frequency appeared. The number of targets varied from 0 to 9 randomly across trials and participants (N = 28) had to monitor the visual stream and report how many targets were presented at the end of the trial. Together with the gratings, auditory pure tones (3.7 and 4.7 kHz) were played for 100 ms every 600 ms (Fig. 1B). The tones followed a roving oddball paradigm3, such that a sequence of tones of one frequency was repeatedly played, followed by a sequence of tones at the other frequency. The first tone in the sequence was an oddball, which later became a standard. There were 15 oddball sounds on average per trial and participant. Each trial lasted for 60 s, after which a response display prompted participants to enter their response by using the 0–9 keyboard keys. Within each block, one randomly selected trial contained only visual stimuli (i.e., no tones), which served as a no-sound baseline for subsequent analyses.

The EEG data were pre-processed offline using the FASTER18 automated pipeline (see “Methods”). The continuous data were segmented into 800 ms epochs, time-locked to the onset of every tone in the audio–visual trials (−200 to 600 ms), baseline-corrected using a −50 to 50 ms window around stimulus onset, and linearly detrended. For visual-only trials, the continuous data were similarly segmented, starting from the onset of the first grating per trial, into 800 ms epochs during which five gratings were presented. Bad epochs and eye movement artefacts were identified and removed from further analyses. The remaining epochs were spatially filtered using a surface-Laplacian to help distinguish between evoked responses to auditory and visual responses, and these were averaged per participant, yielding event-related potentials (ERPs, Fig. 2A).

Inspection of the ERPs across the whole scalp revealed strong visual responses time-locked to the grating onsets, which were most prominent over posterior scalp areas (Fig. 2A). To identify potential effects of auditory expectations on visual evoked responses, a region-of-interest analysis focusing on posterior electrodes contrasted ERPs following oddball and standard sounds (Fig. 2B). Auditory expectation violation yielded a stronger posterior negativity about 320 ms post-stimulus and a stronger positivity about 545 ms, coinciding with the third (300–450 ms) and fourth gratings (450–600 ms). Overall, these analyses revealed that violation of auditory expectation impacted posterior sites over the visual cortex with a significant 300 ms delay. Interestingly, the effects arose both early (20 ms) and relatively late (100 ms) after grating onsets.

Next, we investigated the effects of auditory expectation on auditory ERPs. To remove the visual evoked responses, the ERP waves from the visual-only trials were subtracted from the audio–visual epochs. The subtraction revealed auditory responses which were most prominent over central-medial scalp areas (Fig. 2C). A region-of-interest analysis of auditory evoked potentials was conducted over a subset of four bilateral central electrodes to contrast responses to standard sounds and oddballs (Fig. 2D). The region-of-interest analyses revealed a robust auditory oddball effect across three distinct periods. The first, MMN period (100–200 ms17,19) was characterised by a stronger negativity for oddballs than standards, with a peak at around 170 ms. The second, P3a period (200–300 ms11,12) was characterised by a stronger positivity for oddballs than standards with a peak at around 250 ms. The third, RON period (400–500 ms11,12) was, again, characterised by a stronger negativity for oddball than standard sounds, with a peak at around 420 ms.

Overall, region-of-interest analyses of the univariate evoked responses revealed robust effects of expectation violation over posterior and central-medial scalp sites. Next, we estimated feature-specific neural representations of sound pitch and grating orientation using multivariate analyses. First, we used multivariate linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to characterise pitch-specific neural responses (see Methods). Briefly, using a cross-validation algorithm, the auditory epochs were divided into a training and a testing set. The training set comprised the same number of epochs following oddball and standard sounds, and was used to train the LDA model. The trained model was then applied to the testing set to extract pitch-specific neural time series. Of note, rather than extracting the predicted class membership (i.e., high vs low pitch), the distance from a multidimensional decision boundary was extracted. This provided a continuous measure of how pitch-specific the recorded neural responses were, with higher scores indicating greater pitch-specificity. Figure 3A shows the time-course of the extracted pitch-specificity signals. It reveals feature-specific neural responses from 120 to 218 ms on average across conditions. Contrasting pitch-specificity between standard and oddball sounds revealed significant differences both early (78–100 ms) and late (168–266 ms), consistent with an earlier onset and longer sustained pitch-specific neural responses following oddball sounds than standard sounds. These findings conceptually replicate16,20 stronger feature-specific neural coding of unexpected visual stimuli than expected stimuli, but in the auditory modality.

The second stage of multivariate feature-specific analyses focused on characterising orientation-specific neural responses to every grating over each 150 ms period (i.e., the test epoch), time-locked to the grating onset. The remaining training epochs were binned into 16 overlapping bins relative to the angular difference between the test orientation and the orientations presented in the training epochs (Fig. 3B, left). Next, visual evoked potentials were computed by averaging epochs per bin. To minimise the impact of auditory responses, these analyses used a selection of 12 posterior electrodes which showed the strongest neural responses to visual stimuli (Fig. 3B, middle). Finally, a time-resolved, multivariate Mahalanobis distance21,22 was computed between the EEG in the test trial and the average EEG per training bin. This yielded a time-resolved profile of neural similarity between the test and training epochs (Fig. 3B, right).

If the neural response in the test epoch is orientation-specific, then the similarity across training bins should resemble a bell-shaped curve with a high similarity for bins with a small difference (e.g., 0) and low similarity for bins with a large difference (e.g., ±π). The similarity matrices (16 bins × N times) were estimated separately for audio–visual and visual-only trials, separately for gratings following standard and oddball sounds within audio–visual trials. Analogous similarity matrices were calculated for the different gratings (1st–4th) presented over the wider, 600 ms interval between each successive pair of sounds. Inspection of the estimated similarity matrices (Fig. 3C) revealed a strong, statistically significant orientation-specific neural response in all analysed conditions. Consistent with a gradual build-up of feature-specific tuning, these responses emerged ~100 ms after grating onset and continued for the remaining 50 ms period. To compare the strength of feature-specific responses across experimental conditions (visual-only, oddball sounds and standard sounds), we summed the estimated similarity per bin over the 50 ms window of significant orientation responses.

All similarity profiles followed a characteristic bell shape with a peak over the bins containing the same orientations as the orientation in the test trial (i.e., 0). This profile closely resembles neural impulse-response functions23–25. To characterise differences in visual tuning across different experimental conditions and gratings presented at different times, a non-parametric index of the profile width (see “Materials and methods”) was estimated separately for audio–visual and visual-only trials, and for all four gratings in the sequence (Fig. 4A, right). A uniform tuning profile (i.e., no tuning) would yield a value of 0, whereas higher values reflect a narrower tuning profile and, consequently, stronger tuning. A repeated-measures ANOVA of tuning strength with main terms for trial type (audio–visual vs visual-only) and stimulus (1st–4th grating) showed that neither the main effects nor their interactions were statistically significant (all Fs < 1.81, all ps > 0.152), indicating that orientation tuning was comparably strong with and without an accompanying sound (Fig. 4A, right panel).

The final EEG analysis contrasted tuning strength to gratings presented after an oddball sound and after a standard sound (Fig. 4B, right). A repeated-measures ANOVA of tuning strength, with main terms for sound type (oddball vs standard) and stimulus (1st–4th grating), revealed a statistically significant interaction between the sound type and the stimulus (F(3, 81) = 2.92, ηp2 = 0.10, p = 0.039). Planned contrasts between tuning strengths for gratings following oddball and standard sounds using a two-tailed paired-samples t-test showed that only the contrast for the fourth grating was statistically significant (t(27) = 2.58, p = 0.016, all other ts < 1, all other ps > 0.350). Relative to oddballs, the orientation-tuning strength to gratings that followed standard sounds was about 50% weaker. Taken together, comparisons of tuning strengths for gratings following oddball and standard sounds revealed that expectation violation in one modality (i.e., audition) improves sensory processing—specifically, orientation tuning—in another modality (i.e., vision), but in a delayed fashion, approximately 500 ms after the onset of the unexpected, task-irrelevant sound.

As a final check of behaviour, we inspected distributions of signed error magnitudes (i.e., the difference between the reported and the correct number of targets, Fig. 4C, left) in the audio–visual and visual-only trials. Participants overestimated in trials with a small number of targets (0–4) and underestimated in trials with a larger number of targets (5–9), implying a regression-to-the-mean effect. Critically, however, no differences were observed between audio–visual trials and visual-only trials. These observations were supported by a mixed-effects general linear model (GLM) of signed error magnitudes across individual trials, with trial type (audio–visual vs visual-only) and number of targets (0–9) as fixed effects and participant as a random effect. The GLM analyses showed a statistically significant main effect of the number of targets (βM/SEM = −0.51/0.01, z = −35.11, p < 0.001), but neither the main effect of trial type nor its interaction with the number of targets was statistically significant (all p ≥ 0.297).

To characterise response precision across different trial types, the distributions of absolute error magnitudes across trials were inspected. Precision decreased as the number of targets increased (Fig. 4C, middle). The trial type, again, had no influence on behaviour. These observations were confirmed by a mixed-effects GLM of absolute error magnitudes with trial type and number of targets as fixed effects and participant as a random effect. While there was a significant main effect of the number of targets (βM/SEM = 0.28/0.01, z = 21.50, p < 0.001), neither the main effect of trial type nor the two-way interaction was significant (all p ≥ 0.455).

Finally, to determine whether oddball sounds interfered with performance on the visual task, the distributions of absolute error magnitudes in audio–visual trials were inspected as a function of the different number of oddballs per trial. There was a high degree of overlap between the different conditions (Fig. 4C, right). A mixed-effects GLM over absolute error magnitudes, with the number of oddballs per trial as the fixed effect and participant as a random effect, revealed no statistically significant main effect of the number of oddballs on response precision (p = 0.268).

Discussion

According to influential predictive coding theories2,29,30, the brain has a remarkable ability to track and exploit statistical regularities in the environment, as evidenced by differential neural responses to expected and unexpected stimuli observed unimodally for both auditory and visual stimuli3,4. Here, we investigated whether expectation violation within the auditory modality modulates neural representations of concurrent visual stimuli. We confirmed that unexpected but task-irrelevant sounds in our paradigm yielded typical neural signatures of expectation violation. More importantly, our findings also revealed that unexpected sounds increase the fidelity of neural representations of visual stimuli—specifically orientation information—demonstrating a cross-modal generalisation of the unimodal effects of expectation violation. Importantly, these cross-modal visual effects arose much later (~500 ms) than the unimodal auditory effects, implying that distinct neural mechanisms are responsible for unimodal and cross-modal expectancy effects.

There are many examples of interactions between incidentally paired visual and auditory stimuli in the literature. The McGurk effect9, for example, reflects illusory speech perception (e.g., phoneme /d/) when listening to the spoken /b/ while watching mouth movements for /g/. Audition and vision interact in non-linguistic contexts, too. The stream-bounce effect10 reflects a difference in how two circles moving along intersecting trajectories are perceived at the moment when their paths cross: Without a sound, the circles appear to continue moving through one another, whereas they appear to bounce off one another if a sound is played at the critical moment. Finally, incidental pairing of at-threshold task-relevant visual stimuli with supra-threshold task-irrelevant sounds has been found31 to improve visual sensitivity, demonstrating the functional significance of audio–visual interactions. Our findings are broadly consistent with this literature on obligatory audio–visual interactions by showing cross-modal effects of expectation violation from an otherwise irrelevant source of (auditory) input. Further, our findings are consistent with recent studies that have reported unimodal effects of expectation violation on visual orientation tuning in human observers26, and analogous visual tuning in individual neurons in mouse primary visual cortex16. In those studies, a visual roving oddball paradigm was used to engender expectation in which several gratings were shown in a rotating sequence (e.g., 0°, 15° and 30°), creating an expectation about the next likely visual stimulus (i.e., 45°). The rotating sequence was interrupted at random intervals by presenting an out-of-sequence grating with a random orientation (e.g., −45°). Comparing responses of neurons in the primary visual cortex to expected and unexpected gratings revealed significantly stronger tuning for unexpected stimuli both in awake and anaesthetised animals.

Together, recent unimodal findings and the present cross-modal effects are consistent with the influential predictive coding framework2,29, which postulates that sensory representations are subject to inhibitory feedback from associative brain regions. These associative areas construct models which capture spatiotemporal regularities in the sensory environment and generate predictions about future sensory events. Sensory inputs that match predictions are suppressed, and only the differences between predicted and actual inputs (i.e., prediction errors) are passed on, signalling the need for model updating. Because unexpected stimuli are not subject to inhibition, within-modality sharpening of orientation tuning for unexpected stimuli is readily explained by predictive coding16. A key distinction between the present investigation and previous work, however, is that the sequence of visual orientations was always random in the current study, and thus could not be subject to any suppressive influence from predictive models of visual input. How, then, might our finding of stronger visual orientation tuning following unexpected sounds be explained?

The suppressive signals that generate auditory expectation–violation effects within a modality could propagate between modalities via existing anatomical and functional connections. The literature shows that early auditory and visual areas of the mammalian brain are connected both directly and via the thalamus31–33. Due to tight spatial audio–visual coupling, the relevant functional connections were likely strengthened in our task, giving rise to the cross-modal effects of auditory expectation violation. We found that auditory expectation violation modulated visual processing measured as univariate evoked responses at occipital sites and orientation population tuning. Cross-modal expectation-violation effects, however, did not emerge until around 500 ms, potentially reflecting involvement of a wider network that mediates information transfer between auditory and visual domains. A similar time course of audio–visual interaction effects was reported by Hessler et al.34, who used a variant of the auditory oddball paradigm to investigate the impact of expectation violation on evoked responses over occipital sites while participants fixated a blank visual display. The authors found that auditory oddballs triggered a P3 component which peaked around 500 ms post-stimulus, whereas the P3 was absent following standard sounds. Together with our results, these findings suggest that delays in signal transmission from modality-specific centres, such as the primary visual and auditory cortices, may determine the temporal dynamics of cross-modal effects of expectation violation.

It is also possible that auditory oddballs served as an alerting cue that captured attention and increased arousal. Consistent with this notion, we observed robust neural correlates of expectation violations (P3a and RON components), which have been interpreted as reflecting attentional capture11,12. Empirical support for the notion that oddballs may increase attentional capture comes from a study by Zhao et al.35 who used eye tracking in an auditory oddball task as participants fixated blank displays. Following auditory oddballs, the rate of microsaccades—an index of visual attention—decreased transiently 150–400 ms post-stimulus. Similarly, the pupil dilation rate—an index of arousal—increased transiently from 200 ms post-stimulus. Previous work has shown that attention strongly modulates feature-specific processing, primarily by sharpening feature-tuning curves27,28. Here, analyses of pitch-specific neural responses revealed robust differences between standard and oddball sounds around 200 ms after the sound, consistent with the idea that auditory expectation violation engendered a transient arousal increase shortly after the sound onset. If the cross-modal effects on orientation tuning observed in the current study also arose due to alerting effects from the auditory oddballs, we might have expected them to have arisen around 200 ms after the oddball sound. In fact, they did not emerge until around 500 ms, suggesting that the cross-modal effects of expectation violation were not a mere consequence of alerting. Moreover, if auditory oddballs had an alerting effect, they should have led to better visual target detection in the audio–visual trials than in the visual-only trials, but there was no such effect. Further work will be needed to characterise the potential contribution of attention and arousal to the cross-modal expectancy effects reported here.

In summary, we have shown that modality-specific predictive models affect neural processes beyond their unisensory domain by modulating perceptual representations of stimuli that are distinct from the sensory inputs from which the predictive models are generated. This cross-modal modulation is likely a consequence of spatiotemporal coupling between the two modalities. Our findings have important implications for general models of perception and cognition, such as predictive coding theory2,29. According to this account, a mismatch between expected and actual sensory input—a prediction error—serves as a signal to update an existing model of the sensory environment. Our findings suggest that model updating may not be restricted to the primary sensory domain, but that prediction errors may trigger a generalised multimodal refresh of the perceptual system.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-eight healthy adult human volunteers (23 female, Mage/SDage = 22/5 years) took part in the experiment for monetary compensation at a rate of 20 AUD/h. The sample size was selected on the basis of prior EEG studies21,22 that have reported robust feature-specific neural responses. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing by self-report. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of The University of Queensland (Approval 2016001247) and was conducted in accordance with the Human Subjects Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to experimental testing.

Stimuli and procedure

Visual stimuli comprised oriented sinusoidal gratings (50% luminance contrast) shown on a black background within an annulus (28° of visual angle, i.e., dva outer diameter, 5 dva inner diameter) at the monitor centre (Fig. 1A). Throughout a trial, a stream of gratings was presented for 50 ms every 150 ms (i.e., 6.7 Hz). The grating orientations were randomly sampled from a set of 36 orientations, ranging from 0°–180° in 5° steps; their phase varied randomly between 0 and 0.5. A white-noise mask was presented for 100 ms between every two gratings. Most gratings had a spatial frequency of 0.75 cycles/dva. Occasionally, a target grating with a higher spatial frequency of 1.5 cycles/dva appeared. Participants had to monitor the visual stream and report how many targets were presented at the end of the trial. The number of targets varied randomly from 0 to 9 (5 targets on average per trial and participant).

Concurrently with the visual stimuli, auditory pure tones (3.7 and 4.7 kHz) were played every 600 ms (i.e., 0.6 Hz) for 100 ms (Fig. 1B). The presentation of tones followed a standard roving paradigm3, such that a sequence of tones of one frequency was repeatedly played, followed by a sequence of tones at the other frequency. The number of repetitions per sequence was randomly sampled from a Poisson distribution with a mean of 1.5 and a minimum of four repetitions. The first tone in the sequence was an oddball, which later became a standard. There were 15 oddball sounds on average per trial and participant. The stimuli were presented for 60 s in each trial, after which a response display appeared comprising a blinking vertical line. Participants had maximally 6 s to enter their response by using the 0–9 keyboard keys. The entered number was coloured green if correct, and red if incorrect. The number remained on the screen for 250 ms, after which the next trial started. Prior to commencing the main session, participants completed a practice session comprising six blocks of two trials each (i.e., 12 practice trials). The main experimental session comprised 12 blocks of six trials (i.e., 72 × 60 s trials). Within each block, one randomly selected trial contained only visual stimuli (i.e., no tones), which served as a no-sound baseline for subsequent analyses.

Apparatus

The visual stimuli were presented on a 24-in. LED monitor (screen resolution 1920 × 1080 pixels, refresh rate 100 Hz). The auditory stimuli were presented via a flat, circular speaker (33 mm diameter) mounted on the monitor centre. Participants were seated approximately 57 cm in front of the monitor, and the head-to-monitor distance was fixed using a chin rest. The stimuli were generated using PsychoPy36,37 on a Dell Precision T1700 computer with a Quadro K4000 graphics card, running Microsoft Windows 7 Enterprise, 64 bit. Brain activity was recorded using a BioSemi ActiveTwo EEG system comprising 64 Ag/AgCl scalp electrodes positioned in the 10–20 layout, sampled at 1024 Hz.

Behavioural analyses

The main focus was on error magnitudes (i.e., the difference between the presented and the reported number of targets) as a function of the trial type (visual-only vs audio–visual) and the number of oddballs in audio–visual trials. The data were analysed using mixed-effects general linear modelling with participant as a random factor, trial type as a categorical predictor and the number of presented target gratings and oddball tones as continuous predictors.

Electroencephalography analyses

The EEG data were pre-processed offline using the FASTER18 automated pipeline. Briefly, the continuous EEG data were re-referenced to the average electrode, low-pass filtered at 99 Hz using a 4th-order Butterworth filter, and notch-filtered at 50 Hz to remove line noise. Bad channels (three on average across participants) were identified and replaced using interpolated data from the neighbouring channels, after which all channels were re-referenced again to the average electrode. The continuous data were segmented into 800 ms epochs, time-locked to the onset of every tone in the audio–visual trials (−200 to 600 ms), baseline-corrected using a −50 to 50 ms window around stimulus onset and linearly detrended. The experimental protocol involved a continuous and rapid succession of visual and auditory stimuli. To minimise effects of evoked responses from the previous epoch, the period for baseline correction was centred on the stimulus onset rather than using a more conventional, pre-stimulus period (e.g., −100 to 0 ms). For visual-only trials, the continuous data were similarly segmented, starting from the onset of the first grating per trial, into 800 ms epochs (−200 to 600 ms) during which four gratings were presented. Bad epochs (330 out of 2592 epochs on average across participants) were identified and removed from further analyses. Eye movement artefacts were identified using independent component analysis and regressed out. The remaining epochs were high-pass spatially filtered using a surface-Laplacian to help distinguish between evoked responses to auditory and visual stimuli. We used spherical spline surface-Laplacian with parameters m = 4 (spline stiffness), n = 10 (order of Legendre polynomial) and λ = 10−5 (regularisation). These values align with the defaults in MNE-Python’s surface-Laplacian method, apart from the Legendre polynomial (n), which was lower than the default and resulted in a lower degree of high-pass spatial filtering. The epochs were averaged per participant, yielding event-related potentials (ERPs, Fig. 2A).

To characterise effects of auditory expectation violation on univariate ERPs over scalp areas that were most sensitive to onsets of visual stimuli, a region-of-interest analysis was conducted by averaging ERPs over occipital electrodes (O1, Oz, O2 and Iz, Fig. 2B). The region-of-interest ERPs following expected, standard sounds and unexpected, oddball sounds were compared. As the strength of expectation should co-vary with how frequently the standard is repeated, we analysed the fourth repetition of the standard to keep the expectation strength constant.

To characterise auditory responses in audio–visual epochs independently of visual responses, the ERPs in visual-only epochs were subtracted from the ERPs in audio–visual epochs (Fig. 2C). Auditory ERPs to oddball tones and the fourth tone in the sequence of standard tones were contrasted in a subset of bilateral frontal and central electrodes (FC3, FC4, C3 and C4, Fig. 2C). These electrodes were selected on the basis of existing literature that investigated effects of auditory expectation violation17,38 and following visual inspection of the topographies observed in the present study (Fig. 2D).

To estimate feature-specific neural responses to sounds and gratings, two different multivariate analyses were conducted. Auditory, pitch-specific responses were estimated with multivariate linear discriminant analysis using all 64 electrodes as predictors and two pitches (high vs low) as criteria. The analyses used a tenfold stratified cross-validation algorithm. All epochs were first split into ten equal sets, which were used for testing. The remaining epochs were used as training sets to train the LDA model. The training sets always comprised an equal number of standard and oddball sound epochs. Therefore, the trained LDA model reflected only differences in neural responses to different pitches rather than auditory expectation. The LDA model was trained on the training set using a regularised covariance matrix39, and then applied to the testing set to estimate pitch-specific neural responses. The LDA yields a continuous measure whose sign reflects the predicted class membership (i.e., positive = high pitch, negative = low pitch) and whose magnitude reflects the distance from the multidimensional decision bound. We used the magnitude as an index of pitch-specificity of neural responses to different sounds. To be able to average across different pitches, the LDA output for low-pitch epochs was multiplied by −1. To analyse whether and how violation of auditory expectations influenced feature-specific neural encoding of sounds, the estimated pitch-specificity signals were averaged across epochs and contrasted between sound types (oddball vs standard, Fig. 3A).

Visual, orientation-specific responses were characterised using multivariate non-parametric estimations of feature tuning21,22. The tuning was estimated separately for visual-only and audio–visual epochs. To characterise the effects of expectation violation, the orientation tuning following the fourth standard sound was contrasted with the tuning following oddball sounds. The long, sound-locked epochs were split into four shorter epochs (−50 ms to 150 ms), time-locked to the onset of visual stimuli, and tuning analyses were conducted separately for each visual stimulus. To better characterise visual responses independently of auditory responses, the analyses included only a subset of 12 posterior electrodes (Fig. 3B) with the strongest visual responses. Briefly, for each shorter test epoch (visual-only, standard tone and oddball tone), and position in sequence (first, second, third and fourth grating after each tone), the remaining, training epochs were binned into 16 equidistant, overlapping bins relative to the angular distance (θ) between the grating orientations presented in the test and training epochs (−180° to 180° in 22.5° steps, 45° width). The epochs per training bin were then averaged, yielding a bin-specific ERP. Using a regularised covariance matrix39, a multivariate Mahalanobis distance (dtθ) between test trial and ERP per bin was computed for each time sample (t). The vector of estimated distances across bins per time sample was mean-centred and multiplied by −1 which transformed distances into similarity estimates (stθ=−(dtθ−∑i16dti16)). If orientation-specific information is contained in the EEG data, then the similarity should be higher for bins closer to the orientation shown in the test trial than for bins further away. The overall orientation tuning strength was quantified as a dot product between the similarity estimates per bin and the cosine-transformed bin-centres (τt=S⃗1×16⋅cos(Θ⃗16×1)). This non-parametric index yields zero if the similarities across bins are uniformly distributed (i.e., no tuning) and increases monotonically the more closely the profile of similarities approximates a bell-shaped curve (i.e., strong tuning). To improve signal-to-noise ratio, tuning strength was computed over a 50 ms time window at the end of the short epochs, during which the similarity estimates were statistically significantly different from zero at p < 0.05 (FDR-corrected, Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) using one-sample, two-tailed t-tests.

Statistics and reproducibility

The sample comprised 28 participants who completed 72 trials, each lasting 60 s with 60 audio–visual trials and 12 visual-only trials. On average across participants, 1044 epochs contributed to the EEG analyses of visual-only neural responses and 914 epochs each to audio–visual analyses of responses to oddball and standard sounds, respectively. Inferential analyses were conducted using one-sample t-test against zero, repeated-measures ANOVAs and mixed-effects GLMs as appropriate. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05. False-positives were controlled using the false-discovery rate algorithm40.
