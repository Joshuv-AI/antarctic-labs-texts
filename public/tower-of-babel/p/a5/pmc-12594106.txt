Hypertension and vascular health in the young
Authors: Shobha S Natarajan, Kevin E Meyers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewThis review examines the role of vascular health in children and adolescents with systemic hypertension. Like other areas of target organ damage (TOD), arterial dysfunction can have deleterious effects over time, leading to adverse cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction or stroke as an adult.Recent findingsLiterature in the last few years has focused on TOD to the vascular tree in pediatric patients with hypertension. These data also included vascular dysfunction in ambulatory and masked hypertension, particularly in secondary causes of hypertension, which emphasize the importance of ambulatory BP monitoring in these patients. While subclinical TOD exists early on, longitudinal studies that follow diverse populations with hypertension from childhood into late adulthood are needed to determine if abnormal vasculature is independently associated with suboptimal cardiovascular outcomes. Consistent conduct of these tests would more formally incorporate them into pediatric clinical practice.SummaryHypertension is a chronic medical condition that needs a multipronged treatment approach. The vasculature is an important yet understudied area of TOD. Longitudinal studies in this realm could link subclinical disease in youth to adult adverse events and develop more precise management to mitigate these events and to enable a better quality of life.Keywords: hypertension, target organ damage, vascular health

INTRODUCTION

Systemic hypertension is a known independent risk factor for cardiovascular events in adulthood [1–3]. When examining outcomes of hypertension in children, measures like cardiovascular events or mortality are less useful. However, markers of subclinical target organ damage (TOD) can serve as more proximal outcome measures showing that the effects of hypertension are not just limited to adulthood but also result in adverse impacts during childhood. At a young age, many of these markers can be reversible. In this review, we will briefly discuss the epidemiology of hypertension and then focus on vascular dysfunction as an area of TOD. Specifically, the review highlights clinical studies about pulse wave velocity (PWV) in the aorta and carotid artery intima–media thickness (CIMT) as noninvasive ways of measuring arterial target organ damage in hypertension. We will end with gaps in knowledge and areas of opportunity to better link hypertension and vascular injury to adverse cardiovascular events in adults.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF HYPERTENSION

The age-adjusted prevalence of hypertension among US adults at least 20 years of age was estimated to be 46.7% in 2017–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), 50.4% for men and 43% for women, which equates to an estimated 122.4 million adults at least 20 years of age who have high BP. As far as adolescents, in a more recent study of NHANES, while obesity and prediabetes increased from 2001 to March 2020 (just as the COVID pandemic ensued), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cigarette use, and inactive physical activity decreased among US adolescents aged 12–19 years, whereas elevated BP, diabetes, and poor diet quality remained unchanged [4▪▪]. The risk of developing cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) begins in childhood (see Fig. 1). One of the early and most significant American multicenter studies, organized by15 cooperating centers, to determine pathophysiological aspects of atherosclerosis in youth was the Pathobiological Determinants of Atherosclerosis in Youth (PDAY) study [5].

High BP (BP) or hypertension is a modifiable risk factor in youth for the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Rates of elevated BP and hypertension among youths have been tracked over several decades and have paralleled the increasing prevalence of childhood obesity according to a study using NHANES) III (1988–1994) and continuous NHANES (1999–2008) data [6]. Compared to the previously mentioned NHANES study, prevalence of elevated BPBP in children has significantly increased from 1988 to 2008, associated with obesity and Na intake. In 2017, new diagnostic criteria established age, sex, and height-specific percentile tables for children and aligned adolescent (≥13 years) BP categories with those of adults [7]. This clinical practice guideline enabled a streamlined approach to the identification of hypertension and to make cutoffs congruent with those in adults, which have shown associations with cardiovascular outcomes. Interestingly, there are no consistent cutoffs between normal BP and hypertension among guidelines. In fact, there is still an under-recognition of hypertension in the pediatric population [8] (see Fig. 2). Thus, recent guidelines on pediatric hypertension are very important, despite a period of decreasing rates of hypertension in the early 21st century, this CVRF is now increasing again. The prevalence of high BP in adolescents is roughly 15%, with 2–5% of those being classified as hypertensive patients [7,9,10] (see Table 1).

HYPERTENSION AND VASCULAR HEALTH IN THE YOUNG

In adults with hypertension, vascular changes are associated with adverse cardiovascular events like myocardial infarction and stroke [11–15]. Furthermore, in a recent article from Chung et al.[16▪], the authors performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to show that children and adolescents with hypertension do have a higher PWV and CIMT compared to those who are normotensive. Thus, like the heart, kidney, brain, and the eyes, the vascular system is an important area of TOD in the setting of hypertension in the young.

ARTERIAL STIFFNESS IN HYPERTENSION

PWV measures the speed at which the mechanical wave propagates along the arterial tree and is a useful surrogate marker for arterial vessel stiffness. The higher the velocity, the stiffer the artery (see Fig. 2). The carotid–femoral PWV (cfPWV) has been the gold standard for TOD in the vascular system in hypertension [17,18]. While various methods exist for measuring arterial stiffness, PWV is a noninvasive, simple measurement method that has the potential to be incorporated into clinical practice.

PWV has garnered clinical attention, particularly in monitoring patients suffering from vascular diseases, such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus. PWV can be measured in various ways including Doppler ultrasound, oscillometric measurement, and MRI. In adults, a PWV ranging from 6 to 10 m/s is normal, and a higher velocity indicates potential for a cardiovascular adverse event [19–22]. Therefore, its utility has now extended to pediatric preventive cardiology, aiding in identifying and stratifying cardiovascular risk for earlier management to hopefully prevent adverse events in adulthood.

Over the last decade or so, there have been a few studies that have presented normative values for PWV in children and adolescents, making this biomarker an increasingly useful method of assessing a subclinical area of TOD earlier in life [23–26,27▪]. One recent study of 1856 adolescents from the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children, UK birth cohort were followed for 7 years. Arterial stiffness progression temporally preceded worsening structural and functional cardiac damage in youth with increased SBP and insulin resistance partly mediating the relationships. That study concluded that interventions aimed at attenuating premature cardiac damage in adolescents and young adults may consider a simultaneous treatment of both arterial stiffness, elevated BP, and insulin resistance [28▪▪].

While hypertension and obesity are often comorbid conditions, children with hypertension exhibit increased PWV independent of obesity [29–31]. Furthermore, PWV rises incrementally per unit increase in BP and across BP categories from normotensive to prehypertensive to hypertensive youth [32]. A recent study stratified individuals into low-risk (SBP <75th percentile, n = 155), mid-risk (SBP ≥80th and <90th percentile, n = 88), and high-risk BP categories (≥90th percentile, n = 139), based on age-specific, sex-specific, and height-specific pediatric BP cut points. Many parameters that were collected, including PWV, increased across groups and were associated with TOD (systolic and diastolic cardiac function and urine albumin/creatinine ratio) after controlling for BP. The conclusion emphasized the need for primary prevention early on to mitigate this risk factor [33]. PWV is also elevated in patients with hypertension as a secondary cause, including rheumatologic disorders, kidney disease, solid-organ or stem-cell transplant, coarctation of the aorta, sickle cell disease, and obesity [34,35▪,36].

In a systematic analysis of studies from 1974 to 2021 on the association of ambulatory hypertension with subclinical cardiovascular target organ damage, 136 studies showed that both primary and secondary hypertension were associated with an elevated PWV [35▪]. To determine whether a specific BP phenotype, based on clinic and 24-h ambulatory BP (ABP), is associated with markers of target organ damage in otherwise healthy youth, a recent study enrolled 373 adolescents in a cross-sectional study of the relationship of BP to cardiovascular risk [Study of High BP in Pediatrics: Adult Hypertension Onset in Youth (SHIP-AHOY)]. Clinic BP was measured by auscultation and categorized using the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics guideline. 24-h ambulatory BP monitor was also used. Carotid–femoral PWV and multiple measures of cardiac function were worse in the masked and ambulatory hypertensive groups [37]. Another recent study from the same group showed similar findings, which emphasizes the importance of ABPM in measuring vascular TOD in hypertension [38▪▪].

INCREASED CAROTID INTIMA–MEDIA THICKNESS IN HYPERTENSION

CIMT is a measurement of the thickness of the inner two layers of the carotid artery wall, the intima and media. It's a noninvasive test, typically performed using ultrasound, that helps assess the extent of atherosclerosis, a condition where plaque builds up inside the arteries (see Fig. 3). Hypertension and an increased CIMT in adults are associated with future cardiovascular adverse events [39]. One recent study from Norway studied BP variability and management of young ischemic stroke survivors – 283 individuals followed for 5 years – and noted that SBP variability was independently associated with an abnormal CIMT (>0.9 mm) and remained associated with all areas of TOD at 5 years [40]. Another study of 2555 adults indicated that obesity s associated with indices of arterial remodeling and atherosclerosis, as defined by CIMT and left ventricle mass index, and that hypertension mediates these associations [41].

Carotid intima–media thickness is a measurement of the thickness of the inner two layers of the carotid artery wall, the intima and media. It is a noninvasive test, typically performed using ultrasound, that helps assess the extent of atherosclerosis, a condition where plaque builds up inside the arteries.

Normative data for CIMT have been published for children, and some studies also associate CIMT with obesity [42]. There are also links between hypertension and an elevated CIMT in children and adolescents. A recent study in CIMT in hypertensive children and adolescents noted that in 40 adolescents (20 hypertensive and 20 normal patients, all without TOD), the hypertensive patients had elevated CIMT [43]. Tagetti et al.[44▪] looked at secondary hypertension in patients with kidney disease and noted that in 44 children and adolescents, 45% had masked hypertension, and 23 (52%) of those individuals had an increased CIMT, concluding that these patients would benefit from ABP monitoring, earlier management, and closer follow-up to manage hypertension. Kiechl et al.[45] noted that SBP and arterial hypertension, among other risk factors, predicted cIMT progression in adolescents, perhaps indicating early intervention for high BP in youth to limit risk of heart disease as an adult. In a study by Chiesa Scott et al.[46], fat-free mass and SBP have been identified as the only modifiable risk factors associated with CIMT in children. This study, too, noted that obesity and hypertension may require developing novel approaches to mitigate arterial dysfunction in the long-term.

ENDOTHELIAL DYSFUNCTION IN HYPERTENSION

The endothelium is the thin layer of cells lining blood vessels. The endothelium is not merely a passive lining, it is a dynamic, active tissue that plays a crucial role in, regulating blood vessel tone, preventing blood clots, controlling inflammation, and regulating cell growth. Endothelial dysfunction, a hallmark of hypertension, is characterized by reduced vasodilator production and increased vasoconstrictor and inflammatory cytokine generation, which contributes to elevation of the BP and vascular damage. Early detection and intervention are crucial to prevent long-term vascular complications [47].

Atherosclerotic changes can be attributed to early endothelial damage in individuals with hypertension. Dogan et al., explored the relationship between endothelial dysfunction and hypertension in newly diagnosed children without TOD, using CIMT, flow-mediated dilatation (FMD), and functional capillaroscopy parameters. The group analyzed the differences between nocturnal BP dipper and nondipper patients. Significant differences were observed between the hypertensive patients and controls in terms of CIMT (P = 0.04), brachial artery FMD (P = 0.02), and functional capillary density (P < 0.001). This study demonstrated that vascular consequences associated with essential hypertension emphasize the importance of early detection and management of hypertension [43].

Kos et al. investigated the relationship of macro-vasculature and micro-vasculature and hypertension in children. Fifty-two children were enrolled in this study; 26 normotensive and 26 with primary hypertension, both sexes, 9–17 years old. Flow-mediated vasodilation in the brachial artery, among other parameters, were impaired in hypertensive children compared to normotensive children. Thus, even in childhood, primary hypertension is associated with attenuated endothelial function, and all changes are independent of BMI [48].

Jurko et al. looked at endothelial function assessed using peripheral arterial tonometry (PAT) in normal-weight and overweight adolescents with essential and white-coat hypertension, with a focus on conventional and novel indices of reactive hyperemia (RHI) as potential early markers of vascular damage in the pediatric population. AUC, reflecting the overall hyperemic response, was the only parameter that showed a significant effect of group (normotensives vs. white-coat hypertensive patients vs. essential hypertensive patients) on endothelial function compared to RHI, FRHI, peak response, and time to peak response. This study was likely the first one to associate these novel biomarkers with primary hypertension at this age [49].

This more recent literature gives emerging evidence that endothelial dysfunction develops in the early stages of primary hypertension. Assessment of endothelial function in children/adolescents has been simplified and made practicable for clinical use due to the availability, for example, of Endothelial Peripheral Arterial Tonometry (EndoPatTM), thus, making the endothelium a potential target for directed treatment to prevent cardiovascular events later in life.

GAPS IN RESEARCH AND OPPORTUNITIES

Clearly, abnormal arterial function is an area of TOD in hypertension that, in adults, is a risk factor for adverse events. The question is whether testing for these noninvasive, subclinical vascular markers in youth and targeting BP management to reverse these abnormalities would mitigate the risk of cardiovascular disease in adulthood. We need consistent, large-scale longitudinal data that covers a wide array of ethnicities and socioeconomic backgrounds to fully understand the range of BPs to distinguish normal values versus hypertension. These data should also include ABP monitoring and would also add to existing, normative data sets for vascular parameters in youth. Long-term studies would shed light on which pediatric populations have a higher risk of adverse events from hypertension so that targeted, early treatment may include reversing the vascular abnormalities as well as targeting other areas of TOD. Maternal and fetal factors for hypertension and their effects on vascular health over the long-term would further refine the management of hypertension in youth. From a practical standpoint, research on measuring and reporting biomarkers of vascular health using similar devices and protocols would enable medical teams to incorporate these measurements into pediatric clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

Systemic hypertension is a chronic medical condition that can cause significant complications and even death in adulthood. When combined with other CVRFs like obesity, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, the risks of irreparable damage are even higher. Many organs are affected by hypertension, including the arterial tree. While there is still work to be done, our fields are closer to incorporating noninvasive vascular testing into pediatric practice so that medical teams can use the results to help manage hypertension at a time when this area of TOD is reversible to improve long-term outcomes.
