IVUS-Guided Intravascular Lithotripsy of the Juxtarenal and Visceral Coral Reef Aorta
Authors: Florian Elger, Rukiye Secer, Marcello Silvano, Eberhard Grambow, Giovanni F Torsello, Giovanni B Torsello
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA patient with coral reef aorta at the level of the renal and visceral arteries was treated endovascularly. We used intravascular lithotripsy for improving stent expansion and intravascular ultrasound as an intraoperative diagnostic tool. The renal artery periscope technique maintained renal perfusion after placement of the aortic endograft.Key Words: aortic stenting, atherosclerotic aortic disease, coral reef aorta, intravascular lithotripsy, IVUS, periscope

History of Presentation

A 79-year-old woman with a history of claudication presented with rest pain in both legs. On clinical examination she was obese, had mild dyspnea, and had no palpable pulses on either leg. Her blood pressure was 185/95 mm Hg.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of arterial hypertension and dyslipidemia complicated by congestive heart failure. Three years before presentation, the patient had undergone coronary bypass surgery for 3-vessel coronary artery disease. Postoperatively, she developed acute renal failure. Temporary hemodialysis and stenting of the right renal artery were performed. The left renal artery was already occluded, and the kidney had shrunk.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for critical limb ischemia includes sensory neuropathy, nerve root compression, and restless leg syndrome, which were ruled out by neurologic examination.

Investigations

The ankle-brachial index (ABI) was decreased to 0.55 (right) and 0.53 (left), with monophasic Doppler signals in the femoral arteries on Duplex ultrasound. Her baseline creatinine value was 2.4 mg/dL.

On computed tomography angiography (CTA) (Video 1), she was found to have 2 severely calcified aortic stenoses. The distal stenosis was located at the level of the juxtarenal aorta (Figure 1). The left renal artery was completely occluded by calcification, and the right renal artery had previously been treated with a bare-metal stent, which was directed slightly downward in its aortic portion.

Preoperative Computed Tomography Angiography

The image at level of the renal arteries shows severe calcification of the aorta, which completely occludes the outlet of the left renal artery. The patency of the right renal artery is maintained by a bare-metal stent.

The proximal aortic stenosis was found at the origin of the celiac artery, which was stenotic at the junction with the aorta.

Management

We began the procedure with a percutaneous retrograde transfemoral approach. After advancing a 12-F sheath and placing a pigtail catheter just above the proximal stenotic lesion, an aortogram was obtained (Figure 2). Both aortic lesions were crossed with a 0.014-inch guidewire. Subsequently, an intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) pullback (Core M2 System, Philips Volcano) confirmed 2 high-grade aortic stenoses at the level of the ostium of the previously stented renal artery and at the level of the celiac artery, with a strong superficial calcium load (Video 2). The minimal lumen diameter at the level of the juxtarenal stenosis was 2.1 mm, with a reference vessel diameter of 28 mm (Figure 3).

Pretreatment Procedural Aortography

Aortography was performed after crossing the stenotic lesions with a guidewire and positioning a pigtail catheter. Two aortic stenoses are detectable at the supraceliac and juxtarenal levels. A renal stent maintains patency of the right renal artery, whereas the left renal artery is completely occluded.

Preprocedural Intravascular Ultrasound of Pararenal Aorta

Aortic intravascular ultrasound shows juxtarenal high-grade aortic stenosis, with a minimum diameter (MIN. D) of 2.1 mm, a maximum diameter (MAX. D) of 3.6 mm, and an estimated vessel cross-sectional area (FLÄCHE) of 6.8 mm2. The finding is consistent with that of computed tomography angiography.

After several failed attempts to cannulate the right renal artery, we decided to treat the aortic calcification first. For this purpose, we used an 8.0 mm × 60 mm M5+ intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) balloon (Shockwave Medical) inflated up to 4 atm with total of 120 pulses (60 pulses per stenosis). The aortic lumen was dilated, and cannulation of the right renal artery stent could be performed without difficulty.

IVUS showed an enlargement of the aortic lumen to 8 mm and an increased movement of the aortic wall synchronized with the cardiac cycle (Video 3). Under balloon embolic protection of the solitary kidney, predilatation was performed with a 12 mm × 10 mm predilatation was performed with a 12 mm × 100 mm percutaneous transluminal angioplasty balloon catheter (Advance LP, Cook Medical). We then placed a 12 mm × 40 mm bare-metal stent (ProtégéTM GPSTM Self-expanding Peripheral Stent, Medtronic) at the level of the juxtarenal aorta and a 7 mm × 39 mm VBX balloon-expandable covered stent (W.L. Gore & Associates) in the right renal artery as periscope. After placing balloons in a kissing conformation, the angiographic results were acceptable. Because the celiac artery showed retrograde filling through the wide-open superior mesenteric artery, the stenosis was not treated with a stent.

Outcome and Follow Up

No adverse event occurred postoperatively, and the patient’s symptoms disappeared completely. The next day, the ABI was 0.80 (left) and 0.72 (right). Postoperative CTA showed good positioning and expansion of both stents (Figure 4).

Follow-Up Computed Tomography Angiography

Postoperative imaging showed good patency of both the aortic bare-metal stent and the renal periscope bridging stent. The yellow dotted line indicates the centerline measurement within the VBX stent.

Discussion

Rock-hard, eccentric, irregular heavily calcified atherosclerotic lesions of the juxtarenal and visceral aorta are rare. Aortic endarterectomy is an effective treatment,1 and it has good results in experienced centers.2 Postoperative mortality is high (11.6%), and multimorbid patients are not good candidates for open surgery. Because our patient had multiple comorbidities, we opted for endovascular techniques with IVL and aortic stenting.

Endovascular treatment of coral reef aorta (CRA) located at the thoracoabdominal transition by stent-graft placement has been reported.3 Given the involvement of the aortic branches, simple stent grafting was not a feasible option in our case. The literature shows that ballon-expandable stents4 and stent grafts5 may lead to complications and conversion to open repair. Nitinol self-expanding stents offer several advantages such as the smaller French profile and a lower risk of rupture.6 However, because the radial force is not sufficient in severe calcified lesions, vessel preparation is mandatory. Pretreatment with IVL supported by simple balloon angioplasty improved subsequent stent expansion and reduced the risk of rupture.

To avoid a peripheral embolism, we planned to occlude the renal stent temporarily with an angioplasty balloon catheter. However, the eccentric aortic calcifications made cannulation challenging. After IVL of the calcified aortic lesion, embolic protection with a PTA balloon catheter could be performed without difficulty. Because larger IVL balloon catheters were not available, an 8-mm balloon was used. After the postdilation technique, the aortic lumen was considered sufficient for subsequent stenting. Alternatively, 2 lithotripsy balloons in a kissing conformation can be used simultaneously.7

To maintain perfusion of the solitary kidney, a covered balloon-expandable stent was placed using the periscope technique. Veger et al8 showed that the parallel grafting technique can also be useful in the context of suprarenal CRA; however, long-term results of this technique need further investigation.

Finally, our case demonstrates the important role of IVUS as a less invasive imaging technique to visualize vessel wall, plaque composition, and accuracy of the revascularization procedure. According to the recently published Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions expert consensus paper,9 there is growing evidence to support the increased use of supporting IVUS to reduce adverse events and prolong the patency of lower extremity revascularization procedures. Although angiography remains the predominant imaging modality in peripheral interventions, IVUS is a useful adjunct providing information for planning the procedure and assessing the revascularization outcome, especially in calcified vessels.

Conclusions

If CRA is at level of the take-off of aortic branches, IVL can be a safe and effective vascular preparation technique before aortic stenting with a nitinol bare-metal stent.
