Geochemical fingerprinting of plume in Suswa silicic volcanics, Kenya rift
Authors: Lu-Bing Hong, Yu Wang, Liang Ma, Wei-Liang Huang, Zhi-Jie Jia, Jian-Bing Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Kenya plume drives rift formation and magmatism along the Kenya Rift, yet its components remain obscured by lithospheric overprinting on mafic volcanics. We present whole-rock geochemical data for mantle-derived silicic rocks from Suswa, sampled at the reworked craton margin of the Kenya Rift where continental lithosphere profoundly influences mafic rock compositions. These rocks exhibit high SiO2 (54.9–60.4wt.%), total alkalis (Na2O + K2O = 9.3–13.8wt.%) and lithophile elements (Rb, Th-U, Nb-Ta and rare earth elements (REE); typically 10–100× chondrite and primitive mantle values), alongside low MgO (< 1.3wt.%). Their isotopic signatures show variable 87Sr/86Sr (0.7036–0.7056), depleted Nd-Hf isotopes (143Nd/144Nd = 0.51282–0.51285, 176Hf/177Hf = 0.28291–0.28293) and moderately radiogenic Pb isotopic ratios (206Pb/204Pb = 19.99–20.20, 207Pb/204Pb = 15.73–15.84 and 208Pb/204Pb = 39.82–39.97). The more depleted Nd isotopes compared to those of mafic rocks ( < ~ 0.51275) in the reworked craton margin and the northern Tanzania Craton, indicate minimal lithospheric incorporation. Low MgO contents, positive correlations of MgO with Al2O3, CaO, TiO2, P2O5, Ba, Sr and Eu, and characteristic REE and trace-element patterns support fractionation involving olivine, pyroxene, plagioclase, Ti oxide and apatite. Low Ba/Nb ratios, constant Rb/Nb and La/Nb ratios, and a negative 87Sr/86Sr–SiO2 correlation preclude significant crustal contamination in most samples. Integrated Sr-Nd-Pb-Hf isotopic and trace-element systematics, combined with regional data, reveal a multi-component mantle source: plume-derived HIMU and depleted common mantle (C) components, plus a lithosphere-derived EMII component. Quantitative two-component mixing models, using the plume end-member (C modified by HIMU-derived melt) and the lithospheric mantle end-member (EMII modified by HIMU-derived melt), indicate 10–20% lithospheric contribution to the Suswa source. Critically, the identification of the C component within these depleted rocks, supported by literature data, confirms its presence within the Kenya plume. This underscores the imperative of targeting volcanic sequences with minimal lithosphere contamination to resolve the primary plume components beneath continents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22868-5.Keywords: East african rift, Kenya plume, Suswa silicic volcanics, Depleted mantle common (C) components, Plume-lithosphere interactionSubject terms: Geochemistry, Volcanology

Introduction

The East African Rift System (EARS), a major continental rift extending from Afar throughout Ethiopia before bifurcating into the Western and Eastern (Kenya) Rifts around the Tanzania Craton (Fig. 1a), is widely regarded as a classic example of plume-driven continental rifting1–13. This setting provides an exceptional opportunity to investigate extensional magmatism, plume-lithosphere interaction and rift dynamics.

(b) Tectonic units and the recent volcanoes within the Kenya Rift after Macdonald and Scaillet29, including the Chyulu Hills volcanic field (black triangle), located 100–250 km east of the southern Kenya Rift (Off-Rift)39. Driven by the seismically imaged Kenya plume, which extends into the lower mantle beneath the Tanzania Craton14,15, the Kenya Rift transects the Late Proterozoic mobile belt (MB), the reworked craton margin (RCM) and the northern Tanzania Craton (NTC) (Fig. 1b). Associated magmatism has migrated along the rift axis over time16 (Fig. 1a). Sr-Nd isotope compositions of plume-related mafic volcanics across the Kenya Rift typically cluster around an enriched endmember attributed to the Kenya plume (143Nd/144Nd ≈ 0.51275)4,17. This signature is more enriched than that of the globally recognized common mantle component (C)18 or FOZO19, which exhibits depleted isotopic characteristics (143Nd/144Nd ≈ 0.5129)20,21. Whereas the C component signature is clearly recorded in mafic volcanics from the MB11,22–25, it is largely obscured in those from the RCM and NTC. Rocks in these regions typically display more enriched Sr-Nd isotopes4,17, fueling ongoing debate regarding the Kenya plume’s intrinsic enrichment.

Enriched lithospheric mantle contamination represents the prevailing hypothesis to explain both the enriched common component signature attributed to the plume and the absence of the C component in RCM and NTC volcanics4,17,24,26,27. This hypothesis predicts that the C component signature should be detectable in RCM and NTC volcanics exhibiting minimal lithospheric input and more depleted isotope compositions. To test this prediction, we present whole-rock geochemical data for silicic volcanics from Suswa, located within the RCM of the Kenya Rift. Critically, these rocks possess the most depleted Nd-Hf isotopes reported for any RCM and NTC volcanics, offering new constrains on the nature of the Kenya plume.

Geological background and sample description

Magmatism in the Kenya Rift has generated over 900,000 km3 of igneous rocks28. Activity initiated in the Turkana depression of the MB at ~ 35–30 Ma, propagated southward into the RCM by ~ 20–16 Ma, and reached the NTC by ~ 8–5 Ma16 (Fig. 1a). This voluminous activity is attributed to the Kenya plume4,8, which initiated magmatism in southern Ethiopia at ~ 50 Ma and subsequently migrated to its present locus beneath Lake Victoria within the Tanzania Craton2 (Fig. 1a).

The Central Kenya Peralkaline Province (CKPP29, located within the RCM (Fig. 1b), comprises five Quaternary–recent peralkaline salic volcanic complexes (Menengai, Longonot, Suswa, Eburru and Olkaria) and three mafic lava fields (Elmenteita, Ndabibi and Tandamara)(Figs. 1b–c). Interpreted as plume-related17, this province demonstrates persistent Kenya plume activity within the RCM for ~ 20 Ma.

Suswa volcano, the southernmost edifice of the CKPP, commenced activity at ~ 0.24 Ma30. Its eruptive products form a compositionally diverse volcanic suite ranging from SiO2-undersaturated to SiO2-oversaturated types, encompassing trachytic volcanics, carbonate-rich trachytes, and variably porphyritic (strongly to sparsely phyric) trachyte-phonolite lava flows and tuffs31. The examined samples are predominantly vesicular, exhibiting textures ranging from aphyric to phyric. Mineralogically, phenocrysts are dominated by alkali feldspar, with subordinate clinopyroxene and minor olivine, all set in a groundmass of alkali feldspar, apatite, magnetite and devitrified glass.

Analytical methods

Whole-rock compositional analyses were performed at Wuhan Sample Solution Analytical Technology Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China) using protocols detailed at www.samplesolution.cn. We determined major elements via wavelength dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometry on a ZSX Primus II instrument, analyzing fused glass disks at 50 kV voltage and 60 mA current with precision better than 3%. For trace elements, we employed inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using an Agilent 7700e system after digesting powdered samples in an acid mixture within clean Teflon bombs; precision was better than 5% for most elements. Subsequent Sr-Nd-Pb-Hf isotopic analyses were conducted on a Neptune Plus multi-collector ICP-MS (MC-ICP-MS) operated in static low-resolution mode. Following HF-HNO3 acid digestion and cation-exchange column purification, we corrected mass bias through internal normalization using 88Sr/86Sr = 8.375209, 146Nd/144Nd = 0.7219, 205Tl/203Tl = 2.38714 and 179Hf/177Hf = 0.7325. We monitored instrument drift with NBS987 (Sr), GSB04-3258-2015 (Nd), NBS981 (Pb), and Alfa Hf standards. Analyses of USGS BCR-2 yielded 87Sr/86Sr = 0.705007 ± 5 (2SE), 143Nd/144Nd = 0.512639 ± 5, 206Pb/204Pb = 18.757 ± 1, 207Pb/204Pb = 15.627 ± 1, 208Pb/204Pb = 38.742 ± 1, and 176Hf/177Hf = 0.282853 ± 6—values consistent with published values (87Sr/86Sr = 0.705026 ± 20, 143Nd/144Nd = 0.512638 ± 15, 206Pb/204Pb = 18.753 ± 8, 207Pb/204Pb = 15.621 ± 4, 208Pb/204Pb = 38.725 ± 22, 176Hf/177Hf = 0.282869 ± 9)30,32.

Results

Whole-rock compositions of the Suswa volcanics and nearby Tandamara volcanics are provided in Table S160,61,62. The Suswa volcanics range from SiO2-undersaturated (0–12% normative nepheline) to SiO2-oversaturated (0–6% normative quartz), classifying as trachyandesites to phonolites (SiO2 = 54.9–60.4wt.%, Na2O + K2O = 9.3–13.8wt.%; Fig. 2a). They exhibit moderately high Al2O3 (13.9–17.8wt.%) and Fe2O3total (7.3–10.0wt.%), but low TiO2 (< 1.3wt.%), MgO (< 1.3wt.%), CaO (< 3.2wt.%), and P2O5 (< 0.4wt.%). These characteristics confirm their evolved nature relative to nearby Tandamara volcanics (e.g., 2–5wt.% MgO and 54–57wt.% SiO2; Fig. S1). Siderophile elements (Ni, Co, Cr, and Sc < 12 ppm) are depleted in the Suswa volcanics, whereas lithophile elements (Rb, Th-U, Nb-Ta and rare earth elements (REEs)), are highly enriched (typically 10–100× chondrite and primitive mantle values34; Figs. 2b–c). Most lithophile elements show broad negative correlations with MgO, though Ba, Sr, and Eu exhibit positive correlations (Fig. S2). Their chondrite-normalized REE patterns display strong light REE (LREE) enrichment with flat middle-to-heavy REE profiles and pronounced negative Eu anomalies. Primitive mantle-normalized trace element patterns resemble HIMU-oceanic island basalts (OIBs) (e.g., positive Nb-Ta anomalies, and negative U-K-Pb anomalies), but feature significant negative Ba-Sr-Ti anomalies. This contrasts sharply with the Tandamara volcanics, which exhibit EMII-OIB affinities (positive K and Ba anomalies, slightly negative Nb-Ta and Zr-Hf anomalies, and significant negative Sr and Ti anomalies) (Fig. 2c).

Isotopically, the Suswa volcanics show variable 87Sr/86Sr (0.7036–0.7056) but restricted ranges for 143Nd/144Nd (0.51282–0.51285), 176Hf/177Hf (0.28291–0.28293), 206Pb/204Pb (19.99–20.20), 207Pb/204Pb (15.73–15.84), and 208Pb/204Pb (39.82–39.97), forming flat-lying arrays in isotope space (Figs. 3 and S3). Discussion

Crustal contamination

The role of crustal processing in the CKPP peralkaline volcanics is extensively documented, evidenced by near-horizontal contamination trends toward elevated 87Sr/86Sr ratios on Sr-Nd and Sr-Pb isotope diagrams24,36. Similar isotopic patterns observed in the Suswa volcanics (Fig. 3) may indicate potential crustal contamination. Incorporation of continental crust, characterized by high SiO2, elevated 87Sr/86Sr, and enrichment of large ion lithophile elements (LILEs: Rb, Ba, Th, U, Sr, LREEs, and Pb) relative to high field strength elements (HFSEs, Nb, Ta)37, would typically increase SiO2 content, LILEs/HFSEs ratios, and 87Sr/86Sr in affected magmas36,38. To evaluate crustal input in the Suswa volcanics, we utilize sensitive indicators: Ba/Nb, Rb/Nb, La/Nb, and 87Sr/86Sr ratios. The Suswa samples exhibit low Ba/Nb (< 6), relatively constant Rb/Nb (0.23–0.63) and La/Nb (0.39–0.80), with no clear correlations between these ratios and magmatic evolution indices (e.g., SiO2, Sr and La; Figs. 4a–c). Furthermore, their 87Sr/86Sr ratios generally decrease with increasing SiO2, except in one sample (SiO2 > 59 wt%; Fig. 4d). These characteristics, specifically low Ba/Nb, invariant Rb/Nb and La/Nb, absence of Ba/Nb-SiO2, Rb/Nb–Sr and La/Nb–La correlations, and inverse 87Sr/86Sr–SiO2 trend, differ fundamentally from diagnostic signatures of crustal contamination (elevated Ba/Nb, Rb/Nb and La/Nb, positive Ba/Nb–SiO2, La/Nb–La and 87Sr/86Sr–SiO2 correlations, and negative Rb/Nb–Sr correlations)36,38. This systematic divergence provides robust evidence against significant crustal contamination affecting most samples.

The Suswa volcanics exhibit variable SiO2, La, Nb, and Sr concentrations, but constant La/Nb, Rb/Nb, Ba/Nb, and Ce/Y ratios. This decoupling supports fractional crystallization (FC) and argues against assimilation and fractional crystallization (AFC). The general negative correlation between 87Sr/86Sr and SiO2 contrasts with the positive relationship characteristic of AFC. One sample with SiO2 > 60 wt% displays elevated 87Sr/86Sr, potentially indicating localized influence of AFC.

Fractional crystallization

The Suswa volcanics exhibit depleted Nd-Hf isotopes (Fig. 3b), consistent with a mantle origin. However, their low MgO contents (< 2wt.%), exceptionally high incompatible element concentrations (Figs. 2b–c), negative correlations between incompatible elements and MgO (Fig. S2), and relatively constant Ce/Y ratios throughout magmatic evolution (Nb and SiO2; Figs. 4e–f) collectively demonstrate these rocks represent highly evolved mantle-derived lavas rather than primary melts. Petrographic observations of minor olivine and clinopyroxene phenocrysts, coupled with low MgO, Cr, Ni, and Sc contents, indicate both olivine and clinopyroxene fractionation. Further fractionation involving Ti oxides, apatite, and feldspar is evidenced by positive correlations of TiO2, P2O5, Al2O3, Ba, Sr and Eu with MgO contents (Figs. S1, S2), pronounced negative Eu anomalies in REE patterns (Fig. 2b), and significant negative Ba, Sr and Ti anomalies in trace element patterns (Fig. 2c).

Although silicic CKPP volcanics are often interpreted as evolved products of associated mafic volcanics31,36, this model cannot account for the Suswa lavas. Key geochemical contrasts exist: positive Nb-Ta anomalies and negative K and Pb anomalies in Suswa sharply contrast with the nearby Tandamara volcanics, which lack pronounced Nb-Ta and Pb anomalies and exhibit a slight positive K anomaly (Fig. 2c). These differences cannot reflect magmatic fractionation, as all relevant elements behave incompatibly (Figs. S1, S2). Crucially, the Suswa volcanics possess significantly more depleted Nd isotopic compositions (0.51282–0.51285) than the Tandamara suite (0.51243–0.51245) (Fig. 3a), thereby precluding an evolutionary relationship.

We therefore conclude that although the Suswa volcanics underwent extensive crystal fractionation (olivine, clinopyroxene, feldspar, Ti oxides, and apatite), their parental magmas are genetically distinct from nearby Tandamara volcanics. This necessitates caution when inferring petrogenetic links between spatially associated silicic and mafic volcanics in the CKPP.

Mantle components in source and their origins

Identification of mantle components.

The Suswa volcanics represent highly evolved mantle-derived lavas exhibiting negligible crustal contamination but significant crystal fractionation. While fractional crystallization modifies trace element concentrations, it leaves Sr-Nd-Pb-Hf isotopic systems and trace element patterns unaffected. These signatures (Figs. 2c and 3) therefore constrain the mantle source components, delineating three distinct mantle end-members (terminology after Hofmann20:

HIMU component: Evidenced by trace element signatures akin to HIMU-OIBs (positive Nb-Ta anomalies, negative K and Pb anomalies, and Rb depletion relative to Nb; Fig. 2c), correlating with moderately radiogenic Pb isotopes (206Pb/204Pb ≈ 20.20; Figs. 3c–f).

EMII component: Characterized by variable 87Sr/86Sr ratios (0.7037–0.7047, excluding one contaminated sample) against restricted Nd-Hf-Pb isotopes, trending horizontally toward EMII with elevated 207Pb/206Pb relative to the NHRL (Fig. 3).

Depleted mantle component: Indicated by depleted Nd-Hf isotopes, specifically higher 176Hf/177Hf than HIMU at comparable 143Nd/144Nd (Fig. 3b). This signature potentially originates from asthenospheric DMM or the plume-associated C component20 (Fig. 3). We attribute it to C based on three lines of evidence. (1) C is a ubiquitous feature in plume-related OIBs, interpreted as primitive lower mantle material19,20. Critically, geophysical evidence confirms the deep-rooted Kenya plume extends into the lower mantle14,15, providing a viable sourcing mechanism. (2) Within the EMI-EMII-HIMU-DMM tetrahedron (Figs. 5 and S4), C represents a convergent end-member for OIBs19. MB mafic lavas within this framework show dual convergence trends: from EMI and from a HIMU-EMII hybrid toward C. This observation aligns with contributions from EMI, HIMU, and C documented in previous studies11,22–25. Similarly, Off-Rift Chyulu Hills lavas (from 100 to 250 km east of the southern Kenya Rift; Fig. 1b), genetically linked to the Kenya plume39, trend from EMII and from a HIMU-EMII hybrid toward C in Sr-Pb and Nd-Pb isotope space (Figs. 3e–f) and within the tetrahedron (Fig. 5). These convergence patterns collectively indicating C within the plume. Crucially, Suswa volcanics define a binary mixing array extending from EMII-HIMU hybrid toward C, further corroborating this component. (3) RCM/NTC rocks and geothermal gases exhibit 3He/4He ratios (4.5–7.8 Ra; where Ra is the atmospheric ratio)40–42 overlapping MORB values (8 ± 1 Ra)43 and regional lithospheric mantle (5.0–7.6 Ra)44,45, but their extrapolated Ne isotopes ((21Ne/22Ne)EX = 0.037 ± 0.001)41 show primordial, OIB-like signatures (~ 0.035)43 distinct from MORB (~ 0.075)43, strongly indicating C’s primitive lower mantle association20.

Origins of EMII and HIMU.

While the C component at Suswa is plume-derived, the origins of EMII and HIMU require further evaluation. Both enriched components could potentially derive from either recycled crust within the convective mantle20,46 or lithospheric metasomes4,17,47. For EMII, a systematic decrease in 143Nd/144Nd ratios of mantle-derived volcanics from MB to RCM/NTC (Figs. 6 and S5) reveals a first-order spatial-compositional trend, strongly implicating lithospheric control4,17,24,26,27, and supporting a lithospheric origin for this component. Mantle xenoliths from RCM and NTC, though isotopically limited, primarily trend toward EMI but include EMII-trending samples (Figs. 3 and 5). Notably, Cohen et al.48 reported a Lashaine garnet lherzolite with definitive EMII signatures: extreme 87Sr/86Sr ratios (~ 0.8360) and low 143Nd/144Nd (~ 0.51127; Table S7). These observations confirm EMII within the sub-continental lithospheric mantle beneath RCM and NTC.

Spatial variations of 143Nd/144Nd ratios with latitude for volcanics within the Kenya Rift. The data show that 143Nd/144Nd ratios decrease numerically from ~ 5°N (MB) to ~ 5°S (RCM/NTC). This latitudinal trend indicates an increasing influence of the lithospheric mantle component southward as the rift approaches the RCM and NTC domains. RCM/NTC and MB volcanics with > 5 wt% MgO were included to minimize crustal contamination effects, with all samples shown in Fig. S5.

Conversely, Suswa volcanics exhibit negative K anomalies (Fig. 2c). Although K-depletion is often attributed to residual K-bearing phases (e.g., amphibole), potentially formed during Pan-African subduction metasomatism17,47,49, this model fails to explain the concurrent positive Nb-Ta and negative K-Pb anomalies observed at Suswa (Fig. 2c), as subducted fluids or hydrous melts typically deplete Nb-Ta while enriching K-Pb50,51. Alternatively, this K-Pb-depleted signature may be intrinsic to the mantle source. The co-occurrence of K-Pb depletion and Nb-Ta enrichment is a hallmark signature of the global HIMU component, linked to deep-seated recycled carbonated oceanic crust20,52–55. The presence of carbonate-rich trachytes at Suswa29,31,56 supports this interpretation. Notably, the HIMU component is also present in MB volcanics. However, the lithospheric thickness beneath MB is too thin (< 70 km)57 to preserve ancient CO2-rich metasomatic veins25, implying a sublithospheric origin. As both MB and Suswa volcanics are products of the Kenya plume, we conclude the HIMU signature is plume-derived.

Contributions of mantle components.

To quantify source contributions, we employed a two-component mixing model between a plume end-member (comprising C modified by HIMU-derived melt) and a lithospheric mantle end-member (comprising EMII modified by HIMU-derived melt). This configuration accounts for the CO2-enrichment and lower solidus temperature of the HIMU component53,58, which facilitates its early melting and subsequent reaction with both the C component in the plume and the EMII component in the lithosphere. Mantle component compositions were defined as follows: Sr-Nd-Pb-Hf isotopic compositions for HIMU are from representative HIMU-OIBs (Mangaia volcanics), while the EMII compositions are from representative EMII-OIBs (Samoan volcanics). The C component’s Sr-Nd-Pb isotopes are from Hofmann20, with its Hf isotopes from Stracke et al.21. Trace element concentrations were estimated from Sr contents using Nd/Sr, Pb/Sr, and Hf/Sr ratios. For the HIMU-derived melt, Sr content and trace element ratios correspond to average values of the Mangaia volcanics. For EMII, Sr content was sourced from Jackson et al.46, and element ratios matched values from Samoan sample ALIA-D115-21 (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr ≈ 0.720546). Full parameters are detailed in Table S8. Results indicate the Suswa mantle source formed by mixing a plume component (95% C + 5% HIMU-derived melt) and a lithospheric mantle component (80% EMII + 20% HIMU-derived melt), with the lithospheric component contributing 10%~20% (Figs. 3 and 5).

Nature of the Kenya plume

Geophysical evidence confirms that the Kenya plume originates in the lower mantle14,15. However, the plume’s intrinsic nature remains ambiguous due to lithospheric overprinting on plume-derived mafic volcanics along the Kenya Rift. Mantle plumes sourced from the lower mantle typically carry the C component characterized by 143Nd/144Nd ≈ 0.512920,21. While this distinctive C component is clearly identified in depleted MB volcanics, it is largely absent in enriched RCM and NTC suites11,22–25. Instead, volcanics across MB, RCM and NTC converge on a common enriched component (143Nd/144Nd ≈ 0.51275) on Sr-Nd isotope diagrams4,17.

A systematic decrease in 143Nd/144Nd ratios in Kenya Rift mafic volcanics toward the Tanzania Craton (Figs. 6 and S5) demonstrates significant lithospheric contributions4,17,24,26,27. Consequently, enriched RCM and NTC volcanics are unsuitable for defining primary plume components due to substantial lithospheric input. The common enriched endmember identified in Sr-Nd isotopes likely represents a plume-lithosphere hybrid, as reinforced by the absence of convergence toward this endmember in Sr(Nd)-Pb isotope plots (Figs. 3e–f) and the EMI-EMII-HIMU-DMM tetrahedron (Fig. 5).

Our study reveals that Suswa volcanics possess depleted Nd-Hf isotopes and radiogenic Pb isotopic signatures, indicating a mantle source comprising plume-derived C and HIMU components, alongside lithosphere-derived EMII (Figs. 3 and 4). This coexistence of multiple components likely reflects plume-lithosphere interaction17. Critically, the identification of the C component in both Suswa and MB volcanics demonstrates its intrinsic presence in the Kenya plume, consistent with that in global mantle plumes. We propose that the enriched common component identified in Kenya Rift Sr-Nd isotopic systematics represents a lithosphere-contaminated hybrid. Significant incorporation of lithospheric materials obscures the plume-derived signatures, explaining the absence of the plume C component in typical RCM and NTC volcanics. This underscores the necessity of screening volcanic sequences for lithospheric overprinting as essential to resolve the compositions and dynamics of this continental mantle plume.

Conclusions

Based on whole-rock compositions of the Suswa volcanics (Kenya Rift), we conclude that:

(1) The Suswa volcanics are mantle-derived silicic rocks, evidenced by high SiO2 and LILEs concentrations, low MgO contents, and depleted mantle-like Nd-Hf isotopic signatures.

(2) Major and trace element systematics indicate significant crystal fractionation involving olivine, clinopyroxene, plagioclase, Ti oxides, and apatite.

(3) Low Ba/Nb ratios, constant Rb/Nb and La/Nb ratios, and variable 87Sr/86Sr ratios, combined with a negative correlation between 87Sr/86Sr and SiO2, preclude significant crustal contamination in most samples.

(4) Integrated Sr-Nd-Pb-Hf isotopes and trace element patterns reveal a heterogeneous mantle source comprising the plume-derived HIMU and C components alongside the lithosphere-derived EMII component. Identification of the C component in these depleted RCM volcanics demonstrates that the Kenya plume intrinsically contains this lower mantle-sourced signature, consistent with the signature of global mantle plumes.
