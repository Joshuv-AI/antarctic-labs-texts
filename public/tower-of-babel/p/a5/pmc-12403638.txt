Gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic immune dysfunction in critical ill patients with multidrug-resistant bacterial colonization and infection
Authors: Zongxin Ling, Wenwen Ding, Xia Liu, Jingchen Zhang, Yiwen Cheng, Zhangcheng Zhu, Lingbin Wu, Xiaocui Xu, Yongtao Gao, Ruilai Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a global health threat, particularly in critically ill patients with multidrug-resistant organism (MDRO) colonization or infection. While evidence suggests the gut microbiota plays a critical role in MDRO colonization and infection, its specific characteristics and the host immune response remain poorly understood.Methods and resultsThis case–control study compared 88 MDRO-infected patients, 100 MDRO-colonized patients, and 86 healthy controls, using 16S rRNA sequencing and cytokine profiling. MDRO cohorts exhibited profound gut dysbiosis, including reduced gut microbial diversity and distinct community structures, reduced beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Prevotella), and expansion of pathobionts—resident microbes with pathogenic potential (e.g., Enterococcus, Klebsiella, Escherichia-Shigella). Enterotype analysis revealed a shift from a Bacteroides-dominated to one Enterococcus-dominated microbiota in both colonized and infected patients compared to controls. Serum cytokine profiling indicated immune dysfunction in MDRO-associated patients. Correlation analysis showed that beneficial genera were negatively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1ra, IL-2, IL-7, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) and positively associated with anti-inflammatory markers, while pathobionts exhibited the opposite trend. Several key differential genera, such as Enterococcus and Klebsiella, either individually or in combination, have been identified as key discriminators of MDRO status. Functional predictions through PiCRUSt observed disruptions in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism in the MDRO cohorts.ConclusionOverall, MDRO colonization and infection lead to gut dysbiosis and immune dysfunction, with microbiota-immune interactions playing a crucial role in disease progression, suggesting the gut microbiota as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target for AMR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07049-2.Keywords: Multidrug-resistant organisms, Gut microbiota, Cytokines, Dysbiosis, Immune dysfunction

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most pressing global health challenges of the twenty-first century [1, 2]. Multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), especially those belonging to the ESKAPE group (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species), are rapidly spreading within healthcare settings, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs) [3]. These MDROs are associated with high mortality rates, extended hospital stays, and substantial economic burdens [1, 2]. In 2021, bacterial AMR directly caused 1.14 million deaths globally [4, 5], with the six leading pathogens accounting for 929,000 AMR-attributable deaths and 3.57 million AMR-associated deaths in 2019 [6]. Current interventions, including antibiotic stewardship and enhanced infection control, have proven insufficient to curb this escalating crisis [7]. Without innovative solutions, models predict AMR-related mortality could approach 1.91 million annually by 2050 [4, 5], underscoring the critical need for novel therapeutic and preventive strategies.

A primary driver of the AMR crisis is the overuse and misuse of antibiotics, which not only selects for resistant strains but also disrupts the host healthy microbiota [8–10]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining host health by providing colonization resistance (CR) against pathogens, modulating the immune system, and contributing to nutrient metabolism [11, 12]. In critically ill patients, the gut microbiota is particularly susceptible to dysbiosis due to factors such as severe illness, stress, mechanical ventilation, and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [13, 14]. This dysbiosis is marked by a loss of beneficial commensal bacteria and an overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens, leading to the breakdown of the gut mucosal barrier and the translocation of bacteria and their toxins into the bloodstream [15, 16]. This, in turn, can trigger a systemic inflammatory response and contribute to the development of sepsis and multi-organ failure. Recent research has demonstrated that restoration of the gut microbiota with fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can re-establish CR [17], which can be used to eradicate MDROs colonization and possibly prevent recurrent MDROs infections [18]. This success highlights the critical importance of the synergistic interactions among the diverse microbial components of the gut microbiota in maintaining robust and enduring CR. Nevertheless, despite these promising developments, the distinct microbial signatures and immune responses associated with MDROs colonization versus active infection remain largely uncharacterized, limiting our ability to develop microbiota-targeted interventions.

To address knowledge gaps, we designed a comprehensive case–control study involving three distinct cohorts: 88 MDRO-infected patients (MI), 100 asymptomatic MDRO-colonized patients (MC), and 86 demographically matched healthy controls (HC). This study aims to systematically investigate the relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic immune dysfunction in critically ill patients with MDROs colonization and infection. By comparing the gut microbiota composition and immune profiles in this cohort, we can identify specific gut microbial taxa and metabolic pathways linked to resistance to MDROs colonization or susceptibility to infection, characterize host immune profiles across various clinical conditions, and investigate disruptions in the microbiota-immune network that may predict the progression from colonization to invasive infection. The findings will provide crucial insights for developing novel diagnostic biomarkers and microbiota-targeted strategies to combat MDROs colonization and infection.

Materials and methods

Participants’ enrollment

In our present prospective observational study, we recruited 88 MI patients, 100 MC patients from the Department of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China between April and December 2024. Additionally, 86 age- and gender-matched healthy individuals, free from MDROs colonization or infection, were enrolled as HCs. The enrollment process followed international consensus criteria for MDRO surveillance [19]. Clinical specimens, including feces, blood, and nasal swabs, were collected for MDROs analysis. MDRO identification employed a combination of phenotypic and genotypic methods. Phenotypic analysis utilized the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux) for automated identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing based on minimum inhibitory concentration, following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines. Genotypic analysis was performed using real-time PCR assays to target specific resistance genes [20]. All testing was conducted in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory, ensuring quality control through the inclusion of ATCC reference strains in each testing batch. MI patients were classified according to the CDC criteria, which included the presence of MDROs and at least two of the following: fever > 38 °C, abnormal WBC count, and local inflammation. MC patients had positive MDRO detection without clinical infection [21]. HCs were screened through telephone and in-person evaluations, with exclusion of those with recent hospitalization, antibiotic use, or confirmed MDRO exposure. Exclusion criteria included any type of tumor; use of probiotics, prebiotics, or synbiotics in the previous month; and pre-existing conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, or other autoimmune diseases. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (reference no.: IIT20230479B), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal guardian, in compliance with the 2024 revision of the Declaration of Helsinki [22].

Sample collection

Fecal and serum samples were collected following standardized protocols to ensure sample integrity and consistency. For fecal samples, approximately 2 g of a fresh fecal sample was collected in a sterile plastic cup, and stored at − 80 °C after preparation within 15 min until use. Serum samples were obtained from fasting participants in the early morning. Trained phlebotomists used sterile vacutainer systems to collect 5 mL of whole blood into serum separator tubes. Following 30 min of clotting at room temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min. The resulting serum was aliquoted into 1 mL sterile cryovials and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Library preparation and sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from 300 mg of homogenized feces using a QIAamp® DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with additional glass-bead beating steps on a Mini-beadbeater (FastPrep; Thermo Electron Corporation, Boston, MA, USA) [23, 24]. DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Electron Corporation). Amplicon libraries were constructed using Illumina-compatible primers 341F/785R, targeting the V3-V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene [23–28]. PCR amplification was carried out with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix, using 50 ng of DNA per reaction. The thermocycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Reactions were performed in triplicates and combined after PCR. Library preparation was done using the TruSeq™ DNA Sample Preparation Kit, and PCR products were extracted with the MiniElute® Gel Extraction Kit. The purified amplicons were quantified using both NanoDrop and Qubit, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and sequenced on a NovaSeq™ 6000 system (Illumina). Negative DNA extraction controls (lysis buffer and kit reagents only) were amplified and sequenced as contamination controls. Library construction and sequencing were carried out at Hangzhou KaiTai Bio-lab.

Data processing

Raw sequencing data (≥ 100 bp, error rate < 1%) were initially processed using QIIME2 (Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2, v2020.11; http://qiime.org/) with default parameters [23–27, 29–31]. Adapter sequences were removed using Cutadapt v2.4, while low-quality, short (< 100 bp), and chimeric reads were filtered out using the fastq_filter module in VSEARCH v2.13.6 [32]. Following quality control, samples were normalized to ensure uniform sampling depth, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified using the DADA2 denoising pipeline. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using the SILVA reference database (Release 138, http://www.arb-silva.de) [33, 34]. ASVs that contributed fewer than 0.005% of total sequences were excluded, in line with best practices [35]. Additionally, sequences assigned to Eukarya, Archaea, Mitochondria, Chloroplasts, and Cyanobacteria lineages were removed to ensure that only bacterial sequences remained for downstream analysis. The resulting ASV table was utilized for further microbiome analysis. Bacterial α-diversity was assessed using the Shannon and Simpson indices, along with richness indices including ACE, Chao1, and observed species, all calculated at 97% sequence similarity. For β-diversity analysis, Bray–Curtis, Jaccard, unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance metrics were computed. These β-diversity distances were visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) [36]. Samples with taxa abundance present in fewer than 50% of the cases were excluded from the analyses. Enterotype classification was performed to group samples based on microbiome similarity, using genus-level abundance data that had been rarefied. This analysis was conducted using the OECloud tools (https://cloud.oebiotech.com). Variations in microbiota composition across samples were analyzed using the Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles (STAMP) software package (v2.1.3) [37] and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) [38]. Focus was placed on taxa with an average relative abundance > 0.01% for biomarker discovery, with an LDA score threshold of > 3.0 and a significance level of p < 0.05. Correlation analysis at the genus level was conducted using the sparse compositional correlation (SparCC) algorithm, applied to the full ASV table. Network visualizations of these correlations were generated using Cytoscape v3.6.1 [39]. To explore functional potential, predictive analysis of microbial community functions was performed using PiCRUSt version 1.0.0, which compares ASV-derived functional predictions to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Microbial functions were categorized according to KEGG pathways at levels 1–3, and comparisons across patient groups were made using STAMP [40, 41].

Systemic inflammatory cytokines analysis

The Bio-Plex Pro Human Cytokine 27-plex assay kit (M500KCAF0Y, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was employed to measure immune markers in the serum, adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions [23–27, 42]. This multiplex assay utilizes Luminex® xMAP® technology, allowing the simultaneous measurement of 27 immune markers, including 16 cytokines, 6 chemokines, and 5 growth factors. The analysis was performed on the Luminex® 200™ system (Bio-Rad), where fluorescence values corresponding to each analyte were recorded. Serum samples were diluted 1:4 with sample diluent buffer, and standard curves were established using known assay standards. Data were acquired with the Bio-Plex Array Reader 2200, with cytokine concentrations expressed in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL), based on the standard curves. Data analysis was conducted using Bio-Plex Manager v5.0 software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) ranged from 5 to 8%, indicating consistency across assays. The assay’s detection limits for each cytokine, as specified by the manufacturer, generally fall within the pg/mL range. To ensure the quality and accuracy of the results, rigorous quality control (QC) measures were employed, including validation of standard curves, checks for dynamic range, and the inclusion of both positive and negative controls to confirm assay performance and specificity. Samples with values that were outliers were flagged for further evaluation, and cytokine concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD) were recorded as half of the LOD to mitigate bias. These QC protocols ensured the reliability of the data throughout the analysis process.

Statistical analysis

Appropriate statistical tests were performed based on the type of data being analyzed. For continuous variables, including α-diversity indices, taxonomic abundance, and cytokine levels, statistical comparisons were made using White’s nonparametric t-test, independent t-test, or the Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To investigate the relationships between microbial abundances, and cytokine levels, Spearman’s rank correlation was employed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and STAMP v2.1.3 software, with graphical representations created using R packages and GraphPad Prism v6.0. For evaluating the discriminative power of key functional taxa, random forest classification was applied, with variable importance determined by Mean Decrease Gini. A higher value of mean decrease Gini indicates that the variable (genera) is more important. The predictive performance of the model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) analysis, which were conducted through OECloud tools. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). An FDR threshold of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Accession number

The sequence data from this study are deposited in the GenBank Sequence Read Archive with the accession number PRJNA1265392 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1265392).

Results

Altered fecal microbiota structure in MDRO-positive patients

In all validated cases of MDRO colonization and infection, the associated illnesses remained effectively controlled during the 30-day observation period preceding the study. Demographic and clinical data, including age, weight, and body mass index, were extracted from hospital medical records for analysis (Table 1). Statistical comparisons showed no significant differences in these baseline characteristics between HC group and MC or MI patients (p > 0.05). Notably, male and female participants were analyzed collectively, as sex ratio comparisons across cohorts yielded no significant disparities: HC (32 female / 54 male), MC (34 female / 66 male), and MI (23 female / 65 male) (all intergroup p > 0.05). Phenotypic characterization identified multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii as the predominant pathogens in our cohort, with comparable prevalence rates observed in both MC and MI groups (p > 0.05). Prior to sample collection, most patients in both groups (MC: 85.0% vs MI: 87.5%, p = 0.67) had received empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy (median duration: 2.5 days, IQR: 1–3 days) during the first 72 h of ICU admission, with comparable antibiotic regimens administered between groups (p > 0.05 for all comparisons), thereby minimizing confounding effects of antimicrobial pressure on microbiota profiles. However, MDRO-positive patients exhibited distinct inflammatory and hematologic profiles: elevated body temperature, white blood cell counts, neutrophil counts, and erythrocyte sedimentation rates (p < 0.05), accompanied by reduced lymphocyte counts compared to HCs (p < 0.05).

A total of 13,247,804 high-quality sequencing reads were obtained, including 5,444,092 reads from HCs, 3,931,442 from MC patients, and 3,872,270 from MI patients, with an average of 48,350 reads per sample. For downstream analyses, data were rarefied to 20,667 reads per sample to ensure even sequencing depth. This normalization identified 9,854 bacterial ASVs across all groups, with a Good’s coverage estimate of 99.93%, confirming near-complete representation of the fecal microbiota. Bacterial α-diversity metrics, including Shannon and Simpson indices, revealed significantly reduced microbial diversity in MDRO-positive patients compared to HCs (p < 0.01; Fig. 1A–B), with no notable differences between MC and MI groups. Similarly, richness metrics (ACE, Chao1, and observed species) were also markedly lower in MDRO- positive patients (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C–E), indicating a loss of bacterial taxa. PCoA based on Bray–Curtis, Jaccard, and UniFrac (unweighted/weighted) distances showed clear separation between HCs and MDRO-positive patients (Adonis test: p < 0.01; Fig. 1F–I), highlighting distinct microbial community structures. A Venn diagram further illustrated that only 923 ASVs (9.4% of the total) were shared among all three groups, while 2,542, 1,616, and 3,895 ASVs were unique to MC, MI, and HC groups, respectively (Fig. 1J). This pattern suggests that MDRO dynamics drive the loss of specific bacterial phylotypes. Notably, HCs harbored a significantly higher proportion of low-abundance ASVs, whereas MI patients exhibited the lowest ASV richness (Fig. 1K), indicating a more complex and stable microbiota in controls. Collectively, these α- and β-diversity analyses reveal profound alterations in the fecal microbiota of MDRO-positive patients, characterized by reduced diversity, loss of rare taxa, and distinct community clustering compared to HCs.

Altered fecal microbiota composition in MDRO-positive patients

Through comprehensive taxonomic profiling of the fecal microbiota, we identified a total of 24 bacterial phyla, 197 families, and 526 genera across all study cohorts. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the microbial landscape was dominated by six phyla—Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Fusobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota—which collectively accounted for over 99.5% of all sequenced taxa (Fig. 2A). Family-level analysis revealed that Bacteroidaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Enterococcaceae were the most abundant families (Fig. 2B). Genus-level profiling identified Bacteroides, Enterococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Prevotella_9, Bifidobacterium, and Klebsiella as the predominant genera (Fig. 2C). Further, the fecal microbiota was categorized into two enterotypes based on genus-level composition, utilizing established methodologies [43]. These enterotypes were defined as Enterococcus-dominated (E1) and Bacteroides-dominated (E2) (Fig. 2D). Figure 2E demonstrates the relative abundance of the dominant genera within the two enterotypes, highlighting the microbiome’s variability and its potential association with MDROs. In HC group, 64.0% of samples (55/86) were classified as E1, while 74.0% (74/100) of the MC group and 84.1% (74/88) of the MI group also fell into the E1 category (Fig. 2F). This shift demonstrates a significant alteration in microbiota composition following MDRO colonization and infection.

The LEfSe analysis revealed significant taxonomic differences between MDRO patients and HCs across multiple phylogenetic levels (Fig. 3). The cladogram illustrates the phylogenetic distribution of differentially abundant taxa, with colored nodes representing taxa showing significant variation (LDA score > 3.0, p < 0.05; Fig. 3A). The radial layout emphasizes taxonomic relationships, from phylum to genus levels. To ensure robust biomarker identification, we applied a stringent relative abundance threshold of 0.01%. The magnitude and direction of these microbial alterations were subsequently quantified in Fig. 3B, which presents the LDA effect sizes for the most distinguishing taxonomic features. HCs displayed a microbiota enriched with beneficial taxa, including butyrate-producing species such as Blautia, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia, lactate-producing Bifidobacterium, and commensals like Prevotella and Bacteroides. These taxa are typically associated with a healthy gut ecosystem, contributing to host health and homeostasis. In contrast, MC individuals showed a selective expansion of Parabacteroides, Klebsiella and Clostridioides, suggesting a shift toward a potentially pathogenic microbial environment without full infection [44]. Upon progression to infection, we observed a marked increase in pathobionts—resident microbes with pathogenic potential like Enterococcus, Klebsiella, and Corynebacterium—indicating a disruption in microbial balance and the onset of dysbiosis [45]. Comparative biomarker analysis highlighted functional divergences in MDRO dynamics, with distinct shifts in pathobionts differentiating colonized from infected states (Fig. 3, Figure S1-S2). In MC patients, the microbial signature was enriched in Escherichia-Shigella, Pelomonas, and Eubacterium_coprostanoligenes_group, while MI individuals displayed significant increases in Pseudomonas, Providencia, and Klebsiella (Figure S3). These shifts point to a progressive disruption of the normal microbiota, facilitating the dominance of pathogenic organisms. Phylogenetic cladogram analysis further emphasized this dysbiosis, illustrating a clear trajectory of microbial changes associated with MDROs, marking the transition from colonization to infection.

Moreover, MetaStats 2.0 analysis revealed significant differences in fecal microbiota between MDRO-positive patients and HCs at the phylum, family, and genus levels. Specifically, MDRO patients exhibited a decrease in Bacteroidota and an increase in Proteobacteria at the phylum level (Fig. 4A). At the family level, eight commensal families, including Bacteroidaceae and Lachnospiraceae, were reduced in MDRO patients (p < 0.05; Fig. 4B), while pathobiont-associated families such as Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae were enriched. Genus-level analysis revealed a loss of beneficial commensals, including Bacteroides, Prevotella_9, Faecalibacterium, Oscillospiraceae_UCG-002, Roseburia, Megasphaera, Sutterella, Agathobacter, Anaerostipes, Eubacterium_hallii_group, Eubacterium_eligens_group, and Coprococcus. Conversely, pathogenic genera such as Enterococcus, Parabacteroides, Klebsiella, and Clostridioides were significantly enriched in the clinical cohort (p < 0.05; Fig. 4C). Notably, Bacteroides, Alistipes, and Lactobacillus were significantly reduced in MI patients compared to HCs. This dysbiosis pattern, marked by the loss of SCFAs-producing bacteria and the expansion of antimicrobial-resistant pathobionts, reflects a microbial shift closely associated with MDRO colonization and infection, with the most severe changes observed in MI individuals.

The structure of the fecal microbiota is shaped by dynamic interactions within its community members. To characterize these ecological interactions, we used SparCC correlation networks with FDR adjustment to analyze ASV co-occurrence patterns (Fig. 5). The analysis revealed that microbial interaction networks simplify following MDRO colonization or infection: healthy controls exhibited the most complex network, characterized by numerous significant positive and negative correlations, while both MC and MI individuals showed reduced connectivity. Notably, the network complexity of MC and MI was largely comparable, collectively indicating a systematic deterioration of microbial community stability and potential loss of functional redundancy upon MDRO colonization or infection.

We employed Random Forest analysis to assess the potential of key functional bacterial taxa in distinguishing patients associated with MDRO from HCs. The results revealed that the Mean Decrease Gini values for Enterococcus, Klebsiella, Roseburia, Pseudomonas, and Bacteroides were significantly higher, indicating the substantial contribution of these genera in predicting MDRO infection (Fig. 6A). Notably, each of these genera exhibited moderate diagnostic performance (AUC >0.70) in distinguishing MI individuals from healthy controls. Among these genera, Enterococcus demonstrated the highest discriminative ability (AUC = 0.82), followed by Klebsiella and Faecalibacterium (AUC = 0.79). When combined into an integrated model, these genera displayed a marked improvement in predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.89 ± 0.08 (Fig. 6B). Similarly, these genera also effectively discriminated MC patients from healthy controls, with an AUC of 0.83 ± 0.13 (Figure S4). Furthermore, the combination of six differential genera—Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Pelomonas, Eubacterium coprostanoligenes group, Pseudomonas, Lactobacillus, and Providencia—enabled the distinction between MI patients and MC individuals (AUC = 0.78 ± 0.10; Fig. 6C). This integrated microbial signature exhibited superior discriminative capacity, facilitating patient stratification based on pathogenic enrichment patterns. These findings position fecal microbiota profiling as a highly sensitive, non-invasive diagnostic tool for AMR surveillance, offering a promising approach for early detection and patient classification.

Predicted functions of the fecal microbiota associated with MDRO

PiCRUSt was utilized to predict the abundances of functional categories based on KEGG orthologs (KO), utilizing closed-reference ASVs to identify metabolic and functional alterations in the fecal microbiota of MDRO-associated patients compared to HCs. Similar patterns of functional alterations were observed in both MC and MC cohorts (Fig. 7 and Figure S5). At KEGG level 2, 9 out of 64 pathways exhibited differential abundance between MC patients and HCs, with the number increasing to 10 pathways in the MI group. In MC patients, pathways involved in carbohydrate metabolism, membrane transport, lipid metabolism, and xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism were significantly enriched, while those associated with amino acid metabolism, cofactors and vitamins metabolism, transcription, and energy metabolism, were notably reduced. In contrast, in MI patients, the abundance of pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, membrane transport, xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism, and lipid metabolism was upregulated, while pathways linked to amino acid metabolism, metabolisms of cofactors and vitamins, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, transcription, energy metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism were significantly downregulated. At KEGG level 3, several differentially abundant pathways were identified in the fecal microbiota associated with MDROs. Specifically, 19 pathways, including the phosphotransferase system (PTS), lipoic acid metabolism, and fructose and mannose metabolism, were more active in the microbiota of MC patients, while 16 pathways, such as thiamine metabolism, bacterial chemotaxis, and histidine metabolism, showed significant reductions. In MI patients, 19 pathways, including PTS, drug metabolism by other enzymes, and fructose and mannose metabolism, displayed increased activity, while 15 pathways, including bacterial chemotaxis, thiamine metabolism, and flagellar assembly, were significantly decreased. These findings provide evidence that MDRO colonization and infection disrupt the functional profiles of the gut microbiota, altering key metabolic pathways that may contribute to the persistence and virulence of MDROs.

Correlations between differential genera and host cytokines

Our cytokine profiling revealed significant immune dysregulation in MI and MC patients compared to HCs. Out of the 27 cytokines analyzed, nine pro-inflammatory mediators—IL-1ra, IL-2, IL-7, TNF-α, IFN-γ, Eotaxin, IP-10, MIP-1β, and RANTES—were significantly elevated (Fig. 8; p < 0.01). In contrast, levels of IL-9 and PDGF-bb were notably reduced. This bidirectional shift in inflammatory regulators and chemokines highlights a significant imbalance in immune homeostasis associated with MDRO colonization and infection. Spearman’s correlation analysis, which examined the relationship between differential gut microbiota composition and cytokine alterations (Fig. 9), revealed that beneficial genera, including Bacteroides, Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Blautia, Roseburia, Collinsella, and Oscillospiraceae_UCG-002, exhibited an inverse correlation with elevated cytokine levels. These genera were positively associated with reduced cytokine concentrations, suggesting their role in promoting immune regulation and maintaining balance. In contrast, pathobionts that are enriched in MDRO patients, such as Enterococcus and Klebsiella, displayed a distinct and opposite correlation pattern, being positively linked to higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and negatively associated with lower cytokine levels. These findings suggest that the remodeling of the gut microbiota, particularly the shift from beneficial to pathogenic genera, plays a critical role in modulating immune responses. Such microbiota changes, coupled with immune disturbances, could significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of MDRO infections, providing valuable insights into how shifts in microbiota composition may either exacerbate or mitigate the severity of these infections.

Discussion

The rise of MDROs represents a global health crisis, challenging conventional infection control and treatment paradigms. Among these pathogens, the ESKAPE group—known for their ability to “escape” antibiotics—employs diverse survival strategies, such as drug inactivation and genetic adaptation, making infections harder to treat [46, 47]. Clinically, MDRO colonization often precedes severe infections, particularly in vulnerable patients such as immunocompromised individuals and ICU patients, where frequent antibiotic use and medical interventions increase transmission risks [48–50]. Alarmingly, these resistant pathogens are no longer confined to hospitals, as community-acquired cases further complicate containment efforts, highlighting the urgent need for new strategies to prevent colonization from progressing to active infection [51]. Central to this challenge is the gut microbiota’s role in CR. A healthy microbiome competitively excludes pathogens via niche occupation, antimicrobial production, and immune modulation [9, 11, 52]. However, dysbiosis—driven by antibiotics or critical illness—compromises these defenses, enabling MDRO expansion and systemic dissemination [13, 53–55]. This breakdown in microbial balance, coupled with immune dysfunction, creates a vicious cycle that sustains MDRO persistence, cementing the gut as a critical battleground in the global fight against antimicrobial resistance.

In the present study, we identified significant alterations in the gut microbiota of MDRO-positive patients compared to healthy controls. Notably, MDRO-positive patients exhibited reduced α-diversity and distinct β-diversity patterns, indicative of microbial dysbiosis. Reduced α-diversity, as measured by Shannon and Simpson indices, is generally linked to poorer health outcomes [56], as it compromises CR—a key defense mechanism against pathogenic invasion. This dysbiotic state facilitates MDRO proliferation by depleting protective commensal anaerobes, which normally compete for nutrients and mucosal adhesion sites, while also diminishing antimicrobial metabolite production and impairing immune regulation [57]. Furthermore, interspecies interactions within the gut microbiota may critically influence infection susceptibility, as microbial cooperation or competition shapes the overall ecological dynamics. Beyond diversity shifts, we observed a compositional reorganization of the gut microbiota, characterized by a transition from a Bacteroides-dominant to an Enterococcus-dominant enterotype, correlating with MDRO status. This shift suggests a potential microbiome disruption index that may serve as a biomarker for dysbiosis [58]. Of particular concern was the significant depletion of beneficial commensals in MDRO-positive patients, including Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, and Bifidobacterium—genera essential for maintaining gut homeostasis. These genera contribute to gut barrier integrity through the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites like SCFAs and by modulating immune responses [59–64]. Their decline weakens mucosal defenses, creating an environment permissive to MDRO colonization [61, 63]. Supporting this, Yip et al. demonstrated that gut microbiota derived SCFAs effectively inhibit carbapenem-resistant E. coli in murine model of intestinal CRE colonization [60]. Similarly, antibiotic-induced depletion of Clostridia—key butyrate producers—has been linked to impaired epithelial oxygenation and heightened susceptibility to Salmonella and Listeria [65], while Lactobacillus promote the expansion of Clostridiales after antibiotic treatment to restrict gut colonization by multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae [66]. Faecalibacterium, a major butyrogenic genus, reinforces epithelial tight junctions [67] and promotes regulatory T-cells (Tregs) differentiation [68], thereby mitigating excessive inflammation. Beyond immunomodulation, Faecalibacterium exhibits direct antagonism against Enterobacteriaceae pathogens like E. coli and K. pneumoniae. This inhibition is likely mediated by SCFAs, which lower luminal pH to restrict facultative anaerobes and disrupt quorum sensing pathways critical for pathogen virulence [69]. Recent findings by Yin et al. revealed a reciprocal exclusion pattern between Faecalibacterium and Enterobacteriaceae in healthy gut microbiota, mediated through competitive mechanisms including SCFA production, iron acquisition, and quorum sensing modulation [70]. However, our co-occurrence network analysis in MI cohort failed to replicate this antagonistic relationship, suggesting MDRO infection disrupts this ecological balance, possibly impairing the competitive role of Faecalibacterium. Clinically, Faecalibacterium has emerged as a diagnostic biomarker for gut health. He et al. identified its depletion in E. coli-infected ecosystems, with low abundance correlating with impaired CR and increased infection severity [71]. Similarly, Roseburia, another key butyrogenic commensal, enhances CR through multiple mechanisms, including butyrate-mediated maintenance of luminal hypoxia [72], enhancement of epithelial barrier function, and suppression of bacterial translocation [73]. Studies have linked Roseburia depletion to increased susceptibility to enteric infections. Dong et al. reported reduced abundances of Faecalibacterium and Roseburia in C. difficile-positive samples compared to controls, alongside expanded Clostridium cluster XIVa species, highlighting the protective role of butyrate-producing taxa in resisting pathogenic invasion [74]. In the treatment of recurrent C. difficile infection with FMT, donor feces with high Roseburia abundance correlate strongly with successful clearance of resistant bacteria in recipients [75]. Bacteroides species may also confer protection against MDROs, though their precise mechanisms remain incompletely characterized [64]. High abundance of Bacteroides is frequently associated with specific metabolic functions, favorable health outcomes, and responsiveness to certain medical interventions [76]. For example, SCFAs produced by Bacteroides promote epithelial cell integrity and enhance intestinal barrier function, thereby strengthening mucosal immunity and impeding pathogen adhesion to the gut epithelium. These metabolites also modulate immune cell function, such as promoting Tregs differentiation and suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB signaling), which are essential for maintaining immune homeostasis [77, 78]. Notably, Bacteroides contribute to mucosal defense through direct interactions with the intestinal mucus layer. By binding to mucin O-glycans, they form a physical barrier that restricts pathogen access to epithelial surfaces, a mechanism critical for preventing translocation of MDROs like Enterococcus and Klebsiella [79]. This protective role is further supported by our study’s finding of a significant inverse correlation between Bacteroides depletion and elevated serum TNF-α levels, suggesting that reduced Bacteroides abundance impairs immunomodulatory capacity and exacerbates systemic inflammation in MDRO-positive patients. The loss of such keystone species likely compromises mucosal immunity and reduces CR against MDROs.

Conversely, we identified a marked enrichment of opportunistic pathogens in MDRO-positive patients, such as Enterococcus and Klebsiella. These pathobionts exhibit intrinsic multidrug resistance and thrive in dysbiotic environments where they exploit compromised CR and impaired host immunity. Previous study has observed that a predominance of Enterococcus, and Klebsiella, linked to lowered gut diversity, is associated with patients suffering from recurrent C. difficile infections [80]. Our findings align with those of Araos et al., who reported higher abundances of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus spp. in MDRO carriers, whereas Bacteroidales and Lactobacillus spp. were more prevalent in non-carriers [81]. Notably, intestinal dominance of Enterococcus spp. in ICU patients has been previously identified as a risk factor for infection and mortality upon admission [55, 82]. This expansion is associated with ICU hospitalization and antibiotic exposure [83]. The expansion of Enterococcus spp. may further exacerbate pathogenicity by modulating gut metabolism to support other pathogens. For example, Enterococcus can enhance C. difficile virulence by releasing amino acids like leucine and ornithine, creating a metabolic microenvironment conducive to toxin production [84]. Mechanistically, within-host evolution of gut pathobionts like Enterococcus gallinarum promotes bacterial translocation across the intestinal barrier, triggering systemic inflammation—consistent with the elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels observed in our MDRO cohort [85]. However, dietary interventions, such as lactose depletion, can suppress Enterococcus overgrowth, as demonstrated by reduced graft-versus-host disease severity in murine models [86]. A significant expansion of Enterococcus was observed in our MDRO-positive patients, which correlated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This finding aligns with the proposed role of Enterococcus in driving microbiota dysbiosis and immune dysfunction, suggesting that targeted dietary interventions could be explored as adjunctive therapies to restore microbial balance in MDRO-positive individuals [87]. FMT has emerged as a promising strategy to restore CR against Enterococcus. In our cohort, the depletion of beneficial taxa such as Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium, coupled with the simplification of microbial interaction networks, highlight the potential of FMT to reintroduce competitive commensals and rebuild ecological complexity within the gut microbiota. Supporting this, a pilot clinical study demonstrated sustained efficacy of FMT in vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) decolonization, with a 50% success rate persisting beyond six months and no reported adverse events [88]. Our functional predictions further reinforce this approach: disrupted metabolic pathways (e.g., carbohydrate and lipid metabolism) in MDRO patients could be restored through FMT-mediated microbial modulation. Murine studies have similarly shown that FMT can effectively reinstate CR and reduce intestinal VRE burden. Additionally, recent research has identified lantibiotic produced by Blautia producta BPSCSK, which selectively targets VRE while preserving beneficial microbiota [52]. This is particularly relevant to our results, as Blautia was significantly depleted in MDRO patients, and its loss may contribute to the unchecked expansion of Enterococcus and other pathobionts. Beyond Enterococcus, Klebsiella—a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family—poses substantial public health threats, particularly through multidrug-resistant hypervirulent (MDR-Hv) K. pneumoniae strains [89]. In our study, Klebsiella was enriched in MDRO-positive patients and strongly correlated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, further underscoring its role in driving both dysbiosis and systemic inflammation. As a prototypical member of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), K. pneumoniae harbors carbapenemases (e.g., KPC, OXA-48, NDM), conferring resistance to last-line antimicrobial therapies and exacerbating treatment complexities in critically ill patients. Our data suggest that functional disruptions in the gut microbiota may facilitate Klebsiella persistence by altering nutrient availability and metabolic competition. Dysregulation of the gut microbiota has emerged as a central driver of CRE dynamics, with shared ecological mechanisms underlying colonization by diverse MDROs. For example, gut microbiota depleted in Bacteroides and enriched in Klebsiella or Parasutterella have been observed in individuals colonized with methicillin-resistant S. aureus, suggesting conserved pathways of microbial dysbiosis that promote pathogen establishment across Gram-positive and Gram-negative taxa [74]. In the context of CRE, the gut microbiota’s role in colonization and decolonization is increasingly evident [90]. Lee et al. demonstrated that successful CRE decolonization via FMT correlates with low baseline Klebsiella abundance and high Bacteroidetes richness [91], a pattern consistent with our observations in healthy controls. Seong et al. further reported that FMT accelerates clearance of NDM-producing K. pneumoniae [92], suggesting that restoring microbial diversity and function could help eliminate MDROs in patients. The therapeutic efficacy of FMT against K. pneumoniae is mediated by multiple interconnected mechanisms, including microbial competition and phage predation. Liu et al. identified an inverse relationship between Klebsiella colonization density and Klebsiella-specific bacteriophages in FMT responders [93], suggesting that phage dynamics could be leveraged to target MDROs in patients with dysbiosis. Additionally, non-pathogenic commensals such as K. oxytoca employ sophisticated nutritional competition strategies to outcompete K. pneumoniae. These protective strains utilize CasA-mediated β-glucoside metabolism and metabolic flexibility to utilize alternative carbon sources, thereby dominating the gut ecological niche and depriving pathogenic Klebsiella of essential nutrients [17]. This form of ecological competition underscores the gut microbiome’s inherent capacity for self-regulation when beneficial commensal populations are maintained or restored.

Beyond the significant changes observed in the gut microbiota of MDRO-positive patients, we also found notable alterations in the predicted functional pathways such as PTS, fructose and mannose metabolism, bacterial chemotaxis, and thiamine metabolism. These findings further suggest that the gut microbiota may play a crucial role in MDRO colonization and infection. Recent studies provide additional support for this hypothesis. For instance, Araos et al. demonstrated through metagenomic predictions that MDRO-positive patients exhibited a higher abundance of genes related to multidrug resistance pathways, such as the two-component system, the ATP-binding cassette system, and PTS. Additionally, MDRO-positive patients showed an increased abundance of genes involved in amino acid and nucleotide metabolism [81], which could be used as potential biomarkers of MDRO colonization. Research on Salmonella Typhimurium has shown that the utilization of monosaccharides, including fructose and mannose, is essential for pathogen survival and colonization in the gut. Mutants lacking enzymes involved in these metabolic pathways displayed reduced colonization [94]. Similarly, Enterococcus faecalis has been shown to rely on fructose and mannose metabolism, as well as the PTS, for survival within host cells, indicating these pathways are crucial for host-pathogen interactions [95]. Additionally, alterations in mannose metabolism have been observed in inflammatory bowel disease, with increased serum mannose concentrations and changes in key enzymes, suggesting a link between microbial metabolism and gut inflammation [96]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that microbial carbohydrate metabolism, including pathways such as PTS and fructose and mannose metabolism, may influence host metabolic health and insulin resistance [95, 97]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of metabolic pathways in the gut microbiota and their potential role in MDRO colonization and infection, reinforcing the idea that gut microbiota functions contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of MDROs.

The gut microbiota-immune axis is central to the pathogenesis of MDRO colonization and infection, with bidirectional interactions driving disease progression [98]. A balanced microbiota normally promotes tolerogenic immune responses, maintaining mucosal homeostasis and preventing excessive inflammation [9]. However, our cytokine profiling of MDRO-positive patients revealed chronic inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-1ra, TNF-α, IFN-γ, Eotaxin) [99]. While this may reflect the general inflammatory response associated with critical illness, bacterial infections—especially those involving MDROs—are likely the primary drivers of the observed inflammation. This inflammation strongly correlates with depletion of beneficial taxa (e.g., Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium) and overgrowth of pathobionts (e.g., Enterococcus, Klebsiella). This dysregulation reflects reduced activity of SCFA-producing commensals, critical for Tregs differentiation and intestinal barrier integrity [100]. For example, exogenous butyrate supplementation in antibiotic-treated mice increases splenic and lymphoid Foxp3+ Tregs [101], while Bacteroides fragilis induces IL-10 via TLR2 activation to suppress colitis [102]. Akkermansia muciniphila enhances mucus layer thickness and reduces LPS translocation through TLR2-mediated immune regulation [103], and Bifidobacterium infantum activates pattern recognition receptors to trigger antimicrobial peptide and cytokine release [104]. Conversely, pathobionts disrupt this balance: Enterococcus and Klebsiella-derived LPS activate TLR4/MyD88 signaling, driving excessive IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ production [105], while Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus) induce inflammation via TLR2 recognition of peptidoglycan and lipophosphatidylic acid [106]. Some pathogens, like C. difficile, exacerbate inflammation through toxins (TcdA/TcdB) that damage intestinal epithelium and recruit neutrophils via IL-8 release [107]. This inflammatory milieu impairs epithelial integrity, facilitates bacterial translocation, and creates a permissive niche for MDRO persistence. Given the critical role of gut microbiota in MDRO colonization and infection, microbiota-targeted therapies offer promising solutions: FMT restores CR and immune balance [108], probiotics (e.g., lactic acid bacteria) enhance SCFA production and immune responses [109, 110], prebiotics and postbiotics regulate microbiota composition to inhibit pathobionts [9], and phage-antibiotic combinations enable targeted MDRO eradication while preserving beneficial microbes [111]. These approaches, alone or in combination, could revolutionize the management of MDRO infections by addressing the root cause of dysbiosis rather than merely suppressing bacterial growth. Although the clinical implications of these therapies are promising, their feasibility and safety—particularly in critically ill patients—must be thoroughly evaluated through well-designed randomized controlled trials. In addition, future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms of microbiota-host interactions, particularly how specific bacterial genera influence immune responses. This mechanistic understanding will be crucial in refining microbiota-targeted therapies and ensuring their successful integration into MDRO treatment regimens.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, the observational nature of the research precludes definitive conclusions about causality. Longitudinal studies tracking microbiota changes before, during, and after MDRO colonization are needed to establish temporal relationships. Second, while PiCRUSt suggested potential metabolic disruptions (e.g., SCFA depletion), these findings require validation through multi-omics approaches. Future studies should combine metagenomics and metabolomics to verify these mechanisms and clarify whether microbiota changes drive or result from MDRO colonization. Third, the study population was limited to a single center, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Variations in diet, antibiotic use, and underlying comorbidities could influence microbiota composition in ways not captured here. Fourth, while we identified correlations between specific bacterial taxa and immune markers, mechanistic studies using gnotobiotic animal models or in vitro systems are required to validate these interactions. Finally, fecal microbial signatures and non-invasive diagnostic models require validation in larger cohorts, with metabolomic analyses to link compositional shifts to functional pathways.

In summary, this study reveals that MDRO colonization and infection are marked by significant gut microbiota dysbiosis, including reduced bacterial diversity, loss of beneficial butyrate-producing taxa (Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia), and expansion of pathogenic genera (Enterococcus, and Klebsiella). Crucially, these microbial shifts from a Bacteroides- to an Enterococcus-dominated enterotype were strongly correlated with systemic immune dysfunction, where the loss of immunoregulatory commensals coincided with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1ra, TNF-α) and diminished anti-inflammatory mediators. The inverse relationship between butyrate producers and inflammatory markers suggests that microbiota-derived metabolites play a key role in maintaining immune homeostasis, while pathobionts like Enterococcus appear to drive inflammation, creating a permissive environment for MDRO persistence. The diagnostic potential of microbial signatures highlights their clinical utility, while the observed microbiota-immune correlations provide mechanistic insights for targeted interventions. These findings emphasize the critical role of gut microbiota-immune crosstalk in MDRO pathogenesis and advocate for microbiota-targeted therapies to restore microbial balance and mitigate immune dysregulation, offering a promising avenue to combat antimicrobial resistance in high-risk clinical settings.
