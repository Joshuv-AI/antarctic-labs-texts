Artificial Intelligence documentation in trauma resuscitation: efficiency requires guardrails
Authors: Samita M Heslin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArtificial Intelligence (AI) documentation systems are being rapidly adopted throughout the healthcare industry as solutions to help alleviate the clinician documentation burden. The use of Ambient AI documentation systems enables passive capture of clinical conversations and the creation of medical documentation. Unlike routine clinical encounters, trauma resuscitation is characterized by fragmented speech, overlapping conversations, temporal complexities, and immediacy of decision-making. All of these aspects could present challenges to the many AI models based on commonly held assumptions about communication. Without trauma-specific safeguards, AI documentation systems may introduce automation bias, speaker identity errors, time relationship errors, and convert uncertainty into definitive statements. This article provides an examination of the risks associated with the use of AI documentation systems in trauma care and proposes several safety guardrails, including mandatory human review of high-risk elements, preserving uncertainty in documentation, structuring documentation of safety-critical data, providing multidisciplinary oversight of implementation, conducting trauma-specific validation testing, and ongoing auditing of performance.

Introduction

As healthcare continues to grapple with documentation burden and electronic health record (EHR)-related clerical work, organizations have explored documentation support approaches to reduce workload.13 Artificial Intelligence (AI) documentation systems have emerged to automate aspects of the documentation process. Ambient AI documentation systems use passive capture of clinical conversations, automated speech recognition, and natural language processing (NLP) to generate draft clinical notes. Additionally, some AI documentation systems incorporate contextual data from the EHR to further enhance the quality and completeness of the clinical notes created. The expectation is that once created, clinicians will review, edit, and finalize the documentation.

The use of AI documentation systems is growing in outpatient clinic settings, emergency departments, and other ambulatory care settings and is increasingly being viewed as a foundation for the clinical documentation of the future. However, every clinical environment has different challenges in the application of AI-assisted documentation systems. The trauma resuscitation area of clinical practice is a high-stakes environment where documentation is not only a means of recording clinical activity but also a safety-critical mechanism for communication across multiple disciplines.

Clinical environment of trauma resuscitation and documentation

The clinical environment of the trauma resuscitation area is dynamic and multidisciplinary. Several clinicians communicate simultaneously during the resuscitative process, roles within the team can change rapidly, and critical decisions must be made in a timely manner while operating under uncertainty.4 The documentation developed in these complex environments is used by multiple downstream teams, including, but not limited to, the surgical, anesthesia, radiology, intensive care unit (ICU), and quality improvement teams. Errors in documentation in the trauma resuscitation area may become magnified and result in adverse clinical decisions downstream from the initial encounter.5

To illustrate a typical clinical scenario in a trauma resuscitation area, consider the following: A patient arrives in the trauma resuscitation area following a motor vehicle collision. The prehospital team reports that the patient was responsive at the scene of the accident, but subsequently became confused while being transported to the hospital. During the resuscitative process, the trauma team leader asks if anyone knows if the patient takes any blood thinners. A family member located in the waiting area responds, “I believe so.” Meanwhile, a nurse reports the patient’s Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is 7. A few minutes later, after the patient is intubated, someone documents that the GCS is now 3T. An ambient AI documentation system would need to translate the fragmented, overlapping, and context-dependent communication into accurate documentation.

Traditional documentation in trauma resuscitation mirrors this level of complexity. Clinicians provide the appropriate contextual background for the clinical findings, preserve uncertainty, and provide temporal relationships. For example, the patient’s clinical status is documented in relation to the effects of sedation or intubation. The clinical significance of a patient’s hemodynamics is interpreted based on the effects of recent interventions. Historical elements of a patient’s history, such as the use of anticoagulants, are qualified for the reliability of the source. These contextual features are important, and not simply a matter of documentation preference. They are necessary for safe interpretation of the documentation.

How ambient AI documentation works

Ambient AI documentation systems are designed to use passive capture of clinical conversations, automated speech recognition, and NLP to generate draft clinical notes. Some systems may also incorporate contextual EHR data to improve the quality and completeness of clinical documentation. Clinicians are then expected to review, edit, and approve the documentation.

This model works fairly well in a structured clinical environment, where one clinician speaks clearly and cooperatively with the patient in a relatively quiet room. Trauma resuscitation, however, does not fit this model. The speech in trauma resuscitation is typically fragmented, overlapping, and often uses shorthand. Significant amounts of clinically important information are communicated through actions rather than clear verbal communication. Therefore, the context of a particular statement or action is often provided by individuals in many roles (eg, paramedics, nurses, physicians, respiratory therapists, bystanders). Without trauma-specific safeguards, AI documentation systems may struggle to represent both what occurred in the trauma resuscitation area and the rationale behind the clinical decisions made.

Documentation automation bias

One of the key challenges is documentation automation bias. Documentation automation bias refers to the phenomenon in which clinicians overrely on AI-generated documentation and perceive it as reliable and complete because it appears professional and well-written.6 It is a recognized hazard in decision support systems and similarly poses a hazard in documentation. For example, an AI-generated statement such as “the patient had a GCS of 15 upon arrival” may mask transient confusion or represent a post-sedation assessment. Similarly, a statement in the documentation, such as “the patient denies taking anticoagulants,” may reflect the AI’s interpretation of an incomplete conversation or unverified history. Finally, a statement in the documentation indicating that a patient was “hemodynamically stable” may not capture that stability was achieved only after administering 3 units of blood.

Trauma-specific failure modes

There are predictable failure modes and design challenges that are particularly relevant to the application of AI documentation systems to trauma resuscitation. These failure modes and design challenges should be viewed as safety-critical considerations for the purpose of implementing these systems responsibly and should not be seen as deterrents to the use of AI documentation systems in trauma resuscitation.

For example, speaker misattribution may occur when the AI system fails to differentiate between pre-hospital reports, family statements, speculative discussion between the team, and direct clinician assessments. The AI system may document “the patient states no medical history,” when in fact a family member was speaking, or may attribute a paramedic’s field assessment to the emergency physician.

Similarly, temporal distortion may occur when an AI system compresses clinically significant sequential events into summary statements. For example, the difference between a patient’s neurologic status prior to sedation versus after sedation, or the patient’s hemodynamic response prior to versus after transfusion may be lost when the AI system transforms time-stamped events into summary statements.

Neurological documentation errors may occur when assessments are recorded without references relative to intubation, intoxication, or agitation.7 For example, documentation of “the patient is alert and oriented,” for an intoxicated patient, or “GCS=8,” without acknowledging that the assessment was completed after intubation, may mislead downstream providers.

Procedural documentation gaps may omit required documentation elements such as the indication for a procedure, whether informed consent was obtained, confirmation of the completion of the procedure, and any complications encountered during the procedure. Such omissions pose both clinical and medicolegal risks.

Finally, privacy and consent considerations are increased in trauma care settings, where patients are often unable to give consent themselves.8 Recording family discussions, law enforcement statements, or social worker discussions without obtaining explicit consent raises ethical and legal concerns.

These limitations can be mitigated through targeted model training, incorporation of structured data into the documentation process, and designing trauma-specific workflows.

Safety guardrails for implementation

Ambient AI documentation systems should be implemented in trauma care with safety guardrails (figure 1).

Safety-centric implementation of ambient artificial intelligence (AI) documentation in trauma resuscitation.

First, clinician accountability must be maintained. Draft documentation generated by AI systems must be subject to mandatory human review of high-risk elements, including primary survey findings, neurologic status, anticoagulation history, procedures performed, and disposition decisions. The reviewer must be identifiable in the medical record and accountable for the accuracy of the documentation.

Second, uncertainty must be noted in the documentation and not resolved prematurely. Documentation systems should allow for documentation such as “unknown,” “unable to assess,” “history unreliable,” or “pending verification” rather than transforming incomplete information into definitive statements. Documentation in trauma care must reflect the reality of incomplete information at the time of resuscitation, and not the appearance of complete knowledge.

Third, structured documentation must be emphasized for safety-critical elements. Unstructured free-text narrative can be susceptible to errors, ambiguity, and misinterpretation when automatically generated. Structured documentation fields for neurologic assessments, procedures, vital sign trends, and transfusion events can minimize the spread of errors and improve data quality for research and quality improvement.9

Fourth, implementation of AI documentation systems must be multidisciplinary. Trauma nurses, prehospital leaders, trauma program directors, and quality improvement teams should participate in the design, deployment, and monitoring of AI documentation systems. Documentation errors should be investigated as patient safety events. A multidisciplinary investigation ensures that the needs of all stakeholders are represented.

Fifth, trauma-specific training and validation must be conducted prior to deployment. AI documentation systems must be trained on trauma-specific conversations, tested using curated trauma scenarios, and validated for accuracy in high-acuity resuscitations before clinical use.

Sixth, continuous audit is necessary. Ambient AI documentation systems should be audited continuously for recurrent error patterns, bias, and unintended consequences. The rate of errors should be measured and used to drive continuous improvement.

International considerations

Trauma systems exist in diverse forms around the world. Trauma systems vary in terms of available resources, staffing models, and medicolegal frameworks. In low-resource settings, the introduction of AI documentation systems may have the benefit of supporting the clinical workload and documentation burden where clinician time is limited and documentation workload is excessive. However, models that are primarily trained on data collected in high-income, English-speaking, non-trauma environments are unlikely to function effectively when deployed in other geographic locations and may exacerbate disparities in care.

Therefore, local validation of AI models is not discretionary—it is obligatory. Any AI model must be tested in the same environment in which it will be used, taking into account local languages, clinical practices, and documentation practices.

Conclusion

AI documentation systems are rapidly gaining traction across healthcare due to their scalability, cost-effectiveness, and ability to reduce the documentation burden of clinicians. Ultimately, AI documentation systems may become a central component of clinical documentation. However, trauma resuscitation is not a standard clinical environment. The trauma bay requires documentation that preserves uncertainty, provides contextualization of findings, and communicates temporal relationships. We must advocate for thoughtful implementation, thorough validation, and ongoing oversight to ensure that the benefits of new technologies in clinical care are realized.
