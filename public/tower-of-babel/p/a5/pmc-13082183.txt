A functional approach to social media assessment: Initial psychometric evaluation of the Social Media Use Functional Assessment
Authors: Albert Malkin, Mark J Rzeszutek, Aly Moscovitz, Kristina Axenova, Erin Walker, Amanpreet Randhawa, Karl F Gunnarsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo establish a behavioral framework for understanding social media use, we assessed the validity of the Social Media Use Functional Assessment (SMUFA), a survey instrument assessing functional categories of use (i.e., attention, escape, sensory, and tangible). Participants included 357 university students. We conducted exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, and after reducing items, a three‐factor model (attention, escape, and sensory and tangible) identified a good fit. We also examined associations between the SMUFA and two other established social media scales. Total SMUFA scores generally correlated with the Social Media Disorder Scale (r = .48) and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (r = .66) and had a weaker correlation with the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire (r = .31). Practical and conceptual issues related to assessing problematic social media use are discussed.Keywords: addiction, convergent validity, factor analysis, functional assessment, scale development, social media

Given the current trends in digital behavior, it is highly probable that a significant portion of readers have checked their social media account(s) within the last hour—or even in the past few minutes. Social media use is rapidly approaching ubiquity, with 70% of American adults using social media platforms such as Facebook (Gottfried & Park, 2025). Like most technological and social innovations, social media use has benefits (e.g., social connection and information dissemination) and challenges (e.g., distracted driving and misinformation; Riehm et al., 2019). Given that the algorithms and business models for most social media platforms are designed to maximize engagement by encouraging constant scrolling, posting, and clicking, some users may develop an addiction to social media (Mujica et al., 2022). The exact proportion of the population at risk of addiction is currently uncertain, with estimates ranging from 5% to 25% (Cheng et al., 2021), meaning that a conservative estimate of people addicted to social media would be in the tens of millions across North America alone. Thus, analyzing the reasons for excessive social media use holds considerable face validity.

Prior research has demonstrated the negative effects of social media use. For example, adolescents who spend more than 3 hr per day on social media are at heightened risk for mental health problems (Riehm et al., 2019). Social media use is also associated with sleep disturbance among young adults. Higher levels of daily use are associated with greater disruption (Alonzo et al., 2021; Levenson et al., 2016). Excessive social media use has been linked to changes in brain structure (He et al., 2017) and withdrawal‐like symptoms that mirror those observed in substance addictions (Stieger & Lewetz, 2018).

Researchers commonly employ a behavioral addiction framework adapted from Griffiths (2005) to evaluate problematic social media use, assessing criteria such as salience, tolerance, mood modification, relapse, withdrawal, and conflict. The above criteria involve spending a significant amount of time thinking about or planning to use, the need to spend an increased amount of time using to achieve desired effects, using as a means to escape negative emotions or to alter mood, failure to reduce use despite attempts to do so, experiencing distress or discomfort when unable to use, and the negative effects of excessive use on aspects of the user's life (Griffiths, 2005). Behavioral addiction frameworks have been used to develop scales that are intended to assess severity (Varona et al., 2022), including the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire (Przybylski et al., 2013), the Social Media Disorder Scale (Van den Eijnden et al., 2016), and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (Andreassen et al., 2017). These scales typically assess frequency of use and effects on daily life. For example, the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire measures frequency, the Social Media Disorder Scale assesses disruption to daily life, and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale captures symptoms like preoccupation and mood modification. Although psychometrically robust, these tools often overlook the functions of social media use. Furthermore, it is unclear whether certain behavioral patterns of use can increase susceptibility to encountering harm.

Social media platform designs, including infinite scroll and push notifications, can create potent contingencies that maintain high rates of use (Flayelle et al., 2023; Montag et al., 2019). Preferred images, videos, and text are accessible contingent on low‐effort responses (clicking, swiping), and these schedules of reinforcement are continuously available across contexts on smartphones. Algorithms detect preferences to maximize reinforcement to maintain and increase use (Flayelle et al., 2023; Montag et al., 2019), yet people differ in why and how they engage with social media. For example, users may use social media to alleviate negative emotions or stress (automatic negative reinforcement) or to seek social approval or attention from peers (social positive reinforcement). Just as Burrows et al. (2020) identified hypothesized functions maintaining smoking (e.g., sensory stimulation, stress reduction, or social interactions), similar distinct functional relations likely maintain problematic social media use. Recognizing that individual motivation influences social media use can provide more precise targets for interventions than simply reducing the frequency of use.

Functional behavioral assessment forms have been commonly used to inform the development of hypotheses about the maintaining variables of behavior targeted for change (mostly challenging behavior). Among these, the Questions About Behavioral Function (Paclawskyj et al., 2000) is a well‐studied informant‐based tool. It assesses five potential functions including attention, escape, nonsocial, physical, and tangible. A domain‐specific tool is the Gambling Functional Assessment (GFA; Dixon & Johnson, 2007), which can be used to develop hypotheses about the functions of gambling including whether the behavior is maintained by social attention, escape, or access to tangible consequences. Similar questionnaires have been developed to assess internet use (e.g., Kyonka et al., 2023), gaming (e.g., Sprong et al., 2014), and smoking (e.g., Burrows et al., 2020). The development of focused domain‐specific assessment tools reflects the need to understand and support complex behavior based on the specific contextual factors that evoke and maintain it.

Standardized functional assessment forms can be completed relatively quickly and provide initial hypotheses about behavior functions without time‐intensive observation or experimental analyses (Desrochers & Fallon, 2014; Peterson & Neef, 2020). Functional assessment forms are accessible and require minimal training. Although functional assessment forms are most effective as part of a comprehensive assessment strategy that includes direct observation and experimental functional analysis, indirect measures provide an alternative when direct observation is not possible (Herzinger & Campbell, 2007).

Identifying the function of behavior allows practitioners to tailor interventions to address those functions directly, thereby increasing the likelihood of behavior change. Social media and internet use are emerging domains in research and practice, yet evidence‐informed behavior‐analytic approaches remain limited (Hassan & Kyonka, 2021; Kyonka, 2021; Pérez‐Wiesner et al., 2025; Plackett et al., 2023). Nevertheless, as with all behavior‐analytic interventions, a function‐based approach is generally advisable (Hanley, 2012; Sturmey, 2020).

Given the knowledge of controlling antecedents and consequences, clinical interventions can be designed to alter relevant motivating operations and to increase adaptive alternative responses that replace or compete with social media use (Guinther & Dougher, 2013). Behavioral activation is one example of a function‐based intervention. Behavioral activation incorporates treatment components such as activity monitoring, activity scheduling, stimulus control, skills training, self‐reinforcement, identifying avoidance patterns, addressing rumination, and integrating mindfulness practices. Similarly, interventions based on acceptance and commitment therapy could be used to address social media use with a focus on skill building and an emphasis on the behavior in context (i.e., function). Sandoz et al. (2022) suggest that acceptance and commitment therapy should employ ongoing functional assessment, whereby practitioners can identify the specific reinforcing consequences, and that interventions can then be tailored to address these functions while promoting psychological flexibility. For example, if escaping aversive events is identified as a primary function, an acceptance and commitment therapy intervention might focus on developing acceptance and mindfulness skills to relate differently to these aversive events and the emotional experiences they may evoke while also clarifying values and building committed action toward meaningful offline activities. However, social media use is likely multiply controlled in many cases, potentially requiring treatment packages based on synthesized contingencies or broader clinical behavior analysis approaches.

Behavior analysts have made strides in assessing online behavior. Kyonka et al. (2023, 2024) developed a tool to assess the degree to which six functions including attention, escape from demand, social avoidance, private events, conditioned tangible reinforcement, and sensory stimulation affect internet use. Although the broad category of internet use does encapsulate social media use, the two are not necessarily interchangeable. Social media use is more likely to involve social contingencies, although not exclusively. Further, different platforms may facilitate different patterns of use (Cataldo et al., 2021; Starcevic & Billieux, 2017). Considering that some patterns of social media use demonstrate addictive characteristics (Hawi & Samaha, 2017), validating functional assessment measures is crucial for advancing and informing treatment strategies.

Typically, the process of validating new scales involves psychometric investigations to establish construct validity (DeVon et al., 2007). An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is conducted to examine the underlying factor structure and refine the scale by removing poorly performing items. Factor analysis is used to achieve parsimony by describing variables under study with the smallest number of numerical descriptors (i.e., factors) instead of scores on each initial variable (i.e., scale items; DeVellis, 2017). Construct validity is also examined by the degree to which a tool accurately assesses phenomena (i.e., the construct) it is intended to measure. Construct validity is typically examined by correlating the new scale with well‐validated measures and checking for expected patterns (i.e., moderate to strong correlations in the expected direction; Westen & Rosenthal, 2003). Validated scales ensure reliable measurement, facilitating effective practice and research communication.

To address the critical gap of assessing social media use in behavioral science research (see Hassan & Kyonka, 2021), we are introducing the Social Media Use Functional Assessment (SMUFA), which was developed to evaluate the attention, escape, sensory, and tangible functions of social media use. We investigate how a behavior‐analytic framework might shed light on factors that contribute to social media use. The purpose of the current study was to examine the construct validity of the SMUFA by (a) conducting an EFA and (b) determining whether the functions identified using the SMUFA relate to patterns of social media use reported via previously validated scales.

METHOD

Participants

Participants were recruited from a Canadian university. Recruitment emails were distributed to all undergraduate and graduate students via the university's mass email system. Participants were compensated with a $10 Amazon gift card for completing the survey. Initial survey respondents included 447 people. Participant data were retained for analysis after removing incomplete SMUFA responses and RelevantID fraud and duplicate scores greater than 0 (scores above 0 indicate potentially fraudulent or bot‐like responses and submissions that are likely duplicates; Qualtrics, 2026). One participant who indicated their age as 120 was removed, although it was possible that this was a typo and unlikely to be a person of that age. In total, 357 participants were included in a convenience sample of undergraduate and graduate students, with a median age of 21 years (SD = 4.56, range: 18–54 years). Procedure

All components of the study were carried out via the Qualtrics survey platform. Participants first completed a screening questionnaire consisting of two questions to verify their age (over 18) and use of social media, followed by signing a consent form. Subsequently, participants completed the following questionnaires: (a) a demographics questionnaire covering gender, age, race and ethnicity, and educational background; (b) a checklist for which participants indicated all social media applications they use (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Reddit, TikTok, etc.)1; (c) the 20‐item Social Media Engagement Questionnaire (Przybylski et al., 2013); (d) the nine‐item Social Media Disorder Scale (Van den Eijnden et al., 2016); (e) the six‐item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (Andreassen et al., 2017); and (f) the 20‐item preliminary version of the SMUFA (preliminary SMUFA), which includes a series of questions to identify four categories of contingencies. These contingencies include attention (i.e., socially mediated positive reinforcement in the form of social interactions), escape (i.e., removal from aversive events), sensory (i.e., nonsocially mediated physiological reinforcers), and tangible (i.e., access to positive reinforcement in the form of information or items). The 20 initial items were adapted from the GFA (Dixon & Johnson, 2007), GFA‐II (Dixon et al., 2018), and GFA‐R (Weatherly et al., 2011). We modified questions to reflect social media use rather than contingencies involved in gambling. For example, we modified the item “I gamble to get a break from work or other difficult tasks” to “I use social media for a break from work or difficult tasks,” and “I find myself feeling a rush, and getting excited, when I gamble” was modified to “When I use social media, I feel a rush of excitement when I see people engage with my posts.” Other items required a greater degree of modification to fit the specific contextual settings of social media use; for example, the item “I like the sounds, the lights, and the excitement that often go along with gambling” was modified to “I use social media to look at pictures and/or videos because I like the way they look.” Also, the content of items related to tangible reinforcers were mostly shifted toward access to information rather than income (e.g., “I use social media to follow links for new products or experiences”). We followed the approach taken by Dixon and Johnson (2007), which is consistent with the four‐function model employed by Iwata et al. (1982/1994). Like the scale construction of the GFA, we composed five questions per contingency, with each item rated on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). SMUFA scores are summarized by the sum of each of the five items per contingency, with the highest sum score potentially indicating the primary maintaining contingency for social media use. We sought feedback regarding the items of the preliminary SMUFA via personal contacts, including heavy social media users, graduate students, and behavior analysts with a focus on addiction and mental health. Following feedback, we made slight changes to the items, resulting in the version of the preliminary SMUFA examined in the current study.

Data analysis

Construct validity analysis

To determine the construct validity of the four‐factor (i.e., function) version of the Preliminary SMUFA, we conducted both CFA using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) and EFA using the psych package (Revelle, 2024). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023). Briefly, CFA and EFA are methods used in survey development to examine how similar responses to questions are to each other. Confirmatory factor analysis is used to determine how well an assumed set of constructs relates to different questions on a survey in terms of similarity and discriminability (Brown & Moore, 2012). In other words, each question is assumed to relate more strongly to one concept than to others, and the strength of this relationship is called its factor loading. Higher loadings mean the question is a good indicator of that concept, and lower loadings suggest that it may not fit as well. For example, using the four‐function assumption, questions related to the attention function of the SMUFA should load onto the Attention factor, escape‐related questions onto the Escape factor, and so on. If attention‐related questions are not sufficiently correlated with each other but correlate highly with escape‐related questions, the model will show a poor fit. This indicates that the survey does not accurately capture the assumed factors (i.e., functions). In contrast, an EFA is used to determine how questions load onto different unspecified factors (Brown & Moore, 2012). The benefit of an EFA is that it does not require specification beforehand, allowing for the identification of how different questions load onto factors together. Thus, EFA can be used to determine which questions might be problematic (e.g., if participants respond to an Attention question in a way that is more similar to their responses to Escape questions than to their responses on other Attention questions).

For the EFA, we used minimum residual (MR) extraction, which identifies factors that best account for the observed patterns of correlations among items. The extracted factors are labeled MR1, MR2, MR3, and MR4 and are numbered by the proportion of variance they explain, with MR1 explaining the most variance, MR2 the second most, and so on, prior to factor rotation (see Revelle, 2024). The proportion of variance refers to how much of the differences in participants' responses—that is, why some people responded differently from others—can be attributed to that factor. The cumulative proportion of variance is the total amount of these differences accounted for when all factors up to that point are included.

The initial CFA assumed four categorical functions for social media use: attention, escape, sensory, and tangible. An EFA was then conducted to explore how varying the number of factors influenced the loading of questions onto these factors. This was also intended to help identify questions that might load onto unintended factors (i.e., functions) or fail to load onto their intended factors. To accomplish this stage of validation, we conducted an EFA with three and four factors. Then, based on the results of what appeared to be the most appropriate model (conceptually and statistically), we removed questions from the Preliminary SMUFA. The reduced 12‐item survey (see Table 3) will henceforth be referred to as the SMUFA (as it is the final version). We then conducted an EFA and CFA on one‐half of the data and the other half of the data, respectively. We treated each half of the data as a “training set” and a “test set” to determine the goodness of fit of the reduced model via the CFA. To determine the relative goodness of fit, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were used. For RMSEA, a lower value indicates a better fit; for CFI and TLI, a higher value indicates a better fit. Generally, an RMSEA of .08 or lower is considered acceptable and a CFI/TLI higher than 0.90 is considered an acceptable fit (McDonald & Ho, 2002). Questions with loadings of >0.40 were considered to have loaded onto a given factor (see Yong & Pearce, 2013, for a tutorial on factor analysis). The EFA used principal axis factoring with a varimax rotation to maximize the independence of the extracted factors and simplify interpretation.

Convergent validity analysis

To evaluate convergent validity, we also conducted a simple correlational analysis of total Preliminary SMUFA and SMUFA scores as well as subscores against the different social media measures. Code and data used for the analyses are available via the Open Science Framework.

RESULTS

Construct validity

Preliminary SMUFA factor analysis

The CFA for the four‐factor model had relatively low fits (CFI = 0.71, TLI = 0.66, RMSEA = 0.10). This suggests the four‐function assumption of the Preliminary SMUFA does not represent the responses. Table 2 depicts the results of the four‐factor EFA. The four‐factor EFA shows which questions of the Preliminary SMUFA loaded together, assuming that there were four underlying factors. Figure 1 displays a scree plot of the Preliminary SMUFA. The y‐axis shows the eigenvalue of each factor, which represents the amount of variance in the data explained by that factor. The x‐axis represents the number of factors tested. Visual inspection (i.e., identifying the point at which the steep decline in eigenvalues levels off, referred to as the “elbow”) indicates that there are likely three or four factors underlying the data (i.e., the steepness of the curve decreases around the fourth factor).

SMUFA factor analysis

Table 2 summarizes factor loadings for the Preliminary SMUFA. By looking at questions that may either be redundant or superfluous, we reduced the assumed number of “functions” to three, with four questions per category. These functions were attention, escape, and sensory and tangible. Table 3 contains the questions that were retained from the Preliminary SMUFA to yield the 12‐item SMUFA (for comparison; Table 3 also contains all 20 Preliminary SMUFA items). The results of this CFA for the updated SMUFA were adequate (CFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.07) and improved relative to the Preliminary SMUFA. To further validate the three‐function SMUFA, we split the total sample into two halves and then ran the CFA on each of these samples with the three‐factor model. Both validation samples had improved metrics relative to the Preliminary SMUFA (CFI = 0.86, TLI = 0.82, RMSEA = 0.09 and CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06). In contrast to the Preliminary SMUFA, the questions assigned a given function loaded with other questions of that function. The three‐factor solution indicates that items within each function share sufficient common variance with their assigned factor, satisfying a prerequisite for internal consistency estimation. Future studies should directly examine the internal consistency reliability for each factor. A scree plot for the SMUFA is shown in the right panel of Figure 1, and it suggests three to four underlying factors.

Convergent validity

Figure 2 displays correlations, distributions, and relations between SMUFA subscales and social media use measures. Below the diagonal are scatterplots with linear regression lines showing relations between variables. The diagonal displays histograms showing the distribution of scores for each measure. Above the diagonal are Pearson correlation coefficients (r values). To read this figure, each cell represents the intersection of a row and column variable. For example, the cell at Row 3, Column 7 shows r = .72, indicating a strong positive correlation between the SMUFA Escape subscale and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale. The corresponding scatterplot at Row 7, Column 3 visualizes this relation, with SMUFA Escape scores on the x‐axis and Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale scores on the y‐axis. The diagonal cells, such as Row 3, Column 3, show the distribution of SMUFA Escape scores as a histogram. SMUFA total scores (M = 24.52, SD = 7.43) were moderately to strongly correlated with the Social Media Disorder Scale (M = 2.83, SD = 2.07) and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (M = 15.40, SD = 5.12), with correlation coefficients of r(355) = .48 and r(355) = .66, respectively. We also found a weak correlation between the total score and the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire (M = 24.76, SD = 8.58), with a correlation coefficient of r(355) = .31. All reported correlations had corrected p‐values less than .001.

We further examined convergent validity at the level of function subscales of SMUFA. The Escape subscale was most highly correlated with the Social Media Disorder Scale and Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, with a moderate to strong correlation (correlation coefficients of .56 and .72, respectively). The function with the weakest relation to the Social Media Disorder Scale and Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale was tangible and sensory, with a correlation of .12 and .31, respectively. However, as illustrated in Figure 2, the sensory and tangible subscale was most strongly related to the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire, whereas the inverse was found for the attention and escape subscales.

DISCUSSION

We examined the factor structure of the SMUFA, a functional behavioral assessment scale designed to develop hypotheses about the function of social media use in university students. Construct validity was examined through EFA and CFA, which suggested that a three‐factor model comprising Attention, Escape, and Sensory and Tangible dimensions best describes the data. To examine convergent validity, we examined the correlation between the SMUFA and other validated social media scales. The total scores from the SMUFA showed moderate to strong correlations with the Social Media Disorder Scale and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, both of which ask participants to indicate usage patterns over the past year. We found a weak total score correlation with the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire, which focuses on usage patterns over the preceding week. The weak correlation may be due to the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire measuring how daily activities and routines are influenced by and incorporate social media rather than more molar patterns that may result in undesirable consequences. Notably, this was the only measure that did not approximate a normal distribution. Instead, it had a negative skew, with more high scores than medium and low scores. In contrast, scores from the other scales generally followed a normal distribution. We examined convergent validity across SMUFA subscales (i.e., function). Escape was most highly correlated with the Social Media Disorder Scale and Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, with a moderate to strong correlation. Given that these scales capture continued use despite negative consequences, it is possible that using social media to escape can place people at greater risk of problematic use. This study's findings suggest further examination of the SMUFA to determine whether the SMUFA provides a basis for function‐based interventions that are directed at reducing harmful or problematic use.

A function‐based approach can shed light on individual idiosyncratic functions of social media use. The predominant function may vary across people and may be influenced by a range of contexts, differing across social media platforms. The current study and others like it (e.g., Kyonka et al., 2023, 2024) may represent important steps toward integrating a behavior‐analytic perspective within the broader social sciences dialogue on social media use (e.g., Odgers, 2024). One of the challenges in addressing problematic social media use is the lack of consensus on its definition and whether it can be classified as an addiction (e.g., Carbonell & Panova, 2017; Griffiths et al., 2014). Some researchers have argued that excessive social media use can lead to addiction, whereas others caution against pathologizing everyday behavior without considering the context and consequences (e.g., Kardefelt‐Winther et al., 2017). Considering function may either complement the behavioral addiction framework or possibly sidestep it altogether via a pragmatic approach. For example, if people display addictive behavior or simply want to reduce their social media use, functional assessment can inform interventions to do so.

A functional‐analytic framework is especially helpful in disentangling patterns of extensive use from problematic use. We found that the Social Media Engagement Questionnaire showed lower correlations with all other measures evaluated in the current study. This means that problematic social media use may not be solely defined by excessive time spent on social media platforms. Like other behavioral addictions, consequences and interference with daily functioning may be more relevant than time spent in identifying problematic use (Billieux et al., 2015).

We found that elevated endorsement of the escape subscale of SMUFA was more strongly correlated with problematic use (as measured by the other scales) than other functions. This finding suggests that using social media as escape or avoidance may be a defining characteristic of problematic social media use. The relation between escape or avoidance and problematic social media use is consistent with a broad understanding of behavioral addictions and pathology in which patterns of negatively reinforced behavior may lead to circumscribed behavior (Hayes et al., 1996). This conceptualization aligns with findings in internet use, internet gaming, and gambling (e.g., Buono et al., 2020; Kyonka et al., 2023; Levin et al., 2012; Melodia et al., 2022; Riley, 2014; Weatherly & Miller, 2013).

Research suggests that direct‐acting contingencies interact with verbal relations in addictive behavior like gambling (Hoon et al., 2008; Zlomke & Dixon, 2006). For social media use, verbal rules about expected outcomes (e.g., “checking social media will make me feel better”) can maintain behavior despite negative consequences. The above‐shared conceptualization across addictive behavior is helpful in formulating treatment that may focus on changing direct contingencies (e.g., via escape extinction, differential reinforcement of alternative behavior, or instructional control; Chazin et al., 2022; Kyonka et al., 2023) and by training additional relations among arbitrary stimuli involved in maladaptive rule following (i.e., in the context of clinical behavior analysis interventions; Belisle et al., 2019). A shared understanding of mechanisms across addictions is promising in informing effective, function‐based treatment to address what people avoid when they scroll on social media, use the internet, play games, or gamble (Kyonka et al., 2023).

At this time, however, it can only be inferred that some functions of use (i.e., escape) may place people at an elevated risk rather than imply that some functions and not others are problematic. A limitation of our study is that the data were self‐reported. Thus, these data carry all the flaws inherent in such data (see Critchfield et al., 1998; Northup, 2000, for discussions on self‐report data). Like most socially important behavior, such as adherence to medication regimens, child accidents, and drug use, social media use does not typically lend itself to direct observation by an external observer. However, Critchfield and Reed (2017) pointed out that researchers are left with a choice between not studying such behavior or making use of alternative methods, such as proxy measures or self‐reports. Given the prevalence of social media use globally, we have chosen the side of having some information rather than none.

Consideration must be given to the fact that social media use may be multiply controlled. People may use some apps for multiple purposes, and some apps may serve some functions more than others (but this is a matter for future research). This notion may be extended to our three‐factor (i.e., three‐function) model as a representation of social media use. Our findings imply that the questions we created to assess tangible and sensory functions were similar enough to be grouped into a single function. Grouping tangible and sensory functions has face validity given that accessing information and visually pleasing stimuli involve contact with positively reinforcing stimuli, but future empirical research must determine whether these constitute the same response class.

Additionally, our convenience sample of university students limits generalizability. However, the current sample is highly relevant given that young adults are the highest users of social media (Gottfried & Park, 2025; Pew Research Center, 2021). Adolescents and young adults may also be more prone to problematic social media use given developmental factors and the importance of peer relationships during this life stage (Throuvala et al., 2019). Future research should use more representative sampling methods to examine use patterns across demographics (e.g., ages, cultures) and contexts (e.g., platforms). The current sample does not account for diverse cultural norms and contextual factors, potentially misclassifying habitual use as addiction or problematic in a population for whom continuous online networking is a cultural norm (Kuss & Griffiths, 2017). In short, broader sampling may reveal important generational and cultural differences.

Our factor‐selection method is an analytic limitation. We determined the number of factors based on eigenvalues and scree plot inspection rather than more rigorous techniques (e.g., parallel analysis) and retained three factors due to conceptual connections to typical functional analyses (i.e., Iwata et al., 1982/1994) despite the possibility of a simpler model being favored (i.e., fewer factors). Results should be interpreted accordingly.

Future directions

We have provided initial evidence for the validity of the SMUFA; however, further research is needed to refine the SMUFA and establish both its validity and clinical utility. Future studies should also work to establish the convergent validity of the SMUFA by comparing its results with those obtained using other behavior‐analytic methods such as behavioral economic measures (e.g., demand and discounting) and other forms of functional assessment (e.g., open‐ended functional assessment interviews and questionnaires). Establishing correspondence between the SMUFA and these methods would increase confidence in the measure's accuracy.

The preferred approach to establishing convergent validity is examining the SMUFA's relation with observed social media use. Unfortunately, naturalistic observation of extended patterns of behavior can be challenging due to practical constraints, but ecological momentary assessment might serve as a valid proxy (Shiffman et al., 2008). Ecological momentary assessment involves repeated real‐time sampling of behavior via mobile devices using short surveys, with potential physiological data and context (e.g., location, light, heart rate, galvanic skin response), thus offering a feasible alternative to retrospective self‐reports. Additionally, recent human factors research supports laboratory‐based direct observation methodologies (e.g., Gao & Chen, 2021; Merbah et al., 2020). Such research could evaluate the feasibility of structured laboratory tasks and controlled environments to observe smartphone interactions, possibly complementing EMAs and naturalistic studies. Furthermore, future research could develop smartphone applications to specifically log social media use in real time and provide an app‐based alternative to naturalistic observation.

Ultimately, the validity and clinical utility of the SMUFA must be determined by its ability to inform effective interventions (i.e., functions identified by the SMUFA should directly guide the design and implementation of function‐based interventions; Rahaman et al., 2024). Because social media is widely available and users tend to be highly verbal, direct contingency management can be difficult. Thus, approaches adapted from clinical behavior analysis (Fisher & O'Donohue, 2006; Guinther & Dougher, 2013) may be best suited to addressing the complexities of problematic social media use. For example, escape‐maintained social media use interventions might employ systematic desensitization of aversive tasks, attention‐maintained social media use may require social skills training, and interventions for tangible‐ and sensory‐maintained social media use could focus on identifying alternative reinforcers. Additional strategies can include behavioral activation (Kanter et al., 2010), which targets avoidance behavior through activity monitoring, scheduling, stimulus control, and other components, as well as acceptance and commitment therapy (Sandoz et al., 2022), which emphasizes ongoing functional assessment and psychological flexibility. If social media use is multiply controlled, treatment packages synthesizing contingencies or broader clinical behavior‐analytic approaches (Dougher & Hayes, 2000; Ferster, 1972) may be necessary. Behavior analysts have made strides in assessing online behavior (e.g., Kyonka et al., 2023, 2024), integrating the SMUFA's function‐based insights into these emerging practices can further refine interventions for problematic social media use.

Given the prevalence of social media use worldwide (i.e., approximately one third of the world's population), an analysis of the function of this behavior can be seen as an example of moving toward a vision of behavior analysis as a general‐purpose science (Heward et al., 2022). We propose that the study of behavior does not get any more “auspiciously under the dome” than studying behavior that may generalize to billions of people (Hantula, 2019). Behavior analysts have a role to play in addressing pressing societal issues, so they need to study social media further. Analyses at the individual level do not address overarching systems‐level issues. Policy changes are needed to address the predatory nature of algorithms designed to maintain screen time for as long as possible for the sake of profit over well‐being. However, rather than waiting for changes in practices at many multinational conglomerates and policies across governments, individual behavior, in context, needs to be accounted for now. The SMUFA is a small step forward in this direction.
