Inflammatory responses in HIV-1 genetic subtypes in relationship with anti-retroviral drug resistance in Minna, Niger State, North Central Nigeria
Authors: Ndukwe Arua Kalu, Mathew Folaranmi Olaniyan, Pius Omoruyi Omosigho, Bukhari Isah Shuaib, Ewean Chukwuma Omoruyi, Isah Sadiq Yelwa, Iyare Godfrey Innocent, Chinedu Udechukwu Aka-Okeke, Aliyu Mohammed Abdullahi, Ibrahim Alhaji Adamu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high genetic diversity of HIV-1, persistent immune activation and rising antiretroviral drug resistance continue to hinder HIV treatment, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa. This study examined inflammatory responses in relation to HIV-1 genetic subtypes and antiretroviral drug (ARVD) resistance among patients experiencing virological failure in Minna, Niger State, Nigeria.MethodologyA cross-sectional design involved people living with HIV-1 (PLWH-1) on ART with virological failure (defined as repeated viral load ≥ 1000 copies/mL). Plasma levels of IL-6, IL-10, hs-CRP, and TNF-α were assessed as markers of inflammation; CD4 + counts and viral load served as immune and virological indicators. Genotypic analysis targeting the HIV-1 protease and reverse transcriptase regions was performed to identify subtypes and resistance mutations.ResultsHIV-1 subtypes G and CRF02_AG predominated. Subtype G was linked to elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels and a higher frequency of drug resistance mutations, suggesting subtype-specific polymorphisms and elevated pro-inflammatory markers which correlated with poor treatment outcomes. Co-infections with HBV, HCV, and Mtb were also common, especially among individuals with heightened inflammatory markers and virological failure, complicating disease progression. IL-6, IL-10, hs-CRP, and TNF-α emerged as potential surrogate biomarkers for predicting virological failure and immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS).ConclusionThe findings underscore the importance of integrating inflammatory profiling and genotypic resistance testing to inform clinical decisions and improve ART outcomes. A personalized approach to HIV care, accounting for viral subtype, immune-inflammatory status, and resistance patterns are recommended to enhance treatment efficacy and reduce HIV-related morbidity and mortality in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12865-026-00871-3.Keywords: HIV-1, Genetic Subtypes, Inflammatory Biomarkers, Antiretroviral Drug Resistance, Virological Failure, Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome, Niger State, Nigeria

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a virus that specifically targets and attacks immune cells that express the CD4 protein, such as CD4 + T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, which are crucial for the body’s defense against infections [1]. By infecting and destroying these cells, HIV progressively weakens the immune system, making the body more vulnerable to other infections and diseases [2]. This immune system impairment leads to the development of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) if untreated, characterized by severe immune deficiency and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections and certain cancers [3]. Since the beginning of the epidemic, 88.4 million (71.3–112.8 million) people have been infected with the HIV virus and about 42.3 million (35.7–51.1 million) people have died of HIV [4]. Globally, 39.9 million (36.1–44.6 million) people were living with HIV at the end of 2023 [5]. An estimated 0.6% (0.6–0.7%) of adults aged 15–49 years worldwide is living with HIV, although the burden of the epidemic continues to vary considerably between countries and regions [4, 5]. The WHO African Region remains most severely affected, with one in every 30 adults (3.4%) living with HIV and accounting for more than two-thirds of the people living with HIV worldwide [6]. HIV-1 remains a global public health challenge, with its impact particularly severe in sub-Saharan Africa, where diverse HIV-1 genetic subtypes circulate [7]. A hallmark of HIV-1 infection is chronic inflammation, which plays a significant role in disease pathogenesis [8]. These genetic subtypes not only influence the clinical course of the infection but also contribute to variations in immune responses, particularly inflammatory responses [9]. Inflammatory responses are often heightened in individuals with HIV-1, and the degree of inflammation has been shown to differ across HIV-1 `subtypes, potentially influencing the effectiveness of treatment strategies, including antiretroviral therapy (ART) [10]. Acute HIV-1 infection induces robust inflammatory responses, which are insufficient to prevent or eliminate virus in mucosal tissues [11]. While establishment of viral set-point is coincident with down regulation of acute innate responses, systemic inflammatory responses persist during the course of chronic HIV infection [12]. Since the introduction of combination antiviral therapy (cART), most HIV-1+ individuals can suppress viremia under detection levels for decades [13]. However, chronic immune activation persists and has been postulated to cause HIV-associated non-AIDS complications (HANA) [13]. The prevalent use of ART is widely used to suppress HIV-1 replication of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), yet the development of drug resistance correlates with a higher likelihood of virological failure [14]. In recent years, there has been growing concern regarding the emergence of drug resistance in individuals undergoing ART. Drug resistance mutations, which compromise the efficacy of ART, are often linked to specific HIV-1 subtypes, potentially exacerbating treatment challenges [15]. The relationship between inflammatory responses, genetic subtypes, and drug resistance is particularly critical in regions like Niger State, Nigeria, where diverse HIV-1 subtypes circulate, and drug resistance rates are rising [16]. Understanding how inflammatory responses vary among different HIV-1 subtypes and their influence on drug resistance patterns is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes [17]. The major defining characteristic of HIV-1 is its high genetic diversity, which is a result of a fast replication cycle, high mutation rate and high recombination rate [18]. This high genetic diversity results in the presence of different variants of the virus in different regions of the world, and the emergence of new variants especially in areas with multiple circulating subtypes continues to occur [19]. There are four phylogenetic groups of HIV-1: M (major), O (outlier), N (non-M/non-O), and the most recent group P [20]. Group M is responsible for the vast majority of global HIV-1 infections, accounting for more than 90% of cases worldwide and driving the global AIDS pandemic [21]. Group M is further subdivided into nine subtypes: A, B, C, D, F, G, H, J, and K. within these, subtype A is divided into six sub-subtypes (A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, and A6), and subtype F into two sub-subtypes (F1 and F2) [22]. Additionally, over 102 circulating recombinant forms (CRFs) and around 100 unique recombinant forms (URFs) have been identified, reflecting the extensive genetic diversity and recombination events characteristic of HIV-1 group M [23]. This diversity contributes to the complex epidemiology and ongoing evolution of the virus globally [24]. Subtype B is the predominant strain of HIV-1 in high-income countries whereas in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), especially in the African continent, the non-B subtypes together with several CRFs and URFs currently drive the epidemic. In sub-Saharan Africa, several studies have reported the presence of multiple HIV-1 subtypes along with a number of CRFs, such as CRF01_AE in Central Africa and CRF02_AG in West Africa [25]. The study aimed to determine inflammatory responses in HIV-1 Genetic Subtypes in Relationship with Anti-Retroviral Drug Resistance in Minna Niger State, North-Central Nigeria. The specific objectives of this study were to assess the demographic distribution and clinical characteristics of HIV-1 in Minna, Niger State, to evaluate plasma IL-6, IL-10, hs-C-Reactive Protein and TNF-ὰ as immune response markers in patients with HIV-1 virological failure (VF: repeated VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL), to investigate the risk and prevalence of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) specific comorbidies (HBV, HCV, Cryptococcus and Mtb) in HIV-1 virological failure subjects using IL-6, IL-10, hs-C-Reactive Protein and TNF- as predictive biomarkers, to ascertain if these inflammatory markers could be used as surrogate biomarkers in predicting Virological Failure among patients living with HIV-1, to determine the distribution of HIV-1 Genetic subtypes in patients experiencing Virological Failure, to determine the prevalence of HIV-1 drug resistance mutation in the Protease and Reverse Transcriptase Genes in patients experiencing Virological Failure, to determine HIV-1 Genetic subtypes in relationship with comorbidities (HBV, HCV and Mtb) in patients with Virologic Failure, to determine HIV-1 Genetic subtypes in relationship with inflammatory, immune and virologic markers in patients with Virologic Failure.

Materials and methods

Study design and study participants

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among HIV-1-infected adults attending the ART Clinic of IBB Specialist Hospital, Minna, Niger State, Nigeria, between January and December 2024. Participants were adults (≥ 18 years) receiving first-line HAART for at least six months and who provided written informed consent.

Virological failure was defined as two consecutive HIV-1 viral load measurements > 1,000 copies/mL. Demographic, clinical, immunological, inflammatory, and virological data were obtained using structured questionnaires and hospital records. Participants were stratified according to virological status into virologically suppressed (VS) and virological failure (VF) groups.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of IBB Specialist Hospital Minna, Nigeria (IBBSH/SUB/615/M2023-07). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Nigerian HIV research ethical guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Study population

The study population involved all the HIV-infected clients attending the ART clinic that have satisfied the eligibility criteria and consented to participate in the study within a six-month study period. This is because the clients are usually given 2–3 months appointment for a refill of their medication and clinical visit. This approach minimized repeated participant enrollment within the study population the opportunity to participate in the study thereby limiting the chance of repetition. Enrolled subjects were monitored, followed longitudinally for immune responses and adherence to Anti-retroviral drugs.

Inclusion criteria

HIV-1 HAART-treated patients who have experienced virological failure within 6 months (VF: repeated VL > 1,000 copies/mL).

HIV-1 HAART-treated patients above 18 years.

HIV-1 HAART-treated patients with or without HBV, HCV and TB co-morbidities.

Exclusion criteria

HAART-treated patients who have not experienced virological failure within 6 months (VF: repeated VL = > 1000 copies/mL).

HAART-treated patients below 18 years.

Data collection instruments

Structured questionnaires

Structured data collection form was designed, demography information, medical history and the clinical characteristics/outcomes data, Environmental/Host related exposures were obtained from patient’s hospital folders. The data were then transferred into the data collection form. Acceptability was determined using some set of questions adapted from a similar study [27].

Sample collection, transportation and storage

Venous blood samples were collected into EDTA and plain vacutainer tubes following standard laboratory procedures. Plasma and serum were separated by centrifugation and stored at − 20 °C pending analysis.

Plasma samples were used for HIV-1 viral load estimation, RT-PCR amplification, sequencing, and subtype analysis, while serum samples were used for the quantification of inflammatory biomarkers (IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and hs-CRP) and screening for HBV and HCV co-infections.

Samples designated for molecular analysis were transported under cold-chain conditions to the Clinical Virology Laboratory, University College Hospital, Ibadan, Nigeria.

Analytical procedure

HIV serostatus was confirmed using the Nigerian national HIV testing algorithm comprising Determine™ HIV-1/2 (USA), Uni-Gold™ (Ireland), and HIV-1/2 STAT-PAK® (USA). HIV type differentiation was subsequently performed using the MULTISURE® HIV Rapid Test (MP Biomedicals, Germany).

CD4 + T-cell estimation was performed using the VISITECT® CD4 Advanced Disease Rapid Test kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

HBV and HCV screening were performed using commercial ELISA kits, while serum concentrations of IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and hs-CRP were quantified using sandwich ELISA assays according to manufacturers’ protocols.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis detection and rifampicin resistance screening were performed using the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay following WHO recommendations.

Plasma HIV-1 viral load quantification was performed using the cobas® 8800 System (Roche Diagnostics, USA) at the Defence Reference Laboratory, Abuja, with a detection limit of 20 copies/mL.

Plasma RNA extraction

Viral RNA was extracted from plasma samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The HIV-1 protease and reverse transcriptase regions of the pol gene were amplified using nested RT-PCR with previously validated primers (Table 1). Amplification was performed using the BIO-RAD C1000™ thermal cycler under optimized cycling conditions.

PCR amplicons were resolved by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the Bioland Scientific Gel Extraction Kit (USA). Purified products were sequenced using the ABI PRISM® 310 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) with BigDye® Terminator v3.1 chemistry.

Sequence quality assessment, subtype determination, and drug resistance mutation analysis were performed using standard bioinformatics tools and reference HIV drug resistance databases.

Sanger sequencing reaction

Sanger sequencing reaction was conducted using ABI PRISM® 310 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). Region-specific primers were used for targeted sequencing: DRPRO6 for the PR coding region and DRRT6L for the RT region. The reactions employed BigDye® Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing chemistry (Applied Biosystems), which utilizes dye-labeled dideoxynucleotides for chain-termination. Post-reaction clean-up involved ethanol precipitation to remove unincorporated dyes, and purified products were analyzed on an ABI PRISM® 310 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) using capillary electrophoresis. Sequence chromatograms were subjected to rigorous quality assessment, and base-calling was confirmed using electropherogram analysis tools to ensure high-fidelity sequence reads for downstream mutation and resistance analysis.

Bioinformatics analysis

Post-sequencing, bioinformatic analysis was performed to assess sequence quality, determine HIV-1 subtype, identify drug resistance mutations, and conduct multiple sequence alignments using relevant databases and tools for precise genomic interpretation (Fig. 1).

HIV-1 sequencing and subtyping

DNA sequencing chromatograms were generated and initially reviewed using FinchTV (Geospiza Inc.) to ensure high-quality base calling and to trim ambiguous or low-quality regions. The cleaned sequences were then aligned and assembled using BioEdit version 7.2, enabling the generation of high-confidence consensus sequences. These consensus sequences were subjected to homology searches using the NCBI-BLAST web interface against the GenBank non-redundant nucleotide database to confirm viral identity. To assess the integrity of the open reading frames, sequences were translated in silico and inspected across all six reading frames. HIV-1 subtyping was performed using MEGA version 11, where multiple sequence alignments were generated and phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbour-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, applying the Kimura 2-parameter model for distance estimation. For subtype assignment, consensus sequences were compared against a curated panel of HIV-1 reference strains representing all major subtypes and circulating recombinant forms (CRFs), obtained from the Los Alamos HIV Sequence Database ([https://www.hiv.lanl.gov] (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov)). Subtype determination was based on the phylogenetic clustering of query sequences with these established references. Sequences that did not cluster cleanly with a single subtype or showed divergent clustering patterns were flagged as potential recombinant forms for further analysis. Final consensus sequences were submitted to the NCBI GenBank database for accessioning.

HIV-1 drug resistance analysis

Drug resistance analysis focused on the protease and reverse transcriptase regions of the HIV-1 genome, utilizing the Stanford HIV Drug Resistance Database (HIVdb) version 9.7 by WHO guidelines (2021) for interpreting transmitted drug resistance mutations (TDRMs). The algorithm assigns resistance scores based on the impact of specific mutations, classifying cumulative scores into five categories: susceptible (< 10), potential low-level resistance (10–14), low-level resistance (15–29), intermediate resistance (30–59), and high-level resistance (≥ 60). All classes of currently available antiretroviral drugs were included in the evaluation. Protease inhibitors (PIs) assessed were atazanavir (ATV/r), darunavir (DRV/r), fosamprenavir (FPV/r), indinavir (IDV/r), lopinavir (LPV/r), nelfinavir (NFV), saquinavir (SQV/r), and tipranavir (TPV/r). Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) included abacavir (ABC), zidovudine (AZT), stavudine (D4T), didanosine (DDI), emtricitabine (FTC), lamivudine (3TC), and tenofovir (TDF). Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) analyzed were doravirine (DOR), efavirenz (EFV), etravirine (ETR), nevirapine (NVP), and rilpivirine (RPV).

Quality control

Research Assistants underwent training on questionnaire administration prior to field contact with the respondents. Pre-testing of the questionnaires was conducted before fieldwork. Quality control measures were implemented on the MULTISURE® HIV Rapid Test (MP Biomedicals, Germany). The appearance of a colored band in the control region functioned as an internal positive procedural control, affirming sufficient specimen volume and accurate procedural technique. Furthermore, external positive and negative controls provided by the manufacturers underwent testing concurrently with each batch of plasma samples. to verify the proper functioning of the test kits. Prior to analysis, the collected data underwent a cleaning process.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Statistics Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographics and clinical characteristics. All parameters were expressed as mean ± SD. The student’s t- test was used to explore statistical differences. Multivariable linear regression was used to determine the association between predictor variables (age, gender, marital status, education level, average monthly income, duration on HAART etc.) while multivariant logistic regression was used for viral suppression to obtain the odd ratio. Level of significant was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 93 participants were assessed, comprising 50 individuals who were virally suppressed and 43 who experienced virological failure. Blood samples were obtained from all participants and analyzed. The results are organized according to the specific objectives of the study. Data were summarized using frequencies, means, standard deviations (SD) and correlation analyses. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student’s t-test, while one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to assess relationships among variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Demographic pattern and clinical characteristics of HIV-1 virally suppressed and virological failure groups in Minna, Niger State

Age distribution

Most participants in the virally suppressed group were between 33 and 42 years (40%), followed by 43–52 years (34%). In contrast, the majority of those with virological failure were older, primarily within the 43–52 years (41.86%) and 53–62 years (23.26%) age brackets.

Gender

Males were predominant in the virological failure group (74.42%) compared to females (25.58%), whereas the virally suppressed group had a more balanced gender distribution (54% male, 46% female).

Educational status

Formal education was more common among the virally suppressed (64%) compared to those with virological failure (58.1%). Informal education was higher among the virological failure group (41.8%).

Marital status

Most participants in both groups were married. However, widowers and divorced individuals were slightly more represented in the virological failure group.

Occupation

Employment was significantly higher among the virally suppressed (86%) compared to the virological failure group (58.1%), suggesting a possible link between employment and better treatment outcomes.

Family structure

Monogamous family settings were slightly more prevalent among the virally suppressed (60%) than in the virological failure group (51.1%). Polygamy was common in both groups.

Transmission risk

Sexual transmission was the most reported mode of infection in both groups (94% in virally suppressed, 90.6% in virological failure), followed by transfusion and intravenous drug use.

Adherence status

Good adherence was much higher among the virally suppressed (66%), while poor and fair adherence predominated in the virological failure group (30.2% and 65.1%, respectively).

Opportunistic infections

Participants with virological failure had a higher prevalence of viral (30.2%), parasitic (20.9%), and multiple infections (30.2%) compared to the virally suppressed group, which had fewer such infections.

WHO staging before cART Initiation

The majority of the virally suppressed group were in early WHO stages (1 and 2), while a significant portion of those with virological failure were in advanced stages (3 and 4).

CD4 count

All virally suppressed participants had CD4 counts ≥ 200 cells/mm³, whereas 97.67% of the virological failure group had CD4 counts < 200, indicating severe immunosuppression.

Viral load

At enrolment, all participants had viral loads ≥ 1000 copies/mL. At follow-up (3–6 months), all virally suppressed individuals had viral loads < 1000 copies/mL, while all virological failure cases remained ≥ 1000 copies/mL, confirming sustained virological failure.

Table 8 evaluates HIV-1 Genetic subtypes in relationship with comorbidities (HBV, HCV and Mtb) in patients with Virologic Failure in Minna Niger State. While subtype G was more common, comorbidities were disproportionately higher in patients with the CRF02_AG subtype. Among these, comorbidities were relatively low: HBV 1 (2.6%), 1 had HCV1 (2.6%), Mtb 3 (7.9%) and non-comorbidities 26 (68%). HIV-1 Subtype CRF02_AG was less common (n = 7, 18.4%) but showed a higher burden of comorbidities relative to its frequency: HBV 4 (10.5%). HCV 2 (5.2%), Mtb 1 (2.6%) and zero non comorbidities.

Discussion

The present study aimed to comprehensively investigate inflammatory responses in HIV-1 genetic subtypes in relation to antiretroviral drug resistance among people living with HIV (PLWH) experiencing virological failure in Minna, Niger State, North Central Nigeria. The findings provide crucial insights into the complex interplay between demographic factors, clinical characteristics, immunologic and inflammatory distinctions, viral genetics, and drug resistance, contributing to a deeper understanding of HIV pathogenesis and treatment challenges and highlighting how these biological factors relate to drug resistance in this region. This discussion will systematically address each of the study’s objectives, comparing and contrasting the results with existing scientific literature. Table 2 presents a detailed demographic and clinical profile of the study participants, differentiating between those with virological suppression (VS) and virological failure (VF). The findings indicate notable disparities between the two groups. In the virological failure group, older individuals (43–52 years and 53–62 years) and males (74.42%) were predominant. This contrasts with the virally suppressed group, which showed a more balanced age distribution and gender ratio. This observation aligns with existing literature suggesting that older age can be a risk factor for virological failure, potentially due to age-related immune senescence, comorbidities, or challenges with long-term adherence [28]. The higher proportion of males experiencing virological failure in this study warrants further investigation, as some studies have indicated sex-specific differences in ART adherence or treatment outcomes [29]. Regarding educational status, formal education was more prevalent among the virally suppressed (64%) compared to those with virological failure (58.1%). This aligns with the broader understanding that higher educational attainment can correlate with better health literacy, adherence to complex medical regimens, and engagement with healthcare services [29]. Similarly, higher employment rates in the virally suppressed group (86% vs. 58.1% in VF) suggest that socioeconomic stability may positively influence treatment outcomes, potentially by facilitating consistent access to medication and nutritional support. This underscores the importance of addressing socioeconomic determinants of health in HIV care, particularly in resource-limited settings like Minna, Niger State. Sexual transmission remained the primary mode of HIV acquisition in both groups (94% in VS, 90.6% in VF), consistent with the generalized heterosexual epidemic observed in sub-Saharan Africa [30]. The most striking difference between the groups was in adherence status and clinical staging. A vast majority of virally suppressed participants reported good adherence (66%), while poor and fair adherence predominated in the virological failure group (30.2% and 65.1%, respectively). This finding strongly reinforces the well-established principle that adherence is the cornerstone of successful ART, as suboptimal adherence directly leads to treatment failure and drug resistance [31, 32]. Furthermore, a significant portion of the virological failure group presented in advanced WHO clinical stages (3 and 4) before cART initiation, and nearly all (97.67%) had CD4 counts below 200 cells/mm³ at enrolment. This indicates severe immunosuppression, which is a known predictor of poorer treatment outcomes and increased risk of opportunistic infections. The persistent high viral loads (≥ 1000 copies/mL) in the virological failure group at 3–6 months follow-up, compared to undetectable levels in the suppressed group, unequivocally confirms the definition of virological failure as per WHO guidelines [28]. Table 3 illustrates the correlations between inflammatory, immune, and virological markers in both the virological failure (VF) and viral suppression (VS) groups. In the VF group, IL-6 exhibited a strong positive correlation with hs-CRP (r = 0.80, p = 0.024) and with viral load at 6 months (r = 0.58, p = 0.014). This is consistent with existing literature that highlights IL-6 as a key pro-inflammatory cytokine whose levels are elevated in HIV infection, particularly in the context of unsuppressed viral replication and chronic inflammation [33]. The strong correlation between IL-6 and hs-CRP is expected, as IL-6 is a primary inducer of hepatic CRP production, making hs-CRP a downstream marker of systemic inflammation [34]. Interestingly, IL-6 also showed a moderate positive correlation with CD4 count (r = 0.44, p = 0.032) in the VF group. While typically higher CD4 counts are associated with better immune health, this positive correlation in the context of virological failure might reflect persistent immune activation, where even the remaining CD4 cells are highly activated and contributing to the inflammatory milieu [35]. This contrasts with the VS group, where IL-6 showed a negative, albeit non-significant, correlation with CD4 count (r = -0.235, p = 0.129), suggesting a trend towards reduced inflammation with immune reconstitution. hs-CRP and TNF-α were significantly correlated in the VF group (r = 0.63, p = 0.048), and hs-CRP showed a very strong correlation with viral load at 6 months (r = 0.952, p = 0.016). This reinforces the role of hs-CRP as a robust marker of systemic inflammation, which is exacerbated by ongoing viral replication [36]. TNF-α also positively correlated with viral load at 6 months (r = 0.75, p = 0.05), supporting its role as a pro-inflammatory cytokine that promotes HIV replication and is elevated in active disease [36]. A significant negative correlation was observed between IL-10 and viral load at 6 months (r = -0.28, p = 0.011) in the VF group. This finding is somewhat paradoxical, as IL-10 is generally considered an anti-inflammatory cytokine, and its levels often increase with higher viral loads in viremic infection as a compensatory mechanism [37, 38]. The negative correlation in this specific context of established virological failure might suggest that while IL-10 is attempting to dampen inflammation, its effectiveness is overwhelmed by the persistent viral replication, or that its chronic elevation leads to immune exhaustion that paradoxically allows for higher viral loads. This complex role of IL-10, where it can be both immune-protective and potentially aid viral persistence, has been well-documented [39]. In the virally suppressed group, while IL-6 and hs-CRP maintained a strong correlation (r = 0.78, p = 0.013), and hs-CRP was associated with TNF-α (r = 0.74, p = 0.020), their correlations with viral load were not significant (p > 0.05). This indicates that while low-grade inflammation may persist even with viral suppression, it is not directly driven by high viral loads, suggesting other factors like microbial translocation or residual viral reservoirs contribute to this persistent inflammatory state [40]. Notably, TNF-α showed a strong positive correlation with CD4 count (r = 0.434, p = 0.004) in the VS group. This could imply that as immune reconstitution occurs, TNF-α may play a more beneficial role in immune surveillance or that its levels are reflecting a healthy, albeit low-level, immune activation necessary for maintaining viral control. The study’s findings confirm that IL-6, hs-CRP, and TNF-α are significantly elevated and correlated with virological failure, underscoring their utility as markers of ongoing inflammation and poor viral control. The complex behavior of IL-10 highlights the intricate immune dysregulation in HIV. Table 4 details the frequency and distribution of immune response markers in individuals with comorbid HBV, HCV, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infections, stratified by viral suppression (VS) and virological failure (VF). The overall IRIS prevalence of 14.6% among comorbidities (HBV: 5.4%, HCV: 3.2%, MTB: 4.3%) in the virological failure group indicates a significant burden of IRIS-related events in this population. This finding is consistent with the understanding that virological failure, characterized by uncontrolled viral replication and severe immunosuppression, creates a fertile ground for opportunistic infections and subsequently, IRIS upon immune recovery or even in the context of persistent immune activation [41]. In participants co-infected with HBV and experiencing virological failure (HBV-VF), significantly elevated levels of IL-6 (195.5 ± 54.66 pg/mL), IL-10 (163.4 ± 9.77 pg/mL), and TNF-α (42.2 ± 20.94 pg/mL) were observed compared to the VS group (p < 0.000001). While hs-CRP was also higher in the VF group, its elevation was less pronounced compared to the other cytokines. These results suggest a heightened pro-inflammatory state and significant immune activation in HBV-VF, indicating a high risk for IRIS development. This aligns with literature demonstrating that HBV co-infection in HIV can exacerbate immune activation and inflammation, contributing to liver pathology and potentially IRIS [40, 41]. The elevated IL-10 in this group, despite its anti-inflammatory role, may reflect a compensatory mechanism that is ultimately overwhelmed by the persistent inflammatory drive. Similarly, the HCV-VF group exhibited statistically significant increases in IL-6 (129.38 ± 61.54 pg/mL), IL-10 (132.45 ± 43.85 pg/mL), and TNF-α (36.4 ± 18.52 pg/mL) compared to the VS group. The hs-CRP levels were also significantly higher in the VF group (p = 2.303E-05). These robust elevations in inflammatory cytokines reinforce the association between virological failure, systemic immune activation, and strong IRIS potential in HCV co-infected individuals. Chronic HCV infection itself is known to induce persistent immune activation and inflammation, which is further amplified in the context of uncontrolled HIV replication [42]. Among individuals co-infected with MTB and experiencing virological failure (MTB-VF), the most distinct IRIS cytokine profile was observed. Markedly elevated levels of IL-6 (166.02 ± 46.55 pg/mL), TNF-α (66.67 ± 18.47 pg/mL), and hs-CRP (11.2 ± 9.27 mg/L) were found compared to the VS group (p < 0.001). Notably, IL-10 levels did not differ significantly between the MTB-VF and VS groups (p = 0.681), despite high absolute values. This pattern, characterized by high pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and hs-CRP, with a less pronounced or plateaued IL-10 response, is highly characteristic of IRIS, particularly in the context of tuberculosis [41]. The findings suggest that MTB co-infection in virological failure elicits a classical IRIS cytokine profile, warranting preemptive IRIS management strategies during ART initiation or regimen switch. The inconsistent significance of hs-CRP across all comorbidities, peaking in MTB-VF, suggests its utility as a predictive marker might be more pronounced in bacterial rather than viral co-infections for IRIS prediction. Virological failure is strongly associated with elevated immune activation markers, especially in the presence of HBV, HCV, or MTB co-infections. IL-6 and TNF-α consistently emerge as key inflammatory drivers and strong predictors of IRIS across all VF groups. The paradoxical elevation of IL-10 indicates a complex, often insufficient, counter-regulatory response. These biomarkers, particularly in the context of MTB co-infection, hold significant diagnostic and prognostic utility for identifying individuals at high risk of IRIS. Table 5, which measures the frequency elevation and mean standard deviations of inflammatory markers, provides compelling evidence for the utility of IL-6, IL-10, hs-CRP, and TNF-α as surrogate biomarkers for predicting virological failure (VF) in HIV-1 patients in Minna, Niger State. The results showed significantly higher elevation frequencies and mean levels of IL-6, IL-10, hs-CRP, and TNF-α in the VF group compared to the virally suppressed (VS) group, with all differences being statistically significant (p < 0.001). Specifically, IL-6 was elevated in 100% of the VF group compared to 72% in the VS group, with mean levels of 82.3 pg/mL in VF versus 13.0 pg/mL in VS. This marked difference underscores IL-6’s strong association with uncontrolled viral replication and chronic inflammation, making it a robust indicator of treatment failure [33]. The persistent elevation of IL-6 even in virally suppressed individuals (72%) highlights the challenge of residual inflammation, but its significantly higher levels in VF clearly differentiate the two states. Similarly, hs-CRP was elevated in 95% of the VF group compared to 68% in the VS group, with mean levels of 9.2 mg/L in VF versus 1.5 mg/L in VS. This further supports hs-CRP as a reliable marker of systemic inflammation that is exacerbated by virological failure [36]. The consistently high elevation of hs-CRP in VF suggests its strong predictive value for identifying patients who are failing treatment. TNF-α was elevated in 98% of the VF group compared to 70% in the VS group, with mean levels of 37.8 pg/mL in VF versus 8.6 pg/mL in VS. This cytokine’s consistent elevation in VF aligns with its role in promoting viral replication and driving inflammation, reinforcing its potential as a predictive biomarker for treatment failure [43]. IL-10, despite its anti-inflammatory properties, was also elevated in a higher percentage of the VF group (97%) compared to the VS group (90%), with mean levels in VF group (113.03 pg/mL vs. 77.05 pg/mL in VS showed only a modest predictive value (p = 0.0015), and its frequency of elevation did not reach statistical significance. While its role is complex, its elevated presence in VF may reflect an overwhelmed compensatory response or contribute to immune dysfunction that facilitates viral persistence [37]. This paradoxical role of IL-10 is consistent with the literature, which describes it as both an anti-inflammatory mediator and a contributor to immune dysfunction by promoting viral persistence and impairing T cell function [44]. The significant difference in IL-10 levels between the two groups still suggests its utility as a marker, albeit one that requires careful interpretation within the broader inflammatory context. The consistently higher frequencies of elevation and mean levels of all four biomarkers (IL-6, IL-10, hs-CRP, TNF-α) in the virological failure group, coupled with their statistically significant differences from the virally suppressed group, strongly suggest that these inflammatory markers can indeed serve as surrogate biomarkers for predicting virological failure except for IL-10 which needs careful interpretation. Their ability to reflect ongoing immune activation and inflammation, which are direct consequences of uncontrolled viral replication, makes them valuable tools for early identification of treatment failure, complementing traditional viral load and CD4 count monitoring. This aligns with the existing literature that advocates for the use of inflammatory biomarkers to predict disease progression and treatment outcomes in HIV [33]. Table 6 details the distribution of HIV-1 genetic subtypes among patients experiencing virological failure. The analysis of HIV-1 genetic diversity in patients experiencing VF revealed a dominance of subtype G (81.5%) and a less frequent presence of CRF02_AG (18.5%). These results are consistent with previous reports from Nigeria and other parts of West Africa with known high genetic diversity of HIV-1, where subtype G is predominant, although the proportion of recombinant forms like CRF02_AG has been increasing due to inter subtype recombination [22, 45, 46]. Subtype CRF02_AG has been associated with specific transmission dynamics and potentially distinct inflammatory responses or drug resistance patterns, which could contribute to treatment failure [47]. The observed subtype distribution in this cohort of virological failure patients highlights the importance of regional surveillance for HIV-1 genetic diversity. Understanding the circulating subtypes is crucial for tailoring public health interventions, optimizing diagnostic assays, and informing treatment guidelines, as different subtypes may exhibit varying responses to antiretroviral drugs and have distinct patterns of drug resistance emergence [48]. The findings from Minna, Niger State, reinforce the notion that HIV-1 is not a monolithic entity, and its genetic heterogeneity must be considered when addressing treatment challenges, particularly in cases of virological failure. Table 7 provide a comprehensive overview of the prevalence of drug resistance mutations (DRMs) in the reverse transcriptase (RT) and protease (PR) genes, respectively, among patients experiencing virological failure. These findings are critical for understanding the mechanisms of treatment failure in this cohort. Among patients with VF, no major mutations were detected in the reverse transcriptase (RT) region (NRTI/NNRTI), while 10.5% SDRMs and 10.5% TSMs had major protease inhibitor (PI) mutations (M46I, I54V, L76V, V82M) which was exclusively observed in those with subtypes G. This might suggest a subtype-dependent mechanism of resistance evolution. Subtype G may have a higher propensity for selecting resistance mutation under drug pressure, possibly due to a less robust inflammatory response and weaker immune surveillance. Additionally, a high number of minor mutations (179%) in the reverse transcriptase (RT) and Protease (PR) Gene regions were recorded, suggesting the presence of multiple polymorphic variants per individual. This finding underscores the growing concern of protease inhibitor resistance even in the absence of RT mutations, consistent with reports from WHO (2022) and Gupta et al. 2022 emphasizing increased PI resistance in treatment-experienced individuals [49]. Notably, the lack of RT mutations contrasts with findings from other Sub-Saharan cohorts, where NRTI and NNRTI resistance remains high due to first-line ART regimens heavily reliant on these classes. This difference may reflect regional ART practices or the influence of HIV-1 subtype on mutation pathways, as some studies suggest that subtype G and CRF02_AG may have differing propensities for resistance development [32]. However, the presence of multiple minor/polymorphic variants per individual mutations, especially in combination, can still contribute to reduced PI susceptibility and should be monitored. The overall prevalence of drug resistance mutations, particularly in the Protease genes, among patients experiencing virological failure in Minna, Niger State, underscores the urgent need for routine drug resistance testing and individualized treatment strategies. These findings align with the global concern regarding increasing drug resistance, especially in resource-limited settings where adherence challenges and limited access to resistance testing can exacerbate the problem [49]. Table 8 presents the prevalence and relationship between HIV-1 genetic subtypes and co morbidities (HBV, HCV, and MTB) in patients experiencing virological failure. The data indicate that certain subtypes are more frequently associated with specific co-infections, which can influence disease progression and treatment outcomes. Findings revealed 12% prevalence rate of co morbidities among this cohort. Among patients with virological failure, subtype CRF02_AG patients had higher rates of comorbid HBV (57.1%), HCV (28.6%), and Mtb (14.3%), while those with subtype G had much lower comorbidity frequencies (HBV and HCV at 2.6%, Mtb at 7.9%). This supports the hypothesis that CRF02_AG may predispose individuals to immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)-associated infections, possibly due to more intense immune activation and systemic inflammation. These observations are consistent with earlier findings that link elevated inflammatory cytokines with the development of IRIS, particularly in co-infected individuals [25]. The elevated cytokine levels in CRF02_AG-infected individuals may thus reflect not only higher IRIS risk but also subtype-specific immune dynamics. These findings suggest a potential link between specific HIV-1 genetic subtypes and the prevalence of certain comorbidities in the context of virological failure. The consistent association of CRF02_AG and Subtype G with HBV, HCV, and MTB co-infections in this cohort is particularly relevant given their high prevalence in West and Central Africa, including Nigeria [22]. This observation aligns with existing literature indicating that the geographical distribution of HIV-1 subtypes often overlaps with the endemicity of other infectious diseases [10]. The interplay between HIV-1 subtypes and co-infections is complex. Different subtypes may influence the host’s immune response, potentially altering susceptibility to co-infections or affecting their clinical course. For instance, some studies suggest that certain subtypes might be associated with distinct inflammatory profiles that could impact the progression of co-infections [13]. The presence of these co-infections, in turn, can exacerbate chronic inflammation and immune activation, further contributing to virological failure and the emergence of drug resistance [41]. Understanding these subtype-comorbidity relationships is crucial for developing targeted public health interventions and integrated care models. For example, in regions where CRF02_AG is prevalent and co-infections with HBV, HCV, and MTB are common, comprehensive screening and management strategies for these comorbidities should be prioritized for PLWH, especially those experiencing virological failure. This integrated approach can help mitigate the compounded challenges posed by co-infections and optimize overall patient outcomes [17]. Table 9 also elucidates the relationship between HIV-1 genetic subtypes and inflammatory, immune, and virologic markers in patients experiencing virological failure. The data reveal subtype-specific patterns in these markers, suggesting that the genetic makeup of the virus may influence the host’s response to infection and treatment. Despite the lower prevalence of CRF02_AG, its clinical implications appear more significant in some immunologic and inflammatory domains. This subtype was associated with higher TNF-α levels (84.97 pg/mL vs. 29.20 pg/mL in subtype G, p = 0.065) and markedly higher CD4 counts (304.5 cells/mm³ vs. 140.4 cells/mm³, p = 1.19E-05) in Table 9. These differences may suggest subtype-specific pathogenesis mechanisms, potentially through differential immune activation or viral replication dynamics. Other researchers have similarly observed subtype-driven immune responses, though findings vary by region and methodology [37]. In assessing the relationship between HIV-1 subtypes and host responses, patients with CRF02_AG displayed a more activated inflammatory profile. Despite their better CD4 preservation, they exhibited higher levels of IL-10 and TNF-α, both of which have been associated with systemic inflammation and immune exhaustion [50]. This could imply a subtype-dependent modulation of immune tolerance versus activation, with CRF02_AG possibly promoting chronic inflammation even in the face of immune cell preservation. Moreover, while both subtypes had similar viral loads at ART initiation and 6 months (p > 0.7), the pronounced differences in CD4 count and TNF-α may point to qualitative variations in immune damage not captured by viral load alone. These findings echo reports that subtype CRF02_AG may induce stronger inflammatory cascades, contributing to non-AIDS events and comorbidities despite virological control [51]. These findings suggest that certain HIV-1 subtypes, particularly CRF02_AG and Subtype G, may be associated with a more pronounced inflammatory response in the context of virological failure. This aligns with existing literature indicating that different HIV-1 subtypes can elicit varying cytokine profiles and levels of immune activation [13]. A heightened inflammatory state, as reflected by elevated IL-6, hs-CRP, and TNF-α, can contribute to immune dysfunction, tissue damage, and potentially accelerate disease progression, even in the presence of ART [52]. The elevated IL-10 levels across these subtypes in VF further highlight the complex, often ineffective, compensatory anti-inflammatory mechanisms at play [37].

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifactorial nature of HIV-1 virological failure, linking it to persistent inflammation, drug resistance, and HIV-1 subtype diversity. Elevated biomarkers (IL-6, hs-CRP, TNF-α) were predictive of poor outcomes, even in ART-suppressed individuals. Precision medicine, adherence support, and subtype-specific interventions are recommended. Future directions include longitudinal monitoring, pharmacogenomics, gut microbiome research, and integrated biomarker panels. These findings support region-specific strategies to improve HIV outcomes and guide tailored clinical management in high-burden settings like Minna, Niger State.
