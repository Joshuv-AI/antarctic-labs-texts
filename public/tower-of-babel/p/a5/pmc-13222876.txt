Spatiotemporal dynamics of carbon emissions induced by land-use change and their implications for climate resilience in West African drylands
Authors: Issaka Abdou Razakou Kiribou, Kangbéni Dimobe, Tiga Neya, Sintayehu W Dejene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the effects of land-use and land-cover change (LULC) on carbon emissions is crucial for ensuring the sustainable management of dryland ecosystems. This study investigates spatial and temporal changes in carbon stock associated with LULC in one of the key biodiversity hotspots of the West African drylands. The study uses a Landsat imagery dataset spanning 2000, 2010, and 2022, processed in Google Earth Engine using a random forest algorithm. LULC-induced changes in carbon stock are estimated using ecosystem-specific emission factors for the designated study area. Additionally, integrating NDVI and climate stressors enables the projection of vegetation productivity and future carbon trajectories. The findings indicate a substantial decline in wooded savanna (~ 21%), tree savanna (7%), and shrub savanna (5%), alongside a ~ 35% increase in cropland. These transitions generated an estimated 300,000 MgC of carbon stock changes between 2000 and 2022. Climate projections under SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios indicate continued warming and reductions in annual rainfall of 5.414 mm and 9.359 mm, respectively, leading to additional carbon increases of ~ 1.3 MgC/ha and ~ 2 MgC/ha by 2070. These combined pressures accentuate land degradation and climate feedbacks. It emphasizes the need for integrated strategies to enhance climate resilience in West African dryland ecosystems in line with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 15).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47031-6.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Global warming, the phenomenon of increasing Earth’s average surface temperature over the last two centuries, is driven by enhanced atmospheric greenhouse gas emissions1. Globally, the average atmospheric CO₂ concentration in 2021 was 414.71 ± 0.1 ppm, representing a total anthropogenic CO₂ emission above pre-industrial levels of more than 50%2,3. This increase in carbon emissions’ concentration strengthens the effect of greenhouse gases and warms the planet, making climate change a major risk facing the global population today4. Land use and land cover change (LULC) and energy have become the major contributors to this dynamic. Following energy-related emissions, LULC has become the second-largest contributor to carbon emissions and the primary driver of deforestation5. The recent report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) revealed that LULC, including deforestation, accounts for about 12% of global greenhouse gas emissions6. Global anthropogenic carbon emissions from LULC over 60 years (1961 to 2020) were 215 Pg C, and, at the same time, removed 142 Pg C, resulting in a net carbon emission of about 1.21 Pg C year − 17. Moreover, the combined effect of forest regrowth and logging on abandoned land accounts for about 10 to 25% of anthropogenically induced carbon emissions8.

Despite the high added value in climate mitigation and biodiversity conservation through enhanced carbon sequestration, natural reserves are still facing rapid degradation under human pressure9. This situation, characterized by land clearing due to deforestation, alters the carbon sequestration capabilities of natural reserves by destroying vegetation and degrading soil properties10–12, leading to increasing climate change impacts and landscape modification. Due to their effects on terrestrial ecosystems, LULC-induced changes in carbon stocks have become a global concern, particularly in wildlife reserves that serve as carbon sink hotspots13. The assessment of these changes involves detecting differences in the state of an area over time and across space, where satellite imagery plays a crucial role in early warning of ecological shifts and in sustainable land management14. Much research has increasingly emphasized the need to integrate remote sensing carbon accounting approaches. This includes incorporating climate projections for a better understanding of LULC, vegetation dynamics, and climate variability interactions, particularly in vulnerable dryland ecosystems15,16. However, empirical assessments linking carbon emissions, vegetation, and future climate scenarios remain limited for West African drylands.

The international initiatives, such as the REDD+ mechanism, could contribute to achieving climate mitigation objectives in this region, as it encouraged countries to implement reforestation and conservation programs supported by carbon credit offsets8,17. These initiatives are highly important in an ecologically sensitive country such as Burkina Faso, located in the West African Sahel, which is often under pressure from agricultural land expansion and unsustainable grazing18. These pressures modify vegetation structure and carbon sequestration dynamics, potentially contributing to regional greenhouse gas (GHG) and increasing vulnerability to climate change impacts19. Known for its diverse savanna ecosystems, natural reserves cover 14% of the country’s land area20. Many of these reserves are experiencing significant degradation driven by anthropogenic pressure through LULC20. These pressures affect the country’s REDD+ implementation, since the Agricultural Forestry and other Land Use (AFOLU) sector accounts for 83% of total carbon emissions21. Thus, monitoring LULC dynamics provides essential information to support data-driven decisions aimed at mitigating anthropogenic pressure and promoting sustainable land management practices that contribute to ecosystem resilience22,23.

Bontioli Natural Reserve (BNR), one of Burkina Faso’s most important protected areas, continues to experience increasing degradation associated with anthropogenic land-use pressures. These have contributed to substantial shifts in vegetation cover and the destruction of tree species20. Climate change and variability further exacerbate these dynamics, potentially intensifying vegetation decline and ecosystem vulnerability24. Although previous studies have documented vegetation degradation and LULC pressures in the reserve, the extent to which LULC changes contribute to carbon stock changes and reinforce climate variability remains insufficiently quantified. In particular, there is limited spatially explicit information on how LULC transitions influence carbon stock and vegetation dynamics within the reserve. Addressing this knowledge gap requires an integrative assessment of the spatiotemporal dynamics of LULC and its associated carbon release. Such analysis provides valuable insights into potential interactions among climate variability, LULC dynamics, and changes in carbon stocks. Thus, this study aims to assess the spatiotemporal dynamics of LULC and their implications for changes in carbon stock and ecosystem climate resilience in the BNR. It specifically addresses the following research questions: (i) What are the dominant LULC transitions occurring in the BNR over time? (ii) How do these LULC transitions contribute to spatial and temporal patterns of carbon stock changes? (iii) How might projected climate scenarios influence future carbon trajectory dynamics in the reserve?

Therefore, the study specifically aims to: (1) identify the major LULC dynamics and quantify their associated carbon stock dynamics, (2) identify LULC-induced carbon stock change hotspots and assess their spatial and temporal variations in the BNR, and (3) evaluate potential future carbon stock dynamics under selected climate scenarios. By combining remote sensing analysis, carbon accounting approaches, and climate projections, this study provides a landscape-scale assessment of LULC-related changes in carbon stock in a semi-arid natural reserve. The results are intended to support evidence-based sustainable land management planning strategies and to contribute to a better understanding of carbon-climate interactions in West African dryland ecosystems.

Literature review and conceptual framework

The land use change-related carbon stock dynamics mechanism involves the development in a way that the amount of carbon deposited is released through several anthropogenic activities, which include deforestation, soil carbon loss from agriculture, forest degradation, land clearing, biomass burning, and urban development19. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) defines the amount of carbon released from land use as the quantity of carbon dioxide (CO₂) emitted, including other greenhouse gases (GHGs), resulting from anthropogenic land-use change25. It primarily comprises nitrous oxide (N2O), methane (CH4), and carbon dioxide (CO2), which are emitted in the troposphere due to deforestation, the expansion of cropland, and human settlement (urbanization), which significantly contribute to global warming6,26.

Carbon dynamics simulated by the global vegetation model for LULC have been substantially underestimated. This is due to land clearing, tree collection, and the shifting cultivation process that have not been considered27. Understanding changes in LULC carbon-related stocks is crucial for understanding the scientific basis of climate system connections and implications, and is vital for West African dryland carbon neutrality targets. The significant contribution of LULC to carbon emissions in arid and semi-arid regions impacts ecosystem services and biodiversity integrity6,26. Thus, the West African dryland has had a substantial impact on LULC dynamics in the natural reserve, resulting in a ~ 7% loss of forested area in 202228.

In the past, LULC has meaningfully amplified emissions of carbon globally, with South and eastern Asia, Latin America, and Sub-Saharan Africa being the greatest contributing regions in carbon net emissions29,30. This amplified carbon-emission contribution to climate change necessitates that LULC be examined in depth from a conservation and climate-action perspective at the local, regional, and national levels29,31. This could significantly contribute to achieving the goal of the Paris Agreement and many of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), as the dynamic feedback loop between climate and the carbon cycle includes both negative and positive feedback31.

Thus, positive feedback is when temperatures increase, leading to the activation of forest fires, soil respiration, and additional carbon emissions32,33. The negative feedback loop in the climate system occurs when human activities increase carbon emissions, which may stimulate vegetation growth through CO₂ fertilization. This process enhances carbon uptake, which can be affected by various factors related to environmental change34,35. The relevance of this dynamic is that LULC alters local microclimates and land-surface properties, such as evapotranspiration and land-surface temperature. These influence the magnitude of carbon storage and the sensitivity of ecosystems to climate variability in vulnerable regions such as drylands36. Therefore, climate modelling and its projections, such as the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 6 (CMIP6), for understanding future impacts must accurately incorporate LULC dynamics36,37. As a minor shift in climatic conditions in West African drylands often highly impacts people’s livelihoods, exacerbates water scarcity, and land degradation38, incorporating climate scenarios across various socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), is essential for regional resilience39.

To support the sustainability of drylands management in the face of the combined effects of LULC and climate, Nature-Based Solutions (NbS) can be a potential support40,41. For example, the scenarios in climate projection downscaling and their incorporation into temporal and spatial analysis support the implementation of NbS in natural reserve resilience strategies16. The results of this approach could help mitigate future carbon emissions by identifying potential restoration areas with climate-resilient tree species6,42. As the BNR is located in a semi-arid zone, which is highly vulnerable to the effects of climate change and human pressures, assessing LULC-associated carbon emissions is essential to maintaining the socio-ecological system and mitigating ecosystem service loss. Thus, addressing climate-LULC impacts is crucial for effective ecosystem resilience43,44. Understanding LULC-associated carbon emissions and the effects of climate change on protected areas could support people’s livelihoods in this semi-arid region45.

Results

Main LULC change dynamics and their influence on carbon stock

The three periods of LULC dynamics, spanning 2000, 2010, and 2022, revealed the rapid transformation of the reserve landscape over time. The spatial and temporal dynamics of LULC highlighted that the reserve vegetation has changed dramatically from 2000 to 2022, with the wooded savanna and tree savanna being the most impacted by anthropogenic LULC. The area of the tree savanna decreased from 9,809 ha in 2000 to 6,745 ha in 2022. The wooded savanna also displayed these dynamics, with the area significantly reduced from 15,02 ha to 5,667 ha between 2000 and 2022. The changes in LULC show a sharp decline in wooded savanna from 33.3% in 2000 to 12.5% in 2022, representing a loss of 21% over the past twenty-two years (Fig. 1).

LULC-induced carbon stocks dynamics from 2000 to 2022

The temporal dynamic assessment of carbon stocks across LULC classes indicates that Wooded savanna stored the largest share of landscape carbon in 2000, followed by shrub savanna with approximately 600,000 MgC and 200,000 MgC, respectively (Fig. 3A). In contrast, cropland and grass savanna exhibited considerably lower carbon stocks reflecting their lower biomass density.

Positive values indicate carbon gains, while negative values represent carbon losses associated with LULC classes to overall carbon variation over time.

In 2010, a moderate decline in the carbon stock of the Wooded savanna was observed, suggesting a reduction in woody vegetation cover. By 2022, the distribution of carbon stocks across LULC classes had changed substantially. Cropland accounted for the largest share of estimated carbon stock (500,000 MgC), while carbon storage in wooded savanna declined to approximately 300,000 MgC. Despite the wooded savanna having a higher carbon density per hectare, cropland contributes a larger total carbon stock due to its greater spatial extent. Overall, this represents a nearly 50% reduction in the initial carbon stock of the wooded savanna between 2000 and 2022, indicating significant land transformation during the research period. These changes suggest that LULC transitions, particularly the expansion of cropland into natural vegetation areas, have contributed to the redistribution of carbon stocks within the reserve. Such a shift may reflect increasing anthropogenic pressures, such as agricultural expansion or land conversion. However, because this carbon estimate is based on emission factors and approaches, the results may not fully capture the complex ecological processes that influence biomass dynamics.

Carbon stock changes analysis indicates that cropland expansion is associated with substantial carbon losses of −150,000 MgC between 2000 and 2010 (Fig. 3B). This likely reflects the conversion of vegetated land to agricultural areas during this period. Thus, from 2010 to 2022, cropland experienced an even greater carbon loss, approaching − 300,000 MgC, highlighting a continued land pressure and vegetation clearing. Other LULC classes exhibited more moderate carbon dynamics. Grass savannas remained relatively stable during most of the study period, but showed a slight decline by 2022. The gallery forest also maintained relatively stable carbon stocks with minor reductions in the later period. Shrub savanna consistently exhibited lower carbon storage capacity, reflecting its limited woody biomass. In general, the analysis suggests a LULC trade-off, in which cropland expansion contributed to carbon losses. At the same time, some natural vegetation classes exhibited localized or temporary increases in estimated carbon storage.

Moreover, at the temporal scale, monthly carbon dynamics reveal pronounced seasonal variability between 2000 and 2022 (Fig. 4A). Carbon loss was more pronounced during certain years, particularly between 2010 and 2014, coinciding with a high LULC dynamics rate of roughly 6%, compared with 2.5% between 2000 and 2010 (Figure S2). This temporal dynamic reveals that the reserve has experienced an average carbon loss of about − 0.82 MgC, with the average annual carbon stock decreasing from 3.24 MgC in 2000 to 2.42 MgC in 2022.

Temporal carbon stock dynamics: A Monthly carbon stock dynamics from 2000 to 2022, B Monthly average carbon stock changes over the period of 2000 to 2022.

Seasonal analysis further reveals a clear pattern in carbon dynamics (Fig. 4B) Net carbon loss occurs predominantly between October and March, corresponding to the dry season when vegetation productivity declines and biomass burning is more likely. Conversely, carbon gains are observed between April and September, coinciding with the rainy season and increased vegetation growth. These seasonal fluctuations indicate that climatic conditions and vegetation phenology strongly influence short-term carbon dynamics. Moreover, spatial analysis shows that carbon stocks were more concentrated and continuous across the reserve in 2000, whereas by 2022, they appear more fragmented and spatially sparse. This indicates increasing landscape degradation and vegetation loss. The carbon degradation hotspots identified in 2022 correspond closely with areas experiencing significant LULC changes.

Correlation analysis reveals a strong relationship between forest conversion and carbon stock changes (R2 = 0.95), indicating that land cover transitions are a major driver of carbon stock changes (Table 1). In contrast, the relationship between net carbon emissions and total forest carbon stock was moderate.

Discussion

LULC dynamics, carbon stock changes, and climate change feedbacks

This study underscores the pivotal interrelationships between LULC and carbon dynamics in semi-arid ecosystems, which are increasingly exposed to both anthropogenic and environmental stressors. The observed spatiotemporal patterns in LULC reveal significant transformations, predominantly driven by agricultural encroachment. Similar processes have been widely reported across dryland ecosystems, where anthropogenic land-use pressure drives vegetation degradation in carbon storage46. Between 2000 and 2022, woody savanna ecosystems experienced substantial degradation, as evidenced by the conversion to cropland, which increased from 8.5% in 2000 to 34.8% in 2022. Such transitions are associated with significant carbon stock depletion, especially given the higher carbon densities of woody vegetation relative to agricultural land47.

The conservative estimation of carbon stock showed increasing trends aligned with ongoing LULC change. However, these values should be interpreted as model-based estimates derived from emission factors and NDVI-derived biomass proxies rather than direct measurements of ecosystem carbon fluxes. Remote sensing approaches using vegetation indices such as NDVI have been applied to estimate vegetation productivity, including biomass, across large spatial scales48. However, this approach can introduce uncertainty when applied to structurally heterogeneous ecosystems, such as savannas49.

Moreover, savanna ecosystems are often influenced by some disturbance regimes such as fire and grazing50. Such context regulates vegetation structure, biomass accumulation, and seasonal productivity. For instance, fire plays a dominant role in determining savanna carbon dynamics by periodically removing biomass, while maintaining the coexistence of woody and herbaceous vegetation51. Similarly, grazing pressure can further modify vegetation composition and productivity, which, combined with fire regimes, can influence NDVI signals and carbon storage patterns52. This is because disturbance-driven variables were not explicitly quantified in this study; part of the observed variability in estimated carbon stocks may reflect disturbance-driven vegetation dynamics rather than solely LULC.

The SSP1-2.6 and 5–8.5.5 climate projection scenarios revealed a warming trend, accompanied by declines in rainfall and increases in temperature. These projections were used to explore potential environmental pressures on vegetation dynamics rather than to predict future carbon stocks from LULC. This climate variability has been widely recognized as an important factor affecting vegetation productivity and the carbon cycle in set-aside ecosystems53. In drylands, rising temperatures and associated reductions in rainfall may exacerbate drought stress and accelerate land degradation. Similar interactions between LULC dynamics and regional environmental processes have been reported in other regions. For instance, studies from the Yangtze River Delta indicate that large-scale land use transformation can alter regional hydrological processes and land-atmosphere interactions54. These comparisons are intended to provide contextual understanding rather than implying identical ecological mechanisms within the BNR ecosystem. Overall, the results highlight the importance of considering LULC dynamics in climate adaptation land management planning.

Regional context and implications for climate resilience and ecosystem management

In the West African drylands, reductions in vegetation cover due to drought and land clearing may influence local climate patterns through changes in evapotranspiration and surface energy balance55. Although these processes were not directly measured in this study, the observed LULC dynamics suggest potential interaction between land degradation and climate variability that co-evolve over time. As in BNR, vegetation cover degradation associated with anthropogenically driven land conversion may contribute to increases in land surface temperature, including reductions in vegetation productivity. However, the present study identifies spatial patterns of association rather than a direct causal relationship between LULC and climate variables. Similar dynamics have been documented across the Sahel, where agricultural expansion often occurred at the expense of savannas and woodlands, reducing the region’s carbon sink potential and contributing to landscape fragmentation56. These changes have important implications for ecosystem services, climate regulation, and biodiversity preservation in dryland protected areas55.

Despite localized increases in estimated carbon stocks being observed in some LULC classes, these patterns should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of ecosystem regeneration. Without field-based data on vegetation structure, species composition, or ecosystem functioning, it remains unclear whether these changes represent ecological recovery. Thus, temporally increasing vegetation productivity may reflect either actual ecological improvement or methodological variability associated with NDVI-based biomass estimation. Similar uncertainties in interpreting remotely sensed biomass changes have been reported in other savanna ecosystems57. The results of this study nevertheless have an important implication for ecosystem management and climate resilience in drylands. Nature-based solutions (NbS), including assisted natural regeneration, agroforestry systems, and sustainable land management practices, could help regenerate degraded landscapes while enhancing ecological resilience and supporting local livelihoods16. Additionally, climate mitigation mechanisms such as REDD + may provide opportunities to support forest and savanna restoration initiatives, including promoting sustainable land management58. Previous studies in Burkina Faso have demonstrated the carbon sequestration potential of agroforestry parkland and community-based landscape restoration initiatives59. Integrating LULC monitoring with climate adaptation planning could improve decision-making and early identification of land degradation trends.

Limitations and future research direction

This study developed an empirical modelling approach to estimate carbon dynamics using NDVI-derived biomass proxies and emission factors in the semi-arid ecosystem. Despite NDVI being widely used as a proxy for vegetation productivity and biomass estimation, it has known limitations when applied to heterogeneous ecosystems like savanna vegetation. NDVI saturation effects and seasonal vegetation variability may influence biomass estimation accuracy60. Thus, disturbance regimes, such as fire, grazing, and seasonal vegetation dynamics, also influence NDVI signals and can potentially introduce additional uncertainties into carbon stock estimates. Future studies could therefore integrate disturbance-monitoring datasets, such as fire occurrence and grazing intensity, to improve the accuracy of carbon estimation.

Another limitation of this study is the use of emission factors to estimate carbon content. This approach assumes a relatively stable biomass-carbon relationship across vegetation types. However, such relationships may vary with vegetation species, composition, and ecological conditions, which can also introduce uncertainty into biomass estimation61. In addition, soil carbon stocks were not directly measured in the field but were derived from national emission factors tailored to the reserve ecosystem62. This could introduce uncertainties, as soil carbon, a key component of terrestrial carbon budgets, may respond differently to LULC depending on soil types and management practices, particularly in dryland ecosystems63. Classification uncertainties in LULC mapping can propagate into carbon stock estimates, despite not being explicitly quantified in the present study. Future research could incorporate uncertainty analysis and improved classification validation to enhance the reliability of carbon accounting.

Furthermore, future research could integrate field-based biomass sampling, species-level vegetation data, and ecosystem modeling approaches such as the Integrated Valuation of Ecosystem Services and Tradeoffs (InVEST) model. This could enable more comprehensive assessment of both above-ground biomass and soil carbon dynamics while enhancing spatial scales of carbon estimation for more reliable conservation activities64,65. Finally, field validation of biomass estimates and emission factors would effectively enhance the robustness of carbon accounting and support effective climate-adaptive land management strategies in dryland protected areas.

Methods and materials

Study area

Located in the Southwest of Burkina Faso, the Bontioli Natural Reserve (BNR) remains one of the key biodiversity hotspots in the West African dryland (Fig. 8). With an area of 46.8 km², the reserve is managed into two main areas: the Total Reserve, covering 13.8 km²; and the Partial Reserve, covering 33.0 km². The Total Reserve is a strictly protected area with limited human access and is classified as an IUCN Category I. The Partial Reserve design for the species management area falls under IUCN Category IV. A colonial administrative body, similar to other protected areas in the West African Dryland, established the reserve in 1957. The reserve is therefore entirely within the phytogeography of the South Sudanese zone, where the annual average rainfall ranges from 900 to 1000 mm. The vegetation is primarily dominated by trees and wooded savanna66. The dry season runs from November to May, and the average annual temperature ranges from 26 to 32 °C.

Remote sensing datasets

We used Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) imagery to classify LULC for 2022. This approach builds on previous assessments of LULC dynamics from 2000 to 2022 conducted by Kiribou et al. (2024)20. Satellite datasets (Level 2, Collection 2, Tier 1) from both Landsat 7 and Landsat 8, each with a spatial resolution of 30 m, were retrieved and processed in the Google Earth Engine (GEE) data repository67. Additionally, digital elevation data derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission68 was incorporated to enhance classification accuracy. The Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) zone 30 North (EPSG:32630) projection was applied to all spatial datasets. A total of 78 images, comprising 40 for ETM+, 36 for OLI-TIRS, and 1 for DEM, were used. Table 1 summarizes the remote sensing datasets used in this study.

Main LULC class identification

From the eight LULC categories assessed by Kiribou et al. This LULC classification was performed on the GEE platform after the surface reflectance assessment (Fig. S1). We selected optimal images with cloud-free conditions, thereby enhancing classification accuracy. This, therefore, allowed the use of multitemporal images, considering the months with the highest surface reflectance. Supervised machine learning with the Random Forest (RF) algorithm was used, with the data partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets based on ground-truth points. The RF algorithm constructs multiple decision trees, each built from bootstrap samples of the training dataset, and is widely adopted for satellite image classification70. Classification accuracy was assessed using Cohen’s kappa indices computed from confusion matrices generated from the test data (Table S1). Kappa values range from − 1 to 1, with values greater than 0.75 indicating strong agreement. The computed Kappa indices for 2000, 2010, and 2022 were 0.96, 0.92, and 0.89, respectively, confirming robust classification performance.

Map visualization was performed using QGIS software version 3.2071 while Statistical analyses and plotting were conducted using R software version 4.2.172.

In this study, carbon stock refers to the amount of carbon stored in ecosystem pools (e.g., vegetation biomass), expressed in Mg C ha⁻¹, whereas carbon emissions and carbon sequestration describe increases or decreases in carbon stock associated with LULC and vegetation dynamics. As in LULC studies, carbon stock is the most commonly used metric; we consistently use carbon stock change to describe increases or decreases. This avoids confusion between the terms “emission” and “sequestration”.

Rationale for NDVI-based projections

NDVI is a direct measure of vegetation greenness, health, and density, capturing degradation, regrowth, and drought stress factors not fully represented by static LULC classes82,83.

The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 6, CMIP6, of the CHELSA Climatologies with high spatial resolution (30 arc sec, ~1 km) for the Earth’s Land Surface Areas (CHELSA) version 2.1, is used with the two selected shared socio-economic pathways (SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5)81 to assess future carbon stocks. CHELSA is well-suited as the data are based mainly on statistical downscaling of atmospheric temperatures and precipitation. They provide the Global Climate Models (GCMs) with periods including future scenarios from CMIP684–86. The two future projections (SSPs 245 and 585) up to 2070 provide different mitigation challenges. SSP1-2.6 represents a scenario with moderate challenges for both climate change mitigation and adaptation pathways, and SSP5-8.5 is the scenario with significant stock loss pathways87,].
