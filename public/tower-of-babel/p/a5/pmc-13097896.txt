Akkermansia muciniphila confers renal protection in chronic kidney disease: a multi-omics mechanistic investigation
Authors: Yanan Ban, Hailin Zhang, Yan Xu, Fei Chen, Qianqian Wei, Xiaoyan Wen, Lixia Yin, Zhijuan Dong, Qifan Zhou, Wenwen Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAkkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) improved serum metabolism and renal fibrosis in the mouse model of chronic kidney disease (CKD) via the gut-kidney axis, slowing renal function decline. However, the biological components and underlying metabolic pathways remain unclear. Using a CKD mouse model, we aimed to identify the biological constituents of A. muciniphila that drive its regulatory effects in renal injury. Integrated microbiome and metabolomics analyses further elucidated the metabolic mechanisms of renoprotection, providing a theoretical foundation for the development of evidence-based clinical interventions.MethodsA CKD mouse model was established using 5/6 nephrectomy, with sham-operated mice (n = 7) serving as controls. Twenty-eight CKD mice were randomly assigned to four groups and treated with PBS, A. muciniphila, pasteurised A. muciniphila, or A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin by gavage. Serum and kidney tissues were collected to assess renal function, and histopathology was performed to identify the key biological components of A. muciniphila. Faecal samples were subjected to integrated microbiome and metabolomic analyses to identify the metabolic pathways involved in renoprotection. Behavioural experiments were performed to observe the effect of A. muciniphila on the behaviour of CKD mice. Single-factor analysis of variance and post-hoc tests were used for intergroup comparisons.ResultsSerum analysis showed that the levels of serum creatinine, urea nitrogen and cystatin C in mice treated with A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin were significantly decreased. Reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction showed that the renal injury marker Kim-1 was significantly decreased after A. muciniphila intervention. The levels of the renal injury (Ngal) and fibrosis (Col1a1, TIMP-1 and Fibronectin) markers showed a downward trend. 16 S rRNA analysis revealed that, following A. muciniphila intervention, the health index of the intestinal flora in CKD mice was significantly increased; however, the abundances of Turicibacter, Dubosiella and norank_f_UCG-010 were decreased. Metabolomic analysis revealed a strong correlation between A. muciniphila and the tryptophan metabolic pathway. Behavioural experiments showed that the exercise activity and anxiety-like behaviour of CKD mice were significantly improved after intervention with A. muciniphila, and the effect of A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin was better than that of A. muciniphila or pasteurised A. muciniphila alone.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin intervention ameliorates kidney injury, body dysfunction, and anxiety-like behaviour, while delaying disease progression in CKD mice. These effects suggest that bioactive substances secreted by A. muciniphila play a key regulatory role and are closely related to tryptophan metabolism in the intestine. In addition, our results indicate that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota in CKD mice suppresses the regulatory potential of A. muciniphila. This study lays an experimental foundation for future biological mechanism research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04868-1.Keywords: Akkermansia muciniphila, Chronic kidney disease, Gut microbiota, Vancomycin, Gut metabolism

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition marked by structural abnormalities and a gradual decline in renal function. It is projected to become the fifth leading cause of death globally by 2040, presenting a significant challenge to public health systems worldwide [1]. Studies have demonstrated that in response to various injurious stimuli, intrinsic renal cells are activated and transformed into myofibroblasts, which subsequently synthesise and secrete substantial collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) components. This ultimately leads to renal tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis [2]. In recent years, the concept of the “gut-kidney axis” has provided novel insights into the pathogenesis and progression of CKD [3]. Evidence indicated that renal filtration is impaired in CKD, causing the accumulation of uraemic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate (IS) and p-cresol sulfate (PCS), in the gut, contributing to intestinal flora dysbiosis and increased intestinal barrier permeability. This disruption facilitates the translocation of harmful substances across the intestinal mucosa into the systemic circulation, triggering a state of systemic neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which accelerates disease progression and forms a vicious cycle [4]. Long-term oxidative stress and a systemic microinflammatory state induced by chronic diseases cause a decline in body function in patients with CKD, which manifests as increased mental load, decreased motor function and increased complications, affecting the quality of life of patients and increasing the risk of death [5].

Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila ) is a human gut probiotic that has attracted significant attention recently, showing potential benefits in regulating intestinal homeostasis and metabolic function and improving inflammation [6]. Studies have shown that bacterial metabolites and proteins are the primary biological components of probiotics [7]. The metabolic products of A. muciniphila directly influence the host or synergistically interact with the gut microbes to confer probiotic effects. Notably, even after pasteurisation, A. muciniphila retains surface proteins and extracellular vesicles that maintain continuous interactions with the intestinal flora to mediate host effects [8]. Although pasteurised A. muciniphila shows significantly enhanced effects compared with live A. muciniphila in repairing intestinal mucosal barrier and improving metabolism [9], a study has demonstrated that pasteurised A. muciniphila activates the classical nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway by promoting the TLR2-MyD88-NF-κB signalling axis, promoting M-cell maturation that paradoxically increases susceptibility to Salmonella infection in mice [10]. Studies have shown that the abundance of A. muciniphila is significantly lower in patients with CKD than in healthy individuals, and that this decrease is negatively correlated with disease severity [11]. Additionally, animal studies have indicated that A. muciniphila supplementation improves renal function and attenuates renal interstitial fibrosis in CKD models [12]. However, the specific biological components of A. muciniphila that drive its renoprotective effects and the underlying mechanisms by which A. muciniphila modulates serum metabolism and renal fibrosis remain unclear. Therefore, by establishing a CKD mouse model through 5/6 nephrectomy, we aimed to investigate the effects of different A. muciniphila components on renal function, gut microbiota and metabolic profiles in the model mice via interventions involving live A. muciniphila, pasteurised A. muciniphila and A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin. In addition, we sought to elucidate the key biological components of A. muciniphila that contribute to renoprotection in CKD and explore the underlying metabolic pathways.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture and preparation

A. muciniphila (CICC 24917) was obtained from the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection. After successful recovery, the strains were inoculated into BHI liquid medium supplemented with 0.2% mucin and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h. Bacterial concentration was determined spectrophotometrically before each gavage, with a final concentration of 6.32 × 10⁸ colony-forming units (cfu) per mL [13]. Pasteurised bacterial suspension was prepared by heating the A. muciniphila suspension at 70 °C for 30 min under anaerobic conditions.

Animal model and interventions

Forty male SPF-grade C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 20 ± 2 g were purchased from J Gem Pharmatech Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in an environment with a room temperature of 20 ± 2 °C, a relative humidity of 30% − 50%, and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to sterile food and water. After one week of acclimatization, 33 mice were randomly selected to construct CKD model by modified 5/6 nephrectomy [14], and 7 mice were used as blank control by sham operation. During the subsequent 4-week modeling period, 5 mice died following surgery. After 4 weeks of feeding, to promote colonization of A. muciniphila, the rats were treated with quadruple antibiotics (ampicillin sodium 100 mg/kg, neomycin sulfate 100 mg/kg, metronidazole 100 mg/kg, vancomycin 50 mg/kg, solvent was pure water, gave volume 10 mL/kg, twice a day for 1 week. Scavenging intestinal microbes to promote A. muciniphila colonization [15]. The 28 surviving CKD mice were randomly divided into four groups: the model group (n = 6) was treated with PBS, and the akk group (n = 8) was treated with A. muciniphila. The pakk group (n = 7) was given pasteurised A. muciniphila. The vakk group (n = 7) was treated with A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin. A. muciniphila is resistant to vancomycin (MIC > 64 mg/L), and vancomycin can eliminate most of the endogenous intestinal flora and destroy the interaction between intestinal flora in CKD mice. Preliminary observations indicate the role played by A. muciniphila themselves or their metabolites [16]. Bacterial intervention started 3 days after the end of antibiotic intervention, 0.4 ml per mouse, once a day, 5 days per week for 8 weeks. During the intervention, the mice in each group ate and drank regularly, while the mice in the vakk group received bacterial intervention and vancomycin (50 mg/kg) by gavage. At the end of the intervention, 3–5 fresh fecal samples were collected and placed in sterile centrifuge tubes, and frozen at -80 °C. Blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, the kidney tissues were separated under sterile conditions, and stored in 10% formalin solution and liquid nitrogen for subsequent experimental analysis, and their serum was isolated and stored at − 80 °C. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval Number: IACUC-2409009). The preparation of this research report adheres to the requirements of ARRIVE 2.0 (Guidelines for Reporting Results of Animal Experiments).

Renal function

The levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr) and cystatin C (Cys-C) in serum were analyzed using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800, China).

Histological staining analysis

The isolated kidney tissue was fixed in 10% formalin and dehydrated using ethanol and xylene. They were then embedded using paraffin and cut into thin sections. The sections were processed to remove paraffin, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), dehydrated, transparent, sealed, and imaged under a light microscope. The severity of the lesions was scored from 0 to 4 based on the degree of abnormality: the score was 0.5 for slight or very little, 1 for mild or little, 2 for moderate or more, 3 for severe or more, 4 for very severe or a lot, and 0 for normal. To evaluate renal fibrosis, Masson’s staining was performed, and the fibrotic area was quantified using Image J software [17].

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from renal tissue using TRizol reagent according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer, the concentration and purity of RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer, and the HiScript Q RT SuperMix kit was applied for cDNA synthesis reactions. The mixture was prepared according to the reagents provided by Cham Q SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (2X), and then the reaction was carried out in a QuantStudio™5 384-well quantitative real-time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA). The reaction conditions were as follows: predenaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 60 s, and 40 cycles of amplification. All amplifications were conducted in triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method using GAPDH as the internal control gene [18]. Gut microbiota and metabolomics

Analysis of gut microbiota 16 S rRNA sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from murine fecal samples using the Fast Pure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (Yuhua, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR with the primers 338 F (5’-ACT CCT ACG GGA GGC AGC AG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGA CTA CHV GGG TWT CT A AT-3’) [19]. PCR products were purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) and quantified by Synergy HTX (Biotek, USA). The purified PCR products were then subjected to library construction using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit and sequenced on the NextSeq 2000 PE300 (Illumina, USA).

Raw FASTQ files were quality-controlled and filtered using fastp software (version 0.20.0). Paired-end reads were then assembled using FLASH software (version 1.2.11). The sequences were clustered into OTUs at a 97% similarity threshold using the UPARSE software (version 11), during which chimeric sequences were identified and removed. Taxonomic annotation of the OTU representatives was performed using the RDP classifier (version 2.13) software against the Silva 16 S rRNA database (v138) with a confidence threshold of 70%. Alpha diversity indices, including Chaos and Shannon, were calculated using mothur (version 1.30.2) software, and intergroup differences were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. Beta diversity was evaluated through principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and the significance of community structure differentiation was determined using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM). The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) method was employed to identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa from phylum to genus level (LDA score > 1, p < 0.05). A correlation network was constructed based on Spearman correlation analysis (|r| > 0.5, p < 0.05). Calculated p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR), with a significance threshold set at FDR ≤ 0.05.

LC-MS metabolomic analysis

Fecal samples (50 mg) were put into a 2mL centrifuge tube, 400µL extraction solvent composed of methanol and water (4:1, v/v) was added, the sample was ground with a tissue lyser under cryogenic conditions (-10 °C, 50 Hz) for 6 min. The mixtures were subsequently maintained at -20 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to autosampler vials for LC-MS analysis. Metabolite separation was achieved using ACQUITY HSS T3 column, and analysis was performed on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Raw data were preprocessed with Progenesis QI software (Waters, Milford, USA) to generate a finalized data matrix for subsequent statistical analysis. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted using the ropls package in R language (Version1.6.2), and verified the stability of the data. Significantly altered metabolites between groups were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values derived from the OPLS-DA model and p-values from Student’s t-test, and were subsequently assembled into a differential metabolite set. p values were corrected by FDR, and FDR ≤ 0.05 was used as the threshold. Metabolic pathway annotation of these differential metabolites was performed by referencing the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to elucidate their involved biological pathways.

Behavioral tests

This study employed the Open Field Test (OFT) and the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) to comprehensively reflect the motor ability and anxiety state of mice [20–22]. In the open field experiment, each mouse was individually placed in the center of a square arena (50 × 50 × 50 cm), and the total distance and average speed of mice in the unit time were recorded during the experiment. The elevated plus maze consists of two open arms and two enclosed arms intersecting at a central platform, elevated above the floor. The total movement distance and speed of the mice were recorded. Before the formal test, the mice were acclimated to the dim light in the test room for 60 min, and then the mice were placed on different devices and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. All behavioral sessions were automatically tracked and recorded using the AnyMaze video tracking system. After the test of each mouse, 75% ethanol was used to thoroughly clean and dry the inner wall and bottom surface of the experimental chamber to eliminate the interference of the remaining olfactory signal on the subsequent experiment.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups of normal distribution and equal variance data, and then LSD test was used. When variances were not homogeneous, Welch test was applied. For data that did not follow a normal distribution were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test and Dunn test for post hoc comparisons. All data in this study were analyzed by SPSS 27.0, visualized by GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, and plotted by Adobe Illustrator 2025. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A. muciniphila altered the structure of the gut microbiota in CKD mice

We performed 16 S rRNA sequencing to profile the gut microbiota in each group. The Pan/Core and the dilution curve based on the sobs index flattened (Fig. 1A), suggesting that the sample size was sufficient for data analysis. We assessed the differences in microbial composition between groups by α and β diversity indices. For α diversity, Chao and Sobs indices were used to determine community richness, and Shannon and Simpson indices were used to evaluate community diversity. The results showed a significant decrease in community richness and diversity in the vakk group (p < 0.0001), with no significant difference among the other groups (Fig. 1B). PCA and PLS-DA revealed significant differences in intestinal microbial β diversity among the groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). In addition, we evaluated the gut microbial health index (GMHI), which is used to characterise the intestinal health status. The GMHI in the model group was significantly lower than that in the sham group (p = 0.00341). While GMHI in the akk group was significantly higher than that in the model group, the pakk group showed a significantly lower GMHI than did the model group (Fig. 1D). At the phylum level, the intestinal microbial composition of the vakk group was significantly different from that of the other groups (Fig. 1E). At the species level, the abundance of A. muciniphila in the vakk group was significantly higher than that in the other groups (Fig. 1F).

Live A. muciniphila ameliorated serum metabolism and fibrosis in CKD mice

The BUN, Cr, and Cys-C levels in the vakk group were significantly lower than those in the model group (BUN, p = 0.008; Cr, p = 0.0009; Cys-C, p = 0.0066), close to the levels in the sham group and lower than those in the akk group. After pasteurised A. muciniphila intervention, the above indicators showed an upward trend compared with that in the model group (Fig. 2A). RT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of the tubular injury marker Kim-1 was significantly lower in the akk and vakk groups than in the model group (p < 0.01). In addition, Ngal exhibited a decreased (but not significant) expression in the akk and vakk groups compared with that in the model group; however, the pakk group displayed an opposite trend (Fig. 2B). To further investigate the role of A. muciniphila in renal fibrosis, we assessed the expression of fibrosis-related markers, including fibronectin (Fn), collagen type I (Col1a1) and TIMP-1 in renal tissue. The three fibrosis markers were upregulated in the model group. Following treatment with different A. muciniphila components, Fn and Col1a1 were downregulated, whereas TIMP-1 expression was reduced only in the akk and vakk groups (Fig. 2C). Histopathological analyses were performed to confirm these findings. HE staining revealed evident renal damage in the model group, characterised by tubular dilatation, epithelial necrosis, cast formation and capsular fibrosis. These pathological changes were markedly attenuated in the akk and vakk groups, which exhibited only mild inflammatory cell infiltration. Consistent with these observations, the pathological scores confirmed that vakk group (p = 0.0095) and akk groups (p = 0.0361) had significantly lower renal injury scores than did the model group. Masson staining showed numerous blue collagen fibres deposited in the renal interstitium in the model group, whereas the A. muciniphila intervention groups showed reduced fibrosis to varying degrees (Fig. 2E). Statistical analysis of Masson scores verified these observations (Fig. 2D).

Effects of A. muciniphila on motor activity and anxiety state in CKD mice

The OFT and EPM were used to assess the locomotor and anxiety-like behaviours in CKD mice. The results revealed that mice in the model group exhibited a significant reduction in total distance travelled and average speed compared with the sham group mice (Fig. 3A-B). These two parameters were significantly improved (p < 0.05) in the vakk group compared with those in the model group, with the effect being markedly superior to that in the other intervention groups. The locomotor trajectory plots visually corroborated these findings, showing expanded activity ranges in the akk and vakk groups that more closely resembled those in the sham group (Fig. 3C-D).

Associations of gut microbial alterations with renal injury in CKD mice

Based on previous renal indicators demonstrating that the effect of pasteurised A. muciniphila on delaying renal function injury was not evident, the sham and akk groups were regarded as positive groups, and the model and pakk groups were regarded as negative groups. LEfse analysis was used to identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa from phylum to genus level (LDA > 2) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Combined with p-values, the analysis showed that there were 13 differential genera between the sham and model groups, eight differential genera between the sham and pakk groups and 13 altered bacterial genera between the model and akk groups. Twelve bacterial genera were identified in the akk and pakk groups. In addition, 12 bacterial genera were screened via a combination analysis of the above bacterial genera: Negativibacillus, Turicibacter, Dubosiella, Bacteroides, Faecalibaculum, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, Faecalimonas, Ligilactobacillus, Candidatus_Soleaferrea, UCG-005, [Eubacterium]_ventriosum_group and norank_f_UCG-010. The difference analysis between groups showed that the abundances of Turicibacter, Dubosiella and norank_f_UCG-010 increased in the model group and decreased after intervention with A. muciniphila, among which Dubosiella decreased the most significantly (p = 0.00526). These genera showed significantly decreased abundances in the vakk group (Fig. 4A-C).

A. muciniphila modulated gut metabolites in CKD mice

LC-MS revealed the intestinal metabolites in each group of mice. Based on the HMDB compound classification, 3,099 metabolites were identified, belonging to 16 categories. Among these, lipids and lipid-like molecules (29.24%), organic acids and derivatives (24.04%) and organoheterocyclic compounds (12.84%) were the primary components (Fig. 5A). PLS-DA showed distinct clustering of metabolic profiles among the groups, with good intra-group sample aggregation (R2 = 0.7590, p = 0.001), indicating model stability and reasonable group separation (Fig. 5B). In addition, the OPLS-DA analysis revealed a clear separation in each group, and the model parameters indicated a strong predictive capability suitable for subsequent metabolite analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on the above metabolic model, differential metabolites were screened using thresholds of p < 0.05 and VIP > 1 and visualised using volcano plots (Supplementary Fig. 3). We focused on P Cresol Sulfate, a gut-derived uraemic toxin closely associated with the pathological progression of CKD, as well as indole-3-carbinol, and 2-oxindole involved in tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 5C-E). These substances increased to varying degrees in the model group but decreased after intervention with live A. muciniphila, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the vakk group. Correlation analysis revealed that A. muciniphila was negatively correlated with these differential metabolites, whereas Turicibacter, Dubosiella and norank_f_UCG-010 showed positive correlations with the metabolites (Fig. 5F).

Analysis of the intestinal metabolic mechanism of A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin in the regulation of renal function.

To identify the key metabolic pathways through which A. muciniphila improves renal function in CKD, pathway enrichment and topological analyses were performed using the KEGG database. We systematically compared the differences among the healthy controls, disease models and A. muciniphila intervention groups (akk, pakk and vakk). The results visualised in the bubble plots indicated significant enrichment of the tryptophan metabolism pathway across the group. Additionally, degradation of flavonoids and isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathways were enriched in the intervention groups (Supplementary Fig. 4). Discussion

To identify the major biological components of A. muciniphila and the metabolic pathways involved in the delayed decline in renal function, we systematically compared the efficacy of A. muciniphila, pasteurised A. muciniphila and A. muciniphila in combined with vancomycin in CKD mice. We found that live A. muciniphila exhibited superior efficacy compared with pasteurised A. muciniphila. Furthermore, when combined with vancomycin to eliminate confounding gut flora, A. muciniphila significantly reduced the serum levels of BUN, Cr, and Cys-C in CKD mice. In addition, combined A. muciniphila and vancomycin effectively suppressed the expression of Fn and Col1a1 and ameliorated locomotor deficits and anxiety-like behaviours. In contrast, pasteurised A. muciniphila conferred limited benefits, largely mirroring the trends in the model group.

Microbial composition and intestinal microbial health index analyses showed that intervention with live A. muciniphila somewhat improved the imbalance in intestinal microbiota, whereas pasteurised A. muciniphila had limited regulatory effects on the disordered intestinal flora. The intestinal flora in mice treated with A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin showed significantly reduced abundance, and the abundance of A. muciniphila was significantly increased, indicating that vancomycin inhibits the microbiota and promotes the colonisation of A. muciniphila. We identified several bacterial genera associated with CKD progression, providing new insights into the gut-kidney axis in the pathophysiological process of CKD. Consistent with previous reports [23, 24], Turicibacter was enriched in the gut of CKD mice, and its abundance positively correlated with disease severity. However, existing researches have primarily focused on the association of Turicibacter with colonic inflammation and intestinal barrier function [25, 26], its specific role in renal injury remains unclear. We speculated that in the context of CKD, Turicibacter promotes the release of pro-inflammatory such as interleukin (IL)-6, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), contributing to a systemic microinflammatory state [27, 28]. This persistent inflammatory environment could subsequently activate local renal immune responses and enhance oxidative stress, aggravating renal injury and accelerating CKD progression. Concurrently, the abundance of norank_f_UCG-010 was significantly higher in the gut of DSS-induced ulcerative colitis mice than in healthy mice, and its abundance was positively correlated with IL-18 and IL-1β levels. Further mechanistic investigations revealed that norank_f_UCG-010 triggers inflammatory responses by upregulating the mRNA expression of Caspase-1 and IL-1β, compromising colonic mucosal integrity and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [29, 30]. Additionally, Dubosiella has been identified as a significantly enriched and potentially pathogenic bacterium in the intestinal tract of mice with hyperuricaemic nephropathy, and alterations in its abundance are closely correlated with renal function damage and metabolic disorders [31]. Studies have shown that inhibiting the proliferation of Dubosiella in the guts of hyperuricaemic mice regulates intestinal homeostasis, markedly attenuates renal inflammation and apoptosis and ultimately prevents hyperuricaemia-induced renal injury [32]. Our results indicated that Turicibacter, Dubosiella, and norank_f_UCG-010 were positively correlated with the degrees of kidney damage and fibrosis, and their abundance increased in the intestine of CKD mice. Pasteurised A. muciniphila had little effect on these bacteria, with the kidney function showing no evident improvements. However, the growth of the above bacteria was inhibited after intervention with live A. muciniphila. Although intervention with live A. muciniphila markedly inhibited the increase in the abundances of the three bacteria, it could not completely eliminate the disordered intestinal flora in CKD mice; hence, the regulatory role of A. muciniphila was partially restored. Vancomycin eliminated the interaction between these harmful bacteria and promoted the colonisation of A. muciniphila. The renal function in CKD mice treated with vancomycin combined with A. muciniphila showed greater improvement than did that in mice treated with A. muciniphila alone.

We found that the tryptophan metabolism pathway was related to kidney injury. This pathway has been reported in several studies to play an important role in the interaction between intestinal flora and kidney disease [33–36], among which the indole pathway is the key pathway connecting the intestinal flora and kidney disease [37]. As an essential amino acid, tryptophan is metabolised by gut microorganisms into indole, which is subsequently transported via portal circulation to the liver, where it undergoes sulfation to form IS [38]. During impaired renal function, IS accumulates systemically owing to inadequate clearance and acts as an endogenous ligand that persistently activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signalling pathway. The activated AHR promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of PGC1α, a key regulator of mitochondrial biosynthesis, through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, inhibiting mitochondrial energy metabolism and reducing ATP production and inducing senescence and fibrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells [39]. The AHR downstream target genes, such as cytochrome P450 1A1, are upregulated [40]. This leads to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), exacerbating oxidative stress and sustaining a micro-inflammatory environment. This persistent inflammatory milieu serves as a critical mechanism driving renal cell injury, apoptosis and fibrotic remodelling [41]. By analysing the metabolites in the different groups, we found that the levels of key metabolites related to tryptophan metabolism, such as p-cresol sulfate and indole-3-Carbinol, were significantly altered after live A. muciniphila treatment, and this pathway was highly significant in KEGG enrichment analysis. Therefore, the improvement in renal function induced by A. muciniphila may be attributed, in part, to the favourable modulation of the gut tryptophan metabolite profile by its active ingredients, which reduced the accumulation of harmful metabolites. In addition, isoflavone biosynthesis pathway and degradation of flavonoid pathway are closely related metabolic pathways that changed significantly following A. muciniphila intervention. Correlation analysis showed that the shared intermediates in the two metabolic pathways were significantly correlated with A. muciniphila, such as apigenin and daidzein, which have protective effects on the kidneys, were significantly correlated with A. muciniphila. Studies have shown that apigenin confers renal protection in LPS-induced mice by scavenging mitochondrial ROS and blunting the mtROS-RIPK3-MLKL necroptosis pathway [42]. It attenuates renal injury by blocking the STAT3/IL-17 axis, an effect associated with reduced renal CD8 + T-cell and macrophage infiltration [43]. Daidzein reduced oxidative stress and cell apoptosis by inhibiting MAPK pathway, thereby improving CDDP-mediated renal injury [44], suppressing the overexpression of pro-fibrotic miR-33a and miR-27a and reducing renal fibrosis [45].

Our results showed that the efficacy of live A. muciniphila was better than that of pasteurised bacteria and that the efficacy of live A. muciniphila combined with vancomycin intervention was better than that of live A. muciniphila alone. The disordered intestinal flora in CKD mice interferes with the colonisation of A. muciniphila and limits its function. However, vancomycin ameliorated the disordered intestinal flora in CKD mice, creating favourable conditions for the colonisation and function of A. muciniphila. Therefore, this study emphasises the significance of the bioactive components of A. muciniphila and shows that the bacterial protein components of A. muciniphila alone do not effectively delay kidney injury. Although reports suggest pasteurised A. muciniphila is more effective than live A. muciniphila in repairing the intestinal mucosal barrier and improving metabolism [9], some studies have revealed that pasteurised A. muciniphila up-regulates the expression of intestinal microfold cells (M cells) receptor GP2, and induces the formation and maturation of intestinal M cells, promoting infections [10]. In this study, pasteurised A. muciniphila did not significantly improve the kidney function, exercise or anxiety-like behaviour in CKD mice. A possible explanation is that pasteurised A. muciniphila loses its activity and cannot colonise and proliferate in the intestinal mucus layer, resulting in its inability to regulate the disordered intestinal flora structure and inhibit the proliferation of harmful bacteria (such as Turicibacter). Additionally, pasteurisation may disrupt the ability of A. muciniphila to produce key bioactive metabolites with renoprotective effects.

This study shows that the bioactive substances secreted by A. muciniphila may alter the composition of the intestinal flora in CKD and regulate host metabolic homeostasis, alleviating kidney damage and fibrosis and improving anxiety-like behaviour and motor function caused by CKD. In addition, we found that the effect of A. muciniphila is regulated by the overall state of the host intestinal flora. The increased abundances of Dubosiella, Turicibacter, and norank_f_UCG-010 in the disordered gut microbiota suppressed the efficacy of the bioactive substances of A. muciniphila. Vancomycin cleared the disordered intestinal flora and promoted the colonisation and function of A. muciniphila. These findings provide a new metabolic perspective on the renoprotective effects of A. muciniphila and suggest that the tryptophan metabolism pathway drives the regulatory effect of A. muciniphila through the “gut-kidney” axis, offering insights for future research. However, the specific active components of A. muciniphila, as well as the specific mechanism of the tryptophan metabolic pathway, require further investigation.
