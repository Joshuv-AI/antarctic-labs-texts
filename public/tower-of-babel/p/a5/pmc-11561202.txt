Sodium-Glucose Co-transporter-2 Inhibitors in Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus: The Framework for Recommendations for Their Potential Use
Authors: Djordje S Popovic, Dimitrios Patoulias, Theocharis Koufakis, Paschalis Karakasis, Nikolaos Papanas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing prevalence of overweight/obesity, the persistence of inadequate glycemic control among the majority of affected individuals, and the still unacceptably high risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality among population with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D), impose an urgent need for the introduction of non-insulin glucose-lowering agents in the therapeutic armamentarium. Given that their antihyperglycemic mechanism of action is independent of endogenous insulin secretion and that the observed cardio-renal benefits are unrelated to their glucose-lowering properties, one can speculate that the use of sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) could provide benefits in T1D, similar to the ones observed among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and heart failure. Available evidence from randomized controlled trials suggests that treatment with SGLT2is as adjunct to insulin in T1D results in modest reductions in glycated hemoglobin and body weight. Additionally, SGLT2is ameliorate albuminuria, and thus delay or prevent the development of CKD in T1D. However, use of SGLT2is is associated with an increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in T1D. This commentary aims at providing a framework for practical recommendations regarding the potential use of SGLT2is in adults with T1D, based on the individual’s risk level for DKA development, the presence of inadequate glycemic control and related cardio-renal complications.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Diabetic ketoacidosis, Glycemic control, Obesity, Overweight, Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors, Type 1 diabetes mellitus

Background

The growing prevalence of overweight/obesity in the population with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) and the persistence of inadequate glycemic control among the majority of persons with T1D impose an urgent need for the introduction of non-insulin glucose-lowering agents in the therapeutic armamentarium for T1D [1, 2]. Furthermore, a still unacceptably high risk of cardiovascular and renal morbidity and mortality among individuals with T1D requires novel therapeutic approaches including drugs with proven beneficial cardiorenal properties [3].

Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) represent a class of glucose-lowering agents with a unique mechanism of action. This class of agents is firmly established for the management of hyperglycemia in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) individuals, especially among those with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or at high risk for ASCVD, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and heart failure (HF) [4]. Furthermore, they are also used for delaying kidney function decline among individuals suffering CKD, and for reducing all-cause mortality and hospitalizations among persons with HF, even in the absence of T2D [5, 6]. Given that their antihyperglycemic mechanism of action is independent of endogenous insulin secretion, while their cardiorenal beneficial effects are unrelated to their glucose-lowering properties, one can speculate that their use could, at some extent, also provide similar benefits in T1D.

This commentary aims at providing a framework for practical recommendations regarding the potential use of SGLT2is adjunct therapy in adults with T1D. These recommendations are based on available evidence from the randomized controlled trials (RCTs), weighing potential benefits and risks of SGLT2is initiation in adult population with T1D.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Glycemic and Body Weight Effects

Results from the Dapagliflozin Evaluation in Patients With Inadequately Controlled Type 1 Diabetes (DEPICT-1) trial, which enrolled 833 adult participants with T1D and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) 7.5–10.5%, showed that 52-week treatment with dapagliflozin 5 and 10 mg led to reductions in HbA1c (− 0.33% and − 0.36%, respectively) and body weight (− 2.95% and − 4.54%, respectively), compared to placebo [7]. Furthermore, the DEPICT-2 trial, enrolling 813 adults with T1D and HbA1c of 7.5–10.5%, reported that, in comparison with placebo, 52-week treatment with dapagliflozin 5 and 10 mg use was associated with reductions in HbA1c (− 0.20% and − 0.25%, respectively) and body weight (− 4.42% and − 4.86%, respectively) [8]. Empagliflozin as Adjunctive to inSulin thErapy (EASE) program, including 1707 adult participants with T1D with HbA1c of 7.5–10.0%, reported that largest mean placebo-subtracted HbA1c reductions were − 0.28% for 2.5 mg, − 0.54% for 10 mg, and − 0.53% for 25 mg empagliflozin doses, after 52-week treatment in EASE-2 trial and 26-week treatment in EASE-3 trial [9]. At the same time, empagliflozin 2.5/10/25 mg doses, respectively, reduced mean body weight by − 1.8/− 3.0/− 3.4 kg, compared with placebo [9].

Additionally, the Efficacy, Safety, and Tolerability Study of Sotagliflozin as Adjunct Therapy in Adult Patients With Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus Who Have Inadequate Glycemic Control With Insulin Therapy (inTandem1) trial, enrolling 793 adult participants with T1D and HbA1c of 7.0–11.0%, reported HbA1c reductions of 0.25% and 0.31%, and body weight reductions of 3.14 and 4.32 kg with sotagliflozin (dual SGLT1/2 inhibitor) 200 and 400 mg doses, respectively, versus placebo, after 52 weeks of treatment [10]. In inTandem2 trial, which included 782 adults with T1D and HbA1c of 7.0–11.0%, 52-week treatment with sotagliflozin 200 and 400 mg, compared to placebo, resulted in reductions of HbA1c of 0.21% and 0.32%, and of body weight of 2.18 and 2.82 kg, respectively [11]. Finally, inTandem3 trial, enrolling 1402 adults with T1D and HbA1c of 7.0–11.0%, demonstrated a 0.46% reduction in HbA1c, and a 2.98 kg reduction in body weight in participants treated with 400 mg sotagliflozin, versus those receiving placebo, after 24 weeks of treatment [12].

The meta-analysis evaluating the effect of SGLT2is on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics as adjunctive to insulin in adults with T1D demonstrated that SGLT2is therapy provides a significant benefit in terms of increased time in range and reduced glucose variability, without an increase in time below range [13]. Therefore, treatment with SGLT2is as adjunct to insulin among individuals with T1D results in modest reductions in HbA1c and body weight, effects considered as beneficial, based upon the difficulties in achieving adequate glycemic control and normal body weight for persons with T1D.

Chronic Kidney Disease

The pooled analysis from DEPICT-1 and DEPICT-2, including participants with albuminuria, reported a mean difference in change from baseline in urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) of − 13.3% for dapagliflozin 5 mg and − 31.1% for dapagliflozin 10 mg, compared with placebo [14]. On the other hand, no significant change from baseline was noticed in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), with a mean difference in change from baseline of 3.27 ml/min/1.73 m2 for dapagliflozin 5 mg and 2.12 ml/min/1.73 m2 for dapagliflozin 10 mg, versus placebo [14]. Moreover, in a pooled analysis of the EASE program, UACR decreased by 16% and 30% with empagliflozin 10 and 25 mg, respectively, compared with placebo, among participants with baseline UACR ≥ 30 mg/g, while empagliflozin 2.5 mg did not significantly affect UACR [15]. At the same time, empagliflozin 10 and 25 mg, but not 2.5 mg, resulted in short-term changes in eGFR comparable to the ones observed with the use of SGLT2is among individuals with T2D, with 3-week post-treatment eGFR returning to above baseline [15]. Finally, the pooled analysis from inTandem1 and inTandem2 demonstrated that UACR decreased by 23.7% and 18.3% with sotagliflozin 200 and 400 mg, respectively, versus placebo, among participants with baseline UACR ≥ 30 mg/g [16]. Simultaneously, placebo-corrected least squares mean change from baseline in eGFR was − 2.0 ml/min/1.73 m2 and − 0.5 ml/min/1.73 m2, for sotagliflozin 200 and 400 mg, respectively [16].

A relevant, updated, multilevel meta-analysis of RCTs demonstrated a significant reduction in albuminuria levels with SGLT2is treatment as adjunct to insulin for individuals with T1D, whereas a neutral effect on eGFR was documented [17].

Thus, SGLT2is promise to ameliorate albuminuria, and thus delay, or even prevent, development of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) among persons with T1D, a detrimental complication that can be observed relatively early during the disease course.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis and Hypoglycemia

Although hypoglycemia events were comparable across treatment groups, more individuals receiving dapagliflozin had events adjudicated as definite diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) (4.0, 3.4, and 1.9% in dapagliflozin 5 mg, 10 mg, and placebo groups, respectively) in the DEPICT-1 trial [7]. Similarly, during the DEPICT-2 trial, hypoglycemic event rates were similar across treatment arms, while more individuals with events adjudicated as definite DKA were in the dapagliflozin 5 and 10 mg groups compared with placebo (4.1, 3.7, and 0.4%, respectively) [8]. The EASE program reported similar frequency of severe hypoglycemia, while adjudicated DKA occurred more with empagliflozin 10 mg (4.3%) and 25 mg (3.3%) but not with empagliflozin 2.5 mg (0.8%), versus placebo (1.2%) [9]. Adjudicated DKA occurred in 3.4% and 4.2% of participants receiving sotagliflozin 200 and 400 mg, respectively, and 0.4% receiving placebo, while severe hypoglycemia occurred in 6.5% of individuals from each sotagliflozin group and 9.7% of those receiving placebo, during the InTandem1 trial [10]. Similarly, InTandem2 trialists reported that DKA occurred in none of participants receiving placebo, 2.3% of those receiving sotagliflozin 200 mg, and 3.4% of those receiving sotagliflozin 400 mg, while the frequency of documented hypoglycemia was lower with sotagliflozin [11]. Finally, inTandem3 trialists reported higher DKA rate in the sotagliflozin group (3.0%) than in the placebo group (0.6%), while the rates of severe hypoglycemia were similar [12].

The use of SGLT2is is associated with the risk of developing euglycemic DKA. Euglycemic DKA is characterized by serum glucose < 250 mg/dl accompanied with an anion gap metabolic acidosis and ketosis [18]. In addition to the use of SGLT2is, the risk factors for euglycemic DKA include starvation, chronic liver disease, pregnancy, infection, and alcohol use [18]. SGLT2is promote glucosuria which cause a decrease in plasma glucose levels and reduced insulin requirements among individuals with T1D. In the circumstances of carbohydrate shortage, insulinopenia and enhanced glucagon secretion lead to an increased lipolysis and ketogenesis. The absence of severe hyperglycemia in the cases of euglycemic DKA is the result of a decreased carbohydrate intake and/or an increased glucosuria caused by SGLT2is [19].

Real-World Evidence

Numerous real-world studies analyzing the efficacy and safety of the use of SGLT2is in individuals with T1D have been published so far. A retrospective cohort European two-center study, conducted in Spain and Belgium, included 199 individuals with T1D starting treatment with SGLT2is (113 were treated with empagliflozin, 66 with dapagliflozin, and 20 with canagliflozin) [20]. Significant reductions in mean HbA1c (− 0.5%), body weight (− 2.9 kg), and daily insulin dose (− 8.5%) were observed after 12 months [20]. The greatest reduction in HbA1c was noticed among individuals with baseline HbA1c > 8% (− 0.7%), while the most profound weight loss was seen in individuals with body mass index (BMI) > 27 kg/m2 (− 3.5 kg) [20]. Additionally, participants with baseline eGFR < 90 ml/min/1.73 m2 showed an increase in eGFR (4.5 ml/min/1.73 m2), while those with UACR > 15 mg/g showed a decrease in UACR (− 16.6 mg/g) [20]. No severe hypoglycemia events were reported, while 3.5% of participants experienced DKA [20].

The Dapa-ON multicenter retrospective study, including data from 250 individuals with T1D receiving dapagliflozin as add-on therapy to insulin in Spain, reported significant reductions in mean body weight (− 3.3 kg), HbA1c (− 0.6%), and total daily insulin dose (− 8.6%) 12 months after dapagliflozin prescription [21]. Additionally, significant improvements in time in range (+ 9.3%), time above range (− 7.2%), time below range (− 2.5%), and coefficient of variation (− 5.1%) were noticed in the subgroup of participants with available CGM data [21]. Finally, an improvement in UACR was found among individuals with baseline UACR ≥ 30 mg/g (median decrease of 99 mg/g in UACR) [21]. A total of five DKA events were reported in participants with a 12-month follow-up, while no severe hypoglycemia episodes were reported [21].

On the other hand, real-world data of 12-month adjunct SGLT2is treatment in individuals with T1D based on the German/Austrian DPV registry, which included 431 participants who had initiated SGLT2is, demonstrated significant reduction in HbA1c (− 0.63%) but no significant changes in BMI, daily insulin dose, eGFR, and percentage of participants with microalbuminuria, among individuals with available data at the end of the follow-up [22]. The proportion of individuals with at least one severe hypoglycemia in the year prior to SGLT2is initiation and in the first 12 months with an SGLT2is was low (3%), while none of the participants had a DKA in the year before or during the 12 months of SGLT2is therapy [22].

A recent robust real-world study included 1882 participants with T1D treated with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and 992 participants with T1D treated with SGLT2is, for at least 6 months [23]. Although therapy with GLP-1 RAs provided greater reduction in HbA1c (− 0.5%) in comparison with SGLT2is treatment (− 0.2%), the latter enabled preservation of eGFR (+ 3.5 ml/min per 1.73 m2) over a 5-year period, unlike GLP-1 RAs therapy, which failed to do so (− 7.2 ml/min per 1.73 m2) [23]. Additionally, participants treated with SGLT2is were less likely to develop heart failure (HF) (risk ratio (RR): 0.44) and CKD (RR: 0.49), and had a lower risk of hospitalization for any cause (RR: 0.59) when compared with GLP-1 RAstreated individuals [23]. However, the SGLT2is treated cohort had a higher risk of DKA (RR: 2.08) [23].

Practical Recommendations

Several factors should be considered with extra caution prior to potential SGLT2is initiation in adults with T1D, including level of risk for DKA associated with SGLT2is therapy, baseline HbA1c, BMI, and the present risk or presence of DKD, HF, or ASCVD. Of course, it should be highlighted that, since SGLT2is produce only a modest reduction in body weight and BMI, mainly attributed to their diuretic effects, BMI should not be considered as a major determinant for SGLT2is treatment initiation in T1D; however, in cases of sarcopenia, we truly believe that SGLT2is should not be initiated in persons with T1D.

The paramount criterion for appropriate selection of eligible persons with T1D are normal ketone levels (< 0.6 mmol/l blood ketones or negative urine ketones) [24], measured preferably in the fasting state. Ketone level should be assessed prior to SGLT2is initiation, ideally by laboratory testing, as it represents the most precise method for assessing β-hydroxybutyrate levels [24]. However, individual risk factors associated with lifestyle and behaviors, willingness and ability to regularly practice recommended ketone monitoring regimens, as well as ability to appropriately respond to elevated ketone levels, should also be considered [24]. The frequency of ketone self-measurements should be individualized but checking their level should be mandatory in the events of existence of any symptoms consistent with DKA (malaise, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting), changes in diet, activity, or insulin dose, and during concomitant events such as infection, dehydration, surgery, injury, insulin pump occlusion/malfunction, or stress [24]. Self-measurement of capillary blood ketones, specifically β-hydroxybutyrate is the recommended method for ketone level monitoring, but in cases it is not affordable or accessible urine ketone measurements are acceptable [24]. The risk level for SGLT2is therapy associated DKA can be classified into minimal/low, low/moderate, and moderate/high (Table 1) [24]. If the person is found to be eligible, considering the individual risk for DKA development, further contemplation of SGLT2is therapy initiation should be based on the presence of inadequate glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7.0%), nutritional status, and the risk of or presence of CKD, HF, or ASCVD. It appears that the road is now open for the incorporation of SGLT2is into the treatment algorithm of T1D, based on the high burden of cardio-renal-metabolic co-morbidities in this population, as well as the documented beneficial effects of this class across the cardio-metabolic continuum. However, we definitely need dedicated RCTs assessing surrogate morbidity and mortality endpoints; of note, there are ongoing studies [for example, the Sotagliflozin in Patients With Heart Failure Symptoms and Type 1 Diabetes (SOPHIST) trial (NCT064351560) and the Sotagliflozin to Slow Kidney Function Decline in Persons With Type 1 Diabetes and Diabetic Kidney Disease (SUGARNSALT) trial (NCT06217302)] in the field, with results being highly anticipated.
