Muscle sympathetic nerve activity in COPD: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Brady Duiker, Safi Shirazi, Allison Sivak, Michael K. Stickland, Margie H. Davenport, Craig D. Steinback
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease is a primary driver of mortality in COPD. Elevated sympathetic nerve activity is a key proposed mechanism, but the magnitude of this autonomic overactivity has not been quantified in a pooled analysis.ObjectiveTo determine if adults with COPD exhibit higher resting muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) than healthy controls, assess associated cardiorespiratory effects, and evaluate the short-term impact of COPD interventions on MSNA.MethodsFollowing the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, eight databases were searched from inception to 15 May 2025 to inform this systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD420251044931). Eligible studies included adults with spirometry-defined COPD in whom MSNA was measured by microneurography at rest. Primary outcomes were MSNA burst frequency (bursts·min−1) and incidence (bursts per 100 heartbeats). Secondary outcomes included resting heart rate and blood pressure.ResultsA total of 11 studies (171 COPD participants, 105 controls) met inclusion criteria. Compared with controls, COPD was associated with markedly higher MSNA, burst frequency (+18.5, 95% CI 9.4–27.7 bursts·min−1) and burst incidence (+21.3, 95% CI 9.2–33.4 bursts per 100 heartbeats). Resting heart rate was also elevated (+10.7, 95% CI 6.1–15.3 beats·min−1), while blood pressure did not differ significantly. Three studies on noninvasive ventilation or inhaled β2-agonists found no significant pooled changes in MSNA.ConclusionsResting MSNA is significantly elevated in patients with COPD despite normal blood pressure. This chronic sympathetic excitation likely contributes to COPD-related cardiovascular morbidity and exercise intolerance, highlighting the importance of developing therapies that reduce sympathetic nerve activity.

Introduction

With 392 million individuals affected and 3.5 million deaths each year, COPD is the fourth leading cause of death globally and a major source of morbidity and economic burden [1, 2]. The direct annual medical costs attributable to COPD in the United States were estimated at USD 31.3 billion in 2019, a figure projected to nearly double to USD 60.5 billion by 2029. Its burden is disproportionally higher in low-income populations, with prevalence linked to lower household income and education levels [3].

The systemic effects of COPD include elevated cardiovascular (CV) risk, metabolic disturbances, musculoskeletal dysfunction and psychological comorbidity [4–8]. One study found that 97.7% of patients with COPD had at least one comorbidity, with over half reporting four or more [9], and 23% of patients had comorbid CV disease [9]. Indeed, the most common cause of death in patients with COPD is CV disease, not respiratory failure [10, 11].

One proposed mechanism linking COPD to CV disease is sympathetic nervous system overactivity [12–22]. Drivers include chronic hypoxaemia [16], baroreflex impairment [15] and chemoreflex hypersensitivity [14, 16], which can lead to endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, cardiomyocyte injury and systemic inflammation [12, 22]. This neurohormonal activation contributes to progressive CV decline [21, 23].

Muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA), measured via microneurography [24], is considered the gold standard for quantifying sympathetic nervous system activity [13, 18]. Unlike indirect markers such as plasma catecholamines or heart rate variability, microneurography provides a direct, reproducible and reliable assessment of efferent sympathetic tone [18, 21]. This sympathetic tone is elevated in patients with COPD [25, 26]. Increased MSNA correlates with worse outcomes and higher mortality [21, 26]. Conversely, interventions that blunt sympathetic activity, such as β-blockers, have been linked to improved survival, even in patients with COPD who do not have overt CV disease [27].

The primary aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine the magnitude of difference in resting MSNA between adults with COPD and healthy controls. Secondary aims were to assess associated differences in CV parameters (heart rate, blood pressure) and to conduct an exploratory analysis of studies evaluating the acute effects of COPD therapies on these outcomes. We hypothesised that resting MSNA would be significantly elevated in patients with COPD and minimally affected by acute pulmonary interventions. Our goal is to synthesise available evidence and highlight future directions for targeting autonomic dysfunction in COPD.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [28]. The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration no. CRD420251044931). All analyses and reporting adhere to the registered protocol.

Information sources

A comprehensive search strategy was developed by a research librarian (A. Sivak). Multiple databases were searched from inception to 15 May 2025, including MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Trip and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses. No restrictions were placed on language or publication date. Articles in languages other than English, Spanish, Chinese or French were translated via Google Translate for screening, with professional translation for those deemed potentially eligible. Additionally, reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify any missed publications. Duplicate records were removed.

Eligibility criteria

Study design

The study design was restricted to primary literature sources, including cross-sectional, quasi-experimental, cohort, case–control, case reports and randomised control trial studies. Narrative or systematic reviews, meta-analyses, letters, commentaries, editorials and conference abstracts were excluded.

Population

The study focused on adults with COPD at any Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stage. Subjects with related conditions (bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, asthma) were included if they were reported within COPD cohorts. Studies needed to include a COPD group, defined by standard criteria (e.g. spirometry) or as defined by authors, and either a healthy control group or an internal control for comparison (e.g. baseline versus post-intervention).

Exposures

Any intervention was eligible, including no intervention (baseline resting measurements). This encompassed standard care, respiratory therapies (noninvasive ventilation (NIV)) or medications (bronchodilators, β-blockers).

Comparison

Eligible comparators were healthy controls matched by age/sex whenever possible, undergoing similar procedures, or COPD control groups (e.g. COPD patients without a given intervention or with different disease severity). For treatment effects, “treated” versus “untreated” COPD groups were compared.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were MSNA measures: burst frequency (bursts·min−1) and burst incidence (bursts per 100 heartbeats) at rest. Secondary outcomes included resting heart rate (beats·min−1) and blood pressure (systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) in mmHg).

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of all retrieved citations were independently screened by two reviewers (C.D. Steinback and B. Duiker). Articles marked for potential inclusion by either reviewer proceeded to full-text review, which was again performed independently by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (M.H. Davenport). Figure 1 summarises the study selection process.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram of study selection. MSNA: muscle sympathetic nerve activity.

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardised form in Microsoft Excel. Study characteristics (study period, study design, country), population characteristics (number of participants, age, body mass index, smoking status, pack years and GOLD grade), exposure (baseline, medication, treatment, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) % predicted, total lung capacity % predicted, vital capacity % predicted, FEV1 (L), forced vital capacity (L), pH, partial pressure of CO2, partial pressure of O2 and oxygen saturation) and outcomes (burst frequency, burst incidence, resting heart rate, resting SBP, resting DBP, MAP, total activity, amplitude, total peripheral resistance, peripheral vascular bed resistance and cardiac output) were extracted (supplementary table 1). If data were not available for extraction, the corresponding authors were contacted for additional information. Where data were only presented in figures and authors could not be reached via email, data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer (v.3.11; https://automeris.io/), an online tool that supports the extraction of numeric data from graphs [29].

Quality of evidence assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed each study for risk of bias and quality of evidence using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool (https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools). The methodological quality of evidence across studies for each outcome was also assessed by two independent reviewers using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool [30]. Evidence from studies began with a “high” certainty of evidence rating and was downgraded if there were concerns of risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision or risk of publication bias. All research articles were examined for potential sources of bias (i.e. selection bias, reporting bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias and “other” sources of bias). The risk of bias was evaluated following the Cochrane Handbook [31]. Disagreements in bias assessment were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (M.H. Davenport). The risk of bias across studies was rated as serious when studies with the greatest influence on the pooled result (contributing >50% of the weight given in forest plots) presented a “high” risk of bias. Inconsistency was considered serious when heterogeneity was high (I2≥50%) or when only one study was assessed. Imprecision was considered serious when the 95% confidence interval (CI) crossed the line of no effect.

When fewer than 10 studies were included in the forest plot, publication bias was not downgraded. The JBI Critical Appraisal Tool and GRADE assessment are presented in supplementary tables 2 and 3, respectively.

Data synthesis

The meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager v5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen). Mean values and their sds were used in the meta-analyses. If studies reported sem, the sd was calculated by multiplying the sem by the square root of the sample size. For continuous outcomes, the mean differences (MDs) between COPD and control groups (or between treated versus untreated COPD) were calculated with 95% CIs. All analyses used a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird) given the anticipated heterogeneity across studies. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (two-tailed). Heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic (I2≥50% considered high). Where high heterogeneity was present, possible sources were explored via subgroup or sensitivity analyses if data allowed. When outcomes were reported by too few studies or could not be quantitatively pooled, findings were described qualitatively. All statistical procedures followed Cochrane Handbook guidance [31].

Subgroup analyses

A priori determined subgroup analyses were planned to explore whether MSNA differences varied by key subgroups, such as presence of CV comorbidities (e.g. heart failure), COPD severity (GOLD stage or FEV1 % predicted category), sex or specific treatments. However, owing to the small number of studies in many analyses, formal subgroup comparisons were limited.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1480 unique records were identified, of which 529 duplicates were removed. After 951 titles and abstracts were screened, 18 full-text articles underwent detailed review. Ultimately, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria, including one additional paper added after a hand search of the literature (figure 1). The main reasons for exclusion were an absence of direct MSNA measurement or an ineligible study design. The 10 included studies, published between 2001 and 2024, yielded 171 patients with COPD and 105 controls. Seven of the studies used a cross-sectional design to compare patients with COPD with healthy controls, while three explored changes in MSNA among patients with COPD receiving specific interventions (NIV or inhaled bronchodilators) compared with their baseline.

Study characteristics

Key study characteristics are detailed in supplementary table 1. As expected, most COPD cohorts were older adults (mid-50s to early 70s) and were predominantly male, reflecting the higher prevalence of COPD in men across the population [32]. COPD severity was generally moderate to severe, with mean FEV1 % predicted values commonly ranging from approximately 30% to 60% (supplementary table 7). Reflecting the heterogeneity of the disease, the patient cohorts presented with varied respiratory statuses; some studies specifically recruited patients with chronic hypoxemia [16], hypercapnia [13, 17] or both [19], whereas others intentionally focused on normoxic, non-hypercapnic individuals [14, 15, 21].

Hypertension was a frequent comorbidity, and many participants were on standard COPD medications (e.g. long-acting bronchodilators) (supplementary table 5). While some studies excluded major cardiac conditions, others included participants with various comorbidities. Where healthy controls were used, they typically matched patients by age and sex and had normal spirometric results. Three studies focused specifically on within-subject changes in MSNA following distinct interventions: Haarmann et al. [17] and Regmi et al. [13] evaluated the impact of initiating NIV over 20-min and 30-min sessions, respectively, and Haarmann et al. [18] examined both the acute effects following a single dose of inhaled salmeterol (a β2-agonist) and the longer-term effects of its regular, twice-daily use over a period of 4 weeks.

All included studies measured baseline MSNA via microneurography. Reporting metrics varied: six studies provided MSNA as burst frequency (bursts·min−1) and seven reported burst incidence (bursts per 100 heartbeats). Each study also recorded resting heart rate and blood pressure alongside MSNA.

Synthesis of data

Resting heart rate: COPD versus controls

In pooled analysis of seven studies (98 COPD patients, 86 controls), resting heart rate was significantly higher in patients with COPD than in healthy controls, with a MD of 10.69 beats·min−1 (95% CI 6.05 to 15.32 beats·min−1) (figure 2). There was moderate heterogeneity (I2=59%). This suggests that COPD patients have elevated resting sympathetic drive to the heart and/or reduced vagal tone. The certainty of evidence was rated low, downgraded for imprecision (wide CI) and risk of bias due to small sample size.

Resting blood pressure: COPD versus controls

Pooled analysis revealed no significant differences in resting blood pressure between patients with COPD and healthy controls across SBP, DBP or MAP (figure 3).

SBP, analysed across four studies (64 COPD, 53 controls), was comparable between groups (MD 0.64 mmHg, 95% CI −7.05 to +8.33 mmHg), with no heterogeneity observed (I2=0%) (figure 3a). Similarly, DBP from the same four studies did not differ significantly, although there was a nonsignificant trend towards lower DBP in the COPD group (MD −2.25 mmHg, 95% CI −6.13 to +1.64 mmHg; I2=0%) (figure 3b). Finally, MAP analysed across four studies (56 COPD, 50 controls) also showed no significant difference (MD −0.50 mmHg, 95% CI −5.09 to +4.09 mmHg), with low heterogeneity (I2=7%) (figure 3c).

These findings indicate that, despite having significantly higher resting heart rates, patients with COPD in these studies maintained blood pressures similar to their healthy counterparts.

Resting MSNA: COPD versus controls

The primary hypothesis of this paper is that resting MSNA is significantly elevated in patients with COPD compared to controls. Despite elevated heart rate and MSNA in COPD, resting blood pressures were similar to controls.

Burst frequency

Six studies (80 COPD, 69 controls) reported resting MSNA burst frequency (figure 4). The pooled burst frequency was 18.53 bursts·min−1 higher in COPD (95% CI 9.39 to 27.67 bursts·min−1). This difference was highly significant (p<0.0001). This outcome had substantial heterogeneity (I2=84%).

Burst incidence

Four studies (48 COPD, 43 controls) reported burst incidence (bursts per 100 heartbeats) (figure 5). Resting burst incidence was 21.27 bursts per 100 heartbeats higher in COPD (95% CI 9.16 to 33.37 bursts per 100 heartbeats). This is consistent with the burst frequency data and suggests that even when accounting for heart rate differences, patients with COPD have greater sympathetic activity. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2=66%).

Effects of COPD treatment on MSNA and haemodynamic parameters

An exploratory analysis was conducted on three studies that met the inclusion criteria for interventions: two on NIV [13, 17] and one on inhaled β2-agonist therapy (salmeterol) [18].

The individual study findings were mixed. Haarmann et al. [17] applied home NIV for several weeks in hypercapnic patients but found no change in MSNA or heart rate, possibly due to the short follow-up or patient selection. In the bronchodilator study, Haarmann et al. [18] examined both acute and 4-week effects of salmeterol and likewise found no change in sympathetic activity, suggesting that resting MSNA in COPD may not be easily altered by these standard therapies.

By contrast, a more recent study by Regmi et al. [13] found that a 30-min session of effective NIV did significantly reduce MSNA in stable hypercapnic patients with COPD by successfully lowering their arterial carbon dioxide tension levels.

When these studies were combined in a pooled analysis, the results showed no significant overall changes in resting heart rate (MD +1.90 beats·min−1, 95% CI −2.44 to +6.24 beats·min−1), blood pressure (SBP: MD −0.16 mmHg, 95% CI −9.26 to +8.94 mmHg; DBP: MD −6.52 mmHg, 95% CI −16.91 to +3.88 mmHg) or MSNA burst incidence (MD −3.22 bursts per 100 heartbeats, 95% CI −12.04 to +5.59 bursts per 100 heartbeats).

Thus, while one study demonstrated that correcting hypercapnia with NIV can acutely reduce MSNA, the pooled evidence from the few available small studies does not indicate a consistent, significant reduction in sympathetic activity with these short-term therapies (supplementary figure 4).

Discussion

Key findings

In this first systematic review and meta-analysis of MSNA in COPD, we demonstrate a clear pattern of heightened sympathetic nerve activity in patients with COPD relative to control subjects. Both MSNA burst frequency and incidence were significantly elevated. These findings confirm and quantify decades of smaller studies that individually noted sympathetic overactivity in COPD. In pooled analyses, resting MSNA was significantly elevated in patients with COPD, with burst frequency and incidence approximately 19 bursts·min−1 and 21 bursts per 100 heartbeats higher, respectively, than in healthy controls. This heightened sympathetic activity coincided with a mean resting heart rate increase of roughly 11 beats·min−1, indicating augmented sympathetic drive and/or diminished vagal tone. Yet SBP, DBP and MAP did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting compensatory baroreflex or vascular adaptations that buffer arterial pressure in COPD. Short-term interventions (NIV and inhaled β2-agonists) failed to produce significant reductions in MSNA or improvements in resting haemodynamic parameters, although these data are limited. While the certainty of evidence ranged from low to moderate, the cross-sectional, non-interventional comparisons demonstrated a consistent and moderately certain elevation in MSNA, reinforcing a strong association between COPD and sympathetic overactivity. Together, these findings not only support the presence of autonomic dysregulation in COPD but also demonstrate the need for future studies to explore mechanistic pathways and identify potential targets for intervention.

Interpretation of key findings

The elevated MSNA in COPD likely reflects chronic peripheral chemoreflex activation (due to intermittent or sustained hypoxaemia), baroreflex dysfunction, and possibly muscle metaboreflex engagement from deconditioned limb muscles [13, 14, 16]. Interestingly, despite this sympathetic excitation, resting blood pressure did not differ between COPD and controls in our meta-analysis. This apparent paradox has been interpreted in the following ways.

First, patients with COPD may have blunted sympathetic vascular transduction, meaning their blood vessels respond less to a given sympathetic burst. Support for this comes from recent work by Sesa-Ashton and Macefield [33], who found blunted sympathetic vascular transduction and baroreflex sensitivity in severe COPD. In their study, patients with COPD had higher MSNA but not higher blood pressure responses, suggesting insensitivity of the vasculature or counter-regulatory vasodilation (possibly from hypercapnia-induced effects). The disconnect between high MSNA and normal blood pressure is an intriguing finding; it strongly suggests that alterations in sympathetic transduction gain are integral to the pathophysiology of COPD, similar to observations in chronic high-altitude hypoxia where heightened MSNA coexists with reduced neurovascular transduction and no appreciable increase in resting blood pressure [34].

Another explanation for the lack of blood pressure effect is that many patients with COPD were taking medications (e.g. antihypertensives) that modulate blood pressure (supplementary table 6). Most included studies did not exclude treated hypertension, so some COPD participants may have had pharmacologically controlled blood pressure, masking what might otherwise be higher blood pressures. Finally, baroreflex adaptation might also play a role. Chronic sympathetic activation could lead to downregulation of adrenergic receptors or upregulation of baroreflex buffering, maintaining blood pressure at normal levels despite high MSNA. Regmi et al. [13] demonstrated that NIV reduced MSNA by alleviating hypercapnia but did not consistently affect blood pressure. The disconnect between high MSNA and normal blood pressure in COPD is an intriguing finding, and contrasts with conditions such as chronic heart failure or essential hypertension, where elevated MSNA is usually associated with high blood pressure.

Literature comparison

Several individual studies lend strong support to our meta-analysis findings, collectively painting a clear picture of the nature, consequences and mechanisms of sympathetic overactivity in COPD. The initial documentation of “marked sympathetic activation” in chronic respiratory failure by Heindl et al. [16] established COPD as a state of sympathoexcitation. Subsequent work quantified this relationship, with Andreas et al. [21] reporting that MSNA is ∼60% higher in patients with COPD than in controls and that this elevation predicts worse 2-year outcomes.

The functional consequences of this sympathetic overdrive are significant. Haarmann et al. [12] found that elevated MSNA correlated with exercise limitation, implying that heightened vasoconstrictor outflow could impair exercise tolerance by reducing muscle perfusion. Mechanistic insight comes from Phillips et al. [14], who demonstrated that inhibiting the carotid chemoreceptor, a key regulator of sympathetic outflow, acutely reduced both MSNA and arterial stiffness while improving cardiovascular function in patients with COPD. This finding supports the idea that the sympathoexcitation in COPD is a tonic, or continuous, state driven by carotid chemoreceptor hypersensitivity, a concept reinforced by Fatouleh and Macefield [19], who observed that the basal sympathetic tone is elevated rather than simply showing an exaggerated reflex oscillation with the breathing cycle.

Effects of interventions

Our findings on interventions align with individual studies, collectively suggesting that short-term improvements in airflow or ventilation do not acutely alter sympathetic nerve activity in COPD. Notably, Haarmann et al. [18] showed that inhaled β2-agonists improve lung function (e.g. increased FEV1) but do not reduce MSNA. This indicates that bronchoconstriction and lung hyperinflation alone are unlikely to be primary drivers of sympathetic activation. In fact, β2-agonists may slightly increase heart rate via β1-receptor spillover [18], further complicating their effect on autonomic tone.

Similarly, the effects of NIV were inconsistent. While Haarmann et al. [17] found no change in MSNA, a more recent study by Regmi et al. [13] demonstrated that effective NIV could acutely reduce MSNA by alleviating hypercapnia. This suggests that the autonomic dysfunction in COPD may not be linked to altered arterial blood gas status and/or that the effects of NIV require a longer duration or earlier initiation in the disease course to be effective; an interpretation also suggested by Haarmann et al. [17]. In contrast, non-pharmacological interventions such as slow breathing and exercise training have shown promise in reducing MSNA, as demonstrated by Raupach et al. [15]. Although strong evidence in COPD is still limited, these modalities may modulate autonomic function through improved baroreflex sensitivity, reduced chemoreflex drive and enhanced muscle perfusion.

Together, these results suggest that heightened sympathetic activity in COPD is not readily reversible through conventional pulmonary therapies alone. Rather, they underscore the need to explore targeted strategies, such as exercise, breathing retraining or neuromodulatory approaches, to sustainably mitigate autonomic dysfunction in this population.

Clinical implications

The consistent sympathetic overactivity in COPD has important clinical implications. Chronic sympathetic excitation is pro-arrhythmic and contributes to cardiac autonomic dysfunction [35]. Even when resting blood pressure remains in the normal range, high MSNA could help explain the elevated CV risk in patients with COPD (e.g. higher incidence of myocardial infarction and stroke) [12]. These findings suggest that patients with COPD may benefit from therapies targeting autonomic balance.

Potential interventions include optimised oxygen therapy, β-blockers and exercise rehabilitation. Heindl et al. [16] found that short-term oxygen administration decreased MSNA in patients with chronic lung disease, but not in healthy controls. Mechanistically, β-blockers could temper cardiac sympathetic effects and, potentially, central sympathetic output. Exercise training improves baroreflex sensitivity and autonomic regulation in healthy populations. Preliminary evidence in COPD suggests exercise can support autonomic improvement, indicated by increased baroreflex sensitivity [36].

Strengths and limitations

This review's strengths include its comprehensive literature search, adherence to PRISMA guidelines and the first quantitative synthesis of MSNA in COPD. By pooling data, we provide a more precise estimate of sympathetic overactivity in this population. However, the findings must be interpreted in the context of several important limitations.

First, the overall sample size is modest (171 COPD participants, 105 controls), and the data are derived from small, single-centre studies, which limits generalisability. Furthermore, all included studies measured MSNA at rest; therefore, these findings do not capture potential differences in sympathetic responses to dynamic stressors like exercise or hypoxic challenge. The predominantly male composition of the study populations also limits applicability to women, an increasingly prevalent group in the COPD population [32].

Second, there was substantial heterogeneity (I2=66–84%) in MSNA outcomes. While all studies showed a consistent direction of effect, the magnitude varied considerably. This is likely due to clinical diversity in the patient samples, including differences in COPD severity (FEV1 % predicted), respiratory status and comorbidities like heart failure [20]. A qualitative review suggests a trend for more severe disease correlating with higher MSNA, but a formal meta-regression was not feasible.

The potential for confounding from medications, particularly antihypertensives, is also a key concern and could explain the paradoxical finding of normal blood pressure despite elevated MSNA.

Finally, while visual inspection of the funnel plots (supplementary figures 4–10) did not suggest major publication bias, the study by Phillips et al. [14] was a notable outlier as the only study reporting a null effect (figures 4 and 5). Given the small number of included studies, the potential for unpublished null findings cannot be excluded. This review also does not address long-term outcomes, such as whether baseline MSNA predicts mortality or exacerbation frequency.

Conclusion

Despite similar resting blood pressure, MSNA is considerably higher in patients with COPD than in healthy controls. This study demonstrated the durability of sympathetic activation in COPD by showing that the short-term therapies (NIV and β2-agonist) do not lower MSNA or enhance resting CV parameters. These results demonstrate how autonomic dysfunction contributes to the systemic consequences and complications of COPD.

According to this review, sympathetic hyperactivity may be a factor in exercise intolerance and CV risk associated with COPD. Clinically, this implies that pharmacological or lifestyle interventions that directly address sympathetic nervous system activity, in addition to typical pulmonary medications, may offer a novel way to reduce morbidity and death in COPD. Future studies should concentrate on investigating treatments aimed at enhancing autonomic balance in COPD patients.
