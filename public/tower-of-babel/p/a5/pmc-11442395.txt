Successful emergency surgery for accidental ingestion of multiple neodymium magnets in an Argentine tegu (Salvator merianae)
Authors: Kengo UEDA, Akiko UEDA, Kiyokazu OZAKI
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A large Argentine tegu (Salvator merianae) presented with anorexia. Initial radiographs revealed a metallic foreign body in the stomach. The tegu vomited and became inactive two days later. A follow-up radiograph revealed the persistence of the foreign body in the same region. The foreign body was identified as a cluster of multiple magnets resembling neodymium magnets reported missing by the owner. An emergent laparotomy was performed due to gastrointestinal perforations caused by the multiple magnets. The surgical intervention revealed perforations in the walls of the stomach and small intestine and progressing acute peritonitis. Three magnets were extracted from the abdominal cavity and the tegu showed recovery. At 200 days postoperatively, the tegu continued to demonstrate good appetite and energy levels.

The Argentine black and white tegu (Salvator merianae) is a large omnivorous lizard indigenous to the tropical rainforests, savannahs, and semi-deserts of eastern and central South America. While tegus have become increasingly popular as pets in recent years, studies regarding their care and health management are still lacking [11].

Foreign bodies found in the gastrointestinal tract of reptiles have been documented in various species, including turtles [2, 6, 10], crocodiles [8, 20], snakes [18, 22], and herbivorous lizards [3, 14]. However, there is a notable absence of reports regarding carnivorous or omnivorous lizards. Accidental ingestion of neodymium magnets, known for their superior magnetic potency, is a common occurrence in humans, particularly infants. Such ingestion carries a substantial risk of gastrointestinal perforation and intestinal obstruction, often necessitating urgent intervention for removal [4, 7, 15, 19]. Nevertheless, there exists a dearth of literature on similar occurrences in reptilian species. Here, we present the first reported case of an Argentine black and white tegu that inadvertently ingested multiple neodymium magnets, resulting in acute peritonitis due to gastrointestinal perforation that required emergency surgical intervention. The primary aim of this case report is to document the clinical observations, diagnostic procedures, surgical interventions, and subsequent prognostic outcomes.

An 8-month-old male Argentine black and white tegu, weighing 2,043 g, was presented to our hospital with a chief complaint of anorexia. Upon initial examination, the tegu exhibited no signs of abdominal pain, displayed activity, and demonstrated a threat display by opening its mouth. While blood tests showed no discernible abnormalities, radiographic imaging revealed the presence of a radiopaque round-shaped foreign body (1.3 cm × 1.3 cm) in the upper abdomen that was anatomically situated in the stomach (Fig. 1Fig. 1.Radiograph at initial examination. Opting for a strategy of continued observation in anticipation of spontaneous expulsion, we monitored the patient’s condition. However, two days after the initial consultation, the tegu was readmitted to the hospital due to episodes of vomiting and persistent anorexia. The previous day, the owner had recognized the foreign body as resembling neodymium magnets that he utilized for securing pet sheets but had since gone missing. Upon reexamination, the tegu’s decreased activity prompted a repeat radiographic assessment, revealing within the same abdominal region, an impermeable foreign object that was suspected to comprise three neodymium magnets matching the description provided by the owner (Fig. 2Fig. 2.Radiograph at the second visit. Sedation was induced with intramuscular injections of midazolam 1 mg/kg (Midazolam injection 10 mg; Deva, Takeda Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and butorphanol 0.3 mg/kg (Betrophal 5 mg; Meiji Animal Health Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), followed by intravenous administration of alfaxalone 10 mg/kg (Alfaxan; Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) via the tail vein. The patient was intubated with a 2.5-mm uncuffed endotracheal tube and anesthesia was induced with 2% isoflurane in conjunction with oxygen (2 L/min) and maintained with intermittent ventilation. Following anesthesia induction, a 21G needle was inserted through intraosseous access into the right femur to facilitate administration of lactated Ringer’s solution at a rate of 10 mL/kg/hr throughout the surgical procedure (Japanese Pharmacopoeia Lactech Injection; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). During the subsequent rigid endoscopy, it was not possible to observe the condition of the gastric wall or any foreign bodies in the patient, as the endoscopy had to be immediately stopped without injecting air or saline solution due to some discomfort monitored in the tension of the gastric wall. Consequently, an emergency exploratory laparotomy was conducted via a right paramedian abdominal incision. The surgical procedure was executed with the patient positioned in dorsal lateral decubitus, following standard abdominal skin disinfection and laparotomy. When the abdominal wall was incised and the abdominal cavity explored, the bowel was found to be converging into the epigastrium. The magnets and, in turn, the mesentery became firmly attached to the forceps and medicine spoon that were used during the procedure (Fig. 3AFig. 3.Surgery image. (A) A magnet that moved to the abdominal cavity adheres to a medicine spoon. (B) A complex of three neodymium magnets.). The foreign body, which was a cluster of three magnets as evidenced during the radiographic examination, was extracted from the dorsal aspect of the stomach within the abdominal cavity (Figs. 2 and 3B). Adhesions were observed between the stomach and the small intestine, accompanied by perforations in their respective walls at adhesion sites. The perforations resulted in the leakage of gastrointestinal content and the development of peritonitis characterized by white pus and green intestinal fluid adherence (Fig. 4Fig. 4.Surgery image. A fistulous tract is formed in the wall of the small intestine, with a diameter large enough for a cotton swab to be inserted. White pus can be seen around the fistulous tract.). The wound was clean, as if the tissue had been punctured with a metallic instrument, and the walls of both the stomach and the small intestine exhibited fistulae that penetrated through all layers. The magnet had a circular shape with a long diameter of 13 mm. The perforation in the stomach made a circular wound with a size of approximately 16 mm. The perforated portion of the small intestine was slightly larger in size and presented an extended oval shape, with a fistula of about 20 mm to 25 mm in length that penetrated all layers. Accordingly, the perforated regions of the stomach and small intestine were excised and sutured, with the removal of a portion of the discolored lower abdominal fat pad on the right side. Suturing was performed using 5–0 polydioxanone (Ethicon PDS II; Johnson & Johnson, Tokyo, Japan), followed by intraperitoneal lavage with 500 mL of warmed saline (Japanese Pharmacopoeia saline; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Closure of the fascia and muscles was achieved with 3–0 polydioxanone (Ethicon PDSII; Johnson & Johnson), and the skin was everted and sutured with a 3–0 nylon thread (ELP; Akiyama Medical, Tokyo, Japan) using mattress sutures. Postoperatively, the intraosseous needle was removed, and the patient was revived under warm ambient air. After the surgery, daily subcutaneous administration of lactated Ringer’s solution (10 mL/kg) and piperacillin (100 mg/kg) (Japanese Pharmacopoeia Piperacillin Sodium Injection 1 g; Nichi-Iko, Toyama, Japan) was initiated, supplemented with subcutaneous administration of amikacin (2.5 mg/kg) (Japanese Pharmacopoeia Amikacin Sulfate Injection 100 mg; Fuji Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Toyama, Japan) every three days. Bacterial cultures of ascites showed the presence of Escherichia coli and Proteus vulgaris, with susceptibility to piperacillin and amikacin demonstrated via drug sensitivity testing. Histopathological analysis showed granulation tissue formation at the perforation site extending to the serosa. The tissue formation was characterized by infiltration of inflammatory cells, primarily neutrophils, indicative of acute intestinal perforation. Six days post-surgery, dehiscence of a portion of the skin prompted a secondary surgical intervention under the same anesthesia regimen. After the surgery, the patient was given chicken meat cut into small pieces to eat, with the portion gradually increased over the course of a week. After the second surgery, the appetite of the tegu gradually improved, with defecation resuming six days post-procedure. Antibiotics administration was started the day of the first surgery, and all antibiotics regimens were completed one week after the second surgery. Ultimately, the tegu was discharged from the hospital, exhibiting restored appetite and energy levels. At 200 days postoperatively, the tegu continued to demonstrate good appetite and energy levels.

Accidental ingestion in lizards is frequently reported in the herbivorous green iguana (Iguana iguana) [3, 14, 16]. Although a common occurrence, clinical reports of accidental ingestion by large carnivorous lizards are lacking in the literature. Given the lizards’ feeding behavior of consuming small animals in their entirety, including bones, it is plausible that while preying, they may inadvertently ingest foreign objects, like their herbivorous counterparts. Symptoms associated with foreign object ingestion include loss of appetite and vomiting. However, due to irregular feeding patterns, the recognition of such symptoms in lizards can be delayed, potentially leading to the manifestation of severe clinical complications.

Neodymium magnets have been reported to be dangerous foreign bodies with a high tendency to cause crushing of the intestinal wall and the mesentery, fistula formation, and intestinal obstruction when accidentally swallowed by humans, especially children, although there have been no reports of injury due to metal ion leakage from direct contact with gastric juices and other digestive fluids [4, 7, 15, 19]. Neodymium magnets exhibit magnetic forces exceeding 10 times that of general ferrite magnets. The first case of serious intestinal perforation resulting from magnet ingestion in humans was reported in 2002 [17]. Subsequent fatalities have been reported, including the case of a 20-month-old child in 2006 [15]. Following these tragic incidents, legislation has been enacted in the United States and European countries to prohibit the use of potent magnets in toys [5, 7, 22]. Nonetheless, in Japan, these magnets are readily available at low cost in toys or stationery items. The lack of regulatory oversight may thereby potentially foster a rise in ingestion incidents among various animal and reptilian species in the future.

For cases of magnet ingestion in humans, the management strategy varies depending on the number of magnets ingested. Cases with single-magnet ingestion present a low risk; thus, the standard approach involves a period of observation with the anticipation of natural excretion, coupled with regular follow-up assessments. Conversely, in cases involving the ingestion of multiple magnets, serial radiographic examinations are conducted at regular intervals to monitor their position within the gastrointestinal tract. Should the magnets fail to demonstrate movement during successive radiographic evaluations, suspicions arise regarding the possibility of perforation or intestinal entrapment, necessitating prompt consideration of aggressive endoscopic or surgical intervention [12].

In our case, the foreign body was initially presumed to be a single metallic lump on radiographic examination. It was anticipated that the foreign body would eject on its own, as is the case in human patients. However, two days after the initial evaluation, the animal’s activity rapidly decreased, the metallic foreign body was detected in the same anatomic site, and the metallic foreign body was similar in shape to the lost magnet. Radiographs clearly showed that it was three identical-shaped foreign bodies in one piece. This allowed us to speculate that the magnet had perforated the gastrointestinal tract and to perform surgical intervention. Despite the similarity in diameter between the extracted metallic foreign body and some types of single magnets, the foreign body was identified as an aggregate of three magnets. A lack of knowledge about the original magnets’ dimensions may result in a risk of erroneously concluding that only one magnet was involved. In principle, a detailed examination using combined X-ray imaging and computed tomography could help ascertain the presence and extent of associated complications. However, especially in cases involving magnets, the presence of a metallic foreign body could make accurate evaluation more difficult because of disturbing shadows appearing on the surrounding streaks.

Three cases of dogs that ingested magnets have previously been reported, each culminating in gastrointestinal perforation necessitating surgical intervention for successful resolution [9, 13, 21]. These cases underscored a notable disparity between human and animal responses to magnet ingestion, with the delayed recognition of the animal’s condition by owners posing a significant challenge. Consequently, relying solely on the expectation of natural excretion may pose undue risks. Recommendations from a recent study on magnet ingestion in children state that the period of observation and the decision to remove a magnet should not exceed six hr, based on the normal physiological transit time of food in humans. Thus, the physiological transit time of food should be estimated in the various animal species with the maximum delay for surgery decided accordingly [1]. Prompt surgical intervention is recommended when a metallic foreign object remains stationary within the gastrointestinal tract in serial radiographic examinations. This case of a large lizard displaying a decline in energy over two days with symptoms indicative of potential stomach or intestinal perforation required expedited intervention. Despite the absence of a discernible foreign body during emergency endoscopic examination under anesthesia, the decision to proceed with aggressive surgical intervention was based on clinical symptoms and diagnostic findings. This case serves as a reminder of the pivotal role played by prompt exploratory laparotomy in instances where gastrointestinal perforation is suspected based on clinical presentation and investigative results, ultimately preserving life.

During the laparotomy, the strong magnetic force caused the magnet to adhere to the forceps and medicine spoon. In cases where the foreign body is a magnet, even careful forceps operation during laparotomy may cause damage to the gastrointestinal wall and the mesentery. Laparoscopic surgery, which is less invasive, should also be considered; however, the effects of this magnetic force could likewise cause the magnet to stick to the instruments, making surgical operations difficult.

Finally, it is important to always be suspicious of magnets when observing metallic foreign bodies of any shape and to obtain as detailed a report as possible from the owner. A key aspect of the diagnosis is to determine whether a single magnet or multiple magnets have been swallowed, as this is a crucial prognostic factor. If there is a finding indicating a metallic foreign body, multiple X-ray images in different orientations should be taken. Even when deemed a single magnet, if the metallic foreign body is still in the gastrointestinal tract and has not moved after 24 hr, it should be treated as containing multiple magnets with prompt surgical intervention.

There are few case reports of foreign body ingestion in herbivorous and carnivorous lizards [3, 14, 16], and the clinical findings, diagnosis, surgical treatment, and prognosis are largely unknown. In addition, there are no reports of neodymium magnets being accidentally ingested; thus, more reports are warranted.

In conclusion, active surgical intervention in cases where reptiles have accidentally ingested magnets should be considered. To prevent similar accidents in the future, the population should be educated to avoid using magnets in reptile breeding environments.
