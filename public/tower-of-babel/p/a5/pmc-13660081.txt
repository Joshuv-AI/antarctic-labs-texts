High kinship and runs of homozygosity characterize walleye Sander vitreus metapopulation diversity in Lake Champlain, USA
Authors: Peter T. Euclide, Bernard Pientka, Matt J. Thorstensen, Ken M. Jeffries, Wesley A. Larson, J. Ellen Marsden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the roles of philopatry and geography in determining genetic population structure is an important part of managing species characterized by metapopulations. In this study, we examined population structure, kinship, and runs of homozygosity in walleye (Sander vitreus) from Lake Champlain, a large, fragmented lake with four main spawning tributaries that form a metapopulation. Using microsatellite genotyping at 12 loci, we investigated population genetic structure among the four tributaries to examine how spawning site fidelity influenced pairwise genetic distances. Using a high-density Rapture dataset containing thousands of reference-aligned SNP genotypes, we estimated evidence of inbreeding based on runs of homozygosity (ROH) in Lake Champlain and compared findings to four other large walleye populations in North America. The Lamoille and Poultney Rivers were genetically distinct from the Missisquoi and Winooski Rivers, despite evidence of 10% straying among rivers. Within-site kinship and the number of runs of homozygosity (ROH) in the Lamoille and Poultney Rivers were elevated compared to Missisquoi and Winooski Rivers, suggesting ongoing or recent inbreeding at these locations. Comparisons with other large-lake walleye populations revealed that the elevated number of ROHs in Lake Champlain was specific to the Lamoille and Poultney Rivers. We suggest that this inbreeding may be driven by demographic bottlenecks and small effective population sizes (<50 based on microsatellite data) that were potentially facilitated by hatchery practices. Our findings indicate that when combined with other factors, philopatry can have powerful impacts on fine-scale population structure and genomic diversity. We emphasize that metapopulations composed of philopatric sub-populations may be more vulnerable to localized inbreeding than systems with more random dispersal. Our results demonstrate how genetic data can inform conservation strategies, particularly in the face of habitat fragmentation and declining population sizes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-026-02558-0.

Background

Characterizing neutral population structure provides a foundation necessary to study evolution and local adaptation [1, 2] and has long been among the first steps in defining biologically meaningful management and conservation units (e.g. [3, 4]). Having biologically meaningful management units can improve the long-term sustainability of populations by protecting discrete breeding groups and local diversity [5, 6]. Therefore, genetic diversity, dispersal, and gene flow are important considerations for managers and conservation practitioners looking to maintain population abundance in the face of environmental stochasticity [7–9].

Protecting many small populations to support long-term species survival is not a new concept. The original “metapopulation theory” developed by Levin [10] discussed the importance of habitat heterogeneity and proposed that local extinction was more common in small, isolated populations. The concept was quickly adopted into population genetic frameworks, which showed that even populations with some gene flow have non-negative FST values [11]. More recently, “portfolio theory”, which broadly refers to protecting groups of sub-populations to benefit the stability of a larger population, has become a common focus in fisheries and wildlife management (e.g. [12]). These concepts center on a few key points: (1) borders of a “population” are rarely discrete, but instead a population is comprised of a matrix of interconnected sub-populations, and (2) the demographic and evolutionary processes of sub-populations may diverge from the greater population mean. For our purposes, metapopulation refers to the system or lake-wide group of a species, while sub-population refers to a single spawning group or location.

Metapopulation adaptability and stability are increasingly important considerations for conservation and management ecology as species face continued anthropogenic impacts from habitat fragmentation and climate-induced range shifts (e.g. [13–15]). Strong connectivity among sub-populations should help to buffer metapopulation-wide inbreeding and extinction as they allow recolonization following extirpation and help to maintain genetic diversity and adaptive capacity by limiting inbreeding when sub-population abundance is low [16–18]. For many species, connectivity can be retained via protection of habitat corridors [19, 20]. However, for highly philopatric species, physical connection alone does not ensure sub-populations experience high gene flow or recolonization after local extinction [21]. When philopatry is strong, sub-structure may be driven primarily by homing behavior and site fidelity, not geography [22]. For this reason, considering philopatric behavior is important when managing for adaptive capacity and connectivity.

Philopatric fish species in metapopulations often show patterns of genetic structure among spawning sites, even when there are few geographical barriers separating these locations [23, 24]. Philopatry evolved to reduce variability in reproductive success, and fish can be locally adapted to resident spawning areas [25, 26]. However, the strength of philopatry differs among and within species [27]. Breaking of philopatric behavior is known as “straying”, which almost always occurs to some degree and provides a means of gene flow and recolonization of extirpated spawning habitats within a metapopulation [28, 29]. Because of straying, the effective population size of the metapopulation is larger than any individual spawning sub-population, which should protect small sub-populations from inbreeding depression [17, 30, 31]. However, relatively few studies have investigated the extent to which straying alters diversity or limits inbreeding, and those that have primarily focused on salmonids (e.g. [32–35]). If straying rates are low or there is selection against strays that prevents successful reproduction, short-term impacts of inbreeding, such as long runs of homozygosity, may still occur. Quantifying local levels of genetic diversity and comparing them to the broader species-level diversity is important to contextualize the genetic integrity and adaptability of the local metapopulation to inform future management actions [36].

Runs of homozygosity (ROHs) are created when offspring inherit identical haplotypes at multiple genes from both parents [37]. The most common source of this inheritance pattern is inbreeding of closely related individuals. Reproduction among individuals with a very recent common ancestor (i.e., cousin, sibling, or parent-offspring mating) results in long ROHs while more distant common-ancestor mating results in shorter ROHs [38]. Therefore, metapopulations with large effective population size and admixture tend to have fewer ROHs [39]. All individuals contain some ROHs, but because ROHs can be enriched in homozygous deleterious variants, higher numbers of ROHs compared to other sub-populations of the same species can indicate potential inbreeding depression [40]. Because genomic data is necessary for evaluating ROHs, studies incorporating this type of information remain rare outside of model organisms or agricultural studies, leaving a gap in understanding of their prevalence in wild populations [41, 42].

Walleye (Sander vitreus) are a high trophic level predatory species of fish native to North America that live in both large and small lakes as well as some rivers. The age at maturity differs among metapopulations, but walleye generally mature around age 3 to 4 throughout most of their range and can live up to 30 years. Once mature, walleye spawn annually over cobble or gravel in rivers or on open-water cobble reefs in the early spring (reviewed in [43]). Walleye exhibit within-lake genetic structure among sub-populations in large lakes that contain multiple spawning sites, such as the Laurentian Great Lakes, Lake Winnipeg, Manitoba CAN, and lakes Mistassini and Mistasiniishish CAN (e.g. [44–46]). One hypothesized reason for genetic structure is philopatry, which is system-specific. Most estimates of philopatry using non-genetic approaches suggest that about 75% to 95% of walleye return to the same location annually to spawn [47–49]). Because walleye is a highly sought-after species by recreational anglers, substantial management resources have been put into maintaining the abundance of walleye for a long time [50–52]. The primary methods used to protect walleye abundance are protection and restoration of spawning habitat and fish stocking from hatcheries [53, 54]. In well-resourced regions, such as the Great Lakes, these methods have greatly benefited from understanding genetic structure and the extent to which multiple spawning sub-populations of walleye in a lake metapopulation are interconnected (e.g. [55–58]).

In this study, we focus on Lake Champlain, which is a large lake located in the northeastern United States that borders the states of New York and Vermont and the province of Quebec, Canada. Compared to the Great Lakes region, much less is known about the ecology and evolution of walleye in Lake Champlain. Four tributaries contain major walleye spawning aggregations: the Missisquoi, Winooski, Lamoille, and Poultney rivers, though several other streams also contain walleye spawning runs. Lake Champlain, therefore, contains a closed metapopulation of walleye. We hypothesize that (1) walleye genetic structure will be limited due to naturally occurring straying among sub-populations, and (2) relatedness among individuals will be low within each sub-population, similar to other walleye sub-populations from large lakes. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a population genetic assessment of 165 walleye from the four Lake Champlain tributaries using microsatellites and then conducted an assessment of ROHs using a high-density Rapture dataset available for a subset of individuals and compared the proportion, number, and size of ROH in Lake Champlain to four other major walleye sub-populations from large lakes.

Methods

Study location history and context

Lake Champlain is long (about 193 km) and narrow (20 km at the widest point) and is naturally fragmented by large islands and has been artificially fragmented by causeways constructed from the mid-1800s to early-1900s [59]. Therefore, movement among the spawning rivers requires transits through narrow causeway openings and may require some long-distance migrations of more than 100 km. An acoustic telemetry study in which 27 walleye were tracked for up to 3.5 years showed that some walleye make infrequent causeway transits, but these movements occurred outside the spawning season. The study also showed that walleye can have relatively large range size (10 to 25 km), though most individuals had relatively small home ranges throughout the year (3 to 5 km [60]). Overall, acoustic telemetry data indicated that there are no complete barriers among walleye spawning sites and that the metapopulation becomes mixed during the foraging season. Therefore, corridors for adult movement among spawning sites have been established based on these behavioral data.

Very little is known about the early life stages of walleye in Lake Champlain; however, one study showed that larval productivity among at least two of the tributaries appears to be variable [61]. While natural spawning and recruitment do occur, walleye larvae and fingerlings have been stocked into three Lake Champlain tributaries, the Missisquoi, Winooski, and Poultney rivers, since the 1980s by the Vermont Fish & Wildlife Department (VTFWD) to support the fishery. Presently, hatcheries collect gametes from feral broodstock each year and stock the resulting offspring as fingerlings into the donor river to avoid cross-system stocking. Until the early 2000s, walleye were stocked as larvae in addition to fingerlings, and there were at least three known events of cross-stocking of larvae among rivers, but the success of larval stocking was believed to be zero or close to zero, which is why the VTFWD switched to only fingerling stocking later [62]. Early in the hatchery program, spawning assessments indicated that most recruitment appeared to originate from natural reproduction; however, since the mid-2010s, approximately synonymous with a switch to stocking fingerlings, the proportion of stocked fish relative to natural recruits has been growing. This system is like walleye populations throughout the Northeastern United States, where hatchery supplementation to naturally reproducing populations is a common practice (e.g. [54, 63]).

Spawning site philopatry

Spawning site philopatry of walleye in Lake Champlain was estimated using archival VTFWD data describing angler-reported recapture data from walleye marked by the VTFWD with t-bar anchor or jaw tags. The tagging dataset contained 1261 angler-reported recapture events of walleye tagged between 1964 and 2008. All fish were tagged by VTFWD during spawning surveys conducted in rivers during peak spawning periods (March through April). Angler recaptures necessarily took place after peak spawning because the walleye angling season in Vermont purposefully protects spawning fish. However, anglers specifically target rivers in early spring because many walleye are still aggregated in rivers, presumably because they have recently spawned or have not yet spawned. Philopatry and straying rates were calculated as the proportion of walleye recaptured during the late spring spawning season (April and May) in subsequent years in known spawning rivers. Walleye recaptured in the same river as they were tagged were defined as philopatric while individuals recaptured in a different river than they were tagged in were defined as strays. Walleye were almost always harvested when captured so there were no repeat recaptures. The percent of individuals recaptured at each site was broken down to estimate relative straying among the Lamoille, Winooski, and Missisquoi rivers. Because only a small number of fish were ever tagged in the Poultney River (N = 438, relative to ~6000–8000 in the other rivers), and the river receives very little spring fishing effort, the Poultney River was not reviewed in this analysis.

Tissue sampling

All walleye were collected by trained VTFWD biologists under Vermont State standard operating procedures and animal ethics guidelines. Walleye were sampled from four Lake Champlain tributaries (Fig. 1A) using electrofishing during the spawning season in 2015 (Missisquoi River), 2016 (Winooski River), and 2017 (Poultney and Lamoille Rivers). Rivers were sampled in different years because VTFWD gamete collections for hatchery rearing rotate through rivers on a three-year cycle; the Lamoille River was not being stocked as of 2017, but stocking began in this river on a 4-year cycle in 2023. All fin clips were taken from the carcasses of walleye that had been euthanized for state-mandated disease testing using MS-222 submersion as suggested by the American Veterinary Medical Association; therefore, this research was exempt from the University of Vermont Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Fin clips from each fish were stored dry on blotting paper [64] until DNA extractions could be conducted using DNEasy extraction kits (Qiagen) and standard protocols at the University of Vermont, Burlington, Vermont. DNA extracted from each fin clip was used to amplify 13 microsatellite loci (Table S1 and S2; Borer et al., 1999; Wirth et al., 1999; Eldridge et al., 2002), and genotypes for each locus were determined using Genemapper at the University of Vermont genetic facility. Loci were checked for null alleles using microchecker v 2.2.3 [65] and for consistency with Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) in GenALEX; loci that were out of HWE in all four collection sites were removed [66]. Population genetic analysis

Following initial quality control, one of the 13 loci (SviL 4) was removed due to deviation from HWE in all four collections. Genetic structure and diversity were evaluated using microsatellite genotypes from the 12 remaining loci using GenoDive v3 [67]. Genetic diversity was summarized by calculating the number of effective alleles, observed heterozygosity, and inbreeding coefficient (GIS) for each of the four collections. Effective population size (Ne) was calculated using the linkage disequilibrium approach and a minimum allele frequency of 0.05 in NeEstimator [68]. Because walleye have overlapping generations, estimates of Ne more accurately represent the effective number of breeders present in the population [69]. Individual-based genetic structure among sites was determined using principal component analysis (PCA), and sub-population structure was determined using pairwise estimates of Jost’s D [70]. Isolation by distance among sub-populations was evaluated using a permuted Mantel test and Jost’s D as the genetic distance and Euclidean distance among locations in meters as the geographic distance matrix implemented in GenoDive v3 [67]. Finally, Bayesian STRUCTURE analysis was used to quantify the number of genetic clusters without a priori assumptions of geographic sub-structure or the admixture of each walleye to these clusters [71]. STRUCTURE was conducted using all individuals without any location prior for K 1 to 5, a burn-in period of 50,000, and 150,000 steps post burn-in. This process was repeated three times for each value of K. The most likely value of K was determined using the Delta K approach [72].

Kinship

To determine whether individual kinship varied among Lake Champlain sub-populations, we quantified pairwise kinship and then compared the numbers of closely related individuals among sub-populations using chi-square analysis. The pairwise relationships among all individuals were investigated using the Loiselle et al. [73] kinship estimator implemented through GenoDive v3 [67]. This approach measures the relative probability of identity by descent between two compared individuals, assuming random mating, while using the allele frequency across the total dataset. To investigate relatedness across the entire dataset, putatively related individuals were defined as individuals with kinship > 0.25, which in a randomly mating population should be akin to full siblings [73]. The average degree of intra-collection kinship was compared using network diagrams to identify clusters of individuals with high relatedness, and chi-square was used to determine whether collections differed in the total number of related individuals. Because the Loiselle et al. [73] kinship estimator assumes random mating, it is biased by population structure. Therefore, we also calculated pairwise kinship within each collection separately and compared the number of putatively related pairs with kinship > 0.25 among collections using a chi-square test.

Inter-lake comparisons of inbreeding

We quantified the number (i.e., total number of ROH meeting criteria below) and length of ROH (i.e., the total length regions identified as ROH in each individual) in each of the four river sub-populations sampled in Lake Champlain to determine whether any of the sub-populations were experiencing recent inbreeding. We then compared these ROH measures to other large-lake systems with varying degrees of stocking, demographic history, and population size to contextualize Lake Champlain walleye population diversity at the species level. The four comparison sites chosen were: Sandusky River - Lake Erie, USA, Tittabawassee River – Lake Huron, USA, Lake Winnipeg in Manitoba, Canada, and Cedar Bluff Reservoir in Kansas, USA. Sandusky River is an entirely wild sub-population that is part of one of the largest naturally reproducing metapopulations of walleye in the Great Lakes region and has experienced a negligible amount of stocking (Great Lakes Stocking database: https://fsis.glfc.org/stocking/). Two to six million walleye larvae are produced from the Sandusky River annually, with the metapopulation-wide larval production estimated to be between 1 and 2 billion walleye annually [7]. Sandusky River samples were collected by the Ohio Department of Fish and Wildlife as part of a spawning survey. The Tittabawassee River went through a severe bottleneck in the early 1900s and was completely reestablished via stocking of non-local strains of walleye, which took place from the 1970s to 2006. The samples used in this analysis were collected by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources in 1999 during the period when the annual stocking program was ongoing, but natural recruitment was increasing [74]. Samples from Lake Winnipeg were collected from larvae produced from the 2019 Swan Creek Hatchery, which uses annual feral gamete collection for their production, like the approach used in Vermont [75]. Finally, Cedar Bluff Reservoir in Kansas is an entirely introduced and stocked system dependent on hatchery production. Samples from the reservoir were collected by the Kobayashi lab from Fort Hays State University from parents used for gamete collection [75].

A subset of 12 individuals from each Lake Champlain sub-population was sequenced along with 12 walleye each from the Sandusky River, Tittabawassee River, Cedar Bluff Reservoir, and Lake Winnipeg using a RAD Capture panel developed for walleye in the Great Lakes [76, 77]. This panel includes a single 80-nucleotide bait at 2X tiling density for 99,636 sequences containing a SNP that is polymorphic in the Great Lakes; this translates to approximately one SNP marker for every 8000 base pairs (bp) of the walleye genome. Baits were BLASTed against the yellow perch genome [78], keeping only baits that passed “Stringent” BLAST filtering, did not align to the mitochondrial genome, and passed all other Arbor Biosciences’ filtration parameters. The bait closest to the 5’ end of each sequence was retained. Sequences from all samples were aligned to the walleye genome (NCBI: GCA_031162955.1) using the Burrows-Wheeler alignment (BWA) and mem algorithm, and SNPs were called from alignment files using the STACKS2 reference-aligned module [79–81]. SNPs were identified in all individuals and then output separately for each system to retain only SNPs with a genotype rate greater than 90% per-population for that system (R > 0.9). Only individuals with an average depth of coverage across remaining sites of >10X were retained. The resulting variants file was then imported into PLINK v. 1.9, which was used to identify ROHs with a scanning window of 100 SNPs, maximal distance between SNPs of 500 Kb, and minimum SNP density 75 Kb/SNP, and all other parameters left as default in accordance with suggestions provided by Meyermans et al. [82]. The ROH inbreeding (FROH) was then calculated on a per-individual level using the equation FROH= LROH/Laut where LROH is the total length in base pairs of ROHs in an individual’s genome, and Laut is the length of the autosomal genome [82]. Two-way analysis of variance was then used to determine whether FROH, number of ROHs, and the average length of ROHs differed among rivers with the number of genotyped SNPs as a co-variable (x ~ sub-population * number of SNPs). Subsequent pairwise analysis using a Tukey HSD test was then used post hoc to determine which rivers differed.

Data analysis and visualization throughout the study were conducted using R v.4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025), custom scripts, ggplot2, tidyverse, ggsci, and ggraph software packages [83–86].

Population diversity and structure in Lake Champlain

Walleye in all four river sub-populations in the Lake Champlain watershed had similar levels of genetic diversity (Table 2). Across all samples, the estimated effective number of alleles per locus was 4.02 (95% CI = 3.41 to 4.66), observed heterozygosity was 0.71 (95% CI = 0.67 to 0.75), and inbreeding was 0.043 (95% CI = 0.003 to 0.084). Walleye from the Lamoille River had a slightly lower number of effective alleles and observed heterozygosity estimates, and correspondingly had slightly higher inbreeding coefficient estimates than the other three rivers (Table 2). The estimated Nb was small in the Poultney River (Nb = 29.5), Lamoille River (Nb = 33.7), and Winooski River (Nb = 60.7) walleye sub-populations, and much higher in the Missisquoi River sub-population (Nb = 599.9; Table 2). Measures of Jost’s D indicated that allele frequencies of the walleye sub-populations in each of the four rivers differed significantly from each other (Table 3). Kinship

The number of closely related individuals was higher in the Lamoille and Poultney rivers compared to the Winooski or Missisquoi rivers (Fig. 2). When all individuals were evaluated together, there were 55 related individual pairs in Lamoille out of 741 pairwise comparisons and 27 related individual pairs out of 990 pairwise comparisons in the Poultney River. In contrast, only 3 of 990 and 3 of 630 pairwise comparisons were related individuals in the Missisquoi and Winooski rivers (X2 = 99.0, df = 3, p < 2.2×10−16). There were only three inter-river sibling pairs: one between a Missisquoi fish and a Poultney fish, one between a Missisquoi fish and a Winooski fish, and one between a Missisquoi fish and a Lamoille fish (Fig. 2). Runs of homozygosity

To determine whether high-kinship and genetic structure might be resulting in inbreeding, we measured ROHs in all four collections in Lake Champlain and compared them to walleye sub-populations outside of the Lake Champlain basin. Following quality control, 86 walleye met sequencing criteria of 10X coverage per individual: Missisquoi (N = 12), Lamoille (N = 11), Winooski (N = 8), Poultney (N = 12), Sandusky (N = 10), Tittabawassee (N = 12), Cedar Bluff (N = 10), and Winnipeg (N = 11). At least one ROH was detected in all but five individuals. No ROH were detected in two individuals from the Poultney River, three from the Sandusky River, and one individual from the Tittabawassee River. Because these individuals had consistent genotyping rate and sequencing depth as the rest of the sample set, they were retained in the analysis. Fish from Lake Champlain contained an average of 85,820 sites at a SNP density of 0.111 SNPs/bp. Fish from the Great Lakes (the Tittabawassee and Sandusky Rivers) contained an average of 123,432 sites at a SNP density of 0.158 SNPs/bp. Fish from Cedar Bluff Reservoir contained an average of 156,387 sites at a SNP density of 0.201 SNPs/bp. Fish from Lake Winnipeg contained an average of 177,739 sites at a SNP density of 0.228 SNPs/bp. To test for any impact of SNP density on the interpretation, we compared the results of Tukey HSD tests conducted on raw FROH values to FROH values that were standardized by dividing the natural log of FROH by average SNP density for each location. We found that results were consistent between standardized and unstandardized analyses, and therefore unstandardized results were reported for simplicity.

Within Lake Champlain, the FROH, the number of ROHs, and the average length of ROHs differed among rivers (Fig. 3) and were not influenced by the number of genotyped sites (p > 0.05 for all SNP effects and interactions). Walleye from the Lamoille and Poultney Rivers had greater FROH than any other river (F7,70 = 7.75; p = 6.5 × 10−7), including the Missisquoi and Winooski Rivers in Lake Champlain as well as all out-of-basin sub-populations sampled (Fig. 3A). Multiple individuals from the Lamoille and Poultney Rivers also had more total ROHs than walleye from other walleye lakes (Fig. 3B). A Tukey HSD test indicated that the Poultney River contained significantly more ROHs than all other sites except the Winooski River or the Tittabawassee River (F7,70 = 7.42; p = 1.2 × 10−6). The average length of ROH also differed among rivers (F7,70 = 6.78; p = 3.9 × 10−6). However, Tukey HSD indicated that there was no significant difference among any of the Lake Champlain rivers and the Tittabawassee River, but that the Poultney and Lamoille Rivers had significantly longer ROH than the Sandusky River, Lake Winnipeg, and Cedar Bluff Reservoir (Fig. 3C). Discussion

Population genetic and kinship analysis did not support our hypothesis that genetic structure in Lake Champlain would be limited due to straying. Our results suggest that walleye sub-populations in the Lake Champlain metapopulation are strongly philopatric, but that there may be some additional barrier to gene flow beyond site fidelity, resulting in distinct genetic differences among Lake Champlain sub-populations. Likely as a consequence of the robust spatial structure, we found evidence contrary to our second hypothesis that relatedness would be low. Instead, we found that there was elevated kinship and low effective population sizes (<50) within two of the four sub-populations investigated. At the genome level, sub-populations with high kinship in Lake Champlain also contained higher numbers of ROHs and greater total length of ROHs in the genome, suggesting that dispersal among spawning sites may not offset the effects of inbreeding.

Several factors may contribute to the robust genetic structure observed among walleye sub-populations in the study area: isolation by distance (IBD), habitat fragmentation, spawning site philopatry, local adaptation, and demographic changes (i.e., extinction and recolonization, and bottleneck effects). Isolation by distance and habitat fragmentation likely limit the movement of walleye around Lake Champlain, but we found no significant relationship between geographic distance and genetic distance based on a Mantel test. Nonetheless, the Poultney and Missisquoi Rivers are on opposite ends of the lake, approximately 150 km from each other. Therefore, walleye from these rivers may not intermix commonly during the foraging season, depending on the size of their home range. However, in the Great Lakes, walleye consistently migrate hundreds of kilometers per-year to forage and spawn [87, 88]. Based on the limited data that are available, walleye in Lake Champlain appear not to disperse as far from spawning sites as they do in the Laurentian Great Lakes, but long-distance travel to and from the Main Basin of Lake Champlain is not uncommon [60, 89]. Additionally, our analysis of angler recaptures identified one walleye that was tagged in the Lamoille River but recaptured in the Poultney River during the spawning season, confirming long-distance movement. The three northern tributaries are all substantially closer together and therefore even less likely to be isolated by distance alone. Several manmade causeways could limit the movement of walleye among these rivers throughout the year. However, 23% of 22 acoustically tagged walleye tracked in Lake Champlain moved through one or more causeways [60]. In addition, past studies have found very little impact of these causeways on the population genetics of other species of fishes living in Lake Champlain [90–93], and we identified several stray angler recapture events among these three northern tributaries. Therefore, we suggest that factors besides IBD and fragmentation likely contribute to most of the genetic structure observed, as they have in other systems [46, 94, 95].

While difficult to conclusively identify in this study, local adaptation may be a driver of isolation among spatially proximal spawning sites. We were only able to determine that sub-populations in Lake Champlain were differentiated at neutral loci, which indicated that there may be some reproductive barrier preventing gene flow. Investigation with higher-density sequencing approaches may help to identify islands of divergence indicative of local adaptation missed by our assessment that have been suggested in other studies of walleye and other species of fishes [46, 77, 96, 97] Local adaptation to spawning sites could be facilitated by a high propensity for spawning site philopatry in Lake Champlain. Walleye have long been known to be philopatric [98], and both genetic (e.g. [45, 99]), and tagging studies from other systems indicate that walleye express spawning site philopatry (e.g. [49, 94, 100]). Our estimates of 88% to 92% philopatry are consistent with these studies. Our philopatry estimates using angler recaptures may be low, since they only report recaptures post-peak spawning period. Therefore, some fish captured at this time may be in the river for reasons other than spawning. Furthermore, these data may be male-biased because while females tend to travel further distances, males spend longer periods in the river following spawning (e.g. [101, 102]). While we cannot account for sex-biased dispersal, studies in Green Bay found that spawning site fidelity was similar among males and females [94]. Strong philopatry combined with local adaptation to sub-population is, therefore, one plausible hypothesis for the observed population structure.

Both the Lamoille and Poultney rivers had elevated degrees of kinship, while the Winooski and Missisquoi did not. The Missisquoi River and Winooski River historically have had robust walleye spawning aggregations [61]. As aggregate spawners, non-random reproduction within family groups is unlikely. Therefore, the elevated kinship is likely a result of random reproduction between related pairs due to small population size and limited dispersal in or out of the Lamoille and Poultney rivers [103]. A possible explanation for the high kinship in the Poultney River is stocking. The Poultney River fish were collected in 2017 and therefore represent the stocking practices that occurred from about 2000 onward, which entailed random egg and milt collections from feral walleye in the Poultney River every three years. The Poultney River was stocked in 2003 with the progeny of 46 females and again in 2005 with the progeny of 48 females. No male numbers were reported for these years. Starting in 2007, following the invasion of Viral Hemorrhagic Septicemia (VHS) in the Laurentian Great Lakes [104], hatcheries began collecting and spawning a maximum of 25 male-female pairs so that all parents could be disease tested. Consequently, the effective number of breeders used to produce hatchery-reared fish that were introduced into Lake Champlain during the last two decades has been close to a Nb of 50, which is often considered a minimum to avoid inbreeding in a single generation [105]. If stocked fish represent a major component of all returning walleye, the chance of close-kin random mating is likely to be high. This inbreeding trend would be amplified if closely related individuals were sampled for gamete collection in subsequent years, potentially leading to a Ryman-Laikre effect whereby genetic diversity is reduced due to inbreeding [106]. However, the Lamoille River only developed a stocking program in 2023; thus, the high degree of relatedness at this site is more likely due to naturally occurring inbreeding associated with a small population size. Small effective population sizes have been reported in many other walleye populations that were assessed with microsatellite genotyping and have been suggested to result from both boom-and-bust recruitment and philopatry (e.g. [107, 108], consistent with our conclusions. There is also some evidence that walleye in the Lamoille River represent a small river-resident population, as walleye are caught in the river on the spawning site year-round (VTFWD, unpublished data). In both the Lamoille and Poultney sub-populations, our results suggest that walleye may either have recently undergone a genetic bottleneck or are currently experiencing a bottleneck because of low abundance and high spawning site fidelity, which has limited dispersal into these rivers of individuals from the larger Winooski and Missisquoi river sub-populations. In the Poultney River, inbreeding may be accelerated by stocking practices that inadvertently sample and propagate related individuals.

High numbers of ROHs in the Lamoille and Poultney rivers were consistent with the findings of high kinship. These results further indicate that inbreeding may be having an ongoing impact on the genetic diversity in at least the Lamoille and Poultney rivers. While our power to detect all ROHs was limited by the density of available genetic markers, detection of ROH using PLINK remains accurate for datasets with more than 22 SNP/Mb density [109], which was in the range of SNP density for all locations we investigated (Table S3). Even when assuming some ROHs were missed, both rivers that contained high numbers or related pairs contained large numbers of ROHs. Elevated numbers and length of ROHs can indicate inbreeding generally, and ROHs longer than a megabase indicate stronger evidence of recent or ongoing inbreeding, as recombination would have shortened them in the absence of recent inbreeding [41]. Six out of 12 fish in the Lamoille River and four out of 12 fish in the Poultney River contained more than 50 Mbs total of ROH segments, and the average length of individual ROH segments was 2.4 to 2.5 Mbs per individual walleye. Meanwhile, only a single individual out of the 24 individuals evaluated in the Winooski and Missisquoi rivers contained more than 50 Mbs total of ROH segments, and the average length of individual ROH segments was shorter (1.7 to 1.9 Mbs per individual walleye, respectively). Furthermore, the Winooski and Missisquoi rivers both contained numbers and FROH similar to other large walleye sub-populations throughout North America. The systems selected for the analysis outside of the Lake Champlain basin were chosen because they contain ecological similarities to the sub-populations sampled in Lake Champlain. Three of the four systems are from “large” lakes containing a metapopulation with multiple spawning sub-populations of walleye. Additionally, three of the four systems have experienced intensive stocking. All four contain river-run walleye spawning aggregations. While it is difficult to completely control for system-level differences in walleye behavior and genetic diversity, we suggest that these four sub-populations are representative of a broad range of large-lake walleye metapopulations that have had moderate to strong human influence on population abundance over time. Therefore, we conclude that the ROH results indicate that the Lamoille and Poultney rivers likely have substantially more inbreeding than typical walleye spawning populations. Nonetheless, our results should still be viewed as a case study, and additional analysis of many more walleye sub-populations would be necessary to determine how unique the inbreeding identified in Lake Champlain is within walleye as a species.

Genetic structure among sub-populations suggests that gene flow is limited, but both tagging and genetic data identified several walleye that appeared to be potential strays. At the individual level, several walleye collected in the Lamoille and Poultney rivers had genetic ancestry similar to that of walleye collected in the Winooski and Missisquoi rivers. If these fish are first-generation strays or recent backcrosses, this contradicts our finding of high philopatry and would represent an approximately 17% straying rate to the Lamoille River and 8% straying rate to the Poultney River from the Winooski and Missisquoi rivers. Consistent successful reproduction by the 8% of walleye that strayed should be enough to homogenize population structure unless the effective population size of populations is very low, leading to rapid genetic drift [110, 111]. It is possible that these fish were mis-assigned or mis-genotyped because of errors in calling microsatellite genotypes, and are, therefore, not strays from other sites. Also, because our tagging data only included recaptures post-peak spawning, straying rates estimated from angler recaptures represent a high estimate of straying since we cannot be sure that fish captured by anglers had not already spawned elsewhere. However, given that multiple individuals in both rivers were found with this ancestry, genotyping error is an unlikely reason. It is more likely that the fish captured are true strays, but that for some reason they do not contribute to the population, either because they were not reproductively active when captured, or some other reason that is difficult to determine without additional data about the specific timing, location, and reproductive state of the captured individuals that was not recorded.

Conclusions

In line with other studies, we found that philopatry may substantially contribute to genetic structure among sub-populations [22, 112]. Walleye, like many species of fishes, are often studied and managed at the metapopulation level, but there is increasing evidence that fine-scale differences among individual sub-populations can greatly influence the whole metapopulation [7, 12, 113]. The strength of philopatric behavior should also be considered in population genetic and conservation studies, as we found that it may play a major role not just in structuring metapopulations but could also influence the amount of inbreeding that sub-populations experience. During times of high local abundance, philopatry most likely plays an important role in limiting reproductive variance [114]. However, our results suggest that during times of low local abundance or other demographic variance, philopatry may limit gene flow and lead to periods of increased inbreeding that could influence species or population level adaptive capacity [15, 115]. Consequently, metapopulations comprising several philopatric sub-populations may experience different extinction and recolonization patterns compared with truly randomly dispersing metapopulations [116, 117].
