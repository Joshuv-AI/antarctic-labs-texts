A silent tumor, a loud valve: carcinoid heart disease as the first manifestation of a pelvic neuroendocrine tumor
Authors: Pinaki Dutta, Nalini Gupta, Sanjay Kumar Bhadada, Vivek Jha, Raghuraman Soundararajan, Ajay Gulati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarcinoid heart disease (CaHD) is a well-recognized complication of functional neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), typically occurring in the presence of hepatic metastases that permit vasoactive substances to reach the systemic circulation. Its occurrence in the absence of liver involvement is uncommon and may delay diagnosis. We report a 46-year-old woman who was incidentally found to have severe tricuspid regurgitation during preoperative evaluation for uterine fibroids. Further evaluation revealed elevated 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid and markedly increased chromogranin A levels, raising suspicion of a functional NET. Imaging identified a large somatostatin receptor–avid pelvic mass without evidence of hepatic or distant metastasis. The patient underwent successful surgical excision following perioperative octreotide infusion, and histopathology confirmed a Grade 1 well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor arising from the mesosalpinx/peritoneal region suggestive of a primary extraintestinal neuroendocrine tumor. Postoperatively, she demonstrated biochemical improvement and clinical stabilization on somatostatin analog therapy; however, severe tricuspid regurgitation persisted. This case highlights an unusual presentation of CaHD in the absence of hepatic metastasis and underscores the importance of considering neuroendocrine tumors in patients with unexplained right-sided valvular heart disease.Keywords: neuroendocrine tumor, carcinoid heart disease, tricuspid regurgitation, carcinoid syndrome, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, extraintestinal neuroendocrine tumor, mesosalpinx

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms arising from diffuse neuroendocrine cells, most commonly involving the gastrointestinal tract and bronchopulmonary system. A subset of these tumors is functional and secretes vasoactive substances, such as serotonin, leading to carcinoid syndrome, characterized by episodic flushing, diarrhea, and bronchospasm [1].

Carcinoid heart disease (CaHD) represents a major complication of prolonged exposure to circulating serotonin and related mediators, resulting in plaque-like fibrotic thickening of right-sided cardiac valves, particularly the tricuspid and pulmonary valves [2]. It occurs in approximately 20-50% of patients with carcinoid syndrome and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes [3]. Classically, the development of carcinoid syndrome and CaHD is dependent on the presence of hepatic metastases, which allow vasoactive substances to bypass hepatic metabolism and enter the systemic circulation [4].

In contrast, the occurrence of carcinoid syndrome in the absence of liver metastasis is distinctly uncommon and suggests alternative mechanisms such as primary tumors with atypical venous drainage or extraintestinal origin [5]. Primary neuroendocrine tumors arising from the peritoneum, mesentery, or retroperitoneum are exceedingly rare, and in most cases, such lesions represent metastases rather than true primary tumors [6, 7]. However, isolated case reports have described primary mesenteric or retroperitoneal NETs, supporting the possibility of a primary extraintestinal origin [6, 7]. Primary peritoneal neoplasms themselves are uncommon and arise from mesothelial or submesothelial elements, with neuroendocrine differentiation being particularly unusual [8].

We describe a unique case of a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor arising from the pelvic mesosalpinx/peritoneal region, presenting as carcinoid heart disease without hepatic metastasis. This highlights an atypical presentation pathway and the need to consider neuroendocrine tumors in patients with unexplained right-sided valvular heart disease.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old woman was evaluated for heavy menstrual bleeding of 1-year duration and was found on ultrasonography to have multiple uterine fibroids, for which hysterectomy was planned. During routine pre-anesthetic evaluation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe tricuspid regurgitation with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. She was referred to cardiology for further evaluation and surgical clearance.

Cardiac assessment demonstrated isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation with markedly thickened and retracted tricuspid valve leaflets, restricted leaflet mobility, and failure of coaptation, characteristic of organic valvular pathology. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved, with no evidence of left-sided valvular disease. In view of these findings, carcinoid heart disease was suspected. On further history, she reported exertional dyspnea (New York Heart Association class II–III), palpitations, and pedal edema for the preceding 2 years. She also described intermittent, self-limiting episodes of flushing for 6 months. There was no history of diarrhea, wheezing, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, or chest pain.

Given the suspicion of a neuroendocrine etiology, biochemical evaluation was initiated, and she was referred to endocrinology for further workup.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial biochemical evaluation revealed elevated 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) levels of 13.6 mg/24 hours (reference range: 2-9 mg/24 hours), corresponding to 71.2 µmol/24 hours (reference range: 10-47 µmol/24 hours). Repeat assessment (Table 1) confirmed further elevation to 19.1 mg/24 hours (99.9 µmol/24 hours), supporting the presence of serotonin-secreting tumor activity. Serum chromogranin A was markedly elevated at 3073 ng/mL (3073 µg/L) (reference range, <100 ng/mL [<100 µg/L]) consistent with a high neuroendocrine tumor burden.

To localize the primary lesion, cross-sectional and functional imaging were performed. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen demonstrated a lobulated, heterogeneous mass measuring approximately 6.3 × 6.7 × 9 cm in the right iliac fossa, in close proximity to ileal loops (Fig. 1). Functional imaging with Gallium-68 [1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid]-Nal3-octreotide positron emission tomography/computed tomography (68Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT) revealed intense somatostatin receptor (SSTR) expression in a large right iliac fossa mass measuring approximately 6.7 × 6.1 × 10 cm, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 27.7 (Fig. 2). Importantly, there was no evidence of SSTR-avid lesions in the liver, pancreas, spleen, adrenal glands, or elsewhere, indicating absence of metastatic disease.

Fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT demonstrated only mild metabolic activity within the lesion (SUVmax 6.7), consistent with a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor phenotype.

Cardiac evaluation with echocardiography confirmed severe tricuspid regurgitation with markedly thickened and retracted tricuspid valve leaflets, restricted leaflet excursion, and poor coaptation, with preserved left ventricular systolic function and no left-sided valvular involvement. These echocardiographic findings were highly characteristic of carcinoid valvular heart disease (Hedinger syndrome) in the appropriate clinical, biochemical, and imaging context. N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was elevated (values up to 422.6 pg/mL [422.6 ng/L]; reference range, <260 pg/mL [<260 ng/L]), reflecting right-sided cardiac strain.

Alternative causes of isolated severe right-sided valvular disease were systematically considered. There was no clinical history suggestive of rheumatic heart disease, infective endocarditis, congenital structural heart disease, chronic pulmonary disease, including interstitial lung disease, thromboembolic disease, or previously known valvular heart disease. The preserved left ventricular systolic function, absence of significant left-sided valvular abnormalities, and characteristic tricuspid valve morphology favored carcinoid valvular heart disease over alternative etiologies. Overall, the diagnosis of carcinoid heart disease was supported by converging clinical, biochemical, imaging, and echocardiographic evidence, including serotonin excess, markedly elevated neuroendocrine tumor markers, a functional somatostatin receptor–avid pelvic neuroendocrine tumor, characteristic isolated right-sided valvular involvement, and absence of plausible competing etiologies. The absence of liver metastasis suggested an atypical primary site, possibly extraintestinal or peritoneal, allowing vasoactive substances to bypass hepatic metabolism.

Treatment

The patient was initiated on preoperative prophylaxis with continuous intravenous octreotide infusion at a rate of 50 mcg/hour, starting 24 hours prior to surgery and continued for 72 hours postoperatively to prevent carcinoid crisis.

She subsequently underwent exploratory laparotomy, which revealed a 12 × 6 cm tumor arising from the right pelvic region, adherent to the mesosalpinx. The tumor was excised in toto after ligation of feeding vessels. Moderate ascites was noted intraoperatively. The liver appeared congested and firm; however, no metastatic lesions were identified. The perioperative course was uneventful, with no evidence of carcinoid crisis.

Histopathological examination revealed a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor arising from the mesosalpinx/pelvic peritoneum, suggestive of a primary peritoneal neuroendocrine tumor. The Ki-67 proliferation index was 0-1%, consistent with a Grade 1 neuroendocrine tumor (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for chromogranin A, while synaptophysin was non-contributory, overall supporting neuroendocrine differentiation (Fig. 3).

Outcome and follow-up

Following surgical resection, the patient demonstrated significant symptomatic improvement, particularly in flushing episodes and functional capacity. However, early postoperative functional imaging demonstrated a small residual SSTR–avid lesion (∼0.8 × 1.3 cm; SUVmax 6.7); therefore, long-acting somatostatin analog therapy with octreotide long-acting release (LAR) 20 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks was initiated for ongoing biochemical suppression and disease control.

Serial follow-up showed a progressive decline in 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA levels and serum chromogranin A concentrations, consistent with biochemical response. Functional imaging initially demonstrated a small residual SSTR–avid lesion measuring approximately 0.8 × 1.3 cm (SUVmax 6.7) (Fig. 4). Subsequent imaging showed interval resolution of detectable disease activity; however, at a later stage, focal SSTR uptake was noted in the ileal loops (SUVmax 8.1) without evidence of distant or hepatic metastasis.

Octreotide therapy was temporarily withheld during periods of biochemical and radiological remission and subsequently reinitiated upon evidence of residual disease activity as part of ongoing disease monitoring.

At the most recent follow-up, the patient remains clinically stable with New York Heart Association class I–II symptoms, with no evidence of metastatic disease on serial imaging. She continues on octreotide LAR therapy along with diuretic management (torsemide/spironolactone) and is under ongoing biochemical, radiological, and cardiac surveillance.

Discussion

This case illustrates an unusual presentation of a well-differentiated NET, where CaHD preceded classical carcinoid syndrome and occurred without hepatic metastasis. This rare presentation poses diagnostic and pathophysiological challenges and, to our knowledge, represents one of the few reported cases arising from a primary extraintestinal pelvic neuroendocrine tumor.

CaHD results from prolonged exposure of the endocardium to serotonin and other vasoactive substances, leading to fibrotic plaque deposition predominantly affecting the right-sided cardiac valves. While this process is well recognized, it is typically described in the setting of metastatic disease involving the liver, where hepatic metabolism of serotonin is bypassed [2-4]. In contrast, the absence of liver metastasis in our patient suggests an alternative route of systemic exposure to vasoactive mediators. One plausible explanation is atypical venous drainage directly into the systemic circulation, thereby circumventing first-pass hepatic inactivation. Similar mechanisms have been proposed in patients with primary ovarian or retroperitoneal NETs, where venous outflow drains directly into the inferior vena cava [9, 10].

This case highlights that sustained serotonin exposure sufficient to induce carcinoid heart disease may occur even in the absence of hepatic metastasis, challenging the traditional paradigm of portal metabolism dependence.

Another notable aspect of this case is the early cardiac involvement relative to systemic symptoms, with CaHD being detected incidentally during preoperative evaluation. Although flushing was present, it was intermittent and not the predominant clinical feature. Prior studies have shown that cardiac involvement may occasionally precede or overshadow other features of carcinoid syndrome, particularly in patients with sustained subclinical serotonin exposure [11]. This underscores the importance of considering a neuroendocrine etiology in patients presenting with unexplained right-sided valvular disease.

Although flushing was present for only approximately 6 months, symptoms attributable to right-sided cardiac dysfunction, including exertional dyspnea, palpitations, and pedal edema, had been present for nearly 2 years. Given the substantial tumor burden (12 cm) and marked biochemical evidence of serotonin excess, accelerated serotonin-mediated valvular fibrotic injury remains biologically plausible.

The identification of a pelvic mass arising from the mesosalpinx/peritoneal region further adds to the uniqueness of this case. Primary NETs in such locations are exceedingly rare, and in most instances, extraintestinal NETs represent metastatic disease from an occult primary [6, 7]. However, the absence of detectable primary lesions elsewhere on high-resolution functional imaging, along with lack of hepatic involvement, supports the possibility of a primary extraintestinal NET, although this cannot be established with absolute certainty. We acknowledge that no historical echocardiographic or pre-existing cross-sectional imaging prior to presentation was available, which limits definitive exclusion of previously asymptomatic pre-existing valvular pathology. Similar rare cases of primary mesenteric or retroperitoneal NETs have been described, lending support to this hypothesis [7, 9].

From a management perspective, complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for localized well-differentiated NETs and is associated with significant symptomatic and biochemical improvement [12]. In our patient, surgical excision combined with somatostatin analog therapy resulted in normalization of biochemical markers and stabilization of disease. Because postoperative somatostatin analog therapy was initiated in the presence of residual functional disease, symptomatic improvement cannot be attributed solely to surgical tumor resection. Although tumor resection and biochemical control may reduce ongoing serotonin-mediated injury and prevent further disease progression, established carcinoid valvular fibrosis is often structurally irreversible. Consequently, severe tricuspid regurgitation may persist despite successful tumor control, as observed in our patient, consistent with the known natural history of advanced carcinoid heart disease [13]. This emphasizes the need for early diagnosis before advanced cardiac involvement occurs.

This case highlights 3 key clinical insights: first, CaHD may rarely occur without hepatic metastasis; second, cardiac manifestations may precede overt carcinoid syndrome; and third, extraintestinal or peritoneal NETs should be considered in atypical presentations. Recognizing these features is essential for timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary management.
