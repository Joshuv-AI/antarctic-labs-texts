Elevated Creatine Kinase as a Marker for Aggressive GI Malignancy
Authors: Ramya Vasireddy, Joseph Atarere, Jeffrey Iding, Abhinav Sankineni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Klatskin tumor is a rare and aggressive biliary malignancy with a poor prognosis. We present a 72-year-old male with a history of COPD and severe non-ischemic cardiomyopathy who presented with jaundice, pruritus, and fatigue. Laboratory findings revealed marked hyperbilirubinemia and elevated liver enzymes, along with significantly increased creatine kinase (CK). Imaging studies and ERCP confirmed the diagnosis of perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, Bismuth class III, with adenocarcinoma pathology. Despite palliative stent placement, the patient’s bilirubin levels remained elevated, and CK peaked at 45,000 units/L. Due to his advanced disease and comorbidities, he was not a candidate for surgical or systemic therapy and was transitioned to home hospice. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in managing Klatskin tumors and the importance of recognizing significant elevation in CK, which in the absence of signs of muscle breakdown/pain may hypothetically correlate with aggressive forms of gastrointestinal malignancies.

Introduction

Perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, popularly known as Klatskin tumor, is an uncommon, aggressive subtype of cholangiocarcinoma with a poor prognosis. 1 The disease gets its name from Dr. Gerald Klatskin, a renowned hepatologist who first described this rare tumor in 1965.2 The annual incidence of Klatskin tumor in North America is 7000 cases even though it makes up around 50 % of all biliary duct tumors with an associated high mortality rate.3,4 The incidence of cholangiocarcinoma has been increasing globally but despite the increased incidence, making an early diagnosis and treatment has been challenging.5 Patients frequently present with symptoms of biliary obstruction, including jaundice, pruritus, dark urine, pale stools, and fatigue.

In this case, we describe a 72-year-old male with a complex medical history who presented with jaundice, fatigue, and other signs of cholestasis. Investigations revealed significant abnormalities in both cholestatic and hepatocellular liver enzyme patterns, along with elevated creatine kinase levels, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of cholangiocarcinoma This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in managing cholangiocarcinoma, and the importance of creatine kinase (CK) in gastrointestinal malignancies.

Case report

A 72-year-old man with a past medical history of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction presented with fatigue of one month duration. He noticed yellowing of skin with generalized itching, pale stools, and dark urine without associated abdominal pain or muscle pain. On presentation, he was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Labs were significant for total bilirubin 17.7 mg/dL (Normal reference range:0.1–1.2 mg/dL), AST 352 units/L (Normal reference range: 8–33 U/L), ALT 365 units/L (Normal reference range: 7–48 U/L), alkaline phosphatase 977 units/L (Normal reference range: 40–128 U/L), lipase 195 units/L (Normal reference range: 12–53), creatinine 6.73 mg/dL, creatine kinase 3621 units/L (Normal reference range: 32–182) and CK-MB/CK ratio was 1.4. The patient was not on any medications that could account for the elevated creatine kinase. Additional workup, including urinalysis and urine myoglobin levels were not consistent with rhabdomyolysis. Our patient did not have the classic triad of muscle pain, muscle weakness and tea-colored urine. The elevated creatinine was deemed to be likely related to hepatorenal syndrome. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed normal sinus rhythm and troponin was negative. Viral panel for both acute and chronic hepatitis was negative.

Subsequently, non-contrast abdominal CT showed mild to moderate intrahepatic biliary dilatation suggestive of central intrahepatic biliary obstruction concerning for cholangiocarcinoma (Fig. 1). MRCP showed biliary obstructive process at the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts likely secondary to malignant neoplasm (cholangiocarcinoma) (Fig. 2). Gastroenterology performed an Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) that showed a single localized 10 mm stenosis in the hepatic duct bifurcation consistent with Bismuth class III. The left and right hepatic ducts and all intrahepatic branches were diffusely dilated. A sphincterotomy was performed and temporary biliary stents were placed into the right hepatic and common hepatic duct. Pathology revealed foci of adenocarcinoma and angiolymphatic invasion confirming malignancy (Fig. 3).

During the course of hospitalization, CK progressively elevated even prior to an ERCP to around 34,000 units/L, peaked at 45,000 units/L and subsequently trended down to around 6000 units/L towards the end of the hospital stay. Bilirubin was elevated as high as 33 mg/dL and failed to improve despite stent placement. The Bismuth classification helps with guidance for surgical methods and a class III would necessitate a hemi-liver resection along with resection of caudate lobe. Given the extent of his disease, the patient was deemed not a candidate for surgical resection or systemic therapy. In light of limited treatment options and poor prognosis, he ultimately opted for comfort-focused care and transitioned to home hospice.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexities involved in managing Klatskin tumor. Our patient presented with classical symptoms of cholestasis, including jaundice, pruritus, dark urine, and pale stools. Initial laboratory workup revealed significantly elevated liver function tests, total bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase, raising concern for biliary obstruction. Creatine was also markedly elevated, which could be attributed to acute kidney injury in the setting of cholestasis or underlying chronic kidney disease.

Notably, the patient had a persistently and significantly elevated CK level, which peaked at 45,000 units/L during hospitalization. CK is an intracellular enzyme which is normally present in skeletal muscle, myocardium and the brain which is not seen in normal hepatocytes. It is typically a marker of muscle injury and is often associated with conditions such as rhabdomyolysis. However, in this case, the patient had no muscle pain, weakness, or other clinical signs of rhabdomyolysis, making this an unlikely cause of the elevated CK. Other potential causes of CK elevation, such as myocardial infarction, were not evident, and the patient’s ECG and troponin levels were unremarkable. Furthermore, CK elevation has a wide differential diagnosis including inflammatory myopathy, drug/toxin exposure, trauma, acute and chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, muscular dystrophy, neuropsychiatric disorder, malignancy or idiopathic.6 Our patient had acute kidney injury but prior studies have not shown elevation beyond 10,000 units/L in patients with kidney disease and no rhabdomyolysis7

Recent literature suggests that elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels may occur in patients with advanced malignancies, even in the absence of rhabdomyolysis. This finding has been noted particularly in aggressive gastrointestinal cancers. Elevated CK has been reported in association with cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Several case reports and small studies support this association, though the underlying mechanism remains unclear. The mechanism behind CK elevation in malignancy remains unclear, but proposed theories include tumorinduced muscle damage, direct invasion of muscle tissue, or tumor lysis. Elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels may represent a paraneoplastic phenomenon in the setting of advanced malignancy. Although this patient did not exhibit classical features of a paraneoplastic syndrome, the unexplained CK elevation raises the possibility of an atypical or subclinical presentation. The prognosis of these tumors currently is based on TNM staging with metastatic disease having the worst prognosis. Surgical resectability is based on Bismuth classification and that can have a profound impact on prognosis as well. Recognizing CK elevation as a potential marker of aggressive disease or paraneoplastic activity may have prognostic value and could prompt closer surveillance or earlier palliative care discussions in patients with Klatskin tumor. The median CK elevation in gastrointestinal malignancies is noted to be around 75 units/L. Further research is needed to clarify significance of CK in gastrointestinal cancers, including cholangiocarcinoma8–10

In a review of the literature, studies have reported CK elevation and increased CK-MB/CK ratio to be associated with some gastrointestinal malignancies and poor prognosis. A case report by Meffert, G. Et al. described a patient with hepatocellular carcinoma with elevated CK level, especially with the mitochondrial isoenzyme.8 Additionally, two studies have highlighted the association of elevated CK-MB/CK ratio with malignancy and likelihood of metastasis and also to help differentiate benign pancreatic masses from pancreatic adenocarcinoma.9,10 Papalazarou V et al., proposed that the phosphagen system and Creatine kinase B-type (CK-BB) facilitate invasion and migration of pancreatic cancer cells. They noted that small interfering RNA-mediated CK-BB silencing delayed invasion by pancreatic cancer cells. They further hypothesized that CK-BB is over-expressed during Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma progression and supports metastatic dissemination by dynamic protrusion and migration of cancer cells that have a migratory phenotype in association with a rigid, highly adhesive matrix.11 Nakamura et al., reported elevation of CK-MM in a patient with Hepatocellular carcinoma without muscle pain and that the levels declined gradually after surgical resection suggesting an HCC related etiology that was not a paraneoplastic inflammatory myositis syndrome.12

In our patient, the advanced stage of his cholangiocarcinoma (Bismuth Class III) with the finding of elevated CK level is being hypothesized as a sign/marker for aggressive nature of the disease. Despite placement of biliary stents, his bilirubin levels continued to rise, reaching as high as 33 mg/dL, reflecting ongoing cholestasis and the limited palliative benefit of stenting in the context of such advanced disease. Given the patient’s poor functional status, significant comorbidities, and the advanced stage of his cancer, he was not a candidate for surgical resection or systemic therapy. Ultimately, the decision was made to transition to home hospice care, focusing on symptom management and quality of life.

Conclusion

This case of Klatskin tumor with marked CK elevation in the absence of rhabdomyolysis presents a unique challenge in diagnosis and management. This case underscores the importance of recognizing atypical presentations and biochemical abnormalities in patients with Klatskin tumors. Elevated CK levels in the setting of malignancy, while uncommon, may be a marker of aggressive disease and poor prognosis, particularly when more common causes such as rhabdomyolysis have been excluded. At times, it is difficult to locate the primary site of origin of tumor especially amongst the gastrointestinal malignancies and hence CK level along with CK-MB/CK ratio will act as an adjunct in diagnosis and management. Further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying CK elevation in malignancy and its potential role as a prognostic marker.
