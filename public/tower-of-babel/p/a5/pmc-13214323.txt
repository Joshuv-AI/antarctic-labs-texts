Revisiting the death of Louis XIV: a nephrological perspective
Authors: Stanislas Bataille, Philippe Charlier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLouis XIV died in 1715 at the age of 76 years. His death was attributed by contemporaneous accounts to dry gangrene secondary to peripheral arterial disease. Several clinical features described in these accounts may also be interpreted through alternative modern nephrological frameworks, including chronic kidney disease and calciphylaxis.MethodsWe performed a critical reappraisal of contemporaneous medical and lay descriptions of King’s terminal illness, integrating these sources with contemporary knowledge of chronic kidney disease and calciphylaxis. The work followed a hypothesis-driven approach, explicitly acknowledging the inherent limitations of retrospective diagnosis in historical medicine.ResultsHistorical descriptions report progressive, painful ischemic lesions of the lower limb evolving toward necrosis and death from sepsis. Louis XIV presented several conditions currently recognized as calciphylaxis risk factors, including obesity, diabetes mellitus, and possibly chronic kidney disease. When examined through a modern nephrological perspective, features such as a 10-day delay between pain onset and lesion appearance, lesion localization, pain severity, and the absence of traumatic origin are compatible with calciphylaxis.ConclusionBased on a critical reinterpretation of contemporaneous accounts and modern pathophysiological knowledge, this work explores the possibility that Louis XIV’s terminal illness is compatible with chronic kidney disease and calciphylaxis. Although retrospective confirmation is impossible, calciphylaxis remains a plausible hypothesis, highlighting the potential under-recognition of chronic kidney disease and its complications in historical cases.

Introduction

Identifying the causes of death of historical figures is sometimes challenging. In some cases, symptoms and signs are pathognomonic of a precise diagnosis, but in others, the disease ultimately responsible for death remains uncertain or has been incorrectly attributed in light of contemporary medical knowledge [1, 2].

Deaths related to chronic kidney diseases (CKD) have historically been underreported, largely because signs and symptoms of CKD are non-specific and because the conceptual framework of CKD did not emerge until the twentieth century, following the development of urea and creatinine assays [3, 4].

Historical causes of death can be revisited and discussed in the light of advances in medical and scientific knowledge, either through new analyses of bodies or organs, or by reinterpreting the descriptions of signs and symptoms reported by contemporaries [5, 6]. In this context, the retrospective medical diagnosis should be approached as a hypothesis-generating exercise rather than an attempt to establish certainty.

Louis XIV (1638–1715), the “Sun King”, died at the age of 76 years at Versailles on September 1, 1715 after a reign of 72 years. Contemporary accounts attributed his death to gangrene secondary to peripheral arterial disease [7]. Witnesses and physicians described a progressive necrotic lesion of his left limb that led to death within a few weeks. More recently, analyses of the remnants of his heart suggested a fatal fungal infection involving Cyphellophora europea, a fungus known to cause skin infections, supporting the hypothesis of a secondary infection from the cutaneous lesion [5]. Comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and obesity have also been discussed by historians.

In the present work, we explore how modern nephrological knowledge may inform the interpretation of historical descriptions of Louis XIV’s terminal illness. Rather than attempting to establish a definitive retrospective diagnosis, we examine whether calciphylaxis represents a plausible explanatory framework alongside more classical vascular interpretations, while explicitly acknowledging the methodological limits of such an approach.

Methods

The body of Louis XIV was exhumed and lost during the French revolution in 1793. Only his embalmed heart remains preserved in the crypt of the Saint-Denis Basilica, north of Paris [5]. Thus, our analysis is based on a critical reinterpretation of contemporaneous eyewitness accounts describing the King’s terminal illness.

Historical sources were selected according to the following criteria: (i) contemporaneity with the events of 1715; (ii) direct observation of the King during his final illness; and (iii) independence of testimony when possible.

Louis XIV’s physicians Antoine Vallot, Antoine d’Aquin, and Guy-Crescent Fagon wrote a detailed Journal de la santé de Louis XIV (“Health diary of Louis XIV”) from 1647 to 1711, preserved at the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF) [8]. Although the chronicle ends four years before the King’s death, it provides essential information regarding his past medical history.

Guy-Crescent Fagon, the King’s Premier Médecin (chief physician), constantly supervised the royal health from 1693 to the fatal illness and reportedly slept in the King’s chamber during critical episodes. However, Fagon, advanced in age and increasingly infirm, erroneously initially attributed the King’s left leg pain to sciatica. It took him several days to acknowledge his error and accept the diagnosis of gangrene [9, 10].

Louis XIV wished his agony to be public, telling “I have lived among my court; I wish to die among them. They have followed me through all the stages of my life; it is only right that they should see me end it” [10]. As a result, several witnesses recorded and precisely describe their observations of the King’s condition.

Two brothers and royal servants, Jean and François Anthoine, provided the most detailed daily account of the King’s symptoms. The two brothers - who attended to Louis XIV’s most intimate needs at the castles of Marly and Versailles - carefully recorded each of the 23 days of the King’s illness, from the first symptoms to his final breath [9]. Their proximity ensured remarkable observational accuracy, though their narrative was entirely non-medical. Their journal, published in 1880 and accessible at the BnF, remains one of the most valuable lay accounts of the royal death [9].

Louis de Rouvroy, duke of Saint-Simon, peer of France and the King’s godson, also witnessed the final days of Louis XIV. In his Mémoires, he described not only the medical progression but also the ceremonial and spiritual dimensions of the royal death. He famously characterized the scene as “the most moving and admirable spectacle” [10].

Another contemporary courtesan observer, Philippe de Courcillon, Marquis de Dangeau, chronicled the King’s health on a daily basis in his Journal de Dangeau, providing complementary medical and courtly details [7].

Finally, a postmortem examination (autopsy) was performed at Versailles on 2 September 1715, the day after the King’s death, following the royal post-mortem tradition. It was conducted by Dr. Georges Maréchal, the King’s chief surgeon, under the supervision of Dr. Fagon, and in the presence of Jean-Baptiste Doye, Dean of the Faculty of Medicine, and the Faculty’s Regent [11].

This study is based on a critical analysis of contemporaneous medical reports, eyewitness accounts, and autopsy descriptions. Clinical features described in historical documents were systematically reinterpreted in light of current medical knowledge, with particular attention to internal consistency, temporal evolution of symptoms, and compatibility with modern diagnostic frameworks. Given the retrospective nature of this analysis, all interpretations are considered hypothesis-generating and subject to uncertainty.

Results and discussion

Past medical history preceding the last days of the king

Louis XIV’s medical history included: scabies, smallpox, gonorrhea, typhoid fever, dysentery and recurrent indigestion, measles, a surgically treated anal fistula, numerous episodes of gout, tooth loss with the development of a naso-palatal fistula, and a cervical carbuncle [8, 12]. The King also suffered from recurrent “vapors” (a term then referring to neurovegetative or psychosomatic episodes) and recurrent malarial fever episodes.

Two additional chronic conditions merit particular discussion: diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Both could explain the King’s progressive deterioration during his final year of life, as noted by Saint-Simon: “For more than a year, the King’s health had been in decline.” [10].

Louis XIV was very likely affected by diabetes mellitus [12]. He had several risk factors of type 2 diabetes: overweight status, age over 45 years, arterial hypertension, and possibly a family history of metabolic disease [12] (Table 1). The King suffered from extensive tooth loss, delayed wound healing following surgery for an anal fistula, and a recurrent carbuncle on the neck, which all might be complications of chronic hyperglycemia. Most strikingly, he exhibited the cardinal symptom of unquenchable thirst. The King’s urine was examined daily according to the Journal de la santé du Roi, but unfortunately the manuscript provides far more details on bowel movements and bloodlettings than on urinary findings. The sweet-tase of urine as a hallmark of diabetes mellitus (mellitus literally meaning sweet as honey) was first described by Willis in England in 1674 [13], and it remains uncertain whether the royal physicians were familiar with this observation. Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

As the first urea and creatinine assays were available in the 20th century, CKD as a diagnosis of Louis XIV could not be described by his contemporaries [3, 4].

Louis XIV nevertheless presented several risk factors of CKD (Table 1), including probable hypertension, likely type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, and a diet rich in animal protein. His frequent gout attacks also indicate hyperuricemia, often associated with renal impairment. According to the KDIGO (Kidney Disease – Improving Global Outcomes) 2024 clinical practice guideline for the evaluation and management of chronic kidney disease, Louis XIV would warrant systematic CKD screening [14]. To note, although it is not known how long the king had diabetes, even with anti-diabetic treatment, nowadays, up to 40% of diabetic patients develop CKD after 10 years [15, 16].

The autopsy described the kidneys as being “in their natural state”, with a small non-obstructive calyceal stone. This report, which describes kidneys that were macroscopically unremarkable does not formally exclude CKD. Firstly, the term ‘autopsy’ should be interpreted with caution here, as postmortem examination of organs at that time was limited to only a macroscopic and gross inspection (mainly signs of poisoning were screened at this stage). Secondly, in diabetic nephropathy kidney size and macroscopic morphology are preserved in most cases whereas in most other non-cystic nephropathies, kidneys are usually shrunken and lobulated, irregular. In a systematic autopsy series of 140 cases, 26 of the 43 patients (60%) with a histology-proven nephropathy had no suspicion of nephropathy at initial autopsy evaluation, and in these cases, the most frequent diagnosis was diabetes [17]. Taken together, these data indicate that the absence of macroscopic renal abnormality at Louis XIV’s autopsy—conducted without histological examination, by physicians unaware of the concept of diabetic kidney disease—cannot be interpreted as evidence against chronic kidney disease of diabetic origin.

Several clinical features described during the final months are compatible with uremic manifestations, including recurrent lower limb edema, recurrent gout episodes, valvular calcifications of the heart, progressive fatigue and anorexia, and daily nausea during the last month of life [9, 10]. Historians confirmed this decline: “The King diminished considerably during the latter part of the journey to Marly” [10]; “For more than two months the King’s health had been failing” [7].

Taken together, these findings suggest that Louis XIV might have suffered from chronic renal impairment, possibly exacerbated during his final illness, although this remains unproven.

The “official” diagnosis: dry gangrene

From a medical standpoint, dry gangrene refers to localized tissue necrosis resulting from a critical impairment of blood supply. It represents the terminal evolution of peripheral arterial disease and results from severe arterial occlusion or advanced atherosclerotic disease of the lower extremities, leading to absent distal pulses, claudication, and non-healing ulcers [18]. In the case of Louis XIV, physicians diagnosed dry gangrene as the cause of his death based on the clinical appearance of his lesions as described by contemporaries, i.e. the dark pigmentation of the altered tissues [5, 11].

By contrast, wet gangrene, typically caused by anaerobic infection with Clostridium perfringens, is characterized by rapidly progressive myonecrosis, edema, gas formation, and severe systemic toxicity, and is therefore inconsistent with the slow, ischemic, and initially non-infectious clinical presentation described in Louis XIV [5].

Calciphylaxis

Calciphylaxis is a rare but severe vascular disorder defined by calcification and thrombosis of dermal and subcutaneous arterioles, leading to tissue ischemia and cutaneous necrosis. It is primarily a clinical diagnosis, although it remains a challenging condition to recognize, particularly for clinicians less familiar with advanced CKD and its complications. While well recognized in nephrology, it may be less familiar to non-specialists due to its rarity and its association with specific patient populations [17].

Initial lesions are deep and non-visible, producing intense stabbing pain that may precede any cutaneous change by days to months, often mimicking neuropathic or musculoskeletal pain and causing diagnostic confusion [19, 20]. As the disease progresses, indurated, painful, livedoid nodules appear and evolve into ulcerative and necrotic plaques, typically affecting adipose-rich areas such as the thighs, abdomen, and lower limbs. In advanced stages, widespread necrosis and secondary infection lead to sepsis and cachexia, which are the principal causes of death.

Major predisposing factors include chronic kidney disease, poorly controlled diabetes, obesity, hypoalbuminemia, and disorders of calcium–phosphate metabolism, such as secondary hyperparathyroidism and vascular calcifications. The disease carries a high mortality rate, often exceeding 50%. Although most cases occur in end-stage renal disease, non-uremic forms have been well documented in patients with preserved renal and parathyroid function [19–22]. Thus, even if Louis XIV had only mild CKD, the calciphylaxis hypothesis remains valid and plausible.

At the time of Louis XIV, the concept of calciphylaxis was unknown. The clinical picture of progressive lower-limb necrosis was therefore interpreted through the only known diagnosis: gangrene. This misinterpretation persisted in historical accounts. Even today, calciphylaxis remains a specialized diagnosis, largely confined to nephrology, and unfamiliar to most historians and physicians outside this field. However, several clinical and topographical features described in 1715 are not typical of ischemic gangrene and appear more consistent with calciphylaxis: the lack of previous intermittent claudication, the initial localization of the lesion at the knee level, a delay of several days delay between the beginning of pain and the lesion appearance, the description of Koebnerization phenomenon (i.e., the development or worsening of skin lesions at sites of trauma, surgery or pressure) (Table 1).

Risk factors of calciphylaxis

As previously described, Louis XIV, presented several recognized risk factors for calciphylaxis: obesity, diabetes mellitus, arterial disease, hypertension, and possibly CKD (Table 1).

Initial symptoms at the castle of Marly (from August 10 to 13, 1715)

Historical reports agree that the King’s fatal illness began on August 10, 1715 at Marly, when he was reportedly “struck by a weakness of the stomach, of which he had already experienced several previous episodes” [9]. However, the connection between a simple indigestion and the subsequent development of gangrene or calciphylaxis is unclear. Louis XIV had a long history of similar digestive disturbances, raising the question of why this particular episode was perceived by contemporaries as the onset of his final illness (one has to take into account the fact that his father, the King Louis XIII, died of intestinal illness caused by Crohn disease and/or enteric tuberculosis). Was it truly more severe, or simply the moment when the King decided to return to Versailles, marking the beginning of his last act?

The Anthoine brothers next described a “profound disgust” for food—possibly reflecting nausea secondary to dyspepsia or perhaps uremic symptoms—and an “unquenchable thirst”, likely due to worsening diabetic imbalance. From that date, the King appeared asthenic, with fatigue worsening progressively until death. Dangeau noted on August 10 that “he was so weak and exhausted that he could hardly move from his cabinet to his prie-dieu.”.

No additional symptoms were reported before August 13, the date on which the first lower-limb manifestations were observed, either by Saint-Simon, the Anthoine brothers, or Dangeau. It is therefore entirely possible that the digestive and constitutional symptoms observed between August 10 and 13 were unrelated to the vascular and cutaneous process that ultimately led to the King’s death.

Pain and lesions of the left leg

Calciphylaxis is primarily a clinical diagnosis and histological confirmation is rarely needed, which was of course not performed in the 18th century. Our analysis must rely entirely on contemporaneous descriptions of symptoms and lesions. Two elements from the historical accounts are particularly informative for differential diagnosis: temporal dissociation and topographical distribution of lesions. A third element is the absence of previous intermittent claudication of the lower limb.

According to the Anthoine brothers, the King’s illness began on August 13, 1715, when “His Majesty felt a violent pain in the left leg and could scarcely put weight on it.” that Fagon attributed to “sciatica of the leg and thigh”. There was no visible cutaneous change until August 24, when Saint-Simon wrote: “His leg was examined, and black marks appeared.” The Anthoine brothers likewise recorded that “Fagon and all the physicians noted a dark discoloration below the garter line.” Dangeau confirmed: “There are black marks on his leg that make us fear gangrene.”. The 10–12-days interval between pain and necrosis is atypical for ischemic gangrene, in which skin changes appear within minutes or hours of arterial occlusion. Conversely, such a delay is typical of calciphylaxis, where deep dermal ischemia precedes secondary cutaneous necrosis by days or weeks [20].

The location of the initial lesion is also atypical for arterial gangrene. The Anthoine brothers described a dark patch “below the garter line,” and Dangeau noted it appeared “beneath the mark left by the King’s habitual garter.”. Since the garter encircles the upper calf just below the knee, this implies that the first lesion occurred in a proximal rather than distal region of the limb. Peripheral arterial disease lesions preferentially begin in the distal extremities—toes, feet, or heel—where blood flow is diminished [18, 23]. The proximal onset observed here, with extension toward the thigh, is far more consistent with calciphylaxis, which typically involves adipose-rich areas such as the thighs and upper legs [20].

The subsequent evolution of the lesions is variably described. The Anthoine brothers noted an apparent full-leg discoloration as early as August 24, but later (August 29) reported that surgeon Maréchal was “surprised to find the leg mortified up to the knee.” Dangeau likewise mentioned progression to the foot and thigh by the same date: “The gangrene is throughout the foot; it has reached the knee, and the thigh is swollen.”. Autopsy reports, which claimed “gangrene from the foot to the upper body”, are likely exaggerated. Some of the reported thoracoabdominal lesions could nevertheless correspond to calciphylaxis plaques rather than postmortem decomposition.

A further observation by Dangeau argues against ischemic gangrene: “His leg pains him much less when he is in bed.” In arterial occlusion, pain typically worsens when the leg is elevated and improves in a dependent position due to gravity-assisted perfusion—the opposite of what was described [7]. Importantly, Louis XIV never complained of intermittent claudication or pain of the leg before August 1715 and had no sign of peripheral arterial disease.

Finally, the Anthoine brothers provided a striking description suggestive of Koebnerization. Following Maréchal’s deep scarifications of necrotic tissue on August 27, the lesion worsened rapidly: “They found it even more gangrenous than before.”. This phenomenon, in which trauma aggravates necrosis, is well recognized in calciphylaxis and has prompted caution in performing diagnostic biopsies [24]. In addition, Maréchal reported that the lesion extended toward the bone during scarification, suggesting involvement of deeper structures, which may be consistent with the known progression of calciphylaxis.

None of these features constitutes definitive proof; however, taken together, they support a pathophysiological pattern more consistent with calciphylaxis than with simple ischemic and/or infectious gangrene.

Etiology of the King’s death

Louis XIV most likely died from sepsis, which represents the fatal evolution of both gangrene and calciphylaxis, through secondary infection of necrotic lesions, severe malnutrition leading to immunosuppression, and ultimately septic shock. Recent molecular analyses performed on the King’s heart revealed peptides of Cyphellophora europaea as well as peptides associated with severe human sepsis, suggesting a fungal septicemia as the immediate cause of death [5]. Diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease are risk factors of fungal infection [25]. Consequently, while the terminal septic mechanism of death is clear, it provides no discriminating evidence favoring one etiological diagnosis over the other.

Conclusion

Based on a critical reinterpretation of contemporaneous accounts and modern pathophysiological knowledge, this work explores how chronic kidney disease–related vascular disorders, including calciphylaxis, may offer alternative interpretative frameworks for historical clinical descriptions. While dry ischemic gangrene remains a plausible explanation, several temporal and anatomical features reported in 1715 are atypical for peripheral arterial disease alone. Although retrospective confirmation is impossible, this analysis highlights the potential underestimation of CKD and its complications, including calciphylaxis, in historical cases.
