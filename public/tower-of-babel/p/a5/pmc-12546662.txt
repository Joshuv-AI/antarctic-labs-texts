Tumor recurrence in the inferior vena cava following radical nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma: a case report and review
Authors: Ricky Cohen, Hector Gonzalez, James Walsh, Christopher Kelley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal cell carcinoma is a relatively common malignancy in the United States with an estimated incidence of ∼80,000 cases, many of which are found incidentally. Renal cell carcinoma tends to invade vascular structures which can predispose to a host of complications. Although recurrence is common, isolated tumor thrombus recurrence is rare and surgically complex. We report the case of a 70-year-old male with a history of renal cell carcinoma status post nephrectomy 20 years ago who presented with generalized weakness and falls. Imaging revealed an inferior vena cava thrombus and intraluminal biopsy confirmed clear cell renal cell carcinoma. Frequent surveillance is critical for early detection and management of recurrent disease. This case highlights the importance of considering late vascular recurrence even decades after initial treatment.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, IVC thrombus, Recurrent disease

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma [RCC] accounts for ∼2% of all cancers worldwide and is the ninth most common cancer in the United States [1,2]. Most tumors are found incidentally on abdominal imaging and therefore patients do not always present with the typical symptoms of a palpable abdominal mass, flank pain and hematuria [1,2]. With its proclivity for vascular invasion, the renal veins and inferior vena cava [IVC] are a particular point of concern especially for the evaluating radiologist [3]. Local disease without invasion portends a favorable prognosis, however metastatic disease is more often difficult to treat [2]. Treatment of RCC depends on the extent of disease however it focuses on partial or radical nephrectomy, ablation, systemic therapy and active surveillance [2,4]. Surveillance includes serial biochemical tests and imaging at different intervals depending on the specific genotype. While recurrent disease may be seen in as many as 25% of cases, the reported incidence of local recurrence is just 1.6%, and its presence yields surgical complexity due to vascular involvement [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. We present an uncommon case of isolated recurrent tumor thrombus of the IVC 20 years after a right radical nephrectomy for RCC.

Case report

A 70-year-old man with a past medical history of RCC status post right radical nephrectomy 20 years prior, chronic kidney disease [CKD], coronary artery disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus and heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction presented to the emergency department complaining of generalized weakness and frequent falls at home. Initial labs revealed mild hyponatremia at 131 mmol/L (Normal: 135-145), elevated BUN at 51 mg/dL (Normal: 8-23), elevated creatinine at 5.8 mg/dL (Normal: 0.7-1.2), mildly elevated AST and ALT at 127 and 55 U/L (Normal: 10-50 and 0-41), elevated calcium at 11.8 mg/dL (Normal: 8.8-10.2) and leukocytosis at 16.1 K/uL (Normal: 4.5-10.9). Infectious workup was unremarkable.

A Computed Tomography [CT] of the chest, abdomen and pelvis was performed which revealed innumerable perilymphatic micronodules in the lungs, hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, right nephrectomy/adrenalectomy changes and a hypoattenuating mass-like expansile lesion in the suprarenal IVC (Fig. 1). Concern was raised for recurrent RCC as imaging revealed a large tumor thrombus measuring up to 4.5 × 3.5 × 4.9 cm (Fig. 1).

(A) Axial contrast enhanced CT of the abdomen/pelvis at the level of the left kidney reveals a heterogeneously attenuating expansile mass with the lumen of the inferior vena cava (white arrow). Also seen are surgical sutures at the origin of what would be the right renal vein in keeping with known history of right radical nephrectomy/adrenalectomy. A normal left kidney and adrenal gland are noted. (B) Axial contrast enhanced CT of the abdomen/pelvis at the level just proximal to the aortic bifurcation reveals a low attenuating filling defect in the inferior vena cava suspicious for extension of thrombus (white arrow) and (C) Coronal contrast enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis reveals extension of the inferior vena cava filling defect inferiorly into the left common iliac vein (straight white arrow). Additionally noted is lack of enhancement of the left common femoral vein indicating further extension of thrombus (curved white arrow).

Interventional radiology was consulted and performed an intraluminal biopsy of the intracaval mass (Fig. 2). The biopsy specimens obtained were sent to pathology. Histology revealed cells with neoplastic epithelial proliferation that were positive for PAX-8 and carbonic anhydrase, suggesting a diagnosis of clear cell renal cell carcinoma. The patient further underwent PET/CT which demonstrated increased metabolic activity within the expansile mass of the IVC, as well as scattered increased metabolic activity in the lungs and hila suggestive of metastases (Fig. 3).

Inferior vena cava venogram via a right femoral approach reveals a large filling defect corresponding to the expansile mass seen on CT (white arrow). Intra-caval biopsy was obtained.

(A) FDG PET/CT at the level of the left kidney reveals increased metabolic activity in the expansile mass within the inferior vena cava (straight white arrow). Also noted is physiologic activity in the left kidney and visualized bowel (curved white arrows). (B) Contrast enhanced CT of the chest at the level of the right middle lobe bronchus reveals an enlarged right hilar lymph node (straight red arrow) as well as diffuse peribronchovascular nodules (curved red arrows) and (C) FDG PET/CT at the level of the right middle lobe bronchus reveals increased metabolic activity within the right hilar lymph node (straight red arrow) and scattered throughout the airways suggestive of metastases (curved red arrows).

The patient was scheduled for IVC tumor resection and veno-venous bypass. Due to the extent of disease, IVC reconstruction was ultimately required using polytetrafluoroethylene [PTFE] grafting. Follow up imaging revealed a patent graft complicated by adjacent abscess formation. Percutaneous drainage by Interventional Radiology was performed which helped control the source of infection. The patient was eventually medically stable and was discharged to a subacute rehab facility with plans to follow with surgery in an outpatient clinic.

Discussion

Renal cell carcinoma arises from the epithelial cells of the renal cortex [1]. There are over 20 different subtypes of RCC, of which the clear cell subtype is the most common [1]. The current known risk factors for developing RCC include hypertension, smoking history, chronic kidney disease, and environmental exposure to heavy metals [1]. Tumor growth is promoted by inactivation of the von Hippel Lindau [VHL] tumor suppressor gene [1]. RCC has an incidence of ∼400,000 cases worldwide and is more prevalent in men than women, 2.3% and 1.3% respectively [1]. In 2018, RCC accounted for 1.8% of all cancer deaths across the world [1,2].

Most tumors are found incidentally upon abdominal imaging, thus explaining why most patients do not present with the typical triad of flank pain, hematuria and a palpable abdominal mass [1,2]. Imaging the kidney with CT or MRI would usually demonstrate a solid or cystic mass with heterogeneous features and early arterial phase enhancement [1]. Histopathology would demonstrate positive staining for paired box protein 8 [PAX-8] and commonly carbonic anhydrase IX which is a transmembrane protein specific for clear cell carcinoma as was seen in the present case [1]. RCC most commonly metastasizes to the lungs, followed by the lymph nodes, bone and liver in decreasing prevalence [1,7].

Treatment of RCC ultimately depends upon the initial presentation, size and stage of disease [1,2]. Generally, active surveillance may be utilized in cases of small renal masses [1]. Serial ultrasound or CT examinations may be obtained at 3 months, 6 months and then every 6 months until the 3 year mark [1]. Assuming stability, the mass may then be followed annually [1]. Other small renal masses (<3 cm) may be treated by multiple ablative techniques including cryotherapy and stereotactic radiofrequency ablation [SRFA] [1,4]. One meta-analysis assessed the 5 year outcomes of SRFA which demonstrated a local relapse rate of just 5% and no treatment related deaths or serious adverse effects [4].

Partial or radical nephrectomy will be considered for larger masses of local disease [1,3]. Radical nephrectomy [RN] involves resection of the affected kidney, perirenal fat and possibly the ipsilateral adrenal gland and regional lymph nodes [1,3]. Partial nephrectomy [PN] is a nephron-sparing approach involving tumor resection and preservation of the remaining nonaffected kidney [1,3]. In comparison to RN, PN is associated with a decreased risk of developing CKD in the future [1,3]. One study demonstrated that at a 3 year follow-up, 80% of PN patients maintained an estimated GFR <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 versus 35% for RN patients [3]. Systemic therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors and/or tyrosine kinase inhibitors may be used as a form of adjuvant therapy or in cases of advanced metastatic disease [1].

Controversy arises when attempting to assign an individual prognostic factor to the presence of IVC thrombus, typically at initial presentation [7,9]. However, it can be agreed upon that when IVC thrombus is present, distant metastatic disease usually coexists which itself has an unfavorable prognosis [7,9]. Additionally, the presence of IVC thrombus greatly increases the surgical complexity of the case [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. MRI is the gold standard for assessing the level of caval involvement, specifically focusing on its level of invasion into the caval wall [9]. Thrombectomy and partial IVC resection are the primary means of surgical care [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. One study demonstrated IVC resection with PTFE grafting due to the extent of caval involvement, which was ultimately the scenario in our patient’s case [9]. Another study reviewed cases of IVC invasion and noted a median survival rate of 5 months and surgical complication rates in up to 47% of patients depending on the grade of caval involvement – wall invasion, supradiaphragmatic extension, or extension beyond Gerota’s fascia are some determinants of tumor grade [1].

Most relapsed disease tends to occur within 5 years after nephrectomy [1]. When this does occur, isolated IVC involvement tends to be rare [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Upon extensive literature review, less than 30 cases have been reported thus far. In general, locally recurrent RCC bears a poor prognosis with a 5 year survival rate of only 28% according to 1 study [9]. Depending on the stage of disease and extent of involvement, this rate may vary. Because of this, frequent surveillance of patients with a history of RCC is of utmost importance. The exact frequency will depend on the specific genotype, some of which have a higher propensity for recurrence and warrant shorter durations between screenings [10]. Adherence to these protocols will lead to early detection and treatment of recurrent disease.

Conclusion

This case highlights the delayed recurrence of renal cell carcinoma manifesting as isolated tumor thrombus in the IVC 20 years after radical nephrectomy. While vascular invasion is a well established feature of RCC, isolated IVC involvement remains a rare entity that poses significant surgical challenges. Radiology plays a crucial role in identifying caval extent to guide appropriate therapy. Owing to the poor prognosis of recurrent disease, long term surveillance should be implemented for early detection and timely intervention. This will ultimately improve the overall outcome of this uncommon, but clinically significant presentation.
