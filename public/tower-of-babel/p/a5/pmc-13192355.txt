Taking multidisciplinary team care from policy into primary care practice
Authors: Shona Marie Bates, Deborah Saltman, Patricia M Davidson, Michael Wright
Published: 2026

This perspective proposes the concept of multidisciplinary practices as a dynamic rather than a single, fixed model of multidisciplinary teams in primary care. This approach will promote individualised evidence-based team or group care and allow simplified pathways of care for patients requiring differential diagnosis or one-off appointments. This would improve efficiency and effectiveness of primary care and ensure services respond to patient needs rather than under/over servicing their needs using fixed models of care.

Governments globally are placing increasing focus on primary care to respond to challenges addressing health needs in a context of increasing demand. Multidisciplinary teams are commonly promoted to address unmet need, chronic and complex care needs, and workforce shortages, and to maximise the potential of existing providers [1]. In Australia, the 2022 Strengthening Medicare Taskforce made four recommendations for primary care reform, including the introduction of multi-disciplinary teams (MDT) to provide quality person-centred continuity of care and deliver better health outcomes, with limited guidance about what this means in practice [2]. A review of 39 empirical studies shows the different drivers, configurations, applications and outcomes from MDTs in primary care globally [1]. While research on primary care MDTs has commenced in Australia, the learnings and the policy/practice response to those learnings will take years to eventuate (https://www.health.gov.au/news/mrff-22-million-for-research-to-improve-primary-care-in-australia?language=en).

Research about the use of MDTs in health care is not new [3, 4]. The team concept was first adopted from the motor industry in the middle of last century, where members worked in a structured environment, knew what boundaries existed, and who had final authority. The team leader set agreed standards of performance for willing participants who shared the vision and commitment to achieving shared goals [3]. In health care settings, variations of ‘teams’ exist. For example, a patient presenting to a hospital emergency department will experience team care operating within one set of established protocols led by a team leader. Alternatively, a patient with diabetes in the community will experience a different form of team care–group care–where they visit several different specialists (e.g. an endocrinologist, dietician, podiatrist, and ophthalmologist) coordinated by a nurse or general practitioner operating under individual protocols. The form of team care will vary by setting and needs.

MDT use is driven by financial or workforce constraints, or to improve outcomes for specific cohorts of patients; consequently, their form and their outcomes can vary [1, 4]. Models of MDT used elsewhere include providers working together to care for complex patients, and patients having the option to see a different profession depending on need and urgency [1]. There is appetite from primary care providers (PCPs) to work in MDTs, but recognition that the lack of flexibility in funding funding, embedded workforce models, and role ambiguity limits uptake [5]. The assumption that MDTs in primary care are efficient and effective has long gone unchallenged [1]. Yet, as a recent review highlights, they are not necessarily the panacea for improving primary care; MDTs can require ‘taskification’ and result in task transfer, creating fragmentation, the erosion of clinician-patient relationships, compromising continuity and coordination of care and challenging accountability for care [1, 6, 7].

The MDT literature reports some positive outcomes when working with cohorts with specific diseases, such as diabetes, where teams provided additional care [1]. Yet, given increasing multimorbidity, this could lead to further fragmentation. Further, many situations in general practice will not benefit from a team structure; for example, undifferentiated illness and complex multiple health problems with conflicting levels of seriousness and urgency. In such cases, members functioning in a group are likely to be more appropriate than members operating in a team structure [3, 8]. Connected groups (rather than teams) of different disciplines are more feasible where a team structure or joint protocols are difficult to enforce; for example, at the intersection between hospital and community settings. Groups are also be better for patients as they provide a space for distinct but complimentary services [3]. Patients also have different preferences and perceptions of who provides the best care for them–impacted by urgency of need, cultural needs, and prior experiences [1].

We propose, rather than a single fixed model of MDT, the concept of a multidisciplinary practice that provides flexibility in the use of different disciplines (single, complementary, group, and team configurations) to provide optimal care of the individual considering need and context. PCPs and consumers would determine the best care configurations to respond to the needs of individuals, families and communities that deliver high value and quality outcomes (within resource and workforce constraints). The choices would be based on evidence, clinician experience, disciplines available, and patient/family choice. The evidence base will continue to evolve as experience grows.

To support implementation, PCPs would then define work conditions, knowledge, skills, competencies, role clarity and training requirements, psychosocial resources and demands, while working within regulatory frameworks and scopes of practice, to support implementation. Rather than develop pilots, multidisciplinary practices could be provided with the evidence base to inform change, and a regulatory/funding context that fosters innovation and ensure continual improvements [9, 10].

Any changes to the way primary care is delivered would require significant funding reforms. Patient enrolment, another component of the recent Australian reforms, provides a mechanism to enable such change [2]. Change will require additional expenditure to reconfigure practices and pay for additional providers delivering more comprehensive care. Funding needs to account for the increasingly complex social, medical, behavioural, and clinical needs of patients, and the range of acute and chronic conditions patients present with.

Integrating disciplines within primary care practices will require research and agreement on several interrelating factors to provide a clear pathway for implementation. They include different configurations of practice, guiding principles and mechanisms, sustainable funding models, and change management. Ultimately, general practice is diverse and one model cannot fit all practices. Any reform must include flexibility in implementation and ongoing roll out, acknowledging different styles of practice, individual practice derived metrics, diversity of funding models tailor-made to the practice styles, different workforce and patient contexts, patient needs and of course accountability. Any reforms should also incorporate mechanisms for continual learning and improvement [1].

This paper presents a perspective for modernising primary care–tailoring primary care models to contemporary epidemiological trends—relevant to policy makers and primary care providers, incorporating multidisciplinarity into primary care reforms while recognising optimal implementation will differ by patient and practice. This is consistent with prior research that highlights that there is significant variation in the design and implementation of MDT-care elsewhere [1]. Taking such an approach could offer greater return on investment compared to a single fixed model of primary care where patients are likely to be under or over supported. Such an approach would require systems, organisational, professional and patient level change [1]. This would require a policy and funding environment that supports change without reducing current funding; for example, providing funding adjustment mechanisms for multidisciplinary team or group work based on expertise and depth of input, the development of protocols for team care, and controls around who can be involved in the patient’s care. Implementation would require flexibility while safeguarding patients and providers, and ongoing monitoring to ensure outcomes are achieved and expenditure is efficient.
