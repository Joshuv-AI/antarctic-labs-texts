Triple Therapy With Calcineurin Inhibition Enhances Remission in Childhood-Onset Lupus Nephritis
Authors: Kyle Ying-kit Lin, Xu-hui Zhong, Dong-yang Zhou, Andrea Angeletti, Luca Antonucci, Wei Bai, Liangjian Lu, Xiao-yu Liu, Stephen D Marks, Sanjukta Poddar, Rajiv Sinha, Sharon Teo, Marina Vivarelli, Hui-kim Yap, Eugene Yu-hin Chan
Published: 2026

Introduction

Triple therapy, adding calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) to corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), is a promising treatment in lupus nephritis (LN).1,2 CNIs inhibit interleukin-2 secretion and T-cell proliferation,S1 and lower proteinuria by stabilizing podocyte cytoskeleton.S2 In the Aurinia Renal Response in Active Lupus with Voclosporin (AURORA) study, add-on voclosporin improved adult LN remission.3 Nevertheless, it remains untested in pediatric patients, with uncertain long-term safety and limited global access.

Ciclosporin and tacrolimus are the main CNIs with extensive pediatric experience. In adults, tacrolimus-based triple therapy achieved a 73% remission rate at 24 months.4 Data pertaining to childhood LN (cLN) is scarce.5,6,S3 We conducted a multinational, multicentre retrospective cohort study at 7 pediatric nephrology centers in 5 countries to assess the efficacy and safety of ciclosporin- or tacrolimus-based triple therapy in cLN (Supplementary methods). Primary outcome was LN remission.7 Complete remission (CR) was urine protein to creatinine ratio < 0.5 mg/mg; partial remission (PR) was urine protein to creatinine ratio < 3 mg/mg with ≥ 50% reduction.7 Both required stable or improved kidney function.7

A full protocol is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Forty-eight children (31 females, 65%) were treated with triple therapy at 11.7-year-old (interquartile range [IQR]: 9.0–14.6) (Table 1). Two-thirds were East Asians (69%), followed by South Asians (23%). Forty-four patients underwent kidney biopsy: class III (10%), class IV (35%), class III/IV+V (27%), and class V (19%). All patients received corticosteroids for induction, with 73% given intravenous methylprednisolone. Forty (83%) and 8 (17%) patients received MMF and cyclophosphamide as initial induction. The median initial prednisolone dose was 1.0 mg/kg/day (IQR: 0.8–1.3). MMF dose was 1105 mg/m2 per day (IQR: 902–1306), and cyclophosphamide exposure was 1.2 g/m2 (IQR: 0.5–3).

The median time between initial induction and triple therapy commencement was 3 months (IQR: 2–5.5) (1–3 months, 46%; 3–6 months, 31%; > 6 months, 23%). Before triple therapy, 73% were in nonremission, and the remainder were in PR. Median baseline urine protein to creatinine ratio was 3 mg/mg (IQR: 1.7–4.7), and estimated glomerular filtration rate was 99 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (IQR: 86–124). No patient required dialysis. The observation period after triple therapy was 6.3 years (IQR: 3.7–12.8). Twenty-five patients received tacrolimus (0.07 mg/kg per day; IQR: 0.05–0.12). Therapeutic monitoring was performed in 92% subjects, with median trough levels of 5.8 μg/l (IQR: 5.1–7.0). Twenty-three patients received ciclosporin (3.3 mg/kg per day; IQR: 2.5–4.6). Drug levels were monitored in 65%, with median trough levels of 129 μg/l (IQR: 97–165). Treatment regimens are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Forty-three children (90%) achieved CR over a median of 5.3 months (IQR: 3.0–8.6) following triple therapy (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2; Figure 1a). All 13 patients with PR before triple therapy attained CR. Two additional patients in nonremission achieved PR at 4.9 and 8.1 months post-treatment. Three children did not respond; 2 discontinued CNIs and received rituximab, whereas 1 continued triple therapy for 30 months before stopping all immunosuppression.

CR rates were 33%, 56%, 79%, and 85% at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, respectively. PR rates were 10%, 15%, 9%, and 7% at these intervals. Significantly more children achieved CR/PR at 6 and 12 months than at baseline (P < 0.001; Figure 1c). Among those with CR, 24 (56%) and 28 (65%) patients achieved complete resolution of proteinuria at 6 and 12 months, respectively. Complement C3 and anti-dsDNA levels normalized in 89% and 75% children by 12 months. The estimated glomerular filtration rate remained stable throughout. Supplementary Figure S1 depicts trends in estimated glomerular filtration rate, proteinuria, and complement levels.

Triple therapy was given for 17 months (IQR: 9–29). Forty-four patients discontinued triple therapy because of CR (n = 31, 70%), nonadherence (n = 5, 11%), adverse events (n = 4, 9%), nonresponse (n = 2, 5%) and relapse (n = 2, 5%). After discontinuation, most children received prednisolone and MMF (n = 31/44, 70%) (Supplementary Table S1).

During triple therapy (89 patient-years), 3 children experienced 4 kidney relapses, with a relapse rate of 4.5 per 100 patient-years. After discontinuing CNI, 14 patients (29%) experienced 20 relapses in 310 patient-years (relapse rate 6.5 per 100 patient-years). At last follow-up, 5 developed CKD (1 Stage II and 4 Stage III), and 1 progressed to kidney failure. Most (88%) maintained normal kidney function (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2).

Eighteen adverse events associated with triple therapy occurred in 15 patients. Infection was the most common complication (n = 5, 10%). Four ciclosporin-treated patients developed herpes zoster, and 1 tacrolimus-treated patient had pyrexia of unknown origin. Two patients (1 per each CNI group) developed impaired kidney function attributable to CNI-associated nephrotoxicity. They did not undergo kidney biopsy, but their kidney function normalized after dose adjustment and/or CNI discontinuation. Two tacrolimus-treated subjects developed diabetes mellitus, necessitating CNI withdrawal in 1 case. Adverse events are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

We performed a sensitivity analysis on 26 patients who received triple therapy following 3 or more months of standard induction. The median time to initiate therapy was 5.3 months (IQR: 4.0–8.6). Of these, 24 (92%) achieved CR after a median of 4.1 months (IQR: 3.0–8.6) (Figure 1d). One achieved PR, and one remained in nonremission. Similar CR rates were observed at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months which were 39%, 58%, 76%, and 86% on Kaplan-Meier analysis, respectively. PR rates at the same intervals were 15%, 12%, 8%, and 5%. Further subgroup analyses showed that CR and relapse rates were comparable between tacrolimus- and ciclosporin-based regimens (Figure 1b; Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Results). All children with class V LN achieved CR and the CR rate was higher compared to those with proliferative LN, but the time to response was longer (Supplementary Results).

Discussion

Our cohort represented children with cLN who had persistent significant proteinuria after 1 to 3 months of standard induction. The disease was of moderate severity, since none presented with organ- or life-threatening disease, and their kidney function was normal. We found that 90% of children achieved CR with triple therapy over a median of 5.3 months, with remission rates continuing to improve over 6 to 12 months. Sensitivity analysis of patients starting triple therapy after 3 or more months of induction confirmed similar responses. These CR rates exceed the previously reported range of 44% to 78% following standard cLN induction at 12 to 24 months.8,9,S4 Concurrent serological remission was also observed in most patients. These findings suggest that triple therapy enhances LN remission in children with persistent proteinuria who do not exhibit organ- or life-threatening manifestations. The treatment effect was particularly pronounced in pure class V membranous LN, where all patients attained CR after triple therapy. Achieving remission is important as persistent proteinuria may indicate ongoing activity and contribute to progressive kidney damage. Notably, most participating centers used this therapeutic strategy as induction, and resumed dual immunosuppression with corticosteroids and MMF after LN remission.

Safety remains a key concern, particularly infection risk and CNI-associated nephrotoxicity. Our findings indicate that triple therapy is generally well-tolerated, although a retrospective design may underestimate adverse events. Nonetheless, only 5 patients developed infections, mostly herpes zoster successfully treated with acyclovir. Two patients developed kidney impairment presumably attributed to CNI use, who recovered after CNI adjustment.

In our study, we found that efficacy was comparable between ciclosporin- and tacrolimus-based regimens. Drug selection should consider drug availability, cost, and side effect profiles. In this context, cosmetic side effects of ciclosporin, such as hypertrichosis and gingival hypertrophy, may affect adherence in adolescents; however, ciclosporin is crucial for disease control and relapse prevention. Therapeutic drug monitoring is recommended to optimize treatment efficacy and minimize toxicities.S5 Although target MMF-area under curve and trough MMF levels for LN have been established, consensus on optimal CNIs dosing and target drug levels is lacking.7,S6

Our study provides real-world data from a large multiethnic pediatric cLN cohort treated with triple therapy, with extended observation. Limitations include retrospective study design with selection and reporting bias, intercentre protocol heterogeneity, and the lack of controls. Cyclophosphamide nonresponders were switched early to triple therapy, resulting in inadequate exposure. Observed improvements may partly reflect moderate disease severity and the natural course under immunosuppression, rather than effects attributable solely to triple therapy. Generalizability is limited by the predominance of East and South Asians, given the recognized ethnic differences.9 Finally, CNI nephrotoxicity is likely underreported, since protocol biopsy is uncommon in the pediatric population.S7

In conclusion, triple therapy using ciclosporin or tacrolimus can serve as an effective therapeutic approach for cLN with persistent proteinuria, for those patients who do not exhibit organ- or life-threatening manifestations.
