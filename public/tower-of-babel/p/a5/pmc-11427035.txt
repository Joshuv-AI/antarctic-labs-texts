Patient-reported Outcomes After Medial Meniscus Root Tear Repair Versus Other Medial Meniscus Repairs: An Exploratory Meta-analysis of Single-arm Cohorts
Authors: Jared E. Gurba, Nomi S. Weiss-Laxer, Mohammad N. Haider, John M. Marzo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:This study aimed to (1) compare patient-reported outcome measures between patients who underwent medial meniscus root tear (MMRT) repair and those who underwent other types of medial meniscus repair, and (2) identify factors associated with observed differences.Methods:A literature search identified studies reporting PROMs—knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score (KOOS) or visual analog scale (VAS) for pain—after repair of the medial meniscus. Studies were excluded if outcomes were not separated by tear type or reported graphically, different or no outcomes reported, no repair or repair with a sutureless method, follow-up greater than 72 months, case report, and systematic review. Patient-reported outcome measures and demographics were summarized as sample-weighted means and compared using t-tests. Mixed model linear regressions were fit predicting postoperative PROMs adjusted for tear type, preoperative PROMs, and follow-up time. F statistics of type III tests of fixed effects were compared.Results:Eighteen articles were included, n = 10 received MMRT repair and n = 8 other tear repairs. Postoperative values for all KOOS scores were less for the MMRT repair group compared with the other tear repair group, VAS pain was not statistically different. Tear type was a stronger predictor for postoperative PROMs than the preoperative score and duration of follow-up for all KOOS scores, but not VAS pain. On average, the MMRT repair group had shorter follow-up, more women, older age, and greater BMI.Discussion:All postoperative PROMs except for VAS pain were worse for patients who underwent MMRT repair compared with patients who underwent other types of medial meniscus repair. Tear type was a better predictor of postoperative PROMs despite intergroup differences in preoperative PROMs and duration of follow-up. Relative risk factors for MMRT compared with other types of medial meniscus tears include older age, greater BMI, and female sex.

The meniscus is a key structure in the knee, primarily responsible for the distribution and attenuation of loads within the joint space.12345 Function of the meniscus is largely dependent on the integrity of its circumferential fibers, which absorb 40% to 70% of loads, resist meniscal deformation, and prevent excessive loading of the articular cartilage.123456 Loss of the meniscus, or disruption of its circumferential fibers, is associated with biomechanical changes such as increased contact pressures, which ultimately lead to joint degeneration and the progression of osteoarthritis.1,3,4 The integrity of the meniscal structure and its ability to withstand axial loads depends on its attachments to the tibial plateau—in particular the posterior root of the medial meniscus.3,4,7,8 Medial meniscus root tears (MMRTs) in the posterior horn have been shown to be functionally equivalent to a total meniscectomy, with an associated 25% increase in peak tibiofemoral contact pressure.7891011 MMRT compromises meniscal ability to resist hoop stress, resulting in extrusion of the medial meniscus, altered joint kinematics, and accelerated progression of degenerative joint disease.2,7,9101112131415 It has been shown that restoration of anatomic positioning through MMRT repair reduces extrusion of the meniscus and re-establishes contact forces that are observed in the intact knee.2,7,8,13

The unique biomechanical changes associated with MMRT and evidence of subsequent articular degeneration have brought great attention to this pattern of meniscal pathology. MMRT is defined as either an avulsion of the medial meniscus insertion site on the tibial plateau or a complete radial tear within 1 cm of that site.14,16 Medial meniscus root tears account for 10% to 21% of all meniscus tears and represent a substantial proportion of clinically significant meniscal injuries.9,15 The characteristics observed for this injury pattern are bimodal, commonly occurring as a traumatic tear in younger athletes or as a degenerative tear in middle-aged to elderly adults.14,16,17 Major risk factors for MMRT include older age, greater BMI, increased varus alignment of the knee, and female sex.5,14,18 The body of evidence demonstrating the clinical and functional benefits of MMRT repair compared with nonsurgical management and partial meniscectomy is abundant. In addition to improved patient-reported outcomes, many studies show that patients undergoing MMRT repair have slowed radiographic progression of osteoarthritis and decreased rates of conversion to total knee arthroplasty.9,12,16,19 It has been suggested that the degree of meniscal extrusion is associated with worse clinical and radiographic outcomes.2,7,11,2021222324

Meniscal repair is associated with reduced pain, restored functional ability, and a reduction in the progression of degenerative changes in the knee.9,12,16,19 Current literature is lacking studies directly comparing clinical and functional outcomes of patients who have undergone MMRT repair versus patients who have undergone any other type of medial meniscus tear repair. Our primary study aims were twofold: (1) to review the available literature and compare patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) between these two groups and (2) to identify potential factors associated with any of the observed differences in PROMs. These results may provide surgeons with information that would be valuable in counseling and setting expectations for patients who are undergoing MMRT repair. Findings may also provide the basis for additional studies investigating factors which affect the clinical outcome after various types of meniscal repair. We hypothesize that patients who have undergone MMRT repair will have worse clinical and functional outcomes compared with patients who have undergone any other type of medial meniscus repair. In addition, we expect risk factors for MMRT, including older age, greater BMI, and female sex to correlate with the observed differences in outcomes.

Methods

A literature search was conducted using the PubMed database in June 2022 to identify studies that reported PROMs after surgical repair of the medial meniscus. Only studies published since 2005 were included. A search syntax was created using a combination of the following relevant MeSH terms: ([“meniscus” AND “repair”] AND [“radial” OR “longitudinal” OR “horizontal” OR “bucket handle” OR “parrot beak” OR “degenerative”] AND “knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score” OR “visual analog scale”]) OR ([“medial meniscus” AND “repair”] AND [“knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score” OR “visual analog scale”]). Titles and abstracts were screened by the first author. Full-text articles were read to see whether they reported the following PROMs: knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome score (KOOS) and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain at either the preoperative or postoperative (follow-up) assessment.25,26 Risk of bias was assessed through the methodological items for non-randomized studies criteria.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

A study inclusion flow chart was made, and study characteristics were summarized in tabular form. Since most of the included studies did not have both tear types as an intervention arm, meta-analysis combining effect sizes could not be done. Instead, continuous measures such as study demographics (age, percent sex, BMI, and follow-up time) and PROMs (KOOS and VAS) were summarized as sample-weighted means using guidance from the Cochrane Handbook.27 Groupwise means were compared using an independent samples t-test after confirming if the variance between groups should be assumed to be equal or not using the Levene test for equality of variance. If the average KOOS score was not reported, then the average KOOS score was calculated by summing the KOOS subscores (pain, symptoms, activities of daily living [ADL], sport and recreation, and quality of life [QOL]) and dividing by 5. If a conglomerate of the KOOS subscores was reported, then the average KOOS score was calculated by dividing by 5. This reported or calculated average KOOS score is hereinafter referred to as the total KOOS score. Similarly, studies that reported the VAS on a 10-point scale were transformed to a 100-point scale by multiplying by 10. For studies that provided both the preoperative and postoperative assessments, the change in PROMs was calculated by subtracting the mean postoperative value by the mean preoperative value and comparing between the two tear types. Finally, to see whether tear type was a notable predictor of each postoperative PROM while controlling for the differences in the two main confounding variables (differences in preoperative PROMs and duration of follow-up), a mixed model linear regression was done predicting postoperative PROMs using the main effects of tear type (binary), preoperative PROMs (continuous), and duration of follow-up in months (continuous) with an intercept. The F statistic of type III tests of fixed effects was compared between terms to see which one could explain more of the variance. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant, and all analyses were done using SPSS Version 28.0 (IBM Corp).

Results

Forty-two articles from 2005 to 2022 were screened for inclusion. Nineteen articles (45.2%) were excluded for the following reasons: outcomes not separated by tear type, outcomes reported graphically, different outcomes or no outcomes reported, absence of meniscus repair, repair with a sutureless method, duration of follow-up greater than 72 months, case report, and systematic review. Study arms were extracted from the remaining 23 articles and separated into the MMRT repair group, consisting of 13 articles and 19 study arms, and the other tear repair group, consisting of 10 articles and 14 study arms. Six arms consisting of duplicate populations were removed from the MMRT repair group based on completeness of the reported outcomes, sample size, and recency, in that order. Five arms were removed from the other tear repair group for containing most tears with solely lateral involvement or failure to specify tear laterality. By excluding individual study arms, three additional articles were excluded from the MMRT repair group and two were excluded from the other tear repair group, leaving a total of 18 articles included for analysis. Ten studies consisting of 13 study arms represent the MMRT repair group, and eight studies consisting of nine study arms represent the other tear repair group. Eight studies consisting of 10 arms in the MMRT repair group (80.0% and 76.9%, respectively) and seven studies consisting of eight arms in the other tear repair group (87.5% and 88.9%, respectively) had preoperative data (Figure 1). Discussion

This study aimed to identify differences in clinical and functional outcomes of patients who undergo MMRT repair versus other types of meniscus repair, as well as to identify demographic differences that characterize each patient population. Preoperative PROMs were statistically significantly different for patients in the MMRT repair group compared with patients in the other tear repair group. In particular, KOOS pain and KOOS sport and recreation were worse for patients who underwent MMRT repair, while VAS pain was better. The difference in pain assessment can be explained by differential reporting of KOOS scores and VAS pain. Only four out of 13 study arms in the MMRT repair group (30.8%) reported both KOOS pain and VAS pain. None of the study arms in the other tear repair group reported both measures of pain. Thus, these results are not looking at the same patient population and disagreement between the different measures of pain may be expected.

The key findings of this study are that there are differences in postoperative PROMs between patients who underwent MMRT repair versus patients who underwent all other types of medial meniscus repair. All postoperative PROMs, except for VAS pain, were worse for patients who underwent MMRT repair compared with patients who underwent all other types of medial meniscus repair. Although PROMs between the two groups were markedly different at baseline, magnitudes of their mean differences were greater for the other tear repair group than they were for the MMRT repair group, indicating a greater degree of improvement after surgical repair in the other tear repair group. In addition, our mixed model regressions suggested that postoperative differences were more dependent on tear type than preoperative differences and duration of follow-up. Thus, we are able to conclude that postoperative differences in PROMs are not an artifact of each population's preoperative functional status. Rather, we attribute the observed differences in PROMs to the tear type, as well as to differences in each population's unique demographic and clinical features. As anticipated, the distribution of risk factors for MMRT, including older age, greater BMI, and female sex, was statistically significantly different between the MMRT repair group and the other tear repair group.

MMRT is functionally equivalent to a total meniscectomy due to the disruption of circumferential fibers in the meniscus.789,11 In the case of partial or total meniscectomy, Ihn et al3 demonstrated that the observed biomechanical changes were directly proportional to the amount of meniscus excised. We postulate that other meniscus tears, which do not disrupt the circumferential fibers, have some degree of viable meniscus depending on the size of the tear. Larger tears and those involving circumferential fibers would be expected to progress more similarly to MMRTs and affect PROMs in a more similar fashion. Since MMRTs disrupt the attachment of the meniscus, these are more clinically significant injuries, with worse patient-reported measures that are reflected in the results of our comparative review.

The clinical significance of MMRTs may also be related to the degree of meniscal extrusion. In a retrospective study of an MRI database, Costa et al66 highlighted the association between the extent of meniscal extrusion and the severity of cartilage degeneration in various patterns of meniscal tears. In addition to degenerative changes, major meniscal extrusion (>3 mm) was associated with tears that disrupt circumferential fibers, including large radial tears, complex tears, and MMRT. Henry et al45 noted a similar relationship between these tear patterns and increased severity of cartilage degeneration compared with bucket-handle and vertical tears. In a more recent study, Krych et al46 demonstrated that unrepaired MMRT showed increased arthritic changes of the knee as well as increased insufficiency fractures and subchondral collapse compared with unrepaired displaced meniscal flap tear.

These studies demonstrate a more dramatic clinical progression in the presence of MMRT compared with other types of meniscus tears. Meniscal extrusion is a key feature of MMRTs and more severe tears of other types, such as radial tears and complex tears. Repair does not always restore anatomical position, and the persistence of extrusion has been associated with quicker and more severe clinical progression.6,45,46 The technical difficulty of restoring anatomic position in the repair of MMRTs may, in part, account for the differences in PROMs that were observed in this study.

To our knowledge, this is the first comparative literature review to compare outcomes of MMRT repair with other types of medial meniscus repair. As we hypothesized, MMRT carries a higher degree of morbidity and is associated with risk factors, such as older age, greater BMI, and female sex. Future studies that propensity-match and/or stratify patients based on age, BMI, and sex could shed light on the relative prognostic importance of tear type and features that are typical of each patient population.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. There is a statistically significant difference in the follow-up time of the MMRT repair group compared with the other tear repair group. However, we do not believe this difference to be clinically significant, as demonstrated by our mixed model regressions. Despite our results, we would expect PROMs to plateau around 2 years, and if anything, decline thereafter. Thus, the difference in PROMs between the MMRT repair group and the other tear repair group may be underestimated by this study, further supporting our hypothesis. Second, there was a substantial difference between groups about the presence of combined or isolated lateral involvement. 1.7% (6/357) had lateral involvement in the MMRT repair group compared with 25.0% (71/284) in the other tear repair group, limiting our ability to accurately compare MMRT repair with the repair of other isolated medial meniscal pathologies. Additional investigations comparing MMRT repair with other types of medial meniscus repair should exclude involvement of the lateral meniscus. Finally, concomitant and subsequent knee surgeries were variably reported within and excluded from the studies that were evaluated in this comparative review. For example, results may be skewed by the differential presence of anterior cruciate ligament reconstructions, chondral abnormalities, partial meniscectomies, and other procedures. To address this limitation, additional studies should exclude all other knee pathologies and procedures.

Conclusions

All postoperative PROMs except for VAS pain were worse for patients who underwent MMRT repair compared with patients who underwent all other types of medial meniscus repair. Tear type was a better predictor of postoperative PROMs despite intergroup differences in preoperative PROMs and duration of follow-up. Relative risk factors for MMRT compared with other types of medial meniscus tears include older age, a greater BMI, and female sex.
