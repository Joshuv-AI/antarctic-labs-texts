High-fat diet, intestinal microecology and bone loss
Authors: Ning Wang, Xue Tong, Yi-kai Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone, a vital component of the human body, plays a crucial role in maintaining mobility and systemic health. Growing evidence underscores the complex interplay between a high-fat diet (HFD), intestinal microecology, and bone loss. This review consolidates findings across three interconnected mechanisms: (1) HFD compromises bone homeostasis by reducing bone mineral density (BMD) and disrupting microarchitecture, driven by bone marrow adiposity, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation; (2) HFD disrupts intestinal microecology through microbiota dysbiosis (e.g., elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, depletion of Bifidobacterium), epithelial barrier impairment (e.g., suppressed Mucin2 secretion, downregulated tight junction proteins), and immune dysregulation (e.g., Th17/Treg imbalance, diminished IL-10 production); and (3) intestinal microecology imbalances exacerbate bone loss through microbial metabolite alterations (e.g., a deficiency of short-chain fatty acids impairing Treg-mediated Wnt10b signaling), systemic inflammation from barrier leakage, and intestinal immune cell trafficking (e.g., Th17 migration to bone marrow). These interconnected mechanisms point to an indirect pathway by which HFD contributes to bone loss through alterations in intestinal microecology. While this indirect relationship remains insufficiently validated, accumulating evidence highlights the important roles of HFD and intestinal microecology in bone regulation. This review aims to comprehensively examine the connections between HFD, intestinal microecology, and bone loss, with a focus on elucidating these potential mechanisms. Given diet’s profound impact on intestinal microecology, optimizing dietary patterns to rebalance intestinal microecology offers a promising strategy for preventing and treating bone-related disorders.Keywords: High-fat diet, Intestinal microbiota, Intestinal epithelium, Intestinal immune, Bone loss, Dietary patterns

Introduction

Bones provide structural support, protection, and play a role in hematopoiesis and regulation of calcium and phosphorus metabolism [1]. Osteoporosis (OP), a degenerative disease of the skeleton with a high global prevalence, is characterized by pathological changes including decreased bone mass, destruction of bone microstructure, and increased bone fragility, which significantly increases the risk of fracture [2]. Its pathogenesis involves a dysregulation of the dynamic balance between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-dominated bone resorption, a process that is multifactorial and regulated by aging, declining estrogen levels, and abnormal nutrient metabolism [3]. In recent years, studies have found that a high-fat diet (HFD) can interfere with bone metabolic homeostasis and accelerate bone loss by inducing intestinal microbiota dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction, bone marrow (BM) fatty infiltration, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [1, 4–9].

As an important interface for host-microbe interactions, the intestinal microecosystem is composed of the commensal microbiota, the intestinal epithelial barrier, and the intestinal immune system, which play a central role in maintaining nutrient absorption, immune tolerance, and metabolic regulation [10, 11]. Experimental evidence suggests that HFD can lead to an imbalance in the ratio between Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (F/B) in the intestinal microbiota, accompanied by a thinning of the mucus layer of the intestinal epithelium as well as an imbalance in the ratio of inflammatory CD4+ T cells subpopulations (e.g., Th1, Th17) to regulatory T cells (Tregs), and these pathologic alterations have been demonstrated to be significantly correlated with decreased bone mineral density (BMD) [12–14]. However, by adjusting dietary patterns, increasing the intake of probiotics can effectively regulate intestinal microecology and reverse bone loss [15–17]. Therefore, we hypothesized that intestinal microecology may play an important role in the pathogenesis of HFD-induced bone loss and further discuss the possible mechanisms by which HFD promotes bone loss by altering intestinal microecology and restoring intestinal microecological balance by adjusting dietary patterns.

HFD-induced bone loss

In recent years, epidemiological studies have suggested that HFD, particularly those rich in saturated fatty acids, is a risk factor for OP. Fat intake is negatively correlated with BMD [18–20] and may increase the risk of fracture in postmenopausal women and the elderly [21, 22]. Yamauchi et al. found that lipid metabolism dysregulation, particularly elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels, serves as an independent predictor of non-vertebral fracture risk in postmenopausal populations [23]. Genetic evidence from Mendelian randomization (MR) studies further supports this association, showing that elevated LDL cholesterol causally reduces BMD, while lipid-lowering interventions (e.g., statins) may preserve skeletal integrity through modulation of osteoblast-adipocyte balance [24]. Building on this, a recent multi-omics MR study identified lipid-related DNA methylation sites in genes including GREB1 (Growth Regulation by Estrogen in Breast Cancer 1), SREBF2 (Sterol Regulatory Element Binding Transcription Factor 2), and NOTCH1 (Notch Receptor 1) as being genetically associated with BMD, suggesting that dyslipidemia may contribute to bone loss through epigenetic regulation of lipid-related signaling pathways [25]. In addition, Short-term high-fat dietary interventions, like ketogenic diets, have been shown to impair bone remodeling, particularly during periods of metabolic stress like exercise [26, 27]. Animal studies have further substantiated these findings, demonstrating that HFD significantly impacts bone structure and health [16, 28–33]. Zhang et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 80 articles on the lower bone mass of mice fed HFD and found that in most studies, the trabecular bone mass was lost in the femur, tibia, and vertebrae of mice, resulting in systemic OP [34]. Silva et al. conducted a large-scale dietary intervention study involving 1,100 mice and found that although mice fed HFD developed larger and stronger bones on average compared to those on a low-fat diet, HFD diminished the positive correlation between body weight and both bone size and whole-bone strength, ultimately exerting detrimental effects on skeletal health [35]. Similarly, studies in mice and rats have shown that HFD impairs fracture healing and compromises biomechanical properties [36, 37]. It has been reported that maternal feeding of HFD before and during pregnancy was sufficient to predispose offspring to poor bone health [38]. HFD-induced obesity also exacerbates bone loss in ovariectomy (ovx) mice, associated with bone marrow adipose tissue (BMAT) expansion, accelerated cellular senescence, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory markers [39]. However, this effect appears to be modulated by hormonal status and the duration of dietary intervention. McGrath et al. reported that in female mice with intact ovaries, although a 12-week HFD intervention led to marked BMAT expansion, it failed to attenuate bone microarchitecture [40]. These findings suggest that the presence of estrogen may mitigate the detrimental skeletal effects of HFD, and that under estrogen-replete conditions and relatively short intervention durations, BMAT accumulation alone may be insufficient to induce overt bone loss. Furthermore, Scheller et al. found that even after transitioning from HFD to a normal diet, HFD-induced alterations in bone health could not be fully reversed, indicating that some detrimental skeletal effects of HFD are persistent [41]. Consistently, both short-term and long-term HFD exposure have been shown to enhance osteoclastogenic potential, resulting in increased bone resorption activity [42, 43]. Further evidence demonstrates that HFD exacerbates alveolar and femoral bone loss by upregulating inflammation-related genes and promoting adipocyte-derived pro-osteoclastogenic factors [44]. Kim et al. found that inhibiting glucocorticoid (GC) signaling in osteoblasts and osteocytes protects against HFD-induced bone loss [45]. Subsequent studies revealed that HFD upregulates 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 1 (11β-HSD1) in osteoblasts, thereby enhancing GC signaling, which suppresses early growth response 2 (Egr2) expression, reduces glucose uptake, and impairs osteogenesis [46]. Taken together, we found that the mechanism of bone loss induced by HFD involves BM fatty infiltration, inflammatory factors, and oxidative stress. BM adiposity

Adipocyte infiltration in the BM is a prominent feature of bone loss under HFD [16, 47–49]. Adipocytes and osteoblasts in BM are both derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [50]. Studies have found that the mRNA expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (Pparg) in the BM of mice fed HFD is increased, and that PPARγ promotes the differentiation of MSCs into adipocytes while inhibiting osteoblast differentiation [16, 51, 52]. In addition, in vitro studies have shown that PPARγ in preosteoblasts and osteoblasts can be activated, and free fatty acids produced by BM adipocytes can serve as natural ligands for PPARγ, promoting the conversion of osteoblasts into adipocytes [53–55]. PPARγ is a direct regulator of c-fos (an immediate early gene) expression and an important mediator of osteoclast formation, promoting osteoclastogenesis by inducing c-fos expression [56]. Adipocytes can also promote osteoclast differentiation, which may be due to the enhanced expression of receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) in preadipocyte stage cells during the adipogenic differentiation process of MSCs [57]. In addition to classical adipogenic pathways, recent studies have identified intraflagellar transport 20 (IFT20) And WW domain-containing transcription regulator 1 (WWTR1) as key regulators of the osteoblast–adipocyte lineage switch. Their expression is downregulated in osteoblast-lineage cells from both OP patients and HFD-fed mice [58]. Dual deletion of IFT20 and WWTR1 disrupts TGF-β signaling by destabilizing and transcriptionally silencing TGF-β receptor II (TβRII), resulting in excessive BM adiposity and increased osteoclast activity, thereby contributing to skeletal deterioration in both OVX and HFD models [58]. Adipocytes are important endocrine cells capable of secreting a variety of adipokines, including leptin and adiponectin [59]. In women, plasma adiponectin and leptin concentrations exhibit an inverse correlation, with leptin levels increasing and adiponectin levels decreasing as body fat increases [60]. HFD-fed mice demonstrate a similar pattern of elevated leptin and reduced adiponectin levels [61–63]. Interestingly, leptin and adiponectin exert distinctly different effects on bone metabolism under different conditions [64, 65]. ​​In​​ leptin-deficient (ob/ob) mice, central leptin administration ​​was shown to​​ suppress BMAT accumulation ​​while stimulating​​ bone formation [66]. ​​Paradoxically,​​ leptin-deficient and leptin receptor-deficient (ob/ob and db/db) mice exhibit increased bone mass, ​​indicating​​ leptin may ​​also​​ inhibit bone formation via the hypothalamic-sympathetic-β2 adrenergic axis [67]. ​​Under HFD conditions,​​ the leptin receptor activates​​ JAK2/STAT3 signaling, promoting the differentiation of MSCs into adipocytes while inhibiting their osteogenic differentiation [68]. Although​​ in vitro studies show leptin ​​enhances​​ osteoblast activity, ​​this anabolic effect may be overridden by​​ leptin receptor-mediated inhibition in HFD contexts [69, 70]. Collectively, HFD induces elevated leptin levels and activates leptin receptor–mediated signaling pathways, thereby disrupting the balance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation and ultimately impairing bone formation. Clinical studies have shown that adiponectin levels are positively correlated with BMAT and negatively correlated with BMD, particularly in elderly men, postmenopausal women [71–73], though​​ this relationship varies by race in young women [74]. ​​Mechanistically,​​ adiponectin promotes osteoblastogenesis [75–77] and​​ regulates bone homeostasis via RANKL/OPG signaling ​​to enhance​​ osteoblast function ​​while suppressing​​ osteoclastogenesis [78, 79]. Experimental studies demonstrate that HFD downregulates adiponectin expression and activity, while adiponectin supplementation attenuates periodontitis-related bone loss in HFD-fed mice through anti-inflammatory mechanisms [80, 81]. However, these experimental findings on adiponectin’s protective effects appear to contrast with clinical observations of its negative correlation with BMD [72, 73]. Lewis et al. proposed that ​​physiological​​ adiponectin activity favors bone formation, ​​whereas​​ pathological conditions (menopause, obesity, inflammation) ​​may shift​​ its signaling toward bone resorption [82]. Although this hypothesis attempts to reconcile the discrepancies, it does not fully account for the divergence between experimental and clinical findings. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the context-dependent mechanisms by which adiponectin regulates bone metabolism.

Inflammation

HFD induces a chronic low-grade inflammatory state characterized by elevated circulating endotoxin levels, increased free fatty acids concentrations, and upregulated proinflammatory cytokine production, collectively creating an inflammatory milieu that disrupts bone homeostasis [83–85]. HFD leads to significant expansion of visceral adipose tissue and subcutaneous adipose tissue, promoting macrophage infiltration and polarization to the proinflammatory M1 phenotype [86]. These macrophages secrete high levels of inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), thereby establishing a persistent inflammatory microenvironment [86]. It is worth noting that although HFD also promotes significant expansion of BMAT, it is characterized by metabolic adaptive remodeling rather than a typical proinflammatory response [48]. This phenotype is different from both the significant inflammatory response of peripheral adipose tissue and the inflammatory activation state of aging-related BMAT (upregulation of IL-6 expression) [86, 87]. This difference suggests that the functional state of BMAT may be regulated by both age and metabolic stress, reflecting the heterogeneous response mechanism of adipose tissue to different pathophysiological stimuli. Within this inflammatory context, TNF-α and IL-1β exert cell-specific effects on bone metabolism [88, 89]. In osteocytes, TNF-α activates NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, leading to upregulated expression of RANKL and sclerostin, which promotes osteoclastogenesis [90, 91]. TNF-α further enhances osteoclast activity by amplifying RANKL-induced TRAF6/NF-κB/MAPK signaling and concurrently suppresses osteoblast differentiation through inhibition of Runx2 nuclear translocation and activation of PPARγ [92–94]. Meanwhile, IL-1β drives osteoclastogenesis by upregulating the expression of IGF2 and chemokines in non-osteoclasts and inhibits osteoblast differentiation by inhibiting the BMP/Smad signaling pathway [95–97]. Compounding these pro-resorptive effects, HFD also significantly reduces the production of IL-10 in BM-derived macrophages, an anti-inflammatory cytokine that directly inhibits osteoclast differentiation by targeting osteoclast precursors and suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production, thereby mitigating bone resorption [98, 99]. This integrated inflammatory network under HFD conditions contributes to the disruption of bone remodeling balance, favoring bone loss.

Oxidative stress

HFD induces oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the antioxidant defense system [100, 101]. Excessive ROS are produced in the bones of mice fed HFD, while antioxidant enzymes and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) are weakened, and the glutathione/oxidized glutathione ratio decreases [52, 102]. This change activates FOXO transcription factors, which antagonize Wnt signaling and inhibit β-catenin-mediated transcription, thereby reducing osteoblast differentiation and osteogenesis [103–105]. Excessive ROS promotes the oxidation of RUNX2, leading to its proteasomal degradation, which in turn suppresses osteoblast proliferation and differentiation [106]. Simultaneously, ROS enhances the recruitment and differentiation of osteoclast precursors by upregulating RANKL expression, leading to increased bone resorption [107, 108]. ROS not only directly damage osteoblasts and osteoclasts but also modulate bone remodeling by activating multiple redox-sensitive signaling pathways, including the MAPK, NF-κB, and NLRP3 inflammasome cascades [109, 110]. Moreover, oxidative stress impairs the function of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), inhibiting their osteogenic differentiation while promoting adipogenesis [111]. The elevated release of free fatty acids from adipocytes further intensifies oxidative stress in osteoblasts, thereby perpetuating bone deterioration [112]. Chen et al. found that HFD induces premature senescence in BMSCs and reduces their proliferation and osteogenic ability [113]. This is because HFD leads to reduced vitamin D receptor (VDR) signaling in BMSCs, and VDR can reduce BMSCs’ aging by reducing intracellular ROS levels and preserving mitochondrial function [113]. Additionally, ROS promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by activating signaling pathways such as NF-κB, while inflammatory mediators in turn stimulate further ROS generation, forming a self-amplifying loop between oxidative stress and inflammation [114–116].

HFD-induced alterations in intestinal microecology

Overview of intestinal microecology

Intestinal microecology is a dynamic functional unit comprising the intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelial barrier, and the host’s immune system. While existing studies primarily focus on the structure and function of the intestinal microbiota [117, 118], there remains a lack of comprehensive exploration into the metabolic regulation of intestinal epithelial cells and the dynamic response mechanisms of the intestinal immune system. Importantly, these components do not operate in isolation but form a complex regulatory network through microbial-epithelial interactions, immune-microbial signaling, and other pathways [119–121]. This integrated perspective is essential for understanding the mechanisms by which external factors, such as diet, modulate host health.

Intestinal microbiota

The mammalian gastrointestinal tract contains trillions of microorganisms, mainly colonized by indigenous bacteria, but also including archaea, viruses, fungi, and protozoa [122, 123]. Due to the complexity of the microbiota, little was known about their functional connections, and the development of metagenomic, metabolomic, and metatranscriptomic technologies facilitates the deciphering of this mechanism [124]. The intestinal microbiota can extract energy and nutrients from food due to its genes encoding glycosaminoglycan degradation. In addition, the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and the biosynthesis of amino acids, vitamins, and lipids are also highly dependent on intestinal microbiota [125, 126]. The intestinal microbiota is also required for the development and differentiation of the host intestinal epithelium and immune system [123, 127]. Microbial colonization triggers intestinal epithelial cells’ (IECs) expression of RegIIIγ, a secreted C-type lectin, particularly in Paneth cells [128]. Furthermore, SCFAs enhance colonic epithelial barrier function by affecting epithelial O2 consumption and stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) [129]. The intestinal microbiota can also induce the development of immune cells and the secretion of interleukin, playing an important role in host defense against pathogens [130–132].

Intestinal epithelium

The intestinal epithelium is a single cell layer that absorbs nutrients, provides effective defense against intraluminal toxins, antigens, and intestinal microbiota, and regulates intestinal immune responses [133, 134]. The intestinal epithelium constructs different types of barriers to spatially isolate intestinal microbiota and immune cells in the lamina propria, including physical and chemical barriers [133]. The small intestine secretes less mucus than the large intestine but contains Paneth cells that secrete chemical barrier molecules, including antimicrobial peptide (AMP), lysozyme (LZ), and regenerating islet-derived protein type 3 (Reg3) family proteins [135, 136]. In the large intestine, the chemical barrier is less pronounced than in the small intestine but effectively isolates the symbiotic microbiota from the intestinal tissues, relying on three distinct physical barrier systems: the aggregation of Mucin2 (MUC2) secreted by goblet cells to form a reticular structure firmly within the mucus layer; a sugar-coated glycocalyx on the microvilli of IECs, and cellular connections that firmly link IECs [135, 137]. Tuft cells in intestinal epithelium can produce humoral factors that regulate intestinal immunity [138], and microfold (M) cells can deliver antigens to intestinal immune cells to promote intestinal adaptive immune responses [139]. Therefore, intestinal epithelium may serve as a sensor between intestinal microbiota and intestinal immune cells to mediate the balance between both, maintaining symbiotic relationships and intestinal homeostasis [133].

Intestinal immune system

Gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) accounts for approximately 70% of the entire immune system, and it performs various regulatory and effector functions [140, 141]. The immune system of the small intestine mainly includes T cells and innate lymphoid cells that produce IL-17 and IL-22, as well as intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) and Tregs, which are responsible for protecting IECs and maintaining the ability to digest and absorb food [142, 143]. The large intestinal immune system includes mucosal immunoglobulin A (IgA)-producing plasma cells and a large number of Tregs, which can be kept away from the symbiotic microbiota of the large intestine to prevent inflammatory responses against them [142, 143]. The intestinal microbiota and metabolites play a crucial role in the formation of intestinal immunity. Studies have shown a significant reduction in IgA-secreting plasma cells in the intestines of germ-free (GF) animals and the maturation of CD4+ T cells and Tregs is impaired, while SCFAs can regulate Tregs homeostasis in the colon of GF mice [144–147]. The intestinal immune system employs multiple mechanisms to sense intestinal microbiota and their functional output, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), Nod-like receptors (NLRs), G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and aryl hydrocarbon receptors (AHR) [141]. At the same time, the intestinal epithelium continuously samples and responds to the antigen load in intestinal lumen and presents it to the immune system. If the epithelium is damaged, the load of antigens entering the submucosa of the epithelium will increase, triggering a series of immune responses and leading to the development of the disease [148].

Therefore, intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and intestinal immune system are symbiotic, influencing each other and regulating and balancing each other. They allow selective absorption of nutrients while limiting the acquisition of pathogens and foodborne antigens, maintaining normal physiological function of intestine (Fig. 1).

Impact of HFD on intestinal microecology

Effects on intestinal microbiota

Particular diets may promote or inhibit the growth of specific bacterial strains, thus leading to changes in host fermentation metabolism. Intestinal microbiota can interact with intestinal epithelium and intestinal immune system to affect local and systemic immune responses and the regulation of inflammation [149].

HFD significantly influences the composition and metabolic functions of the human intestinal microbiota. In a clinical short-term intervention trial, a 5-day HFD rapidly increased the abundance of bile-tolerant bacteria (Alistipes, Bilophila, and Bacteroides), while reducing plant polysaccharide-degrading taxa (Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale, and Ruminococcus bromii) [150]. These changes were associated with reduced fecal butyrate and increased aromatic compounds, disrupting intestinal homeostasis and promoting systemic inflammation. Notably, HFD-induced dysbiosis persisted post-intervention, suggesting long-term metabolic consequences even after dietary normalization [150]. In a long-term intervention (6 months), higher fat intake led to decreased intestinal microbiota diversity, increased bile acid-tolerant genera (Alistipes, Bacteroides), and decreased beneficial Faecalibacterium and SCFAs. These changes correlated with elevated fecal pro-inflammatory metabolites (e.g., arachidonic acid) and plasma inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, Creactive protein), linked to cardiometabolic risk [151]. Additionally, in individuals at risk for metabolic syndrome, HFD reduced total bacterial load, whereas low-fat, high-carbohydrate diets enhanced the growth of beneficial microbiota (Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii), highlighting the potential of dietary modulation in supporting metabolic health [152]. A study conducted a meta-analysis of 27 studies on diet, proving that HFD can repeatedly alter intestinal microbiota, and HFD can improve the ratio of F/B in most animal studies [13]. Hildebrandt et al. found that HFD decreased Bacteroidetes and increased Firmicutes and Proteobacteria in mice, exacerbating microbiota dysbiosis [153]. HFD-fed ovx mice showed a decrease in Alloprevotella, Lactobacillaceae, and Prevotella, and an increase in Bifidobacteriales [154]. A study has reported that after a 16-week HFD intervention, mice exhibited an increase in Enterobacteriales and a decrease in Bacteroidales and Clostridiales [155]. Li et al. found that the abundance of SCFAs-producing bacteria in mice on HFD was reduced, resulting in a significant reduction in SCFAs [156, 157]. In a 6-month randomized controlled-feeding trial, HFD induces a decrease in SCFAs and an increase in palmitic acid and stearic acid, arachidonic acid in the population [151]. Butyrate, propionate, and acetate in SCFAs are volatile fatty acids produced by colon microbiota, which can reduce intestinal pH value, inhibit pathogenic microorganisms, and increase the absorption of certain nutrients [158, 159]. A report showed that propionic acid prevented Th17-mediated proinflammatory immune responses by inducing functional Tregs to increase IL-10 secretion after treatment of HFD-fed mice [160]. Cani et al. found that changes in intestinal microbiota induced by antibiotic treatment reduced metabolic endotoxemia and cecal LPS content in HFD-fed mice, indicating that intestinal permeability and metabolic endotoxemia could be controlled by altering intestinal microbiota [161].

Effects on intestinal epithelium

HFD-fed significantly decreased the expression of regenerating islet-derived 3-gamma (Reg3g) and phospholipase A2 group-II (PLA2g2) in the jejunum, impairing intestinal epithelium’s chemical barrier [162, 163]. The colon transepithelial resistance of HFD-fed mice was 26% lower than that of control mice, and the barrier resistance was reduced [164]. Studies have found that HFD can stimulate small intestine IECs proliferation through β-catenin signaling; however, it also causes a metabolic shift toward fatty acid β-oxidation in the small intestine IECs and impairs colonocyte mitochondrial function [165, 166]. HFD reduces the level and secretion of MUC2, which may be due to high fat triggering goblet cell stress and stimulating the endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein reaction pathway, resulting in enhanced misfolding of MUC2 and reducing its synthesis/secretion [12, 167]. In addition, HFD greatly reduces the expression of tight junction (TJ) proteins, such as occludin, zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), and claudin-1, and significantly increases intestinal permeability [12, 161]. This may be related to the fact that a higher bile acid level in intestinal lumen changes TJ dynamics or increases local inflammatory cytokines to damage TJ or dietary fat directly changes the distribution and expression of TJ [12, 168, 169].

Effects on intestinal immune system

The intestinal immune function is shaped by local environmental factors such as dietary composition and bacterial metabolites [142]. The study found that the number of IELs and Lamina propria Lymphocytes in the small intestine was reduced after 3 weeks of HFD-fed [170]. Surprisingly, similar changes occurred even after 1 day of HFD-fed, illustrating the important role of dietary structure for intestinal immune function [142, 170]. After 12 to 16 weeks of HFD-fed, the proportion of Th1 cells and CD8+ T cells that produce interferon-γ (IFN-γ) increased in the small intestine and colon, the proportion of CD4+ Foxp3+ Tregs decreased, and the percentage of NKp46+ CD4− innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) that produce IL-22 decreased. Moreover, the decrease of Tregs may also be accompanied by the decrease of IL-10, suggesting that HFD can lead to the pro-inflammatory transformation of intestinal immune cells, thereby increasing intestinal permeability and disrupting systemic metabolic homeostasis [14, 171]. IL-22 and IL-10 have been shown to protect intestinal mucosal barrier and endocrine function [172, 173]. Interestingly, the number of Th17 cells varied between reports. Some reports found that HFD-fed mice for 4 weeks and those fed for 10 weeks showed decreased Th17 cells in the small intestine [174, 175], while other studies have found no change in Th17 cell numbers after 12–16 weeks of HFD feeding, which may be related to the time and method of modeling [14]. Meanwhile, IL-17-producing γδ T cells increased in the intestines of HFD-fed mice, probably because γδ T cells can produce IL-17 independently of IL-23 signaling, thereby promoting epithelial barrier function by regulating the localization of TJ protein occludin, thus reducing excessive permeability after injury [14, 176]. Intestinal microecology imbalance promotes bone loss

Intestinal microbiota

Earlier studies have suggested a potential association between intestinal bacteria overgrowth and lower BMD in the lumbar spine and femoral neck in certain populations [177, 178]. Ni et al. explored the relationship between intestinal microbiota and BMD by conducting two-sample MR analysis using data from published genome-wide association studies. They found several bacterial taxa that might potentially influence bone mass changes (e.g., Clostridiales and its associated family Lachnospiraceae) [179]. Similarly, Wang et al. proposed that the relationship between intestinal microbiota and BMD may be present across the lifespan, with the Firmicutes phylum being particularly associated with BMD across different age groups [180]. In older adults, changes in microbial composition were associated with lower BMD, especially in the lumbar spine and femoral neck [181]. Through a survey of postmenopausal women in Mexico and Japan, researchers found that BMD in postmenopausal women was associated with alterations in the abundance of specific microbiotas [182, 183]. Meanwhile, a Chinese multi-ethnic cohort study found that the regulation of intestinal microbiota may be a potential strategy to mitigate BMD decline [184], and further studies have supported the potential of microbiota modulation in bone health management [185, 186]. Lin et al. observed elevated levels of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), an intestinal microbiota-derived metabolite, in patients with OP [187]. In animal studies, Schwarzer et al. found that 8-week-old male GF mice had lower femur length and bone mass compared with conventionally reared (CONV-R) mice [188]. Sjögren et al. found that 7-week-old female GF mice had increased bone Mass compared with CONV-R mice and had reduced bone mass when GF mice were colonized with normal intestinal microbiota for 1 month [189]. This difference is speculated to be related to the sex, strain, age, and colonization time of the animals used, but it also further illustrates that intestinal microbiota has an important impact on bone synthesis and decomposition. Yan et al. investigated the effects of short-term (1 month) and long-term (8 months) microbiota transplantation on bone remodeling in GF mice. They found that short-term transplantation led to an acute reduction in bone mass, whereas long-term transplantation increased bone mass. This difference may be attributed to the intestinal microbiota providing a net Anabolic stimulus to bone via the modulation of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling [190]. Meanwhile, studies have found that sex steroid–depleted mice raised in GF conditions are protected against trabecular bone loss, and supplementation with Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG can prevent bone loss in CONV-R mice with sex steroid–depleted, and supplementation with Escherichia coli has no protective effect [191]. This indicates that intestinal microbiota is the core of bone loss in mice caused by sex steroid deficiency, and supplementing different bacterial strains has different effects on reducing bone loss.

The intestinal microbiota and its metabolites affect bone remodeling and promote bone resorption and formation [192]. The intestinal microbiota affects intestinal pH value, and different microbiota have different effects on nutrient absorption, indicating that alterations in intestinal microbiota can affect the absorption of nutrients related to bone development, such as calcium and vitamin K, and have an important influence on regulating bone metabolism [193–195]. In addition, the composition of intestinal microbiota is an important factor in regulating intestinal mucus layer [196]. Studies have found that the colonic mucus layer of GF mice is different from that of conventionally fed mice [196, 197]. Through quantitative proteomics, it was found that MUC2 in the colon of GF mice was significantly reduced, and lower amounts of MUC2 may make it more susceptible to bacterial penetration [196]. This may be because intestinal microbiota induces colonic goblet cell development and mucus barrier assembly in an ERN2 (an endoplasmic reticulum transmembrane protein)-dependent manner, but how intestinal microbiota regulates Ern2 gene expression remains unknown [198]. The mucus layer is an important component in maintaining intestinal barrier, and any changes in intestinal permeability may increase levels of osteoclastic factors and affect bone density [199]. Sjögren et al. found that the immune status of GF mice was changed, and the number of pro-inflammatory cytokines and CD4+ T cells in BM decreased [189]. They further discovered that the regulation of bone mass by intestinal microbiota depends on NOD1 and NOD2 signaling, whereas NLRs are pattern recognition receptors and represent a key component of innate immunity [200]. Studies have suggested that SCFAs can reduce the pH value of intestinal contents and increase the available free calcium in the intestine, and SCFAs can promote calcium transport in colon cells and effectively promote calcium absorption [201–203]. A Human study using sodium propionate supplementation for 14 days demonstrated a significant reduction in serum bone resorption markers, like CTX-I, suggesting the potential anti-resorptive effects of SCFAs in humans [204]. Current studies have found that SCFAs can induce metabolic reprogramming of osteoclasts, leading to the enhancement of glycolysis at the expense of oxidative phosphorylation, downregulating osteoclast genes such as TRAF6 and NFATc1 and reducing inflammation through the induction of Tregs, thus regulating systemic bone mass [204, 205]. Dietary SCFAs supplements have been reported to reduce PPARγ expression and activity [206], and PPARγ inhibits osteoblasts and promotes osteoclast formation; thus, SCFAs may promote bone formation by reducing PPARγ expression and activity [53, 56, 206]. Notably, beyond SCFAs, microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites such as indole acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) have recently been shown to attenuate bone loss through AHR-dependent mechanisms. Chen et al. demonstrated that AHR activation by IAA/IPA directly binds to the promoter of β-catenin, thereby enhancing IL-10 secretion from intestinal lamina propria to bone marrow. This IL-10-mediated crosstalk suppresses osteoclastogenesis while promoting osteoblast differentiation [207]. Studies have found that supplementing antibiotic-treated mice with SCFAs can restore IGF-1 and bone mass to levels comparable to those in non-antibiotic-treated mice [190]. IGF-1 is a hormone known to have a role in bone growth [208]. Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is also a key regulator of bone development [209], and it has been reported that PTH requires butyrate to play its role in bone regulation [210]. It has been found that palmitic acid in long-chain fatty acids can impair osteoblast formation and mineralization by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin and BMP2/Runx2/Smads pathways, and induce osteoblast apoptosis and dysfunctional autophagy [211, 212]. In summary, intestinal microbiota and its metabolites have an important effect on bone remodeling.

Intestinal epithelium

The integrity of intestinal epithelial barrier is crucial for bone homeostasis, as damage to the epithelial barrier leads to increased antigen load entering the epithelial submucosa, thereby initiating the activation of immune cells and the local production of osteoclastogenic cytokines, affecting bone density and bone health [199, 213]. A study finds that ovx increases intestinal permeability in mice, causing translocation of bacterial products such as LPS and flagellin that increase activation of T cells in the lamina propria of intestinal wall [191]. In ovx mice, Paneth cell shedding and LZ secretion are decreased, intestinal microbiota changes and inflammatory factor infiltration are increased, and bone loss is induced [135, 213]. Studies have found that the colon tissue morphology of ovx mice changes, goblet cells atrophy, mucus layer becomes thinner, TJ proteins ZO-1 and occludin expression decrease, and serum LPS increases [214, 215]. Meanwhile, glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) promotes intestinal cell proliferation and improves intestinal mucosal barrier function, and in the colon tissue of ovx mice, Glp2r (the main mediating receptor for GLP-2) gene expression is reduced [215]. Moreover, enterochromaffin cells in IECs secrete gut-derived serotonin (GDS), which inhibits osteoblast proliferation through the Htr1b receptor and cAMP response element binding protein (CREB), and when inhibiting the synthesis of GDS in intestinal prevented bone loss in ovx mice [216–218]. It was also found that Winnie mice carrying Muc2 gene mutations had reduced osteoblasts and increased osteoclast numbers, which may be due to higher levels of the free circulating form of GDS, leading to greater activation of Htr1b receptor on preosteoblasts promoting bone loss [219].

Intestinal immune system

Previous studies have found an upregulation of TNF-producing T cells in the BM [220]. TNF enhances RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis by engaging the TNF receptor p55 expressed on osteoclast precursors, which is a key mechanism of bone loss in ovx mice [220]. However, the origin of BM TNF+ T cells is unclear. Subsequent studies by Yu et al. found that ovx expanded intestinal TNF+ T cells and Th17 cells, increased their S1P receptor 1-mediated (S1PR1-mediated) egress from intestine and enhanced their subsequent influx into the BM through CXCR3- and CCL20-mediated mechanisms [221]. Blocking this transport pathway prevented bone loss in mice, suggesting that blocking intestinal T cells migration may be a therapeutic target after postmenopausal bone loss. It has been reported that butyrate can induce the expansion of Tregs in intestine and BM and regulate bone anabolic metabolism through the regulation of CD8+ T cells Wnt10b production mediated by Tregs [205]. Guo et al. found Th17/Treg imbalance in the mesenteric lymph nodes of ovx rats, increased TNF-α and IL-17 expression in the colon and BM, and decreased TGF-β and IL-10 expression; OP caused by estrogen deficiency can be ameliorated when the Th17/Treg balance is restored [215]. In addition, it was found that effector memory CD4+ T cells in intestine mediated bone loss in food-allergic bowel disease model mice by producing IL-4 predominance [222]. However, current studies on the interaction between intestinal immune cells and bone metabolism mainly focus on T cells, and the underlying mechanisms are complex and mostly unknown. We speculate that an imbalance in intestinal microecology may drive bone loss through a tightly interconnected network involving intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and immune system, together constituting a pathogenic triad. In ovx models, intestinal barrier disruption frequently serves as an initiating event. Loss of goblet cells and downregulation of MUC2 increase intestinal permeability, facilitating the translocation of microbial products (e.g., LPS), which activate lamina propria immune cells and promote systemic inflammation [191, 215]. Microbial dysbiosis exacerbates this process by reducing the production of protective metabolites like SCFAs and tryptophan derivatives. These metabolites normally maintain barrier integrity and immune tolerance by inducing Tregs via histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition and activating AHR signaling [207, 223]. Their depletion amplifies both barrier and immune dysregulation, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle. Immune dysregulation, in turn, links local intestinal disturbances to downstream skeletal effects: Th17/Treg imbalance enhances osteoclastogenesis, and lymphocyte trafficking to bone marrow drives inflammation-induced bone resorption [205, 215]. Blockade of lymphocyte egress (e.g., via S1PR1 or CXCR3 inhibition) preserves bone mass in ovx models, even in the presence of persistent dysbiosis [221]. However, the temporal and causal order of these events remains incompletely understood. Future studies should integrate longitudinal multi-omics with time-resolved analyses to disentangle these relationships. This includes clarifying pathway independence and hierarchical relationships through combinatorial interventions in genetically modified models, and dynamically profiling microbiota, epithelium, and immune system interactions to identify critical therapeutic targets. These strategies are critical for developing targeted therapies that effectively disrupt the pathogenic gut–bone axis.

HFD promotes bone loss by altering intestinal microecology

HFD increases the F/B ratio in intestinal flora, the decrease of Bifidobacteriaceae and Lactobacillus, and dysbiosis in the microbiota triggers an increase in intestinal trimethylamine (TMA), which is metabolized by the liver, which increases circulating TMAO; TMAO could promote osteoclast differentiation by activating ROS-dependent NF-κB signaling pathway [13, 224–226]. Concurrently, TMAO simultaneously impairs osteoblast function by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress via the PERK/ATF5 axis, thereby suppressing bone formation and exacerbating OP progression [227]. HFD increases Proteobacteria and causes the growth of intestinal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) Levels, and LPS activates the innate immune system through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), leading to intestinal inflammation, damage of TJ, increased intestinal permeability, leakage of LPS into the blood, and increased circulating LPS [228–231]. In bone tissue, this microbial-derived LPS promotes osteoclast activity and differentiation by upregulating RANKL, IL-1, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and TNF-α [231]. Mechanistically, LPS activates the TLR4/MyD88 signaling cascade in bone marrow-derived macrophages, triggering NF-κB nuclear translocation and enhancing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, thereby accelerating bone loss [228]. Since the effects of TNF-α and IL-1β on bone have been discussed previously, they are not further addressed here. IL-6 promotes osteoclastogenesis by upregulating RANKL expression in osteoblasts and osteocytes via ERK1/2 and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways, while also contributing to bone loss by inducing osteocyte apoptosis through MAPK/STAT3 activation and stimulating proinflammatory cytokine production [232, 233]. Moreover, HFD induces an increase in Bilophila wadsworthia (Proteobacteria phylum), reducing the expression of microbial genes involved in butyrate metabolism [234]. Butyrate has been shown to enhance intestinal epithelial barrier by assembling TJ and stimulating bone formation by regulating WNT10B expression mediated by Tregs [205, 235]. Meanwhile, HFD leads to a 100-fold decrease in Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) [236]. Studies have found that A. muciniphila can regulate TJ to reduce intestinal barrier damage, degrade complex mucin glycoproteins, and produce acetate and propionate [237, 238]. Oral administration of A. muciniphila to fractured mice can significantly accelerate fracture healing. This effect is related to the improvement of intestinal barrier function, manifested by upregulation of mRNA expression of TJ proteins, including Ocln, Jam3, Cldn-2, -3 and -15, and downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines Tnf-α and Il17A [239]. Further evidence demonstrates that extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from A. muciniphila can accumulate in bone tissue, where they attenuate OP by enhancing osteogenic activity and suppressing osteoclast formation, thereby preserving bone mass and strength [240]. One study found that feeding mice an HFD of saturated fatty acids but restricting food intake, increased fractional calcium absorption and reduced BMD in mice [241]. When fat intake is not restricted, HFD-induced duodenal oxidation state could significantly down-regulate expression of calbindin-D(9 K), PMCA(1b), and sodium-calcium exchanger, thus causing an inhibitory effect on intestinal calcium absorption [242]. In addition, HFD increases intestinal GDS synthesis, which in turn inhibits osteoblast proliferation through the Htr1b receptor and CREB [217, 243, 244]. A report suggests that obesity rather than HFD itself causes bone loss and that this bone loss can be reversed by endogenous intestinal SCFAs produced by the non-obesity mice (HFD-fed) activated free fatty acid receptor 2 and inhibited histone deacetylases, promoting Tregs proliferation and inhibiting osteoclastogenesis [245]. Although this is different from other studies, it suggests that the balance of intestinal microbiota and immune system plays an important role in bone health. Currently, specific studies demonstrating the direct effects of SCFAs on bone health in human models are lacking, and further clinical studies are recommended to robustly confirm these effects. Studies have found that microbiota-induced Th17 cells exert protection by regulating the lipid absorption of intestinal epithelium in an IL-17-dependent manner; however, HFD can eliminate Th17-induced microbiota to disrupt intestinal immune homeostasis, and IgA immune cells in intestinal are reduced, and the pro-inflammatory transformation of some immune cells which are not conducive to the control of intestinal inflammation and destroys systemic metabolic homeostasis [175, 246]. In summary, HFD leads to microbial dysbiosis, intestinal epithelium dysfunction, and imbalance of intestinal immune system, affects the effective absorption of intestinal nutrients, increases intestinal permeability, promotes the translocation of pathogens into the bloodstream, and causes systemic inflammation, resulting in systemic effects, including bone loss (Fig. 2).

What can be done to restore the adverse effects of HFD on intestinal microecology?

Diet is an important modifiable factor affecting the occurrence and development of OP [247]. Fabiani et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 20 studies on the association between different dietary patterns and the risk of fracture or low BMD and identified three popular dietary patterns: “Healthy,” “Milk/dairy,” and “Meat/Western,” found that the “Healthy” and “Milk/dairy” patterns were associated with a reduced risk of low BMD and fracture, the “Western” pattern was inversely correlated [248]. The “Western” pattern typically involves a high intake of fats, proteins, refined carbohydrates, sodium, and phosphorus [249], among which excessive fat intake can impair bone remineralization and negatively affect bone metabolism [250, 251]. Therefore, adopting healthy dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet (MD), which is based on plant-based foods such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes and nuts, fish, olive oil, as well as reducing the intake of red meat and saturated fatty acids, is thought to be beneficial to bone health due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [7]. Studies found that adherence to MD is positively correlated with BMD (especially in middle-aged and elderly women), and also helps menopausal women reduce fat mass and maintain muscle mass [7, 252]. In addition, MD can beneficially affect intestinal microbiota and related metabolites, with higher abundances of Bacteroidetes, Prevotellaceae, and Prevotella, and lower abundances of Firmicutes and Lachnospiraceae, accompanied by increased levels of SCFAs production [253, 254]. Enhancing endogenous SCFAs production through dietary interventions may serve as an effective strategy for modulating osteoclast activity and inhibiting bone resorption [204].

In addition to dietary patterns, supplementation with probiotics and prebiotics has shown promise in improving bone health [203, 255]. Probiotics are active microorganisms that can benefit the host when consumed in sufficient quantities, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [256]. Prebiotics are compounds selectively utilized by host microorganisms and metabolized into health-promoting substances, including fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), inulin, galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), lactose derivatives, and soluble corn fiber (SCF) [257]. Xiao et al. conducted a 9-week dietary intervention involving whole grains, traditional Chinese medicinal foods, and prebiotics in 123 centrally obese individuals, which significantly reduced endotoxin-producing bacteria (Enterobacteriaceae and Desulfovibrionaceae) and increased intestinal barrier–protecting microbiota, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [258]. Studies have shown that Lactobacillus reuteri alters intestinal microbiota of ovx mice, preventing ovx-induced CD4+ T cells proliferation and ovx-induced trabecular bone loss [259]. Prevotella histicola can upregulate Muc2 mRNA expression in the ileum and colon of ovx mice, mitigating intestinal leakage in mice [260]. Behera et al. found that treating obese mice with probiotics or their derived metabolites, indole-3-propionic acid, can upregulate the mRNA transcription of TJ protein genes (Ocln and Cldn-5) in colon tissue, promoting colonic normal structural restoration and improving osteoblast mineralization [261]. Zhang et al. showed that FOS/GOS could significantly increase the biodiversity of intestinal flora and SCFAs concentration in HFD-fed mice, upregulate the expression of intestinal connexin, and then reverse high intestinal permeability and inflammatory cytokines, and ultimately prevent HFD-induced osteopenia [16]. However, individual responses to dietary and microbial interventions can vary substantially due to differences in baseline intestinal microbiota composition, age, genetic background, and habitual dietary intake [262, 263]. Clinical studies on probiotic and synbiotic interventions for bone health typically administer daily doses of 1 × 10⁹ to 1 × 10¹⁰ colony-forming units (CFU) in humans [264, 265], while higher doses (e.g., 1 × 10¹¹ CFU) are mostly investigated in animal models [266]. Intervention durations vary by target outcomes: 8–12 weeks are sufficient to detect changes in bone turnover markers (e.g., CTX) and systemic inflammation, whereas ≥ 6-month interventions are typically required to assess effects on BMD, especially in postmenopausal women [264–266]. Prebiotics are typically administered in doses between 5 and 15 g/day. While generally well-tolerated, high doses of prebiotics may cause gastrointestinal discomfort such as bloating or gas in some individuals [267]. These factors highlight the need for personalized approaches and further clinical trials to optimize intervention strategies for different populations.

Moreover, recent studies have found that exercise can reduce bone loss in male mice caused by HFD, possibly by changing the composition, structure, and abundance of intestinal microbiota to regulate bone [225]. The specific mechanism is that exercise modulates the composition of the intestinal microbiota, which influences bile acid metabolism. This modulation leads to the production of specific bile acids that activate signaling pathways, such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and TGR5, which play key roles in regulating bone metabolism, enhancing osteoblast activity, and reducing osteoclast activity, thereby improving bone health and ameliorating osteopenia [268]. Therefore, changing dietary patterns, reasonable intake of probiotics and prebiotics, and appropriate exercise are significant for protecting bone health.

Nonetheless, individualized assessment and tailored interventions are essential for maximizing clinical efficacy. Precision nutrition strategies incorporating metagenomic profiling may help identify individuals more likely to benefit from specific dietary or microbial interventions. For example, individuals with a Bacteroides-dominant microbiota may derive greater benefits from omega-3 fatty acid supplementation, while those with an overrepresentation of Firmicutes could respond more favorably to interventions designed to promote Akkermansia abundance. Further multicenter clinical trials are needed to validate these personalized strategies [269, 270].

Conclusion

Emerging studies suggest that interactions among the intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and intestinal immune system may jointly contribute to bone homeostasis through complex mechanisms. A review of the literature indicates that, while several animal studies have reported associations among HFD, alterations in intestinal microecology, and bone loss, the causal relationships and underlying biological pathways have yet to be fully elucidated. To date, direct clinical evidence linking HFD-induced bone loss to intestinal microecological changes is lacking. Most human studies have focused on microbial composition and diversity, with epithelial barrier function and immune dynamics largely overlooked. This “single-dimensional trap” hampers a full understanding of the human gut–bone axis. To overcome this limitation and achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the gut–bone axis in humans, future clinical studies should adopt a systematic and integrative approach. This includes the incorporation of multi-omics strategies involving metagenomic sequencing, metabolite profiling, and immune cell characterization; noninvasive assessment of intestinal barrier integrity through serum zonulin levels or intestinal permeability tests; and the design of targeted interventions through microbiota-modulating agents or immune-supportive nutritional regimens. In addition, immunological evaluation based on secretory IgA levels in feces or the distribution of gut-associated lymphocyte subsets in peripheral blood may provide further insight into mucosal immune status under different dietary conditions. This multidimensional framework is essential to advance mechanistic understanding of how dietary factors shape the gut–bone axis and to support clinical research into diet-driven bone health interventions.

While mouse models have been valuable for exploring how HFD may affect bone remodeling through intestinal microecology, directly translating these findings to humans remains challenging due to significant interspecies differences. The human intestinal microenvironment is far more complex than that of mice, not only in terms of microbial diversity, where keystone genera like Faecalibacterium and Akkermansia are often underrepresented or absent in murine models, but also in key physiological aspects, including intestinal transit time, luminal pH, epithelial structure, mucus composition, and immune regulation. These differences can lead to divergent responses to dietary fat, complicating the extrapolation of microecological findings from mice to humans. In addition, fundamental differences in skeletal physiology and bone remodeling dynamics between mice and humans further limit direct applicability. For example, mice have a much higher bone turnover rate, distinct trabecular architecture, and lack Haversian systems in cortical bone, which are prominent in humans. Moreover, hormonal regulation of bone metabolism, mechanical loading patterns, and lifespan-related bone loss trajectories differ substantially across species. Nevertheless, animal studies remain valuable for uncovering fundamental mechanisms across multiple components of the intestinal ecosystem. Mouse models have advanced our understanding of the relationships between intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, intestinal immune system, and bone metabolism. Some of these findings, particularly those related to SCFAs and inflammatory regulation, are now beginning to receive support from emerging data in human cohorts. To improve the translational relevance of future research, studies should incorporate humanized gnotobiotic models, gut-on-a-chip systems, and longitudinal multi-omics profiling across diverse populations.

In addition, the therapeutic potential of microbiota modulation in bone health is still in its infancy. While early studies suggest that probiotics may help restore intestinal balance and improve bone health, there is a lack of well-controlled clinical trials to validate these interventions. Most clinical studies suffer from small sample sizes, insufficient diversity in participants, and a lack of long-term follow-up, limiting their generalizability and reliability. Furthermore, variations in experimental diets and reliance on microbiome sequencing alone complicate the interpretation of findings. To overcome these limitations, it is essential to expand clinical trials to include larger and more diverse populations, standardize dietary interventions, and integrate additional technologies such as genomics, metabolomics, and proteomics. These approaches would enable more comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms linking intestinal health and bone metabolism. Another important challenge is the need for a deeper exploration of the role of the intestinal epithelium and immune system in bone health. While the intestinal microbiota is often the focus of research, the intestinal barrier and immune responses are equally critical in regulating bone remodeling. Disruption of the intestinal epithelial layer can lead to increased intestinal permeability, allowing microbial products like LPS to enter the bloodstream and activate systemic inflammation. This inflammation can, in turn, exacerbate bone loss. Moreover, the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory immune cells in the intestinal, such as Tregs and Th17 cells, is crucial for maintaining both intestinal homeostasis and bone health. Future studies should investigate how these immune responses interact with intestinal microbiota and the epithelial barrier to influence bone metabolism.

In summary, while current research has provided valuable insights into the link between HFD, intestinal microecology, and bone loss, many questions remain unanswered. The underlying mechanisms—particularly how intestinal microbiota, intestinal epithelium, and immune system converge to affect bone health—are still not fully understood. To advance the field, more sophisticated models, larger clinical trials, and the integration of cutting-edge technologies are needed. These efforts will not only clarify the complex interactions between diet, intestinal health, and bone metabolism but will also inform the development of more targeted and effective interventions for preventing and treating bone-related disorders, particularly in aging populations.
