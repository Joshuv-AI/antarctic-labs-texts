Reimagining nursing practice in the era of AI: a qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis of nurses’ lived experiences
Authors: Mohmmadjavad Veisimankali, Khalil Alimohammadzadeh, Ghasem Begloo-Amin, Mohammadkarim Bahadori, Abbas Abbaszadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare is transforming nursing practice, introducing both opportunities and challenges. This qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis examines nurses’ encounters with AI technologies across clinical, managerial, and educational domains. It aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of nurses’ lived experiences with AI technologies across clinical, managerial, and educational domains, identify barriers and facilitators to its integration, and derive an interpretive framework for nurses’ positioning in AI-enhanced care environments.MethodsFollowing PRISMA and ENTREQ guidelines, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase for peer-reviewed qualitative studies from January 2017 to June 2025. The SPIDER framework defined eligibility, focusing on nurses’ experiences with AI. Two reviewers independently screened 284 deduplicated records, with 26 studies included after full-text review. Data were extracted using a tailored form, and quality was assessed via the CASP checklist. A combined meta-ethnography and thematic analysis synthesized findings, generating themes through iterative coding and consensus. Verbatim quotes ensured fidelity to nurses’ voices, with methodological rigor maintained through reflexivity and member checking.ResultsFive themes emerged from 26 studies: (1) Enhancing Clinical Efficiency and Decision-Making, where AI improves risk prediction and workflows; (2) Navigating Barriers to AI Integration, highlighting technical and organizational challenges; (3) Ethical and Cultural Considerations, emphasizing patient autonomy and bias concerns; (4) Evolving Nursing Roles, reflecting shifts to supervisory and technical competencies; and (5) AI’s Role in Enhancing Communication, noting its facilitation and depersonalization risks. Nurses value AI’s efficiency but stress user-friendly design and ethical safeguards. Continuous training is needed to balance technical skills with empathy.ConclusionAI significantly enhances nursing efficiency and decision-making but introduces technical, ethical, and role-related challenges. User-centered AI design, comprehensive training, and ethical frameworks are essential to address barriers and biases. Nurses’ evolving roles require balancing technical proficiency with humanistic care. Future research should explore longitudinal impacts to ensure AI supports equitable, patient-centered nursing practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01356-0.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming the healthcare landscape, reshaping traditional roles, clinical workflows, and decision-making processes across various disciplines. Within this evolving terrain, nursing practice—long anchored in human interaction, clinical judgment, and holistic care—is increasingly intersecting with digital technologies [1]. The global adoption of AI in healthcare has accelerated since 2020, driven by challenges like the COVID-19 pandemic, which highlighted the need for efficient, contactless tools such as predictive analytics and robotic assistance [2]. AI technologies in healthcare range from diagnostic support systems and predictive analytics to robotic assistance and automated documentation, marking a significant departure from manual, time-intensive approaches. As AI technologies become more sophisticated and integrated into hospital infrastructure, it is imperative to examine how these technologies are experienced and perceived by frontline professionals such as nurses [3].

Nurses, as the largest group of healthcare providers globally, play a pivotal role in ensuring the quality, safety, and continuity of patient care. Their practice spans multiple dimensions, including direct clinical care, patient education, emotional support, interdisciplinary coordination, and ethical advocacy [4]. The incorporation of AI into this multifaceted role introduces both opportunities and challenges. On one hand, AI can enhance nursing efficiency by reducing documentation burdens, supporting clinical decisions, and improving early detection of patient deterioration [5].

Despite the growing proliferation of AI technologies in healthcare, the body of literature examining nurses’ real-world encounters with these technologies remains fragmented and heterogeneous. Existing studies vary widely in focus—some explore attitudes and acceptance, while others address practical implementation barriers, ethical dilemmas, or changes in workflow [6–9]. Moreover, most reviews to date have been either technologically centered, examining specific AI tools, or institutionally focused, emphasizing policy implications [10–13] without systematically integrating nurses’ voices. This fragmented landscape necessitates a comprehensive synthesis to consolidate current evidence and foreground the nursing perspective in AI integration.

Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review and meta-synthesis is to provide a comprehensive understanding of how nurses across different healthcare systems experience, adapt to, and critically engage with artificial intelligence technologies. This review not only maps existing evidence but also contributes to theory-building by generating an interpretive framework for understanding nurses’ positioning in AI-enhanced care environments. This study sheds light on nurses’ multifaceted encounters with AI, revealing how it enhances efficiency and decision-making while exposing ethical dilemmas, role shifts, and communication challenges—insights that bridge gaps in the literature and guide the development of nurse-centric AI technologies for equitable, humanistic care [14]. The research is particularly significant given the underrepresentation of nursing-specific perspectives in the AI literature, the global urgency to modernize healthcare delivery. The guiding review question was: “What are the lived experiences of registered nurses with artificial intelligence technologies in clinical, managerial, and educational healthcare settings?” Therefore, the purpose of this qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis is to provide a comprehensive understanding of nurses’ lived experiences with AI technologies across clinical, managerial, and educational domains, identify barriers and facilitators to its integration, and derive an interpretive framework for nurses’ positioning in AI-enhanced care environments.

Methods

This study employed a systematic review and meta-synthesis methodology, combining meta-ethnography [15] with thematic analysis [16], guided by PRISMA and ENTREQ guidelines [17, 18]. The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251125528) prior to commencement. The guiding review question was: “What are the lived experiences of registered nurses with artificial intelligence technologies in clinical, managerial, and educational healthcare settings?” (PICo: Population = registered nurses; Interest = lived experiences/perceptions/encounters with AI; Context = healthcare settings).

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, to identify peer-reviewed articles published in English between January 1, 2017, and June 30, 2025. This timeframe was selected to capture studies reflecting the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in contemporary nursing practice, aligning with the rapid advancement of AI technologies in healthcare. The final search was conducted on June 30, 2025. The timeframe from January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2025, was selected to capture the rapid evolution and widespread integration of contemporary AI technologies (particularly machine learning and deep-learning applications) in healthcare, which accelerated markedly following key technological breakthroughs and the COVID-19 pandemic’s emphasis on digital, contactless solutions. Keywords included “artificial intelligence,” “nursing practice,” “nurse experiences,” “clinical decision-making,” “healthcare technology,” and “ethical considerations.” The search strategy utilized Boolean operators and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) as follows: (“artificial intelligence” [MeSH] OR “AI” OR “machine learning” OR “decision support systems”) AND (“nursing” [MeSH] OR “nurses” OR “nursing practice” OR “nursing care”) AND (“qualitative research” [MeSH] OR “qualitative study” OR “nurse perceptions” OR “nurse experiences”). Synonyms such as “deep learning,” “clinical informatics,” and “nurse perspectives” were included to broaden the search scope.

Search results were imported into EndNote X9 for initial management and duplicate removal, with screening subsequently conducted using Rayyan systematic review software.

Reference lists of included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant articles. Finally, 26 studies were included. The number of records retrieved was 1416. Detailed search strings for each database are provided in Fig. 1.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a tailored extraction form developed for this study. The form was developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis (2020) recommendations for qualitative systematic reviews and meta-synthesis, supplemented by Cochrane Handbook guidance on data extraction for reviews of qualitative evidence [20, 21]. This approach ensured comprehensive and consistent capture of study characteristics while allowing context-specific adaptation relevant to AI technologies in nursing practice. The form captured study characteristics (author, year, country, aim), participant details (number, role, clinical setting), methodology (design, data collection methods), analysis approach, and key findings (themes, sub-themes, participant quotes). The form was pilot-tested on three studies to ensure clarity and consistency. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted for consensus when needed. For mixed-methods studies, only qualitative data (e.g., quotes, thematic findings) were extracted, excluding quantitative metrics to maintain focus on qualitative synthesis. The completed data extraction table is provided as Supplementary file.

Approach to data synthesis

The data synthesis combined the meta-ethnography approach by Noblit and Hare (1988) [15] with the thematic analysis framework by Braun and Clarke (2006) [16].The Noblit and Hare meta-ethnography emphasizes reciprocal translation, synthesizing concepts across studies to create a comprehensive understanding while preserving their contextual meaning. However, challenges in applying reciprocal translation, such as difficulties in selecting a starting study or ensuring conceptual robustness [22, 23], were addressed by integrating Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis to enhance clarity and manage complex data.

The synthesis proceeded in three phases. In the first phase, findings from included studies were imported into MAXQDA (version 2024) and read multiple times to identify meanings and patterns. Manual line-by-line coding was conducted by one researcher, assigning descriptive labels to verbatim quotes and study findings, with their sources referenced. Codes were created to capture the essence of each segment, and similar concepts were grouped under existing codes or new codes as needed. Quotes addressing multiple concepts were assigned to multiple codes. A third researcher reviewed the initial codes to ensure accuracy and consistency. For mixed-methods studies, only qualitative components (e.g., participant quotes, thematic findings) were coded.

In the second phase, codes were systematically compared and organized into sub-themes based on shared meanings. Sub-themes were grouped inductively into main themes, ensuring relevance to the included studies’ concepts and nurses’ AI encounters. Themes were determined through an inductive process, where patterns emerged from the data without preconceived categories, refined via iterative discussions among all authors to ensure alignment with primary study findings and nurses’ voiced experiences. This process involved iterative discussions among all authors to refine sub-themes and ensure meaningful connections to the data.

In the third phase, a “line of argument” synthesis was conducted to develop analytical themes, elucidating relationships between concepts and providing theoretical insights into nurses’ experiences with AI. This step involved all authors, with consensus reached through discussion to ensure themes captured the complexity of the data. The resulting themes were supported by evidence from included studies, with direct quotes illustrating key findings. This combined approach ensured a robust synthesis that balanced interpretive depth with structured thematic organization, clarifying the opportunities, challenges, and implications of AI in nursing practice. Quality appraisal

Each study was independently evaluated by two reviewers using the CASP qualitative checklist [24], assessing study objectives, methodology, reflexivity, ethical considerations, data analysis rigor, and findings clarity. Reviewers rated items as ‘Yes,’ ‘Can’t tell,’ or ‘No,’ resolving disagreements through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. All studies were included in the synthesis regardless of quality scores to preserve diverse insights, with the appraisal enhancing interpretive rigor. Quality appraisal results are included in the supplementary file.

Screening process

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts of 284 records post-deduplication against inclusion/exclusion criteria, including hand-searching of reference lists of included studies, as reflected in Fig. 1. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted as needed. Of these, 38 records underwent full-text review. Exclusions included: 5 studies for inappropriate populations (e.g., non-nurses), 4 for irrelevant outcomes (e.g., technical AI focus), and 3 for lacking peer review. Ultimately, 26 studies were included in the final synthesis (Fig. 1).

Methodological rigor

The study prioritized verbatim quotes from primary studies to ensure fidelity to nurses’ experiences. The research team, comprising members with expertise in qualitative methods and nursing, maintained a reflexive journal to document biases and ensure interpretations aligned with primary data. Member checking was conducted through a webinar with practicing nurses, who confirmed the findings’ resonance with their AI experiences. This process, combined with graduate-level training in qualitative research, ensured transparency and adherence to methodological principles.

Results

Overall characteristics of included studies

The systematic review included 26 qualitative studies published between 2017 and 2025 (20 studies in 2024–2025, reflecting the recent surge in AI adoption in nursing). Twenty studies used purely qualitative designs (primarily descriptive qualitative, phenomenological, or interpretive approaches), while six employed mixed-methods designs with a robust qualitative component. Sample sizes ranged from 8 to 114 participants (median = 23), with a total of 678 nurses and nursing professionals across all studies. Participants included registered nurses (frontline staff, n = 15 studies), nurse managers/leaders (n = 5), nursing educators/students (n = 6), and interdisciplinary teams. Studies were conducted in 11 countries, with the highest representation from Türkiye (n = 4), China (n = 3), Egypt (n = 3), Saudi Arabia (n = 3), and Bangladesh (n = 2); the remainder spanned Sweden, Germany, Canada, the Netherlands, Australia, the UK, the USA, South Korea, Spain, and Iran. Settings included acute care hospitals (ICU, emergency, surgical, cardiology, neonatal; n = 14), home healthcare and long-term care facilities (n = 4), nursing education/simulation laboratories (n = 5), and regulatory or mixed environments (n = 3). AI technologies examined encompassed clinical decision support systems/predictive analytics (n = 12), nursing/assistant robots (n = 5), chatbots/large language models (n = 4), AI sensors for monitoring (n = 3), and wound-care or triage-specific tools (n = 2). Fields of nursing covered general/clinical practice (n = 12), critical/neonatal care (n = 6), wound/perioperative care (n = 3), education (n = 4), and management/regulation (n = 1). Detailed characteristics for each study, including author/year, country, design, sample size and participants, data collection method, setting, AI technology, field of nursing, and key findings, are provided in Supplementary File. These studies collectively represent a broad, globally diverse evidence base that underpins the meta-synthesis.

AI technologies examined included clinical decision support systems, predictive analytics, nursing robots, and chatbots, applied in fields such as critical care, wound care, obstetrics, and nursing education.

Theme 1: enhancing clinical efficiency and decision-making

AI significantly enhances nursing practice by leveraging advanced data processing, predictive analytics, and automation to improve clinical decision-making and operational efficiency. This theme captures how AI empowers nurses to deliver high-quality, timely care by providing real-time insights, reducing manual workloads, and aligning interventions with evidence-based standards across diverse settings [25–32].

Subtheme 1.1: improved situational awareness and risk prediction

AI tools, including clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and predictive models, enhance nurses’ ability to anticipate patient risks by analyzing extensive datasets. These systems provide early warnings for conditions like acute kidney injury or pressure injuries, enabling timely interventions. In critical care, AI-generated early warning scores improve patient safety, while in geriatric care, AI offers comprehensive patient overviews, facilitating rapid assessment [25, 27, 28, 31, 32].

“If you see that these people are at a risk for an AKI or something, I would be more apt to ask about the urine output or their last [hemato]crit values.” [25].

“We will enter our patient’s data, such as age, weight, and comorbidities, and the system will automatically generate an individualized PI risk score for us.” [27].

Subtheme 1.2: streamlining workflows and reducing workload

AI automates repetitive tasks like documentation and monitoring, allowing nurses to focus on direct patient care. Technologies such as speech-to-text documentation and AI-driven chatbots provide rapid guidance in emergencies, enhancing efficiency. During the COVID-19 pandemic, AI-enabled sensors and robots reduced physical contact with patients, improving safety and operational efficiency [26, 27, 29, 30, 33].

“AI…frees me up to spend more time with patients…It lets me focus…like talking to patients and creating care plans.” [26].

“If there is a talk-to-text feature, it can make our documentation process much faster and more efficient and greatly reduce our workload.” [27].

Subtheme 1.3: enhancing evidence-based practice

AI integrates clinical guidelines with patient-specific data to deliver tailored, evidence-based recommendations, ensuring consistent and high-quality care. Nurses benefit from AI-driven suggestions for interventions like wound care or medication adjustments, which align with current research and reduce variability in practice [27, 32, 34].

“The system pulls in evidence-based guidelines and patient history to suggest the best wound care approach.” [32].

Theme 2: navigating barriers to AI integration

AI adoption in nursing faces technical, usability, and organizational challenges that hinder its seamless integration into clinical workflows. This theme explores the barriers nurses encounter, emphasizing the need for improved training, user-friendly interfaces, and organizational support to maximize AI’s benefits [25, 26, 31, 35–38].

Subtheme 2.1: cognitive burden associated with AI interfaces and outputs

Nurses report difficulties with AI tools due to insufficient training, non-intuitive interfaces, technical issues like unreliable networks, and excessive alerts or complex data outputs that overwhelm them in high-pressure settings. Accessibility concerns, such as designs incompatible with visual impairments, further complicate adoption. Frequent notifications, such as pop-up alerts, are often ignored, reducing effectiveness, while unclear or dense data reports can obscure critical information, leading to frustration and diminished trust. Integrating AI outputs into electronic health records (EHRs) remains challenging, as nurses have varied preferences for data presentation [25, 35–37, 39].

“Lack of knowledge and dealing with AI [is a barrier].” [35].

“It was very interesting! Yet, it drew my attention because I am colourblind and it might be interesting to consider colours when designing the layout of resources like this.” [36].

“If it was a popup message, it probably wouldn’t be as nice to have than if it’s on the side … because sometimes stuff just pops up all the time on the computer, and … you just exit out of it.” [25].

“AI data and reports are sometimes confusing. We need clear explanations in plain language to use AI effectively in patient care.” [26].

Subtheme 2.2: resistance and organizational barriers

Resistance to AI arises from fears of job displacement, unfamiliarity, and lack of organizational support. Nurses often feel unsupported due to limited training resources or insufficient management commitment. Fostering a technology-positive culture and investing in education are crucial to reducing resistance [35, 37].

“Some nurses are hesitant because they think AI might take over their roles.” [37].

Theme 3: ethical and cultural considerations in AI use

AI adoption raises complex ethical and cultural issues, including patient autonomy, algorithmic bias, and cultural competence. This theme examines how nurses navigate these challenges to ensure AI supports equitable, patient-centered care. Nurses advocate for transparency, accountability, and culturally sensitive AI design to maintain trust and fairness in healthcare delivery, particularly for diverse populations [26, 31, 40–42].

Subtheme 3.1: preserving patient-centered care and autonomy

Nurses emphasize that AI should enhance, not replace, human judgment to prioritize patient preferences and autonomy. In sensitive settings like geriatric or pediatric care, AI recommendations must align with patients’ and families’ values. Transparency about AI’s role is critical to maintaining trust and preserving the nurse-patient relationship, ensuring care remains patient-centered [26, 31, 40].

“The patient’s wishes should be considered. Or, if the patient can no longer decide for themselves, the relatives in any case.” [40].

“Patient autonomy is key…AI informs, but doesn’t replace, advocating for patients’ wishes in care.” [26].

Subtheme 3.2: addressing bias and cultural sensitivity

AI algorithms trained on non-diverse datasets risk perpetuating biases, leading to inequitable care for marginalized groups. Nurses report that some AI tools fail to account for cultural nuances, such as specific health beliefs, which can compromise clinical accuracy. Developing AI with diverse training data and cultural competence is essential for equitable care delivery [26, 41, 42].

“AI bias worries me. Biased training data can lead to unfair care. We need diverse data to ensure AI fairness.” [26].

“KATETM is simply looking at patient’s vital signs, to determine the acuity. So that made me very skeptical of KATETM.” [41].

Subtheme 3.3: ethical accountability and transparency

Nurses stress the need for clear accountability in AI-driven decisions to prevent ethical lapses. Understanding how AI generates recommendations is critical for explaining decisions to patients and ensuring accuracy. Concerns about liability highlight the importance of transparent documentation of AI’s role in care, fostering trust and ethical integrity [26, 31].

“We need to know exactly how AI reached its conclusions to explain it to patients and avoid errors.” [31].

Theme 4: evolving nursing roles and responsibilities

AI is profoundly reshaping nursing roles, requiring nurses to adapt to new responsibilities while preserving the empathetic core of their profession. This theme explores how nurses transition to supervisory and collaborative roles, develop technical competencies, and balance technology with human connection. As AI becomes integral to healthcare, nurses must navigate a dynamic landscape where they oversee technology, collaborate across disciplines, and uphold the relational aspects of care. This evolution demands continuous learning, adaptability, and a commitment to maintaining the human essence of nursing, ensuring that technology enhances rather than overshadows patient-centered practice [26, 30, 33, 35, 37, 43].

Subtheme 4.1: shifting to supervisory and collaborative roles

As AI integrates into clinical practice, nurses increasingly take on supervisory roles, validating AI outputs to ensure their accuracy and relevance. This shift involves critically evaluating AI recommendations, such as risk scores or treatment suggestions, before incorporating them into care plans. Nurses also collaborate with interdisciplinary teams, including IT specialists and data analysts, to optimize AI use, particularly in complex scenarios like the COVID-19 pandemic, where technologies like robots required coordinated efforts. This role enhances nurses’ expertise, positioning them as key decision-makers in technology-driven care environments [26, 33, 38].

“With AI, my role is more oversight…I ensure AI outputs make sense before using them in patient care.” [26].

“Clear communication and teamwork are important for supporting AI integration.” [33].

Subtheme 4.2: balancing technology and human touch

Nurses underscore that AI cannot replicate the empathy, compassion, and relational skills central to nursing. While AI enhances technical efficiency, over-reliance risks diminishing the human connection that defines quality care. In educational settings, AI tools like chatbots improve clinical skills but may neglect soft skills like empathy, requiring careful integration to preserve holistic care. Nurses advocate for using AI as a supportive tool to complement, not replace, their ability to provide emotional support and build trust with patients, particularly in culturally sensitive or emotionally complex situations [35, 37, 43].

“I think it would be better if the chatbot could provide us with some opportunities to improve other skills except history-taking, like using the correct words and phrases to comfort the patient.” [43].

“AI will never be a replacement for real nurses.” [35].

Subtheme 4.3: developing new competencies

AI integration demands that nurses acquire advanced technical skills, such as interpreting complex AI outputs, troubleshooting system issues, and navigating digital interfaces. Continuous professional development is critical to ensure nurses remain confident in using AI tools effectively. This includes training in data literacy, AI system management, and critical evaluation of algorithmic outputs. As healthcare becomes increasingly technology-driven, nurses must bridge traditional clinical expertise with digital proficiency, ensuring they can leverage AI to enhance care while maintaining their professional standards [30, 37].

“We need ongoing training to keep up with AI advancements and use them confidently.” [30].

Theme 5: AI’s role in enhancing communication

AI significantly influences communication dynamics in nursing, both facilitating and complicating interactions among nurses, patients, and interprofessional teams. This theme explores how AI tools enhance information sharing, streamline handoffs, and bridge communication gaps, while also posing challenges due to design limitations and the need for human-like interaction. Nurses value AI’s ability to improve clarity and efficiency in communication but emphasize the importance of user-centered design to ensure tools support meaningful interactions without depersonalizing care. This dual role of AI as both a facilitator and a potential barrier underscores the need for thoughtful implementation to optimize communication in clinical practice [44–49].

Subtheme 5.1: impact on interprofessional and patient communication

AI enhances communication by improving situational awareness and streamlining information exchange among healthcare teams and with patients. Tools like AI-driven handoff systems prioritize critical patient information, enabling clearer and more focused discussions during care transitions. In settings like critical care and emergency nursing, AI supports decision-making by providing real-time data, fostering better collaboration among interprofessional teams. For patient communication, AI tools like chatbots or video technologies bridge gaps, particularly in telehealth or remote care, by offering accessible platforms for interaction. However, nurses report mixed outcomes, noting that some AI technologies lack the depth needed for nuanced communication, prompting calls for enhanced interaction capabilities to better align with clinical and patient needs [44, 45, 49].

“AI → general situational awareness…I think it makes you more present to the physical patient as well.” [45].

“I would have liked…an option where I could select ‘find out more’.” [49].

Subtheme 5.2: AI tools as communication facilitators or barriers

The design of AI tools significantly influences their effectiveness as communication facilitators. Nurses appreciate user-friendly interfaces that enhance efficiency, such as speech-to-text documentation, which streamlines record-keeping and allows more time for patient interaction. However, poorly designed tools or those lacking human-like interaction can hinder communication, making interactions feel mechanical or impersonal. Nurses express a preference for AI technologies that mimic natural communication patterns and incorporate intuitive features, such as customizable interfaces or culturally sensitive outputs. Innovations like real-time translation or interactive chatbots are seen as promising, but their success depends on user-centered design that prioritizes clarity, accessibility, and empathy in communication [46–48].

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-synthesis illuminates the multifaceted encounters of nurses with artificial intelligence (AI) technologies across clinical, managerial, and educational domains, synthesizing findings from 26 qualitative studies. The five emergent themes—Enhancing Clinical Efficiency and Decision-Making, Navigating Barriers to AI Integration, Ethical and Cultural Considerations in AI Use, Evolving Nursing Roles and Responsibilities, and AI’s Role in Enhancing Communication—provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how AI is reshaping nursing practice. These findings underscore AI’s transformative potential to augment clinical efficiency and decision-making, while highlighting critical challenges related to implementation, ethical concerns, role adaptation, and communication dynamics. This discussion situates these themes within the broader context of nursing practice, healthcare delivery, and technological advancement, offering insights into their implications for practice, policy, education, and future research.

The first theme highlights AI’s capacity to enhance nursing efficiency through advanced data processing, predictive analytics, and automation. AI-driven tools, such as clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and predictive models, enable nurses to anticipate patient risks, streamline workflows, and align interventions with evidence-based standards. For instance, it was demonstrated how AI-generated early warning scores and individualized risk assessments improve patient safety in critical and geriatric care settings. These findings align with prior literature suggesting that AI can reduce cognitive burdens and enhance situational awareness [50]. However, the reliance on AI for decision-making raises questions about over-dependence, as nurses must retain clinical judgment to validate AI outputs. This balance is critical to ensure that AI serves as a supportive tool rather than a replacement for professional expertise, a concern echoed in studies emphasizing the importance of human oversight [51]. The integration of AI into evidence-based practice also suggests a shift toward data-driven care, which could standardize interventions but risks overlooking patient-specific nuances if not carefully managed.

The second theme reveals significant barriers to AI adoption, including technical challenges, information overload, and organizational resistance. Nurses frequently report difficulties with non-intuitive interfaces, insufficient training, and unreliable systems, which hinder seamless integration into clinical workflows. These findings resonate with existing research, which identified resistance to change as a pervasive issue in nursing due to inadequate training and support [52]. Alarm fatigue and excessive data outputs further complicate AI use, as frequent or unclear alerts can overwhelm nurses in high-pressure environments [53]. This issue underscores the need for user-centered design principles that prioritize intuitive interfaces and clear data presentation [54]. Organizational barriers, such as limited management commitment and fears of job displacement in the included studies, highlight the importance of fostering a technology-positive culture [55]. These challenges suggest that successful AI integration requires not only technological refinement but also robust organizational strategies, including comprehensive training programs and leadership support to build confidence and reduce resistance.

The third theme underscores the ethical and cultural complexities of AI adoption in nursing. Nurses emphasize the importance of preserving patient-centered care and autonomy, advocating for AI to complement rather than replace human judgment. This aligns with ethical frameworks proposed by existing research, which stress the need for transparency and accountability in AI-driven decisions [56]. Concerns about algorithmic bias that were introduced in the included studies, particularly in tools trained on non-diverse datasets, highlight the risk of inequitable care for marginalized populations. For example, it was noted that AI technologies, which rely solely on vital signs, may fail to account for cultural or contextual factors, leading to skepticism about their accuracy [57]. These findings underscore the need for culturally competent AI design, incorporating diverse training data to ensure equitable outcomes. Transparency in AI processes is also critical, as nurses require clear explanations of algorithmic recommendations to maintain trust and accountability [58]. These ethical considerations are pivotal for aligning AI with nursing’s humanistic foundations, ensuring that technological advancements do not compromise patient dignity or fairness.

The fourth theme illustrates how AI is reshaping nursing roles, positioning nurses as supervisors of technology, collaborators in interdisciplinary teams, and lifelong learners adapting to new competencies. The shift to supervisory roles, where nurses validate AI outputs, enhances their decision-making authority but demands critical evaluation skills [12, 19]. This evolution aligns with existing research that noted nurses’ pivotal role in coordinating AI-driven technologies during the COVID-19 pandemic [59]. However, balancing technical proficiency with the human touch remains a core challenge, as nurses in the included studies stress that AI cannot replicate empathy or relational care. This tension reflects broader debates about the depersonalization of care in technology-driven healthcare systems [60]. The need for continuous professional development, including data literacy and AI system management, underscores the dynamic nature of nursing in an AI-enhanced landscape [61]. These findings suggest that nursing education must evolve to include digital competencies while reinforcing the profession’s empathetic core, ensuring that nurses remain central to patient-centered care.

The fifth theme highlights AI’s dual role as both a facilitator and a potential barrier to communication in nursing. AI-driven tools, such as handoff systems and chatbots, improve information sharing and situational awareness, particularly in critical care and telehealth settings. However, poorly designed interfaces or mechanical interactions can depersonalize communication, reducing its effectiveness [62]. Nurses’ preference for user-friendly, culturally sensitive AI tools underscores the importance of human-centered design. Meanwhile, AI technologies that mimic natural communication patterns to enhance clinical interactions [63]. The mixed outcomes of AI in communication highlight the need for iterative design processes that involve nurses as end-users to ensure tools are intuitive, accessible, and aligned with clinical needs.

In comparison to other AI technologies, such as those in diagnostic medicine (e.g., AI for radiology imaging) or general healthcare (e.g., AI chatbots for patient triage), nursing-specific AI like robots and decision support systems emphasize humanistic integration, with greater focus on ethical biases and role shifts—unlike physician-centric AI, which often prioritizes accuracy over relational care (e.g., as seen in studies on AI in oncology diagnostics) [64, 65]. This highlights nursing’s unique need for AI that balances efficiency with empathy, differing from more algorithmic-focused applications in other fields.

The findings have significant implications for nursing practice, policy, and research. In practice, healthcare organizations must prioritize user-centered AI design, incorporating nurses’ feedback to address usability challenges and reduce cognitive overload. Training programs should focus on building technical competencies while reinforcing ethical and cultural awareness to ensure AI supports equitable, patient-centered care. Policymakers should advocate for regulations that mandate transparency and accountability in AI algorithms, addressing biases and ensuring cultural competence. Future research should explore longitudinal impacts of AI on nursing roles, patient outcomes, and healthcare equity, particularly in underrepresented settings. Comparative studies across diverse healthcare systems could further elucidate contextual factors influencing AI adoption. Additionally, interdisciplinary research involving nurses, data scientists, and ethicists is needed to develop AI technologies that align with nursing’s humanistic values.

Recommendations for nursing practice, policy, and education

Based on the meta-synthesis of nurses’ lived experiences, the following actionable recommendations are offered:

Nursing practice: Healthcare organizations should implement user-centered AI design processes that incorporate frontline nurses as co-designers and mandate intuitive interfaces, plain-language explanations of AI outputs, and customizable alert systems to reduce cognitive burden and alert fatigue.Policy: Regulatory bodies should require algorithmic transparency, bias audits using diverse datasets, and clear accountability frameworks that designate nurses as the final clinical decision-makers when AI tools are used.Education: Nursing curricula and continuing professional development programs must integrate mandatory AI literacy modules (data interpretation, critical evaluation of outputs, ethical reasoning) while preserving emphasis on humanistic care and empathy.Implementation: Organizations should establish interdisciplinary AI governance committees that include nurse leaders and conduct regular training and simulation exercises before full deployment of AI tools. These recommendations directly translate the five emergent themes into concrete strategies to ensure AI enhances rather than undermines humanistic nursing practice.

Limitations

This study has somre limitations. First, the inclusion of only English-language peer-reviewed studies may introduce language bias, potentially excluding valuable insights from non-English literature. Second, the qualitative focus limits generalizability to quantitative outcomes or broader populations, as findings are interpretive and context-specific. Finally, reliance on secondary data from primary studies could be influenced by publication bias, where positive or novel AI experiences are overrepresented. Third, the meta-synthesis method itself carries inherent limitations, such as the subjective nature of interpretive synthesis, which relies on researchers’ perspectives to translate and integrate concepts across studies, potentially introducing interpretive bias despite efforts like reflexivity and consensus-building [15, 16]. Fourth, heterogeneity across the included studies—in terms of AI tools (e.g., ranging from predictive analytics to nursing robots), settings (e.g., critical care vs. educational environments), and nurse roles (e.g., frontline clinicians vs. managers)—may affect the depth and uniformity of theme synthesis, although this diversity strengthens the review’s breadth. Fifth, potential publication bias exists, as reliance on secondary data from primary studies could overrepresent positive or novel AI experiences, while geographic bias is evident with limited representation from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where AI adoption may face unique socioeconomic and infrastructural challenges not fully captured here. Finally, variability in study quality, as assessed by the CASP checklist, means that some included studies had methodological weaknesses (e.g., limited reflexivity or sample diversity), which could influence the robustness of the synthesized findings; however, all studies were retained to preserve diverse perspectives, with quality considerations informing interpretive rigor.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-synthesis of 26 qualitative studies reveals the transformative impact of AI on nursing practice, encapsulated in five key themes: Enhancing Clinical Efficiency and Decision-Making, Navigating Barriers to AI Integration, Ethical and Cultural Considerations in AI Use, Evolving Nursing Roles and Responsibilities, and AI’s Role in Enhancing Communication. AI enhances nursing efficiency through predictive analytics, automation, and evidence-based recommendations, improving patient safety and reducing workloads. However, barriers such as technical challenges, information overload, and organizational resistance hinder seamless adoption. Ethical concerns, including algorithmic bias and threats to patient autonomy, underscore the need for transparent and culturally sensitive AI technologies. Nurses are adapting to new roles as supervisors and collaborators, balancing technical proficiency with empathy, while AI’s impact on communication highlights its potential to streamline interactions alongside risks of depersonalization. These findings provide a nuanced understanding of nurses’ lived experiences with AI, highlighting both its potential and its challenges in reshaping clinical practice. To maximize AI’s benefits while addressing its challenges, healthcare organizations should invest in comprehensive training programs that equip nurses with technical and ethical competencies, fostering confidence in AI use. AI developers must prioritize user-centered design, incorporating nurses’ feedback to create intuitive, culturally sensitive tools that minimize cognitive overload and enhance communication. Policymakers should establish regulatory frameworks to ensure algorithmic transparency, mitigate biases, and promote equitable care delivery. Nursing education curricula should integrate digital literacy and AI-related skills while emphasizing the preservation of empathy and patient-centered care. Stakeholders must act urgently to translate these insights into actionable strategies, ensuring AI evolves as a true partner in nursing. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to assess AI’s long-term impact on nursing roles, patient outcomes, and healthcare equity, particularly in diverse and resource-limited settings. By aligning AI development with nursing’s humanistic values and implementing the recommendations outlined above, stakeholders can ensure that technological advancements enhance rather than undermine the essence of nursing practice.
