Bone mineral density, prolactin, estradiol, testosterone levels and related research in male chronic patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Pan Jing, Yili Zhang, Xiaopeng Yin, Ye Fu, Jianjun Su, Qinmei Yuan, Xicheng Liu, Xiaofen Zhu, Zhongfeng Zhang, Qing Tian, Xiaobin Zhang, Fei Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSchizophrenia is a severe mental disorder that often requires long-term oral administration of antipsychotic medication. Long-term use of antipsychotic drugs has been associated with alterations in bone mineral density (BMD) and hormone levels, increasing the risk of femoral neck fractures. This study sought to investigate changes in BMD and serum levels of prolactin (PRL), estradiol (E2), and testosterone (T) in male patients with chronic schizophrenia, and the correlations between BMD and these hormonal parameters.MethodsA total of 83 male schizophrenia patients (hospitalization duration ≥ 5 years) who were long-term inpatients at Yangzhou Wutaishan Hospital, and 62 age-matched male normal controls were enrolled. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was used to assess the psychiatric symptoms of the schizophrenia patients. Quantitative Computed Tomography (QCT) was used to measure lumbar spine BMD in all participants. A chemiluminescence immunoassay was used to measure serum levels of PRL, E2, and T.ResultsMean BMD was significantly lower in the patient group (n = 83; 108.8 ± 26.9 mg/cm³) than in the control group (n = 62; 124.5 ± 27.1 mg/cm³ p < 0.01). Within the patient group, normal bone mass was observed in 32 cases (38.6%), osteopenia in 36 (43.4%), and osteoporosis in 15 (18.1%), while the control group comprised 39 cases (62.9%) with normal bone mass, 18 (29.0%) with osteopenia, and 5 (8.1%) with osteoporosis, representing a significant intergroup difference (p < 0.05). Furthermore, serum PRL concentrations were significantly higher in patients (28.8 ± 11.7 ng/ml) than in controls (13.6 ± 10.2 ng/ml; p < 0.01), whereas serum E2 concentrations were significantly lower in the patient group (55.2 ± 14.0 pmol/L) relative to controls (72.4 ± 16.6 pmol/L; p < 0.01). No significant difference was observed between the patient and control groups regarding serum T concentrations (13.5 ± 3.8 nmol/L vs. 14.6 ± 4.3 nmol/L; p > 0.05). Based on serum PRL concentrations, the patient group was further divided into a hyperprolactinemia subgroup and a normoprolactinemia subgroup. PRL levels differed significantly across these two subgroups and the control group (p < 0.01). BMD values were 102.5 ± 25.7 mg/cm³ in the hyperprolactinemic subgroup, 114.9 ± 26.9 mg/cm³ in the normoprolactinemic subgroup, and 124.5 ± 27.1 mg/cm³ in controls, with overall significant variation among the three (p < 0.01). Post-hoc LSD comparisons revealed that BMD in hyperprolactinemic patients was significantly lower than in both normoprolactinemic patients (p < 0.01) and controls (p < 0.01). Besides, serum E2 and T levels differed significantly across these three groups (p < 0.01). Correlation analysis showed that in the chronic male schizophrenia patient group, BMD was significantly negatively correlated with serum PRL (r = -0.366, p = 0.001), and significantly positively correlated with serum E2 (r = 0.248, p = 0.024) and T (r = 0.370, p = 0.001).ConclusionsBMD in male patients with chronic schizophrenia was significantly lower than that in the normal control group, indicating that schizophrenia patients on long-term antipsychotic medication may be more susceptible to increased bone metabolism, osteopenia, or osteoporosis compared to the general population. A potential contributing factor to the decrease in BMD in chronic male schizophrenia patients may be the elevated serum PRL levels, which could lead to alterations in estrogen levels within the body, thereby further affecting bone metabolism. There may be significant correlations between serum PRL, T, E2 levels, and BMD in male schizophrenia patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Bone mineral density, Prolactin, Estradiol, Testosterone

Introduction

Schizophrenia represents a prevalent psychiatric condition encountered frequently in clinical settings. It typically presents with a gradual onset during young adulthood, and often follows a chronic, protracted course characterized by a high relapse rate. The main clinical manifestations involve multifaceted impairments in perception, thinking, emotion, and behavior. These deficits profoundly impact daily functioning and social integration, placing a heavy burden on the individuals, their families, and society as a whole [1–3]. The global lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia reportedly ranges from 3.8‰ to 8.4‰ [4]. Given that mental disorders account for approximately 8% of the global total disease burden, schizophrenia remains a major public health issue [5–7].

The primary treatment for schizophrenia is antipsychotic pharmacotherapy, which typically requires long-term administration. Research has found that the incidence of certain physical diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, metabolic syndrome, sexual dysfunction, and osteoporosis, is higher among individuals with schizophrenia compared to the general population [8, 9]. Among these, osteoporosis represents a critical threat to physical health by increasing the risk of fractures. Specifically, the risk of femoral neck fractures in patients with schizophrenia is typically twice that of the general population [10].

Specific antipsychotic medications (such as risperidone and sulpiride) can induce hyperprolactinemia. This antagonism is mediated by dopamine D2 receptor antagonism in the anterior pituitary gland, thereby reducing dopamine’s inhibitory effect on prolactin secretion [11]. Elevated prolactin (PRL) levels are now understood to inhibit the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus via the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. This suppression subsequently leads to hypogonadism, reduced secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and ultimately, decreased secretion of estradiol (E2), testosterone (T), and progesterone. Estrogen has been established as a critical regulator of human bone metabolism. It inhibits osteoclast activity and promotes osteoblast expression and type I collagen production. Estrogen also influences the synthesis of 25-hydroxyvitamin D and the intestinal absorption of calcium. A decrease in estrogen levels may consequently increase a patient’s risk of developing osteoporosis [12–14]. Current evidence suggests that T also influences osteoblast activity, thereby influencing bone formation. Low levels of testosterone are associated with osteopenia and/or osteoporosis [15–18].

Notably, prolactin exerts sexually dimorphic effects on bone metabolism. To avoid the confounding influence of cyclic estrogen fluctuations and to isolate the specific pathway of D2 receptor antagonism-mediated Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal (HPG) axis inhibition, the present study focused exclusively on male subjects. We conducted a comparative analysis of male patients with chronic schizophrenia and healthy controls to delineate the relationship between bone mineral density (BMD) and the serum profiles of PRL, E2, and T.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

The subjects were screened from male inpatients diagnosed with schizophrenia who were undergoing long-term hospitalization in the psychiatric ward of Yangzhou Wutaishan Hospital. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yangzhou Wutaishan Hospital. All participants were informed of the study’s content, and written informed consent was obtained from either the enrolled participants or their legal guardians. Male patients (aged 30–60 years) were eligible for inclusion if they were right-handed, had an elementary-level education or higher, and met the diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [19]. Participants were required to have a hospitalization and continuous monotherapy duration of ≥ 5 years, with the cognitive capacity to independently complete clinical assessments. Exclusion criteria comprised a history of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), comorbid organic mental disorders, intellectual disabilities, or other severe psychiatric and physical conditions, including central nervous system, cardiopulmonary, or sensory impairments (e.g., color blindness or hearing loss). Furthermore, individuals with a history of alcohol or substance dependence, those receiving bone-modulating therapies (such as glucocorticoids), and patients who were immobilized or bedridden for extended periods were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 83 patients were enrolled based on the above criteria. The healthy control group comprised 62 male residents from Yangzhou City (aged 30–60 years) with at least an elementary education. Eligible participants were required to have no personal or family history of psychiatric disorders. Additional exclusion criteria included central nervous system diseases, severe physical illnesses, and alcohol or substance dependence. Furthermore, individuals with sensory impairments (color blindness or hearing loss), a history of bone metabolism disorders, or recent use of medications known to affect bone mineral density were excluded to ensure a representative baseline for comparison.

Methods

Collection of clinical data

Two senior psychiatrists conducted clinical assessments of the patient group based on the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia. Detailed general information (including age, years of education, smoking status, height, and weight) was collected for both the patient and control groups. Besides, for the patient group, data on age of onset, disease duration, and the type and dose of antipsychotic medication were collected. The doses of all antipsychotic medications were converted to chlorpromazine equivalents.

Assessment methods for psychiatric symptoms

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was employed to assess the presence and severity of psychotic symptoms in the patients [20]. The PANSS consists of three subscales: the Positive Scale (7 items), the Negative Scale (7 items), and the General Psychopathology Scale (16 items). The assessment was conducted comprehensively based on information provided by the patients and their relatives, as well as clinical psychiatric examinations. Symptoms were rated on a 7-point scale of increasing psychopathological severity: (1) absent, (2) minimal, (3) mild, (4) moderate, (5) moderate-severe, (6) severe, (7) extreme. Higher scores indicated more severe psychiatric symptoms.

BMD measurement

BMD was measured using quantitative computed tomography (QCT) on a Toshiba Aquilion 64-slice CT scanner [21, 22]. A Mindways 5-sample solid QCT phantom was placed beneath the subject’s lower back and lumbar region during a spiral scan of the lumbar spine. The acquired data were transferred to a Mindways QCTpro workstation for analysis and three-dimensional image reconstruction. BMD was measured in the trabecular bone of the L2 to L4 vertebrae. The BMD value for each subject was obtained by averaging the measurements from these three vertebrae. All lumbar QCT examinations were performed by the same attending radiologist. The measured BMD values were compared with age-matched normal reference values. Based on lumbar QCT measurements, osteoporosis was defined as a mean BMD < 80 mg/cm³, osteopenia as 80–120 mg/cm³, and normal BMD as > 120 mg/cm³.

Measurement of PRL, E2, and T Levels

Fasting venous blood (5 ml) was collected from the antecubital vein of all subjects between 8:00 and 9:00 a.m. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 r/min for 10 min. The separated serum was then aliquoted into 0.5 ml centrifuge tubes and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. Serum levels of PRL, E2, and T were measured using a Beckman Coulter Access 2 chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Beckman-coulter Inc., USA). Patients were divided into an elevated-PRL group (PRL ≥ 25 ng/ml) and a normal-PRL group (PRL < 25 ng/ml) based on their serum prolactin levels. All measurements were performed by specialized personnel in the hospital’s clinical laboratory. The intra-assay coefficient of variation was less than 4%, and the inter-assay coefficient of variation was less than 7%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in qualitative data between groups were compared using the chi-square test. We had performed Shapiro-Wilk normality tests and clarified in the Statistical Analysis subsection that years of education, duration of treatment, PANSS negative score, general psychopathology score, PANSS total score, and chlorpromazine equivalents were non-normally distributed (p < 0.05), thus analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests. All other continuous variables were normally distributed and analyzed using independent t-tests. One-way ANOVA was used to compare differences among the three groups (with the LSD method for pairwise comparisons), and correlation analysis was performed using Pearson or Spearman’s rank correlation. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to identify risk factors associated with BMD. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of general characteristics

The patient cohort comprised 83 individuals with a mean age of 46.5 ± 5.0 years and an average educational attainment of 9.3 ± 2.1 years. The mean BMI was 24.4 ± 2.2 kg/m², and the ratio of smokers to non-smokers was 53:30. The mean age of disease onset was 22.1 ± 3.6 years, with a mean disease duration of 24.4 ± 6.3 years. The antipsychotic medications used were sulpiride (n = 17), risperidone (n = 20), chlorpromazine (n = 24), perphenazine (n = 13), and clozapine (n = 9). The mean chlorpromazine equivalent dose was 525.8 ± 196.7 mg/d. The healthy control group comprised 62 subjects, with a mean age of 45.2 ± 3.8 years, a mean education duration of 9.0 ± 2.9 years, and a mean BMI of 24.2 ± 3.0 kg/m². The ratio of smokers to non-smokers was 39:23. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, years of education, BMI, or smoking status (p > 0.05). The BMD was significantly lower in the patient group (108.8 ± 26.9 mg/cm³) compared with the healthy control group (124.5 ± 27.1 mg/cm³) (p < 0.01). Significant differences in bone mass distribution were observed between groups (p < 0.05): in the patient group (n = 83), 38.6% (32) had normal bone mass, 43.3% (36) had osteopenia, and 18.0% (15) had osteoporosis. Conversely, the healthy control group (n = 62) showed higher rates of normal bone mass (62.9%, n = 39) and lower incidences of osteopenia (29.0%, n = 18) and osteoporosis (8.1%, n = 5). The serum PRL level in the patient group was (28.8 ± 11.7) ng/ml, which was significantly higher than that in the healthy control group (13.6 ± 10.2) ng/ml (p < 0.01). The serum E2 level in the patient group was 55.2 ± 14.0 pmol/L, significantly lower than that in the healthy control group (72.4 ± 16.6) pmol/L (p < 0.01). The serum T level in the patient group (13.5 ± 3.8 nmol/L) was comparable to the healthy control group (14.6 ± 4.3 nmol/L) (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Correlation analysis of BMD

In the healthy control group, BMD showed no significant correlation with age, BMI, E2, or T. However, a significant negative correlation was found between BMD and PRL (p < 0.01). In the patient group, BMD showed no significant correlation with BMI, age of onset, chlorpromazine equivalents, or PANSS positive score. BMD was significantly and negatively correlated with age, PRL, disease duration, treatment duration, PANSS negative score, general psychopathology score, and PANSS total score (p < 0.05; p < 0.01). Conversely, BMD showed a significant positive correlation with E2 and T (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study found that male patients with chronic schizophrenia exhibit significantly lower BMD compared to healthy controls, indicating that this population may face a substantially higher risk of osteoporosis in this patient population. Consistently, Jung et al. conducted a case-control study comparing 327 patients with schizophrenia and 125 healthy controls, measuring BMD at the lumbar spine, right proximal femoral neck, and trochanter using DXA [23]. They found that the BMD in the schizophrenia patient group was significantly lower than in the control group, with gender differences observed within the patient group. The prevalence of osteoporosis and the lifetime prevalence of fractures were also significantly higher in schizophrenia patients. Wang et al. reported that BMD in first-episode schizophrenia patients showed no difference from healthy controls before treatment but became significantly lower than controls after one year of antipsychotic medication and was significantly negatively correlated with PRL levels [24]. Shen et al. found a high proportion of reduced BMD in the lumbar spine and femoral neck among young and middle-aged Chinese male schizophrenia patients, with the fracture risk in patients with low BMD being more than double that of patients with normal BMD. However, some studies report inconsistent results [25]. Doknic et al. found that DXA-measured lumbar spine BMD in 26 outpatients with long-term treated schizophrenia was lower than in 35 healthy subjects, but the difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, no difference in femoral neck BMD was found between the two groups [26]. Nevertheless, a recent meta-analysis indicated that BMD in schizophrenia patients is significantly lower than in the general population [27], which is consistent with the findings of our study.

Moreover, serum PRL concentration in the schizophrenia patient group was significantly higher than that in the healthy control group, while serum E2 levels were significantly lower. There was no significant difference in serum T levels between the patient and control groups. This suggests the presence of abnormalities in sex hormone levels in male patients with chronic schizophrenia undergoing long-term antipsychotic treatment. Over the years, numerous studies have reported elevated PRL in schizophrenia patients [28–33], consistent with the findings of this study. This finding indicates that increased PRL levels in schizophrenia patients are due to the blockade of anterior pituitary D2 receptors by antipsychotic drugs, although the effect on PRL elevation varies with the mechanism of action of different types of antipsychotics [34].

Existing literature regarding serum E2 and T levels in schizophrenia patients remains characterized by significant inconsistencies. Bulut et al. compared 38 male schizophrenia patients with 38 matched controls and found that the patient group had significantly higher PRL and significantly lower E2 than the control group [33]. Okita et al. reported that male schizophrenia patients had significantly higher PRL and significantly lower T than the control group, while E2 levels showed no significant difference compared to controls [30]. Kinon et al. and Agarwal et al. both reported decreased serum T levels in male schizophrenia patients [35, 36]. These studies suggest considerable variation in sex hormone levels among schizophrenia patients, which may be associated with numerous factors such as gender, medication, and disease course [37, 38]. Dhume et al. found that gonadal hormones such as testosterone, FSH, and LH were significantly decreased in first-episode schizophrenia patients, indicating that sex hormones may also play an important role in the pathophysiological mechanisms of schizophrenia [39]. Some studies have also found that adjunctive sex hormone therapy in schizophrenia patients can not only alleviate symptoms but also improve cognitive function [40, 41].

After stratifying the patient group into elevated-PRL and normal-PRL subgroups based on serum PRL levels and comparing them with the healthy control group, significant differences were found in BMD, PRL, E2, and T concentrations among the three groups. Some study found significantly reduced BMD in the elevated-PRL subgroup among male inpatients with schizophrenia. This finding suggests that male schizophrenia patients with hyperprolactinemia are more susceptible to decreased BMD, reduced bone mass, and osteoporosis [42]. Kinon et al. suggested that, compared to females, hyperprolactinemia during antipsychotic treatment in male schizophrenia patients may be associated with lower bone mass [35]. The potential mechanisms are twofold: on one hand, elevated PRL in male schizophrenia patients may directly affect the skeleton, increasing bone turnover rate; on the other hand, hypogonadism associated with elevated PRL in male patients, leading to decreased E2 and T levels, may also contribute to increased bone resorption and reduced BMD [43]. The results of this study indicate that bone formation was significantly reduced and bone resorption significantly increased in the elevated-PRL patient group, and that serum E2 and T concentrations were also significantly decreased. This confirms that elevated serum PRL levels in male schizophrenia patients lead to the suppression of sex hormones [44] and may increase bone resorption. The measurement of bone metabolism markers facilitates early clinical identification of compromised bone health in patients with schizophrenia [45–47].

The correlation analysis in this study showed that BMD was significantly and negatively correlated with serum PRL in both the control and patient groups, consistent with findings reported in China and abroad. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, this finding suggests that PRL might independently influence bone metabolism even in healthy individuals, possibly via direct effects on osteoblast PRL receptors. Another possibility is that PRL influences calcium-regulating hormones, such as parathyroid hormone (PTH) or 25-dihydroxyvitamin D, which in turn affect BMD. However, when the patient group was subdivided based on serum PRL levels, a significant correlation between BMD and serum PRL was observed in the elevated-PRL subgroup, but not in the normal-PRL subgroup [48]. In the patient group, BMD was also significantly negatively correlated with disease duration, treatment duration, PANSS negative score, general psychopathology score, and PANSS total score. Conversely, a significant positive correlation was observed with E2 and T. Regression analysis indicated that PRL and general psychopathology score were factors associated with decreased BMD in the patient group, while smoking was a protective factor. This confirms that elevated PRL in schizophrenia patients leads to abnormal BMD [49]. The potential reasons are twofold: on one hand, high PRL levels can directly affect bone metabolism, leading to bone loss; on the other hand, high PRL levels inhibit the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, and the subsequent decrease in sex hormones also exacerbates bone loss in patients. Moreover, the degree of BMD reduction may be associated with the duration of hyperprolactinemia. The correlation between increased general psychopathology score and decreased BMD may suggest that reduced physical activity and dietary intake in patients with severe psychiatric symptoms affect bone mass levels. The finding that smoking is a protective factor in this study may be because smoking lowers PRL levels in schizophrenia patients [50]. We propose that this finding is more plausibly explained by unmeasured confounders (such as higher BMI or physical activity in smokers) than by a causal protective effect of smoking. While smoking lowers PRL, PRL is not the sole determinant of bone metabolism, and smoking adversely affects bone via oxidative stress, vascular toxicity, and other pathways. Hence, our results should not be interpreted as indicating that smoking protects bone. In this study, we found that chlorpromazine equivalents were not significantly correlated with BMD. This may be attributed to interindividual variability in PRL response, such that PRL may be a more direct determinant of BMD than drug dose itself, or to sample heterogeneity across antipsychotic classes that cannot be fully captured by chlorpromazine equivalents.

Bulut et al. [12] found that serum E2 and T levels were negatively correlated with lumbar spine BMD in male patients with schizophrenia. However, other studies have reported no association between serum E2 and T concentrations and lumbar spine BMD in male patients with schizophrenia, whereas in female patients, serum T was positively correlated with lumbar spine BMD. Besides, no significant correlation was found between serum E2 and T concentrations and forearm BMD [44]. The differences in sex hormone levels and their correlation with BMD observed in this study are not entirely consistent with previous research. One potential limitation of the present study is its exclusive focus on male patients with schizophrenia, thereby precluding examination of sex-based differences in hormone profiles that may arise from inherent physiological distinctions between males and females. Nonetheless, some studies suggest that it remains uncertain whether risk factors for osteoporosis differ between men and women. Future research with larger sample sizes and balanced gender ratios is warranted [51, 52].

There is a growing body of evidence substantiating that numerous factors influence the development of osteoporosis [53–57]. Besides, it has been suggested that obesity serves as a protective factor for BMD in patients, although a BMI > 40 kg/m² appears to be a risk factor for osteoporosis [58]. However, this study found no significant correlation between BMD and BMI. This absence of correlation may be attributable to BMI values among participants being predominantly within the normal range. Besides, the low sex hormone levels in male schizophrenia patients may also have influenced their BMI.

In summary, this study found that male patients with chronic schizophrenia on long-term medication exhibit decreased BMD and abnormal levels of serum PRL and sex hormones (E2, T). A correlation was also observed between BMD and serum levels of PRL, E2, and T. This study is subject to several constraints. The lack of a female cohort prevents the exploration of gender-based differences in bone loss, while the absence of data on smoking load, alcohol intake, sunlight exposure, and physical activity limits the ability to control for these known modifiable risk factors for osteoporosis. Although the study converted patients’ daily antipsychotic doses into chlorpromazine equivalents, it did not differentiate the differences in bone density and sex hormone levels between the PRL-elevated and non-PRL-elevated groups, nor did it analyze the effects of different medications on BMD. Future research should include female subjects, increase sample size, collect data from first-episode patient groups, investigate additional factors related to osteoporosis, and consider gender-specific risk factors when intervening in individuals with bone loss. This will provide a stronger research foundation to better serve clinical practice and improve attention to the bone health of patients with schizophrenia.
