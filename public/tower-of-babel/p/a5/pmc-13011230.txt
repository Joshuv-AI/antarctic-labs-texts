Effects of sprayed corn bran on production performance and intestinal function of growing stage magang geese
Authors: Xue Li, Xinyuan Peng, Yinghua Song, Qingyun Cao, Xiaoli Liu, Ben Lukuyu, Hui Ye, Yongwen Zhu, Kashif Saleemi Muhammad, Lin Yang, Jie Pan, Wence Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development and utilization of unconventional feed such as sprayed corn bran (SCB) have effectively alleviated the shortage of feed raw materials, although its application in meat geese production has rarely been reported. Therefore, this experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of SCB on production performance, serum biochemical indices, antioxidant indices and intestinal development in 35∼70-day-old Magang geese. A total of 504 35-day-old Magang geese were randomly divided into 6 groups and fed diets supplemented with 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25% SCB respectively. The results demonstrated that different addition levels of SCB exerted no significant effect on the production performance of 70-day-old Magang geese (P>0.05). The leg muscle rate of 10% group was significantly higher than that of 5%, 20% and 25% groups. Elevated MDA contents in serum and liver were observed in the 20% and 25% groups. The depth of ileal crypt in 25% group was significantly lower than that in 0% and 5% groups. In addition, the relative abundance of Actinobacteria was significantly lower in the 10% SCB group than in the 0% SCB group. Significant increases in the abundance of Eubacterium_brachy_group and norank_f_Peptococcaceae were observed in the 25% group, with a non-significant increasing trend noted in other groups. Collectively, under the experimental conditions, the dietary inclusion of SCB at levels below 20% exerted no adverse effects on the growth and development of 35∼70-day-old Magang geese.

Introduction

The shortage of feed resources has always been an important factor restricting the healthy and sustainable development of waterfowl breeding in China. In recent years, with the rapid development of animal husbandry and feed industry in China, domestic feed consumption continues to increase, resulting in insufficient supply of feed raw materials such as corn and soybean meal (San Martin, et al., 2016). Notably, SCB has been widely used in livestock and poultry production in recent years. It is considered a byproduct obtained by spraying corn slurry on corn brans and drying them, characterized by good fiber digestibility and high protein, energy, and amino acid content (Hao, et al., 2021), with dry matter content ranging from 89.8 to 92.4%, crude protein content ranging from 17.0 to 25.1%, crude fiber content ranging from 6.1 to 14.1%, crude fat content ranging from 1.2 to 3.1%, crude ash content ranging from 4.0 to 7.5%, and total energy ranging from 16.48 to 17.78 MJ/kg (Jiang, et al., 2021). Furthermore, SCB has been reported to contain high levels of minerals and amino acids, exhibiting high absorption rates. Therefore, SCB can be used as a partial substitute for corn and protein raw materials (Lin, et al., 2022).

At present, SCB is used in rabbits, sheep and ducks, but there is little research on meat geese. It has been established that adding SCB to the diet of meat rabbits can enhance production performance, nutrient apparent digestibility, and slaughter rate (Al-Dobaib, 2010). A study has found that adding SCB to the diet of meat ducks can increase the relative weight and length of the intestine, promoting intestinal development (Wang, et al., 2022). In addition, adding SCB to animal feed can promote intestinal growth and development and also has different effects on the gut microbiota of different animals (Zhang, et al., 2018). The optimal dietary inclusion levels of SCB in Cherry Valley ducks are no more than 5%, 10%, and 20% during the starter phase (1∼14 d), grower phase (15∼35 d), and finisher phase (36∼56 d), respectively (Shu et al., 2017). As a herbivorous waterfowl, Magang geese possess distinct digestive physiological characteristics, including a longer intestinal tract, higher cecal fermentation efficiency, and significantly greater tolerance to crude fiber and neutral detergent fiber compared with meat ducks. Therefore, in the present study, dietary SCB levels of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% were established to investigate the optimal dietary inclusion level of SCB in Magang geese. This study was therefore designed to determine the nutritional components and metabolic energy of SCB and to investigate the effects of different inclusion levels on production performance, serum biochemical indexes, antioxidant indexes and intestinal development of 35-70-day-old Magang geese. It provided a reference for the scientific application of SCB in the diet of meat geese.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and bird management

This experiment was conducted at the South China Agricultural University Research Cooperation Base of Jinqian Feed Co., Ltd. (Dongguan, Guangdong). 504 healthy 35-day-old Magang geese were randomly divided into 6 groups, with 6 replicates in each treatment group, each replicate consisting of 14 geese. Based on the broken rice soybean meal feed, the inclusion of SCB was 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%, respectively. The diet components and nutrient levels are shown in Table 1. The goose pens were thoroughly rinsed and disinfected before the experiment began. The commercial feed provided by Jinqian Feed Co., Ltd. (Dongguan, Guangdong) was fed in the early feeding stage (0∼34-day-old), and the feed formula in Table 1 was fed in the late feeding stage (35∼70-day-old). The temperature, humidity, and immune procedures during the feeding period were carried out in accordance with routine operating procedures. At the age of 35 days, the experimental geese were weighed and raised in separate columns for the purpose of calculating their growth performance after the experiment. Growth and slaughtering performance

The formal experiment was initiated when the geese were 35 days of age. Feed intake, initial and final body weights, and the occurrence of mortality were recorded for each pen to calculate ADFI, ADG, and F/G.

At the end of the test, geese with weights close to the average were selected to be slaughtered by jugular vein bloodletting. After wet plucking and draining, full eviscerated rate, half eviscerated rate, leg muscle rate, chest muscle rate, abdominal fat rate and other relevant slaughter performance indicators were calculated according to NY/T823-2020 poultry production performance terms and measurement calculation methods.

Sample collection and organ index calculation

At the end of the experiment, the goose with a weight close to the average was selected from each replicate for slaughter. The goose was euthanized by jugular vein bleeding. After wet plucking and draining the water, relevant slaughter performance indicators were calculated according to NY/T823-2004. Serum was prepared, and the heart, liver, kidney, spleen, pancreas, glandular stomach, muscular stomach and bursa of Fabricius were collected. Organ weights were recorded and organ indices were calculated. The calculation formula was as follows:Organ index (g/kg) = organ weight (g) / live weight (kg)

Separated chest muscle and leg muscle were used for meat quality determination. Duodenum, jejunum and ileum were partially emptied of contents for length and weight determination, and the other part was carefully washed off with a syringe, and intestinal segments without extrusion were selected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fresh cecal chyme and liver were collected respectively, frozen with liquid nitrogen in a sterile freezing tube and stored at −80°C. Geese that have undergone wet plucking are not used for histological evaluation and cryopreservation of samples.

Determination of chest and leg muscle quality

The pH value of the chest and leg muscles was measured 45 min after slaughter using a portable pH meter. The electrode tip was completely inserted into the muscle with a depth of about 1 cm, and three different positions were measured for each sample. At the end of each measurement, the electrode tip was rinsed with distilled water and blotted with neutral filter paper before the next sample was detected. Then, the meat samples were stored in zip-lock bags at 4°C and measured again at 24 h.

Meat color was determined using an automatic colorimeter 45 min after slaughter. First, the chest and leg muscles were dried with neutral filter paper and carried out in the indoor natural light. Each sample was measured at 3 different positions, and the muscle color was read after the value was stable (L represents brightness, a represents red, and b represents yellow). At the end of the assay, the meat samples were placed in zip-sealed bags and stored at 4°C, and the samples were measured again at 24 h.

For cooking loss determination, samples were stored at 4°C immediately after collection and analyzed 24 h later. Before the sample determination, about 30 g muscle (without fascia and fat) was weighed, and the weight was recorded as W1. Then each sample was placed in a closed sealed bag, and the sample was completely immersed in a 75°C thermostat water bath. When the center temperature of the sample reached 75°C, the time began and was maintained for 10 minutes. Its cooking loss was calculated according to the following formula.Cooking loss(%)=[(W1-W2)/W1] × 100%

After the cooked samples were cooled to room temperature, long strips (without fascia and fat) of 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm3 in width and thickness were trimmed along the direction of muscle fibers, and the direction of muscle fibers was adjusted to be perpendicular to the machine. Each sample was repeated for 3 times, and the measured muscle value was expressed as shear force (N).

Determination of biochemical indexes and antioxidant capacity

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (Assay methods of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry), Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Assay methods of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry), Glutamyltransferase (GGT) (Assay methods of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry), Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Assay methods of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry), Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Dinitrophenylhydrazine colorimetry), Albumin (ALB) (Bromocresol green method), Total protein (TP) (Biuret method), Total bilirubin (T-BIL) (Bilirubin oxidase method), High density lipoprotein (HDL) (Chemical clearance method), Low density lipoprotein (LDL) (Direct measurement method), Total triglyceride (TG) (Enzymatic method), and Total Cholesterol (TC) (Oxidase method) were detected by fully automatic biochemical analyzer (7180, LABOSPECT). An appropriate amount of liver tissue was thoroughly ground with PBS buffer to prepare liver homogenate; this step was omitted for serum. After centrifugation, the supernatant was sucked out, and the protein content was determined by BCA method. Then the concentrations of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) (Hydroxylamine method), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (Hydrogen peroxide method), catalase (CAT) (Ammonium molybdate method) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Thiobarbituric acid method) in liver were determined using the kit provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute according to the operating procedures on the instruction manual.

Determination of short chain fatty acid content in cecal chyme of geese

Take the cecal chyme and transfer it into a centrifuge tube. Add ultrapure water and vortex for 3-5 min. Centrifuge at 12000 r/min for 5 min. Add 25% metaphosphoric acid solution to the supernatant, mix thoroughly, and incubate in an ice-water bath for 30 min, shaking every 10 min. Centrifuge at 12000 r/min for 5 min. Filter the supernatant with a 0.22 μm filter membrane and prepare it for testing. Use an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph for detection, with the following instrument parameters: DB-wax capillary column (30 m × 320 μm × 0.25 μm), FID detector, detector temperature at 250°C, air flow rate at 450mL/min, hydrogen gas flow rate at 40mL/min, carrier gas flow rate at 43mL/min, injection volume at 4.5 μL, injection port temperature at 250°C, and pressure at 10.5 psi. Maintain an initial column temperature of 80°C for 3 min, then increase it at a rate of 10°C/min to 160°C and maintain it for 1 min.

Histomorphology of small intestine

Fresh tissues were removed after 24 h of fixation, repaired with a scalpel in a fume cupboard, dehydrated and embedded. Wax blocks were cut into 4-6 μm thick sections, deparaffinized, washed, stained and sealed for microscopic observation. Villus height, muscle thickness, and crypt depth in intestinal tissue were measured using ImageJ software, and villus/crypt ratio was calculated.

16S rRNA sequencing of cecal digesta microorganisms

Genomic DNA was extracted from the samples and detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) using an ABI GeneAmp®9700 PCR thermocycler. The PCR products were pooled and detected by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. The PCR products were recovered by cutting the gel with the AxyPrepDNA gel recovery kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA). QuantiFluor™ -ST Blue fluorescence quantitative System (Promega USA) was used for quantification. After library construction, Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) was used for sequencing according to the standard procedure of Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. We used fastp software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.20.0) to control the quality of the original sequence, and used the FLASH (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, Version 1.2.7) for assembly. UPARSE software (http://drive5.com/uparse/, version 7.1) was used according to 97% of the similarity of sequence OTU clustering and remove chimeras. The RDP classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, version 2.2) annotated each sequence for species classification, compared the Silva 16S rRNA database (version 138), and an alignment threshold of 70% was set.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using SAS9.2 software for one-way ANOVA. Duncan method was used for multiple comparison analysis, and GraphPrism8.0 software was used to create a bar chart. At a significant level of P < 0.05, the experimental results are presented as mean and standard error of the mean ± SEM values.

Effect of SCB on the intestinal health of 70-day-old Magang geese

Discussion

Growth performance is an important indicator for evaluating animal production performance, as it is closely related to animal species, feed composition, and feeding management (Rodriguez, et al., 2021). Ding et al. reported that adding 10% corn distiller’s dried grains with solutions to the diet had no adverse effects on growth performance, serum biochemical indexes and meat quality of cherry valley ducks (Ding, et al., 2022). Zhang et al. found that adding corn bran and Clostridium butyricum to weaned piglets could increase acetic acid-producing bacteria in intestine and reduce pathogenic bacteria (Zhang, Sun, Chen, Nie, Zhao, Guan, Lei, He, Chen, Johnston, Zhao and Ma, 2018). Consistent with the above results, this study proved that adding less than 25% SCB to the diet had no adverse effects on the growth performance of 70-day-old Magang geese. It indicated that SCB could be added to the diet of 35∼70-day-old Magang geese to reduce the feed cost.

Slaughtering performance is also an important indicator for evaluating the production performance of meat animals, reflecting the deposition of nutrients in animal muscle tissue. Chest and leg muscles were the main distribution areas of carcass muscles, and their quality directly affects the meat production performance of animals (Ceccobelli, et al., 2022). Research has found that different levels of SCB have no significant effect on the slaughter rate, eviscerated carcass yield, abdominal fat rate, and breast muscle rate of mule ducks (Arroyo, et al., 2019).

This study found that different amounts of SCB had no significant effect on the slaughter rate, eviscerated carcass yield, breast muscle rate, and abdominal fat rate of 70-day-old Magang geese. Notably, the leg muscle rate in the 10% and 15% SCB groups was distinctly higher than that in the 25% group. This outcome may be attributed to the rich amino acid profile of SCB, wherein moderate inclusion levels support leg muscle development in Magang geese. Conversely, the high non-starch polysaccharides content in SCB could negatively influence slaughter performance. Therefore, adding excessive SCB to the diet of Magang geese can affect their leg muscle growth, while adding 10% and 15% SCB to the diet can promote animal growth and improve meat production performance.

The organ index can reflect the growth, development, and health status of animals, and can be used to determine whether the development status and immune function of poultry are reasonable (Al-Khalifa, et al., 2012). This study found that the use of different SCBs had inapparent diversity on the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, gizzard, glandular stomach, thymus, pancreas and bursa of Fabricius index of 70-day-old Magang geese. It was found that feeding SCB reduced the heart weight of meat ducks at 42 days old (Saree, et al., 2017). Compared with other poultry, the gizzard of geese has strong rotational movement ability, and promotes the subsequent acidic environment in the stomach to dissolve chyme better (Chen, et al., 2016), indicating that Magang geese are more adaptable to SCB.

Serum biochemical indices also indicated that dietary supplementation with 5% SCB was conducive to reducing ALP activity, whereas the 25% SCB group adversely affected ALB and LDL metabolism. These results suggest that an appropriate SCB inclusion level can alleviate the hepatic metabolic burden of Magang geese (He et al., 2021). As ALB is synthesized in the liver, a high SCB inclusion level may directly interfere with intestinal amino acid absorption, thereby triggering compensatory hepatic ALB synthesis and leading to elevated serum ALB levels (Xie et al., 2024). The observed increase in LDL indicated that a high dietary SCB level might promote hepatic cholesterol accumulation (Li et al., 2022).

An important prerequisite for maintaining the body's antioxidant homeostasis is the balance between oxygen free radicals and the antioxidant defense system (Sztretye, et al., 2019). Antioxidants in the body can form an antioxidant defense system to protect the body from oxidative damage (Wojtunik-Kulesza, et al., 2016). When biofilms are attacked by oxygen free radicals, they form the product MDA, whose content reflects the body's antioxidant capacity (Thakur, et al., 2019). T-SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, as the main enzymatic antioxidant systems, mainly play a role by clearing free radicals and reactive oxygen species, breaking down and blocking the peroxidation reaction chain (Shu, et al., 2023). Ruan et al. demonstrated that the GSH content in plasma of laying ducks fed with rape seed meal with corn distillers dried grains with solutions diminished observably, while the MDA content increased significantly (Ruan, et al., 2018). The significantly increased MDA levels in the 20% and 25% SCB groups indicate that high inclusion rates augment free radical content and cell damage, thereby reducing the overall antioxidant capacity of Magang geese.

The intestine is the largest digestive organ in geese, and the integrity of the intestinal structure is the physiological basis for the digestion and absorption of nutrients and normal individual development, and is a necessary condition for intestinal barrier function and mucosal immune response (Dong, et al., 2021). Animal digestion and absorption of nutrients are mainly achieved through circular folds on the intestinal tract, intestinal villi, and columnar epithelial cells on the villi (Kawamoto and Sasai, 2023). Therefore, intestinal villus height and crypt depth determine the contact area between nutrients entering the intestine and the intestinal tract. The larger the villi to crypt ratio, indicates a stronger ability of the intestine to absorb nutrients (Zhong, et al., 2023). This experiment found that different amounts of SCB had no significant effect on the intestinal morphology and structure of the duodenum and jejunum, as well as the villus height, muscle layer thickness, and villus to crypt ratio of the ileum in 70-day-old Magang geese. The significantly lower ileal crypt depth in the 25% SCB group may stem from individual variations in intestinal development and digestibility. More importantly, such effects may be driven by anti-nutritional factors in SCB, including non-starch polysaccharides, phytic phosphorus, resistant starch, sulfite ions (Liu, et al., 2023). These factors are known to impede the absorption of nutrients such as amino acids, cause insufficient energy supply to intestinal epithelial cells, inhibit the development of intestinal villi and crypts.

A stable microbial community structure can assist in the digestion and absorption of nutrients, promote the growth and development of intestinal epithelial cells, and resist the invasion of foreign pathogens (Fang, et al., 2023). The composition and richness of gut microbiota can be altered by factors such as animal age, physiological status, feeding environment, and diet (Winders, et al., 2023). Notably, dietary SCB significantly increased the abundance of Eubacterium brachy_group and norank_f_Peptococcaceae in the cecum of 70-day-old Magang geese. Similarly, an increase in Eubacterium_brachy_group has been observed in the rumen fluid of cows fed high-moisture ear corn. It has been reported that Eubacterium brachy_group may be related to the regulation of intestinal microbiota-bile acid-FXR axis by Lonicerin to alleviate hepatic steatosis induced by high-fat diet in mice. A significant increase in the abundance of Eubacterium_brachy_group was also found in Pediococcus acidilactici SW-HX39 in improving hyperuricemia, suggesting the potential of this bacterium in improving goose gout. The abundance of norank_f_Peptococcaceae was increased in the cecum of layers supplemented with multi-enzyme complex, suggesting that norank_f_Peptococcaceae plays an important role in the regulation of amino acid metabolism and antioxidant capacity.

In summary, dietary inclusion of SCB at levels up to 25% had no adverse effects on growth performance, organ indices, or meat quality in 35∼70-day-old Magang geese. Notably, the 10% SCB group exhibited an increased leg muscle rate. However, when SCB inclusion exceeded 20%, serum and liver MDA content rose significantly, indicating reduced antioxidant capacity. Based on various indicators, it is recommended that the use of SCB in the diet of 35∼70-day-old Magang geese should not exceed 20% under the conditions of this experiment.
