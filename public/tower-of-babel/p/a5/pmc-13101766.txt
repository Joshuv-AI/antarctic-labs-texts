Reconsidering GVHD prophylaxis in cord blood transplantation
Authors: Joseph A Clara, Karen Ballen
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Yamasaki et al1 report a nationwide analysis of >3000 adults comparing methotrexate- and mycophenolate-based graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis after adult cord blood transplantation among patients with CPX-351–eligible high-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Using Japanese registry data, the authors examined the impact of these approaches on acute GVHD, engraftment, viral complications, and survival in a population with substantial baseline risk.

Cord blood transplantation requires clinical decisions under constraints that differ from adult donor transplantation. Cord blood grafts contain fewer mature T cells and typically reconstitute adaptive immunity more slowly, whereas early outcomes are shaped by delayed hematopoietic recovery and infection risk. Therefore, many centers have favored a calcineurin inhibitor combined with mycophenolate mofetil rather than methotrexate, often based on concerns that methotrexate adds mucosal toxicity and further delays recovery.2 Beyond its distinct immunobiology, cord blood transplantation has also played a critical role in expanding access to allogeneic transplantation for patients lacking suitably matched adult donors, including ethnically diverse populations, with large registry analyses demonstrating comparable survival across groups, further underscoring the importance of refining platform-specific approaches to GVHD prophylaxis.3 The analysis by Yamasaki et al challenges this assumption. In their cohort, methotrexate-based prophylaxis was associated with lower rates of acute GVHD, accompanied by slower neutrophil engraftment. Importantly, overall and disease-free survival were superior with methotrexate-based prophylaxis in the non–therapy-related AML subgroup, whereas no uniform survival advantage was observed across the full cohort, underscoring that the impact of GVHD prophylaxis choice is context dependent rather than absolute.1

These findings highlight a recurring theme in cord blood transplantation: no single variable in isolation determines optimal GVHD prophylaxis. Faster neutrophil recovery remains an important objective, but its clinical value is reduced if accompanied by higher rates of acute GVHD or severe viral complications. Conversely, lower rates of acute GVHD are desirable but not if achieved at the cost of delayed hematopoietic recovery in a platform already vulnerable to early infections and nonrelapse mortality. Rather than identifying a superior regimen, this study helps quantify offsetting risks that likely account for the convergence of long-term outcomes.

The disease context selected by the authors further sharpens the relevance of these trade-offs. The cohort is restricted to CPX-351–eligible disease, encompassing therapy-related AML, AML with myelodysplasia-related changes, and related secondary AML subtypes.1 CPX-351 eligibility functions here as a shorthand for adverse disease biology associated with a high baseline risk of treatment failure, including relapse after transplantation.4 Prior work has shown that among adults with high-risk AML defined by measurable residual disease positivity at transplantation, cord blood transplantation was associated with a lower relapse risk and favorable survival, highlighting the capacity of cord blood grafts to exert potent graft-versus-leukemia effects even in biologically adverse settings.5 In this context, early competing risks are closely linked. Delayed hematopoietic recovery amplifies infection risk, acute GVHD often necessitates prolonged immunosuppression, and severe viral complications such as human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) encephalitis carry substantial morbidity in cord blood transplantation.6, 7, 8 Prior work has shown that cord blood graft biology, characterized by delayed T-cell recovery, confers a uniquely high risk of HHV-6 reactivation.7 Within this framework, the lower incidence of HHV-6 encephalitis observed with methotrexate-based prophylaxis in the Yamasaki cohort strengthens the clinical relevance of the observed trade-offs, even in the absence of a survival difference across the full cohort.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. As with many retrospective transplant studies, the analysis emphasizes acute GVHD and early complications, whereas relapse biology, immune reconstitution, and patient-reported outcomes are less well captured. This emphasis reflects historical trial design priorities and is increasingly misaligned with contemporary AML transplantation, in which relapse remains the leading cause of treatment failure and posttransplant strategies are increasingly risk adapted.9 In addition, standard donor-recipient HLA matching in this cohort was primarily performed at the antigen level, introducing unmeasured allelic disparity that may influence GVHD risk and complicate attribution of immunologic effects to prophylaxis strategy alone. Even when overall survival is similar, the nature of early complications may meaningfully affect morbidity and subsequent immunosuppression.

These findings also underscore how strongly graft biology shapes acceptable prophylaxis trade-offs. In adult donor transplantation, particularly with peripheral blood stem cell grafts, posttransplant cyclophosphamide–based prophylaxis has reduced chronic GVHD and shortened time on immunosuppression.10 However, the clinical performance of posttransplant cyclophosphamide is closely tied to the high T-cell content and rapid immune reconstitution characteristic of peripheral blood grafts. Cord blood transplantation occupies a distinct immunologic niche, and extrapolating prophylaxis paradigms across graft sources risks obscuring the platform-specific trade-offs highlighted by Yamasaki et al.

Therefore, the most instructive signal in this analysis may lie not in the absence of a universal survival advantage but in the subgroup-specific findings. Among patients with non–therapy-related CPX-351-eligible AML, in which disease biology remains adverse yet potentially more amenable to graft-versus-leukemia effects, methotrexate-based prophylaxis was associated with superior overall and disease-free survival.1 This suggests that in biologically high-risk but transplant-modifiable disease, reductions in acute GVHD and severe viral complications meaningfully influence long-term outcomes, even at the cost of delayed neutrophil recovery. In contrast, in therapy-related AML, in which outcomes are often dominated by intrinsically aggressive biology, reductions in GVHD and viral complications did not translate into a survival benefit. Whether these findings should change practice remains uncertain. Although methotrexate-based prophylaxis confers clear reductions in acute GVHD and HHV-6 encephalitis, adjusted analyses indicate that its independent effect on relapse-free survival is modest. In programs in which viral complications and acute GVHD drive early morbidity, methotrexate-based prophylaxis may merit renewed consideration, whereas mycophenolate-based approaches may remain preferable in situations where delayed hematopoietic recovery is the principal concern. As transplant platforms, supportive care, and posttransplant strategies continue to evolve, GVHD prophylaxis in cord blood transplantation should be viewed not as a fixed choice between agents but as a lever whose impact varies with disease biology and competing early risks. Viewed in this light, the findings by Yamasaki et al do not establish methotrexate-based prophylaxis as uniformly superior, but they do reopen the conversation around its role in cord blood transplantation. Long regarded as excessively toxic in a platform characterized by delayed hematopoietic recovery, methotrexate emerges here as a viable option with distinct advantages in reducing acute GVHD and severe viral complications. Therefore, this study invites a reassessment of long-held assumptions and supports a more nuanced, context-dependent approach to GVHD prophylaxis selection, grounded in evidence derived from one of the largest adult cord blood transplantation cohorts reported to date.
