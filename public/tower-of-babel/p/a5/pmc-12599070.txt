Improving the care pathway for people living with HIV aged 70 years and older: the impact of geriatric screening in an infectious diseases unit (AUTO-HIV 70)
Authors: Marie Berthomier, Clément Lahaye, Claire Chatron, Céline Lambert, Émilie Goncalves, Natacha Mrozek, Christine Jacomet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith increased life expectancy of people living with HIV comes increased rates of multimorbidity and frailty, which services are not currently managing well. A geriatric day hospital (GDH) screening program was offered in our infectious disease department for all people living with HIV aged 70 years and older coming for follow-up, with the aim to provide more holistic ageing care. In order to evaluate our professional practice, we assessed the proportion of participants who had a planned change in their care pathway following this screening, and describe the population who benefited from this GDH. MethodsFrom July 2021 up to January 2024, a cross-sectional study was performed. Demographic data was collected from participant’s medical records along with various validated geriatric screening scores. Changes in the care pathway included referrals to a health professional and/or a change in treatment. Changes were indicated by the results of the screening program, planned by the clinician if the changes were considered relevant and if the participant agreed, and finally implemented or not at the next consultation. The percentage of participants with a change in their care pathway following the geriatric screening test is presented with a 95% confidence interval. The factors associated with it were studied using the Chi-squared test or the Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables.ResultsA total of 63 people living with HIV, aged 75.2 ± 4.6 years, were included, 57.1% of whom had at least three comorbidities. According to the geriatric screening evaluations, a change in the care pathway was indicated in 56/63 (88.9%; 95%CI: 78.4 to 95.4%) people living with HIV, and planned by the doctor with the participant’s agreement in 34/63 (54.0%; 95%CI: 40.9 to 66.6%) participants. Factors associated with a planned change in the care pathway were Dat’AIDS mortality score (p=0.02), EPICES score (p=0.03), Fried frailty phenotype (p=0.007) and GFST (p=0.04). At least one planned change in the care pathway was implemented before the next consultation six months later in 30/63 (47.6%) people living with HIV.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility of organising geriatric screening in an infectious diseases unit. The proportion of changes that were implemented highlights the relevance of using these screening tests. It is, however, important to improve some of the screening tests used and raise awareness amongst people living with HIV and their doctors.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the institution’s data processing register under number M23JC0704 and approved by the ethics committee (IRB00013412, “CHU de Clermont Ferrand IRB #1”, number IRB 2023CF089).Keywords: PLHIV, Frailty, Mortality score, EPICES score, Fried score, Ageing

Background

Since the development of combination antiretroviral therapy and the introduction of measures to prevent opportunistic infections and comorbidities, particularly cardiovascular comorbidities and cancer, life expectancy of people living with HIV has improved significantly [1–3]. As a result, people living with HIV who have a CD4 count greater than 500 and have been on antiretroviral therapy (ART) for more than six years have a mortality rate on par with the general population [4]. In addition, deaths from AIDS-defining illnesses are now a minority of cases whilst deaths from non-HIV related causes such as cancer and cardiovascular disease are increasing [5, 6].

The National Institute for Statistics and Economic Studies predicts that by 2070 three in ten people living in France will be aged over 65, with an increase of 5.7 million in the number of people aged 75 and over [7]. The ageing of the population is therefore a major public health issue that also affects people living with HIV [8, 9]. In 2021, there were 111,371 adults living with HIV in all the French COREVIH (Regional Coordination to fight against HIV and STIs), with a median age of 53 (compared to 49.7 in 2015). Of these 27% of people living with HIV were aged over 60 (compared to 16% in 2015 and 11% in 2011), and 3% aged 75 or older [10, 11]. This projected ageing of the population requires a change in the way care is administered, with an increased need for geriatric assessment.

Older age is associated with higher rates of frailty and lower rates of independence in activities of daily living [12]. Frailty is a concept that reflects a state of vulnerability, a deprived situation in which the person experiences a decline in their functional reserves, reducing their ability to adapt if a stressful event occurs [13]. Fried describes that this phenotype is associated with falls, impaired mobility, disability in activities of daily living, hospitalisation and death [14]. However, this condition can be improved by implementing programmes that include dietary management and physical activity [15]. These programmes appear to be effective in improving functional capacity, mobility and weight gain [16, 17]. Priority should therefore be given to the initial identification of frailty, which should be assessed in all older people in order to provide appropriate care as early as possible and thus preserve their independence [18]. Comprehensive geriatric screening is the gold standard and consists of a multi-dimensional assessment of activities of daily living, frailty, nutrition, functional status, a social and financial assessment, an emotional and cognitive assessment, comorbidities and a review of the participant’s medications [19]. The European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS) recommends screening for frailty and adapting our care of older people living with HIV [20–23]. Indeed, this population often experiences compounded harms that intersect ageing, HIV status and other vulnerabilities [24]. Accelerated ageing due to chronic inflammation and persistent immune system activation as well as polypharmacy risks, exacerbates their physical and heath related impairments [25]. Mental decline may be exacerbated by depression, anxiety, loneliness, grief and survivor guilt [26]. Social harms are marked by a dual stigma, HIV and age, and may result in further marginalisation and isolation with little familial support [27]. Economic difficulties are exacerbated by insufficient income, housing instability and few long-term care settings with integrated HIV care [28]. Limited mobility can prevent people living with HIV from attending appointments regularly [29]. Most HIV care systems lack geriatric expertise and have inadequate geriatrics training for healthcare professionals [30]. To address these concerns, geriatric assessment programmes for people living with HIV have been implemented in the literature, mostly in the United States of America and the United Kingdom [20, 31–40]. They all target populations aged 50 years and older, with the exception of one program targeting people living with HIV aged 60 years and older. Interestingly, most of these programmes, with a few exceptions, do not include an evaluation of their impact or acceptability [32, 38].

For many years, our Department of Infectious and Tropical Diseases has attempted to provide a detailed and global assessment of people living with HIV aged 50 years and over focusing on cardiovascular risks, cancer screenings, updating vaccinations, intensifying activities of daily living and encouraging healthy food and smoking cessation [41]. At the time of EACS geriatric recommendations, two large, purely descriptive French studies focused on people living with HIV aged 70 years and over and revealed a high frailty score in these participants, so we decided to try to implement a comprehensive geriatric assessment of this population [42, 43]. Unfortunately, the number of geriatricians was very limited. In this context, our teams agreed to perform geriatric screening tests for HIV-infected people, age 70 and older, who were admitted in our outpatient day clinic. It consisted of an overall assessment of the person, similar to a standardised geriatric assessment, but carried out by an infectious disease specialist rather than a geriatrician. At the end of the day, changes in the care pathway were indicated based on the results of the geriatric assessment and planned if the changes were considered relevant by the HIV clinician and if the participant agreed.

In order to assess the relevance of performing these screening tests in our outpatient day clinic, we attempted to evaluate the number of participants who had a planned change in their healthcare management based on the screening test results.

Methods

Study and ethical agreements

This study was an evaluation of professional practice, which required registration with the data protection authority of the Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital (study registered in the institution’s data processing register under number M23JC0704) and approval by the ethics committee (IRB00013412, “CHU de Clermont Ferrand IRB #1”, number IRB 2023-CF089).

Conduct of the study

From July 2021 to January 2024, people living with HIV who attended the geriatric outpatient day clinic at the Infectious Disease Unit of Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital were invited to participate in the study by their HIV clinician. The clinician provided the participants with information about the study and obtained their consent. Once consent had been obtained, a geriatric screening day was scheduled for the following weeks.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: people living with HIV, at least 70 years old, being followed by the Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital and having agreed to undergo geriatric screening in an outpatient day hospital setting. The only exclusion criteria was refusal of the assessment and data collection.

Each participant’s geriatric screening results were recorded in their medical file by a clinical team consisting of a nurse, doctor and pharmacist.

The pharmaceutical interview was performed by a pharmacist and including a medication review and reconciliation. This consisted of collecting all of the participant’s prescriptions, discussing the medication modalities and adverse effects with the participant, and calling the participant’s community pharmacy if necessary [50, 51]. Polypharmacy was defined by the intake of ≥ 5 medications (including both ART and non-ART) [52]. Medication adherence was measured using a medication adherence assessment test [53]. Drug-related problems, such as drug-drug interactions, were investigated using a University of Liverpool HIV drug checker [54]. Anticholinergic cognitive load was also reported a posteriori for each person [55].

The relevance of performing this outpatient geriatric screening test is based on the number of participants who had a planned change in their care pathway as a result of this screening test. A change in healthcare management was defined as at least one planned referral to a health professional and/or a change in the participant’s medications. We assumed that without the performance of a geriatric screening test, no change in healthcare pathway would arise.

Follow-up after geriatric screening

At the standard follow-up visit (which could take place 6 to 12 months after the outpatient geriatric hospital visit), the planned referrals to specialists were reviewed and confirmation that they had taken place was obtained. In the case of therapeutic drug changes requiring the opinion of another specialist, confirmation that the opinion of the specialist had been sought and whether the therapeutic drug change had been made was obtained.

Other variables

The variables required for the study were collected from each person living with HIV’s electronic medical record in e-NADIS® software [56]. This electronic medical record is implemented by healthcare providers which then benefit from an annual quality control by clinical research associates.

The following personal identification and socio-demographic variables were collected: age, gender, level of education, presence of home help, presence of a home nurse, living at home or in a nursing home.

We collected the following medical data: date and mode of HIV acquisition, CDC AIDS stage at diagnosis, duration of ART treatment, current ART, CD4, CD4/CD8, HIV viral load, HBV, HCV and CMV serologies, comorbidities, number of treatments, use of anticholinergics (Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden scale or ACB scale), tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption (more than 10 drinks a week), use of other toxic substances and Dat’AIDS mortality score.

Geriatric screening’s study file, sociodemographic and medical data were implemented in an anonymised data collection form, numbered in order of participant arrival, and then each form was transferred to REDCap software (Research Electronic Data CAPture, Copyright 2006–2013 Vanderbilt University) with the appropriate number. Follow-up data were added to the electronic form after the follow-up visit had taken place.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 15; StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). All tests were two-sided, with an alpha level set at 5%. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages, and quantitative data as mean ± standard deviation or median [25th; 75th percentiles], according to their statistical distribution. The percentage of participants with a planned change in their care pathway following geriatric screening is presented with a 95% confidence interval (CI). We hypothesised that without this geriatric screening, no change in healthcare pathway would be made. Factors associated with a change in healthcare pathway following geriatric screening were studied using the Chi-squared test or the Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables. The same analyses were performed to study the factors associated with referral to a geriatrician, when the indication for referral was given.

Results

During the study period, 124 people living with HIV met the inclusion criteria of age over 70 representing 9.6% of the active list of people living with HIV followed in our centre. A total of 63 participants benefited from an outpatient day hospital geriatric screening. Reasons for non-inclusion in the study were the lack of offers to take part in the programme from HIV clinicians, refusal to participate, loss to follow-up, relocation, or planned geriatric screening after the end of our study.

Of those who declined the geriatric screening at their last visit, 86.9% were male, with a median age of 74.3 (± 4.6) years. These characteristics did not differ statistically from those of the participants.

Demographic and medical characteristics of the included population

The socio-demographic and medical characteristics of the study population are detailed in Table 2. The study population was 82.5% male, with a mean age of 75.2 (± 4.6). The mean duration of HIV infection was 24.7 (± 7.9) years, and the mean duration of ART was 21.9 (± 6.7) years. All in all, 96.8% of participants were virally suppressed and immunity was considered to be restored in most of them. Almost 89% of participants had at least one comorbidity and 57.1% of participants had at least three comorbidities. The most common comorbidities were dyslipidaemia (52.4%) and high blood pressure (50.8%), followed by psychiatric disorders (33.3%), diabetes and ischaemic heart disease (23.8% each). Polypharmacy (at least five drugs) was detected in 82.5% of participants.

Geriatric screening

The results of all geriatric screening tests are detailed in Table 3. In total, 26 participants (41.3%) were identified as deprived, 6 (9.5%) were malnourished and 22 (34.9%) had at least one positive response to the depression screening test. A total of 41 (65.0%) participants were identified as frail and pre-frail according to the Fried criteria and/or with a positive GFST. Ten participants (15.9%) were identified as frail according to the Fried criteria -all but one GFST-positive. Twenty-one participants (34.9%) had a positive GFST (of whom 9 were identified as frail according to the Fried criteria).

Changes in healthcare pathway

At the end of the geriatric screening day, 56/63 people living with HIV (88.9%; 95%CI: 78.4 to 95.4%) had an indication for a change in their healthcare pathway: 56/63 (88.9%) had at least one indication for a referral to a healthcare professional, and 10/63 (15.9%) had at least one indication for a change in therapy, either because of a significant drug interaction or to optimise therapy. Of the 34 participants with an indication to see a geriatrician, 29 (85.3%) had an indication for at least one other appointment (social worker, dietician, physiotherapist, other specialist opinion on drug interactions, other professional).

Finally, 34/63 participants (54.0%; 95%CI: 40.9 to 66.6%) had a planned change in their healthcare pathway at the end of the assessment. We report that 26 of them had one planned change in their care pathway, seven participants had two, and one had four changes. Moreover, 27/63 participants (42.9%, 95%CI: 30.5 to 56.0%) had at least one planned referral to a health professional and 11/11 (100%) reported that therapeutic changes had been made at the end of the outpatient day hospital visit.

The number of referrals indicated by the geriatric test scores and the number of referrals ultimately planned are detailed for each referral area in Fig. 1. It should be noted that the most common referrals were to geriatricians and physiotherapists, while the least common referrals were to the dieticians, psychiatrists and social workers. Participants could be referred to other professionals depending on the needs identified during the assessment. These included rheumatologists, cardiologists, pulmonologists, otolaryngologists, smoking cessation services, vascular surgeons and pain specialists.

The follow-up visit took place on average 6.9 ± 2.3 months after the geriatric screening day. Nine participants had planned follow-up and one was lost to follow-up. Amongst participants with a planned change in their care pathway, 30/34 (88.2%) had implemented at least one change in their care pathways before their next follow-up visit. Of these changes, 23/27 (85.2%) were referrals and 10/10 were therapeutic changes (some participants had referral plus therapeutic changes). Thus, of the total study population, 30/63 (47.6%, 95%CI: 34.9 to 60.6%) participants had at least one implemented change in their healthcare pathway. It is noteworthy that the professionals to whom participants were referred were very likely to contribute to the optimisation of care.

Analysis of factors associated with a planned change in healthcare management at the end of geriatric screening

We analysed demographic and medical factors (HIV characteristics, comorbidities and mortality score) as well as geriatric score results, associated with planned change in care pathway - referral to a health professional and/or change in treatment. These factors are shown in detail in Tables 3 and 4.

Discussion

This analysis of our professional practice showed the relevance of performing geriatric screening tests in an infectious disease unit for people living with HIV aged over 70, in addition to the global social and medical healthcare provided to patients over 50 years old.

Indeed, the number of changes in healthcare pathways planned based on the results of geriatric screening tests attest the importance of performing these tests in our patient day hospital setting: more than half of the participants (54%) had a change in their healthcare pathway planned at the end of the geriatric day hospital. Amongst sociodemographic and medical characteristics, factors associated with a planned change in healthcare pathways were an increased mortality score (DAT’AIDS score), social vulnerability (EPICES score) and frailty (Fried’s criteria and GFST score). The mortality test and the frailty tests had never been assessed by our team before. There was no association between modifications in healthcare and level of independence (IADL score), nutrition (MNA-SF score), neurocognition (IHDS score) or depression (two-question screening adapted from PHQ2), domains that had been ever feared by the medical team during medical healthcare provided to patients over 50 years old.

To appreciate this work, one must know that the French health care system is a social insurance system with one of the highest levels of public funding in Europe and one of the lowest out-of-pocket expenses [57]. Primary and secondary health ambulatory care is usually provided by self-employed doctors, dentists and medical auxiliaries (including nurses and physiotherapists) working in their own practices with partial public financing, but, for people living with HIV, their care is often provided by salaried staff in hospitals and health centres without any out-of-pocket spending [11]. Nonetheless, inequality in the distribution of health care professionals remains a considerable barrier to equity and a long-term care reform including public coverage of long-term care is still pending [57]. Home help services are available to people aged sixty of above to support with their activities of daily living but a financial contribution may be required based on income [58]. All people have access to a social worker whether in the community of hospital free of charge.

In many intergrated care programmes, a comprehensive geriatric assessment is conducted to identify older people’s problems and care needs but different instruments and procedures for conducting them are in place [59]. To our knowledge, our study depicts the first geriatric screening program systematically offered to people living with HIV aged 70 years and older. An overview of our work experience could help professionals and researchers from a range of healthcare systems to choose the right instruments and procedures for integrated care programmes for older people living with HIV.

The first issue is whether all people living with HIV aged 70 years and over would benefit from a geriatric assessment as only 50.8% of eligible people underwent geriatric screening. The possibility of having a geriatrician and a multidisciplinary team including a nurse, pharmacist, and social worker in an HIV primary care clinic was also a challenge for Condo et al. in Mount Sinai Hospital. In their study, among 183 appointments scheduled, only 62% of participants came to their appointments [60]. In our study, the reasons for non-inclusion were a person’s refusal or lack of recruitment into the study by their HIV clinician. A selection bias may be present, as those without complaints could be more likely to decline to participate. Those who declined to participate did not differ by age or gender. Similarly, HIV clinicians may have been less likely to perform geriatric screening tests on those patients appearing to be in less need of such things. Yet, it should be noted that the population included in the study was generally functionally independent. The fact that participants, prior to the study, did not ask for a geriatric health check argues in favor of extending the screening program to all people living with HIV more than 70 years old. Improving professional practice among the medical staff and providing convincing arguments in favor of a geriatric screening program are ongoing. Efforts to raise patient awareness by patient education are also ongoing.

A second positive outcome was that geriatric screening in an infectious diseases department meant that only those with a genuine indication would be referred to a geriatrician, thereby preventing overwhelming the limited geriatric resources available. The results show that 19% of the study population − 1 out of 5 - was referred to a geriatrician. Of the scores used in our study for referral to a geriatrician (Fried, GFST, IHDS), Fried was the most discriminating. Using the Fried criteria, we identified frailty in 15.9% of people living with HIV more than 70 years old, whereas Allavena et al. found frailty in 13.5% of SEPTAVIH participants. Our two studies had similar study populations in terms of age, gender and age-related comorbidities [42]. As well, a GFST positive score recommended a geriatric referral but the recommendation was only followed by HIV clinicians half the time. In the general population, almost 95% of participants with a positive GFST who were referred for geriatric assessment were frail or prefrail, which is consistent with the 90.5% (19/21) in our study [61]. Yet, it seems to be overly sensitive and not sufficiently specific test. Let us note that the GFST has rarely been used in people living with HIV, we found its use only in a standardised geriatric assessment program conducted in New York [36]. Notwithstanding, the geriatric outpatient screening day proposed seems to us to be effective in identifying the need for geriatric referral and really confirms the relevance of performing a geriatric screening assessment. In addition, it reduces the geriatrician’s screening workload. And importantly, it does not add too much to the workload of the HIV clinician and the team as they are already in the habit of providing global HIV care from the start of treatment and reinforced annually starting at age 50. And, notably, these patients compose less than 10% of the active file. Nonetheless, it should be noted that the proportion of people living with HIV at older ages is expected to increase in the coming years. Recommendations for care that are specific to older people will grow and increase this proportion of the workload.

Our work contributes to the support and organization of holistic geriatric care. There is limited data regarding models in developing countries and long-term care settings [62, 63].

The French healthcare system and the hospital-centred HIV care allow for such an organisation [11, 57]. But, our work has revealed many challenges that integrated comprehensive geriatric care faced and we have to fully examine them.

Firstly, polypharmacy and drug-drug interactions are thorny issues [64]. Recommendations to reduce ARV treatment in multimorbid people has become a top priority for HIV clinicians [65]. Of note, the participants included in our study, 1 to 5 years later than in SEPTAVIH, were more likely to be on dual therapy (63.5% in our study versus 28.2% in SEPTAVIH). This may partly explain our median number of 8 daily medications [6;11], which is lower than the 13 [2;38] daily medications reported by Greene et al. in people living with HIV aged 60 years and older, with a median of 4 antiretrovirals [66]. The pharmacist intervention revealed only 5 interactions requiring therapeutic modification (7.9%), which is lower than that found by this author who reported 11% of drug interactions requiring therapeutic modification and 70% of cases where the interaction required consideration of therapeutic modification [67]. Furthermore, the participants of our study had surprisingly few anticholinergic medications: 6.4% of participants had an ACB score ≥ 4, compared with 15% of participants with treatments with a significant anticholinergic effect in the same study. Finally, the number of therapeutic modifications indicated and carried out was relatively low compared to the rate of polypharmacy. The pharmacist intervention remains essential and all therapeutic drug modifications were carried out.

Secondly, the HIV clinician acceptance of performing a geriatric screening in an infectious disease unit was a major obstacle as well as the acceptance of the people living with HIV. The discrepancy between the number of referrals indicated based on the screening test, and the number of referrals planned and actually implemented and scheduled is quite large. There were some scores that excessively indicated a referral but that were not deemed clinically relevant. Furthermore, based on a review of the medical file and proposals made during previous consultations, it was deemed that some changes were not appropriate. Conversely, there were scores that seemed appropriate but were insufficiently heeded by the HIV clinician or the participant declined the offer. This discrepancy between the number of consultations indicated and those planned had to be analysed in detail.

It should be noted that in most cases where a referral to a physiotherapist was indicated but not implemented, care was already underway. The majority of referrals to social workers and/or dieticians were not suggested by the HIV clinician.

Almost half of the participants were advised to see a social worker because of an abnormal EPICES score and/or IADL, but only two appointments were made. First, the IADL could be abnormal because certain daily tasks were usually performed by the partner. Therefore, this situation did not require the provision of home help and did not warrant a referral to the social worker. In addition, a high EPICES score could indicate social isolation without loss of independence or financial difficulties, in which case a social worker would have little room for manoeuvre. Nevertheless, a study of American veterans aged 55 and over (HIV + and HIV-) showed that social isolation was associated with an increased risk of hospitalisation and death among people living or not with HIV [65]. Peer groups are underdeveloped in Auvergne and self-stigmatisation of people living with HIV may prevent them from benefiting from the social support networks available for the general population [68]. It would therefore be very useful to overcome any form of self-stigmatisation from the very beginning of care, through acceptance and commitment therapies, and to have more options for socially isolated people who lack support system.

More than 40% of the participants were at risk or likely to be malnourished according to the MNA-SF. In comparison, studies in the general population aged 75 years and older in Germany and Spain found that 15–20% of people living with HIV were at risk of malnutrition according to the MNA or MNA-SF [45]. Referral to a dietician was offered very rarely compared to the number of people for whom it was indicated. Some HIV clinicians prescribed oral nutritional supplements, but did not report them on the forms. It should also be noted that there was a period of several months during the study when there was no dietician in the outpatient clinic, and that participants of lower socioeconomic levels could not consult a non-hospital dietician, as these consultations are not reimbursed. In any case, the nutritional status of older people living with HIV has to be better addressed.

At last, an abnormal IHDS score - which is a screening of cognitive impairment and not a diagnostic score - was not associated with the prescription of geriatric consultation in people for whom it was indicated, unlike the Fried criteria and the GFST [14, 47, 48]. The prevalence of neurocognitive disorders in people living with HIV remains very heterogeneous depending on the tests used and also on the study population [69–71]. In our study we chose the IHDS, which has been developed and validated for the detection of HIV-related neurocognitive disorders and was positive in 48% of participants. One explanation of the poor account taken of this criterion for referral to a geriatrician is that the neurocognitive damage induced by HIV had been known and stable for many years, to the extent that the HIV clinician saw no benefit in referring them to a geriatrician. The MOCA test used in other studies to screen for HIV-related cognitive impairment, as well as impairment associated with neurodegenerative diseases, appears to be more relevant and is now proposed in the next GDH [72].

Of the 22 participants identified as being at risk for depression, 15 had a history of depression and 10 were still being treated for depression at the time of their geriatric outpatient screening day. They were offered referral to a psychiatrist, but most of the participants declined the offer. In these cases, several options are possible for the physician according to the participant’s wishes: a psychiatric treatment may be started or changed if already prescribed, or a referral to a psychologist may be suggested. We hope that the HIV clincian’s follow-up will focus on monitoring mood trends.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of our study include the single-centre nature of the study and the relatively small size of the study population. However, to our knowledge, this geriatric screening leading to proposals of change in the care pathway combined with the evaluation of the planning and implementation of these changes has rarely been described. Note that adjustments were not feasible in statistical analysis due to sample size. These results are largely descriptive and could potentially be biased by confounding or selection bias.

Among the variables collected, we did not look for a history of falls, which is an important prognostic factor in the elderly population. If a fall had been reported, physiotherapy sessions could have been offered immediately. Similarly, hearing and vision loss were not systematically assessed, although these are also very important prognostic factors in this elderly population.

Frailty criteria were measured in a less precise way than in other previous studies of frailty. This can be explained by the need to adapt the measure for better feasibility in real life. In fact, the geriatric screening involved multiple assessments, which resulted in a much longer assessment time than a standard consultation, estimated at about 1.5 h per participant.

As the geriatric screening was carried out by infectious disease physicians and a nurse with a long history of working in infectious diseases, it is likely that the lack of geriatric expertise may have led to poorer performance than if the assessment had been carried out by a geriatrician from the outset. However, it should be noted that the screening was based on scores and thresholds that had been previously established with a geriatrician.

Strengths of the study

To our knowledge, this study is the first to report and evaluate a geriatric screening program systematically offered to people living with HIV aged 70 years and older by a team of nurses/pharmacists/physicians in an infectious disease unit, rather than by a geriatrician from the outset. Collaboration between geriatric medicine and infectious diseases took place both before the screening, when the scores were selected, and after the screening. Collaboration also took place at a later stage if the geriatrician saw the participant in consultation for a more detailed assessment at the end of the screening.

The originality of our study lies in the combination of a screening program with an evaluation of the change in the healthcare pathway based on this screening. This real-life study therefore provides initial information on the feasibility and contribution of geriatric screening in outpatient day clinics for people living with HIV aged 70 and over. This is in addition to standard follow-up consultations and annual global cardiovascular risk and cancer screening over 50 years of age. Now, in our department, a geriatric assessment is available as part of the continuity of care for each person concerned.

Conclusions

Following a geriatric assessment, 54% of people living with HIV aged 70 years and over had a planned change in their healthcare pathway and 19% of our participants were referred to a geriatrician. This study shows that an HIV clinician using a geriatric screening tool can successfully identify those who would benefit from a multidisciplinary approach and that this leads to improvements in care. Some improvements for better screening tools in certain domains are needed, for example the replacement of IHDS by the MOCA. Better acceptance of these screening tests by HIV clinicians and by people living with HIV is also important. This can be improved by developing appropriate medical trainings and patient education materials. Social support, nutritional assessments and referrals to physiotherapists are not to be left out of the equation.
