Bioinformatic analysis of differentially expressed mitochondrion-related genes, immune cell infiltration, and diagnostic value in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Lingjia Tang, Ningning Wu, Yao Zhu, Hong Xu, Yuxuan Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation are critically implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, a systematic exploration of key mitochondrion-related genes (MRGs) in AD, and their specific roles in reshaping the immune microenvironment and serving as diagnostic biomarkers, remains insufficient.MethodsTo address this, we conducted an integrative bioinformatics analysis. Differentially expressed MRGs were identified from public AD transcriptomic datasets. Their biological functions were elucidated through enrichment analyses. The correlations between core MRGs and ssGSEA-derived immune-cell signature enrichment scores were quantified using transcriptome-based computational analysis. Finally, machine learning models were constructed and validated to assess the diagnostic potential of identified MRG signatures.ResultsA robust set of dysregulated MRGs was identified in AD brains, showing predominant enrichment in pathways of oxidative phosphorylation and energy metabolism. Notably, the expression of key MRGs correlated significantly with altered infiltration abundances of specific immune cell types, including neutrophil-, eosinophil-, NK CD56bright cell-, and T follicular helper cell-related signatures. A diagnostic model constructed from a refined MRG signature exhibited promising predictive accuracy, with area under the curve (AUC) values reaching approximately 0.82 in the training cohort and around 0.74 in independent validation cohorts.ConclusionOur study defines a novel landscape of MRGs in AD, deciphers their tight crosstalk with the immune microenvironment, and establishes a promising MRG-based signature for AD diagnosis. These findings provide fresh insights into the potential molecular interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation in AD and nominate candidate mitochondrion-related biomarkers and regulatory mechanisms that warrant further experimental and clinical validation.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that represents a profound global health crisis, inflicting severe cognitive decline, functional impairment, and immense socio-economic burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [1–4]. The definitive histopathological hallmarks of AD are the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [3–5]. These canonical pathways have dominated therapeutic and diagnostic research for decades, leading to revised diagnostic criteria that incorporate these biomarkers [6, 7]. Despite significant advances, effective disease-modifying treatments remain elusive, underscoring the complexity of AD pathogenesis and the urgent need to explore supplementary and interactive pathological mechanisms to identify novel therapeutic vulnerabilities and reliable diagnostic markers [2, 8].

Beyond Aβ and tau, two interconnected pathological pillars—mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation—have garnered substantial evidence as critical drivers of disease progression [9–12]. Mitochondria are essential for neuronal bioenergetics, calcium buffering, and apoptosis regulation. In AD, mitochondrial integrity is compromised, leading to bioenergetic deficits, excessive reactive oxygen species production, and impaired dynamics [10, 11, 13]. Transcriptomic and proteomic studies have consistently revealed alterations in genes related to oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial biogenesis in AD brains [9, 11]. Concurrently, sustained neuroinflammation, characterized by activated microglia, astrocytes, and infiltrating peripheral immune cells, exacerbates neuronal damage [12–14]. Notably, specific immune cell subpopulations, such as neutrophils interacting with microglia or distinct T-cell subsets, have been recently implicated in AD pathogenesis in a sex-dependent or context-specific manner [13–15].

Intriguingly, these processes do not occur in isolation. A burgeoning body of literature posits a vicious cycle: dysfunctional mitochondria can release damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) that activate innate immune responses [16–18], while pro-inflammatory cytokines can further disrupt mitochondrial function [19–21]. Recent studies have begun to probe this interface, revealing mitochondrial proteomic signatures in peripheral T cells of AD patients and highlighting the role of specific mitochondrial proteases like Caseinolytic Protease P (CLPP) in immune and stress responses [22–27]. However, a critical gap persists. Most research investigates these domains (mitochondrial genes vs. immune signatures) separately. While pioneering bioinformatic studies have developed immune infiltration-related diagnostic models [16, 17], a systematic, genome-wide interrogation that specifically focuses on mitochondrion-related genes (MRGs), elucidates their differential expression landscape in AD, and precisely maps their correlations with finely resolved immune cell subpopulations (e.g., CD4 + T cells, M2 microglia) remains largely unaddressed. This gap limits a holistic understanding of the mitochondrial-immune axis and hinders the development of biomarkers that capture this essential crosstalk.

The integration of advanced bioinformatics and machine learning offers a powerful paradigm to bridge this knowledge gap. Multi-omics integration and network-based analyses enable the identification of key drivers from complex biological data [18–20]. Machine learning algorithms are particularly adept at distilling high-dimensional transcriptomic data into robust, minimal diagnostic signatures with high predictive accuracy [16–18]. Furthermore, sophisticated deconvolution algorithms now allow for the precise estimation of specific immune cell type abundances from bulk tissue RNA-sequencing data, moving the field beyond generic “neuroinflammation” metrics to a detailed cellular census [16]. By applying this integrated analytical pipeline—which combines differential MRG expression analysis, functional enrichment, detailed immune infiltration profiling, and machine-learning-based biomarker discovery—we can transition from observing associations to inferring mechanistic networks and building predictive tools. This approach holds significant promise for deciphering the specific MRGs that functionally link mitochondrial homeostasis to immune dysregulation in AD, a task beyond the reach of conventional, single-pathway investigations.

To address this, our study implemented a comprehensive bioinformatic strategy. First, we integrated multiple AD transcriptomic datasets to identify a core set of consistently dysregulated MRGs. Second, we performed functional enrichment analysis to uncover their implicated biological pathways. Third, we quantified the infiltration landscape of diverse immune cell subpopulations in AD and constructed detailed correlation networks between pivotal MRGs and specific immune cell types. Finally, we developed and validated a diagnostic model based on a refined MRG signature using machine learning. This work provides an integrative perspective on the mitochondrial-immune interplay in AD and may help generate hypotheses for understanding disease-associated molecular changes and prioritizing candidate diagnostic biomarkers for further validation.

Materials and methods

Microarray data acquisition and preprocessing

Transcriptomic profiling data for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and matched normal controls were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE5281 dataset (platform: GPL570, Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array), comprising 87 AD and 74 control post-mortem brain samples, served as the primary discovery cohort. The GSE1297 dataset (platform: GPL96, Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array), consisting of 22 AD and 9 control samples, was employed as an independent validation cohort. According to the available dataset information, GSE1297 included AD subjects with varying disease severity and disease-related indices such as MMSE and neurofibrillary tangle scores. However, harmonized Braak staging information was not uniformly available for all samples across GSE5281 and GSE1297. The present analysis was performed based on AD versus control status and was not restricted to only advanced-stage AD or stratified by Braak stage. The identified genes should therefore be interpreted as AD-associated mitochondrial markers in the analyzed public cohorts rather than Braak-stage-specific biomarkers. Raw expression data were preprocessed by adding a pseudocount of 1 followed by log₂ transformation to stabilize variance. Probes not mapped to a unique gene symbol were removed, and expression values for multiple probes corresponding to the same gene were averaged to obtain a single representative value per gene per sample. To minimize dataset-specific technical variation, the discovery dataset (GSE5281) and validation dataset (GSE1297) were preprocessed and analyzed separately rather than being directly merged.

GSE5281 was used for differential expression analysis, feature selection, immune infiltration analysis, and diagnostic model construction, whereas GSE1297 was used only for independent external validation of hub gene expression and diagnostic performance. This strategy was adopted to reduce the influence of cross-platform batch effects between GPL570 and GPL96. The GSE5281 dataset includes post-mortem brain samples from multiple brain regions; in the present study, these samples were analyzed together according to AD versus control status, and brain-region-stratified differential expression or immune-infiltration analyses were not performed.

Because complete and harmonized sample-level metadata, including age, sex, post-mortem interval, medication history, disease stage, and detailed brain-region information, were not uniformly available across the public datasets, these variables could not be systematically incorporated as covariates in the statistical model. Therefore, the potential influence of unmeasured biological and technical confounders was considered when interpreting the results.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis between AD and control samples was performed using the limma (Linear Models for Microarray Data) package (v3.50.0) in R (v4.2.1). A moderated t-statistic was applied, and genes with an absolute log₂ fold-change (|log₂FC|) > 1 and an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) < 0.05 were defined as statistically significant DEGs. The results were visualized using a volcano plot generated with the ggplot2 package (v3.4.4). For subsequent focused analysis, all significant DEGs were ranked according to adjusted p-value, and the top 300 most statistically significant DEGs in total, regardless of up- or down-regulation direction, were selected for intersection with the mitochondrion-related gene set. This cutoff was used as a focused screening strategy to prioritize robustly dysregulated genes and reduce potential noise in downstream feature selection and network analyses. Alternative thresholds or expanded input schemes (e.g., all significant DEGs or all detectable mitochondrial genes) may generate distinct DE-MRG sets and require future robustness validation, particularly when sample sizes, platform coverage, or mitochondrial gene annotations differ across public transcriptomic cohorts and evolving reference databases substantially.

Screening of Mitochondrion-Related Genes (MRGs)

A comprehensive list of 1,136 human genes encoding proteins with strong mitochondrial localization evidence was obtained from the MitoCarta3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways). The intersection between the identified AD-related DEGs and the MitoCarta3.0 gene set was determined using the VennDiagram package (v1.7.3), defining the core set of Differentially Expressed Mitochondrion-Related Genes (DE-MRGs). The expression pattern of these DE-MRGs across samples was visualized in a clustered heatmap using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.13.1).

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological roles of the DE-MRGs, enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment covering Biological Processes (BP), Molecular Functions (MF), and Cellular Components (CC), as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, were performed. Terms and pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. The top 15 most significantly enriched terms/pathways were selected for visualization via chord diagrams to illustrate gene-term relationships.

Construction of Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network

The functional associations among the DE-MRGs were investigated by constructing a PPI network using the STRING database (v11.5, https://string-db.org). An interaction confidence score threshold of > 0.15 (low confidence) was applied to capture a broad connectivity landscape.

Because the number of input DE-MRGs was relatively small, the initial low-confidence threshold was used as an exploratory setting to avoid excessive loss of potentially relevant mitochondrial interactions. To evaluate whether the hub-gene prioritization was dependent on this threshold, additional sensitivity analyses were performed by reconstructing the PPI network using alternative STRING confidence thresholds, including medium-confidence and high-confidence settings. The resulting network structures and retained hub genes were compared with those obtained from the initial analysis. The resulting network was downloaded and subsequently visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software (v3.9.1).

Feature gene selection via LASSO regression

To refine the DE-MRG set into a minimal diagnostic signature, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed. Analysis was performed using the glmnet package (v4.1-3) in R. The binary disease status (AD vs. control) was set as the dependent variable, and the expression matrix of DE-MRGs served as independent variables. A ten-fold cross-validation was implemented to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ), specifically selecting the λ value corresponding to the minimum mean cross-validated error (lambda.min). To reduce the risk of overfitting, LASSO feature selection was performed only in the GSE5281 training cohort. The penalization procedure shrinks the coefficients of less informative variables toward zero, thereby reducing model complexity and retaining only genes with non-zero coefficients under the selected λ value. The independent validation cohort GSE1297 was not used during feature selection or parameter optimization. Genes retaining non-zero coefficients at this optimal λ were selected as candidate feature genes. To further assess the stability of LASSO-based feature selection, we repeated the analysis using alternative regularization criteria, including lambda.min and lambda.1se, and compared the retained feature genes across settings. Genes repeatedly selected across different parameter settings were considered to have higher feature-selection stability.

Identification of hub MRGs

To identify high-confidence hub genes, an integrative approach was used. The candidate feature genes derived from the LASSO regression analysis were intersected with the top-ranking central nodes from the PPI network (identified by high degree centrality within Cytoscape). The overlapping genes from this intersection were defined as candidate hub MRGs and were further evaluated by external dataset validation, diagnostic performance, and sensitivity analyses before being considered final hub MRGs. Their expression patterns were visualized in a correlation heatmap using the pheatmap package. Differential expression of these hub genes was further validated by comparing their levels between AD and control groups in both the training (GSE5281) and external validation (GSE1297) datasets.

Evaluation of diagnostic performance

The diagnostic efficacy of each individual hub MRG was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. GSE5281 was used as the discovery/training cohort, whereas GSE1297 was used as an independent external validation cohort. No samples from GSE1297 were included in the process of differential expression analysis, LASSO-based feature selection, PPI-based hub-gene prioritization, or nomogram construction. ROC analysis in GSE1297 represents an out-of-sample validation of individual hub-gene diagnostic performance. ROC curves were generated separately for each hub gene in both GSE5281 and GSE1297. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the pROC package (v1.18.0). A nomogram integrating the three final hub MRGs was constructed in the GSE5281 training cohort and evaluated using internal calibration. External validation of a fixed-coefficient combined three-gene model was not performed in the present study because of the limited sample size and platform differences of the validation cohort.

Construction and validation of a diagnostic nomogram

An exploratory multivariate nomogram was constructed based on the expression levels of the confirmed hub MRGs using the rms package (v6.3-0) in R. The nomogram was built on the GSE5281 training cohort. Its calibration, reflecting the agreement between predicted and observed AD probabilities, was assessed using a calibration curve with 1,000 bootstrap resamples for internal validation.

Construction of miRNA-mRNA-Transcription Factor (TF) regulatory network

To explore the upstream regulatory context of the hub MRGs, potential transcriptional and post-transcriptional interactions were investigated. Predicted transcription factors (TFs) regulating the hub MRGs were retrieved using the NetworkAnalyst platform. Experimentally validated miRNAs targeting the hub MRGs were obtained from the miRTarBase (v8.0). These interaction data (TF-MRG and miRNA-MRG) were integrated and visualized as a comprehensive regulatory network using Cytoscape. The regulatory network was interpreted as a hypothesis-generating analysis to explore potential upstream mechanisms of hub MRG dysregulation. Regulators connected with more than one hub MRG were considered potentially important candidates because they may coordinate the transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation of multiple mitochondrial genes.

However, these predicted or database-derived interactions were not considered evidence of causality and require future experimental validation.

Analysis of immune-related signature enrichment

The relative enrichment scores of 24 immune-cell-related signatures within the AD and control brain samples (GSE5281 dataset) were estimated using the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm. Because ssGSEA was applied to bulk brain transcriptomic data, the resulting enrichment scores should be interpreted as relative immune-related transcriptional signatures rather than direct measurements of absolute immune-cell numbers. These signatures may reflect peripheral immune-cell infiltration, resident glial activation, vascular or BBB-associated changes, or shared inflammatory gene-expression programs. The ssGSEA was implemented via the GSVA package (v1.42.0) in R, using well-curated gene signatures for specific immune cell populations. Differences in immune cell enrichment scores between the AD and control groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Results were visualized using ggplot2.

Correlation analysis between hub MRGs and immune infiltration

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between the expression levels of each hub MRG and the ssGSEA-derived enrichment scores of significantly altered immune cell types. Correlation coefficients (ρ) and corresponding p-values were calculated. Scatter plots with regression lines, generated using ggplot2, were used to visualize significant correlations (p < 0.05).

Results

Data preprocessing and identification of Mitochondrion-Related Genes (MRGs)

The GSE5281 dataset, comprising 87 AD and 74 healthy control post-mortem brain samples, served as the discovery cohort for initial analysis. Differential expression analysis, applying thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05, identified a total of 1,042 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). This set consisted of 549 significantly upregulated and 493 significantly downregulated genes in AD samples compared to controls, as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 2A). The top 300 most significantly dysregulated genes from this list were selected for subsequent investigation. To pinpoint genes implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction, we intersected this refined DEG list with the human mitochondrial proteome catalog from MitoCarta3.0. Functional enrichment analysis of the identified MRGs

To elucidate the biological significance of the 15 identified DE-MRGs, we performed systematic Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Within the GO framework, these genes were significantly enriched across key categories. For Cellular Component (CC), the most prominent term was “mitochondrial inner membrane.” In Biological Processes (BP), enrichments were observed for “cellular respiration” and “energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds.” Molecular Function (MF) enrichments are detailed in the corresponding figure. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the DE-MRGs were primarily involved in “Oxidative phosphorylation,” “Thermogenesis,” and “Chemical carcinogenesis - reactive oxygen species” pathways (Fig. 3A). The relationships between individual genes and these significant functional terms, as well as the shared involvement of genes across multiple terms, are comprehensively detailed in the chord diagrams (Figs. 3B-E).

Construction of a Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network

To elucidate the functional associations among the proteins encoded by the identified MRGs, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database with the 15 DE-MRGs as input. The resulting network comprised 12 interconnected nodes and 33 edges, indicating a significant degree of functional connectivity. This network was visualized and further analyzed using Cytoscape software (Fig. 4A). Highly connected genes within the PPI network, hereafter referred to as PPI central genes, included OCIAD1, MPV17, NUDT2, DGUOK, SDHB, UQCRC2, HSDL2, MRPS23, DHRS7B, MRPL37, UQCRC1, and CLPP.

Identification of feature genes via LASSO regression

To refine the biomarker signature for AD and prevent model overfitting, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was employed for feature selection. The analysis, performed on the expression data of the initial DE-MRGs, identified a subset of genes whose expression patterns were most strongly associated with AD status. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined through ten-fold cross-validation (Fig. 5A), leading to the selection of eight genes with non-zero coefficients (Fig. 5B). These eight feature genes were: MPV17, NUDT2, HSDL2, DHRS7B, CLPP, STAR, SLC25A18, and SPHKAP.

Integration and validation of hub MRGs

To converge on a set of high-confidence hub genes, an integrative approach was adopted. The eight feature genes derived from LASSO regression were intersected with the twelve core nodes from the PPI network. This intersection yielded five overlapping genes, which were designated as candidate hub MRGs: MPV17, NUDT2, HSDL2, DHRS7B, and CLPP (Fig. 6A). A heatmap visualizing the expression of these five genes clearly distinguished AD samples from controls in the training set (Fig. 6B). Subsequent validation of their differential expression across independent cohorts revealed a consistent pattern. In both the training set (GSE5281) and the external validation set (GSE1297), the expression levels of MPV17, DHRS7B, and CLPP were significantly downregulated in the AD group compared to controls (Fig. 6C, D). In contrast, the differential expression of NUDT2 and HSDL2 did not reach statistical significance in the validation cohort. Consequently, three genes—MPV17, DHRS7B, and CLPP—were robustly validated and defined as the final hub MRGs for all subsequent analyses. To examine whether hub-gene identification was sensitive to the STRING confidence threshold or LASSO parameter setting, we performed additional sensitivity analyses. When the PPI network was reconstructed using more stringent STRING confidence thresholds, the overall number of retained interactions decreased, as expected. Nevertheless, MPV17, DHRS7B, and CLPP remained consistently supported by the integrated evidence from differential expression, feature selection, network prioritization, and external validation. Similarly, comparison of LASSO feature-selection results under alternative regularization settings showed that these three genes were among the most stable candidates. These findings indicate that the final hub MRGs were not solely dependent on the initial low-confidence PPI network or a single LASSO parameter setting. This cross-parameter consistency further supports their prioritization while recognizing that functional experiments remain necessary for definitive mechanistic validation in AD.

Construction and performance of diagnostic models based on hub MRGs

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the identified hub MRGs (MPV17, DHRS7B, and CLPP), we constructed a classification model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that these genes collectively could effectively distinguish AD patients from healthy controls. In the training cohort GSE5281, the individual AUC values were 0.821 for DHRS7B (95% CI: 0.755–0.888), 0.819 for MPV17 (95% CI: 0.753–0.884), and 0.768 for CLPP (95% CI: 0.697–0.840) (Fig. 7A–C).

External validation of the diagnostic model

The robustness of the diagnostic model was further validated using the independent external dataset GSE1297. ROC analysis in the independent validation cohort GSE1297 yielded AUC values of 0.742 for DHRS7B (95% CI: 0.567–0.918), 0.732 for MPV17 (95% CI: 0.549–0.916), and 0.737 for CLPP (95% CI: 0.519–0.956) (Fig. 7D–F). The three-gene nomogram was constructed using the GSE5281 training cohort and assessed by internal calibration. Because of the relatively small sample size and platform differences of GSE1297, external validation of a fixed-coefficient combined three-gene model was not performed in the present study.

Furthermore, as an exploratory analysis, we integrated the expression levels of these three hub MRGs into a nomogram in the GSE5281 training cohort to estimate AD probability (Fig. 8A). The calibration curve of the nomogram showed acceptable internal agreement between predicted and observed outcomes in the training cohort (Fig. 8B). However, because a fixed-coefficient combined three-gene model was not externally validated in GSE1297, the nomogram should be interpreted as exploratory.

Construction of a miRNA-mRNA-transcription factor regulatory network

To explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms of the hub MRGs, we constructed a comprehensive miRNA-mRNA-TF interaction network. Using miRTarBase, we identified 131 miRNAs potentially targeting DHRS7B, MPV17, and CLPP transcripts. Concurrently, data from NetworkAnalyst revealed 17 transcription factors (TFs) predicted to regulate the expression of these genes. Integration of these interactions resulted in an exploratory co-regulatory network comprising 151 nodes, including 3 final hub MRGs, 17 TFs, and 131 miRNAs, with 220 edges (Fig. 9A). This network was retained in the main text to provide an upstream regulatory context for CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B. Specifically, it highlights candidate transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulators that may contribute to the downregulation of these mitochondrion-related genes and may help generate hypotheses regarding the connection between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-related transcriptional alterations in AD. However, because these interactions were derived from regulatory databases, they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of regulatory causality. This regulatory network provides an upstream perspective for interpreting the coordinated downregulation of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B in AD. Rather than serving only as a list of predicted regulators, the network highlights potential transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms that may converge on mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes.

Regulators connected to multiple hub MRGs may be of particular biological interest because they could simultaneously influence mitochondrial proteostasis, mitochondrial DNA maintenance, and redox-associated metabolic processes. Several TFs shown in the network, such as STAT1, JUN, and TP53, are broadly involved in cellular stress, inflammatory signaling, or injury-response pathways, suggesting that AD-associated inflammatory and stress-related signals may contribute to the suppression of mitochondrial homeostasis genes. Meanwhile, miRNAs targeting the hub MRGs may provide an additional post-transcriptional mechanism for maintaining reduced MRG expression. Therefore, this network prioritizes candidate regulatory axes for future mechanistic studies linking upstream regulatory dysregulation, mitochondrial impairment, and immune microenvironment alterations in AD.

Landscape of immune-related signature enrichment in AD

The immune-related transcriptional landscape in AD was characterized by applying the ssGSEA algorithm to quantify enrichment scores of 24 immune-cell-related signatures and immune functions. Comparative analysis revealed significant alterations in the AD group relative to controls (Fig. 10A). Given the bulk-tissue nature of the transcriptomic data, these altered immune-cell signatures should be interpreted as relative enrichment of immune-associated transcriptional programs and cannot distinguish BBB-mediated peripheral immune-cell infiltration from resident glial inflammatory activation. Specifically, macrophage-, neutrophil-, eosinophil-, plasmacytoid dendritic cell-, T helper cell-, and effector memory T cell-related enrichment scores were significantly increased in AD. Conversely, mast cell-, NK CD56bright cell-, and T follicular helper cell-related enrichment scores were decreased. A correlation heatmap further delineated the interplay among these immune subsets (Fig. 10B), showing, for instance, positive correlations between neutrophils and B cells/eosinophils, and negative correlations between NK CD56bright cells and B cells/macrophages.

Correlation between hub mrgs and immune-related signature enrichment

We investigated the relationship between the hub MRGs and altered immune-related signature enrichment scores using Spearman correlation analysis. Lollipop plots illustrated significant correlations (Fig. 10C-E). MPV17 expression showed a positive correlation with NK CD56bright cell- and TFH-related enrichment scores, but a negative correlation with neutrophil- and eosinophil-related enrichment scores. Similarly, CLPP was positively correlated with NK CD56bright cells and negatively correlated with eosinophils and neutrophils. DHRS7B exhibited a positive correlation with TFH and negative correlations with neutrophils and eosinophils. These findings suggest a potential association between the expression of these mitochondrial genes and immune-related transcriptional profiles in AD. The consistency of the correlation direction across the three hub MRGs further supports the robustness of this association. Specifically, lower expression of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B was generally associated with higher enrichment of neutrophils and eosinophils, whereas relatively higher expression was associated with NK CD56bright cells and TFH cells. This pattern suggests that mitochondrial gene suppression may be accompanied by a shift toward a more pro-inflammatory immune microenvironment. Nevertheless, because immune infiltration was estimated from bulk transcriptomic data, these results should be interpreted as correlative and hypothesis-generating. In particular, macrophage-like or myeloid immune signatures may partially overlap with microglial transcriptional programs in brain tissue, while lymphoid and granulocyte-related signatures may reflect either peripheral immune involvement or inflammation-associated transcriptomic changes near the vascular or BBB interface.

Discussion

This study employed an integrated bioinformatics approach to elucidate the role of mitochondrion-related genes (MRGs) in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with a specific focus on their intersection with the brain’s immune microenvironment. By analyzing transcriptomic data from AD and control brains, we identified a core set of dysregulated MRGs. Through a multi-step analytical pipeline involving differential expression analysis, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction, and machine learning-based feature selection, we converged on three hub MRGs—CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B—that were robustly downregulated in AD. Furthermore, we demonstrated their significant diagnostic potential and revealed distinct correlations between their expression and specific immune cell subpopulations, including neutrophils, eosinophils, NK CD56bright cells, and T follicular helper (TFH) cells. These findings suggest that CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B may represent candidate molecular nodes associated with both mitochondrial dysfunction and immune dysregulation in AD. Because the PPI network was relatively small, network centrality was interpreted only as a gene-prioritization strategy rather than as direct mechanistic evidence. Thus, the final selection of these three hub MRGs was supported by multiple lines of evidence, including differential expression, LASSO-based feature selection, external validation, diagnostic performance, and biological plausibility. Previous studies have suggested that mitochondrial dysfunction and immune alterations may already occur during mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and recent bioinformatics studies have identified mitochondria-related candidates such as NDUFA1 and NDUFS5 in MCI or late-onset AD. However, CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B have not been systematically evaluated together in relation to immune-cell infiltration in MCI cohorts.

Therefore, our findings may extend the current mitochondrial-immune framework in AD, while their relevance to MCI and AD progression requires validation in longitudinal or stage-stratified datasets. The miRNA-mRNA-TF regulatory network further provides a potential upstream regulatory framework for these hub MRGs. This analysis was retained in the main text because it helps prioritize candidate TFs and miRNAs that may regulate CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B and thereby contribute to mitochondrial gene dysregulation in AD. From a mechanistic perspective, transcriptional regulators associated with cellular stress or inflammatory signaling, together with miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation, may influence mitochondrial proteostasis, mitochondrial DNA maintenance, and redox-associated metabolism. Nevertheless, the network remains exploratory, and the predicted or database-derived interactions require experimental validation in AD-relevant cellular or animal models.

Our results align with and extend the growing body of evidence implicating mitochondrial dysfunction as a central player in AD pathogenesis, beyond the canonical amyloid and tau pathologies [2, 3]. Our findings are biologically plausible when interpreted in the context of previous evidence on mitochondrial dysfunction in AD, MCI, and other neurodegenerative disorders. CLPP encodes the proteolytic core of the mitochondrial ClpXP protease complex and is involved in mitochondrial protein quality control and mitochondrial proteostasis[28, 29]. Although direct CLPP-centered evidence in AD remains limited, impaired mitochondrial proteostasis and mitochondrial stress responses have been increasingly recognized as contributors to AD-related neuronal vulnerability. In other neurodegenerative contexts, particularly Parkinson’s disease, CLPP has been linked to α-synuclein-associated mitochondrial proteotoxic stress, supporting the broader relevance of CLPP-mediated mitochondrial quality control in neurodegeneration.

Therefore, the downregulation of CLPP observed in our study may reflect impaired mitochondrial proteostasis in AD rather than serving as definitive evidence of a causal disease mechanism.

MPV17 is a mitochondrial inner membrane protein involved in mitochondrial DNA maintenance.

Pathogenic variants in MPV17 are known to cause hepatocerebral mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome, a disorder characterized by mitochondrial DNA depletion, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and neurological manifestations [30, 31]. Although MPV17 has not been extensively studied in AD or MCI, mitochondrial DNA instability, mitochondrial DNA damage, and mitochondrial DNA-derived inflammatory signaling have been implicated in AD pathobiology.

Thus, reduced MPV17 expression may be biologically relevant because impaired mitochondrial DNA maintenance could contribute to energy failure, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage-associated inflammatory signaling [32, 33]. Compared with CLPP and MPV17, DHRS7B is less well characterized in neurodegenerative diseases. DHRS7B belongs to the dehydrogenase/reductase family and is predicted to participate in oxidoreductase activity and lipid-related metabolic processes. Given that redox imbalance, lipid metabolic disturbance, and mitochondrial dysfunction are closely associated with AD pathogenesis, DHRS7B downregulation may indicate disruption of metabolic or redox homeostasis. However, because direct experimental evidence linking DHRS7B to AD, MCI, or other neurodegenerative diseases is currently scarce, this gene should be interpreted as an exploratory candidate requiring further functional validation. Previous studies in MCI and late-onset AD have also supported the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction at early or prodromal disease stages. For example, mitochondria-related candidate genes such as NDUFA1 and NDUFS5 have been reported in MCI/late-onset AD diagnostic analyses. However, the three hub MRGs identified in the present study, CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B, have not been systematically evaluated together in relation to immune-cell infiltration in MCI cohorts. Therefore, our results extend the existing mitochondrial framework by nominating these genes as candidate mitochondrial-immune-associated markers in AD, while their stage-specific relevance and potential involvement in MCI-to-AD progression remain to be validated in longitudinal and experimentally supported studies.

Beyond their individual gene-level implications, CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B may be interpreted within a broader framework of mitochondrial energy failure, mitochondrial quality control, mitophagy, ferroptosis, and mtDAMP-mediated immune activation. CLPP participates in mitochondrial proteostasis through the ClpXP protease system; therefore, reduced CLPP expression may indicate impaired removal of misfolded or damaged mitochondrial proteins, which could compromise respiratory-chain stability and mitochondrial quality-control capacity. Previous experimental evidence has also linked CLPP deficiency to altered mitochondrial protein synthesis, neurodegeneration, oxidative phosphorylation dysfunction, and neuroinflammatory responses, supporting the broader relevance of CLPP-mediated mitochondrial quality control in neurological disease contexts [34]. MPV17 is involved in mitochondrial DNA maintenance and has recently been linked to mitochondrial glutathione import and ferroptosis regulation in non-neuronal disease models, suggesting that MPV17 downregulation may increase vulnerability to oxidative lipid damage and ferroptosis-related mitochondrial injury [35]. DHRS7B is less well characterized, but as a member of the dehydrogenase/reductase family, its reduced expression may reflect disruption of redox-associated metabolic homeostasis. Although direct evidence linking DHRS7B to AD is limited, a related dehydrogenase/reductase family member, DHRS7, has recently been implicated in immune-cell interaction abnormalities and inflammatory signaling in an inflammatory disease context, suggesting that this gene family may warrant further exploration in immune-metabolic regulation [36]. These mitochondrial disturbances may further interact with mitophagy and inflammatory signaling. Inefficient mitochondrial quality control or mitophagy can result in the persistence of damaged mitochondria, leading to excessive ROS production, mitochondrial DNA instability, lipid peroxidation, and the release or extracellular transfer of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns. mtDAMPs, including mitochondrial DNA, mitochondrial proteins, and mitochondria-derived vesicular cargo, can activate innate immune pathways and promote neuroinflammatory responses. Recent studies have highlighted mitochondria-derived vesicles and extracellular vesicles as emerging mediators of mitochondrial stress communication, immune regulation, and neurological disease-associated mitochondrial signaling [37, 38]. In this context, the observed negative correlations between the three hub MRGs and pro-inflammatory immune-cell signatures, such as neutrophils and eosinophils, may reflect a potential link between impaired mitochondrial homeostasis and immune microenvironment remodeling in AD. Nevertheless, these mechanistic connections are inferred from bioinformatics associations and prior literature, and therefore require direct validation in cellular, animal, and patient-derived experimental systems.

The immune-deconvolution findings should also be interpreted in relation to the glial and BBB-associated inflammatory axis in AD. The increased enrichment scores of neutrophils, eosinophils, T-cell subsets, and macrophage-like signatures do not necessarily indicate direct BBB transmigration of these cells, because bulk brain transcriptomic profiles may capture overlapping signals from peripheral immune cells, endothelial or vascular-associated changes, and activated resident glia. Microglia and astrocytes are central mediators of AD neuroinflammation, and representative markers such as AIF1, CX3CR1, P2RY12, TMEM119, TREM2, and C1QA/C1QB/C1QC for microglia, and GFAP, AQP4, ALDH1L1, SLC1A3, GJA1, and SERPINA3 for astrocytes, are important for interpreting cell-type-specific inflammatory responses.

CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B are not canonical microglial or astrocytic markers; rather, they are mitochondrion-related genes that may influence glial function indirectly through mitochondrial proteostasis, mitochondrial DNA maintenance, redox balance, ROS production, mitophagy, and mtDAMP-related signaling. Therefore, the observed associations between these MRGs and immune-cell signatures may reflect a broader mitochondrial-glial-immune interaction rather than a simple peripheral immune-cell infiltration event.

A novel and significant aspect of our findings is the connection between these mitochondrial hub genes and the altered immune landscape in AD. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed a pro-inflammatory shift characterized by significant increases in neutrophils and eosinophils, alongside decreases in NK CD56bright and TFH cells. This profile aligns with recent studies highlighting the involvement of innate immune cells like neutrophils in AD neuroinflammation and vascular dysfunction [39, 40], and suggests a potential impairment in immunomodulatory cell populations [41]. The surprising and insightful finding lies in the consistent negative correlations between the three hub MRGs (CLPP, MPV17, DHRS7B) and pro-inflammatory neutrophils/eosinophils, coupled with positive correlations with the diminished NK CD56bright and TFH cells. This inverse relationship suggests a potential mechanistic link: impaired mitochondrial function in brain cells, signaled by low expression of these MRGs, may create or perpetuate an inflammatory microenvironment that favors the recruitment and activity of specific myeloid lineages while suppressing certain lymphoid subsets [42–44]. This observation provides a plausible cellular mechanism for the established concept that mitochondrial-derived damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) can drive neuroinflammation [23]. Our data suggest that deficiencies in CLPP and MPV17, by fostering proteotoxic stress and mtDNA instability, may amplify the release of such mtDAMPs. This, in turn, could activate resident microglia [22] and recruit peripheral immune cells like neutrophils, forming a “neuroinflammatory axis” that exacerbates disease progression [45]. The correlation with TFH cells, though requiring further investigation, hints at a possible interplay between mitochondrial health and adaptive immune regulation in the brain.

Our findings contribute significantly to the evolving theoretical model of AD as a disease of disrupted cellular metabolism and maladaptive neuroimmune communication. They provide empirical support for the “mitochondrial-immune axis” hypothesis, which posits a bidirectional, pathogenic feedback loop between these two systems [21, 23]. We identify specific molecular players (CLPP, MPV17, DHRS7B) whose dysregulation may initiate or feed into this loop. From a systems biology perspective, these MRGs can be viewed as critical nodes whose failure disrupts the broader network of cellular homeostasis, leading to predictable downstream consequences: energy depletion, oxidative stress, proteostasis collapse, and immune activation. Furthermore, the moderate-to-good diagnostic performance of these MRGs in an independent validation dataset suggests that their expression patterns may capture reproducible AD-associated transcriptomic alterations, although further validation in larger and clinically well-characterized cohorts is required. This supports the notion that mitochondrial dysfunction is not a late-stage epiphenomenon but an integral component of the disease process with biomarker potential. Our constructed nomogram translates this biological insight into a potential clinical tool, offering a step towards a multi-parameter, mechanism-based diagnostic approach.

While this study offers novel insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based on publicly available transcriptomic datasets from post-mortem brain tissue. This introduces inherent limitations, including potential confounding factors from brain region, sex, age, agonal state, post-mortem interval, medication history, disease stage, and other unmeasured sample-level variables. In addition, although the discovery and validation datasets were analyzed separately to reduce cross-platform batch effects, residual dataset-specific effects cannot be fully excluded. Such factors may influence both mitochondrial gene expression and immune-cell deconvolution estimates derived from bulk transcriptomic data. Therefore, the associations observed between hub MRGs and immune cell infiltration should be interpreted as exploratory and correlative. Future studies using larger harmonized cohorts with complete brain-region annotation, covariate information, Braak staging, MCI samples, and longitudinal MCI-to-AD conversion data are needed to validate the robustness and stage-specific relevance of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B. The cellular heterogeneity of bulk brain transcriptomic data also means that observed expression changes and immune-deconvolution signatures cannot be unequivocally assigned to specific cell types or processes, such as neurons, microglia, astrocytes, endothelial/BBB-associated cells, or true peripheral immune-cell infiltration. In addition, because the discovery cohort contained samples from different brain regions, regional heterogeneity may have contributed to the observed mitochondrial gene-expression and immune-signature differences.

Future studies employing single-cell or single-nucleus RNA sequencing are crucial to delineate the cell-type-specific expression patterns of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B. Second, our findings are correlative. This limitation also applies to the miRNA-mRNA-TF regulatory network. Although the network was constructed using established regulatory databases and provides biologically plausible candidates, the predicted TF-MRG and miRNA-MRG relationships were not experimentally validated in this study. Therefore, the network should be regarded as a prioritization tool for future mechanistic studies rather than definitive evidence of regulatory causality. While we propose mechanistic links based on established gene functions and observed immune correlations, causal relationships cannot be inferred from bioinformatics analysis alone.

Functional validation in in vitro and in vivo models is essential. For example, genetically modulating the expression of these MRGs in neuronal or glial cultures and assessing subsequent impacts on mitochondrial function, mtDAMP release, and inflammatory signaling would test our hypotheses directly. Similarly, examining how knocking down these genes affects immune cell profiles in animal models of AD would be highly informative. Third, reliance on microarray data from two primary cohorts, though validated across datasets, necessitates replication in larger, more diverse, and multi-center cohorts, including those with longitudinal design and detailed clinical stratification (e.g., by APOE genotype, disease stage). Although the diagnostic performance of individual hub genes was externally evaluated in GSE1297, the combined three-gene nomogram was constructed and internally calibrated only in the GSE5281 training cohort. External validation of a fixed-coefficient combined model was not performed because of the limited sample size and cross-platform differences of the validation dataset. The relatively small size of GSE1297 may limit the precision and stability of ROC estimates, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals.

Therefore, the diagnostic performance of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B should be regarded as preliminary and requires validation in larger independent cohorts. Moreover, complete and harmonized Braak staging information was not available for all samples; we could not perform Braak-stage-stratified analyses or determine whether the identified MRG signature is specific to early, intermediate, or advanced AD pathology. The current findings should therefore be interpreted as AD-associated differences in the analyzed cohorts rather than stage-specific or clinically validated diagnostic markers. Future studies incorporating larger harmonized cohorts, Braak staging, MCI samples, prospective validation, and longitudinal MCI-to-AD conversion data will be necessary to clarify the diagnostic utility and disease-progression relevance of CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B. Integrating other omics data, such as proteomics or metabolomics from cerebrospinal fluid or blood, could strengthen the biomarker potential and reveal additional layers of regulation. For future diagnostic translation, the three-gene signature should be validated in larger, multicenter, and prospective cohorts with complete clinical and pathological annotations. In particular, studies including MCI, early AD, and longitudinal MCI-to-AD conversion samples are needed to determine whether CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B have value for early diagnosis or disease-progression monitoring. Because the current analysis was based on post-mortem brain tissue, future work should also test whether this signature is detectable in clinically accessible samples, such as blood or cerebrospinal fluid. A fixed-coefficient diagnostic model should then be externally validated and compared with established AD biomarkers using AUC, 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, calibration, decision-curve analysis, and incremental diagnostic value before clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this integrated bioinformatics study identified CLPP, MPV17, and DHRS7B as candidate mitochondrion-related genes that were downregulated in the analyzed AD transcriptomic datasets and showed exploratory diagnostic relevance. The observed associations between these genes and ssGSEA-derived immune-related enrichment scores suggest a potential link between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-related transcriptional alterations in AD.

However, these findings are computational and correlative, and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of validated clinical biomarkers, therapeutic targets, or direct mitochondrial-immune crosstalk. Further functional experiments, single-cell or single-nucleus analyses, and validation in larger independent clinical cohorts are needed to determine the mechanistic relevance and potential clinical applicability of these genes in AD.
