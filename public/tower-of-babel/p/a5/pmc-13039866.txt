Antiviral innate immunity induces alpha synuclein phosphorylation at serine129 in neurons independent of aggregation
Authors: Dustin L Heiden, Camille Merrick, Robert C Evans, Alexander J Rajic, Madeline Slater, Ravi Mahalingam, Vicki Traina-Dorge, Jennifer N Berger, Leslie J Berg, J David Beckham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlpha synuclein (asyn) is expressed in neurons and is associated with the pathogenesis of synucleinopathies like Parkinson’s Disease. Asyn aggregates are associated with phosphorylation at serine position 129 (pS129), which can be induced by environmental triggers, including viral infection, and the risk increases with aging. We show that brain tissue from West Nile Virus (WNV) infected patients exhibit increased expression of pS129 asyn in neurons of brain tissue. We found that WNV infection, Poly I:C treatment, and type 1 interferon (T1 IFN) treatment elicit the acute induction, followed by rapid degradation of pS129 asyn in olfactory pathways and primary cortical neurons. Induction of pS129 asyn was induced at these early time points independent of asyn aggregates. In IFN receptor knockout neurons, virus infection and Poly I:C stimulation do not induce pS129 asyn, implying that pS129 asyn is dependent on the type 1 interferon receptor. Our findings suggest that infections with DNA and RNA viruses and subsequent antiviral immunity can trigger the acute formation of pS129 asyn, and T1 IFN stimulation in neurons can trigger post-translational modifications in asyn. Further studies evaluating the interactions between pS129 asyn and interferon signaling may provide a common trigger for the formation of pathogenic asyn species.Subject terms: Microbiology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alpha synuclein (asyn) is a small 140 amino acid, intrinsically disordered protein that is largely expressed in neurons where it can be found in various subcellular compartments, such as presynaptic terminals, the cytoplasm, and the nucleus1,2. Under physiological conditions, asyn exists as a natively unfolded monomer that can undergo multiple post-translational modifications, form multimers on plasma membranes, and is reported to form helically folded tetramers,1–4. Asyn is thought to contribute to the pathogenesis of synucleinopathies through a process of insoluble aggregate and fibril formation, eventually leading to the formation of Lewy aggregates that are associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD)5. Previous studies have shown that these aggregates largely contain phosphorylated serine129 asyn (pS129 asyn)6. Together, asyn aggregates and pS129 asyn are important post-translational modifications that are a defining pathological hallmark of synucleinopathies, which are a group of neurodegenerative diseases that include dementia with Lewy bodies, multiple system atrophy, and PD7,8.

Asyn aggregation is a multi-step process that is influenced by various genetic and environmental factors9,10. Patients with pre-motor symptoms of PD can present with olfactory dysfunction, suggesting that olfactory pathways may play an important role in the pathogenesis of PD11. Recent data indicate that neuroinflammation and viral infections may be important triggers for post-translational modifications of asyn12–14. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that individuals who suffered neurotropic and non-neurotropic viral infections, such as severe influenza, have an increased risk of PD and other neurodegenerative diseases as they age15,16. West Nile Virus (WNV), Western Equine Encephalitic Virus (WEEV), and Japanese Encephalitic Virus (JEV) have all been shown to induce Parkinsonian symptoms in humans and PD-like pathology in murine models17–19. Others have shown that WNV-induced encephalitis is associated with symptoms and features of synucleinopathies like PD and Dementia with Lewy Bodies20,21. Intranasal WEEV infection in a murine model was shown to induce the rapid loss of dopaminergic neurons, Parkinsonian symptoms, microglial activation, and the formation of proteinase K-resistant asyn aggregates in olfactory pathways19. However, the mechanism by which virus infection induces asyn post-translational modifications is not known. We have previously shown that asyn knockout mice infected peripherally with WNV exhibited increased viral growth in the central nervous system (CNS), increased disease severity, and increased mortality compared to wild-type (WT) mice17. Subsequent studies showed that asyn plays a chemotactic role in the recruitment of immune cells during viral infections of the gastrointestinal system22–24. Our group recently demonstrated that asyn-null human neurons exhibited increased viral growth due to decreased interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression associated with decreased localization of STAT225. Together, these studies suggest that asyn expression plays a role in antiviral immunity by supporting type I interferon (T1 IFN) signaling in neurons. However, the mechanisms linking interferon signaling with asyn post-translational modifications has not been evaluated.

In this study, we analyzed brain tissue from patients with WNV encephalitis and found evidence of increased pS129 asyn in neurons that also expressed increased STAT2. To investigate how antiviral responses in the olfactory pathway impact asyn post-translational modifications, we developed an intranasal WNV infection model that results in encephalitis and increased ISG expression throughout the brain. We show that virus infection, Poly I:C treatment, and T1 IFN stimulation trigger the formation of pS129 asyn that is quickly degraded in primary neurons and olfactory pathways. During these acute time points, phosphorylation at S129 asyn was not associated with asyn aggregates. We also show that innate immune induction of pS129 asyn is stimulated downstream of the T1 IFN receptor. These data provide mechanistic insights into the physiological triggers of pS129 asyn expression through a type I interferon-dependent mechanism in neurons.

Results

Phospho-S129 alpha synuclein localizes with STAT2 in neurons of patients with WNV encephalitis

We have previously shown that asyn restricts RNA virus growth in the brains of mice by supporting expression of ISGs and the T1 IFN response17,25. Our prior data and others demonstrate a link between viral infections and expression of pS129 asyn in neurons; However, the mechanism for pS129 asyn expression remains undefined.

First, we determined if pS129 asyn is associated with interferon signaling in WNV encephalitis patients. Midbrain tissue from the substantia nigra pars compacta from 5 WNV encephalitis patients compared to 4 control patients was evaluated with immunofluorescence analysis (IFA) and confocal imaging for co-localization of pS129 asyn and STAT2 in MAP2 positive cells as a marker for neurons (Supplementary Table 1). Using IFA of histology brain sections, we determined that brain tissue of individuals infected with WNV expressed increased pS129 asyn compared to control brain tissue (Fig. 1a, b). In WNV encephalitic human brain tissue, pS129 asyn was present in MAP2+ cells, indicating that acute expression of pS129 asyn occurs in neurons and is associated with increased STAT2 expression (Fig. 1c, d). Together, these data show that RNA virus infection in human brain tissue induces pS129 asyn in neurons exhibiting increased STAT2 expression.

Intranasal West Nile virus infection causes encephalitis in a murine model

Prior studies show that intranasal inoculation with Western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) induces infection in olfactory pathways in association with pS129 asyn expression in mice3. Given the localized virus spread, this model provides a time-dependent approach to evaluate pS129 asyn expression along olfactory pathways. Based on these prior studies, we developed a murine model of WNV olfactory infection to define the role of virus infection in pS129 asyn expression in olfactory pathways. We inoculated 8- to 12-week-old C57BL/6 mice intranasally (i.n.) with 106 plaque-forming units (PFU) of TX02 WNV (TX02 strain) or mock inoculum (Fig. 2a). The dose was selected from pilot studies evaluating the lowest dose that produced reproducible viral encephalitis and weight loss. Following i.n. inoculation, mice infected with WNV exhibited significant weight loss compared to mock-inoculated control mice over the course of 7 days post-infection (DPI) (Fig. 2b). Mice were sacrificed at 3- and 7-DPI, and the olfactory bulbs, cortex, midbrain, and cerebellum brain regions were collected to determine the number of viral genomes present in different brain regions distal to olfactory neurons. We found 106–107 WNV genome copies were detected in the olfactory bulb and cortex, 105 WNV genome copies in the midbrain, and 104 genome copies were detected in the cerebellum, indicating widespread viral infection and replication of the brain through olfactory pathways (Fig. 2c). Next, we assessed the antiviral response in each brain region by measuring the production of the canonical ISGs including mx1, ifit1, and trim25. By 3 DPI, mx1 production had increased by 2 to 3-fold across each brain region. By 7 DPI, mx1 expression had differentially increased between each brain region, and mx1 increased by 1800-fold in the olfactory bulbs, 6000-fold in the cortex, 45,000-fold in the cerebellum, and 23,000-fold in the midbrain (Fig. 2d). Ifit1 expression increased 2-fold by 3 DPI, and at 7 DPI, ifit1 expression increased by approximately 200-fold in the olfactory bulbs, cerebellum, and midbrain, and 500-fold in the cortex (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Trim25 increased by 2-fold in the cerebellum, and 7- to 10-fold in the olfactory bulbs, cortex, and midbrain by 7 DPI (Supplementary Fig. 1b). These data demonstrate that following i.n. inoculation, WNV infects and rapidly infiltrates along olfactory pathways and elicits transcription of ISGs.

Since WNV is naturally spread peripherally through mosquito bites in the skin, we also infected C57BL/6 mice by a peripheral route, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection, with 106 PFUs of TX02 WNV and monitored their weight over the duration of infection (Fig. 2e). We assessed infectious spread by determining the number of TX02 WNV genome copies in the cerebellum, midbrain, cortex, and olfactory bulb brain regions over these acute time points. Mice infected i.p. with WNV did not lose weight as quickly as those infected i.n. group, nor did they have a high level of WNV genome copies in brain tissue at these early time points (Fig. 2e, f). Next, we assessed ISG production of mx1, ifit1, and trim25 in mice inoculated i.p. with WNV. ISG production increased at 9 DPI in the i.p. inoculated WNV group despite a low level of WNV genome copies (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). The increases in ISGs are indicative of an interferon response in each brain region by 9 DPI. Finally, we evaluated the expression of snca, the asyn gene, in both i.n. and i.p. infected mice and observed a two-fold decrease in snca transcripts in the olfactory bulb, cortex, and cerebellum of the i.n. infected group by 7 DPI (Fig. 2h). This decrease in snca expression has been observed in previous studies with WNV infections and has been associated with neuronal death26. Additionally, snca expression in the midbrain of mice inoculated with WNV i.n. did not exhibit any changes in scna gene expression, which is likely due to the time point of the assay and synaptic distance from the inoculation site. Similarly, the mice inoculated with WNV i.p. did not exhibit the same trend of decreased snca expression but did exhibit a significant increase in snca production in the olfactory bulbs 9 DPI, likely due to early innate immune responses related to peripheral infection prior to viral neuroninvasion and neuron death in the i.p. model (Fig. 2i). These data show that WNV induces ISG expression in olfactory pathways associated with differential regulation of snca gene expression. Since these studies suggested a role for immune-induced changes in snca expression, we next evaluated the role of the innate immune response in asyn expression patterns in neurons.

Infection with DNA or RNA viruses elicits pS129 asyn expression across species

Next, we determined if WNV infection in olfactory pathways is associated with acute pS129 asyn expression and asyn aggregate formation. C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with mock inoculum or WNV (104 PFU, i.n.), and olfactory bulb tissue was harvested from mice on days 3 and 7 post-infection. Western blot analysis of the olfactory bulb showed evidence of increased expression of pS129 asyn compared to mock-inoculated mice at both day 3 and day 7 post-infection (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 2). Interestingly, there was a trend towards decreased expression of pS129 asyn at day 7 post-infection compared to day 3 post-infection. Next, we determined if mice exhibited evidence of asyn aggregates by isolating protein from the olfactory bulbs of mice. Protein lysates were analyzed using the LEGEND MAXTM alpha synuclein aggregate ELISA from BioLegend®. This kit detects asyn aggregates with minimal cross-reactivity to pS129 asyn monomers27. ELISA of lysates from WNV-infected mice did not exhibit evidence of aggregate formation at day 3 and day 7 post-infection (Fig. 3b). To determine if pS129 asyn expression was specific for RNA virus infection, we next evaluated banked tissue from macaques inoculated with simian varicella virus (SVV), a DNA virus. We found that SVV infection induced expression of pS129 asyn in the dorsal root ganglion of macaques during acute infection (Fig. 3c, d). Similarly, in mice inoculated with herpes simplex virus (HSV-1) by corneal excoriation, we found a significant increase in pS129 asyn in the trigeminal ganglion compared to mock-inoculated mice (Fig. 3e, f). These data suggest that a broadly conserved antiviral mechanism underlies virus-induced pS129 asyn expression in neurons instead of a virus species-specific response.

West Nile Virus infection induces pS129 asyn expression in primary neurons

WNV-infected human and mouse brain tissue exhibited increased expression of pS129 asyn in neurons. Next, we determined the mechanism by which virus infection may induce expression of pS129 asyn in neurons. We utilized murine primary cortical neurons derived from E18 embryos as previously described25. We inoculated primary cortical neurons with WNV (TX02, MOI = 10) or vehicle control and determined the time course of ISG expression that may be associated with asyn post-translational modifications (Fig. 4a). Following WNV infection, mx1 and ifit1 production increased by 8- and 2-fold, respectively, compared to mock inoculation by 4 h post-infection (hpi). Mx1 and ifit1 expression peaked at a 1024- and 64-fold increase relative to vehicle-treated neurons by 48-hpi (Fig. 4b). Next, we evaluated pS129 asyn and alpha synuclein aggregate expression at these early time points 4-, 24-, and 48-hpi. Using a cell-based phospho-serine129 alpha synuclein ELISA to measure changes in pS129 asyn, we found that pS129 asyn expression significantly increased by an average of 3-fold compared to mock vehicle control at the early time point of 4-hpi (Fig. 4c). However, the increase in pS129 asyn quickly wanes as WNV infection continues with a significant decrease in pS129 asyn expression by 48-hpi (Fig. 4c). Next, we determined the production of asyn aggregates in WNV-inoculated neurons at the same time points post-infection, as described above. We found that WNV infection did not induce asyn aggregate formation compared to control-treated neurons (Fig. 4d). Next, we determined if low viral inoculum or viral entry was sufficient to induce pS129 asyn. At a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) as early as 4 h post-infection, WNV still induced pS129 asyn (Supplementary Fig. 3a), implying that viral gene expression was not required to induce pS129 asyn expression. Next, we determined if viral entry was necessary to elicit pS129 asyn by inoculating primary cortical neurons with WNV for one hour to allow for initial viral binding and viral entry only. Interestingly, we found no evidence of increased pS129 asyn expression following viral binding at room temperature or 4 °C (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Finally, we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) on WNV-infected cortical neurons compared to mock-inoculated neurons at 4-, 24-, and 48 h post-infection. We found evidence of increased pS129 asyn expression by 24 h post-infection that did not colocalize with thioflavinS, a stain that detects protein aggregates (Fig. 4e, f). Previous reports show that Pre-formed Fibrils (PFFs) are positive for pS129 asyn and for thioflavinS, thus are a good positive control for asyn aggregates23,28,29. PFF-treated cortical neurons exhibited increased thioflavinS signal, whereas WNV infection did not significantly increase thioflavinS expression above mock-inoculated control neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). By 48 h post-infection with WNV, the degree of cell death in the neuron cultures prevented rigorous quantification of thioflavinS or pS129 asyn. These data demonstrate that WNV infection in the primary neurons induces pS129 asyn expression independent of aggregate formation that resolves by 48 h post-infection in primary neurons.

Acute viral infection and viral RNA sensing induce type 1 Interferon production exclusively

Viral infections and viral RNA sensing induce a state of innate antiviral immunity in neurons, which is characterized by the synthesis and secretion of a family of proteins called interferons30. Despite the importance of virus-induced neuronal interferon responses, the interferon subtypes that regulate neuron ISG expression are not well characterized in the literature. There are three groups of interferons: type 1, type 2, and type 3 interferons (T1 IFN, T2 IFN, and T3 IFN), and we evaluated the role of each interferon type during viral infection and RNA sensing in neurons. To determine which interferons are produced by neurons in response to viral infection and RNA sensing, we infected mouse primary cortical neurons with TX02 WNV (MOI 10) or used a mock control. We collected the cell supernatants and assessed the production of T1, T2, or T3 IFNs. Using ELISAs that detect all IFN-alpha (IFNa) subtypes and IFN-beta (IFNb), we found that WNV (MOI = 10) infection induced IFNa and IFNb by 24-h post-infection (Fig. 5a). WNV-infected primary neurons did not produce IFNy (T2 IFN) or IFNL (T3 IFN) (Fig. 5b).

We next determined the role and time course of viral RNA sensing in the production of T1, T2, and T3 interferons. Mouse primary cortical neurons were treated with the viral RNA mimetic and TLR3 agonist, Poly I:C (25 μg/mL), for 4, 8, and 24 h. Poly I:C treatment induced IFNa secretion by 24 h and significantly increased IFNb secretion by 8 h post-Poly I:C administration (hpa) (Fig. 5c). IFNy and IFNL (T2 and T3 IFN, respectively) were not secreted by mouse primary neurons treated with Poly I:C (Fig. 5d). Since glial subtypes are capable of producing T2 and T3 IFN, we evaluated expression of T2 and T3 interferon as a marker of glial contributions to interferon production. Since we do not detect T2 or T3 IFN production in response to viral infection or TLR3 stimulation, it suggests our primary neuron cultures are composed mostly of neurons. These data show that primary neurons respond to viral infection and viral RNA detection by producing T1 IFN as early as 8 h post-administration.

While Poly I:C can induce expression of interferons, interferon signal amplification should be decreased in alpha synuclein knockout (asyn KO) neurons through paracrine interferon signal transduction based on our previous studies17,25. To evaluate this, we isolated primary cortical neurons from wild-type (WT) and asyn KO mice as described above. Primary neurons were treated with Poly I:C (25 μg/mL) and supernatant was harvested at the indicated time points post-treatment for ELISA analysis of IFNa and IFNb production (Fig. 5e, f). We found that asyn KO neurons exhibited a significant decrease in both IFNa and IFNb production compared to WT neurons. While T1 IFN production was significantly decreased after Poly I:C treatment, its production was not fully eliminated in asyn KO neurons, because Poly I:C can trigger interferon production through Nfkappa-B dependent pathways using pathogen detection mechanisms.

Viral sensing in primary neurons induces reversible pS129 asyn

Viral infections of neurons induce a cascade of cellular events, including viral RNA sensing and innate antiviral immune responses that may contribute to asyn post-translational modifications. WNV-infected neurons sense viral RNA through the expression of pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) receptors, such as toll-like receptors (TLRs)31. To determine if viral sensing through TLR signaling is involved in asyn phosphorylation, we treated mouse primary cortical neurons with a known TLR3 agonist and viral RNA mimetic, Poly I:C (25 μg/mL), compared to control-treated neurons and measured the expression of genes known to be upregulated following Poly I:C treatment using RT-qPCR. Following Poly I:C treatment, we found a >125-fold increase in mx1 and ifit1 expression as early as 4 h post-administration, indicating that mouse primary cortical neurons respond to Poly I:C at time points as early as 4 h (Fig. 6a). Next, we determined if viral RNA sensing induced expression of pS129 asyn. We treated cortical neurons for 4-, 8-, and 24-h and found a significant but small 1.5-fold increase in pS129 asyn expression at 4 hpa and 8 hpa with Poly I:C (Fig. 6b). We next completed IFA on primary cortical neurons treated with Poly I:C and observed increased pS129 asyn expression independent of thioflavinS staining (Fig. 6c, d). Of note, poly I:C treatment of primary neurons failed to increase thioflavinS expression compared to mock-treated control neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

Type 1 interferon stimulation induces pS129 asyn expression independent of aggregation in primary neurons

Given that acute viral infection and viral sensing elicited the production and secretion of T1 IFN in mouse primary cortical neurons, we next determined the ability of T1 IFN signaling to induce asyn phosphorylation. We treated mouse primary cortical neurons with IFNa2 (100 ng/mL) or mock control and found that primary neurons treated with IFNa2 significantly increased gene expression of the canonical interferon-stimulated genes, mx1, ifit1 and trim25 (Fig. 7a). Mx1 exhibited a 256-fold increase compared to mock-treated neurons, ifit1 expression significantly increased 16-fold by 2 h post-IFNa2 treatment, and trim25 reached a 6-fold increase 4 h after IFNa2 treatment (Fig. 7a). To determine if T1 IFN treatment induced expression of pS129 asyn and asyn aggregates, we isolated protein from primary neurons treated with IFNa2 or vehicle control and analyzed the lysates for pS129 asyn and asyn aggregates. Following treatment with IFNa2, we observed a rapid 2-fold increase in pS129 asyn levels 30 min after treatment (Fig. 7b). At these same early time points, we did not detect the presence of asyn aggregates following IFNa2 (Fig. 7c). Next, we evaluated pS129 asyn expression using IFA. We again found that IFNa2 treatment induced expression of pS129 asyn as early as 30 min after IFNa2 treatment of primary neurons (Fig. 7d, e). Similar to poly I:C treatment, IFN treatment of primary neurons failed to increase thioflavinS expression compared to mock-treated control neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4d). The increase in pS129 asyn expression began to decrease by 2 h post-IFNa2 treatment, indicating again that IFNa2-induced pS129 asyn is rapidly reversible during IFN signaling.

Because our studies revealed that asyn phosphorylation occurred 30 min after IFNa2 treatment, we hypothesized that asyn phosphorylation is linked to T1 IFN receptor engagement. To investigate, we made mouse primary neurons from wild-type C57BL/6 mice and Ag129 mice, which lack functional T1 and T2 IFN receptors. Primary WT and Ag129 cortical neurons were treated with IFNa2 or vehicle control for 30 min. Using a cell-based ELISA for pS129 asyn, we assessed the levels of pS129 asyn after IFNa2 treatment. There was a 2-fold increase in the expression of pS129 asyn in WT primary neurons, as we saw previously (Fig. 7f). However, the increase in pS129 asyn was not observed in Ag129-derived neurons (Fig. 7g), implying that the interferon receptor is required for IFN-dependent activation of pS129 asyn. To validate this observation using an interferon stimulus independent of the IFN receptor, we treated Ag129 primary neurons with WNV or Poly I:C. At 4 h post-treatment, we found that virus and poly I:C treatment did not upregulate pS129 asyn in IFN receptor knockout neurons (Fig. 7h). Together, these data suggest that asyn phosphorylation is associated with activation of the T1 interferon receptor and subsequent downstream signaling events in neurons.

Discussion

Asyn is a small, intrinsically disordered protein that is primarily expressed in neurons32. Asyn can be found in various neuronal subcellular compartments, such as the synapses and nuclei, can be secreted from neurons, and can undergo various post-translational modifications, suggesting a potential multifunctional role for asyn within neurons1,33. Prior studies have shown that asyn functions as an important synaptic protein and participates in the regulation of synaptic vesicle function1,34,35. We hypothesize that, like many other neuronal proteins, asyn may play additional roles in the neuron following post-translational modifications that may increase the risk for PD with aging. Our prior data and other studies have shown that asyn functions to support type I interferon responses, and that viral infections can induce expression of pS129 asyn17,19,25,36–38. However, the mechanisms linking viral infection and pS129 asyn expression are not currently known. In our current study, we show that acute, virus-induced antiviral sensing, and type I interferon signaling in neurons result in a transient increase of pS129 asyn expression followed by rapid resolution of expression.

Acute interferon-induced expression of pS129 asyn is not associated with aggregate formation in these acute models of infection and interferon stimulation. Of the post-translational modifications, pS129 asyn is of particular interest since it is a major constituent of Lewy body aggregates, the pathological hallmark of synucleinopathies, like PD5,39–41. We did not find asyn aggregates in our system, potentially due to several factors, including virus-induced cell death42. However, it is also possible that repeated neuronal injuries, which do not result in the death of the host, are required to initiate aggregation or additional loss of function of downstream pS129 asyn degradation events are required to observe increases in asyn aggregation. For example, it is possible that autophagy dysfunction or lysosomal alterations associated with neuronal stress are required along with acute innate immune triggers of pS129 asyn to result in aggregate formation43,44. Several studies have been conducted that link pS129 asyn to mitochondrial stress, autophagy inhibition, and inflammatory stimuli43,45,46. Therefore, it is likely that developing synucleinopathy pathology over time is the result of both stimulation of pS129 asyn and inhibition of the rapid degradation we found in our experiments. Furthermore, our studies in primary neurons do not incorporate the important role of microglia in the contributions to neuroinflammation, and the interactions between interferon-induced PTMs between mature neuron networks and microglia in the brain need further study. Further studies are also needed to fully investigate important potential interactions between innate immune stimulation of pS129 asyn and degradation of post-translational modifications prior to aggregate formation in neurons and microglia.

Recent data suggest that there is a strong association between viral infections and the development of neurodegenerative diseases in humans later in life15,16. Previous studies in a mouse model have shown that infection with the neuroinvasive virus, Western Equine Encephalitic Virus, in olfactory pathways induces asyn aggregation19. Additional studies have shown that inoculation of various strains of influenza or SARS-CoV-2 in mice induces pS129 asyn, but these studies did not delineate whether the virus-induced asyn aggregates colocalized with expression of pS129 asyn36–38,47,48. Induction of pS129 Asyn during H1N1 influenza infection was prevented by oseltamivir (Tamiflu®) antiviral treatment, suggesting that viral load and intensity of the immune response may play a role in risk for pS129 asyn expression in olfactory pathways36. A recent study showed that treating inducible pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived human dopaminergic neurons with pre-formed fibrils and proinflammatory cytokines resulted in asyn aggregates that increased in size over time, indicating that the immune response exacerbates Lewy pathologies49. Others have shown that T1 IFN plays a role in increasing neurological disease severity50,51. However, the complete ablation of neuronal T1 IFN signaling results in increased protein aggregation, potentially due to associated autophagy dysfunction52. It may be that exceeding a certain intensity of innate immune triggers is required for the formation of pS129 asyn, whereas additional alterations in the breakdown of pS129 asyn are needed to develop asyn aggregation following interferon stimulation. Together, these data indicate a role for T1 IFN in the expression of pS129 asyn. However, it is not currently known if pS129 asyn has a homeostatic function or is just a sign of neurodegeneration pathogenesis. Additional studies of the turnover of pS129 asyn following T1 IFN stimulation are required to fully determine the role of phosphorylation and the role of Serine129 in aggregate formation.

This study increases our understanding of how CNS viral infections mechanistically impact synucleinopathy pathogenesis by defining mechanisms by which viral infection of neurons and neuronal antiviral immunity acutely alter asyn post-translational modifications and may contribute to the accumulated risk for PD during aging. Our study elucidates a direct link between neuronal innate antiviral immunity and alpha synuclein phosphorylation by providing a novel perspective on how DNA and RNA viral infections may contribute to the pathogenesis of synucleinopathies. We show that acute infection with neurotropic viruses, such as WNV, HSV-1, and SVV, induces pS129 asyn expression in neurons. Since the increase in pS129 asyn is preserved across species and broad viral families, it indicates that pS129 asyn levels are elevated in response to a conserved host mechanism rather than a virus-specific effect. While our acute infection models did not yield asyn aggregation, we suspect that the rapid increase in pS129 asyn may prime neurons for the development of aggregates, thus raising the potential that repeated, chronic, or severe viral exposures could have long-term implications for synuclein pathogenesis.

These findings also demonstrate the capacity of neurons to produce interferons in response to antiviral immune stimulation. We show that primary neurons can produce IFNa and IFNb (T1 IFN) but not T2 or T3 IFNs. T1 IFN signaling rapidly induces reversible pS129 asyn expression that localizes to the cell body and the nucleus of neurons. This observation builds on our previous observation of T1 IFN-mediated nuclear translocation of total asyn in primary neurons25. The increase in pS129 asyn does not happen in IFN receptor-deficient murine neurons, suggesting that asyn phosphorylation occurs downstream of neuronal IFN receptor signaling. Together, these data provide mechanistic insight into previous studies linking viral infection to asyn pathologies17,19,25,36.

A recent study showed that pS129 asyn expression rapidly increased following picrotoxin-induced neuronal depolarization. Additional data from this study demonstrated that the increase in pS129 asyn was mediated by the calcium-dependent activation of PLK2, suggesting that PLK2 is important for asyn phosphorylation53. Our data show that primary neurons treated with T1 IFN rapidly increase pS129 asyn expression on a timescale similar to what was previously reported. Recent work has shown that T1 IFN signaling induces calcium influx into neurons, an event associated with PLK2 activation and subsequent asyn phosphorylation54–56. However, it is not currently known whether T1 IFN treatment results in PLK2 activation.

This study makes important strides in examining the interactions of innate antiviral immunity and asyn post-translational modification in neurons. pS129 asyn may have an adaptive function in response to cellular stress responses that becomes maladaptive in response to chronic stimulation, such as repeated viral infections or persistent immune response. Additionally, there are other cellular events that likely contribute to the expression of pS129 asyn and aggregates that were beyond the scope of this work. We did not address the interplay between antiviral immunity and cellular stress pathways, like autophagy, lysosome function, or oxidative stress, but growing evidence suggests that cellular stress in conjunction with chronic neuroinflammation may result in the formation of asyn aggregation49. Understanding the conditions that contribute to the pathogenesis of synucleinopathies is vital in determining which therapeutic avenues to pursue. Our study shows that pS129 asyn expression increases in response to acute viral infection as a common response downstream of T1 IFN signaling in neurons and results in the rapid and reversible phosphorylation of asyn. This suggests that asyn phosphorylation may have a physiological role in neuronal antiviral immunity, and that acute interferon responses may provide an important common pathway linking a wide array of environmental factors as triggers for post-translational modifications of asyn. In conclusion, these data contribute to the growing body of evidence that asyn phosphorylation is tied to normal neuronal function53,57.

Methods

Immunohistochemistry of human tissue

Unstained slides prepared from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue were obtained from various brain regions from human patient cases at autopsy. Standard 5 μM sections were provided from midbrain substantia nigra pars compacta, basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, frontal cortex, and superior cerebellum, which were obtained from WNV-positive cases or control cases. Slides were deparaffinized in HIER (Abcam Universal Protocol) and permeabilized with Triton X and Tween20. Sections were labeled with primary antibodies against AF568-conjugated pS129 alpha synuclein (Abcam, EP1536Y), Chicken anti-human MAP2 (Abcam, 4542), and rabbit anti-human STAT2 (Invitrogen, 44-362G) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Tissue was labeled with the appropriate secondary antibodies, FITC anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher) and AF 680 anti-chicken IgG (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at room temperature. All slides were treated with Autoflourescence quencher kit (Vector Labs). Slides from a patient with Parkinson’s disease and increased phospho-S129 asyn (pS129 asyn) expression were used as a positive control. Slides were stained and imaged as previously described25. IHC was imaged using Olympus Spinning Disc confocal with z-stacks, substantia nigra was identified using brightfield and nuclear stain (DAPI), and the mean immunofluorescence intensity was obtained for individual MAP2+ cells masked to exclude microglia. Co-localization in MAP2+ cells was measured between STAT2 and DAPI, STAT2, and pS129 asyn to obtain the Pearson co-localization coefficient using the Olympus analysis platform.

Mice

All mouse experimental protocols were approved by the UT Southwestern Medical Center IACUC. Mice were age-matched, between 8 and 12 weeks old, and were sex matched. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane until unresponsive to toe pinch stimuli. Following the administration of anesthesia, mice were inoculated with 106 PFUs of TX02 West Nile Virus intranasally or intraperitoneally. This dose was chosen from a dose–response curve showing the lowest dose resulting in significant encephalitis symptoms and weight loss in the murine model. Mice were weighed daily during the infection time course. Wild-type C57BL/6 and Ag129 mice (Strain #029098) were purchased from Jackson Labs. Swiss Webster mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories.

Primate studies

Simian varicella virus (SVV) infection of non-human primates and collection of tissue samples were performed in the Tulane National Biomedical Research Center (TNBRC) in accordance with the recommendations of the US Department of Agriculture Animal Welfare Act regulations and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol for SVV infection and tissue analysis was approved ty the IACUC of Tulane University and the TNBRC. The dorsal root ganglionic tissues utilized in these studies were banked tissues from previously published work4. Six male SVV-seronegative Indian Rhesus macaque (RM, Macaca mulatta), 7 years of age, were inoculated intrathecally with 1.5 × 105 plaque-forming units of wild-type SVV-infected rhesus lung fibroblast cells. All six developed acute zoster rash within 7–10 days post-inoculation, and skin punch biopsies and dorsal root neuron samples were obtained from acute and latently infected animals as well as control animals.

Viruses

TX02 West Nile virus infections

Stocks of TX02 West Nile virus were grown in C6/36 Aedes albopictus (ATCC CRL-1660) and titered using veros as previously described17. Briefly, Vero cells were inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions for 1 h. After 1 h, the inoculum was replaced with a 1.5% CMC overlay for 4 days. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA and stained with crystal violet. The visible plaques were counted, and virus concentrations were calculated. For murine infections, mice were inoculated with either 104 or 106 PFU of TX02 West Nile Virus intranasally or intraperitoneally, and weights were monitored daily. The viral dose is indicated in the figures above. Mice losing greater than 20% of their bodyweight prior to the end of the study were euthanized and excluded from the study. WNV genome copies in mouse brain tissue were quantified using RT-qPCR.

HSV-1 mouse infections

For Herpes Simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) inoculations, the corneas of anesthetized mice were scarred using a 31-gauge needle 9 times using a crosshatch pattern. Subsequently, 105 PFU HSV-1 was administered to the eye in 10 μL via pipette. Mice losing more than 15% bodyweight prior to the end of the study were euthanized and excluded from the study. HSV-1 strain F was procured from ATCC (#VR733) and passaged over Cercopithecus aethiops kidney cells (Vero cells, ATCC CCL-81) at 34 °C. Viral titers for all viruses were quantified in Vero cells by standard plaque assay.

Primary neuron generation

All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Primary cortical neurons were prepared from the E18 (embryonic day 18) embryos of wild-type Swiss Webster mice or Ag129 mice. Briefly, mice were euthanized following institutional IACUC protocols. Cortical brain tissue from E18 mice was isolated and placed into Hanks balanced salt solution (HBSS) on ice. The tissues were transferred to a 0.25% trypsin + HBSS solution for 15 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Next, the tissues were dissociated via trituration and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The neurons were pelleted via centrifugation at 1000 × g and resuspended in neuronal plating media (Neurobasal media, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.5 mM glutamine, 0.5% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2% B27 (Gibco)). Neurons were then counted and seeded on poly-d-lysine-coated plates (either 6-, 12-, or 96-well plates). 24 h post-plating, a half media change using neuron “feeding” media (Neurobasal media, 10 mM HEPES, 10 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.5 mM glutamine, and 0.5% penicillin-streptomycin). Media changes were performed every 4 days until the cortical neurons were mature at 12 days post-plating. Quality control using immunofluorescence analysis for NeuN- and GFAP-positive cells shows >95% neurons based on cell counts.

Primary neuron treatments

After neurons matured for 12 days, they were infected with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 or 1, treated with 1 μg/mL of pre-formed fibrils (PFFs) (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-61596, see below for how PFFs were generated), treated with 25 μg/mL of high molecular weight Poly I:C (InvivoGen, tlrl-pic), or treated with 100 ng/mL recombinant murine IFNa2 (R&D Biotechne, 10149-IF) for the desired time courses.

Generation of pre-formed fibrils

Pre-Formed Fibrils (PFFs) were generated as previously described28. Briefly, recombinant mouse alpha synuclein PFFs, Type 1 (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-61596), was reconstituted in sterile 1× PBS and sonicated using a probe tip sonicator (Sonics and Materials Inc, GEX1130) under sterile conditions. PFFs were sonicated for 3 sets of 24 times with 30 s between each set to prevent heating and aerosolization of the PFFs. Each pulse was one second on and one second off with the sonicator power set to 10%. PFFs were sterilely filtered using a 0.22 μM filter. Finally, primary cortical neurons were given 1 μg/mL of PFFs at 5 days post-plating. PFF-treated primary cortical neurons were imaged 7 days post-treatment (day 12 of neuron culturing) with ThioflavinS staining as previously described (see below)28.

RT-qPCR

Following treatment, mouse brain regions of interest were isolated and placed in 0.5 mL of RNAlater (AM7024) and stored at 4 °C until ready for processing. For processing, brain regions of interest were weighed and placed in 1 mL of RNAsolv (Omega Bio-tek, R6830), where they were then dissociated using the stock RNA isolation_01 protocol on the Miltenyi OctoMacs. The E.Z.N.A. total RNA kit II (Omega Bio-tek, R6934) was used to isolate RNA from tissues. Alternatively, the E.Z.N.A. total RNA kit I was used to isolate RNA from primary neurons (Omega Bio-tek, R6834). RNA quality and quantity were determined using a nanodrop. Viral genome copies were quantified using a standard curve derived from an IDT gene fragment that matches the sequence of the TX02 WNV envelope protein. Alternatively, fold change in genes of interest relative to gapdh was determined using the Livak method58. Pre-designed probes from Bio-Rad were used for qPCR of genes of interest.

TX02 WNV Eprotein Fwd Primer: AAATAGTCCGCAGCCGTT

TX02 WNV Eprotein Rev Primer: AATATCAAGTACGAAGTGGCCA

TX02 WNV Eprotein Probe: FAM-GCCTGCTCTACCAAGGCAATAGGAA-.

IFN-alpha, beta, gamma, and lambda ELISAs

Briefly, supernatants were collected from primary neurons after the previously described treatments. Supernatants were then analyzed for IFN-alpha (PBL Bioscience, 42120), IFN-beta (PBL Bioscience, 42400), IFN-gamma (Thermo Fisher, BMS606-2), and IFN-lambda2/3 (R&D Biotechne, DY1789B, DY008B) using commercial kits as per the manufacturers’ instructions. Samples were run in triplicate. Standards and blanks were run in duplicate. Finally, the OD values were measured using the CLARIOstar Plus plate reader in accordance with the manufacturer’s directions. The concentration of IFNs secreted from primary neurons following treatment was calculated using the standard curves.

Biolegend LEGEND MAX alpha synuclein aggregate ELISA (449407)

Brain tissues were isolated and placed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, 89901) with 1× Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher, 78441) on ice. Immediately after collection, protein was isolated from tissue using the Miltenyi OctoMACs Protein_01 protocol. Protein was isolated from primary neurons removed from plates using cell scrapers, RIPA buffer, and 1× Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice. Next, samples were mechanically dissociated using insulin syringes and were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. Protein extracts were frozen down at -80 °C until all samples could be run simultaneously. Protein concentrations for samples were calculated using BCA assays as previously described17. 25 μg of protein from tissues and 10 μg of protein from primary neurons were used in accordance with Biolegend manufacturer protocols. Each sample was run in triplicate, and standards were run in duplicate. Next, the OD values of each well were measured using the CLARIOstar Plus plate reader in accordance with the manufacturer’s directions. The amount of alpha synuclein aggregates present was determined using the standard curve27.

Phospho-serine129 alpha synuclein Cell-based ELISA

Phospho-serine129 Alpha Synuclein cell-based ELISA kit was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Antibodies.com, A102235). The primary neurons were washed four times between each step. Briefly, primary neurons were seeded at 40,000 cells per well in poly-d-lysine-coated 96-well plates. After the neurons matured and were treated, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min at room temperature. Next, fixed cells were incubated for 20 min in quenching buffer to reduce background signal. Cells were permeabilized with the permeabilization buffer provided in the kit for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated in 1× of Gapdh or phospho-serine129 alpha synuclein primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies were applied at room temperature for 1.5 h. Substrate and stop solutions were used, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cells were then stained with crystal violet solution for 30 min at room temperature. Crystal violet solution was removed from the cells and replaced with SDS solution. Absorbance was recorded at 562 nm. The OD at 562 nm was used to normalize the OD 450 nm value based on cell density. OD values were measured using the ClarioSTAR Plus plate reader.

Western Blotting

Western Blotting for pS129 alpha synuclein was performed as previously described59. Briefly, protein was isolated from various brain regions and quantified using BCA assays. 15 μg of protein from the denoted brain regions were loaded into the wells of a 12% Criterion TGX Precast Midi Protein Gel (Bio-Rad, Cat# 5671043) and run for 70 min at 130 V. Next, we used the Bio-Rad Trans-Blot SD Cell system to perform a semi-dry transfer from the gel to a PVDF membrane as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfer was run at 20 V for 60 min. Following the semi-dry transfer, the PVDF membrane was washed three times using MilliQ H2O. Since synuclein proteins have been reported to poorly embed into membranes, the membranes were incubated for 30 min in 4% PFA with gentle agitation using an orbital shaker to enhance protein binding to the membrane. The membrane was washed 8 times with 1× PBS. Membranes were incubated in blocking buffer (2% BSA in 1× TBST) for 1 h. Membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C for pS129 asyn (1:500, Cell Signaling, 23706), alpha synuclein (1:500, Sigma Millipore, mabn1817), and beta actin (1:2000, Cell Signaling, 4970). After 3 washes, the membranes were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488+ goat anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, Cat# A11034) and Alexa Fluor 647+ goat anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen, Cat# A32728) to detect the proteins of interest. Finally, the membrane was imaged for relevant fluorophores using a Bio-Rad Chemidoc MP Imaging System.

Immunocytochemistry

Primary neurons were imaged as previously described25. Briefly, primary neurons were grown on pre-treated chamber slides (Thermo Fisher, 154852) and treated after reaching maturity. After treatment, cells were fixed using 4% PFA and washed with 1× PBS three times. Cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in DPBS and blocked with blocking buffer (5% goat serum DPBS solution) for 1 h. Primary neurons were stained with primary antibodies against pS129 alpha synuclein (Cell Signaling, 23706) and West Nile Virus Envelope protein (Thermo Fisher, MA5-35917) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Cells were stained with the appropriate secondary antibodies, goat anti-mouse Alexa 568 (Thermo Fisher, A-11004), goat anti-rabbit Alexa 647 (Thermo Fisher, A-21244), and goat anti-rabbit Alexa 568 (Thermo Fisher, A-11036), for 1 h at room temperature. After antibody staining, cells were stained with ThioflavinS (SelleckChem, S6825) as previously described60. Primary neurons incubated with pre-formed fibrils were used as a positive control for ThioflavinS staining as previously described60. Cells were then stained with 50 ng/mL of DAPI (D1306) for 10 min at room temperature. Slides were sealed with coverslips using Prolong Gold antifade reagent and stored at 4 °C in a light-protected area until imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 with Airyscan. Images were analyzed using Fiji for increases in mean fluorescent intensity and cellular localization of proteins of interest.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each experiment was repeated a minimum of three times or had a minimum of three biological replicates. Graphing and statistical analysis were conducted using GraphPad Prism v.10.5.0 software (GraphPad Prism, San Diego, USA). All data are reported with the mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). T-tests and One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons testwere used for statistical analysis on parametric data. Normality was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-parametric data was analyzed using either the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. Asterisks indicate the degree of significant differences compared with the controls are listed in each figure legend to specifically indicate p-values from each individual statistical test. Significance analysis and additional pertinent information for comparison of data are specified in the source data and figure legends.
