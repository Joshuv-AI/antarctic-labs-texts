Effects and mechanisms of probiotics supplement on hypertension
Authors: Zhe Jiang, Changlin Zhai, Chaojie He, Liang Shen, Guanmin Tang, Lijia Xu, Haojie Yang, Huilin Hu, Jungang Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global population of individuals suffering from hypertension is estimated to surpass 1.28 billion. Uncontrolled hypertension makes contributions to the development of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Emerging evidence indicates a significant correlation between hypertension and gut microbiota. As an intestinal regulator, which could confer health benefits to the host in adequate amounts, probiotics may become a novel approach to regulating blood pressure without side effects. Therefore, we overview the antihypertensive effects and the potential mechanisms of probiotics supplement on hypertension.

INTRODUCTION

As a major global public health issue, hypertension imposes significant economic burdens on individuals and society, with over 1.28 billion people suffering from it [1]. Hypertension makes a significant contribution to the development of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, such as myocardial infarction, heart failure, cerebral vascular accident, terminal renal disease and premature death [2]. It is well established that genetics, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, sympathetic overactivity, endothelial dysfunction and environmental influences contribute to this ever-rising prevalence of hypertension [3,4]. Despite the availability of numerous antihypertensive drugs, a plenty of patients still fail to achieve recommended blood pressure (BP) targets. Therefore, it is necessary to find novel methods to control hypertension. Emerging evidence indicates a significant correlation between hypertension and gut microbiota. Hypertension can cause gut microbiota dysbiosis characterized by the increase of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio and the decrease of microbial richness while contributing to gut barrier dysfunction characterized by the increase of intestinal permeability and the decrease of intestinal tight junction proteins [5,6]. In turn, researches involving fecal microbiota transplantation from hypertensive patients to germ-free mice have demonstrated that gut microbiota modulates BP [7–9]. Thus, preserving and restoring gut microbiota homeostasis could be a potential therapeutic strategy for hypertension. Probiotics, described as ‘live nonpathogenic microorganisms that when administered in adequate amounts confer health benefits to the host’, may become a novel approach to regulating BP without side effects [10]. As a meta-analysis reported, daily consumption of probiotic products exceeding 109 colony-forming units (CFU) significantly lowers BP [11]. Therefore, we overview the antihypertensive effects and possible mechanisms of probiotic supplementation on hypertension.

ANTI-HYPERTENSIVE EFFECTS

Preclinical studies

Evidences showed that supplementing different strains of probiotics exerted antihypertensive effect in preclinical studies (Table 1). Most Lactobacillus strains reduce SBP by 9–37 mmHg in various hypertensive models. Takano et al. and Miguel et al. reported that one-time administrating fermented milk containing Lactobacillus helveticus strains CPN4, CP190, and CP191 reduced BP by 6.3–41.2 mmHg [12,13]. Sipola et al. [14] found that long-term consumption of sour milk fermented by L. helveticus (LBK-16H strain) attenuated hypertension progression in spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs). Chen et al. demonstrated that feeding L. helveticus H9-fermented milk for once or multiple times over 7 weeks significantly reduced BP in SHRs, but not in normotensive rats [15]. What is more, long-term administration of Lactobacillus fermentum CECT5716 (LC40) at a dose of 3.3 × 1010 CFU/day in drinking water progressively reduced SBP at the end of the 5 weeks without significantly influencing heart rates [16]. Supplement of LC40 can also exert antihypertensive effects in tacrolimus-induced hypertensive rats at a dose of 5 × 108 CFU/day for 2 weeks [17] and in SHRs at a dose of 109 CFU/day for 13 weeks [18]. However, chronic LC40 treatment at 109 CFU/day did not prevent the increase in SBP in deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)-salt rats [19] or the rise in both SBP and DBP in NOS inhibitor NG-nitro-l-arginine methyl ester (l-NAME)-induced hypertensive rats [20]. A 4-week supplementation of Lactobacillus plantarum did not reduce BP in l-NAME hypertensive rats as well [21]. However, studies have shown that fermented milk from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum SR37-3 and SR61-2 exhibits antihypertensive activity in l-NAME-induced hypertensive rats [22]. Different strains exert different antihypertensive effects in the same type of model, showing that various strains may produce different BP-lowering effects. Furthermore, it was reported that long-term oral administration of milk fermented by L. plantarum Y44, Lb. bulgaricus Lb100 or Lc. lactis AM1 reduced the BP significantly in SHRs. Both Lb.Lb100 and L. plantarum Y44 demonstrated notable antihyperlipidemic effects on SHRs [23,24]. L. casei C1, isolated from Malaysian probiotic drinks, demonstrated antihypertensive potential when administered to SHRs rats for 8 weeks [25]. Hsu et al. demonstrated that probiotic treatment with L. casei at 2 × 108 CFU/day prevented high-fructose diet-induced hypertension and protected against hypertension in male adult offspring [26]. In high-fat-diet (HFD)-fed rats, chronic supplement of Lactobacillus rhamnosus M9 or Lactobacillus fermentum 296 also displayed antihypertensive effect [27,28]. These results suggest that various Lactobacillus strains may have varying antihypertensive effects on different types of hypertensive animal models. It is established that SHR model is the‘gold standard’model in the research of essential hypertension [29]. HFD-fed rat simulates a diet-induced hypertension, which is a typical representative of metabolic syndrome [30]. The DOCA-salt rat is a model of salt-dependent hypertension, which is commonly used to simulate primary aldosteronism [31]. Lactobacillus could exert an antihypertensive effect in SHRs and HFD-fed rats rather than in DOCA-salt rats and normotensive rats, indicating that Lactobacillus may exhibit an antihypertensive effect in patients with essential hypertension and diet-induced hypertension without influencing normotensive individuals. What is more, compared with SHR and HFD-fed rats, DOCA-salt rats always have a higher BP [32]. Therefore, it seems that supplementing with Lactobacillus is more suitable for hypertensive patients with slightly elevated BP. Apart from Lactobacillus, supplement of Bifidobacterium or Clostridium butyricum can also exert hypotensive effect. Zhang et al. found that after 16 weeks of administering Bifidobacterium lactis M8 at a dose of 4 × 109 CFU/day to high-fructose-fed rats, the median SBP and DBP decreased by 16.92 and 18.56%, respectively, compared to the control group [27]. Luo et al. [33] demonstrated that long-term consumption of C. butyricum significantly reduced the SBP at the end of 6 weeks in SHRs. Several studies reported that administration of dairy productions containing different probiotics also displayed hypotensive effect. An early study demonstrated that consuming dairy products with Enterococcus faecium and two Streptococcus thermophiles strains for 8 weeks reduced low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level and SBP [34]. Kong et al. [35] showed that supplementation of probiotic yogurt fermented by S. thermophiles (6.1 × 108 CFU/ml) and Lactobacillus bulgaricus (4.8×108 CFU/ml) significantly reduced BP, heart rate, and cardiac function in SHRs at a dose of 2 ml/100 g body weight over 7 weeks. It was reported that administering a formulation containing L. fermentum strains 139, 263, and 296 at a dose of 109 CFU/ml over 4 weeks significantly reduced the SBP in HFD-fed rats [36]. A study demonstrated that an 8-week oral administration of a fermented soy milk probiotic cocktail comprising L. casei, L. acidophilus, L. bulgaricus, S. thermophilus, and B. longum significantly reduced SBP in rats [37]. Jiang et al. found that long-term gavage of E. faecium CP640 and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus CP658 fermented milk significantly reduced BP in SHRs without causing adverse effects. Synergistic effect between various probiotics may contribute to their antihypertensive effects.

Clinical studies

Meta-analyses have reported that Lactobacillus exerts an antihypertensive effect, with a maximum reduction in SBP of 3.05 mmHg and DBP of 1.51 mmHg [38,39]. A study reported that sour milk fermented with L. helveticus and Saccharomyces cerevisiae significantly reduced SBP by 14.1 ± 3.1 mmHg and DBP by 6.9 ± 2.2 mmHg in older hypertensive volunteers [40]. Similarly, a mean reduction of 5.2 (±8.1) mmHg in SBP and 1.7 mmHg in DBP has been observed in borderline hypertensive men [41]. Consumption of powdered fermented milk with L. helveticus CM4 can exert antihypertensive effects on subjects with hypertension or mild hypertension without undesirable side effects [42]. What is more, supplement of L. plantarum also displayed hypotensive effects in clinical studies. In a population of smokers, a 6-week dietary supplementation with L. plantarum led to reduced SBP and improved metabolic alterations [43]. Sharafedtinov et al. [44] observed that administering cheese containing the probiotic L. plantarum to obese hypertensive patients significantly reduced BMI and BP compared to the control group. A randomized double-blind clinical trial showed that administrating soy milk with L. plantarum significantly reduced SBP/DBP in type II diabetes mellitus patients [45]. However, a study found that milk fermented with L. plantarum did not significantly reduce BP in postmenopausal women with metabolic syndrome, indicating probiotics may exert different effects on different populations [46]. Combination therapy of probiotic strains seems exert few hypotensive effects in clinical trials. A 6-week randomized, double-blind, controlled, parallel, factorial study demonstrated that consuming capsules with the probiotic strains L. acidophilus La5 and B. animalis subsp. lactis Bb12 did not influence BP [47]. In addition, another clinical trial showed that an 8-week supplement with a probiotic cocktail containing L. paracasei, L. rhamnosus, L. acidophilus, and B. lactis (each at a dosage of 109 CFU) in 19 hypertensive women reduced SBP by 5 mmHg and DBP by 2 mmHg without statistically significant [48]. Recently, Hannah et al. observed that coadministration of Limosilactobacillus reuteri NCIMB 30242, L. plantarum UALp-05 and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis B420 did not result in significant changes in SBP, DBP, fasting glucose levels, triglycerides, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [49]. Compared to preclinical studies, clinical data show significantly smaller and inconsistent antihypertensive effects. The following are the possible reasons for this phenomenon. Firstly, different from specific mechanism-induced hypertensive models, there is a mixture of mechanisms contributing to elevated BP in hypertensive patients, such as inflammation and disorder of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS). Different hypertensive mechanisms may influence the antihypertensive effect of probiotics. Secondly, the dosage, duration and strain type of probiotic administration are different in preclinical and clinical studies. However, these factors may make significant contributions to the antihypertensive effect. What is more, there are a myriad of confounding factors in clinical studies, such as age, gender, regional, and ethnic differences. Although there is no clear direct evidence of regional or ethnic differences in the BP-lowering effects of probiotics, individuals from different regions or races often have different compositions of gut microbiota [50,51]. And individual differences in microbiota structure, such as diversity and abundance of microbiota, may be influenced by genetic, dietary, and environmental factors, indirectly leading to the heterogeneity of probiotic intervention effects. To determine whether age, gender, or regional and ethnic differences can influence the effectiveness of probiotics, large randomized, long-term, and multicenter clinical studies are needed to confirm this in the future.

ANTI-HYPERTENSIVE MECHANISMS

According to the above studies, Lactobacillus strains seem to have a superior antihypertensive effect compared to other strains. Studies have shown that Lactobacillus strains have more relevant antihypertensive mechanisms compared to other strains, primarily by producing angiotensin I-converting enzyme-inhibitory peptides, ameliorating endothelial dysfunction, improving oxidative stress, modulating inflammation, preventing gut dysbiosis and gut barrier dysfunction and influencing short-chain fatty acids (Fig. 1).

The mechanisms of probiotics supplement on hypertension. During proteolysis, probiotic supplements could produce angiotensin I-converting enzyme-inhibitory peptides that may exert antihypertensive effects. Probiotics could also ameliorate endothelial dysfunction by promoting NO production and enhancing NO bioactivity to lower BP. Oxidative stress and inflammation can be modulated by probiotics through the decrease of •O2−, GSH, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α production as well as the increase of IL-10 production. What is more, probiotics could prevent gut dysbiosis and gut barrier dysfunction. In addition, probiotics could influence short-chain fatty acids which exhibit an important role in modulating BP by upregulating GPR41 and GPR43 expression while downregulating Olfr78 expression in the blood vessel.

Produce angiotensin I-converting enzyme-inhibitory peptides

The RAS is recognized as an important hormonal system in the development and progression of hypertension [52–54]. In the RAS, angiotensin I-converting enzyme (ACE) is responsible for converting angiotensin (Ang) I into angiotensin II, which progresses hypertension through both increasing the release of aldosterone and arginine-vasopressin and decreasing the production of nitric oxide (NO) [55]. ACE can also hydrolyze and inactivate the vasoactive bradykinin, causing vasoconstriction [56]. Thus, inhibiting ACE is a key therapeutic target for hypertension treatment. Some probiotics, particularly Lactobacillus strains, are able to generate some bioactive peptides with ACE-inhibitory character during proteolysis [57,58]. Takano et al. first identified the ACE-inhibitory peptides Val-Pro-Pro (VPP) and Ile-Pro-Pro (IPP) from milks fermented by various L. helveticus strains [12]. A double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial demonstrated that 4 weeks of consumption of casein hydrolysate tablets containing VPP and IPP significantly lowered blood pressure without clinically adverse events [59]. Hayes et al. demonstrated that the strain L. animalis DPC6134 can degrade casein into other peptides with ACE-inhibitory activities, including NIPPLTQTPVVVPPFIQ, IGSENSEKTTMP, SQSKVLPVPQ, MPFPKYPVEP, and EPVLGPVRGPFP. Although less efficient than captopril, these peptides compare favorably with other ACE-inhibitory peptides derived from foods [60]. Additionally, Quirós et al. identified two other bioactive peptides (Leu-His-Leu-Pro-Leu-Pro and Leu-Val-Tyr-Pro-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro-Ile-Pro-Asn-Ser-Leu-Pro-Gln-Asn-Ile-Pro-Pro) in Enterococcus faecalis-fermented milk, showing antihypertensive effects following both acute and long-term oral administration to SHRs [61]. Despite uncertainties regarding specific peptides, milks fermented with L. helveticus strains CPN4, R211, R289, CHCC637, CHCC641, and LP01; E. faecalis strains CECT 5827, 5727, and 5728; L. plantarum strains NTU 102 (102FM) and QS670; and Lactococcus lactis subsp. cremoris LP25 have been demonstrated to contain ACE-inhibitory peptides and exhibit antihypertensive activity in vivo[62–66]. These findings further support the ACE-inhibitory peptides generated from probiotics play a vital role in the antihypertensive effect.

Ameliorate endothelial dysfunction

It is widely acknowledged that endothelial dysfunction contributes to the development of chronic NO synthesis blockade-induced hypertension [67]. Probiotic strains such as L. rhamnosus GG, L. helveticus, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus reuteri, and Bifidobacterium have been reported to induce NO production in macrophages when present in sufficient quantities [68,69]. Yang et al. [70] demonstrated that recombinant L. plantarum effectively reduced blood pressure and elevated plasma NO levels in SHRs, suggesting L. plantarum could stimulate NO production. Soy milk fermented with L. plantarum or Streptococcus thermophilus was shown to stimulate endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) activity, thereby promoting NO production [71]. Another study conducted by Gómez-Guzmán showed that long-term administration of L. coryniformis CECT5711 (K8) and L. gasseri CECT5714 (LC9) (1 : 1) increased eNOS phosphorylation levels in SHRs without altering eNOS gene or protein expression [16]. It is established that enhanced NO bioavailability can prevent aortic tissue remodeling, thereby attenuating hypertension [72]. Toral et al. found that chronic probiotic treatment with Lactobacillus coryniformis reversed endothelial dysfunction in obese mice by increasing NO bioavailability [73]. What is more, chronic LC40 administration has been reported to protect agonist-induced NO bioactivity and enhance endothelium-dependent relaxation in the aortic and mesenteric arteries of tacrolimus-treated mice [17].

Improve oxidative stress

Apart from stimulating eNOS activity, NO production and NO bioactivity, supplement of probiotics can ameliorate vascular oxidative stress to ameliorate the endothelial dysfunction as well. Intensified stress exerted by hypertension promotes to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) like •O2− [74]. In turn, excessive •O2− production progresses the development of hypertension through aggravating endothelial dysfunction. It is well established that •O2− is primarily derived from nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase, which scavenges NO to form peroxynitrite, thereby reducing NO bioavailability in the vascular endothelium [75,76]. It was observed that certain strains of Lactobacillus exhibited antioxidant property, such as L. fermentum 296, L. paracasei subsp. paracasei NTU 101 (101FM), L. plantarum NTU 102 (102FM), L. plantarum MNZR, and L. plantarum TWK10 [65,72,77–79]. Toral et al. [16] and Gómez-Guzmán et al. [73] found that chronic intake of L. coryniformis, LC40 or K8 plus LC9 prevented the increased expression of the catalytic NADPH oxidase subunit NOX-1 in the aortas from HFD-fed mice and SHRs. It is reported that LC40 administration reduced NADPH oxidase activity in tacrolimus-treated mice, primarily through NOX2 down-regulation [17]. These findings indicate that some probiotics may attenuate hypertension by lowering •O2− levels in the vascular wall through inhibiting NADPH oxidase. Moreover, long-term consumption of L. coryniformis elevated the mRNA expression of antioxidant enzymes that were down-regulated in HFD-fed mice [73]. A study showed that 8 weeks of Lb. casei strain C1 supplementation in SHR led to a significant high glutathione (GSH) level, indicating that the probiotics treatment helped restore GSH levels and subsequently reduced hypertension-induced oxidative stress [25].

Modulate inflammation

Host inflammation is correlation to the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory actions, which is primary modulated by helper T cells (Th) and regulatory T cells (Treg). Compared to the control group, SHRs exhibited an increased number of Th17 cells in aortas and a decreased amount of Tregs [80]. It is established that Th17 cells could produce pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-17. The abnormal levels of IL-6 can damage vascular endothelium and elevate blood pressure in hypertensive patients. IL-17 is able to progress hypertension by inducing endothelial dysfunction via Rho-kinase signal [81]. As the main cytokine secreted by Tregs, IL-10 can attenuate the endothelial dysfunction of hypertension through reducing NADPH oxidase activity, subsequently improving hypertension [82]. Furthermore, it has been documented that macrophages are capable of producing pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), which plays a role in the pathogenesis of hypertension [83]. Probiotic consumption is thought to inhibit proinflammatory cytokine production [84]. For example, Tenorio-Jimenez et al. [85] observed that a 12-week daily administration of L. reuteri V3401 capsules at dose of 5 × 109/day was linked to reduced inflammation biomarkers in obese adults aged 18–65 with metabolic syndrome, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8. Toral et al. [17] found that consumption of LC40 increased IL-10 levels and decreased IL-17 levels in the plasma in a dose dependent manner in tacrolimus-induced hypertensive mice. Luo et al. demonstrated that supplementing C. butyricum at a dose of 108 CFU/ml reduced the levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-17, while increasing the level of IL-10 in SHRs circulation [33]. Furthermore, administration of probiotics, especially LC40, was reported to influence the activation of T cells within the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN) and the aortic ring. It was also reported that LC40 treatment inhibited Th17/Treg polarization caused by tacrolimus in MLN [17]. Robles-Vera et al. found that administration of LC40 inhibited the T cells-mediated upregulation of α4β7 (an integrin) and CCR9 (a chemokine receptor) in MLN. What is more, LC40 supplement could prevent antigen-presenting cells from activating and migrating to the MLN, suppressing the production of IL-6 and IL-17 from Th17 and promoting the production of IL-10 from Treg. Aortic RORγ (a marker of Th17 infiltration), TNFα, IL-6 and IL-17 levels were significantly elevated by tacrolimus or l-NAME treatment and decreased by LC40 administration [20]. In addition to LC40, supplementation with C. butyricum could enhance anti-inflammatory responses by decreasing levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-17 and increasing level of IL-10 while ameliorating the increase of Th17 and the decrease of Treg in the spleen and aorta from SHRs [33]. What is more, it was reported that chronic administration of Bifidobacterium breve CECT7263 (BFM) could decrease Th17 and increase Treg in MLNs to normalize the balance of Th17/Treg from DOCA-salt rats [19].

Prevent gut dysbiosis and gut barrier dysfunction

Imbalanced gut microbiota is usually characterized by increased F/B ratio and reduced acetate-producing and butyrate-producing bacteria. It results in the aberrant expression of tight junction proteins within the intestinal mucosa, specifically zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, thus increasing the permeability of intestinal barrier, allowing microbial products like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to penetrate mesenteric fat, trigger immune responses, and stimulate inflammatory cytokine production, ultimately contributing to hypertension development [8,86,87]. Chronic treatment with the probiotic LC40 was shown to reduce F/B ratio and increase butyrate-producing bacteria with significantly lowing blood pressure in both SHRs and tacrolimus-induced hypertensive rats [88,89]. Although LC40 supplementation did not significantly affect blood pressure, it normalized the F/B ratio in l-NAME-induced hypertensive rats [20]. Apart from LC40, it was reported that chronic administration of BFM, L. plantarum Y44, L. plantarum Y16, and C. butyricum could also reduce the F/B ratio in SHRs [18,24,33]. And L. coryniformis administration could enhance gut barrier function by altering the comprise of gut microbiota, contributing to reduced plasma LPS levels and improvement of vascular oxidative stress [90]. In addition to restoring the microbial balance, administration of probiotics can directly improve gut barrier dysfunction. Chronic LC40 consumption resulted in an upregulation of occludin mRNA expression in the colonic tissue of tacrolimus-treated mice and it led to an increase in both ZO-1 and occludin expression in the colonic tissue of l-NAME-treated mice [17,20], indicating the potential of LC40 to enhance intestinal integrity. Long-term BFM treatment was found to preserve barrier integrity by increasing occludin and ZO-1 mRNA levels in the colons of DOCA-salt rats and SHRs, significantly preventing endotoxemia absorption into the systemic circulation [18,19]. These results indicates that supplement of probiotics may attenuate hypertension through preventing gut dysbiosis and gut barrier dysfunction.

Influence short-chain fatty acids

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), as circulating metabolites produced by gut microbiota, are critically involved in the pathogenesis of hypertension. G-protein-coupled receptor (GPR) 41, GPR43, GPR109a, and olfactory receptor 78 (Olfr 78) are the primary receptors of SCFAs [88,89]. It was reported that SCFAs exerted antihypertensive effect via stimulating GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109a [91]. And decreased blood pressure could be observed in Olfr78 knockout mice, suggesting stimulating Olfr 78 could exert hypertensive effect [87]. Probiotic therapy with Lactobacillus casei was shown to lower the plasma acetate levels, reduce the expression of Olfr78 in the kidney and increase the expression of GPR43, potentially exerting protective effect on maternal high-fructose-induced hypertensive rats [26]. It was observed that chronic administration of C. butyricum ameliorated the reduction in GPR41 and GPR43 mRNA expression in the aorta of SHRs [33]. These findings indicate that probiotics may positively influence hypertension by upregulating GPR41 and GPR43 expression while downregulating Olfr78 expression.

DISCUSSION

With the in-depth study of gut microbiome and the mechanism of hypertension, more and more evidence has been observed that gut microbiome was associated with hypertension. With safe and nonpharmacological characters, probiotic therapy has become a novel and potential approach to prevent and treat hypertension. This review indicates that probiotic supplementation can lower blood pressure by generating ACE-inhibitory peptides, enhancing vascular oxidative stress and endothelial function, reducing inflammation, and restoring microbial and gut barrier balance. Up to date, administration of the Lactobacillus products such as LC40 has been exclusively studied and displays significantly hypotensive effect. However, the hypotensive effects of other types of probiotics like Bifidobacterium and Clostridium need more studies to be proven. What is more, although a meta-analysis showed that intake of probiotic products exceeding 109 CFU/day could induce significant antihypertensive effect [11], there is no literature indicating the optimal dosage and duration of probiotic supplementation required to exert an optimal antihypertensive effect. In addition, at present, there is a lack of research to verify specific strain mechanisms in humans and whether the antihypertensive effect of administration probiotics varies across different age, gender, regional, or ethnic populations. Therefore, more studies need to be conducted in the future to address the gaps mentioned above, and we believe that probiotics will someday serve as a novel antihypertensive treatment.
