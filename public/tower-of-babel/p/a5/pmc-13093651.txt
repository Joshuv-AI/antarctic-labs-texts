Trust Emerges From Shared Attention: Behavioural and Neural Evidence From Dual EEG Hyperscanning
Authors: Yuzhan Hang, Wei Wu, Runjin Li, Liangjiecheng Huang, Satoshi Shioiri, Xiaosong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrust is central to human cooperation, yet the cognitive and neural mechanisms through which it emerges remain poorly understood. Here, we tested whether shared attention fosters trust behaviour and its neural underpinnings. Pairs of participants engaged in a joint flanker task to manipulate attentional alignment, followed by a multi‐round trust game, whereas neural activity was recorded using dual EEG hyperscanning. Behaviourally, participants in the shared attention condition invested more and responded faster than those in the separated condition, with effects evident from the first round and persisting across repetitions. Neurally, shared attention was associated with increased prefrontal oscillatory power in the theta, alpha, and beta bands, stronger beta‐band functional connectivity between prefrontal and posterior regions, and enhanced inter‐brain theta synchronisation in right frontal areas. Together, these findings demonstrate that shared attention promotes trust through a multi‐level mechanism spanning local oscillatory activity, intra‐brain connectivity, and cross‐brain coupling, establishing shared attention as a minimal yet robust pathway for trust formation beyond deliberative or experience‐based accounts.Keywords: dual EEG hyperscanning, functional connectivity, inter‐brain synchronisation, shared attention, trust

Introduction

Trust is fundamental to human interaction. It enables cooperation, supports social cohesion, and underpins a wide range of interpersonal and institutional relationships. Defined as the willingness to accept vulnerability while maintaining positive expectations of others' actions (Rousseau et al. 1998), trust involves both risk‐taking and positive social expectations. To investigate this construct, researchers have employed behavioural paradigms such as the trust game, investment game, prisoner's dilemma, and public goods game (Berg et al. 1995; Van Lange and Visser 1999). Among these, the trust game has become a widely used tool: one player (the trustor) decides how much money to send to another (the trustee), and the trustee decides how much to return. Despite the uncertainty, trustors often send more than half of their endowment (Berg et al. 1995), suggesting a general human propensity to trust.

Classical theoretical frameworks have sought to explain how trust emerges. Social exchange theory suggests that trust develops through repeated interactions in which individuals learn to expect mutually beneficial outcomes (Axelrod and Hamilton 1981). Social identity theory argues that shared group membership fosters trust by reducing psychological distance and activating in‐group biases (Tajfel and Turner 2004). From an evolutionary perspective, reciprocal altruism posits that trust is favored when cooperation enhances long‐term fitness (Trivers 1971). Although distinct, these accounts share a common assumption that trust is shaped by accumulated experience—through interaction history, social categories, or structural incentives. For example, reciprocity has been identified as a behavioural marker of experience‐based trust formation (King‐Casas et al. 2005).

Beyond experience‐based accounts, trust can also emerge rapidly from heuristic processes. Heuristic‐based models suggest that individuals often rely on minimal cues to make trust decisions efficiently, without engaging in deliberative reasoning (Epstein 1991; Fazio 1986; Chaiken 1989; Petty and Cacioppo 1986; Lewicki and Brinsfield 2011). For example, people are more likely to trust messages endorsed by high‐status sources even when the content is identical (Petty et al. 1983), and can form trustworthiness judgements based on facial appearance within 100 milliseconds (Willis and Todorov 2006; Todorov et al. 2009). Other cues, such as affiliative primes (Bargh 2006; Watanabe and Laurent 2020), gaze direction, or vocal tone (Backer and Alain 2014; Schneider 2006), can similarly trigger trust‐related responses. Together, these findings demonstrate that trust can arise rapidly and under minimal information, highlighting the importance of identifying the perceptual and cognitive mechanisms that support such rapid trust formation.

Building on evidence that trust can emerge rapidly under minimal information, recent work in social and cognitive neuroscience suggests that early trust responses reflect intuitive mechanisms that support cooperative social decision‐making. Rand et al. (2014) demonstrated that cooperative choices are often driven by intuitive processes, with behavioural and neural signatures consistent with rapid value integration rather than deliberative reasoning. Complementing this, Yamagishi et al. (2017) showed that trust decisions rely on generalised expectations of others' trustworthiness, indicating that intuitive tendencies and internalised social expectations jointly shape initial trust formation.

In parallel, advances in two‐person neuroscience have introduced hyperscanning methods that record neural activity from interacting individuals simultaneously, enabling researchers to quantify inter‐brain synchrony (IBS) as a neural marker of real‐time interpersonal alignment (Hasson et al. 2012; Redcay and Schilbach 2019). IBS has been documented across diverse social contexts—including cooperation, communication, teaching–learning interactions, and parent–child engagement—indicating that neural coupling reflects a general mechanism through which individuals coordinate cognitive states during social interaction (Leong et al. 2017; Reindl et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2022; Alonso et al. 2024). Extending beyond individual‐level behavioural models, hyperscanning research highlights that trust and other cooperative decisions recruit interpersonal neural processes that unfold across interacting partners. Enhanced inter‐brain connectivity is consistently observed in frontal regions during cooperative and trust‐related tasks, reflecting shared evaluative processes and reciprocal prediction of a partner's actions (Liu et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2018). Contemporary second‐person neuroscience frameworks similarly emphasise that social decision‐making relies on dynamic coupling between brains, enabling individuals to form shared representations and coordinate expectations in real time (Redcay and Schilbach 2019; Nam et al. 2020; Czeszumski et al. 2020). These perspectives together position trust as a decision process emerging from the interaction between intuitive evaluation and dyad‐level neural attunement.

One promising mechanism is attention. Many of the cues that influence trust, such as facial appearance, gaze, and sound, are governed by attentional processes (Backer and Alain 2014; Carrasco et al. 2004; Kuhn et al. 2008; Schneider 2006). Attention determines which stimuli are perceived, prioritised, and encoded into social evaluations. Subtle forms of attentional alignment between individuals, such as jointly tracking an object or sharing a visual focus, may therefore influence interpersonal trust (Barbato et al. 2020). In social contexts, attention can be broadly categorised into shared and separated states. Shared attention occurs when two individuals focus on the same stimulus while being mutually aware of each other's engagement (Carpenter and Tomasello 1995; Mundy and Newell 2007). This state does not require verbal communication or explicit coordination; rather, it arises from the recognition that another person is attending to the same event. In contrast, separated attention refers to situations where individuals attend to different stimuli without such awareness. Most prior studies have focused on individual attentional cues or responses to automated systems (Bayliss and Tipper 2006; Manssuer et al. 2015; Karpinsky et al. 2018), leaving open whether shared attentional states shape trust.

The joint flanker task provides a well‐established paradigm for examining the cognitive effects of shared attention. In this task, two participants perform a flanker paradigm simultaneously, each responding to specific target letters while ignoring distractors. When participants are aware of their partner's task, they exhibit slower reaction times to distractors that correspond to the partner's targets, which is known as the joint flanker effect (Atmaca et al. 2011). This effect is interpreted as evidence of automatic processing of a partner's task‐relevant information, indicating that shared attention engages social‐cognitive processes such as goal attribution and perspective‐taking. Although these findings demonstrate that attentional alignment can influence cognitive processing, it remains unclear whether such alignment extends to downstream social decisions like trust.

Shared attention and trust converge in the prefrontal cortex, a region that supports both attentional control and social evaluation (Klein et al. 2009; Diamond 2011). By flexibly allocating attention while assessing others' intentions, the prefrontal cortex enables individuals to align their focus with social partners and evaluate trustworthiness (van den Bos and Güroğlu 2009). Neural oscillations in this region provide a temporal basis for linking attention with social decision‐making. Alpha rhythms enhance selective attention by filtering irrelevant information and prioritising socially meaningful cues (Helfrich and Knight 2016), while theta rhythms support conflict monitoring, learning, and adaptive adjustments during social interactions (Cavanagh and Frank 2014; Soltani Zangbar et al. 2020). These rhythmic patterns not only reflect local prefrontal computations but also promote functional connectivity (FC) with other regions involved in social cognition (Friedman and Robbins 2022; Jobson et al. 2021), facilitating the integration of perceptual and evaluative information across interacting partners. These intra‐brain mechanisms provide the foundation upon which interpersonal alignment can emerge.

EEG hyperscanning work indicates that distinct oscillatory frequencies support different components of interpersonal alignment. Theta‐band synchrony has been linked to shared cognitive control, prediction of others' actions, and the coordination of joint behaviour (Leong et al. 2017; Szymanski et al. 2017). Alpha‐band synchrony is commonly associated with joint attentional engagement and the alignment of perceptual processing between partners (Balconi and Vanutelli 2018), whereas beta‐band synchrony has been implicated in higher‐order social coordination, including the maintenance of shared goals and the integration of socio‐contextual information (Novembre et al. 2017; Balconi and Angioletti 2023). These frequency‐specific mechanisms provide a functional basis for examining whether shared attention modulates intra‐ and inter‐brain coupling in ways that shape subsequent trust decisions. Beyond intra‐brain processes, hyperscanning studies have shown that inter‐brain synchronisation in frontal regions increases during joint attention and cooperative decisions, and predicts trust behaviour (Sun et al. 2019). Together, these frequency‐specific findings converge with hyperscanning evidence showing that frontal inter‐brain synchronisation reliably increases during joint attention and cooperative decision‐making, and that stronger frontal coupling predicts subsequent trust behaviour (Sun et al. 2019).

Research on joint attention shows that neural signatures such as alpha‐band suppression and gaze‐related coupling emerge when two individuals attend to the same stimulus, reflecting enhanced processing of others' attentional states (Liu et al. 2018). Behavioural evidence likewise demonstrates that synchronised engagement increases perceptual sensitivity to a partner's actions and improves joint performance, indicating that shared engagement sharpens the perceptual systems required for coordinated interaction (Valdesolo et al. 2010). These attentional and perceptual adjustments enable individuals to monitor and interpret a partner's behaviour more efficiently, establishing a shared reference frame that supports rapid social evaluation. Consequently, joint attention may function as a perceptual mechanism through which individuals can form trust‐relevant impressions quickly and with minimal information. Together, this evidence suggests that shared attention may foster trust through a multi‐layered neural system spanning local oscillations, intra‐brain connectivity, and cross‐brain coupling.

The present study tested whether shared attention provides a core mechanism for the emergence of trust. Moving beyond cue‐based accounts, we combined behavioural and hyperscanning EEG measures to examine how attentional alignment shapes both trust behaviour and its neural substrates. We hypothesised that participants in the shared attention condition would invest more than those in the separated condition, and that these behavioural effects would be accompanied by enhanced prefrontal oscillatory activity. Given the complexity of brain dynamics, we further explored whether intra‐brain functional connectivity and inter‐brain synchronisation contributed to attentional alignment effects on trust.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Science and Technology of China (approval No. 2023‐ky087). A total of 118 participants (53 females; mean age = 20.01 years, range = 14–28 years) were recruited. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation; those under 18 years of age provided additional parental consent. Only participants with no prior experience of the trust game were eligible to take part. Participants were randomly assigned to either the trustor or trustee role within one of two attentional conditions (shared or separated). Each pair was composed of individuals who were strangers to one another. Of the initial sample, 62 participants were assigned to the shared attention condition and 56 to the separated condition. Nine pairs were excluded due to substantial missing EEG data from one member of the pair, leaving 50 valid pairs (100 participants; 44 females; mean age = 20.02 years), with 25 pairs in each condition. The sex distribution was relatively balanced across conditions, and no significant demographic differences (e.g., age, educational level) were observed after data cleaning. All participants received compensation of ¥90 for their participation.

Procedure

Participants were paired and randomly assigned to one of two attentional conditions (shared or separated). Within each pair, one participant was designated as the trustor and the other as the trustee, yielding four experimental groups: trustors–shared, trustees–shared, trustors–separated, and trustees–separated. The role labels ‘trustor’ and ‘trustee’ were not disclosed to participants prior to the task to prevent biasing their expectations. To avoid potential confounds such as familiarity or nonverbal communication, pairs were composed of strangers and seated in a shielded room, separated by a lightproof curtain that eliminated visual and verbal contact. Each participant sat approximately 60 cm from a computer monitor (resolution: 1280 × 780, refresh rate: 60 Hz; Figure 1A).

After providing informed consent, participants completed the General Trust Scale (GTS; Yamagishi and Yamagishi 1994) to measure baseline trust beliefs. The GTS consists of six items assessing expectations of honesty and trustworthiness, rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). During questionnaire completion, experimenters fitted participants with EEG caps to record neural activity (Figure 1B).

Joint Flanker Task

The joint flanker paradigm extends the classic flanker task (Eriksen and Eriksen 1974) by introducing mutual awareness: pairs of participants performed the task simultaneously while being aware of each other's assigned targets (Atmaca et al. 2011). At the beginning of the session, participants completed 20 practice trials to familiarise themselves with the task. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 600–1800 ms (randomised), followed by a stimulus display. This randomised interval reduced predictability and ensured naturalistic responses. Stimuli consisted of five letters presented in a horizontal row, with the central letter serving as the target (Figure 1C).

Distinct target letters were assigned to correspond with roles in the subsequent trust game: trustors responded to ‘H’ and ‘K’ and trustees to ‘S’ and ‘C’. When the central letter matched one of their designated targets, participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing the assigned key (‘F’ for trustors, ‘J’ for trustees). When the central letter was not their target, they withheld a response until the next trial. Each trial lasted 4 s, terminating with a key press or advancing automatically if no response was made within the time limit. The task comprised four blocks of 80 trials (320 trials total), with a two‐minute break between blocks.

Stimuli appeared in four forms: Identical, congruent, neutral and incongruent. In the identical condition, all five letters matched the target (e.g., HHHHH, SSSSS). In the congruent condition, the central target was flanked by the participant's own target letters (e.g., KKHKK, CCSCC). In the neutral condition, the target was surrounded by letters unrelated to either participant's targets (e.g., UUHUU, UUSUU). In the incongruent condition, the central target was flanked by the partner's target letters (e.g., CCHCC, HHSHH).

The procedure was identical across attentional conditions, with one critical difference. In the shared attention condition, instructions at the start of the task included an additional sentence specifying the partner's target letters, ensuring mutual awareness. In the separated attention condition, this information was omitted, so participants had no knowledge of their partner's targets. As a result, incongruent and neutral trials were functionally equivalent in this condition. Importantly, in all conditions, participants were instructed only to respond to their own targets and were never asked to monitor their partner's letters.

Trust Game

Following the joint flanker task, participants completed a multi‐round trust game to assess trust behaviour (Berg et al. 1995; Johnson and Mislin 2011). In each round, the trustor received an initial endowment of ¥10 and decided how much to invest with the trustee, choosing among 0%, 30%, 50%, 70%, or 100%. The invested amount was automatically tripled and transferred to the trustee, who then decided how much to return to the trustor using the same percentage options. Each decision phase lasted 4 s. If no choice was made within the time limit, the trial advanced automatically and the decision was coded as missing. To promote intuitive responding while maintaining sufficient time for meaningful choice, each decision phase was constrained to a 4‐s window. Time‐limited decisions help minimise inter‐individual variability in deliberation time and encourage heuristic‐based choices, which aligns with theoretical accounts of rapid trust formation (Rand et al. 2014; Yamagishi et al. 2017). The selected duration also falls within the range commonly used in social and neuroeconomic paradigms (typically 2–6 s; Delgado et al. 2005; Fu et al. 2019; Krueger et al. 2007; van den Bos and Güroğlu 2009), providing a balance between experimental control and task feasibility. Participants played nine rounds in total. Earnings were updated after each round but participants were not informed of their cumulative payoff or their partner's decisions. To ensure that decisions reflected trust‐related preferences rather than strategic optimisation, the task instructions did not emphasise maximising individual or joint earnings.

Data Analysis

Joint Flanker Task

All analyses were performed in MATLAB R2018a. Reaction times (RTs) exceeding three standard deviations from a participant's mean were excluded. For each participant, average RTs were calculated separately for the four stimulus forms. Paired‐sample t‐tests were then conducted to compare RTs across experimental groups and stimulus forms.

Trust Game

To verify that participants had comparable baseline trust levels before the trust game, trust scores from the GTS were compared between the shared and separated attention conditions using independent‐samples t‐tests. Within each attentional condition, average investments and RTs were calculated separately for trustors and trustees across all rounds.

To examine effects of attentional condition, role and trial progression, two three‐way mixed ANOVAs were conducted on investments and RTs, organised into 2 (condition: Shared vs. separated) × 2 (role: trustor vs. trustee) × 9 (rounds) designs. Initial trust was assessed by comparing first‐round investments between the shared and separated conditions using an independent‐samples t‐test. To explore how trust developed across rounds, a piecewise linear regression model with 3000 bootstrap iterations was applied to reduce overfitting (Equation 1). In this model, round number was treated as the independent variable (x) and investment as the dependent variable (y), with a breakpoint (x 0 ) estimating the change in trust‐building rate.

Finally, the total amount retained by trustors and trustees after nine rounds was calculated to quantify summed benefits. Higher summed benefits indicate greater trust between the pair. Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare summed benefits between the shared and separated attention conditions.

EEG Preprocessing

EEG signals were recorded using a hyperscanning device (Neuracle Technology, Changzhou, China) with two BioSemi 64‐channel elastic caps, each connected to an identical amplifier, at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. Active electrodes were positioned on the left (M1) and right (M2) mastoids as offline references. Vertical and horizontal electro‐oculogram (VEOG, HEOG) electrodes were placed above and below the left eye, respectively and an electrocardiogram (ECG) electrode was placed on the nasal bridge. Electrode impedance was kept below 10 kΩ throughout the experiment.

Preprocessing was conducted in EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig 2004) in MATLAB R2018a. External channels (VEOG, HEOG, ECG) were removed, and the data were re‐referenced to the mean of M1 and M2, leaving 59 channels. Signals were band‐pass filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz to remove line noise. Automatic artifact rejection was applied in EEGLAB to identify bad channels and segments; bad channels were interpolated using the average of neighbouring electrodes. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then applied (threshold = 0.85) to remove muscle, eye, heart, line noise and channel noise artifacts (Delorme et al. 2007; Bahador et al. 2021). After cleaning, EEG data were segmented into epochs of 4 s (the duration of each phase), with four phases per round × nine rounds for each participant.

Oscillatory Power

EEG signals from each 4‐s phase were transformed into the frequency domain using a fast Fourier transform with a 20% tapered cosine window, yielding a frequency resolution of 0.25 Hz. Spectra were averaged across rounds for the decision phases, separately for trustors and trustees in both attentional conditions.

Theta (3–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) amplitudes were calculated by averaging spectral power within each band across four ROIs: prefrontal (Fpz, Fp1, Fp2), frontal (Fz, F1, F2), central (Cz, C1, C2) and occipital (Oz, O1, O2), following Yu et al. (2016). Group comparisons were conducted using one‐way ANOVAs separately for each band, ROI and role.

Intra‐Brain Functional Connectivity

Pearson correlations were computed between all channel pairs (59 channels) across nine rounds for each participant and role. Correlation matrices were Fisher Z‐transformed (z = atanh (r)) and averaged to obtain a stable FC matrix. Independent‐samples t‐tests with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction identified significant FC differences between conditions and roles (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Our analyses targeted covariation in oscillatory amplitude across spatially distributed ROIs; thus, Pearson correlation was selected to quantify intra‐brain connectivity. Amplitude‐based coupling was central to our research question and Pearson correlation is well established for this purpose (Cohen et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2020). Phase‐based metrics such as phase‐locking index are optimised for detecting phase synchrony and intentionally suppress zero‐lag relationships, making them less appropriate for amplitude co‐fluctuation.

Significant channel pairs were grouped into functionally coherent clusters using hierarchical clustering. Electrodes were then grouped into 13 anatomically defined ROIs (Yu et al. 2016). For each ROI pair, the proportion of significantly connected channels was calculated, and ROI pairs with ≥ 90% significant links were classified as strongly connected. Fisher Z‐values from these connections were correlated with participants' average investments across rounds.

Inter‐Brain Synchronisation

Inter‐brain connectivity between trustors and trustees was quantified using phase‐locking value (PLV; Lachaux et al. 1999; Burgess 2013). PLV was selected because, in hyperscanning, zero‐lag phase coupling cannot arise from shared dipoles or field spread and therefore provides a valid measure of interpersonal synchrony. PLVs were calculated across homologous brain regions during the two maintenance phases of each trial (2 phases × 9 rounds) and averaged across rounds. Electrode selection for the PLV topographies followed an anatomically constrained, data‐driven procedure. ROIs were defined based on prior hyperscanning literature and the spatial distribution of PLV values. Within each ROI, the electrode with the highest average PLV across participants was selected as the representative channel. This procedure ensured that channel selection was systematic and reproducible rather than arbitrary.

To assess behavioural relevance, PLV values were correlated with two measures: (1) the combined investments of trustor and trustee averaged across nine rounds and (2) the total summed benefits across the pair. The former reflects direct trust behaviour and the latter reflects its economic benefit. To improve model fit, the retained amount was log‐transformed. These measures were then correlated with neural synchronisation values across relevant brain regions.

Results

Flanker Effect

We compared RTs across stimulus forms in the shared and separated attention conditions. A robust flanker effect was observed: participants responded faster when central targets were flanked by identical or congruent letters than when flanked by neutral or incongruent letters (ps < 0.001). Critically, in the shared attention group, RTs were significantly longer for incongruent than neutral stimuli (p < 0.001; Figure 2), whereas this effect was absent in the separated group. These findings indicate distinct behavioural patterns between attention conditions, with shared attention increasing participants' sensitivity to their partner's target letters and producing greater interference under incongruence.

Panel B shows the corresponding RTs for the shared attention group. Panel C depicts the RT differences between the incongruent and neutral stimuli in shared and separated attention groups.

However, a direct comparison of interference scores (Incongruent—Neutral) between groups did not yield a significant difference, t(98) = 0.69, p = 0.49, Cohen's d = 0.14, indicating that the Group × Congruency interaction was not statistically reliable. We therefore interpret these between‐group differences cautiously. Nonetheless, the presence of a clear interference effect in the shared condition is consistent with prior demonstrations of joint flanker processing and confirms that the attentional manipulation was effective at the within‐group level.

Behavioural Results From the Trust Game

Participant characteristics were comparable across the shared and separated attention groups (Table 1). The two conditions did not differ significantly in age or gender distribution and all participants were right‐handed. Baseline dispositional trust, assessed using the GTS, was also comparable between groups (shared: M = 3.41, SD = 0.55; separated: M = 3.51, SD = 0.44), with no significant difference observed (t(98) = −0.97, p = 0.34). These results confirm that the subsequent behavioural differences in the trust game cannot be attributed to pre‐existing differences in demographic composition or general trust.

Investment patterns diverged significantly over time between the shared and separated attention conditions. Participants in the shared attention condition consistently invested more across rounds, and the disparity relative to the separated condition increased with repetition. Investments remained higher in the shared condition throughout the experiment (Figure 4), with a steeper initial increase. To assess the robustness of this effect, a bootstrap procedure was implemented. From the total sample of 50 pairs, 25 pairs were resampled with replacement 3000 times and a piecewise linear function was fitted to each resampled dataset, yielding 3000 slope estimates. A subsequent t‐test on these slopes confirmed that the early increase in investment was significantly larger in the shared attention condition than in the separated condition (p = 0.029; Figure 4).

A corresponding three‐way ANOVA for RT revealed significant main effects of role (F(1, 24) = 24.93, p < 0.001), trial number (F(8, 192) = 5.43, p < 0.001) and group (F(1, 24) = 6.52, p = 0.011). The interaction between group and role was also significant (F(1, 24) = 4.59, p = 0.033), paralleling the investment findings. As shown in Figure 3, the reaction‐time effect of shared attention was more pronounced for trustees, consistent with the greater deliberative demands inherent to the trustee role. Collectively, these results indicate that shared attention enhances trust‐related behaviour, with the effect particularly pronounced for trustors.

EEG Results

Local Activity Analysis

The first EEG analysis compared neural oscillation amplitudes in the theta, alpha and beta frequency bands across key brain regions between the shared and separated attention conditions. Amplitudes were extracted from prefrontal (Fpz, Fp1, Fp2), frontal (Fz, F1, F2), central (Cz, C1, C2) and occipital electrodes (Oz, O1, O2) during the decision phase of the trust game. These electrodes were selected on the basis of topographical results, which indicated large amplitudes under both attention conditions (Figure 5). For trustors, significant group differences were found in the prefrontal region across all three frequency bands. Participants in the shared attention condition exhibited higher spectral power than those in the separated condition for theta (F(1,48) = 8.43, p = 0.006), alpha (F(1,48) = 5.03, p = 0.030) and beta (F(1,48) = 6.04, p = 0.018) bands (Figure 5A). In contrast for trustees, no significant differences were found across any band (ps > 0.266; Figure 5B). These results suggest that trustors in the shared attention condition engaged stronger prefrontal activation during trust decision‐making. Accordingly, the prefrontal area was selected as ROI for subsequent analysis.

We then examined the association between neural activity and trust behaviour. For trustors, prefrontal amplitudes in all three bands were positively correlated with average investment across rounds: theta (r = 0.371, p < 0.001), alpha (r = 0.300, p < 0.001) and beta (r = 0.253, p < 0.001; Figure 6A). No significant correlations were found for trustees (|r|s < 0.042, ps > 0.456; Figure 6B). Further analyses showed that these correlations were mainly driven by the shared attention group. In this group, alpha (r = 0.253, p < 0.001) and beta (r = 0.198, p = 0.003) amplitudes were significantly associated with the investment behaviour, while theta was not (r = 0.128, p = 0.212; Figure 6C). No significant correlations were found in the separated condition (|r|s < 0.113, ps > 0.101; Figure 6D). These results suggest that prefrontal activity in the shared attention condition is more strongly linked to trust decisions.

To compare the strength of these associations across conditions, we conducted bootstrap tests (5000 iterations) on the difference in regression slopes between the shared‐ and separated‐attention groups. Significant slope differences were observed for frontal theta (p = 0.03) and frontal beta (p = 0.01), indicating stronger associations in the shared‐attention condition. No reliable difference was found for frontal alpha (p = 0.11). Thus, the relationship between prefrontal oscillatory amplitude and trust behaviour was selectively enhanced under shared attention for theta‐ and beta‐band activity.

Intra‐Brain Functional Connectivity

To characterise intra‐brain FC, we first examined overall connectivity patterns for trustors and trustees across theta, alpha and beta bands (Figure 7A1–B3). Both groups showed broadly similar large‐scale connectivity across conditions. However, statistical comparisons revealed significant differences between shared and separated attention conditions (Figure 7C1–F3). For trustors, the shared attention condition elicited stronger FC across all three frequency bands, particularly involving prefrontal connections (Figure 7C1–D3). By contrast, trustees showed relatively few significant differences (Figure 7E1–F3). These findings indicate that attentional alignment modulates functional integration more strongly in trustors, consistent with their role in initiating trust behaviour.

We then assessed whether strength of intra‐brain FC was associated with trust behaviour, pooling participants across attention conditions. As shown in Figure 8, trustor's investment correlated positively with beta‐band connectivity between the prefrontal and right central regions (r = 0.336, p = 0.017; Figure 8A), as well as between the prefrontal and right temporal regions (r = 0.303, p = 0.032; Figure 8B). Thus, intra‐brain FC in these regions is significantly associated with the amount invested. These results suggest that enhanced integration across task‐relevant brain regions, particularly involving the prefrontal cortex, is linked to increased trust behaviour.

To determine whether this pooled correlation reflected individual‐level variability or group differences, we conducted partial correlations controlling for attention condition. Neither prefrontal–right central FC (r = 0.22, p = 0.13) nor prefrontal–right temporal FC (r = 0.18, p = 0.23) remained significant when controlling for condition, and within‐condition correlations were also non‐significant. Pooling was used to complement the condition‐specific analyses by examining whether intra‐brain FC predicted trust behaviour at the individual level; however, the follow‐up analyses indicate that these pooled effects should be interpreted as group‐driven rather than reflecting individual‐level correlations.

Inter‐Brain Synchronisation

To investigate whether shared attention influenced synchronisation between pairs, we compared PLVs between the shared and separated attention conditions. Stronger inter‐brain synchronisation was observed in the shared attention condition, particularly in the theta band. This effect was most pronounced in the right hemisphere (Figure 9A1–B3), including right frontal, central, right central, right temporal, parietal and right parietal areas.

Since EEG data typically contains a large number of channels, leading to redundancy, the channels with the highest correlation within these regions were chosen for the following analyses to reduce noise and redundant information (Figure 9A1). In this case, six channels, F4, Cz, FC6, T8, POz and P4, were selected. A significant difference was observed merely in the theta oscillation of right frontal (t = 3.05, p corrected = 0.033) regions, with higher synchronisation consistently found in the shared attention (Figure 9C).

To assess behavioural relevance, we examined correlations between neural synchronisation and trust behaviour. Theta right frontal synchronisation correlated significantly with both combined investments (p = 0.029; Figure 10) and total retained money from both shared and separated conditions (p = 0.026; Figure 10). Across all conditions, neural synchronisation was consistently higher in the shared attention condition compared to the separated attention condition, reinforcing the role of shared attention in trust behaviours. Partial correlations controlling for attention condition were non‐significant (PLV vs. the sum of investments: r = 0.23, p = 0.11; PLV vs. the sum of money retained: r = 0.24, p = 0.10) and within‐condition correlations were also non‐significant. Thus, the pooled relationships in Figure 10 primarily reflected group differences between attention conditions rather than individual‐level associations.

Discussion

Our findings show that shared attention with another person promotes trust. Behaviourally, participants in the shared attention condition invested and reciprocated more, indicating that attentional alignment increases willingness to engage in trust decisions. At the neural level, prefrontal oscillations in the theta, alpha and beta bands tracked variation in trust behaviour, while beta‐band FC between prefrontal and posterior regions further predicted trust investments. Beyond intra‐brain dynamics, trust was also supported by inter‐brain synchronisation in right frontal regions, reflecting shared neural states that emerge from attentional alignment. Together, these results demonstrate that shared attention enhances trust through three complementary neural mechanisms: elevated prefrontal oscillatory activity, strengthened beta‐band FC between prefrontal and posterior regions, and frontal inter‐brain synchronisation.

Heuristic‐based accounts propose that individuals often rely on minimal information to make trust decisions efficiently, without engaging in deliberative reasoning (Lewicki and Brinsfield 2011). For example, people are more likely to trust messages endorsed by high‐status sources even when the content is identical (Petty et al. 1983), and can form trustworthiness judgements from facial appearance within 100 milliseconds (Willis and Todorov 2006). Extending this work, the present study demonstrates that trust can be initiated even when social information is extremely limited. Participants in the shared attention condition invested more from the very first round of the trust game, despite the absence of communicative cues, feedback, or shared goals. The only common element was recognition of a partner's simultaneous engagement and attentional alignment. This suggests that trust can emerge without familiarity or reciprocity, arising instead from co‐presence in a shared attentional state. Notably, this difference persisted across repeated rounds, with the shared condition consistently showing higher levels of investment. In addition to this overall condition effect, the two roles involve distinct cognitive demands. Although shared attention had a stronger impact on trust behaviour for trustors, the reaction‐time effect was more pronounced for trustees. This asymmetry is expected, as trustees must evaluate the trustor's prior decision before responding, making their choices more deliberative and therefore more sensitive to attentional context than first‐mover decisions (McCabe et al. 2003; Krueger et al. 2007). However, the overall shortening of decision times under shared attention also suggests that attentional alignment may broadly promote more rapid, intuitive judgements, over and above role‐specific differences.

Across both attentional conditions, prefrontal oscillations in the theta, alpha and beta bands tracked trust behaviour, with each band reflecting a distinct functional role. Frontal theta power, linked to conflict monitoring and risk evaluation (Schutter and van Honk 2006; Cavanagh et al. 2009), may capture cognitive engagement during trust decisions under uncertainty. Alpha activity over frontal and frontocentral regions, associated with socio‐emotional processing and self–other merging (Oh et al. 2022; Song et al. 2022), may reduce psychological distance and facilitate identification‐based trust. Beta oscillations, which were particularly prominent, have been implicated in reward processing and the inhibition of doubt (Engel and Fries 2010; Mas‐Herrero et al. 2015), suggesting a role in reinforcing cooperative intent. Together, these results highlight the prefrontal cortex as a central hub in integrating cognitive, affective and motivational processes to support trust behaviour.

Variations in trust behaviour were explained by beta‐band FC between the prefrontal cortex and posterior regions, specifically the right central and right temporal areas. Prefrontal–central connectivity likely reflects the coordination of attentional alignment with executive control, whereas prefrontal–temporal connectivity may support social inference and affective integration. Prior work has shown that beta oscillations facilitate the coordination of distributed networks involved in reward processing and sustained cooperation (Cohen et al. 2007; Huster et al. 2013). Convergent neuroimaging evidence further indicates that social decision‐making, trust and joint attention rely on coordinated interactions between prefrontal and posterior/temporal regions. Functional MRI studies have demonstrated coupling between lateral and medial PFC and temporoparietal, temporal pole and posterior cortices during trust games, reciprocity and joint attention (Redcay et al. 2012; Elison et al. 2013; Billeci et al. 2017; Bellucci et al. 2019; Lu et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021, 2023). EEG work similarly links beta‐band oscillations in temporoparietal and posterior regions to joint attention and mentalisation processes (Park et al. 2018; Soto‐Icaza et al. 2019) and MEG studies show that beta‐band activity mediates long‐range prefrontal–posterior communication supporting network coordination and cognitive control (Engel and Fries 2010; Huster et al. 2013; Gui et al. 2018; Spadone et al. 2021). Extending this view, our findings suggest that enhanced prefrontal–posterior beta FC provides a neural pathway for integrating attentional control with social affective processes, thereby supporting the emergence of trust under shared attention.

The inter‐brain synchronisation findings provide convergent evidence that shared attention shapes trust decision‐making through interpersonal neural alignment. This aligns with hyperscanning research showing that frontal synchronisation supports interpersonal coordination and trust, indicating that shared attention not only modulates individual neural processing but also fosters alignment across partners (Sun et al. 2019). A broad literature demonstrates that frontal inter‐brain synchrony reliably emerges during cooperative behaviour (Funane et al. 2011; Cui et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2018) and during social alignment tasks that require dyad‐level attentional coordination. Coordinated leader–follower interactions, such as in face‐to‐face guitar duets, further increase frontal phase coherence and naturalistic paradigms show synchronisation within frontal regions during live eye contact (Sänger et al. 2012; Hirsch et al. 2017). Extending these findings, frontal theta synchrony has also been shown to correlate with the sense of joint agency in leaders during coordinated action (Shiraishi and Shimada 2021), suggesting that theta‐band coupling may support shared intentionality and role‐based coordination. Prior work with parent–child and adult–infant dyads similarly demonstrates that frontal synchrony indexes shared engagement during cooperative tasks (Leong et al. 2017; Reindl et al. 2018).

In EEG hyperscanning, stronger frontal theta synchrony has been observed during human–human cooperation, underscoring theta‐band activity as a marker of reciprocal prediction and genuinely interactive decision‐making (Hu et al. 2018). In addition, frontal theta interpersonal coupling increases during cooperative team‐based gameplay, with stronger synchronisation emerging under low task load and within shared task contexts (Strang et al. 2015), highlighting the sensitivity of theta‐band alignment to interpersonal task structure and the extent to which individuals perceive themselves as jointly engaged. Frontal theta activity has also been implicated in conflict monitoring and cognitive control (Cavanagh and Frank 2014; Wang et al. 2020) and theta‐band coupling across brains has been linked to cooperative interaction (Hu et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2020). The stronger theta coupling observed under shared relative to separated attention therefore suggests that attentional alignment facilitates trust by enhancing dyadic coordination at a neural level and supporting more fluent social interaction. Notably, these effects emerged even without explicit communication, highlighting the potency of attentional co‐presence in fostering interpersonal trust.

Limitations

Despite the advantages of combining behavioural measures with dual‐EEG hyperscanning to isolate the effects of shared attention on trust, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study used a between‐subject design, which may introduce inter‐individual variability that cannot be fully eliminated. Future work could employ within‐subject or cross‐over designs to assess attentional alignment effects more directly within the same individuals. Second, although the sample size was adequate for the behavioural and EEG analyses, replication with larger, more diverse and more representative populations would help establish the robustness and generalisability of the findings across cultures and ethnicities. Third, the experimental setting restricted direct interaction between partners, which was necessary to isolate shared attention from other social cues but may limit ecological validity. Real‐world trust interactions typically involve richer communicative channels, and future research could examine how attentional alignment interacts with communication, facial cues, or shared goals in more naturalistic environments.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that trust can arise under minimal social conditions through attentional co‐presence. Even without communication, visual cues, or feedback, shared attention enhanced trust behaviour, indicating that attentional alignment itself serves as a reliable basis for trust formation. Neural evidence revealed a multi‐level mechanism: prefrontal oscillations supported cognitive and affective engagement, intra‐brain beta‐band FC between prefrontal and posterior regions integrated attentional and socio‐emotional processes, and inter‐brain frontal synchronisation reflected dyadic alignment. Together, these findings converge on the conclusion that shared attention provides a powerful foundation for interpersonal trust, highlighting a minimal yet robust pathway through which social bonds can emerge.
