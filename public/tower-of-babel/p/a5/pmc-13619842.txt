Self‐calibrated B1 + and B0 Field Inhomogeneities Estimation (SAFE) in 3D Magnetic Resonance Fingerprinting
Authors: Mengze Gao, Xiaozhi Cao, Daniel Raz Abraham, Zihan Zhou, Nan Wang, Congyu Liao, Yang Yang, Hanlin Sun, Hongwei Li, Yan Bai, Yu Shen, Yaping Wu, Meiyun Wang, Kawin Setsompop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo develop SAFE, a self‐calibrated framework to estimate B1 + and B0 field inhomogeneities directly from conventional magnetic resonance fingerprinting (MRF) acquisitions and improve its quantification accuracy.MethodsSAFE utilized a two‐step approach. First, two physics‐informed image markers are extracted from the MRF data to create a magnitude and a phase image that are highly correlated with B1 + and B0 inhomogeneities, respectively. Second, a deep learning (DL) network is applied to map these markers to quantitative field maps. SAFE was tested on 3D Spiral Projection Imaging MRF brain acquisition at 3T, where the network was trained and validated across a multi‐site, multi‐vendor dataset (N = 358) from healthy volunteers and clinical population. The capability of SAFE to be applied to unseen MRF sequences with different signal preparations and flip‐angle trains through adaptively retraining without the need for additional training data was also tested.ResultsSAFE achieved normalized‐root‐mean‐square‐error within 3% against gold‐standard field‐calibration scans on both B1 + and B0 maps (N = 32), with high performance remaining on datasets from scanners that the training data were acquired from. T1 and T2 biases were shown to be effectively corrected on healthy volunteers, a patient with brain tumor, and a pediatric subject. Tissue quantification accuracy after SAFE‐estimated field correction was validated on a large patient cohort (N = 86). SAFE was also demonstrated to be adaptable to different MRF sequences without acquiring additional training data.ConclusionBy combining physics‐informed image markers with DL, the proposed SAFE framework enables calibration‐free estimation of B1 + and B0 field inhomogeneities at 3T for whole brain MRF.Keywords: deep learning, field inhomogeneity, MR fingerprinting, quantitative MRI

Introduction

Magnetic resonance fingerprinting [1] (MRF) enables fast quantitative imaging. However, accurate quantification necessitates the characterization and incorporation of B1 + and B0 field inhomogeneities into the image reconstruction and parameter fitting process to avoid spatial bias and blurring artifacts [2, 3]. Although separate calibration scans can characterize these imperfections, such procedures prolong scan time, complicate workflow, and often require co‐registration. While fast calibration methods have been developed [4, 5], estimating inhomogeneities directly from MRF data remains desirable to obviate the need for additional acquisitions.

Along this theme, tailored MRF acquisition approaches [6, 7] have been developed to jointly estimate field imperfections and tissue parameters by enhancing MRF's signal sensitivity to field imperfections. Nevertheless, conventional MRF sequences, including those optimized via Cramer‐Rao Bound [8, 9], do not contain sufficient independent sensitivity to these field imperfections, where, for example, a change in its signal time‐course could be attributed either to a change in B1 + or a change in T2. Consequently, the joint fitting process can become ill‐posed, leading to poor field estimation and biased tissue parameter maps.

Traditional techniques such as autofocus [10] utilize phase information to estimate B0 through iterative searching; however, it might fail in regions where B0 or its variations are large. Recently, deep learning (DL) approaches have emerged as data‐driven alternatives. However, many approaches are based on identifying blurring artifacts for B0 estimation [11], which can be difficult to decouple from complex anatomical variability. To address these limitations, in this work, a tailored DL approach is developed to accurately and robustly estimate B1 + and B0 inhomogeneities directly from conventional MRF data. Accordingly, the self‐calibrated B1 + and B0 field estimation (SAFE) for MRF is proposed. With SAFE, two physics‐informed image markers are extracted from the MRF data to form a magnitude and a phase image marker that are highly correlated only with B1 +/B0 inhomogeneities, respectively. These markers are then mapped to field map estimates via a DL network. This strategy circumvents the ill‐posedness of the aforementioned joint estimation and ensures robust performance by providing the network with physics‐guided inputs. As a proof of concept, SAFE was implemented on a 3D Fast imaging with steady‐state precession (FISP) MRF acquisition [12, 13] with 3D spiral projection imaging (3D‐SPI) readout for whole brain imaging at 3T, where the network was trained on diverse multi‐vendor dataset. The model was demonstrated to provide accurate B1 + and B0 field maps. Furthermore, a novel sequence‐adaptive retraining scheme was validated, allowing the application of SAFE to new, unseen MRF sequences without reacquiring new training data.

Source code along with an example set of paired MRF data with B1 + and B0 maps from calibration scans across 20 subjects are publicly available at https://github.com/oagmz/SAFE.git.

Methods

Background on Data Acquisition, Image Reconstruction, B1 + and B0 Inhomogeneities and Their Corrections

The SAFE framework was developed and evaluated on a FISP‐MRF sequence [12], wherein 500 repetition times (TRs) with varying FAs (Figure 1A) were acquired following an adiabatic inversion pulse. A tiny‐golden‐angle‐shuffling (TGAS) 3D‐SPI readout [13] was utilized for data acquisition (Figure 1B), and spatiotemporal subspace reconstruction [14, 15] was employed using five temporal basis [16].

During the RF excitation, B1 + inhomogeneity, arising from coil design as well as patient‐specific dielectric properties [17], induces spatial variability in the MRF FA train that deviates from the prescribed one, potentially causing spatially varying bias in the T1 and T2 estimates. In typical MRF sequences, T1 sensitivity is derived primarily from data acquired subsequent to the initial B1 +‐insensitive adiabatic inversion pulse, while T2 sensitivity is mainly obtained from the subsequent varying FA train [9]. Thus, B1 + inhomogeneity predominantly biases the T2 map (Figure 1D). Signal evolutions under different levels of B1 + variabilities can be simulated and incorporated into the MRF dictionary and used in a joint field imperfections and tissue parameters fitting process. However, such joint fitting can become ill‐posed, yielding biased tissue parameter maps and poor B1 + estimation (see Figure 2B, 1st and 3rd column, to compare flip‐angle map estimated from joint fitting vs. from B1 + calibration scan). Alternatively, utilizing a B1 + map from calibration scan as prior significantly improves the fitting condition [16].

During the spiral readout, B0 inhomogeneity, influenced by magnet imperfections and patient‐induced susceptibility variations [18], causes additional spatiotemporal phase modulation, resulting in image blurring (Figure 1C). To correct this, a B0 map along with multi‐frequency interpolation [19] (MFI) can be incorporated into the subspace reconstruction. For 3T brain imaging targeted in this work, five demodulation frequencies ωm = [−200:100:200] Hz were used.

Beyond their individual effects, the interaction between B1 + and B0 inhomogeneity is also worth noting. Since typical RF excitation in MRF is bandwidth‐limited, particularly with water‐only excitation [16], B0 inhomogeneity noticeably reduces the effective FA, where the effective spatial FAscaling is a multiplicative combination of B1 + inhomogeneity and this B0‐induced flip‐angle reduction [4]. This effective FAscaling can then be accounted for during the dictionary fitting described above.

The SAFE Approach

The MRF signal evolution is simultaneously sensitive to tissue properties as well as spatial variability in FA‐scaling (FAscaling) and B0 inhomogeneity. The aim of SAFE is to create image markers to isolate part of the acquired MRF signal that is mainly correlated to either FAscaling variability or B0 inhomogeneity and utilize a DL model to estimate FAscaling map and B0 map robustly from these image markers. Note that if desired, B1 + map can be further extracted from the estimated FAscaling map and B0 map by dividing out the B0‐induced FAscaling effect.

Here, MFA correlates well with the FAscaling map that contains the multiplicative effect of B1 + inhomogeneity and B0‐induced flip‐angle reduction.

In addition to the signal contribution generated by the current RF excitation, the overall image at a particular TR is also affected by the excitation spin‐history and signal refocusing, which are dependent on the tissue properties. For tissues with long T2s (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid), significant additional stimulated echo contributes to the overall signal at the large FA time‐point, as observed in the cerebrospinal fluid regions of MFA in (Figure 2B). These complex, non‐linear dependencies make the MFA‐to‐FAscaling mapping deviate from the ideal theoretical curve. This is the motivation for employing a DL algorithm to implicitly resolve these tissue‐dependent biases from underlying anatomy and provide accurate FAscaling estimates.

Image Marker for B0 Estimation

SAFE leverages the phase information of the reconstructed images for robust B0 estimation. A common phase‐based B0 estimation approach is to estimate B0 using image phase differences at multiple echo times (TEs). In the MRF acquisition, data is typically collected at a single TE, but an average‐contrast image at a second TE can be formed by combining data of the readout rewinders across different TRs (Figure 3A,B). The phase difference of the two average‐contrast images at different TEs can then be used to form the image marker, MB0:

Figure 3 C indicates that the MB0 contains high similarity to the B0 map obtained from a separate field‐calibration scan, but with a certain level of phase wrapping due to the relatively long ΔTE (8 ms), and incoherent aliasing artifacts caused by the higher undersampling rate of the rewinder trajectory. These nonlinear imperfections necessitate a subsequent DL algorithm to resolve by leveraging strong prior knowledge of the expected spatial smoothness of the B0 maps.

SAFE Network Architecture and Training

Two DL networks were trained with input MFA or MB0 respectively and output corresponding FAscaling or B0 map. Both networks utilized a U‐Net [20] structure with skip connections removed (Figure S1). During each encoding block, volume size decreased by a factor of 8 while channel size expanded by a factor of 2; the decoding path performed the reverse operations. Each convolutional layer employed a 3‐by‐3‐by‐3 kernel, followed by instance normalization and LeakyReLU activation. A final convolution layer with a 5‐by‐5‐by‐5 kernel was used to enforce spatial smoothness. Training deployed the Adam optimizer and L1 loss, calculated within a high confidence masked region (via BET [21]).

Sequence‐Agnostic Adaption

The DL network training requires matching input and output pairs, which are sequence specific. As such, the model weights trained on one MRF sequence can't be directly applied to another. A wide variety of MRF sequences have been developed, so it would be helpful to be able to adapt the training dataset obtained for one sequence for use to train the SAFE‐networks of any other MRF sequences without having to reacquire new training data.

Herein a sequence adaptive scheme, Adaptive‐SAFE, is developed (Figure S2), where the B1 +, B0, PD, T1, and T2 maps, extracted from paired MRF and field‐calibration scan of the original MRF sequence, are used to simulate the target sequence's signal evolution based on its sequence parameters. A synthetic image marker, MFA_new, can then be extracted from this simulated data and paired with the original FAscaling map to retrain the FAscaling estimation network. If the target sequence has the same readout trajectory, it's unnecessary to retrain the B0 estimation network. But if this isn't the case, simulation of the MRF forward model is necessary including the new trajectory and B0 map, so that I^out_new and I^in_new can be obtained to create MB0_new, which can then be paired with the original B0 map to retrain the B0 estimation network.

In this work, Adaptive‐SAFE was tested on two new target sequences. The first is a FISP‐MRF sequence that utilizes 1000 TRs compared to our original MRF sequence (500 TRs), while also employing markedly different FA train that we adopted from Figure 1B in Jiang et al. [12] In the subsequent sections, we will refer to this sequence as “1000TRs‐MRF.” The second is the ViSTa‐MRF [22] (Visualization of Short Transverse relaxation time component MRF) sequence which has been designed for quantitative myelin water fraction mapping. To sensitize its acquisition to myelin water, ViSTa‐MRF contains a double inversion preparation module as well as markedly different flip‐angle train over 660 TRs. In this work, we focused on testing the adaptability property of SAFE for FAscaling, thus, the same 3D‐SPI readout is employed for both sequences and only the paired synthesized FAscaling datasets from the proposed sequence adaptive scheme were used to retrain the FAscaling estimation network respectively.

Data Acquisition

All data were acquired on 3T GE and Siemens scanners with approval of the institutional review board with informed consent obtained.

3D FISP‐MRF: Data were acquired at 1 mm isotropic resolution over the whole brain using 3D‐SPI acquisition with TGAS readouts. The sequence used a 1.7 ms rect pulse for water‐selective excitation, a 500‐TR FA train, over a 2‐min scan as per [16]. The readout duration was 6.7 ms, followed by a 1.3 ms rewinder.

1000TRs‐MRF: Data were acquired at 1 mm isotropic resolution with 3D TGAS‐SPI readout, with a different FA train over 1000 TRs, resulting in a 6‐min scan.

ViSTa‐MRF: Data were acquired at 1 mm isotropic resolution with 3D TGAS‐SPI readout, featuring a Cramér–Rao lower bound optimized FA train over 660 TRs, resulting in a 5‐min scan [22].

Ground truth field maps: Gold standard B1 + and B0 maps were acquired using a Bloch‐Siegert pulse sequence [5] and a dual‐echo gradient echo sequence respectively, using efficient 3D spiral projection readout [4]. Both were acquired with 4 mm isotropic resolution and a total scan time of 16 s and were subsequently registered to the MRF dataset. The FAscaling maps were calculated from the B1 + and B0 maps based on the spectral response of the excitation pulse (Figure 2E top‐left panel).

A SAFE model was first trained on 358 3D‐FISP MRF datasets from adult healthy volunteers (N = 137) and patients (N = 221) acquired from 6 scanner models across 4 institutions (Stanford University, UCSF, Henan People's Hospital, and Third Hospital of Mianyang; see Table S1) and tested on subjects across 8 different GE and Siemens scanner models, consisting of 5 subjects from each of the 6 scanners used in acquiring training data, and 1 subject from each of 2 additional scanners to demonstrate generalizability.

The 1000TRs‐MRF and ViSTa‐MRF Adaptive‐SAFE models for FAscaling map estimation were retrained separately on 20 respective synthesized 3D datasets (generated from original sequence's training data) and tested on 3 datasets from healthy volunteers for each sequence.

It can be observed from Figure 5 that tissue quantification is primarily biased by FAscaling‐induced artifacts, which T2 maps are particularly susceptible to. Thus, an additional T2 quantification of different brain regions via SAFE‐estimated field correction was performed on a larger patient cohort (N = 86). Figure S3 shows that the T2 values obtained via SAFE‐estimated FAscaling correction (orange) are essentially identical to ones obtained from reference FAscaling correction using separate B1 + and B0 calibration scans (cyan), while T2 values without correction (purple) contain significant bias. In brain segments that spans across a large portion of the brain (e.g., cerebral cortex and cerebral white matter), there is a high variance of FAscaling across the cohort relative to the reference which propagates into T2 estimations. On the other hand, smaller brain regions in the mid and lower parts of the brain (e.g., putamen and hippocampus) typically experience a higher FAscaling than prescribed which causes the uncorrected T2 to be lower than expected.

Discussion

In this study, we developed SAFE, a self‐calibrated MRF field‐estimation framework that combined physics‐informed image markers with DL to estimate FAscaling and B0 field inhomogeneities. The model was demonstrated to provide B1 + and B0 field maps with normalized root‐mean‐square error (NRMSE) within 3% against gold‐standard references obtained from calibration scans across multi‐site, multi‐vendor, multi‐population, as well as multi‐sequence scenarios without the need for training data reacquisition, where Adaptive‐SAFE was employed.

In this work, we focused on conventional 3D SPI‐MRF sequences with different FA and contrast preparation. In general, the level of B0‐induced blurriness is dependent on the readout trajectory employed. For other 3D MRF readout trajectories, for example, 3D stack‐of‐spirals, B0‐induced blurring can be smaller compared to an equivalent 3D spiral‐projection of the same readout duration due to the absence of the blurring along the z‐direction. Nonetheless, there could still be significant blurring in high‐susceptibility regions necessitating B0 estimation and correction for better quantification. Moreover, SAFE/Adaptive‐SAFE should be applicable on other MRF's excitation approaches (e.g., various FA/diffusion prep) and/or acquisition trajectories (e.g., rosette [23], cones [24], and echo planar time‐resolved imaging [25, 26]) that have FAscaling sensitivity and multi‐TEs information to create independent field‐sensitive image markers.

The SAFE method should benefit both prospective and retrospective MRF studies. For prospective acquisitions, it streamlines clinical workflow and reduces scan time by eliminating the need for field‐calibration scans. For retrospective studies, it should improve tissue quantification fidelity of previously acquired datasets where calibration scans were omitted.

Limitations of the current study are noted below, which also provide avenues for future work. Firstly, the generation of MB0 requires rewinder readout in the sequences and no overlapping with the crusher gradients, otherwise it would preclude the sampling of usable data for B0 estimation. Secondly, SAFE's agnostic property was demonstrated with consistent readout on 3T brain MRI. Alternative trajectories (e.g., 3D cones, rosette), rewinders and/or field strength may introduce a different level of phase wraps and undersampling artifacts to MB0, necessitating further investigation on a sequence‐adaptive scheme for MB0 under these conditions. Thirdly, in scenarios that contain large spatial FAscaling variations (e.g., ultra‐high field), an image from a TR with high FAnominal can be of much lower flip angle in certain regions and overflipped at others, compromising MFA accuracy. A potential solution is to utilize a set of MFA generated from taking ratios of images across a range of TRs with different FAnominal and utilize these as inputs to the network.
