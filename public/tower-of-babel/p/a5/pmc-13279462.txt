Aβ‐ and tau‐associated neuroinflammatory signatures in Alzheimer's disease

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONNeuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression, with distinct responses linked to amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathology. Two detrimental waves of neuroinflammation have been proposed, the first during early Aβ accumulation, and the second during widespread tau deposition. However, the neuroinflammatory signatures associated with each phase remain unclear.METHODSWe studied 63 individuals who underwent Aβ and tau positron emission tomography imaging along with cerebrospinal fluid profiling of 368 inflammation‐related proteins. Protein contrasts between cognitively unimpaired Aβ‐negative and Aβ‐positive individuals defined the signature of the Aβ phase, while comparisons between cognitively impaired Aβ‐positive individuals with early versus late tau pathology characterized the tau phase. Gene Ontology enrichment identified biological processes associated with differentially expressed proteins.RESULTSDuring the Aβ phase, 34 proteins were downregulated and mapped to 157 biological processes, including Toll‐like receptor signaling, nuclear factor kappa beta activation, and cytokine production. The tau phase showed upregulation of 23 proteins associated with 82 biological processes enriched in adaptive immune responses. A set of 48 biological processes and three proteins including interleukin 4 receptor, cystosolic phospholipidase A2, and secretoglobin family 3A member 2 showed opposite regulation patterns, being downregulated in the Aβ phase and upregulated in the tau phase.DISCUSSIONOur results revealed distinct neuroinflammatory signatures and biological processes associated with Aβ‐ and tau‐dominant stages of AD. The reversal in protein expression patterns across these stages underscores the need for stage‐specific neuroimmune therapeutic strategies.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is defined neuropathologically by extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles, yet mounting evidence indicates that neuroinflammation is a third, equally dynamic, axis of pathology that both shapes and responds to Aβ and tau deposition. 1 , 2 Using in vivo positron emission tomography (PET) imaging of microglial translocator protein (TSPO), we recently demonstrated two temporally distinct waves of detrimental neuroinflammation, an early wave coincident with cortical Aβ accumulation in cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals and a second wave that emerges when tau pathology becomes widespread in cognitively impaired (CI) Aβ‐positive patients. 3 Although TSPO PET has been proven to be a helpful tool for understanding neuroinflammation in AD, especially topographically, it cannot capture the neuroinflammatory signatures associated with protective and detrimental effects of neuroinflammation.

Large‐scale, unbiased cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomic studies have begun to catalog hundreds to thousands of proteins altered in AD and to reveal molecular signatures linked to innate immunity, synaptic dysfunction, and blood‐brain‐barrier breakdown. 4 , 5 , 6 However, most of these efforts relied on clinically defined cohorts without PET confirmation or without staging tau pathology. Consequently, the phase‐specific inflammatory signatures that accompany the hierarchical progression of AD pathology remain poorly resolved.

Here, we leverage a deeply characterized cohort that combines multimodal PET imaging with a 368 CSF inflammatory proteomic panel to delineate the stage‐specific neuroinflammatory signatures and Gene Ontology biological processes that accompany cortical Aβ deposition and the sequential spread of tau pathology across the AD continuum.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Study participants were from the Translational Biomarkers in Aging and Dementia (TRIAD) cohort and underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessments, including the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) evaluation. CU individuals exhibited no objective cognitive impairment and possessed a global CDR score of 0. CI patients were characterized by global CDR scores higher than 0. To include CI participants in the AD continuum, only Aβ+ participants were included in the CI group. 7 All participants underwent PET scans for Aβ ([18F]AZD4694) and tau ([18F]MK6240), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All participants provided written informed consent, and ethical approval was obtained from the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) PET Working Committee and the Douglas Mental Health University Institute Research Ethics Board. Participants received compensation commensurate with the number of study procedures completed and the duration of participation.

PET and MRI acquisition and processing

T1‐weighted MRIs were collected at the MNI using a 3T Siemens Magnetom system. Whole brain, high‐resolution structural images were acquired using a magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence. T1‐weighted images underwent correction for intensity inhomogeneity and field distortion, were coregistered, and normalized to MNI template space. PET data were acquired on a Siemens High Resolution Research Tomograph and corrected for attenuation, motion, dead time, decay, and scattered and random events. [18F]AZD4694 and [18F]MK6240 images were acquired at 40 to 70 minutes and 60 to 90 minutes postinjection, respectively. PET images were automatically aligned with the native T1‐weighted MRI using linear transformations. 8 The PET images were subsequently registered to the MNI space by applying the transformations from the PET to native MRI and from the native MRI to the template space. PET images were smoothed to achieve a final resolution of 8‐mm full width at half maximum. Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated using the whole cerebellum gray matter for Aβ PET and the inferior cerebellar gray matter for tau PET. Neocortical Aβ SUVR was estimated from the precuneus, prefrontal, orbitofrontal, parietal, temporal, and cingulate cortices and converted to the Centiloid scale. 9 A cutoff of Centiloid > 12 was used to determine Aβ positivity (corresponding to moderate‐to‐frequent neuritic plaques as classified by the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease 10 and demonstrated to represent subtle Aβ pathology in CU individuals 11 ). Tau PET Braak‐like stages were calculated from corresponding brain regions and positivity was defined based on the CU young group. 12 Early tau positivity was based on Braak stages I to IV, and late tau positivity on Braak stages V and VI. 12 Regions of interest were defined using the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) atlas.

CSF proteomics

CSF samples were analyzed for 368 proteins using a comprehensive library of validated, high‐quality assays for inflammatory biomarkers: the Olink Explore 368 Inflammation proteomic panel. This panel uses proximity extension assay (PEA) technology for measurement. Protein concentrations are expressed as Normalized Protein Expression (NPX) values, an arbitrary unit on a Log2 scale derived from normalized Ct values. The assay's validation data are available on the Olink website (www.olink.com). For quality control (QC) purposes, each target protein had NPX values scaled, and individual points were excluded if they were above quantile 2 or below quantile –2. No individuals or target proteins were excluded from the analysis. A list of the 368 proteins is provided in Table S1 in supporting information.

Statistical analysis

We categorized our cohort into four disease severity stages: CU Aβ− (n = 18), CU Aβ+ (n = 17), CI Aβ+ with early tau deposition (n = 17), and CI Aβ+ with late tau deposition (n = 11). 3 We used analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey correction for multiple comparisons to explore differences between groups for continuous variables, such as age, years of education, and Aβ Centiloid. For categorical or ordinal variables, including sex and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, we applied the chi‐squared test. We performed CSF proteomics analysis comparing CU Aβ– and Aβ+ individuals, and CI individuals at early and late stages of tau pathology. We performed differential expression analysis (DEA) for the CU Aβ– versus CU Aβ+ and CI early tau versus CI late tau using a linear regression model, with protein levels as the dependent variable, group status (CU Aβ– vs. CU Aβ+ or CI early tau vs. CI late tau) as the independent variable, and age and sex as covariates. Only genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05, based on the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, were defined as differentially expressed genes and included in subsequent analyses. We then conducted functional enrichment analysis (FEA) of Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes (BP) using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.6.2). 13 , 14 We clustered the GO BP terms by semantic similarity and represented the similarity matrices as GO networks using the GOSemSim package (v2.18.1). 15 , 16 For visualization of the networks, we used the RedeR package (version 2.2.1) 17 and DiVenn (version 2.0) tool. 18 Then, we curated (by a consensus of at least two authors) the clusters of the GO BP terms based on their biological interpretation.

Aβ‐ and tau‐associated neuroinflammatory proteins

We measured 368 inflammation‐related proteins in the CSF to identify the neuroinflammatory protein signatures associated with the Aβ and tau phases of AD. In the early disease stage, the presence of Aβ was associated with the downregulation of 34 proteins and upregulation of two proteins (Figure 1A). The proteins that best differentiated CU Aβ– from Aβ+ were polypeptide N‐acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 3, iduronate 2‐sulfatase, and secretoglobin family 3A member 2 (SCGB3A2). In late disease stages, the presence of widespread tau pathology was associated with the upregulation of 23 proteins and downregulation of one protein (Figure 1B; the proteins that best differentiates CI Aβ+ individuals with early tau from late tau were thioredoxin‐dependent peroxide reductase, mitochondrial; cystosolic phospholipidase A2 (PLA2G4A); and SC3GBA2. The interleukin 4 receptor (IL4R), PLA2G4A, and SCGB3A2 proteins were those that best differentiated Aβ– from tau‐associated microglial activation, displaying a great reduction in the Aβ phase and a great increase in the tau phase. FEA was used to identify biological processes associated with the protein abnormalities found in the Aβ and tau phases. We observed 157 downregulated biological processes enriched in the Aβ phase (between Aβ– and Aβ+ individuals) and 82 upregulated biological processes enriched in the tau phase (Table S2 in supporting information) with a confidence of at least 80% (q value < 0.2). The main hubs of proteins participating in the identified biological processes of the Aβ phase were IL4R, interleukin (IL)‐18, IL‐18R1, Toll‐like receptor 2, C‐type lectin domain family 7 member A (CLEC7A), and advanced glycosylation end‐product specific receptor (Figure 1C), whereas the main hubs for the tau phase were IL‐4R, IL‐12RB1, IL‐1B, and PLA2G4A (Figure 1D).

Biological processes involved in the Aβ and tau phases of AD

Figure 2 shows the network of biological processes enriched in each phase in which we mapped the degree of connectivity for each process in the node size. In the Aβ phase, we found an overall downregulation of innate and adaptive immune functions characterized by the downregulation of several biological processes related to T cell regulation, differentiation and activation, regulation of nuclear factor kappa beta (NFκB) activity, and cytokine production (Figure 2A). On the other hand, in the tau phase, we observed an upregulation of T cell–related inflammation response, regulation of apoptotic pathways, and glial activation (Figure 2B).

Unique and shared inflammatory processes between Aβ and tau phases of AD

We found 102 biological processes uniquely enriched in the Aβ phase and 40 uniquely enriched in the tau phase (Figure 3; Table S2). Forty‐eight inflammation‐related biological processes were common to the Aβ and tau phases all of them being downregulated in the Aβ phase and upregulated in the tau phase (Figure 3; Table S2). These processes included cytokine production, T cell activation, and regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses. Biological processes related to well‐known inflammatory pathways, such as the NFκB, the Toll‐like receptor, and the c‐Jun N‐terminal kinases (JNK), a family of proteins that play a central role in stress signaling pathways, were found to be downregulated only in the Aβ phase. Processes involved in the regulation of apoptotic pathways and hormone secretion were upregulated uniquely in the tau phase (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

Integrating CSF proteomics with Aβ and tau PET staging revealed two waves of neuroinflammation in AD, an Aβ‐associated wave characterized by a broad downregulation of innate‐ and adaptive‐immune proteins, and a tau‐associated wave marked by pronounced upregulation of T cell, apoptotic, and glial‐activation pathways. Additionally, we identified three proteins that are downregulated in the Aβ‐related wave and upregulated in the tau‐related wave.

Proteomics analysis of CSF revealed that distinct neuroinflammatory signatures in response to widespread Aβ and tau pathologies were associated with broad downregulation and upregulation, respectively, of inflammation‐related proteins. Previous single‐nuclei analyses in human post mortem brain tissue have supported that Aβ and tau pathologies may be associated with distinct neuroinflammatory phenotypes. 1 , 19 In the Aβ phase, we observed downregulation of 34 inflammatory proteins and 157 GO processes in Aβ‐positive individuals, spanning Toll‐like receptor signaling, NFκB activation, and T‐cell differentiation. For instance, one protein decreased in the Aβ phase, CLEC7A, has been reported to promote microglial activation, which in turn stimulates Aβ clearance. 20 Its downregulation during this phase might be preventing Aβ plaque clearance. Another well‐reported protein that was found decreased in the Aβ phase is IL4R. IL4R response to Aβ is notable because IL‐4 is known to polarize microglia toward a phenotype that increases autophagy leading to an increased Aβ clearance and its loss may therefore tip microglia toward a hypophagocytic, dysfunctional state that permits plaque expansion. 21 , 22 Together, these data suggest that early Aβ pathology induces a broad downregulation of key receptors and signaling hubs essential for effective Aβ clearance.

In the tau phase, the presence of widespread tau pathology is marked by the rise of 23 proteins and upregulation of 82 biological processes. Biological processes related to adaptive immune responses, particularly T‐cell activation, were exclusively upregulated in the tau phase of microglial activation. Single‐nuclei transcriptomics of human AD cortex further confirms a shift from an innate to an adaptative immune response, increase CD8+ T‐cell response during tauopathy progression, indicating that tauopathy uniquely engages peripheral adaptive immunity. 23 Notably, an animal study demonstrated that mouse models exhibiting tau pathology, but not Aβ alone, developed unique adaptive immune responses. The depletion of T cells in these models prevented tau‐mediated neurodegeneration. 24 Similarly, in human post mortem AD brains CD3+ T cells correlated with tau but not Aβ pathology. 25 Based on this, it can be hypothesized that therapeutic strategies targeting adaptive neuroimmune processes are more likely to show a benefit in individuals in the tau phase of microglial activation.

Interestingly, three proteins—IL4R, PLA2G4A, and SCGB3A2—that have already been shown to be associated with AD in previous studies 26 , 27 presented the greatest contrast between phases, with marked downregulation in the Aβ phase and upregulation in the tau phase. This bidirectional behavior of these proteins can point out these proteins as potential markers to identify patients entering an Aβ‐ or tau‐centric inflammatory phase, offering an optimal time window for interventions targeting each stage of neuroinflammation. Large studies have already stratified AD into proteomic subtypes related to innate immunity and blood–brain barrier breakdown, yet the absence of imaging‐confirmed staging in those cohorts or neglecting the effect of tau spread obscured interpretation of temporal relationships. 2 Our work starts to fill that gap and may explain why pan‐anti‐inflammatory drugs show mixed efficacy. Our results argue that treatments should have specific targets and be administered to individuals at specific stages. For instance, agents that boost IL‐4 signaling or restore microglial phagocytosis might benefit individuals in the Aβ phase, whereas therapies aimed at curbing T‐cell cytotoxicity or PLA2G4A activity could be reserved for the tau phase. Such precision could enhance risk–benefit profiles and reduce trial failures.

Our study has limitations. Our results are based on cross‐sectional design; therefore, longitudinal analysis will be critical to confirm whether the signature found in Aβ phase inexorably transitions to tau‐associated adaptive immunity or whether genetic and environmental modifiers (e.g., infections, APOE ε4 status) can accelerate or arrest this switch. Due to the modest sample size of our study, large investigations that integrate deep proteomics with rigorous Aβ and tau staging are highly desired for validating the phase‐specific protein panels identified here. Finally, our inflammation‐focused 368‐analyte panel, although comprehensive for immune pathways, may miss other relevant axes of disease biology such as synaptic or vascular dysfunction. Unbiased mass‐spectrometry and multi‐omics approaches could broaden the landscape of stage‐dependent biomarkers.

Together our results revealed neuroinflammatory signatures of the Aβ and tau phases of AD. These findings highlight the need for biofluid markers that can capture stage‐specific immune alterations and serve as readily accessible endpoints for trials that target neuroinflammatory pathways. Finally, our study strongly supports that successful immunomodulation in AD will require stage‐specific strategies that respect this dynamic immunological landscape.
