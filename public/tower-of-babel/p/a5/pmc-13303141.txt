The Role of Augmented Reality in Advancing Medical Training Techniques: A Pilot RCT Study on Cricothyroidotomy
Authors: Hyunchung Cho, Jongchul Kim, Sumin Kim, Hansol Chang, Sejin Heo, Minha Kim, Se Uk Lee, Wonchul Cha, Taerim Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCricothyroidotomy is a critical procedure for managing difficult airways, yet it is infrequently performed, leading to limited familiarity among medical professionals. This study explores the effectiveness of augmented reality (AR)-based training using HoloLens compared to traditional non-head mounted device (HMD) training in teaching cricothyroidotomy. This study aims to confirm the effect of technical practice using AR on participants’ performance ability, performance confidence, learning self-efficacy, and practical satisfaction, and to present basic data for using AR in medical education.Materials and MethodsAll participants received initial guidance via PowerPoint (PPT) on laptops. The AR group performed the procedure with three-dimensional models via HoloLens, while the non-HMD group used the initial PPT guidance. Effectiveness was assessed through a pre- and post-test design, including cricothyroidotomy estimated time, skill confidence, NASA-Task Load Index, System Usability Scale, and participant satisfaction.ResultsThe average pre-test procedure time for the AR group was 188 seconds, and 150 seconds for the non-HMD group. In the post-test, the AR group recorded 146 seconds and the non-HMD group 136 seconds, showing no statistically significant difference in procedure time (p>0.05). The HMD group showed a significant improvement in confidence levels (median increase from 1.0 to 5.0), and both groups demonstrated similar proficiency in cricothyroidotomy performance over time. Satisfaction rates were similar, but the AR group reported lower mental demand.ConclusionDespite initial technical challenges, AR showed potential for enhancing learning and confidence without compromising performance, indicating the need for further research to optimize AR in medical training.Keywords: HoloLens, medical education, cricothyroidotomy, augmented reality

INTRODUCTION

Medical staff training in hospitals is essential. Medical students find it challenging to increase their medical knowledge and gain confidence with only traditional two-dimensional learning because the medical education system is extensive, diverse, and has a protracted learning cycle. Among those, critical care techniques, especially for upper airway obstruction patients, are important techniques that determine patients’ lives.1 In patients with respiratory failure, tracheal intubation techniques are frequently performed, whereas cricothyroidotomy has comparatively few actual cases, resulting in a lack of medical staff with actual experience or technique.2,3 Such infrequently performed procedures may cause difficulties when a case suddenly occurs. In the case of tracheal intubation, research indicates that between 51 and 75 experiences are required to achieve a first-attempt success rate of more than 80 percent.4 In an environment where it cannot be done frequently and it is difficult to gain experience with actual patients, such as cricothyroidotomy, there was a need for a platform where beginners could easily practice and gain experience.5 This platform was then used as the technical education platform for this research.

During recent years, medical education environments have faced multiple forms of disconnection that have limited students’ opportunities for hands-on training.6 These challenges can arise from temporal factors, such as pandemics like COVID-19 or MERS that restricted in-person instruction;7 physical factors, such as large-scale educational disruptions exemplified by national-level medical school crises;8 and human factors, including the shortage or unavailability of supervisors who can provide direct guidance during procedural training.9 As a result, the proportion of non–face-to-face education, such as online and recorded instruction, has markedly increased, and while these methods ensure educational continuity, they also highlight the absence of real-time supervision and interactive feedback that are essential for skill acquisition.10 To address these limitations and enhance hands-on learning in remote education settings, innovative technologies are being incorporated into medical curricula.11 Among these, virtual and augmented reality (AR) tools offer unique potential for replicating hands-on experiences and providing real-time feedback that traditional online education lacks.

Medical education through the metaverse, such as virtual reality (VR) and AR, can digitize reality and perform technical education in dimensions and precision beyond the limitations of existing simulator classes and online classes through four areas: augmentation, simulation, external, and intimate.12 Research on medical education through AR and VR is being actively conducted, and research results have been reported indicating that it is more effective than conventional education methods.13 Microsoft Corp.’s (Redmond, WA, USA) HoloLens2 device used for education in this study is a head mounted device (HMD), which can implement a mixed reality (MR) that combines VR and AR and has the advantage of being able to use virtual world images with the real world. Accordingly, it is expected that users can immerse themselves in training, and the benefits of these devices will help them efficiently self-learn high-level skills that require repetitive face-to-face training.14

AR enables multiple trainees to remotely observe the patient interaction as well as display and collaboratively interact with a variety of clinically relevant “holograms” such as educational figures. Eventually, the use of HoloLens and similar MR devices in medical education, specifically for teaching medical students, has already been trialed.15,16,17

The primary aim of this research is to address a significant gap in medical education, particularly in the training for cricothyroidotomy. Traditional methods of training often rely on expensive porcine models, which limit the frequency and accessibility of practical experiences for students.2 This limitation is concerning given the critical nature of cricothyroidotomy, especially in emergency situations like those encountered in emergency rooms.6 VR has been shown to improve learning, empathy, immersion, confidence, and satisfaction in difficult practical education,18,19 but more research is needed.20 However, there is a lack of research on the effect of education using AR. Thus, this study aims to confirm the effect of technical practice using AR on participants’ performance ability, performance confidence, learning self-efficacy, and practical satisfaction, and to present basic data for using AR in medical education. This innovative approach not only aims to enhance the learning experience but also to improve the preparedness of medical personnel in managing airway emergencies, ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes in critical care settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cricothyroidotomy instructions production process

The entire process of the cricothyroidotomy was filmed by medical staff in the hospital and used for making the presentation instructions. The entire procedure—from checking the Melker kit (C-TCCS-500, COOK®, Bloomington, IN, USA) equipment to finishing the procedure—was described in both the PowerPoint (PPT) and HoloLens instructions. Using the Microsoft Dynamics 365 (Microsoft Corp.) Guide, step-by-step instructions for the AR program were created using the same text and visuals as the PPT. Three-dimensional (3D) models were also presented in the AR program to help the participants find the exact spot to make the incision. The thyroid membrane model was made by our biomedical engineering team using a 3D printer. The model was produced using Autodesk Fusion 360 software and printed using 1.75 mm PLA+ 3D filament through a SnapMaker 3-in-1 printer with Fused Deposition Modeling.

Study design

We conducted a randomized, prospective mixed-method study during the period from November 2022 to December 2022 at a tertiary teaching hospital with 78000 emergency department (ED) admissions per year. Fig. 1 shows the flow diagram for the study. Using the lottery method, we randomly assigned the participants into two groups: the non-HMD group and the HMD group. The non-HMD group used a laptop presenting the procedure manual, and the HMD group used AR-based instructions through an HMD (HoloLens2, Microsoft Corp.). All participants were provided with the preparation materials (Melker kits) for the trapezius incision, and a total of five training sessions were conducted. Two weeks later, considering the washout period, a skill post-test was conducted once again to see if the educational contents of the skill were well remembered. This study was conducted using a self-created model for the procedural training test; therefore, no ethical concerns were needed.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in this study was overall estimated procedure time in each group and the survey results, which was conducted after the test. The secondary outcome was the non-parametric test conducted on the confidence survey results before and after the procedure. Specifically, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the before and after confidence scores within each group. This test was chosen due to its suitability for non-normally distributed data, which was necessary given the distribution of our scores. This test assesses whether there is a statistically significant difference between two related samples, in this case, the confidence levels measured before and after the training.

In addition, the tertiary outcome was based on the NASA-Task Load Index (TLX) survey, a well-established method for assessing subjective mental workload. It is designed to randomly assign scores between 0 and 100 to six questionnaire items and then calculate the difficulty of the overall job based on their average. A variety of models are used to get an overall workload score from a weighted average of ratings on six subscales: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration level. One of the anticipated benefits of evaluating job difficulty using this method is the ability to determine the causes of the difficulty. In other words, the purpose of evaluating job difficulty is to prevent a rapid decline in human performance through effective job difficulty management, so identifying the factors influencing job difficulty enables more effective job difficulty management.

Further outcomes included the results of the satisfaction survey administered following the procedure, two separate cricothyrotomy performance ratings assessed by experts [a 3-point task-specific checklist and global rating scale (GRS)], the usability test results for the HoloLens device, and the comprehensive survey with participants. In the satisfaction survey, all 30 participants completed the entire procedure and rated the responses to the questions on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree) and the overall results of three survey categories: learning satisfaction, cognitive load, and recommendation. The cricothyroidotomy checklist was developed in response to observations of frequent, yet crucial errors made by inexperienced operators in the study by Siu, et al.21 A stage received a score of 0, 1, or 2 depending on how well or how poorly it was performed. The GRS employs broader descriptors and emphasizes the subject’s overall performance rather than the details of the manual task. To address specific feedback that could not be covered by the previously listed outcomes, we conducted brief open-ended questions with participants who used HoloLens to gather their evaluations of the system. All survey instruments are provided in the Supplementary Material (only online).

Participants

Participants were selected based on specific eligibility criteria: they had to be physicians working in the ED or emergency medical technicians (EMTs) with no prior experience using HoloLens, available for the study duration, and willing to participate in both initial training and follow-up assessments. Convenience sampling was used due to the pilot nature of the study and the practicality of recruiting within a single medical center. Recruitment was conducted within Samsung Medical Center through internal staff bulletin boards and posters. The sample size calculation was informed by previous research and expert recommendations. Specifically, we referenced a prior study on a different medical procedure (ventilator setup) which demonstrated that a sample size of 20 participants per group (total 40 participants) was sufficient to yield statistically significant results.22 Additionally, usability expert Nielsen and Landauer23 has consistently shown that evaluating at least five participants can uncover 85% of usability problems. Taking into account the anticipated follow-up dropout rate of 30% over the 2-week course, we aimed to recruit a total of 30 participants, allocating 15 to each group. This adjustment was made to ensure that we would have an adequate number of participants completing the study to detect meaningful differences between the AR and non-HMD groups. Given that this is a pilot study, we did not use specific software for sample size calculation. Instead, we based our sample size on existing references and comparable studies, ensuring our sample was adequate for preliminary findings.

Statistics

All continuous variables are described as mean±standard deviation (SD) and median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables are described as numbers and percentages. The differences between groups are evaluated using the independent t-tests. For all statistical analyses, p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center in Seoul (IRB number: 2022-07-130) on 25 July 2022. It was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Since HoloLens 2 is not anticipated to pose any additional harm to participants beyond those encountered in normal daily life, participants were fully briefed on the use of the HoloLens 2 within the research, ensuring they were aware of the specific aspects of their participation and could give informed consent based on a comprehensive understanding of the research involvement and the technology used. Participants provided informed consent by signing an IRB-approved consent form before participating in the study. The consent form specified that participants could withdraw their consent at any time and request the destruction of their collected data. Regarding privacy, personal information collected included gender, age, clinical experience, department, and job title, all of which were anonymized using unique study numbers for analysis. As for compensation, participants who completed both sessions of the study received a total of KRW 50000 as a participation incentive.

Primary outcome: cricothyroidotomy estimated time, subgroup analysis

Between-group analysis revealed that at the pretest, the HMD group required significantly more time to complete the procedure compared with the non-HMD group (205.3±42.6 seconds vs. 160.2±35.8 seconds; p=0.004) (Fig. 2). At the posttest, however, there was no significant difference between groups (145.8±15.2 seconds vs. 135.6±25.4 seconds; p=0.419). Within-group analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in procedure time from pre- to posttest in the HMD group (p<0.001), whereas the non-HMD group did not show a significant change.

Cricothyroidotomy estimated time of each pre/post-test session. HMD, head mounted device.

In this study, two subgroup analyses were conducted in total (Figs. 3 and 4). The first subgroup was based on skill confidence recorded after performance, and the skill time was compared based on how participants rated themselves on a scale of 1 to 5. The second group was comprised of emergency medicine or anesthesia students in their senior and junior years, with the senior and junior years’ performance times compared. The performance time was analyzed by dividing it into the average time performed five times during the pre-test and the time performed 2 weeks later during the post-test; the results are depicted in Figs. 3 and 4. In the HMD group, the post-test performance time was reduced overall, including participants with a post-training confidence score of 5, whereas there was no change in the performance time of participants with 5 points in the non-HMD group.

Secondary outcome: before-after confidence non-parametric test

As a second outcome, we conducted a non-parametric test (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) on the confidence measured before and after the training (Table 2). Both the HMD and non-HMD groups demonstrated significant increases in self-reported confidence following training (Table 2). In the HMD group, the median confidence score improved from 2.0 (IQR 1.5–3.0) before training to 4.0 (IQR 4.0–5.0) after training, and this change was statistically significant (p=0.002). Similarly, participants in the non-HMD group reported an increase from a median of 2.0 (IQR 2.0–3.0) prior to training to 4.0 (IQR 4.0–4.0) after training, also indicating a statistically significant improvement (p=0.001).

Tertiary outcome: NASA-TLX

We used the NASA-TLX as the tertiary outcome, a well-known method for calculating subjective mental workload. Except for performance questions, a lower score indicates a more positive response (Table 3). Results showed that the HoloLens2 trained group experienced a moderate mental demand. The non-HMD trained group reported slightly lower mental demand. Physical demand was marginally lower for the HoloLens2 group compared to the non-HMD group. The temporal demand followed a similar pattern, with the HoloLens2 group reporting lower temporal demand than the non-HMD group. Despite being an HMD group that had never received education via HoloLens, the HMD group's frustration score was rated highly because the education guide was as well organized as that of the non-HMD group. However, due to unfamiliarity with the device, the non-HMD group was confirmed to have a higher mental demand.

Survey outcome: satisfaction survey after training

There was no significant difference in the overall rate between the two groups (Table 4). In evaluating training satisfaction and cognitive load, participants who received cricothyrotomy education with HMD reported levels of overall satisfaction that were similar to those trained without HMD, as reflected by median scores of 5 in both groups. The adequacy of information provided by the guide was rated slightly higher by the HMD group, with a median score of 5, nearing statistical significance (p=0.05), compared to the non-HMD group’s median score of 5 (IQR 4–5). The guide’s organization received positive ratings, with the HMD group providing a median rating of 5 (p=0.07). The training tools were considered satisfactory, with the HMD group showing a statistically significant preference for the muscle memory aspect of skill training (p=0.003). Both groups rated their satisfaction with learning the skills independently using the guide with a median score of 5, with the HMD group’s satisfaction achieving statistical significance (p=0.03).

Cricothyroidotomy performance results (GRS, task-specific checklist)

The proficiency in cricothyroidotomy was quantitatively assessed using two metrics: the GRS and a task-specific checklist (Table 5). The GRS scores, with a total possible score of 35, showed a progressive improvement over time in both HMD and non-HMD groups; however, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed at any time point (all p>0.05). Initially, the HMD group started with a lower median score (18, IQR 12.5–21) compared to the non-HMD group (21, IQR 15.5–23). By the fifth round, the HMD group’s median score had increased to 25 (IQR 23.5–32), while the non-HMD group scored 32 (IQR 25.5–33). At the two-week post-test, median scores were 28 (IQR 24–33) for the HMD group and 29 (IQR 23–32) for the non-HMD group, with no significant difference between groups (p=0.818).

For the task-specific checklist (total possible score of 8), both groups achieved near-perfect performance over time. The HMD group’s median score started at 7 (IQR 6.5–8) at the first round and reached a perfect score of 8 (IQR 8–8) by the fifth round, which was maintained at post-test. The non-HMD group scored higher at the first round (median 8, IQR 7–8), a difference that was statistically significant (p=0.015), and maintained a perfect median score of 8 (IQR 8–8) from the fifth round onward. By the fifth round and post-test, no significant between-group differences remained (p=0.350 and p>0.999, respectively).

These findings suggest that both AR-based (HMD) and traditional (non-HMD) training methods were effective in improving cricothyroidotomy performance over time. Although the HMD group began at a lower proficiency level on the GRS, they demonstrated a comparable learning trajectory, with no statistically significant differences from the non-HMD group at any assessment point. Similarly, both groups converged to equivalent task-specific checklist performance by the fifth round, indicating that AR-based training is non-inferior to traditional methods for teaching this procedural skill.

Participant open-ended question

Through 3D models in the HoloLens, participants were able to comprehend the location of the technique quickly and intuitively, which they considered helpful. Additionally, they stated that education can be conducted unsupervised with a single HoloLens device. Due to the nature of the device, however, initial adaptation requires some time, and the inability of the program window to be positioned within the same field of view as the technical model was cited as an inconvenience.

“It took some time to adjust at first, but it was good to be able to conduct education without any supervision with just a single HoloLens.” (Participant H01)

“It wasn’t boring because it was more realistic than the existing PPT class. In the future, I hope to add functional features such as setting a time limit to increase proficiency in actual emergency situations.” (Participant H04)

“We were provided with additional guides to the skill model as a 3D model, which made it convenient to proceed with the skill. DISCUSSION

This study established whether instructors can successfully deliver instruction while utilizing AR tools for an emergency procedure known as cricothyroidotomy. The performance times were not significantly different, and the HMD group received higher satisfaction ratings. Despite initial technical difficulties, the skill time improved over time, and when it was tested 2 weeks later, the performance was comparable to the PPT group.

We used a variety of questionnaires to evaluate this study, and the results revealed that the participants were, on average, satisfied. Research on the effectiveness of difficult practical education using VR has been conducted regarding learning, empathy, immersion, learning confidence, and satisfaction, and positive effects can be expected,18,19 but more research is still needed.20 However, there are not many studies on the effects of education using AR. Therefore, this study aims to confirm the effect of technical practice using AR on participants’ performance ability, performance confidence, learning self-efficacy, and practical satisfaction, and to present basic data for using AR in medical education, especially in the critical care area.

In this study, the NASA-TLX and a satisfaction survey including learning satisfaction and cognitive load were conducted in order to evaluate the quality of skills and investigate educational satisfaction. The HMD group demonstrated greater cognitive load and performance on NASA-TLX questions. In addition, the HoloLens-based education platform was well understood, and the successful execution of a given skill on the job was recorded as an extension of this understanding.

In conventional cricothyroidotomy training, pig-larynx cadaver models with skin overlays are commonly used; however, their high cost and limited reusability reduce training efficiency. In this study, a cost-effective 3D-printed model tailored for the procedure was developed, addressing the economic limitations associated with animal-based models. Furthermore, by integrating AR into the training environment, this approach partially alleviated the temporal and physical disconnection issues highlighted earlier—enabling learners to practice repeatedly regardless of time and location constraints. As demonstrated by the comparable performance outcomes between the two groups, AR-based simulation training provided an efficient and accessible alternative that preserves educational value while mitigating the barriers of cost, time, and physical access.

This study has some limitations. First, the study was not double-blinded. This means that the participants were aware of the intervention they were receiving, which could have influenced their behavior and responses, leading to bias. Furthermore, the simulator used in the study was different from the existing ones, which may have impacted the results. This difference in simulation could have affected the participants’ ability to transfer their skills from the simulations to using the actual pig model. In addition, there was an imbalance in group composition, as the HMD group included proportionally more EMTs compared to the non-HMD group. Given that physicians and EMTs typically differ in their baseline anatomical knowledge and prior exposure to invasive procedures, this imbalance may have influenced the initial learning curve, potentially contributing to the lower baseline performance observed in the HMD group. However, it is noteworthy that despite this initial disadvantage, the HMD group ultimately achieved a level of proficiency comparable to the physician-dominant control group. This finding suggests that AR may serve as an educational leveler, supporting learners with less prior experience to reach a comparable competence point, that, while preliminary, warrants further investigation. Finally, the participants’ average age was relatively young, and as such, they may have been more digitally accepting and proficient, particularly with the use of the HoloLens, which could have influenced the results. Therefore, while the study provides valuable insights into the use of AR in medical education, these limitations must be considered when interpreting and generalizing the results. Future studies may consider addressing these limitations to further elucidate the role of AR in medical education.

As one of the first pilot randomized controlled trials comparing AR-based HoloLens training with conventional methods for cricothyroidotomy, this study provides empirical evidence that complements prior feasibility reports and expands the understanding of AR’s educational value in emergency procedural training. By integrating quantitative and qualitative evaluations—including performance accuracy, workload assessment, and user feedback—our work offers a comprehensive perspective on both the feasibility and pedagogical impact of AR-based procedural education. The study’s limitations, including the absence of double-blinding and the unique simulation environment, underscore the necessity for more comprehensive, diverse research designs. As a pilot investigation with a small sample size, this study focused on exploring preliminary trends rather than performing complex subgroup analyses. Accordingly, reflective interpretation of the observed differences was prioritized over statistical expansion. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials should adopt stratified sampling approaches—considering profession and prior procedural experience—to address group imbalances and strengthen both internal and external validity. Our findings, while promising, emphasize that fully realizing AR’s potential in medical training requires addressing its integration challenges, understanding the varied learner needs, and conducting rigorous comparative and longitudinal studies.
