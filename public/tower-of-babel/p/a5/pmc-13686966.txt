Neuro-regulator role of H2S in astrocyte activation and its effects on neurological damage and behavior of VPA-exposed rats
Authors: Haoran Wang, Lingyuan Yang, Jingyi Hu, Yi Jiang, Yuting Zhang, Junyu Ren, Lijie Wu, Mingyang Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder increasingly associated with aberrant astrocyte activation. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) has recently emerged as a key regulator of astrocyte function, yet its role in ASD remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigated the association between H₂S modulation, ASD-like phenotypes, and astrocyte activation. In valproic acid (VPA)-exposed rats, H₂S were modulated using α-lipoic acid (LA) or aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA), a cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) inhibitor. Behavioral performance, neuronal injury, astrocyte phenotypes, H₂S levels, CBS expression and the homocysteine/cystathionine ratio were evaluated in vivo and in primary astrocytes. VPA-exposed rats exhibited reduced H₂S levels and CBS expression, an increased homocysteine/cystathionine ratio, ASD-like behavioral deficits and neuronal injury, and astrocyte activation toward a neurotoxic A1 phenotype. Elevation of H2S by LA was associated with improvements in ASD behavioral performance, reduced neuronal damage, reduced inflammatory cytokines and suppressed astrocyte activation, with changes in astrocyte marker expression towards a more A2-like, neuroprotective profile. Conversely, AOAA reversed these beneficial effects. Collectively, these findings suggested enhanced H₂S signaling is linked to improvements in ASD-like behavioral and neuropathological abnormalities, alongside modulation of astrocyte activation involving the CBS/H₂S pathway. These findings highlight the potential involvement of the CBS/H₂S axis in ASD-related pathology.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Neuronal physiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

The clinical characteristics of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a pervasive neurodevelopmental disease, include repetitive behaviors and social behavior disorders1. According to the latest report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the prevalence of ASD among 8-year-old children in the United States has reached 1 in 312. In China, the prevalence of ASD in children aged 6–10 years is approximately 1%, consistent with data from the World Health Organization3. ASD has become one of the fastest-growing neurodevelopmental disorders worldwide. However, its etiology remains unclear, recent scholars generally attribute it to disruptions in neuronal and glial development4.

Neuroglial cells, which mainly include microglia and astrocytes, play crucial roles in the central nervous system (CNS), with astrocytes being the most widely distributed5. Astrocytes are essential for various brain functions during early development and adulthood, including neurogenesis, synaptic development, synaptic transmission and plasticity, thereby influencing behavior under both physiological and pathological conditions. Upon injury or stimulation, astrocytes become reactive, typically marked by increased expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)6. Reactive astrocytes can be classified neurotoxic A1 and neuroprotective A2 phenotypes7. Evidence suggested that astrocytes were activated 0in ASD, for instance, GFAP levels in the brains and cerebrospinal fluid of children diagnosed with ASD were three times higher than those of controls8. In addition, GFAP immunostaining levels were increased in the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of VPA-exposed rat model, which served as ASD models9. Genome-wide association studies have indicated that 65% of high-confidence ASD-associated risk genes were expressed in astrocytes10. Furthermore, abnormalities in astrocyte calcium signaling could cause ASD-like behaviors in mice11. Overall, aberrant astrocyte activation may disrupt neuronal function, contributing to the characteristics of ASD.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) has been recognized as the third endogenous gas signaling molecule in cells, functioning as a neural regulator and active substance in the brain12. It impacts both physiological conditions and pathological processes throughout the body. Increasing evidence has revealed that H2S possesses anti-inflammatory and protective effects in neurological diseases13. Endogenous H2S in the CNS is primarily produced by cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) in astrocytes14. CBS catalyzes the irreversible condensation of homocysteine into cystathionine, contributing to the endogenous H2S production through the desulfuration reaction involving cysteine and homocysteine as substrates15. Notably, studies have shown that astrocytes produce H2S at a rate 7.9 times higher than that of microglia16. Inhibition of CBS can reduce H2S content in models of Down syndrome and cerebral ischemia-reperfusion17–19. Moreover, H2S can regulate astrocyte activation; recent studies found that increasing H2S levels can inhibit astrocyte activation and improve memory deficits in a hyperhomocysteine sepsis model20. Additionally, elevated H2S levels can inhibit astrocyte activation and sensory defects in a peripheral diabetic neuropathy model21. In cerebral ischemia-reperfusion models, increased H2S content plays a neuroprotective role by decreasing A1 astrocytes levels and increasing A2 astrocytes18,19. Our previous work revealed reduced H2S levels in the plasma of children with ASD and in the brains of ASD mouse models, along with decreased CBS protein levels in the brain22. However, whether H2S regulates astrocyte activation and polarization in ASD has not yet been reported.

Therefore, we used a VPA-induced ASD rat model to investigate the effects of modulating endogenous H2S content. We administrated α-lipoic acid (LA, a modulator of endogenous H2S production) and aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA, a selective inhibitor of CBS) to VPA-exposed rats. We examined changes in external behavioral characteristics, neuropathological damage, and astrocyte phenotypes. Our goal was to determine whether modulating H₂S metabolism is associated with ASD pathological changes and to explore astrocyte activation within the CBS/H2S pathway, thereby providing mechanistic insight into ASD.

Methods

Animals and treatments

Adult male and female Wistar rats (Charles River, Beijing, China) were housed in plastic cages under controlled conditions (22 ± 2°C; 50 ± 10% humidity) with a standard 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to tap water and standard chow. The rat model of ASD was induced by prenatal exposure to VPA as previously described. Briefly, VPA (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in 0.9% saline (250 mg/mL) and administrated intraperitoneally (600 mg/kg) to pregnant rats on embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5). An equivalent volume of saline was administered to the control rats. Pregnant rats were individually housed and allowed to deliver and raise their own litters. The offspring were weaned on postnatal day (PND) 21 and randomly allocated to experimental groups. Only male offspring were used in this study to minimize hormonal variability and eliminate potential sex-related confounding effects.

Lipoic acid (LA; Yabao Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., China) and aminooxyacetic acid (AOAA; CBS inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich) were freshly dissolved in 0.9% saline. For long-term intervention, LA (20 mg/kg) and AOAA (10 mg/kg) were administrated once daily via tail vein injection from PND21 to PND2723,24. The control and VPA rats received equal volume of 0.9% saline. Repeated daily administration ensured sustained pharmacological exposure.

Rats were randomly divided into five groups as follows: ① CON (control rats injected with saline); ② VPA (VPA rats injected with saline); ③ LA (VPA rats injected with 20 mg/kg LA); ④ AOAA (VPA rats injected with 10 mg/kg AOAA); ⑤ LA + AOAA (VPA rats first injected with 20 mg/kg LA followed by 10 mg/kg AOAA with a 4-hour interval between administrations).

Behavioral testing was conducted on PND28, 24 h after the final administration. All procedures followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Harbin Medical University for Medical Sciences (HMUIRB20200007). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Primary astrocytes culture and treatment

Primary mixed glial cultures were prepared from PND 3 male pups following a previous protocol. Briefly, brains were dissected in chilled PBS, cerebellum, midbrain, and cortex meninges removed, and tissue fragments gently triturated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (ExcellBio, China), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (MeilunBio, China), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, USA). The suspension was filtered, centrifuged (1600 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C), and resuspended in complete medium. Cells were resuspended in T25 culture flasks and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ incubator. The medium was replaced after 3 days and every 2 days thereafter. After 7–10 days, primary astrocytes were collected for subsequent experiments.

Primary astrocytes were divided into five groups by culture medium administration as followed: ①CON (control rats-derived astrocytes treated with vehicle); ②VPA (VPA rats-derived astrocytes with vehicle); ③LA (VPA rats-derived astrocytes with 1 mg/mL LA), ④AOAA (VPA rats-derived astrocytes treated with 1 mg/mL AOAA), ⑤LA + AOAA (VPA rats-derived astrocytes sequentially treated with 1 mg/mL LA followed by 1 mg/mL AOAA, with a 24-h interval after medium replacement)22. After 24 h of administration, the cell culture medium and cells were harvested.

RNA sequencing

The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by Shanghai Bohao Biotechnology corporation. Total RNA from primary astrocytes was extracted and stored in RNase free cryopreservation tube. The criteria for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were |log2 Fold Change | > 1 and p < 0.05 (VPA vs CON). Functional enrichment analyses were performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) (http://geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.kegg.jp) databases. GO and KEGG pathways were analyzed through DAVID, and visualization was conducted using R (ggplot package).

Behavioral testing

Behavioral testing was captured by video cameras and analyzed using the SMART (Spontaneous Motor Activity Recording and Tracking) v3.0 (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). Rats were allowed to acclimate for 5 min before experiments were initiated. Apparatus was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to preclude olfactory cues. To minimize potential carryover effects between tests, behavioral testing was conducted in the order of social play test, open field, self-grooming, and marble burying. All behavioral experiments were performed during the light cycle between 09:00 and 17:00 under blinded conditions.

Social play test

The social play test was used to assess social play behaviors. On PND 28, rats were isolated overnight and habituated (5 min) in a test cage (45 × 45 × 60 cm) filled 5 cm deep with fresh wood chips. Next, a sex- and age-matched novel rat was introduced for 15 min. The behaviors of the social play test included social exploration (sniffing any part of the body of the partner), pinning (rotating onto the dorsal surface, being pinned by another rat), pouncing (soliciting the other to play, by attempting to nose or rub the nape/neck of the partner), social evasion (upon solicitation, avoiding contact by leaping, running or turning away from the other rats), self-grooming and inaction. The behaviors were recorded and scored by blinded observers.

Open field test

Hyperactivity behaviors were analyzed using the open field test. The open field was created out of a non-transparent gray Plexiglas box with a top opening in four identical square arenas (45 × 45 × 40 cm). Rats were individually placed in the center to initiate a 10-min test, during which the total distance movement and resting time of spontaneous activity were recorded.

Self-grooming test

In the self-grooming test, rats were scored for spontaneous grooming behaviors. Rats were placed individually into a white cage (48 × 35 × 20 cm) and observed for 10 min. The cumulative time spent grooming all body regions, which included paw licking, unilateral and bilateral strokes around the nose, mouth, and face, paw movement over the head and behind the ears, body fur licking, body scratching with hind paws, tail licking, and genital cleaning was recorded.

Marble burying test

The marble burying test was used to measure restricted and repetitive behaviors (RRB). Rats were placed in a clean cage (48 × 35 × 20 cm) filled 5 cm deep with fresh wood chip bedding material. Then, an arrangement of 20 marbles in a 4 × 5 grid was placed on top of the bedding. After 30 min exploration, the number of marbles buried (i.e., covered with wood chips by more than two-thirds volume) was recorded.

MTT assay

Primary astrocytes derived from control rats were used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of LA. Primary astrocytes were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with LA at different concentrations for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The medium was then removed and 100 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to solubilize formazan precipitate. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek Synergy H1, Agilent Technologies).

H2S release assay

H2S nanoprobe DNS-Az was kindly gifted by Dr. Zhang group (Daqing Campus of Harbin Medical University). Equal volumes of LA solutions (1 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL, 0.25 mg/mL, 0.125 mg/mL, 0.0625 mg/mL, 0.0375 mg/mL) were mixed with 10 mM glutathione (GSH) (B2104115, Aladdin) for 10 min, followed by addition of H2S nanoprobes. After an additional 10 min, fluorescence intensity was detected at excitation wavelength (λex) = 340 nm, and the emission wavelength (λem) = 535 nm. H2S level was calculated based on a standard curve22.

Detection of H2S levels

Plasma (100 μL) from rats and primary astrocytes culture medium (100 μL) were mixed with 150 μL methanol (Tianli, Tianjing), vortexed, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). Supernatants (100 μL) were incubated with DNS-Az (0.4 µL, 200 mM) for 10 min and fluorescence intensity recorded as above22.

Quantitative-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampal, cortical tissues and primary astrocytes with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After reverse transcription (Bio-Rad T-100 thermal cycler), qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. and their sequences were listed in Supplementary Table 1. All transcript levels were normalized to GAPDH expression. Fold-change expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted from hippocampal and cortical tissues, and the concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal concentrations of protein were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies (CBS, Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein [GFAP], Complement Protein C3[C3], S100 calcium binding protein A10[S100A10], GADPH) and corresponding secondary antibodies (for details, see Supplementary Table 2). Protein bands were visualized by a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad Labomouseories) with an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Beyotime) and quantified with ImageJ/Fiji software. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Hippocampal and cortical tissues were homogenized in PBS with added protease inhibitor. Supernatants were collected and the concentrations of homocysteine and cystathionine were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (no. YX-080325R, YX-032519R, Sinobestbio, Co., Ltd, China).

Histopathological analysis

Preparation of brain sections

Rats were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains tissue was then isolated and sequentially dehydrated in sucrose solution, embedded in OCT, and serially sectioned into 6 μm thick sections in the coronal plane using a Leica CM1950 cryostat (Leica, Germany).

Immunofluorescence

Brain sections were washed with PBS, and then blocked with goat serum for 1 h. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (GFAP, C3, S100A10), followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h. Images (20×) were captured on a Zeiss fluorescence microscope and analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji software to quantify C3/GFAP- and S100A10/GFAP-positive cells.

Nissl staining

Brain sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and the sections were washed with PBS, followed by incubation of the brain tissues with Nissl stain solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The sections were subsequently dehydrated in gradient ethanol, immersed in xylene, and finally sealed with neutral gum. Nissl-Positive cells were imaged under a Nikon light microscope (20×) and quantified with Image J Fiji software.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain sections were incubated in anti-NeuN (1:6000, Proteintech, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody and briefly incubated in diaminobenzidine (Proteintech) for colorization. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated with absolute ethanol, and embedded in neutral gum. NeuN-positive cells were photographed under a Nikon light microscopy (20×).

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), which were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or paired Student’s t test, with Sidak’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons (comparing group CON versus VPA, group VPA versus LA, group VPA versus AOAA, group LA versus LA + AOAA) was used. The exact F and p values of one-way ANOVA were provided in Supplementary Data 1. All reported p values were two-tailed, and the statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 level.

Results

RNA-seq in primary astrocytes

RNA sequencing of primary astrocytes from VPA-exposed and control rats identified a total of 4544 DEGs (|log₂FC | > 1, p < 0.05), including 1662 up-regulated and 2882 down-regulated genes (Supplementary Data2). Notably, the expression of CBS was significantly reduced in VPA-exposed astrocytes (Fig. 1A, B). GO analysis identified 30 enriched terms with the top 10 in each category shown in Fig. 1C. Biological process (BP) terms were enriched for axon guidance, nervous system development, and modulation of chemical synaptic transmission; cellular component (CC) terms for glutamatergic synapses, neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, and axons; molecular function (MF) terms for structural constituents of protein binding, calcium ion binding, and identical protein binding. Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted pathways such as calcium signaling, cytoskeleton organization in muscle cells, axon guidance, and dilated cardiomyopathy, among the top 10 (Fig. 1D). To further characterize astrocyte states, we integrated RNA-seq data with previously reported A1- and A2-associated gene sets. The overlapping genes were visualized by heatmap analysis (Fig. 1E). Overall, genes associated with A1 astrocyte markers exhibited high expression levels in VPA-exposed astrocytes compared with control, whereas, genes related to A2 astrocyte markers were down-regulated.

Effects of LA and AOAA on H2S and CBS

The selected concentration of LA did not cause significant cytotoxicity in the primary astrocyte-derived from CON rats (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B, in the presence of GSH, LA significantly increased fluorescence intensity comparing to CON group, indicating that stable H2S release over the tested range. Compared to CON group, H₂S levels were reduced in VPA group in both astrocyte culture supernatants and rat plasma (p < 0.01), and were restored by LA intervention (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in H2S content between the VPA and AOAA groups (p > 0.05). Moreover, LA + AOAA significantly attenuated the LA-induced increase in H2S content (p < 0.05, Fig. 2C, D). Western blot analysis revealed that decreased CBS protein level in hippocampus and cortex of VPA rats (p < 0.01), upregulated after LA (p < 0.05) in both the hippocampus and cortex. AOAA alone did not differ from VPA group (p > 0.05), whereas LA + AOAA significantly decreased CBS protein level compared to the LA group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2F, H). The mRNA expression of CBS followed a similar pattern to the protein level in the hippocampus, cortex and astrocytes (Fig. 2E, G, I). To further evaluate CBS-related pathway engagement, we additionally measured the homocysteine/cystathionine ratio, which reflects CBS-dependent transsulfuration. In the hippocampus, VPA group showed a significantly increased homocysteine/cystathionine ratio compared with CON group whereas LA treatment significantly reduced this ratio relative to VPA group (Fig. 2H). Although AOAA alone did not significantly alter the ratio relative to VPA group, the LA + AOAA group showed an upward trend compared with LA group. In the cerebral cortex, a similar trend toward an increased ratio in the VPA group was observed, although the differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2K). Collectively, in VPA-exposed rats, LA treatment increased H2S and CBS content, while AOAA inhibited these LA effects.

Effects of modulating H2S on ASD-related phenotypes

In the social play test (Fig. 3A), VPA-exposed rats exhibited fewer play behaviors, such as pinning, pouncing, and social exploration, with more social evasion, self-grooming, and inaction compared to controls (p < 0.05). LA treatment significantly increased pinning, pouncing, and social exploration, while decreased social evasion, self-grooming, and inaction (p < 0.05). AOAA treatment alone increased social exploration behaviors (p = 0.0147), and when combined with LA, reversed the LA’s beneficial effects on pinning, pouncing, and social exploration, and increased social evasion and inaction (p < 0.05). In the open field test (Fig. 3B), VPA-exposed rats exhibited hyperactivity (greater distance movement and less resting times) compared to controls (p < 0.05). LA treatment normalized both measures, while AOAA inhibited these effects of LA (p < 0.05); AOAA alone did not differ from VPA. In the self-grooming and marble-burying test (Fig. 3C), VPA-exposed rats displayed more stereotypical behaviors (p < 0.05). LA treatment significantly reduced stereotypy, while, AOAA blocked these improvements from LA (p < 0.05). There was no significant improvement between the VPA and AOAA groups. We also investigate the potential effects of LA or AOAA administration on control rats. The results showed that LA, AOAA, and LA + AOAA treatments to control rats did not produce significant changes in social play behavior, locomotor activity, marble-burying behavior, or self-grooming compared with control rats (Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, these results indicated that the ASD model replicated core ASD symptoms, including social interaction deficits and repetitive behaviors. LA treatment improved ASD-like behaviors, while AOAA inhibited these effects of LA.

Effects of modulating H2S on neuronal damage

Nissl staining revealed well-organized neurons with abundant Nissl bodies in CON hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG regions and cortex (Fig. 4A). In contrast, VPA-exposed rats showed irregular, sparse neurons with disintegrated or absent Nissl bodies. LA administration rescued Nissl morphology across hippocampus and cortex. Quantification showed a significant reduction in the density of Nissl-positive cells in VPA-exposed rats (p < 0.0001), which was significantly ameliorated by LA treatment (p < 0.01) in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus and cortex. AOAA treatment further decreased the density of Nissl-positive cells in the CA1 and cortex (p < 0.001), while no significant difference in the CA3 and DG regions between the VPA and AOAA groups (p > 0.05). Moreover, AOAA counteracted the effect of LA in the CA1 and CA3 and cortex (p < 0.05).

NeuN-positive cells with intact and round nuclei were densely packed in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus and cortex in control rats, but sparsely packed in VPA-exposed rats (Fig. 4B). LA treatment rescued this neuronal damage. Significant decreases were observed in the density of NeuN-positive cells in VPA-exposed rats compared to the control rats (p < 0.05). LA treatment significantly increased the density of NeuN-positive cells in the hippocampal CA1, CA3, DG regions and cortex. AOAA treatment significantly decreased the density of NeuN-positive cells in CA1 and DG regions and the cortex (p < 0.05), with no change in CA3. Moreover, AOAA inhibited the effect of LA (p < 0.05).

Together, these results suggested that LA played neuroprotection against VPA-induced neuronal injury, whereas AOAA counteracted this protective effect.

Effects of modulating H2S on astrocyte phenotypes

GFAP and C3 protein levels were significantly higher in the VPA group compared to the control group, while S100A10 protein level was lower in both the hippocampus and cortex (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A, B). LA treatment significantly decreased GFAP and C3 protein levels and increased S100A10 level (p < 0.05). AOAA alone intervention did not affect GFAP, C3, and S100A10 protein levels. However, the LA + AOAA group showed significant upregulation of GFAP protein levels (cortex) and C3 (hippocampus and cortex) with lower S100A10 (hippocampus and cortex) compared to the LA group (p < 0.05). Consistently, the mRNA expression of GFAP, C3, and S100A10 followed similar trends in the hippocampus, cortex and primary astrocytes (Fig. 5C–E).

Discussion

The evidence gathered from this study, along with our previous research, suggested that H₂S-related signaling is associated with astrocyte activation, neuronal integrity, and ASD-like behaviors in VPA-exposed rats. Elevating H₂S levels induced by LA treatment prevented astrocyte overactivation, changes in astrocyte-associated markers toward a more neuroprotective A2-like profile, reversed neuropathological damage, and improved social deficits, repetitive behaviors, and hyperactivity. Conversely, inhibition H₂S hydrolysis by CBS offset these beneficial effects, suggesting that the CBS/H₂S pathway may contribute to protective mechanisms in ASD pathology.

RNA-sequencing and molecular validation revealed that VPA-exposed rats exhibited significantly reduced expression of H2S related synthetase - CBS. There are three key enzymes that produce H2S: CBS, Cystathionine g-lyase (CSE) and 3-mercaptopyrouvate sulfotransferase (3-MST)25. CSE is predominantly in the liver, kidneys, blood vessels, trachea, and lungs, while CBS and 3-MST are primarily located in the CNS, with CBS being preferentially expressed. Consistent with this regulatory pattern, both H₂S levels and CBS expression were diminished in the brain and primary astrocytes of VPA-exposed rats, accompanied by an increased homocysteine/cystathionine ratio, supporting the presence of disrupted H₂S metabolism in ASD-related pathogenesis.

Currently, several exogenous H2S donors have been developed, including inorganic compounds like Na2S and sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS), as well as the thio-reagent GYY4137, which release H2S in aqueous environments. However, inorganic donors release H2S rapidly, resulting in a transient burst that may cause cellular toxicity, whereas GYY4137 releases H2S more slowly but may produce toxic by-products such as carbon monoxide during hydrolysis26,27. In contrast, LA has been reported to increase H2S more gradually and to exhibit relatively low toxicity. Therefore, in this study, LA was used as a modulator of endogenous H₂S metabolism rather than a canonical H₂S donor. LA may elevate H₂S levels through two mechanisms: (1) LA, as a disulfide compound synthesized endogenously in mitochondria, can release H2S via its decomposition product, dihydrolipoic acid28. (2) LA also enhances the expression of CBS, thereby promoting endogenous H2S production. Previous reports have shown that LA can elevate CBS expression in the heart29. Our results also confirmed that LA treatment increased both mRNA and protein levels of CBS in hippocampus, cortex and primary astrocytes. Furthermore, the decreased homocysteine/cystathionine ratio observed after LA treatment in VPA-exposed rats was consistent with partial restoration of CBS-related transsulfuration. Conversely, AOAA, a CBS inhibitor, attenuated the beneficial actions of LA but did not significantly reduced H2S levels, or CBS expression at the transcript or protein level when administered alone. The minimal effect of AOAA alone may be explained by a floor effect, as CBS expression and endogenous H₂S levels and CBS expression are already markedly reduced in VPA-exposed rats. Under these conditions, further inhibition of CBS activity may produce only limited additional changes, resulting in an apparent lack of effect. Taken together, these findings support functional involvement of the CBS/H₂S pathway, although they do not exclude additional H₂S-independent or AOAA off-target mechanisms.

Notably, our findings indicated that increased H2S levels were associated with amelioration of hyperactivity, repetitive behaviors, and social deficits in ASD model rats, whereas inhibition of H2S synthesis reversed these positive effects on autism-like behaviors. Our results were consistent with previous reports that elevated H2S levels can alleviate cognitive impairment, memory deficits, and hyperactivity in other neurological models30,31. Importantly, we found single or combined administration of LA and AOAA did not significantly affect behavioral performance in control rats, suggesting that the behavioral changes observed in VPA-exposed rats were more likely related to modulation of pathological processes rather than nonspecific drug effects under normal conditions. It should be noted that locomotor activity may influence the behavioral interpretation; however, despite increased activity, VPA-exposed rats showed reduced social interaction, and the effects of LA and AOAA were not fully aligned with locomotor changes, indicating that social behavioral alterations are not solely attributable to general activity levels. With respect to neuropathological damage, our results demonstrated that VPA-exposed rats exhibited neuronal injure, characterized by disintegrated or missing Nissl bodies and decreased NeuN-positive cells in the hippocampus and cortex. Following increased H2S levels, Nissl bodies reappeared and NeuN-positive cells increased in the ASD model. In contrast, when H2S levels decreased, this neuroprotective effect disappeared. These findings were supported by prior studies that elevated H2S levels exhibited neuroprotective effects in multiple neurological disease. For instance, in the Parkinson’s mouse model, increased H2S levels reduced neuronal loss and promoted the proliferation of neural stem cells in the subventricular zone32. In an Alzheimer’s rat model, heightened H2S levels mitigated neuronal damage through antioxidant pathways30. Overall, our results affirmed the neuroprotective effects of H2S against neuropathological damage in ASD.

Our RNA-seq analysis indicated that VPA exposure induced significant astrocyte activation, characterized by increased neurotoxic A1 astrocytes related signatures and decreased neuroprotective A2 astrocytes related signatures. Consistently, our in vivo experiments further supported this pattern. Moreover, modulation of H2S levels were associated with coordinated changes in astrocyte marker profiles, with elevated H2S favoring a more A2-like profile, whereas decreased H2S levels by inhibition its synthesis reversed these effects. This aligned with previous studies on the effects of H2S in cerebral ischemia-reperfusion models by regulating astrocyte activation18,19. In addition, we further found that LA-induced elevation of H₂S was accompanied by reduced inflammatory cytokine levels (e.g., IL-1β and iNOS), which suggesting that H₂S-related regulation of astrocyte-associated changes may occur within a broader neuroinflammatory context. Although the precise mechanisms by which H₂S regulates astrocyte phenotypes in ASD require further investigation, several signaling pathways may be involved, such as RhoA/ROCK signaling, large-conductance Ca2+-dependent potassium (BKCa) channel. Furthermore, astrocyte-neuron and microglia–astrocyte interactions are crucial for brain function and disease progression33. However, we could not confirm whether H2S directly alleviated neuronal injure or indirectly exert neuroprotective effect by regulating astrocyte activation and glial crosstalk remains unclear. Thus, further investigation is needed to elucidate these mechanism.

While our study has yielded important results, we acknowledge certain limitations. First, the rats in this study are exclusively male, which may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly considering sex differences in ASD prevalence and astrocyte biology2,34. Additionally, mechanistic studies using co-culture systems or astrocyte-specific genetic manipulation will be necessary to delineate whether H₂S acts directly on neurons or primarily via glial regulation. Finally, we could not rule out the influence of elevated peripheral H2S levels due to the systemic administration employed in this study, therefore, a brain-targeting drug delivery system may offer a more effective approach.

Previous evidence has demonstrated the potential involvement of the endogenous neuromodulator H2S in neurological disorders. In the present study, LA-induced elevation of H2S levels was correlated with reduced neuronal damage and improved core autism-like behavior, accompanied by reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, suppression of astrocyte overactivation, and changes in astrocyte marker expression toward a more A2-like, potentially neuroprotective profile. The partial attenuation of these beneficial effects by AOAA highlights the CBS/H₂S axis as a key mechanistic pathway in ASD-related pathology. However, given the pleiotropic effects of LA and AOAA, additional H₂S-independent mechanisms cannot be excluded. Overall, this research suggests that modulation of endogenous H2S may represent a potential strategy for the treatment of ASD, although further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms and evaluate safety.
