Assessment of knowledge and practice of childhood vaccination among mothers of under five year children in Jimma city government health facilities, South-West Oromia, Ethiopia
Authors: Regea Dabsu, Getenet Beyene, Adane Mihret, Abel Abera Negash, Gemeda Abebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately one-fourth of the deaths that occur each year among under five year children are still caused by vaccine-preventable diseases. Increasing vaccination-uptake, lowering dropout rates, and preventing and controlling vaccine-preventable diseases are all made possible by mothers’ good knowledge and practices. To assess knowledge and practice of childhood vaccination among mothers of under five children attending pediatric departments at Jimma City goverment health facilities, Southwest Ethiopia. Using cross-sectional study and convenient sampling technique, 420 mothers attending goverment health facilities in Jimma City to seek medical care for their under-five children from August to October 2023 were included. Interviewer based questionnaires were used for knowledge and practice. Epidata 3.1 was used to enter the data, which were then analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, and bivariate logistic regression was used to identify potential variables for multivariate regression at p values less than 0.25. At P < 0.05, the association was deemed significant. In this study, 277 women (66%, 95% CI 61.7–70.6) had good immunization practices, while 296 mothers (70.5%, 95% CI 66.2–74.9) had good knowledge. Mothers with two or fewer children were twice as likely to have good practices as compared with their counterparts (AOR: 1.82; 95% CI 1.085–3.037; p = 0.023), and mothers whose husbands were farmers were less likely to have good practices (AOR: 0.38; 95% CI 0.205–0.717; p = 0.003). In this study, mothers’ vaccination practices were increased with their level of knowledge (AOR: 3.70; 95% CI 2.173–6.290; p < 0.001). The finding of this study reported that more than two third of mothers had good knowledge and while two third had good practice. However, less than one third of the mothers had inadequate knowledge while one third had poor practice. Awareness creation and improving adherence to the vaccination schedule among mothers of different socio-demographic status” is required.Keywords: Knowledge, Mothers, Practices, Under five year children, Vaccine preventable diseaseSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPD) remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children under 5 years of age in developing countries, including Africa 1,2 . In 2023, 13,100 under-five deaths occurred every day, and about 4.8 million died, an intolerably high number of largely preventable child deaths globally. Therefore, improving child survival remains a matter of urgent concern. Among the 84% of all under-five deaths that occurred in the world in the same year, Sub-Saharan Africa took 58% and South Asia took 26% of the deaths. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to be the region with the highest under-five mortality rate in the world (68 deaths per 1,000 live births), where 1 in 15 children died before reaching their fifth birthday2.

Vaccination averts over 2 million deaths each year in developing countries 3 and is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions of the twentieth century in terms of number of deaths averted per year 4. Notably, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that among children, death caused by infectious diseases is reduced by 2–3 million each year by using vaccines5. To implement this, WHO has established a framework, the Global Vaccine Action Plan that aims to have a world free of VPDs by 2020. Unfortunately, most children around the world, especially in developing countries did not reach the targeted global vaccination coverage of 90% 6,7.In addition, vaccination continues to decline in 2021, with 25 million children missing out on life-saving vaccines, more than 2 million in 2020, and 6 million in 2019 for diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis-containing vaccine8.

To prevent VPDs, various countries have introduced expanded programs on immunization for under five children. To implement this program, children are usually taken to the health facilities by their parents or caregivers to receive age-appropriate doses of antigens 9. Therefore, parents play an important role in the vaccination of their children 10, as they are the decision makers for their children11, and their level of knowledge significantly affects the vaccination of their children 12. Lack of good knowledge about childhood vaccination was believed to be the root cause for the resurgence of VPDs, whereas good knowledge about childhood vaccination was believed to reduce the incidence of VPDs 13.Some of the major factors that influence dropout of vaccination uptake include inadequate knowledge about vaccination, literacy and education level, occupation of mothers, inadequate information from health workers, and limited information among mothers14.

The African population’s perception on the usage of vaccines differs greatly throughout the continent. As previous report indicates, there is suspicion and false-information about their use in some countries, while is a good knowledge and acceptance of their advantages in others 15. However, the death rate for-under-five year-children in sub-Saharan-Africa, particularly in East Africa, is-fifteen-times greater-than that of high-income countries 16. To reduce this mortality rate, simple, affordable primary preventive strategies, like immunization can prevent or treat almost half of these deaths 17. According to the WHO reports, total childhood immunization is low in East Africa; nonetheless, 69.21% of infants between the ages of 12 and 23 months received all recommended immunizations, with notable geographical heterogeneity. And also in Ethiopia, pooled complete vaccination coverage was 58.92%, according to a comprehensive review and meta-analysis 18. In Jimma City, the practices of vaccination booklet retention rate for childhood immunization among mothers/ parents were 70.0%.Whereas vaccine coverage at 14 weeks of infants’ age was 49% for under five year children while 50% for infants under the age of one year which was low in both age groups 19.

Different factors were significantly affect mothers’ childhood knowledge and practices level. These were female gender, higher educational degree, and having a higher number of children20,23. Socio-demographic factors such as parents’ age, family income, and family size were also reported as associated factors of parents’ knowledge and practice towards children’s vaccines 6,13,21,22. Delivery places, and sources of vaccination information 23,24,lack of information on the vaccination schedule and husband disagreement and had impact on childhood vaccination25,26.

Despite the importance of mothers’ knowledge and practices toward childhood vaccination, various studies in Ethiopia showed the presence of a knowledge and practices gap across the country with varying degrees of understanding and adherence to the recommended schedules26–28. This has a potential impact on childhood vaccination coverage to prevent VPDs. For instance, the overall prevalence of full vaccination coverage among children in Ethiopia was 60% 29, and in the recent study report the national coverage of child immunization was 34.3% with a large variation in different regions, ranging from 14.8% in Afar to 83.5% in Addis Ababa 30. This report showed the existence of knowledge and practices gap to vaccinate children and prevent VPDs.

There is a scarcity of up-to-date data regarding mothers’ knowledge and practice of childhood vaccination in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the knowledge and practice of childhood vaccination among mothers of under-five children attending pediatric outpatient departments at Jimma City government health facilities in the Southwestern Oromia region of Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study period and area

The study was carried out in Jimma City, southwest of the Oromia regional state, Ethiopia. Jimma is one of the oldest cities in Ethiopia, established in 1830. The city has a total population of 239,022 of which 40,634 were under five year children and with an elevation of 1718 m and an area of 1005 km2. The city is 341 km away from the capital city, Addis Ababa. The city has 2 governmental hospitals (Jimma University Medical Center and Shanen Gibe General Hospital) and 4 health centers (Bacho Bore, Jimma, Mendara Kochi, and Kefitegna Hulet health centers). All these health facilities provided pediatrics health services for under five children. Data were collected from all these six government health facilities from August 1 to October 30, 2023.

Study design and sample size determination

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among mothers attending the two hospitals and four health centers health facilities to seek medical care for their children in Jimma City, Southwestern Ethiopia. The sample size was determined using a study conducted at Wadla Woreda, Northeast Ethiopia 27, which reported 65.1% and 55.3% for good knowledge and practice, respectively. To take the largest possible sample size, p = 55.3% for practice was used with a 5% margin of error using the formula n = (Zα/2)2 p (1-p)/d2 = (31), n = 3.842* 0.553(1–0.553)/0.0025 = 380. Therefore, the sample size became n = 380. By considering a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size determined was 420.

Study population and enrollment

The study population was all mothers of children under 5 years old attending pediatrics departments to seek medical care for their child at six government health facilities in Jimma City during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

Mothers who had come to the health facilities with their under-five-year-olds to seek medical care at the pediatrics department and were voluntary to give consent were included using convenient sampling techniques until total sample sizes were achieved. Mother who came with above five year children and not volunteer was excluded.

Data collection tools and procedures

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect the data on sociodemographic characteristics and related factors for knowledge and practices that were extracted from previously published related studies27,32,33. Twelve qualified and experienced nurses conducted in-person interviews to gather data, utilizing interviewer-administered instruments that addressed various variables based on the study’s goals.

Data quality assurance and control

The tool was translated initially from English to Amharic and Afaan Oromo (the two local languages of the respondents) and then back to English for consistency. The questionnaires were pretested on 5% of the total sample size at the Nekemte health center, and some corrections were made based upon the comments forwarded from data collectors before actual data collection. The primary investigator made frequent assessments for the completeness and consistency of the collected data daily and took measures accordingly. Cronbach’s alpha for the knowledge and practice scales were calculated and reported as follows:-value > 0.7 were often reported as acceptable, > 0.8 were good and > 0.9 were excellent for assessing if survey items reliable to measure the same concept for both knowledge and practices.

Study variables

Dependent variables: Knowledge and practices.

Independent variables: Socio-demographic profiles related information of mothers (Age, residence, education level, income, marital status, occupation, Number of Parity, Tetaus toxoid vaccination,place of delivery, means of transportation, time spent to reach vaccination site and time spent at vaccination site.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were entered into Epidata version 3.1, transported, and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. In both the knowledge and practice sections, response options included “yes” and “no/I don’t know. To categorize responses into poor and good, the cut-off points were determined based on the percentile distribution of scores as < 75% for poor and > = 75% for good based on two levels using modified bloom’s cut-off points.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. A bivariate logistic regression was carried out to determine candidate variables (at p < 0.25) for the multivariable logistic regression model. Variables that were significant at a p-value of < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were selected for multivariable logistic regression. Variables with a p-value of < 0.05 in the multivariable logistic regression were considered significant.

Results from the logistic regression were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CI. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test was used at p > 0.05 to test the fitness of the model with the p value of 0.07.To quantify the degree of strong correlation between two or more independent variables,variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated and reported based on; VIF = 1 indicates no multicollinearity,1–5 indicates tolerable multicollinearity, > = 5 indicates significant multicollinearity, and > = 10 indicates severe multicollinearity.

Discussion

This study has assessed about childhood vaccination knowledge and practices of mothers of under five years children attending Jimma city government health facilities to seek medical care for their children. According to this study’s findings, 70.5% of participants knew about childhood vaccinations (95% CI 66.200–74.900). Although there are significant knowledge gaps, the majority of mothers with under five children who participated in the knowledge assessment had a fair understanding of childhood vaccinations. This is consistent with earlier research report from Saudi Arabia’s Qassim region, which found 69.5% 34 and Nigeria, which claimed 70.0% 35.

However, present study result is greater than research from Ethiopia that found 65.1% in Wadla Woreda, North East Ethiopia 27, 55.0% in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia 26 and 52.3% in Shashemene Referral Hospital, West Arsi Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia 28 as well as research from Nigeria that found 49.1% in Sokoto 24, 44.8% in Edo State 35, 17.0% in Lagos State 36 and 54.9% in Malaysia 54.9% 37. This discrepancy can result from the study participants’ occupations, places of living or other uninvestigated factors. In the study conducted in Wadla Woreda North East Ethiopia, 60.0% of participants were from rural areas and 63.2% were housewives by occupation, 80.0% of study participants in the current study lived in urban areas and 47% were housewives by occupation35. In Shashemene Referral Hospital, West Arsi Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia 28, about 77.4% mothers involved in the study were housewife and in Nigeri Benin City, Edo State 35 among participants, 66.0% were unskilled. A study in Jordan and India found that parents’ awareness of children vaccinations was significantly influenced by their place of residence38,39.

However, the results of the current study were less than those of earlier reports that indicated 87.2% in Jedda City 20, 78.7% in Jordan 38, and 76.6% in Nigeria 40 had good knowledge ratings on childhood vaccinations. The maternal education level may be the cause of this variance in the knowledge score 41. About 20% of the participants in this study had no formal education, compared to 97.0% in a study done in Nigeria 36, Egypt42 and in Jordan 56.3% 38 who had completed primary and higher school and 94.8% in Jeddah City 38. Lack of education may result in a diminished capacity to locate, comprehend, and use health information42,43.

In the present study, about 66% of the mothers had good vaccination practices (95% CI 61.700–70.600). The practice assessment among mothers of under five children highlights generally positive engagement with vaccination schedules .This is consistent with a Debra Tabor study from Ethiopia that found that 66.4% of mothers had good childhood immunization practices 44.

The result was less than that of other studies that found 84.0% in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia 26, 80.5% in Saudi Arabia34, 87.2% in Jeddah city 20, 91.6% in Srinagar, Kashmir Valley, India 24, 90.2% in India 45, and 77.5% in Jordan 38. This variation could be due to difference in maternal education level. In Addis Ababa, 82.0% of study participants had completed primary school or higher, in Saudi Arabia, 61.8% had earned a bachelor’s degree, in Jeddah City, 94.8% had completed secondary school or higher, in India, 40.3% had completed the tenth grade, and in Srinagar Kashmir Valley, India, 61.6% had completed primary school. This is in contrast to the current study, where 29.3% had no formal education, 39.2%, 18.5%, and 13.9% had completed primary, secondary, and college, respectively. According to a study conducted in Uganda, children whose mothers had completed post-secondary education had twice the likelihood of receiving all recommended vaccinations 43. This demonstrated a relationship between appropriate childhood immunization practices and mother knowledge38,41,42.

However, compared to other research, the current study was higher than earlier study in Ethiopia 55.3% in Wadla Wored North East Ethiopia 27, 42.3% in Shashemene Referral Hospital, West Arsi Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia 28; 35.5% in Egypt 42, and 54.9% in Malaysia 37. This might be due to mother who took part in the studies in Wadla Wored 65.1% 27, Shashemane Referral Hospital, West Arsi Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia 52.3% 28; and Egypt42 56.0% had lower knowledge scores in contrast to the current study (70.5%), which may influence mothers’ immunization practices for their children44.

Regarding associated factors, logistic regression analysis revealed two factors that were linked to the likelihood of adopting childhood immunization practices among mothers. In this instance, mother who had fewer than or equal to two children had twice the likelihood of practicing proper childhood vaccinations compared to mothers who had more than three and above children. This is consistent with an East African survey that found mothers with fewer children were practicing childhood vaccination more32,47. However, studies in Jiddah city 20, Guatemala 48, and Myanmar 49 found that when the number of births rose, there was a greater likelihood of contact with health facilities and an improvement in practices. This variation might be due to lack of attention of mothers in different settings. In addition to this, fathers’ occupation was also found to be another related factor. Compared to their counterparts, mothers whose husbands worked as farmers had a lower likelihood of having good childhood vaccination practices. This is consistent with a study conducted in Uganda that found parents who worked in agriculture had less vaccination practices for their children43. And in another study also mothers whose husband have extremely high paying jobs are more likely to have good vaccination practices than those who have very low-paying jobs, such as those who are unemployed or unskilled 50. This might be because of father offer vital support through decision making, males roles in childhood vaccination are just as important as mother roles 51.

The results of the current study demonstrated a substantial positive association between the participants’ knowledge and practice. Good children immunization practices were four times more common among mothers with good knowledge. This is consistent with earlier research carried out in Jordan 38, Damietta Governorate, Egypt 42, and the Vettathoor Panchayath in Malappuram district, India 46. There were significant association between respondents’ knowledge and practice were also noted in Ethiopia 44 and Nigeria 36 as well.

Strength and limitation of the study

This study has its own strength since it is based on primary data and also included six health institutions, thus, can be used as base-line data for further intervention programs. The study has some limitation; first most of the mothers participated in this study were from urban areas and difficult to generalize to the general population, second since the data on the independent variables were based on self-reports by mothers, this might be associated with recall bias as well as over report, third, because the study design was cross sectional, the results likely indicated association but not causality and fourthly, the study was facilities based study therefore could not representative of community level study and also using non-probability method can affect the result generalizablilty to the general population..

Conclusion

In this study, the majority of research participants had good knowledge and practice. There was a substantial correlation between mother knowledge level and practices regarding childhood vaccinations in the study area. The number of births of mothers and the occupation of the father were correlated to mothers’ childhood vaccination practice. Improving childhood vaccination schedule adherence among mothers of different number of births status and husband with different occupation is required in the study area.
