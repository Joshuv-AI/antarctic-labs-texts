Machine learning model for multi-factor risk prediction in airport construction under non-stop operations
Authors: Xian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAirport construction under non-stop operations presents unique safety challenges due to complex multi-factor interactions that traditional qualitative methods cannot adequately address. To address this, a study was conducted on 412 construction events (comprising 103 risk incidents and 309 routine events) at a major international hub airport between 2019 and 2024. First, a risk factor system encompassing six key categories, including personnel, environment, equipment, management, facilities, and operations, was developed, represented by 42 indicator variables. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied. Subsequently, the XGBoost classifier was trained, achieving an accuracy of 92.7%, an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.875, and a recall rate of 85.7%. For model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were utilized to quantify feature contributions and elucidate the risk transmission mechanism. Five core risk factors were identified: flight density, visibility, the timeliness of NOTAM release, peak hours, and the experience of construction personnel. Key thresholds were determined: flight density of 35 flights per hour, visibility of 3 km, and a 2-h delay in NOTAM release. SHAP analysis evaluated the synergy of operational pressure variables. These findings provide a foundation for integration into operational risk warning systems, supporting differentiated risk management and offering a data-driven approach to balancing efficiency with construction safety.Keywords: Airport construction, Non-stop operations, Machine learning, Risk prediction, XGBoost, SHAP analysis

Introduction

The global expansion of the aviation industry has intensified the urgency of airport infrastructure development. For major hub airports, full operational shutdowns entail substantial economic losses, making construction under live operations the necessary default mode. The construction process interacts closely with flight operations, raising safety concerns related to altered flight paths and potential intrusions into operational airspace, even when construction is confined within the airport perimeter. In response to these risks, the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration mandates “systematic risk assessment, along with automated protection means”1,2. Safety management under the dual pressure of 'construction and operation’ faces a dilemma between efficiency and safety: overly conservative measures restrict the airport’s throughput capacity, while aggressive plans may lead to accidents. Given this inherent trade-off, the International Civil Aviation Organization has identified construction without airport closure as a key risk source within the airport safety management framework, requiring the establishment of a data-driven dynamic assessment mechanism3,4. However, traditional qualitative methods such as expert scoring and checklists are insufficient to meet the needs of refined management when dealing with multi-factor interactions, non-linear relationships, and real-time changes5. Risk assessment methodologies in safety engineering have evolved significantly with the adoption of data-driven approaches. Dizdar and Koçar developed artificial neural network-based frameworks for occupational safety risk evaluation, demonstrating the feasibility of machine learning in identifying accident patterns from historical data6,7. Multi-criteria decision-making methods such as MULTIMOORA have also been applied to systematic safety assessment, thereby providing frameworks for integrating multiple risk dimensions2. Similar MCDM frameworks have been employed to evaluate complex aviation system alternatives, reflecting the increasing interdependencies in airport operations and infrastructure8. These methodological advances, though developed in different industrial contexts, establish theoretical foundations for applying similar approaches to airport construction safety scenarios.

Literature review

Human factors in aviation and construction safety

Early approaches to airport safety risk assessment relied on human expertise and qualitative methods, structuring risk analysis around human, environmental, and organizational factors using frameworks such as the SHEL(L) model and HFACS9. Such frameworks have been further applied to evaluate human factors in aviation maintenance operations, demonstrating their versatility across different aviation contexts10. Studies on the causes of aviation accidents show that over 80% of accidents involve complex coupling of multiple factors11, and this interaction is even more pronounced in live-construction settings, where operational stress, environmental conditions, and personnel fatigue interact dynamically. Personnel-related variables—construction worker experience, air traffic controller workload, and communication efficiency—constitute a critical risk dimension. Recent research integrating AHP-based human factors analysis with real-time physiological monitoring in aviation systems further highlights the critical role of operator workload assessment in operational safety12, as do studies on adaptive pilot monitoring and physiological state identification during real flights13,14, yet such human-factors considerations have been insufficiently explored in airport construction contexts.

Aviation operations and risk assessment methods

The evolution of risk assessment progressed with the introduction of statistical analysis. Chen et al. applied an advanced entropy grey relational analysis method to identify the causative factors of aviation accidents15, and Boyd analyzed flight decision-making behavior under extreme weather conditions16. However, there was insufficient exploration of the multi-factor coupling mechanism. More recently, machine learning has emerged as a third-generation approach, as evidenced by the systematic review of artificial intelligence applications in aviation safety by Demir et al.17. Complementary NLP-based analyses of aviation safety reports further illustrate how data-driven methods are increasingly applied to identify interconnected operational, human, and organizational risk factors18. Extreme weather events caused by global climate change exacerbate environmental uncertainty; Akay & Tarhan found an upward trend in climate-related aviation accidents19, and Kwasiborska et al. confirmed that reduced visibility significantly affects the operational safety of instrument landing systems20.

Airport infrastructure and technological approaches

In the domain of airport construction, Al-Ghzawi & El-Rayes applied machine learning to predict the impact of airport expansion construction on air traffic operations21; Gondia et al. developed a dynamic risk model for construction sites22; and Zhang et al. realized real-time safety assessment of airport ground operations based on spatiotemporal graph convolutional networks23. Meanwhile, patent-based analyses of airport security technologies and studies on renewable energy integration at airports illustrate the increasing technological complexity of airport systems, within which construction activities must be safely coordinated24,25. These studies demonstrate the applicability of data-driven methods in airport operations, yet none have developed an integrated framework combining aviation-specific operational factors with ground construction risk prediction using interpretable ensemble methods.

Complex nonlinear interactions exist among operational stress factors (flight density, peak hours), environmental factors (weather, visibility), and management factors (NOTAM delays, personnel fatigue). Together, these elements constitute a high-dimensional, dynamic risk network that defies simple linear analysis. In such a complex, non-stationary risk environment, conventional statistical models, which often rely on linear and independent assumptions, fail to capture critical interaction effects and threshold behaviors among risk factors. In contrast, machine learning methods, particularly ensemble techniques based on decision trees, excel at processing high-dimensional feature spaces and uncovering the complex, non-linear structures inherent in this data26,27. SMOTE can mitigate the common problem of class imbalance that occurs especially within a cybersecurity context28,29, while SHAP value analysis provides interpretability for black box modeling techniques30,31.

This study is based on actual operational data from 412 construction events at a major international hub airport in eastern China (2019–2024). From this data, a risk factor framework comprising 42 characteristic variables was constructed. These variables are categorized into six domains: personnel, environment, equipment, management, facilities, and operational risk. Furthermore, critical thresholds for key parameters were identified and defined, including a flight density of 35 flights per hour, visibility below 3 km, and a NOTAM release delay exceeding 2 h. Based on comparing five typical algorithms, XGBoost is determined as the optimal model (accuracy of 92.7% and AUC of 0.875). Both oversampling by SMOTE and changing class weights have solved the issue of class imbalance successfully. The resultant model achieved an accuracy above 83% across all testing scenarios. Bootstrap validation provided a more precise estimate, yielding a 95% confidence interval for accuracy of [88.6%, 94.7%]. The model provides a foundation for developing a risk warning system, and it is capable of optimizing construction plans, dynamic resource allocation, and human–machine collaborative decision-making, thus offering theoretical support and practical methods for enhancing the scientific rigor and precision of airport safety management.

Research gap

Despite growing research on aviation safety and construction risk management, three critical gaps remain. First, aviation safety research predominantly focuses on in-flight operations and accident investigation, while ground construction under live operations remains underexplored; recent systematic reviews confirm that the vast majority of AI/ML studies in aviation address flight-phase risks and pilot behavior rather than ground construction scenarios17. Second, construction safety research lacks incorporation of aviation-specific operational variables such as flight density, NOTAM communication delays, and air traffic control workload. Third, there are few cross-disciplinary studies that systematically integrate multi-dimensional factors—personnel, environment, equipment, management, facilities, and operations—within a single interpretable predictive framework for airport construction.

Research objectives

To address these gaps, this study pursues the following objectives: (1) Develop a multi-dimensional risk factor system integrating aviation-specific operational variables with conventional construction risk indicators; (2) Construct and validate an interpretable machine learning model for predicting risk events during airport construction under non-stop operations; (3) Identify critical risk thresholds and elucidate nonlinear interactions among risk factors using SHAP analysis; (4) Provide a data-driven framework for differentiated risk management that balances operational efficiency with construction safety.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study focuses on a large hub airport in eastern China that serves 45 million passengers annually, possesses three runways, operates on a 24/7 schedule, and accommodates an average of 1,200 flights daily. Situated in a subtropical monsoon region, the airport experiences approximately 45 days of low-visibility conditions, 15–20 days of typhoon effects, and approximately 70 concurrent construction projects each year. A total of 412 construction events spanning January 2019 through December 2024 were included in this study. Although multiple projects operated concurrently at any given time, each project was treated as an independent observation, as individual projects were managed by separate construction teams, governed by distinct safety plans, and subject to independent NOTAM notifications. Temporal overlap was accounted for through operational context variables such as flight density and peak-hour indicators, which capture the shared pressures that co-occurring projects experience. Residual correlation from unmeasured shared conditions among concurrent projects cannot be entirely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation of this single-site design. To ensure comprehensiveness, data were integrated from five primary channels: (a) official aviation safety reporting systems, (b) the airport’s operational database, (c) internal construction project records, (d) meteorological observations, and (e) NOTAM issuance logs.

Risk events in this study were defined as construction-related incidents that resulted in any of the following outcomes: (1) documented safety violations requiring corrective action, (2) near-miss events recorded in the airport safety management system, (3) operational disruptions affecting flight schedules, or (4) incidents requiring formal reporting to the Civil Aviation Administration of China. Events were classified as binary outcomes (risk vs. normal) at the project level, with classification performed by certified safety officers based on standardized incident reporting protocols. A project was labeled as a risk event if one or more qualifying incidents occurred during its execution; the number of incidents per project was not separately modeled in the current binary classification framework. The binary outcome was chosen because the limited number of risk events (n = 103) would not support reliable estimation of count-based or ordinal models across 42 predictor variables, and because the primary operational objective is to determine whether a given project is likely to trigger any safety incident, aligning with the go or no-go decision logic used in construction scheduling practice. Among the 412 construction events, 103 were classified as risk events (risk event incidence rate of 25.0%), and the remaining 309 as normal operation events. There were also different types of constructions, namely runway maintenance (216, 52.4%), taxiway renovation (128, 31.1%), and apron repairs (68, 16.5%). Construction events occurred during three phases, namely before and during COVID-19 (2019, 68 events and events from 2020–2022, 187 events, respectively), and after COVID-19 (2023–2024, 157 events). There are 42 feature variables, and they are divided into six broad groups of risk factors, namely personnel (8 variables), environment (9 variables), equipment (6 variables), management (7 variables), facilities (7 variables), and operations (5 variables). All of these have gone through rigorous quality control, and they have all achieved a high rate of completion of 98.5%.

Risk factor identification and characterization

Following the ICAO Safety Management Manual and the 'Regulations on Airport Construction Management without Stopping Operations’ issued by the Civil Aviation Administration of China, a risk factor system encompassing six dimensions and 42 characteristic variables was developed. The personnel dimension encompasses 8 variables, such as construction personnel qualification, crew workload, air traffic controller fatigue, and inter-team communication efficiency. The environmental dimension (9 variables) covers meteorological conditions (e.g., visibility, wind speed, precipitation, temperature), lighting, and seasonal factors. The equipment dimension consists of 6 variables, including the type, number, and condition of construction and aircraft equipment. Finally, the management dimension (7 variables) addresses the rationality of construction plans, timeliness and accuracy of NOTAMs, emergency plan completeness, and safety training scope. The facilities dimension comprises 7 variables, which pertain to: runway and taxiway availability, temporary closure areas, temporary signage, and obstacle management. The operations dimension consists of 5 variables, including: flight density, peak-hour flight density, and ground operations complexity.

The raw data undergoes systematic feature engineering to meet the requirements of machine learning modeling. During the data cleaning phase, box plots and Z-scores are used to identify outliers, which are then processed based on the business scenario. For missing values (missing rate < 5%), median imputation is used for continuous variables, and mode imputation is used for categorical variables. For feature standardization, continuous numerical variables are standardized using Z-score normalization to ensure comparability of features of different dimensions. Categorical variables are converted to binary vectors using one-hot encoding; for example, construction type (runway/taxiway/apron) is encoded as 3 binary features, and flight time (peak/off-peak) is encoded as 2 binary features. To address the 25.0% class imbalance problem (103 risk events vs. 309 normal operations), a strategy combining SMOTE oversampling28 and class weight adjustment is used to ensure the model’s sensitivity to risk events. All preprocessing steps learn parameters only on the training set and apply them to the test set to avoid data leakage. SMOTE oversampling was applied exclusively to the training portion of each cross-validation fold and the final training set, ensuring complete separation from validation and test data to prevent information leakage.

Machine learning model construction

This study developed a complete machine learning risk prediction pipeline (Fig. 1), spanning data collection through model application. In the model construction phase, five mainstream machine learning algorithms were selected to comprehensively evaluate their performance in the task of predicting risks associated with airport construction without interrupting operations. Random Forest (RF)32, as a classic ensemble learning method, constructs multiple decision trees and uses voting to make decisions, exhibiting strong feature selection capabilities and robustness to outliers. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)26, built on a gradient boost model, demonstrates strong performance on medium-sized structured data sets through optimal regularization and pruning, and can also identify feature importance. In contrast, Support Vector Machine (SVM), utilizing its kernel function, supports non-linear classification, and has strong generalizability on small sample sets. Deep Neural Networks (DNN) utilize deep, nonlinear architectures for automated feature learning. The selected model suite covers major machine learning paradigms, including traditional classifiers, ensemble methods, and deep learning, enabling a comprehensive performance comparison. The 80/20 train-test split ratio was selected to balance sufficient training data with robust evaluation, following established conventions in machine learning literature. The class weight ratio of 3:1 was determined through preliminary experiments to optimize the trade-off between recall and precision for the minority class. Ten-fold stratified cross-validation was employed to ensure stable performance estimates while maintaining class distribution across folds. The modelling approach assumes that historical patterns of risk occurrence remain representative of future conditions and that the 42 feature variables adequately capture the primary risk drivers. Measurement uncertainty arises from several sources, including subjective assessments of personnel experience levels, instrument precision in meteorological measurements, and temporal resolution of operational records.

Framework of airport construction risk prediction modeling process (without stopping operations).

The dataset was split into a training dataset (330 instances, 82 risk events, and 248 normal operations) and a testing dataset (82 instances, 21 risk events, and 61 normal operations) based on an 80/20 stratified sampling ratio, where the percentage of risk events was kept close to 25.0% in each set. Ten-fold stratified cross-validation was applied for hyperparameter tuning and model selection on the training dataset, keeping a stable percentage of risk events (approximately 25%) for each fold. Grid search and Bayesian optimization were applied as a combined search strategy to find the optimal hyperparameters for each model. The class imbalance was mitigated by applying both SMOTE oversampling28 and a class weight adjustment (with a 3:1 weight assigned to risk events in the loss function), thereby making sure that the model was not insensitive towards risk events. The trained model is applied on an independent testing dataset. The model’s performance was evaluated on an independent test set using standard metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC-ROC). Interpretability of model was improved through feature importance and SHAP value analysis.

Model evaluation metrics

In this study, a multi-dimensional evaluation system is adopted to fully evaluate the prediction capability of the machine learning model, ensuring that it has sufficient accuracy, stability, and interpretability for practical applications. In classification capability assessment, accuracy, precision, and recall based on independent test sets and cross-validation outcomes are used as criteria. Accuracy can be used to evaluate prediction capability, and precision can be used to evaluate risk warning reliability, while recall can be used to evaluate ability to identify actual risk events. F1 measures, as a harmonic average of precision and recall, can effectively reflect trade-offs between them. Confusion matrix analysis quantifies a model’s predictive performance through four fundamental outcomes: True Positives (TP), False Positives (FP), True Negatives (TN), and False Negatives (FN).

Specificity can be used as a metric to evaluate the ability of model prediction on normally operating samples. The graph of AUC-ROC can effectively display sensitivity and selectivity of model prediction capability through a two-dimensional relationship between True Positive Ratio (TPR) and False Positive Ratio (FPR), and its value closer to ‘1’ means that model has better discrimination capability.

To test its stability and ability to generalize, this research used tenfold stratified cross-validation on the training dataset, computing the average and standard deviation of each of these metrics. To more effectively test its flexibility and ability to generalize into realistic scenarios, this research developed a comprehensive scenario validation plan, such as time of day (day vs. night airport construction), stratified weather conditions (excellent, mediocre, severe), construction type (runway maintenance, taxiway renovation, and apron repair), and realistic case studies. The interpretability of the model was analyzed through feature importance ranking and SHAP value analysis30. Reference was made to XGBoost’s gain index and marginal contribution calculation, aiding in determining the contribution of each feature towards model prediction and unravelling feature effect directionality and non-linearities. To test model robustness, the Bootstrap resampling method33 (1000 times) was used to calculate the 95% confidence intervals of each index, and sensitivity analysis was used for model sensitivity towards changes in sample size.

Results

The sum of risk events of all factors is larger than the sum of actual risk events (616 vs. 103). The findings indicate that one risk event may have combined effects of several different risk factors, and this phenomenon has great significance for interaction analysis of multi-factors in future studies.

Risk factor analysis

To determine the essential risk factors and examine the correlation between each feature, this study applied univariate chi-square tests () , to assess the statistical association between each feature and risk occurrence, followed by univariate logistic regression for determining odds ratio (OR) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). While multiple testing correction was not applied due to the exploratory nature of this analysis and the subsequent validation through SHAP values, the consistency of findings across statistical and machine learning methods strengthens the reliability of identified risk factors. Applying a Bonferroni or similar correction would be overly conservative given the correlated nature of the 42 features, and the chi-square tests served as a preliminary screening step whose results were independently corroborated by feature importance ranking and SHAP value analysis rather than used as the sole basis for causal inference. Pearson correlation coefficient and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) tests have been applied to these features for multicollinearity tests. In univariate chi-square tests, it was observed that 17 feature variables significantly affected risk events ( < 42, p < 0.05). Operational variables, flight density (χ2 = 38.6, p < 0.001), had the most prominent effect on risk events, and when flight density was above 35 flights/hour, risk events occurrence rate increased from an average of 25.0% to 41.8%. Environment variables, visibility ( = 35.4, p < 0.001), and precipitation intensity ( = 28.9, p < 0.001), were significantly associated with risk events. Of all managerial variables, timeliness of NOTAM notification ( = 32.7, p < 0.001), proved to be most significantly influencing risk events. If the release was delayed by more than 2 h, then the risk incidence rate is as high as 47.1%, which is 2.3 times that of on-time releases. Among these personnel-related factors, years of experience of construction workers were significantly associated with a reduced risk ( = 26.3, p < 0.001). The risk incidence was 38.2% when workers had fewer than three years of experience, but dropped sharply to 15.7% (a 58.9% relative reduction from the low-experience group rate) for those with over eight years of experience. Regarding air traffic controller (ATC) workload, risk incidence was significantly higher when controllers performed more than four hours of continuous, non-rotated work (33.6%) compared to regular duty patterns (20.3%).

Multivariate correlation analysis showed complex interactions of risk factors. As indicated in Fig. 2, flight density and peak-hour characteristics were strongly positively correlated (r = 0.76, p < 0.001), suggesting that these two variables co-occur and act together on most instances. In contrast, as expected, there was a significant negative correlation between visibility and precipitation (r = -0.68, p < 0.001). A moderate positive correlation existed between the timeliness of NOTAMs and the rationality of construction plans (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), indicating a synergistic effect among management factors. The variance inflation factor (VIF) test results showed that the VIF values for all features were less than 4.5, with a maximum of 4.32 (flight density and peak-hour), indicating the absence of severe multicollinearity and meeting the prerequisites for machine learning modeling.

Heatmap of the correlation of key risk factors (Top 15 features).

Based on logistic regression-based univariate risk assessment, this study identified thresholds and odds ratios for 15 core risk factors. When flight density exceeded 35 flights/hour, the adjusted odds ratio (OR) for risk occurrence was 3.76 (95% CI: 2.28–6.21, p < 0.001); the OR was 3.18 (95% CI: 1.94–5.21, p < 0.001) when visibility was less than 3 km; and the OR reached 4.28 (95% CI: 2.56–7.15, p < 0.001) when the delay in NOTAM release exceeded 2 h. The OR for construction during peak hours was 2.64 (95% CI: 1.68–4.15, p < 0.001), and the OR for nighttime construction was 1.87 (95% CI: 1.23–2.84, p = 0.003). These quantified risk thresholds provide a scientific basis for subsequent early warning system development and construction management decisions.

Its overall performance was the highest across all metrics, confirming its suitability for processing medium-sized structured data. Compared with the suboptimal model, XGBoost had an accuracy of 4.9% and 6.1% more than Stacking and Random Forest, respectively (92.7% vs 87.8% and 92.7% vs 86.6%). These represent substantial improvements for risk prediction and translate into fewer misjudgments per 100 construction events, thereby reducing associated safety costs and incident risk.

By comparison, all of Stacking (purple), Random Forest (blue), DNN (green), and SVM (cyan) have a significantly lower true positive rate than XGBoost at each false positive rate threshold, meaning that XGBoost is far more optimal than other methods concerning sensitivity and specificity for risk detection.

Optimal model performance

Based on comprehensive performance analysis, the XGBoost model was selected as the best prediction model and carried out detailed analysis of its performance on an independent testing dataset (n = 82). As shown in Table 3, this model correctly predicted 76 samples (18 + 58) on the testing dataset, achieving an accuracy of 92.7%. Among the 21 actual risk events, 18 were correctly predicted, yielding a recall rate of 85.7%. The remaining 3 were misclassified as normal operations, constituting false negatives (FN). This high recall rate indicates that the system effectively identifies the majority of true risks, maintaining a low false negative rate of 14.3%. Of the 61 normal operation instances, 58 were correctly identified, yielding a specificity of 95.1% with only 3 false positives (FP). This high specificity is crucial in practice, as it minimizes false alarms and prevents unnecessary operational interventions, thereby reducing the risk of alert fatigue among decision-makers.

The precision rate is 85.7% with 18/21 correct, meaning that if the prediction output indicates the existence of risks, there is more than an 85% rate that the risks do indeed exist. Furthermore, the negative predictive value reaches 95.1% (58/61), indicating that when the model predicts normal operation, there is a greater than 95% probability that the project is indeed safe, providing reliable support for construction scheduling decisions. The F1 value is 85.7%, achieving an appropriate trade-off between precision and recall. This balance is critical for the present risk prediction problem, as the consequence of a false negative (failing to identify a real risk) is significantly more severe than that of a false positive. An undetected risk may lead to safety incidents, whereas a false alarm primarily incurs additional management overhead. The false negative rate is just 14.3% (3/21), with the false positive rate of 4.9% (3/61). Although it maintains the rate of false alarms, the rate of missed alarms is minimized, resulting in superior prediction capabilities with symmetry (FN = FP = 3), thereby witnessing the applicability of the model in the real world.

A sensitivity analysis of the classification threshold shows that the model maintains stable overall performance at the default threshold of 0.5. As expected, lowering the threshold improves recall, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to identify potential risks, at the expense of a higher false positive rate. Conversely, raising the threshold improves precision and reduces false alarms, but leads to a decline in recall. This threshold trade-off provides airports with flexible adjustment options for different risk appetites: hub airports with low risk tolerance can choose a lower threshold to maximize risk capture capabilities; small and medium-sized airports with frequent construction and limited management resources can choose a higher threshold to reduce early warning interference and optimize resource allocation.

Feature importance analysis

The result of the feature importance analysis of the XGBoost model (flight density 12.1%, visibility 10.9%, NOTAMs 9.9%) shows strong consistency with the result of the analysis conducted in Section "Risk factor analysis", with χ2 values of 38.6, 35.4, and 32.7, respectively. As indicated in Fig. 4, the cumulative importance of the top 15 key variables reaches 88.7%, implying that risk prediction is dominantly dependent on these key variables. Flight density is the most important predictor, with an importance score of 0.121 (12.1%). This finding is consistent with the univariate analysis in Section "Risk factor analysis", which identified flight density as having the strongest significant association with risk = 38.6, p < 0.001. The second and third most important variables are visibility (0.109, 10.9%) and the timeliness of disseminated NOTAMs (0.099, 9.9%), respectively. Together, these three variables form the core set of predictors for risk. Additionally, factors related to peak activity hours (0.085, 8.5%) and construction workers’ familiarity with risks (0.075, 7.5%) also demonstrate considerable importance.

Feature importance ranking chart (Top 15 features). Note Top 15 features contribute 88.7% cumulatively; values are relative importance scores based on Gain.

Environmental factors represent the second highest priority category in the risk assessment. Among these, visibility, wind speed, and precipitation rank 2nd, 7th, and 9th respectively, collectively accounting for 20.7% of the total risk weight. This underscores the significant impact of weather conditions on risks associated with continuous construction work. Among the facility variables, the variables of available runway length and temporary closure area locations demonstrated the highest marginal contributions, ranking 6th (6.3%) and 8th (4.9%) respectively. Their combined contribution accounted for 11.2% of the total. Within the management category, NOTAM timeliness (ranked 3rd) and the availability of emergency plans (ranked 10th) were also identified as critical factors. Together, they contributed 13.9% of the total risk weighting, highlighting the importance of information communication and emergency preparedness in the overall risk profile.

SHAP value analysis further revealed nonlinear relationships between individual features and model predictions. As shown in Fig. 5, flight density exhibits a significant threshold effect: when the density is below 25 flights/hour, the SHAP value is close to 0; when the density is between 25 and 35 flights/hour, the SHAP value gradually increases; when the density exceeds 35 flights/hour, the SHAP value increases sharply (average + 0.52), indicating that high-density operations are a strong signal of risk. Visibility exhibits a negative effect: low visibility (< 3 km) significantly increases risk (SHAP value average + 0.48), while high visibility (> 5 km) suppresses risk (SHAP value average − 0.35).

SHAP value summary diagram (bee colony diagram, Top 15 features). Note Positive SHAP values increase risk; Negative values decrease risk. Color indicates feature value magnitude.

SHAP value analysis revealed that delays in NOTAM release exert a critical effect on information communication. The average SHAP value surged to + 0.61 once delays exceeded two hours, marking a clear risk threshold. Moreover, this delay effect was found to be amplified during peak hours, demonstrating synergy with high flight density. When both variables had high values, the sum of the SHAP values far exceeded the sum of the SHAP values individually, clearly showing positive interaction between operational stress variables. Construction personnel experience had a protective effect, wherein having personnel with more than 8 years of experience made the SHAP value negative, hence decreasing the possibility of risk prediction with an average value of − 0.37.

Model validation

To quantitatively evaluate the model’s performance, this study systematically validated the XGBoost model under various conditions. The temporal analysis revealed high predictive accuracy across different periods. Specifically, on the daytime construction dataset (06:00–22:00, n = 48), the model achieved an accuracy of 93.5%, an AUC of 0.891, and a recall of 91.7%. In contrast, its performance on the nighttime dataset (22:00–06:00, n = 34) was lower, with corresponding values of 89.2%, 0.851, and 81.8%. This moderate decline is likely attributable to the more complex conditions inherent to nighttime construction, such as limited lighting and increased worker fatigue. However, the accuracy rate of 89.2% is still much higher than the conventional method, with the false-negative rate maintained below 18.2% to ensure the identification of crucial risks effectively.

The stratified validation of weather conditions also affirmed the adaptability of the model to different weather conditions. Model accuracy exhibited a clear negative correlation with deteriorating weather conditions. Specifically, accuracy was highest (95.2%; AUC = 0.903) under good weather conditions, defined as visibility > 5 km, no precipitation, and wind speed < 5 m/s. It decreased to 85.7% (AUC = 0.862) under moderate conditions (3 km < visibility ≤ 5 km, or light precipitation, or 5 ≤ wind speed < 8 m/s), and reached its lowest point of 83.3% (AUC = 0.823) under severe conditions (visibility ≤ 3 km, or moderate to heavy precipitation, or wind speed ≥ 8 m/s). In particular, even under severe weather conditions, the recall rate of the model for high-risk events was 83.3%, which indicated that the model was able to effectively provide warnings even under severe weather conditions.

Validation based on construction type differentiation reveals the model’s cross-scenario generalization ability. The prediction accuracies for runway maintenance, taxiway renovation, and apron repair construction were 92.3%, 90.0%, and 90.0%, respectively, indicating stable performance across various operational scenarios. In backtracking validation using real-world cases, the model successfully issued warnings for 7 out of 8 major risk events in the test set, achieving a warning accuracy of 87.5%. The events for which warnings were not issued involved sudden hydraulic system failures of construction equipment. Such rare events have a small sample size in the training data, and the model’s ability to identify these types of emergencies needs improvement.

The model’s robustness was further assessed using the bootstrap resampling method, with 1000 resampled datasets generated from the test set. The results showed that the 95% confidence intervals for accuracy, AUC, and recall were [88.6%, 94.7%], [0.857, 0.893], and [83.2%, 88.1%], respectively. The standard error for each metric was below 2.5%. The sensitivity analysis revealed that the AUC value of the XGBoost model changed only by 0.018, from 0.875 to 0.857, even if the size of the sample for the train set was reduced by 20%. With the help of the multi-dimensional validation outcomes, the XGBoost model demonstrates strong predictive capabilities, adaptability, and operational efficacy within the studied context, providing a reference for risk management related to the construction of airports. Application to other airports would require local calibration to account for differences in operational scale, climate conditions, and regulatory frameworks.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical gap between aviation safety and construction management research. While prior aviation safety studies have primarily focused on in-flight operations, this study integrates air traffic management considerations with ground construction processes. It does so by quantifying and incorporating aviation-specific risk factors, such as a flight density threshold of 35 aircraft per hour and 2-h NOTAM delay. These are safety dimensions that have not yet been addressed in other studies, which largely focused on operational efficiency21. Based on the existing dynamic risk models developed for construction sites, the current study introduces aviation-specific dimensions, which include operational stress and communication22. The accuracy achieved by the XGBoost model (92.7%) is comparable to or somewhat higher than the 80–85% range commonly reported for construction injury prediction models34, though direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given differences in dataset characteristics and risk definitions across studies35,36.

Beyond comparison with construction safety models, recent aviation safety studies also provide useful benchmarks for contextualizing our model’s performance. Liu et al. (2024) proposed a new risk level identification model for aviation safety that employed a fine-tuned SBERT model combined with GAN-based oversampling to address sample imbalance in ASRS data, reporting improved classification accuracy for underrepresented risk categories37. This study addresses a similar class imbalance challenge (25% risk events) using SMOTE oversampling and class weight adjustment, achieving comparable robustness with a recall of 85.7% for the minority class. A systematic review by Nanyonga et al. (2025) of machine learning analytic methods for aviation accident research confirms that supervised learning methods, particularly tree-based ensemble models, have proven effective across diverse aviation safety scenarios, which is consistent with the finding that XGBoost outperforms deep learning and SVM on our medium-sized dataset38. Furthermore, the XGBoost model’s accuracy of 92.7% and AUC of 0.875 compare favorably with the 77–93% accuracy range reported in recent aviation ML studies, though direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given differences in problem formulation, data sources, and risk definitions across studies. Given the class imbalance in this dataset (25% risk events), recall (85.7%) and AUC (0.875) provide more reliable indicators of model performance than accuracy alone. The recall of 85.7% with the imbalance ratio of 25% demonstrates the model’s robustness against the issue of biased classification towards the majority class39,40. The risk effect of NOTAM delay had a greater OR value of 4.28 than the effect of visibility, with OR = 3.18, and flight density, with OR = 3.76, indicating the importance of information communication. Despite the construction risk incidence rate being higher at night, 31.2%, than in the daytime, 20.8%, the accuracy rate of 89.2% was attained for nighttime construction risks.

SHAP interaction analysis reveals a synergistic amplification effect of operational stress factors (flight density and peak hours), demonstrating the mutual reinforcement among risk factors41. The threshold jump range identified by SHAP analysis (25–35 flights/hour) mutually validates the critical value determined by statistical analysis (35 flights/hour), demonstrating the complementarity of traditional statistical methods and machine learning methods. The variance inflation factor (VIF < 4.5) confirms the absence of collinearity among features, providing a robust foundation for SHAP interaction analysis42. Dual validation by tenfold cross-validation and 1000 bootstrap resampling (95% CI: [88.6%, 94.7%]) confirms the model’s robustness33,43. Research has demonstrated that tree-based ensemble methods outperform deep learning on medium-sized datasets (n = 412)44,45. The successful application of the SMOTE oversampling technique validates the applicability of the imbalanced-learn toolkit in the field of safety prediction46. Interpretable system implementations transform the “black box” into an analytical framework that reveals the mechanisms of risk formation47, which is crucial for model deployment in safety–critical domains48.

This study provides a flexible tool to address the “efficiency-safety” dilemma in aviation construction management. The tool’s flexibility is demonstrated by its adjustable risk thresholds, which enable differentiated management strategies. For instance, setting a lower threshold (0.3) at hub airports can increase recall to 92%, prioritizing the detection of all potential risks, whereas a higher threshold (0.7) at smaller airports can reduce the false positive rate to 2%, minimizing unnecessary operational disruptions. Furthermore, the tool is designed for practicality. Tree-based ensemble models such as XGBoost are generally recognized for computational efficiency, enabling rapid inference suitable for operational deployment. The transition from post-event review to pre-event warning represents a paradigm shift compared to traditional assessments (several days). Identified risk transmission paths enable targeted interventions. This includes optimizing construction periods for operational stress factors, establishing meteorological early warning linkages for environmental factors, and strengthening process standardization for management factors. The protective effect of construction personnel experience (risk reduced by 58.9% for those with over 8 years of experience) provides a quantitative basis for human resource allocation49,50. Multi-scenario validation shows that the model’s accuracy remains at 83.3% even under severe weather conditions.

It is worth noting that airport construction increasingly takes place within the context of broader infrastructure transitions, including sustainability objectives and operational resilience goals; recent studies on renewable energy integration and emerging energy technologies at airports illustrate this growing technological complexity25. Construction activities must be planned not only to minimize safety risks but also to align with long-term airport development strategies, where multi-criteria decision frameworks applied to sustainable aviation fuel selection exemplify how environmental objectives and operational constraints must be jointly evaluated51. The risk prediction framework developed in this study could complement strategic infrastructure planning by providing quantitative safety assessments that inform construction scheduling and resource allocation decisions. Future research could explore how sustainability-related construction activities (e.g., installation of renewable energy infrastructure on airport grounds) introduce distinct risk profiles that may require model recalibration.

Limitations and future work

Several limitations of this study should be explicitly acknowledged to ensure methodological transparency. First, data dependency is a major constraint. The model is trained on data from 412 construction events at a single major hub airport in eastern China, and its performance may vary when applied to airports with different operational scales, climatic conditions, and regulatory frameworks. Second, the model’s ability to identify rare events (such as sudden equipment failures, n = 4) remains limited due to insufficient training samples. While SMOTE addresses moderate class imbalance, it may introduce synthetic noise for extremely rare event types39; future studies should explore advanced oversampling strategies such as ADASYN or distributional balancing approaches. Third, differences in scale, climate, and facilities among different airports require localized model calibration52, and generalizability to fundamentally different operational contexts (e.g., airports in arid climates, military-civilian dual-use airports) has not been tested. Multi-airport collaborative validation, ideally incorporating data from airports of varying operational scales and climatic regions, would be essential to establish the broader applicability of the proposed framework.

Compared to traditional methods, machine learning has significant advantages in accuracy (92.7% vs 70–75%), efficiency (milliseconds vs days), and consistency. However, expert judgment retains unique value for handling novel or ambiguous situations. Recent research on the impact of AI on aviation safety culture emphasizes that deploying AI in safety–critical domains requires careful attention to human-AI collaboration, trust calibration, and organizational transparency53. Accordingly, a human–machine collaborative decision-making model is recommended, aiming to combine analytical precision with contextual adaptability.

Future research can be expanded in several directions: introducing time-series features to capture the impact of construction progress, developing online learning mechanisms to adapt to regulatory changes, integrating multi-airport data for transfer learning, and deeply integrating with safety management systems to form an intelligent decision-making platform. Furthermore, recent advances in real-flight physiological monitoring demonstrate the feasibility of capturing operator cognitive and physiological states in operational environments14; incorporating such human-factors monitoring data into construction risk models could improve the real-time assessment of personnel-related risk variables such as fatigue and workload.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a machine learning-based risk prediction model for airport construction under non-stop operations, achieving an accuracy of 92.7% (95% CI: 88.6%-94.7%) and recall of 85.7% on an independent test set. Five core risk factors with critical thresholds were identified: flight density exceeding 35 flights per hour, visibility below 3 km, NOTAM release delays exceeding 2 h, peak-hour operations, and construction personnel experience. Theoretically, this study establishes a multidimensional risk factor system and elucidates the complex, nonlinear interactions among factors using SHAP value analysis. This approach also quantitatively validates the synergistic effect between operational pressure and environmental factors. Methodologically, the study follows a complete pipeline from data collection to model interpretation. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of five major algorithms demonstrates the superiority of tree-based methods over deep learning for medium-sized datasets. In practice, the model demonstrates potential for development into a risk warning system, though deployment at other airports would necessitate local calibration and validation. Differentiated risk management can be achieved through threshold adjustment. It provides a flexible tool for solving the “efficiency-safety” dilemma and supports construction plan optimization, dynamic resource allocation and human–machine collaborative decision-making. The limitations of this study and directions for future work have been discussed in detail above.
