Biomanufacturing Potential of Streamlined Cells
Authors: Alvaro R. Lara, Marie B. Andersen, Alexander A. V. Madsen, Kathrine Gravlund Fønss, Marta Irla, Hilal Taymaz‐Nikerel, Luz María Martínez, Max Daniel Dicke, Marcel Mann, Jørgen B. Magnus, Guillermo Gosset
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA series of Escherichia coli streamlined strains was developed by removing the expression of genes encoding extracellular structures and unessential enzymes. The streamlined strains exhibited improved metabolic performance, including lower overflow metabolism and ATP maintenance coefficient, as well as a higher growth rate, compared to their parental strain. The intracellular levels of ATP were monitored using a genetic sensor, showing the improved resource stewardship of the streamlined cells. The streamlined strains were tested as cell factories to produce plasmid DNA (pDNA) in batch cultures, exhibiting a 23% increase in the specific pDNA production rate, compared to the parental strain. Recombinant protein expression was evaluated in microbioreactors in batch and fed‐batch modes. In batch mode, recombinant protein yield from biomass was up to 82% higher in the streamlined strains than in the parental strain. Furthermore, in fed‐batch mode, the recombinant protein yield was 79% greater in the streamlined cells compared to the parental strain. Our results show the benefits of reducing cellular complexity on the biomanufacturing of pDNA and recombinant proteins in culture schemes typical of industrial settings.

Introduction

Cellular minimization is an attractive strategy to enhance the performance of bioproduction hosts. From a bioprocess perspective, reducing cellular complexity can help to minimize the energy spent on dispensable activities (Luisi et al. 2006). Such energy savings may lead to higher product formation, or more robust cellular performance under industrial bioreactor environments (Löffler et al. 2016; Zieringer et al. 2021). Genome minimization has the potential to provide data to better understand the basic mechanisms of life at the cellular level and has been applied to obtain minimized cells. The common approach is to remove large genome portions containing unessential genes to obtain the smallest genome size that does not affect cell growth. Genome reduction has been applied to industrial workhorses like Escherichia coli (Kolisnychenko et al. 2002), Bacillus subtilis (Reuß et al. 2016), Pseudomonas putida (Lieder et al. 2015), and Corynebacterium glutamicum (Hemmerich et al. 2020). Genome reduction has been useful to obtain Schlegelella brevitalea strains with higher expression of heterologous biosynthetic gene clusters (Liu et al. 2021a). However, massive deletions of the genome may remove relevant unknown genes and do not always result in improved bioprocess performance (Amendola et al. 2024).

Rather than the deletion of extensive portions of the genome, the elimination of highly expressed, but useless proteins is a more attractive approach. This can increase the availability of ribosomes and amino acids for protein production (Valgepea et al. 2015). It has been reported that an E. coli strain streamlined following this approach exhibited a reduced maintenance coefficient and robust performance under heterogeneous environmental conditions (Ziegler et al. 2021; Cordell et al. 2024). Cellular streamlining can be pursued by deleting transcriptional factors (Pohlen et al. 2025). Lastiri‐Pancardo and coworkers (Lastiri‐Pancardo et al. 2020) developed a quantitative modelling technique to reduce the proteome burden with a minimal set of transcriptional factor deletions. Streamlined cells obtained by this approach resulted in increased plasmid DNA (pDNA) production (de la Cruz et al. 2020) and recombinant protein under aerobic and oxygen‐limited regimes (Lara et al. 2024). In the present contribution, the proteome‐reduced strains were further engineered by removing genes coding for extracellular structures and unessential enzymes that are expressed at a high level. A series of streamlined cells was obtained, and their metabolic performance was characterized. Also, they were evaluated for pDNA and recombinant protein production in batch and fed‐batch modes, exhibiting superior performance, compared to the wild‐type strain, under both conditions.

Materials and Methods

Strains

E. coli K‐12 MG1655 was used as wild type (WT). It was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA). Proteome‐reduced mutants derived from the WT were denoted as PR01 (Lara et al. 2024), and PR03 to PR06. A strain named PR02 has been developed and previously reported (Lara et al. 2024), but was not included in the present study. The genotypes of the different strains are shown in Table 1. Strain PR03 was obtained by CRISPR‐Cas. Strain PR04 was obtained by phage transduction from the Keio collection (Baba et al. 2006). Deletions in strains PR05 and PR06 were carried out using the method described elsewhere (Datsenko and Wanner 2000).

Plasmids

ATP availability was monitored using a genetic sensor that was based on the design of Deng and coworkers (Deng et al. 2021). In brief, the sensor contains the ATP‐inducible promoter P 1rrnB that controls the expression of a fast‐folding and fast‐degrading green fluorescence protein (GFP‐mut2), fused with the SsrA protease degradation tag to enable its rapid degradation (Figure 1a). Deng and coworkers reported a good correlation between the GFP fluorescence using this sensor and the measured intracellular ATP. Recombinant protein expression was tested using a protein generator similar to the ATP sensor, except that the strong P trc promoter was used to control GFP expression (Figure 1b). The construction lacked the operator sequence, rendering GFP expression constitutive (Lara et al. 2024). The sequences were synthesized and cloned in the plasmid pUC57‐Kan mini by GenScript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). Plasmid pVAX1 from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, USA) was used as a model for therapeutic pDNA‐based products. pVAX1 is 3.0 kbp long, is a high copy‐number vector, and confers kanamycin resistance. The different strains were transformed using the heat‐shock method.

Schemes of the genetic circuits used. (a) ATP sensor. (b) Constitutive protein generator.

Precultures Development

A first preculture was performed by transferring 20 µL of the cryopreserved cells to 15 mL TB medium contained in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The composition of TB was (in g L−1): yeast extract, 24; tryptone, 20; glycerol, 4. The cells were grown at 37°C and 350 rpm in an orbital shaker of 50 mm shaking diameter for 7–8 h. Next, 20 µL were transferred to 20 mL of mineral medium contained in a 250 mL baffled Erlenmeyer flask and cultured at 37°C and 250 rpm in an orbital shaker of 50 mm shaking diameter until mid‐exponential growth phase (approx. 14–16 h). The mineral medium used was modified from a previous report (Wilms et al. 2001). The medium composition was (in g L−1): (NH4)2SO4, 6.5; NH4Cl, 0.5; K2HPO4, 3.0; Na2SO4, 2.0; MgSO4 ⋅ 7H2O, 0.5; thiamine hydrochloride 0.01; MOPS, 41.85; plus 1 mL of trace elements solution per L of medium. The initial pH was adjusted to 7.4 before sterilization. The trace elements solution composition was (in g L−1): ZnSO4⋅ 7H2O, 0.54; CuSO4 ⋅ 5H2O, 0.48; MnSO4 ⋅ H2O, 0.30; CoCl2 ⋅ 6H2O, 0.54; FeCl3 ⋅ 6H2O, 41.76; CaCl2 ⋅ 2H2O, 1.98; Na2EDTA ⋅ 2H2O, 33.4. After cooling down, the medium was supplemented with glucose at a final concentration of 20 g L−1. For all cultures of plasmid bearing cells, the media were supplemented with 50 mg L−1 kanamycin sulfate. Aliquots from the second preculture were washed with 0.9% sterile NaCl solution and used to inoculate the main cultures.

Cultures in Shaking Flasks

The untransformed strains were characterized in 250 mL baffled shake flasks equipped with optodes to monitor dissolved oxygen (DO) and pH (PreSens Precision Sensing GmbH, Regensburg, Germany). The flasks were filled with 30 mL of mineral medium plus 5 g L−1 glucose and inoculated at an initial optical density (OD600nm) of 0.6. at 37°C and 250 rpm in an orbital shaker of 50 mm shaking diameter. Precultures of strains bearing pVAX1 were carried out under the same conditions, except that kanamycin sulfate was added. Main cultures for pVAX1 production were conducted in 250 mL baffled shake flasks filled with 30 mL mineral medium plus 2.5 g L−1 glucose and kanamycin sulfate, inoculated at initial OD600nm 0.3. All the experiments were run in triplicate.

Cultures in Microbioreactors

GFP expression was evaluated in microtiter plates (MTPs). Batch cultures were conducted in 48‐round well transparent bottom wells MTPs (M2P‐MTP‐R48‐B, Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany) with a liquid volume of 0.75 mL per well, and an initial OD600 of 0.1. Fed‐batch cultures were performed in 48‐round well transparent bottom FeedPlates+ (Cat. Num. SMFR02004_C, Kuhner Shaker GmbH, Birsfelden, Switzerland), which contain a polymer ring in each well that releases 22 mg glucose in 48 h and allows optical monitoring in the BioLector (Sparviero et al. 2023). Each well was filled with 0.75 mL mineral medium inoculated at an initial OD600 of 0.5. The MTPs were covered with a gas‐permeable polyolefin sealing foil (HJ‐Bioanalytik GmbH, Erkelenz, Germany), to reduce evaporation and prevent contaminations. An in‐house‐built spectroscopic screening system capable of measuring four MTPs in parallel (Wandrey et al. 2016a; Wandrey et al. 2016b). With this system, scattered light and fluorescence measurements were performed through the transparent bottom of the microtiter plates, based on the established BioLector technology (Samorski et al. 2005; Kensy et al. 2009). The optical fiber was connected to a spectrofluorometer with excitation/emission monochromators (Fluoromax‐4, HORIBA Jobin Yvon GmbH, Germany). Scattered light was measured at 620 nm using an integration time of 120 ms and a slit bandwidth of 4 nm. GFP fluorescence was measured at 480 nm (λex) and 507 nm (λem) with an integration time of 900 ms and a slit bandwidth of 8 nm. The cultures were conducted at 37°C, 1000 rpm and 3 mm shaking diameter.

Quantification of Metabolite Concentrations

The concentrations of glucose and acetic acid were determined using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) with a refractive index detector using the 1260 Infinity II LC System equipped with a Hi‐Plex H guard (7.7 × 50 mm 8 µm), a separation column (300 × 7.7 mm, 8 µm), and a refractive index detector (all Agilent Technologies, USA). A 5 mM H2SO4 solution was used as eluent at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min and at 40°C.

Plasmid DNA Analyses

Approximately 2 mg of wet biomass was used for pDNA purification using a QIAprep Miniprep Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the instructions of the manufacturer. Plasmid DNA was eluted using 100 µL of TE buffer and quantified by UV spectroscopy in a DS‐11+ Spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, USA). The supercoiled fraction (SCF) was determined by electrophoresis of 100 ng of pDNA in 1% agarose gel in TAE buffer at 100 V for approx. 40 min. A sample of linear plasmid, obtained by digesting a sample with the enzyme BamHI (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), was also loaded. Supercoiled pDNA was identified by the band that migrates faster than the linear plasmid and corresponded to the covalently closed circular (ccc) monomer (Schmidt et al. 2001). Image analyses were performed with the Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Therefore, SCF here is referred to as the ccc monomer. Other isoforms were not quantified.

Calculations

Specific growth, glucose consumption and acetate production rates and yields were calculated by the proper mass balances during the exponential growth. Specific oxygen uptake rate (q O2 ) was calculated using Equation (1): (1) qO2=kLaCL*−CL−dCLdtx

Where kLa is the mass transfer coefficient obtained from the kLa Calculator Tool (PreSens Precision Sensing GmbH, Regensburg, Germany); C L * is the solubility of oxygen in the medium at 37°C and 1 atm pressure; C L is the dissolved oxygen concentration calculated from the DO readings, t is the time of measurement, and x is the biomass concentration. The rate of oxygen concentration changes in the liquid (dCLdt) was calculated numerically from the DO data. The reported q O2 values represent the mean values during the exponential growth phase. The data from fluorescence and scattered light (ScL) intensities are represented as the corresponding reading minus the lowest reading (usually obtained during the first 20 min of culture). The specific fluorescence (SF) was calculated as the mean of the fluorescence divided by the ScL readings at every time point during the period involved. The specific fluorescence emission rate (q F ) was calculated by multiplying the specific fluorescence and specific growth rate values. For comparison between two groups means, unpaired Student's t‐tests were performed.

Flux Balance Analysis

Flux balance analysis (FBA) is used to calculate intracellular fluxes of each biochemical reaction of an organism, as a function of experimentally measured extracellular/uptake rates. FBA was carried out using an E. coli genome‐scale model (Orth et al. 2011) in the COBRA toolbox (Schellenberger et al. 2011). The under‐determined model was optimized for maximum ATP hydrolysis. For constraints of FBA, the measured specific growth rate, consumption rate of glucose, and production rate of acetate were implemented for each strain/condition.

Maintenance rates were obtained using the ATP balance in this model. The ATP balance of the complete metabolic network model can be written as: (2) qATP,ox−∑qiATP−KXµ−mATP=0 where the first term represents the production of ATP in oxidative phosphorylation, the second term represents the net rate of ATP consumption in the part of the metabolic network model of which the ATP stoichiometry is known, including substrate level phosphorylation, µ is the specific growth rate, K X and m ATP represent the growth dependent and growth independent maintenance coefficients, respectively (Taymaz‐Nikerel et al. 2010). m ATP values shown below were calculated via FBA as ATP maintenance requirement in the metabolic model (Taymaz‐Nikerel et al. 2010).

Results and Discussion

Metabolic Characterization of the Streamlined Strains

Cellular streamlining was performed by removing unnecessary functions for the growth of E. coli in suspension. Strain PR01 was obtained based on the proteome minimization scheme developed by Lastiri‐Pancardo and coworkers (Lastiri‐Pancardo et al. 2020). The deletion of operons coding for fimbriae proteins has been demonstrated to confer higher biomass synthesis capacity in E. coli (Qiao et al. 2021). Because fimbriae can be considered dispensable in suspension cultures, the operon fimAICDFGH was deleted from PR01 to obtain strain PR03 (Table 1). The aldehyde‐alcohol dehydrogenase (AdhE, coded by adhE) is among the 5% most abundant proteins in E. coli growing aerobically in glucose (Schmidt et al. 2016; Mori et al. 2021). AdhE catalyzes the two‐step conversion of acetyl‐CoA to ethanol. Although these reactions are important to regenerate NAD+ under oxygen limitation, they are not required under fully aerobic conditions. Therefore, the adhE gene was knocked out in PR03, to obtain strain PR04. In E. coli, the pyruvate kinase activity is performed by the proteins PykA and PykF. PykA accounts for only 7% of the total pyruvate kinase activity (Zhao et al. 2017), while it represents 23% of the total protein mass with pyruvate kinase activity (Schmidt et al. 2016). It can be assumed that PykA plays a minor role, compared to PykF. Furthermore, a PykA‐deficient mutant did not show any growth defect (Zhao et al. 2017). Consequently, strain PR05 was obtained by deleting the pykA gene in strain PR04. Additionally, the pyruvate‐formate lyase coding gene (pflB) was deleted in strain PR05, to obtain strain PR06. The PflB enzyme catalyzes the coenzyme A‐dependent, synthesis of formate from pyruvate under anaerobic conditions. Like AdhE, PflB is also highly expressed in E. coli under aerobic conditions (Schmidt et al. 2016; Mori et al. 2021), but formate synthesis is not considered essential if the cell can carry out aerobic respiration.

All the strains were first characterized in batch cultures in shaking flasks with glucose as a carbon source (C‐source). The kinetic and stoichiometric parameters during the exponential growth phase are shown in Figure 2. The genetic modifications in strains PR01 and PR03 had minimal or no effect on the growth performance of the cells (Figure 2), showing that the streamlining procedure did not cause any growth defects. The only relevant changes were slightly lower q S values (Figure 2d,e, respectively). Similar results were reported for strain PR01 cultures in mini bioreactors with pH control (Lara et al. 2024). According to Qiao and coworkers (Qiao et al. 2021), some increase in biomass formation was expected as a result of the deletion of the fimAICDFGH operon. However, it was not observed for PR03, since no significant increase of YX/S was detected, compared to the WT strain (Figure 2c).

The proteome reduction in strains PR04, PR05 and PR06 resulted in a small progressive but significant increase of the growth rate (µ) and biomass yield from glucose (Y X/S ), compared to the WT strain (Figure 2a,b, respectively). The values of µ and Y X/S of strain PR06 were 21% and 16% higher, respectively, than those of the WT. Proteome reduction had no effect on the acetate yield from glucose (Y acet/S ) for strains PR01 and PR03. Notwithstanding, further cellular streamlining reduced significantly Y acet/S (p < 0.05) (Figure 2c). Notably, the deletion of adhE had a relevant impact on overflow metabolism (OM), since strain PR04 exhibited a 22% decrease in Y acet/S . The additional deletion of pykA did not change acetate production, but the extra knock out of pflB further decreased Y acet/S , which was 33% lower for strain PR06 than for the WT (Figure 2c). A possible explanation of these results is the lower amount of useless protein expressed by PR06. The cellular proteome can be divided into several sectors (fractions) with no overlap between them: one related to fermentation, like OM (ϕf); another related to respiration (ϕr), and a third one for biomass generation (ϕBM), which is growth‐dependent (Scott et al. 2010; Basan et al. 2015; Chen and Nielsen 2019). The sum of these fractions equals a maximum fraction for proteome allocation, ϕmax. However, when useless protein expression, ϕZ, is considered in the system, the proteome allocation can be represented by Equation 3 (Wang 2024): (3) ϕf+ϕr+ϕBM−ϕZ=ϕmax

In the present study, a decrease in ϕZ resulted in increased ϕBM. Although the proteome fractions were not quantified, some relationships are possible: the lower flux to acetate (Figure 2e), and the higher oxygen consumption rate (q O2 ) (Figure 2f) in strain PR06, compared to the WT, may be connected to lower ϕf and higher ϕr, respectively. It has been proposed that ribosome composition (keeping kinetic parameters constant), determines the maximum growth rate (Széliová et al. 2024) and on the number of active ribosomes and their translation elongation rate (Shore and Albert 2022; Matamouros et al. 2023). It is possible that ribosome availability is higher in the streamlined strains, particularly PR04, PR05, and PR06, thus leading to an increase in the maximum growth rate (Figure 2a).

A desired trait of streamlined cells as hosts for bioproduction, is a reduction of the maintenance demand (Ziegler and Takors 2020). Ideally, if fewer resources are used for maintenance activities, there would be a higher availability of these resources to be allocated to product or biomass formation. Cell streamlining may also increase ATP availability, since it has been estimated that nearly 60% of the ATP required for cell synthesis is used for protein translation (Ortega‐Arzola et al. 2024). The ATP consumption rate for maintenance (m ATP ) was estimated for all the strains using the extracellular rates given in Figure 2 and flux balance analysis. The results show that the streamlining process gradually reduced m ATP to almost one‐third of that of the WT strain (Figure 3). An important contribution to the m ATP decrease may be the reduced expression of proteins related to motility in the streamlined strains (Lastiri‐Pancardo et al. 2020). It has been estimated that the ATP consumed for flagellar protein synthesis and flagellar movement consumes up to 10% of the microbial cell ATP (Schavemaker and Lynch 2022). This represents a high ATP cost, particularly because cellular mobility can be considered unnecessary in stirred or shaken cultures. The results shown in Figure 3 are also consistent with a previous report of a reduced maintenance coefficient for glucose in a streamlined E. coli strain (Ziegler et al. 2021). In that study, the authors reported a slight increase of µ in the streamlined strain, but the Y X/S was nearly unchanged, which contrasts with the growth pattern of strain PR06. While acetate production was not reported, in a separate report the streamlined strain was used to produce octanoic acid, and the modified strain accumulated fourfold more acetate in batch culture than the WT strain (MG1655), while the octanoic acid yield and titer remained unchanged (Cordell et al. 2024). This result also contrasts with the reduced OM in strain PR06.

Estimated specific ATP consumption rates for cellular maintenance (m ATP ) for the different strains. Values were calculated via FBA as ATP maintenance requirement in the metabolic model (Taymaz‐Nikerel et al. 2010).

The possibility of higher ATP availability in the streamlined cells was further investigated using a genetic ATP sensor. The sensor expresses a fast‐folding GFP under transcriptional control of an ATP‐inducible promoter (Figure 1a). GFP is fused to a tag promoting its fast degradation. The suitability of this sensor for tracking the intracellular ATP concentration and dynamics has been well documented in a previous publication (Deng et al. 2021). Figure 4a depicts the growth and GFP fluorescence profiles in batch cultures of each strain. In general, the GFP signal increased parallel to the biomass concentration (monitored as scattered light intensity, ScL). GFP fluorescence reached a maximum at the point of growth decline, which can be interpreted as the point of glucose exhaustion (Wewetzer et al. 2015), and decreased together with a slow biomass growth, which corresponds to the reassimilation of acetate produced by OM (Wewetzer et al. 2015). During the stationary phase, the GFP fluorescence remained at a very low and stable value, indicating low energy generation due to a lack of C‐source.

(a) Culture profile of strains bearing the ATP sensor. Biomass was monitored by scattered light intensity (ScL). Shaded bands indicate the standard deviation between replicates. (b) Specific GFP fluorescence calculated over the exponential growth phase. Vertical lines indicate the standard deviation between replicates. The specific fluorescence of all the streamlined strains was significantly different from that of the WT (p < 0.05).

The specific GFP fluorescence was calculated over the exponential growth phase and is depicted in Figure 4b. The increased specific GFP fluorescence suggests that the streamlining process resulted in higher ATP availability. These results agree with those of Figure 3 and with the observed increase in the ATP/ADP ratio in E. coli cells lacking all the fimbriae genes (Qiao et al. 2021). The initial characterization of the streamlined strains demonstrates that the genetic interventions did not cause any negative effect, and that the accumulated streamlining process resulted in improved growth performance, which is probably connected to a better resource stewardship of the cells (Kim et al. 2012).

Plasmid DNA Production in Streamlined Cells

The streamlined cells were evaluated as hosts for bioproduction. The first molecule of interest was pDNA, which is a key molecule for the production of DNA and RNA vaccines, as well as gene therapy products. All the strains were transformed with the high copy‐number plasmid pVAX1 and cultured in batch mode. The values of µ, pDNA yields from biomass (Y pDNA/X ), specific pDNA production rate (q pDNA ) and supercoiled fraction (SCF) were determined during the exponential growth phase and are shown in Figure 5. The streamlined strains displayed a lower µ, compared to the WT, except for PR06, which grew at the same rate as the WT strain (Figure 5a). The Y pDNA/X of strains PR01‐PR05 were not higher than that of the WT. Only strain PR06 exhibited a Y pDNA/X 11% higher than the WT (Figure 5b), which resulted in 23% higher q pDNA . The SCF did not change for strains PR01‐PR04, but increased by 10% and 18% for strains PR05 and PR06, compared to WT (Figure 5d). SCF is a quality attribute of pDNA, and lower SCF values imply lower quality. One of the enzymes introducing negative supercoils into DNA is the DNA gyrase, which requires the energy of ATP hydrolysis to function (Basu et al. 2012). It is possible that higher ATP availability could have influenced the SCF, particularly in strains PR05 and PR06, but in contrast with the unchanged SCF in the other streamlined strains, compared to the WT, even when higher ATP availability is expected.

Performance of the different strains producing pDNA in batch cultures. (a) Specific growth rate; (b) pDNA yield from biomass; (c) specific pDNA production rate; (d) pDNA supercoiled fraction. The values were determined during the exponential growth phase. * Indicates significant difference (p < 0.05), compared to the WT strain under the same conditions.

Taken together, the results of Figure 5 indicate that the streamlined strain PR06 is a better pDNA producer than the WT strain in terms of pDNA yield, production rate, and quality. Notably, this enhancement occurred without any genetic interventions aimed specifically at increasing pDNA production.

Streamlined Cells as Hosts for Recombinant Protein Production

To assess the potential of streamlined cells for recombinant protein production, GFP was expressed constitutively from a strong promoter in batch mode using glucose as C‐source. The culture profiles are shown in Figure 6a. The maximal GFP fluorescence was not influenced by the cellular streamlining, but caused a decrease in biomass synthesis, particularly in strains PR01, PR05 and PR06. It has been previously reported that strain PR01 expressed more flavin mononucleotide‐based fluorescent protein (FbFP) than the WT using the same promoter as in the present study, while the biomass synthesis also decreased (Lara et al. 2024). Deletion of the fim operon (strain PR03) recovered the biomass synthesis without altering the GFP fluorescence, compared to the WT strain (Figure 6a). The additional knockout of the adhE and pflB genes (strain PR05 and PR06), led to lower biomass synthesis, while the GFP expression remained unchanged (Figure 6a). Cellular streamlining increased the specific GFP fluorescence (Figure 6b). Notably, the specific fluorescence was 57% and 82% greater, for strains PR05 and PR06, respectively, than that of the WT strain (Figure 6b). The specific fluorescence emission rate was also greater for the streamlined strains than for the WT. However, the maximum specific fluorescence emission rate was exhibited by strain PR05, which was 2.5‐fold, compared to the WT (Figure 6c). Despite displaying the highest specific fluorescence, the specific fluorescence emission rate of strain PR06 was only 41% greater than that of the WT (Figure 6c). This implies that the additional pflB deletion has a positive effect for pDNA production in batch cultures (Figure 5), but the benefit is not as clear in the case of GFP expression.

(b) Specific GFP fluorescence calculated over the exponential growth phase. (c) Specific GFP fluorescence emission rate calculated over the exponential growth phase. Vertical lines indicate the standard deviation between replicates. The values in (b) and (c) of all the streamlined strains were significantly different from those of the WT (p < 0.05).

The decrease in biomass synthesis in the streamlined strains can be understood from Equation 3 as a redirection of cellular resources to recombinant protein (ϕZ) instead of biomass (ϕBM). The underlying mechanisms of this metabolic trait are beyond the scope of the present work, but could be related to ribosome sequestration, which bind to the multiple mRNA copies of the GFP gene encoded in a high copy‐number plasmid. Ribosome kinetics are considered rate‐limiting for protein synthesis and cell growth in bacterial cells (Liu et al. 2021b). Therefore, should competition for ribosomes between ϕZ and ϕBM occur, the ribosomes may remain bound to the mRNA, leaving fewer ribosomes available for the synthesis of biomass proteins. However, the reason for the reduced growth rate in strain PR06 remains elusive. From a bioprocessing perspective, the results in Figure 6 indicate that the performance of the streamlined cells is attractive, because higher protein yields from biomass will positively impact downstream sequences.

While the streamlined strains displayed several advantages in batch mode, fed‐batch is the most usual culture mode in industrial settings. The streamlined strains constitutively expressing GFP were cultured in fed‐batch mode in MTPs and compared to WT. The cultures were run for 48 h, and the growth profiles are shown in Figure 7a. The results at the end of the culture were, in general, different from those of the batch cultures. In fed‐batch mode, strain PR01 reached the same GFP fluorescence level as the WT, although it produced 10% less biomass (Figure 7a), which resembles the performance of PR01 in batch mode (Figure 6a). Strain PR03 reached 25% higher GFP fluorescence, while it recovered the same biomass level as the WT. This contrasts with results in batch mode, where strain PR03 recovered biomass synthesis, but attained the same GFP fluorescence as the WT (Figure 6a). Like in batch mode, strain PR04 also attained the same biomass as the WT in fed‐batch (Figures 6a and 7a, respectively). Nevertheless, in contrast to batch cultures, strain PR04 achieved 47% greater GFP fluorescence (Figure 7a). Such a remarkable GFP increase was retained in strains PR05 and PR06, while the synthesized biomass decreased (Figure 7a).

(a) Fed‐batch culture profiles of strains expressing GFP constitutively. Biomass was monitored by scattered light intensity (ScL). Shaded bands indicate the standard deviation between replicates. (b) Specific GFP fluorescence calculated over 40 to 48 h of culture. The values in (b) of all the streamlined strains were significantly different from those of the WT (p < 0.05).

The specific GFP fluorescence was calculated over the last 8 h of culture and is depicted in Figure 7b. All the streamlined strains exhibited a higher specific GFP fluorescence, with the PR05 strain being the top producer, displaying a specific fluorescence 79% greater than that of the WT (Figure 7b). This contrasts with the results of batch cultures, in which the highest specific fluorescence was exhibited by strain PR06 (Figure 6b). Nevertheless, the streamlined strains exhibited higher specific fluorescence in batch and fed‐batch cultures, indicating that they are potentially robust to commonly‐employed industrial culture conditions. This contrasts with the results of extensive genome minimization performed on Corynebacterium glutamicum, which resulted in increased production of extracellular recombinant cutinase, but lower production in fed‐batch mode.

Conclusions

The developed streamlined strains exhibited relevant metabolic differences that indicate a more efficient resource stewardship. Even when no specific mutations to increase pDNA or recombinant protein production were introduced, the streamlined cells showed advantages for the production of both classes of molecules. Particularly, the streamlined cells were superior producers of recombinant protein in fed‐batch mode, which is an important result for industrial applications. Overall, it was demonstrated that the streamlining concept applied to E. coli is a powerful tool for designing better production hosts for biotechnological processes.
