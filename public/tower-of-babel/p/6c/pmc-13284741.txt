Reanalyzing the impact of cognitive training over 20 years in the ACTIVE study: Losing the forest in the trees
Authors: William T Dickens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis paper reanalyzes the 20‐year follow‐up to the Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly (ACTIVE) cognitive training trial, which evaluated whether memory, reasoning, and speed‐of‐processing training reduce dementia risk in older adults.METHODSPublished hazard ratios and confidence intervals from the follow‐up were used to conduct Bonferroni–Dunn corrections, sign tests on the pattern of hazard ratios, and parametric modeling.RESULTSAfter Bonferroni–Dunn correction, all confidence intervals include a hazard ratio of 1. Sign tests reject both the hypothesis of no training effect and the hypothesis that only one training type is effective. Generalized method of moments estimation of parametric models suggests a common protective effect for all treatments concentrated in the youngest age group.DISCUSSIONPreregistration strengthens claims of statistical significance, but interpretation need not be limited to the preregistered analytical framework when alternative analyses reveal consistent patterns.

INTRODUCTION

This report reexamines the analysis presented in Coe et al.’s 1 study of dementia outcomes across three treatment conditions which concludes that speed training was effective but that “There was no main effect of memory or reasoning training on risk of ADRD.” 1 Two methodological issues motivate a closer look at the evidence. First, the paper interprets each treatment arm through its individual confidence interval without applying any correction for multiple comparisons. Second, the analysis treats each arm in isolation and does not consider the overall pattern of results: all but one of the 30 hazard ratios reported in the paper indicate improved outcomes from treatment. This pattern carries information that the original analysis does not exploit.

METHODS AND RESULTS

Correcting for multiple tests

When choosing an alpha level for hypothesis testing or constructing confidence intervals, one sets the probability of making a Type I error under the null hypothesis, or the probability that the confidence interval includes the parameter's true value, at alpha. If one constructs 1/α confidence intervals one expects that one of them will not contain the true value of the parameter. Following Bonferroni 2 and Dunn 3 if one is constructing confidence intervals from independent statistics one should construct each confidence interval for (1−α/m)% to ensure that the probability of finding any significant values is α.

The conclusion that only speed training with a booster has efficacy depends on results presented in the bottom half of Table 2 in Coe et al., 1 which reports two sets of six hazard ratios—two for each of the three treatment arms with and without a booster session. Without correcting for multiple tests, two of the six confidence intervals without covariate controls narrowly exclude a hazard ratio of 1.0 and one of the adjusted results exclude 1. However, with six comparisons, a 99.16% confidence interval needs to be used for each set of six estimates to have only a 5% chance of one of the confidence intervals falsely excluding 1 by chance. Making this correction, the confidence interval for the Speed + Booster arm without adjustment for covariates expands to include 1.0, and the interval for the Reasoning + Booster arm without correction expands to ≈ (0.60, 1.05). Similarly, the single covariate‐adjusted interval that excludes 1.0 (again Speed + Booster) expands to ≈ (0.55, 1.02). In other words, if the authors wished to maintain a 5% family‐wise error rate across each set of six tests, none of the individual hazard ratios would be statistically distinguishable from 1.0.

Sign tests tell a different story

The twelve hazard ratios presented in Table 3 in Coe et al. 1 are all computed from independent groups and are therefore statistically independent allowing a sign test of two hypotheses. That table reports hazard ratios for the three main training arms stratified by four baseline age groups—twelve independent estimates in total. Every hazard ratio is < 1.0, indicating a protective effect in every subgroup. While all the individual confidence intervals include 1, a sign‐test of whether all treatments have no effect can be done by computing the probability of observing all hazard ratios being < 1. That probability is P = 1/212≈0.00025. Even under a more generous null hypothesis in which one stipulates that one of the three training types is genuinely effective but the other two are not, the remaining eight estimates would still each have only a P = 1/28≈0.0039 chance of falling < 1.0.

First, a 4‐degree‐of‐freedom Wald test of the global null hypothesis that all hazard ratios equal 1 (i.e., μ = β = δ2 = δ3 = 0) is rejected (Q = 9.77, df = 4). This confirms that the pattern in Table 3 is incompatible with the hypothesis that cognitive training has no effect on dementia risk. Second, a 2‐degree‐of‐freedom Wald test of the hypothesis that the three training types have equal effects after adjusting for age (δ2 = δ3 = 0) yields a test statistic of only Q = 0.15, providing no evidence of differential effects across Memory, Speed, and Reasoning. The three training types behave statistically indistinguishably once we account for the age gradient. This does not mean that the δ coefficients do equal zero. The confidence intervals on the estimates are wide, allowing for a range of effects.

DISCUSSION

Failure to reject the null hypothesis does not mean that an effect is absent; all the reported confidence intervals for hazard rates in Coe et al. 1 include values consistent with substantial protective effects. To support a claim of little or no effect, one would need point estimates close to 1 accompanied by tight confidence intervals—conditions not met in the 20‐year follow‐up. Indeed, inspection of the hazard ratios in the bottom half of Table 2 in Coe et al. 1 shows that the estimates are neither centered near 1 nor estimated with sufficient precision to rule out meaningful benefit.

Despite the pattern of results discussed here, the published analysis emphasizes a single treatment arm because it is the only one that achieves nominal significance both with and without covariate adjustment. Although the preregistered design may have constrained the structure of the original analysis, the interpretation of the results need not be limited in the same way. A broader view of the evidence suggests that the pattern of hazard ratios may be consistent with a common underlying training effect than with differential efficacy across treatment types. Restricting interpretation to a single significant arm has contributed to a narrative that may already be influencing practice, even though the full set of results points toward a different conclusion.
