Assessment and management of arsenic contamination in groundwater from Sanso, Ghana: Health risk implications, source identification, adsorption and immobilization for safe disposal
Authors: Michael Ankapong, Gerheart Winfred Ashong, Albert Amatey Adjaottor, Boansi Adu Ababio, Edward Ebow Kwaansa-Ansah, Mandela Toku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArsenic contamination in groundwater poses global health risks, but safe removal and disposal remain a significant concern for groundwater owners in developing countries. This study aimed to evaluate the health risks, sources, and concentration of arsenic species in groundwater from Sanso, Ghana, and further synthesize and characterize magnetic activated carbon (Fe-CAC) for arsenic removal, and solidify and stabilize the arsenic loaded onto Fe-CAC using cement, nickel oxide, and eggshell waste (AFCNE). Groundwater samples were collected from the Sanso community in Ghana. Arsenic speciation was determined using standard analytical methods and a flame atomic absorption spectrophotometer. Scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction analysis, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis were used to characterize the materials. The findings revealed that mean concentrations of total arsenic (15.463 μg/L) and arsenite (13.645 μg/L) did not conform to the WHO guideline value of 10 μg/L. The multivariate analysis revealed impacts of illegal mining activities, geological influences, and geochemical conditions strongly influencing arsenic enrichment and transport in aquifers. Children showed a higher chronic daily exposure to arsenic than adults, with oral exposure being significantly higher than dermal exposure. The cumulative carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk exceeded 10−4 and 1, respectively, indicating a high potential for skin cancer, bronchitis, and skin lesions. The Fe-CAC adsorption minimized arsenic concentrations from 15.463 to 0.0302 μg/L, accomplishing a removal efficiency of 99.769 %, proving its effectiveness in treating arsenic in groundwater. The leaching test showed a significant reduction in arsenic release, ranging from 1.32 to 0.20 μg/L below the USEPA limit of 5 μg/L, indicating AFCNE long-term stability in immobilizing arsenic and its suitability for safe landfill disposal. This study suggests the need for integrated remediation strategies for long-term arsenic mitigation, continuous monitoring, community awareness, and swift implementation of effective water policy interventions for sustainable groundwater quality.Keywords: Arsenic, Health risk, Adsorption, Stabilization, Safe waste disposal, Ghana

Introduction

Ghana is facing an upsurge of critical global health crises significantly associated with groundwater contamination from arsenic due to anthropogenic activities and non-compliant waste disposal practices, which are primarily attributed to inadequate enforcement of waste management policies [108]. Higher levels of arsenic in groundwater have been reported in areas using hand-dug wells, boreholes, and other mechanized water systems, particularly in underdeveloped countries where monitoring and proper water treatment are lacking [103]. Some countries such as Mexico, China, South Africa, and Ghana also provide significant evidence of arsenic contamination in groundwater for homeowners [44], [49], [59], [6], [60], [81], [84], [85], [86], [97].

The associated health implications from short and long-term exposure to arsenic contamination in drinking water supply systems from these affected countries cause peripheral vascular disease called black foot disease, lung disease, kidney, urinary system, and skin defects, and induce hypertension accompanied by clearly visible premalignant skin lesions, injury to the hemoglobin vessels of the lower limbs that consequently resulted in progressive and liberal gangrene to individuals depending on those systems [2], [37], [58], [94], [95].

In Ghana, currently, arsenic contamination in groundwater aquifers purportedly began about 29 years ago, particularly in mining regions such as Obuasi, Tarkwa, and Bolgatanga as well as several parts of Ghana, primarily due to illegal mining of rocks, geogenic contamination, depth and length of residence time of aquifer [12], [16], [22], [33], [4], [5], [62], [71], [86], [91], [92].

These Ghanaian communities impacted by arsenic contamination report levels as high as 440 µg/L, exceeding the maximum WHO regulatory guideline value of 10 µg/L, posing reduced agricultural activities, social stigma, instability, economic inequalities, and significant health challenges to local communities reliant on contaminated groundwater for domestic and irrigation activities.

Sanso is a Ghanaian settlement in the Obuasi municipal district of the Ashanti region that revolves around illegal mining, agriculture, and informal trading, which are integral to the local economy. Due to water scarcity and security, Sanso residents greatly rely on groundwater, other than pipe-borne water from Ghana Water Company Limited, for domestic and agricultural activities, serving as a primary source of drinking water and irrigation for agriculture. The socioeconomic activities of the Sanso community are likely to be at risk of environmental degradation, poverty, and high cost of living from arsenic contamination that may inhibit the quality of agricultural produce, human health, and destroy biodiversity.

Previous research indicates that groundwater around mine tailings in Sanso has been extensively affected by arsenic contamination, with concentrations ranging from 27 to 297 μg/L [16], [17], [19]. Given these alarming concentrations above regulatory thresholds, primarily associated with bronchitis, skin diseases, and cancers, based on the community's reliance on contaminated groundwater for farming and domestic purposes [16]. There is a pressing need for comprehensive assessment and management strategies to control and mitigate arsenic contamination based on these earlier reports. These actions are in line with Sustainable Development Goal 6, which seeks to provide clean water access to all, improve water quality by 2030, and subsequently protect the environmental and public health of the community.

The detrimental health implications associated with arsenic exposure on human health include carcinogenic risk, such as skin, lung, throat, and colon cancers, and non-carcinogenic risks such as skin lesions, cognitive deficiency, bronchitis, heart and respiratory diseases [16], [29], [94], [95].

Cumulative and individual carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk probabilities, such as hazard quotients (HQ) and cancer risk (CR) greater than their recommended reference doses, in addition to Monte Carlo simulations are common quantitative methodologies for estimating potential long-term health risks rather than immediate health impacts that necessitate monitoring and protective actions to mitigate the exposure [10], [13], [15], [45], [91], [93].

Bivariate and multivariate techniques are essential statistical methods used to analyze multiple variables and simultaneously to identify potential sources and distribution patterns, relationships, and associations of arsenic species contamination in groundwater [10], [13], [14], [15], [7]. Commonly utilized multivariate approaches in research include principal component analysis (PCA), factor analysis (FA), Pearson’s correlation, hierarchical cluster analysis or Dendrograms (HCA) for source apportionment involved in arsenic enrichment in groundwater which can subsequently aid actionable insights to formulate policy for monitoring, mitigate exposure and remediation plans for protecting public health [10], [13], [14], [15], [64], [7].

Adsorption techniques are widely used for the efficient and adaptable removal of arsenic from contaminated water, outperforming other technologies like precipitation, lime softening, and membrane methods [27], [31], [53], [69], [80], [82], [83], [90]. Several studies showed that using modified adsorbents such as zerovalent iron, iron-impregnated activated carbon, Nickel oxide and zirconium oxide coatings, magnetite nickel/nickel boride coated nutshell cigarette soot activated carbon modified with magnetite nanoparticles showed high removal efficiencies (>90) against arsenic and arsenate species in groundwater and wastewater using a critical pH above 7.5, indicating that iron and its modified precursors played an instrumental role in increasing the surface area and adsorption performance of the adsorbent [107], [110], [21], [28], [32], [42], [66], [69], [77], [80], [99]. However, these reports also provided enough limitations that affected the removal efficiency of arsenic and arsenate such as the use of activated carbon as the only adsorbent, acidic groundwater, raw nutshell or agrowaste indicating that without modified adsorbents the removal of arsenic will be ineffective forscale up and unfavorable towards their removal [21], [42], [66], [69], [80], [99].

Solidification is a process that encapsulates hazardous waste, creating a strong, solid material and restricting mobility by reducing surface area and coating it with low-permeability composites. Stabilization techniques involve chemical changes to reduce hazardous waste material leachability, immobilize solubility and bioavailability, and suppress toxic product release into the environment. Solidification and Stabilization (S/S) techniques are used simultaneously in waste treatment for waste minimization and treatment. Portland cement, limestone, lime, pozzolan, fly ash, blast slag, naturally enhanced clays, ferrous sulfate, and ferric salts are commonly used as binders and chemicals to stabilize arsenic-contaminated soils and wastes [8], [96]. In S/S or encapsulation methods, mostly the bonded material in the matrix must be hard to squeeze and inhibit the surface or porous sites that will disable desorption or leaching to occur. Research shows eggshell waste, when combined with activated blast furnace slag or cement, can effectively and innovatively serve as a binding material or stabilizer for arsenic-contaminated soil [20], [23]. Lime usage reduced exchangeable calcium from 29.3 to 7.8 mg/kg, while alkaline pH enhanced the critical reduction of arsenic species exposure in soil [35], [50]. Ferrous salts show long-term stability in slowed arsenic leaching or suppression for a life span of a year, with ferric salt being superior to lime [52]. Chinese researchers also used ferric and ferrous chlorides separately to stabilize arsenic-polluted solid wastes [101], [9]. Fly ash, along with polymers, cement, clays, pozzolans, and ground blast slag, demonstrated promising results in treating and stabilizing arsenic waste for approximately 4 years, enhancing its solidification and stabilization properties. In this study, while cement may serve as a binder, its additives, such as eggshells and nickel oxides, contain calcium hydroxide, carbonates, oxides, and other aggressive minerals that effectively retain or bind arsenic, preventing their release or migration for a long time. However, the eggshells' waste was used in place of limestone since they contain relatively copious amounts of calcium carbonate to suppress the mobility of arsenic in solution, since it was very cheap waste and readily available, and biodegradable. The nickel oxide was also used as a stabilizer to create a competitive adsorption sites in the matrix for surface interaction, hence decreasing the affinity for arsenic mobility and release from the matrix. The arsenic laden magnetic carbon incorporated with metallic non-corrosive nickel oxide, eggshells in addition to the cement will provide greater advantages in high compatibilization and resistance critically dependent on pH impact on the arsenic-incorporated material.

The toxicity characteristic leaching procedure (TCLP) is used to decide whether the solidified and stabilized waste can be discharged into an engineered landfill without harming the ecosystem through leaching [36]. The TCLP method has been frequently used to evaluate stabilization methods [109], [36]. Long-term stabilization tests are needed to determine if waste can be safely disposed of in a non-hazardous landfill site for a long time. The United States Environmental Protection Agency Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (USEPA - TCLP) and the California Waste Extraction Test (WET) are chemical assessments used to assess the toxicity of stabilized arsenic material in landfill conditions (pH 6.5–9.5) [76], [79], [9]. The mass to volume ratio of 1:10 should be avoided and a higher mass used for assessing arsenic leachability and mobility for longer periods than the 18-hour guidelines set by TCLP, KSLT, and WET did not give any realistic leaching [109], [30], [78], [88]. However the TCLP test have certain strong shortcomings such as no shaking during experiments and short contact time in any acidic pH which does not favor ion exchange reaction of arsenic, does not provide enough holistic valuation exercise to ascertain if the waste is hazardous to the environment, whereby this study seeks to fill in those gaps by reinforcing the technique with robust and rigorous factors.

The lack of integrated studies combining health risk assessment, source identification using multivariate analysis, and adsorption and immobilization strategies of arsenic in groundwater has been underexplored and absent. While prior studies have focused entirely on measuring elemental concentrations and health risk assessments, there seems to be no comprehensive investigations that superficially incorporate measuring arsenic contamination, assessing health risks, identifying sources of pollution using multivariate statistical tools, and mitigation and immobilization strategies of arsenic species in groundwater within the Sanso community. In addition some studies have reliably reported on policy suggestion on mitigation strategies in rivers sources [15], however this is the only study that seeks to implement cost effective adsorptive remediative approach and also establish a pilot waste immobilization techniques to control arsenic release into the environment to cater for safe and less costly and efficient disposal technologies. Furthermore, this study emphasizes on the innovative and groundbreaking multifaceted strategy that not only evaluates the arsenic concentrations and health risks in groundwater but also identifies the origins of arsenic enrichment in aquifers using sophisticated statistical tools and utilizes a cost efficient management strategy geared towards solving pollution crisis in a regional context or areas facing similar crisis. Lastly, other studies have also reported on running their leaching tests for as low as 16–18 h and as far as 3–5 days, in acidic and basic environments and low solid to volume ratio [48], [51], [56], however this study reconcentrates and reassess all these shortcomings and it is the first to conduct a 720 h based combined leaching tests on a stabilized arsenic material for longer days, using a high solid-to-volume ratio, and shaking the mixture in a basic environment to ensure a more realistic data is achieved. This study aims to fill the identified gaps by providing an effective integrated analysis which is crucial for developing and implementing pollution control and mitigation measures towards elemental contamination in groundwater and informing policy decisions to protect and preserve public health in the Sanso community and other regional areas facing similar crisis.

Materials and methods

Description of the study site

Sanso (Latitude: 6.1547 ° N and Longitude: −1.70314 ° W) is a Ghanaian settlement in the Obuasi municipality of the Ashanti region (Fig. 1). It is surrounded by sedimentary and igneous rocks and mountains with an abundance of gold ore. Sanso is in the tropical evergreen rainforest zone. Small-scale mining and farming activities are prevalent residential activities of inhabitants within the community. Well and borehole water are the major sources of potable water in the community. The samples were collected from drinking water outlets in May 2022.

Map of the study area.

A total of 30 groundwater samples from ten sampling sites were selected for arsenic analysis. While considering practical constraints, seven of the twenty boreholes identified within the study area were non-operational, while others had severe water shortage limiting the number of usable sampling points. Furthermore, because of the groundwater proximity with nonoperational aquifers and water shortage limitations, samples selected fairly represent the groundwater conditions in the neighborhood.

Sampling

Polyethylene sample bottles were washed with mild soap and tap water, rinsed with distilled water, soaked in a 0.1 M nitric acid water bath for 24 h, and sterilized in an incubator. The samples were introduced into thoroughly washed 500 mL polyethylene bottles, stored in an ice pack, and sealed immediately to prevent contamination. Samples were transported in a cool and dark ice pack container and stored in a refrigerator at 4 ℃ in the Materials Engineering Laboratory (KNUST) before analysis. All samples collected in the vicinity were well labelled.

Determination of total arsenic, arsenite, and arsenate concentrations

The groundwater samples were taken to the Soil Research Institute Laboratory at Kwadaso in Kumasi for acid digestion and determination of arsenite, arsenate, and total arsenic concentration by use of Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) (Agilent 240 FS AAS VGA, U.S.A). The total arsenic concentration was determined by adding 5 mL of the water sample to 5 mL of a 1:1 ratio of 96 % nitric- 37 % hydrochloric acid mixture and heating it to boiling. After that, 2.5 mL of 96 % nitric acid was added to complete the digestion process. The clear solution was topped up to the 25 mL mark with water and filtered with Whatman 0.45 µm filter paper for the determination of total arsenic concentration [25], [46]. The arsenite concentration was determined by adding 2 % nitric acid solution to a 20 mL water sample, and the acidified water solution was filtered using an ashless Whatman 0.25 µm filter paper. The arsenate content was then determined by deducting the quantity of arsenite measured from the total arsenic concentration. The limit of detection (LOD) for As was 0.02 μg/L.

Health risk assessment

A health risk assessment serves as an indicator to understand the potential adverse health effects that can occur when local individuals are exposed to the harmful impacts of arsenic in groundwater over a period [19]. The health risk evaluation was done by quantifying the level of exposure as expressed by the carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health risks [13], [14], [15]. The oral water intake pathway considered in this study was the most probable route of exposure to adults and children commonly exposed to the harmful impacts of arsenic in contaminated groundwater [13], [14], [15], [40], [7].

Oxidation of arsenite to arsenate

For the successful removal of arsenite from water samples, it was best to effectively convert the arsenite into arsenate by oxidation. The pH of the water samples was increased to a pH of 8.0 by adding dropwise amounts of 1 % Na2CO3 followed by the oxidation with 2 mL of 1 M KMnO4 for every 50 mL of water sample for 15 min of contact time to kill germs and bacteria and catalyze the oxidation process [87].

Synthesis of iron-doped activated carbon

Commercial activated carbon (CAC) purposely for water treatment was crushed and sieved using a 90 µm mesh. It was washed with distilled water and dried for two hours in an oven (Heraeus T6 D-63450, Germany) at 110 °C to expel any moisture from the substrate. A mass of 20 g of the commercial activated carbon was immersed in 250 mL of 1 M KMnO4 and stirred using a magnetic stirrer (Heidolph MR 3001 K, Germany) at 700 rpm for 20 min for pre-treatment to remove any impurities. The pretreated mixture was then diluted with 250 mL of distilled water and allowed to stand; the supernatant was decanted and filtered. A mass of 19 g of FeSO4 was dissolved in 250 mL of 1 M H2SO4 and then added to the oxidized activated carbon mixture. The mixture was then stirred at 700 rpm on a magnetic stirrer for 8 h. After stirring, the suspension was allowed to stand, and the supernatant was decanted. The solid residue collected was thoroughly washed with 1 % Na2CO3. The mixture was then soaked in 1 % Na2CO3 for 15 h to neutralize the acid while being consistently stirred with a magnetic stirrer. The supernatant was then decanted. The solid iron-doped commercial activated carbon was washed several times with distilled water until a neutral pH was obtained. The black, shiny solid residue was kept in an oven at 110 °C for up to 9 h until a constant weight was achieved and named Fe-CAC [82], [83]. Characterization

The magnetically-activated carbon and the encapsulated material were characterized using sophisticated tools such as FTIR, SEM, and XRD.

The FTIR analyses under a scanning range of 4000–400 cm−1 helped to establish the functional groups present on the carbon. It also provided evidence of the incorporation of iron onto the surface of the carbon and the efficiency of the encapsulation technique.

Encapsulation of arsenic loaded onto Fe-CAC

To encapsulate, solidify, and stabilize the arsenic incorporated onto the Fe-CAC, firstly, a mass of 5 g nickel oxide was dissolved in 50 mL of 4 M sulphuric acid solution. Next, a mass of 15 g of arsenic-incorporated material (As-Fe-CAC) was added to the solution and stirred for 5 min, followed by the addition of 20 g of cement and stirred for an additional 5 min. Lastly, a mass of 10 g of powdered eggshells dissolved in 30 mL distilled water was introduced under stirring to the mixture for 15 h to achieve uniform mixing. 1 M calcium hydroxide solution was used to adjust the pH to 8. After a pasty mixture was formed, it was transferred into a wooden mold with dimensions 12 cm in height and 10 cm × 10 cm in length to obtain a solidified composite after 3 days. The solidified material was then ground and sieved to particle sizes below 125 μm for leachate test analysis. The stabilized material was identified as AFCNE.

Toxicity characteristic leaching procedure

The batch toxicity characteristic leaching procedure (TCLP) or leaching test was carried out by dispersing 12 g of the stabilized material (AFCNE) in 50 mL of distilled water under stirring at a speed of 80 rpm while using 5 mL of 1 M calcium hydroxide solution to bring the mixture to a pH of 8 to activate arsenic leaching. At acidic pH, As will be reduced, deactivating the As ions' mobility in solutions. This TCLP test was conducted for 30 days, with the extraction of leachate every three days for arsenic analysis. A maximum acceptable amount of toxic waste leachate by TCLP analysis is a threshold of 5 μg/L for safe disposal of stabilized material [9]. In alkaline circumstances (pH 8 and higher), arsenic species are more likely to desorb at a faster rate from the AFCNE composite and discharge into the solution [109], [30], [48], [56], [76], [78], [79], [87], [88], [9]. Given this response, further environmental variability assessments is not necessary to confirm that our immobilization method is practically applicable.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics, such as the means, 95 % confidence interval, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum arsenic concentrations and risk assessments, were estimated for each sample when applied in Microsoft Excel 2019. The coefficient of variation (CV) was computed using a ratio of the standard deviation to mean of arsenic species measured as a percentage. A larger CV% (>10 %) shows greater variability in groundwater quality, implying that various human activities influence significantly to arsenic contamination.

Effect size metrics such as the Cohens d was used to extrapolate how much the measured or estimated values (arsenic concentrations and all risk assessment models) exceed their maximum contaminant levels and tolerable safe limits in practicality, beyond just the statistical significance. Cohen's d is defined as the difference between two means (observed and reference value) divided by a standard deviation for the data. The effect size help quantify how significant the exceedance is rather than stating its above the maximum permissible limit. The Cohen's d was used to quantify the magnitude exceedance and suggest if immediate actions need to be taken. The Cohen's d was interpreted as d < 0.2, implying minimum effect, 0.2 ≤ d < 0.5 imply moderate effects, and d ≥ 0.8 implies high effect.

Using Origin Pro 2022 SR1, the wards technique, sum of squares distances, and correlation-based distances were used to calculate hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) for the arsenic species and sampling locations. The z-scores normalization at N (0,1) was employed for clustering observations based on the sample sites [15].

Pearson's correlation was used to investigate correlations between arsenic species and potential causative factors of arsenic pollution. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) method was utilized to compress an extensive data set into components to estimate the source of groundwater pollution, whether it comes from natural or anthropogenic sources. The HCA approach was used to determine the degree of similarity between the concentrations of arsenic species and to identify the potential sources of contaminants through tree grouping that deteriorate groundwater quality at various sampling locations. Pearson's correlation, HCA, and PCA were analyzed using OriginPro 2022 SR1.

To compare the mean concentrations of arsenic in water samples, a one-sample T-test was utilized at a significant level of 0.05. The one-sample t-test was a test to determine whether, at a significant level of 5 % (α = 0.05), the mean arsenic concentration is significantly greater or less than the maximum residual value of 5 μg/L. By employing the one sample t-test statistics and confidence levels, it provides evidence as to whether to accept or reject the alternative hypothesis, which suggests that the mean concentration of arsenic greater than 5 μg/L (maximum residual value) (HA > 5), in opposition to the null hypothesis, which suggests a mean concentration of arsenic less than or equal to 5 (HO ≤ 5) [13], [14], [15].

Finally, the data obtained from the statistical analysis were compared to the maximum residual limit or standards to ascertain significantly whether they are greater than or less than the standard observations and provide significant and practical observations and implications to this study.

X-ray diffraction

Fig. 3A shows the XRD diffractogram of highly amorphous Fe-CAC. The pattern revealed a broad peak at a 2 θ value of 24.8º corresponding to the hkl lattice plane (001) of the graphitic nature of activated carbon as support for the Fe for Fe-CAC, which also confirms the presence of oxides of iron [41], [68]. The rough peak at a 2 θ value of 24.8º amongst other peaks signifies that the Fe-CAC composite is more amorphous. The presence of iron at a 2 θ value of 43º, which corresponds to the reflection plane of 111 of magnetite, implies successful impregnation of Fe on the surface of the activated carbon [38], [41] according to the pattern COD 9014476. The average crystallite size (D.nm) of Fe on the modified composite of Fe-CAC was 14 nm, which is an indication of high amounts of CAC and less amounts of Fe used during the modification of the composite [34].

XRD results of (A) Fe-CAC and (B) AFCNE.

Fig. 3 shows the mineralogical phases of highly crystalline AFCNE. The smooth and sharp diffraction peaks confirm the crystallinity of the AFCNE material. The material contains an abundance of minerals such as bassanite, albite, quartz, phengite 3 T, rutile, arsenolite, and bunsenite. The XRD pattern of monoclinic bassanite (CaHO4.5S) was characterized by intense diffraction peaks at 14.74°, 25.68°, 29.73°, and 31.95°, corresponding to the COD 9012209 pattern of hkl planes of 110, 310, 220, and 114. The XRD generated peak positions (2θ) and a hkl plane of hexagonal quartz (O2Si) at 26.45° (011) according to the COD pattern 500003 and a tetrahedral phengite 3 T at 8.80° (003) according to the COD pattern 1100017, respectively. The XRD demonstrated the principal presence of cubic bunsenite (NiO) characterized by weak diffraction peaks and hkl planes at 37.34° (111) in correspondence to the COD 9008693 pattern. XRD results confirmed the presence of cubic arsenolite (As2O3), triclinic albite, and tetragonal rutile (O2Ti0.992) phases at 27.92° (222), 28.01° (002), and 27.45° (110), according to COD 9002201, 9009780, and 9001681 patterns, respectively.

It is evident from XRD analysis that after encapsulation, the presence of albite, arsenolite, phengite, and bunsenite showed weak peaks. The findings of the mineralogical phase analysis shed light on these observations, which highlight the significant impact of the encapsulation procedure and materials used during the synthesis of the material on the mineral composition of the AFCNE material.

Based on the statistical analysis, the mean arsenite (13.645 μg/L) and total arsenic (15.463 μg/L) concentrations were statistically greater than (+ve t-stats (16.204 and 19.898), t-critical (1.699) < t-stats (16.204 and 19.898)) the recommended acceptable range of 5 μg/L [70]. In addition, the one sample t-test showed that total arsenic and arsenite concentrations were statistically different (p = 9.43E-19 and 2.24E-16 < 0.05) among all groundwater samples investigated, between the sample mean and their maximum contaminant level.

The Cohen’s d of 2.958 and 3.633 in arsenite and total arsenic variations > 0.8 suggests that the chemical forms of arsenic is much higher than the maximum contaminant level, indicating high non-conformity reinforcing urgent intervention for treatment before usage which can further cause chronic health consequences due to long term exposure.

The WHO and other regulatory bodies do not explicitly provide guideline values for chemical species of arsenic, such as arsenate.

However, the mean arsenic concentration (15.463 μg/L) obtained from Sanso community groundwater in our study was below the maximum contaminant level of 25 μg/L in groundwater, Mexico [29], 27.10 μg/L in groundwater, Sanso [19], 297 μg/L in groundwater from the Obuasi Municipality of Ghana [17], 50 μg/L in groundwater, China [100], and 6150 μg/L in groundwater, South Africa [1], where the degree of aquifer overexploitation and evaporation, depth of aquifer, illegal gold mining activities, old tailing dams, and old waste dumps may have influenced the discrepancy of the results.

The high concentrations of arsenic indicate groundwater unsuitability for domestic and drinking purposes as bioaccumulation of arsenic are prevalent. These high concentrations suggest a need for improved water quality management and treatment before its use for drinking and domesticated purposes. These high levels of arsenic tend to worsen and accumulate in long-term posing significant health consequences and gradually disrupting the proper functioning of the body.

Adsorption efficiency of arsenic removal from groundwater

The effectiveness of arsenic adsorption from Sanso groundwater under fixed conditions was summarized in Table 1. The removal concentration and removal efficiency after adsorption was presented in Table 1. The removal efficiency of chemical species of arsenic ranged between 99.769 % and 99.792 % while the average concentration of arsenite and total arsenic 13.645 and 15.463 μg/L was reduced drastically to 0.0163 and 0.0302 μg/L respectively. The high removal percentage indicates strong performance against arsenic contamination using Fe-CAC, indicating that similar regional or world wide areas seeking to device innovative means of arsenic removal for drinking water and domestic purposes can rely on our novel material for arsenic abatement in their public or private water supplies. Similar studies from Turkey and China reported a 100 % removal yield from the removal of 20 μg/L arsenic [42], [77]. After the adsorption studies, the arsenic concentrations were lower than the maximum acceptable arsenic contaminant level of 5 μg/L for US groundwater owners [70] and the WHO provisional guideline of 10 μg/L [104], indicating that the adsorption treatment made groundwater wholesome for drinking and domestic purposes.

Optimum leaching or significant release of arsenic was achieved at a concentration of 0.2 μg/L, on the 12th day. After the 12th day, the arsenic concentration immobilized in the network of several suppressants was not able to leach or allow the escape of arsenic beyond any significant concentration. Health implications of arsenic in groundwater

The purpose of analyzing health risks from arsenic in groundwater is to systematically identify and construct a baseline database on the prevalence of alarming health consequences associated with short and long-term exposure. Based on the findings of this study, arsenic species trigger severe health implications due to their occurrence in groundwater. Arsenic and arsenite concentrations extremely and significantly exceeded the maximum contaminant level of 5 µg/L and the WHO guideline value of 10 µg/L, indicating that the exposure to individuals poses potential health risks and indicates that remediation measures used in our pilot study should be commercialised and integrated to augment saddled or unavailable clean-up efforts. Despite the fact that arsenate should growing levels due to high bioavailability and occurrence, they are easily absorbed in the digestive tract via oral route which can exacerbate the acute and chronic toxicity in addition to its co-existing species of arsenite and arsenic. However, arsenate even though documented as a less toxic form than arsenic can metabolize into arsenite in the gastrointestinal tract competes with phosphate metabolism, and further inhibits cellular functioning and development. Based on the statistical validity and practical insights into the risk assessment studies, it can be solidly established that short-term exposure to arsenic and other undisclosed contaminants in groundwater induces acute toxicity such as bronchitis, muscle weakness, fatigue, diabetes, keratosis, skin diseases, palpitations, and cholera, in addition to other health consequences that may act as another illness. Based on the high exceedance of the carcinogenic reference dose in both adults and children and local populations, there are likely challenging chronic toxicity consequences such as liver, kidney, liver, colon, prostate, skin, lung, and throat cancers that may go unnoticed due to continuous exposure. However, hypertension commonly known as BP, poor brain function, heart burns, memory loss, takutsobo cardiomyopathy, and many other developmental challenges in children may reflect in the lives of individuals exposed to the chronic carcinogenic effects of arsenic and other undisclosed contaminants in groundwater from Sanso community.

Thirdly the statistical robustness and validity strongly indicate that children are extremely vulnerable groups to the acute and chronic toxicity of arsenic in groundwater although adults are also at high short and long-term risk, establishing the fact that even though children are likely to suffer critically than adults. These children suffer due to their low body weight, smaller body size, inadequate and compromised immunity, and organs for metabolism and excretion of contaminants. This study builds a likelihood of a qualitative approach to assign chronic impairment to foetus growth, birth defects from pregnant women, aged adults, and immunocompromised individuals (individuals with underlying health conditions) will continually be exposed to severe health challenges of acute and chronic treatable and untreatable diseases enhancing the mortality rate.

Finally enhancing public health initiatives and frequent medical screening by environmental health and safety of the municipal district and local health organizations within the vicinity in arsenic – exposed and unexposed individuals are crucial to minimize and reduce the negative health consequences. Nutritional supplements in the form of health aid such as selenium, and folic acid amongst many other affordable supplemental tablets are key drugs under the recommendation of renowned physician to mitigate and control arsenic pollution and enhance cell proliferation, development, metabolism and immune system fortification in humans. However the reduction of arsenic species by the adsorption method primarily minimized the accumulation of arsenic in groundwater from the Sanso community which when practically used may avoid adverse health effects in the future.

Mitigation strategies and policy implications for public health

This study highlights the pressing need for arsenic mitigation techniques, including water treatment systems, routine water quality monitoring, and community awareness initiatives, to inhibit the sources of pollution into groundwater sources in the studied area. To minimize exposure above recommended levels and address the pollution circumstance, Ghanaian authorities, including the Ghana Water Company, Ghana Environmental Protection Agency, National Commission for Civic Education and Water Resources Commission, must implement more restrictive regulations on sources of pollution and illegal mining activities within the study area, promoting community awareness to mitigate arsenic contamination in drinking water sources. Developing economically feasible treatment technologies and sustainable, clean water supplies should be the main goals of long-term plans to reduce arsenic pollution and minimize further adverse effects on health. In addition if without government assistance to clean water sustainable goal in rural areas or areas that are pain striken by severe contamination, residents can join resources in engaging strong pollution remediation experts and organizations to assist them in providing safe and clean water to residents. Policies on public health should encourage cooperation and community involvement with regional stakeholders. To increase public knowledge of the potential risks of arsenic exposure and the significance of providing impacted populations with access to safe drinking water, awareness campaigns by the National Civic Council for Education and health education programs by the Ministry of Health Ghana are essential. Groundwater arsenic levels must be continuously monitored and evaluated to assess the effectiveness of treatments and track potential risks. The creation of strong surveillance systems is necessary for long-term control of arsenic pollution. Finally, as continuous monitoring has been suggested, there is also a need to ensure a variety of cleanup techniques on site to complement the excellent removal process of water in this study.

Limitations and recommendations of this study

The focus of this study was limited to a particular area inside the Obuasi municipality, mainly because of its widespread relationship to ongoing illegal mining operations. Further investigations should emphasize broadening their focus to include the entire municipality because of its ongoing struggle with illegal mining activities. Seven of the twenty-three boreholes identified were damaged and rendered inoperable. This restriction highlights the need for extensive infrastructure evaluations and preservation, which could improve the general availability and representation of data collection. The study's funding constraints restricted the collection of data, potentially reducing understanding of contamination profiles and large sample sizes. Future research should devote more resources to a more comprehensive analysis. Given the low level of consumer awareness, organizational involvement is essential for the Sanso community's protection against hazardous contaminants. Future research directions should explore seasonal variations and continuous surveillance of arsenic contamination dynamics are some crucial factors that may impact the findings of this study. Soil and plants within the Sanso community should be screened for all other potentially toxic elements in addition to the physicochemical and microbial assessment of groundwater. Solidification and stabilization of spent arsenic waste can be used for constructing dams and roads, mass concrete production, and other coarse aggregates in construction activities [47], [54]. Other stabilizers such as food waste, wood ash, pozzolanic, and ceramic waste can be used for stabilizing other hazardous contaminants. Landfill leachates have buffering properties that avoid excessive pH fluctuations, making the encapsulation results in our study applicable to real-world applications. Future research might examine longer-term environmental circumstances for variability of wide number of pollutants from different polluted locations. Based on the poor state of the environment and the financial conditions of the community due to lack of sophisticated treatment technologies, absence of environmental health department in the sequestration of polluted water and consistent insurgency of aftermath galamsey rock sediment interaction with the aquifer, there is conscious need to engineer a simple cost effective method such as adsorption while reducing greenhouse gases in the preparation of activated carbon amongst many other sophisticated treatment process to minimize and eliminate the toxic prevalence, bioavailability and occurrence of these toxic elements. This study also acknowledges a critical limitation involving the sensitivity of the flame atomic absorption spectrometer used in analysing the As and PTE concentrations in this study, as well as the limitation of our methodology's inability to accurately speciate arsenic into arsenate and arsenite, noting that we will consider alternative approaches in the future. Based on the limitation in the inadequacy in exploring full adsorption studies to complement the regenerability and reusability of Fe-CAC adsorbent, it is recommended that, future studies should prioritize a comprehensive investigation of the adsorption isotherms, kinetics, thermodynamics and statistical error analysis to further optimize Fe-CACs' performance and environmental reliability on contaminated groundwater.

Conclusion

In most urban and rural areas, groundwater is the primary source of drinking water. This study accurately measured arsenite, total arsenic, and arsenate concentrations in Sanso community groundwater, comparing them to guideline values and maximum contamination limits. This study utilized multivariate statistical methods like PCA, Pearson correlation, and HCA to investigate the origins and relationships of arsenic species in aquifers. A thorough risk assessment was conducted to estimate the potential health risks associated with drinking groundwater contaminated with arsenic, particularly for adults and children in the Sanso community. A novel iron-impregnated commercial activated carbon (Fe-CAC) adsorbent was synthesized, well-characterized using SEM, XRD, and FTIR, and tested for its potential to remove arsenic concentrations from contaminated groundwater for sustainable remediation. This study developed an arsenic encapsulation technique using eggshell waste, nickel oxide, and cement, providing a safe disposal method and long-term immobilization of arsenic-laden waste. The stability of encapsulated arsenic waste was evaluated over 30 days using a modified and intensified Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) to ensure its long-term environmental safety. Arsenic concentrations, health risks, and leaching test findings were evaluated for statistical and practical significance using descriptive and inferential statistical analysis.

The findings of this study indicated that the mean arsenite (13.645 μg/L) and total arsenic (15.463 μg/L) concentrations in groundwater were practically significant and statistically greater than the maximum contaminant level of 5 μg/L. The multivariate analysis indicated strong significant relationships between arsenite and total arsenic species with high source of groundwater enrichment from similar enrichment activities such as illegal mining activities and the reductive dissolution of arsenic-bearing minerals from the aquifer's water–rock table interactions. The chronic daily intake exposure evaluation revealed that oral consumption was the predominant pathway of arsenic exposure through groundwater, followed by skin contact in both adults and children. The average non-carcinogenic (HI) and carcinogenic risks (TCR) for both adults and children exposed to arsenic-contaminated groundwater were significantly and practically greater than the maximum reference doses of 1 and 10−4, indicating a high likelihood of adverse health consequences such as waterborne diseases and organ cancers. Local populations within the Sanso community have a high potential for health and environmental consequences such as birth defects, stunted growth, cholera, takutsobo cardiomyopathy, and prostate, colon, and skin cancer.

The synthesized Fe-CAC adsorbent effectively removed 99.792 % of total arsenic from groundwater samples, resulting in significant concentrations ranging from 15.463 to 0.0302 μg/L, making it suitable for drinking and irrigation purposes. The stabilization and solidification of arsenic-laden waste material (AFCNE) using a modified TCLP test after 30 days showed strong significant and practical suppression of total arsenic within the range of 1.32–0.27 μg/L. Sophisticated characterization such as FTIR, XRD, and SEM/EDX indicated that the impregnation of Fe onto CAC and the solidification and stabilization of AFCNE were successful.

Although this current investigation offers significant baseline information about risk assessment, remediation and immobilization techniques, arsenic contamination, and origins of contamination in groundwater aquifer systems within the Sanso community, certain drawbacks that could have been fully enriched should be addressed. The smaller sample size and collection due to groundwater shortage and inoperability may have influenced the variability and statistical power of the results. Financial constraints and instrumental challenges also influenced the proper representation of the pollution profile of the community. Deterministic health risks utilized did not indicate the capture

Deterministic risk assessments, based on standard exposure assumptions, utilized in this study do not inherently quantify uncertainty, identify vulnerable individuals, or predict individual outcomes like mortality. The leaching test used was under specific conditions for a target analyte, however, further studies should look at varying the contaminant profile and types and validate the effect of the solidification stabilization approach on a wide variety of contaminants. The multivariate analysis for source attribution of arsenic transport and enrichment would further need geochemical modeling and isotopic characterizations to refine the target source influencing the prevalence. The sampling area scope was too small, few monitoring programs for that community were being done, also seasonal and spatial variability of data was not considered, and cost-effective results-oriented remediation and immobilization approach should be considered to offer valuable insights into the understanding and improvement of risk assessment accuracy, mitigation of arsenic contamination and prepare safe guidelines for disposal of waste generated from the process. Alternative remediation techniques to augment what we used in our study can be considered for cleanup of groundwater. Monte Carlo simulations should be used to invigorate deterministic health risk assessments to very vulnerable populations and immunosuppressed

As part of the policy and practical recommendations for environmental safety and public health, strict enforcement of maximum contaminant standards and implementation of consistently regular groundwater monitoring programs in affected communities is crucial. Advocacy for awareness and educational programs on the health implications of drinking water without treatment and clean and pure water consumption practices are crucial. This study suggests a strong scalable Fe-CAC-based water treatment technology for widespread applicability in areas suffering from similar problems. This study also suggests proper waste management of arsenic-laden adsorbents through solidification and stabilization techniques.

Arsenic contamination and non-compliance to regulatory threshold in groundwater pose significant and practical acute and chronic toxicity challenges particularly to children than adults. Based on the health risks estimated, rapid and immediate enforceable trackable actions need to be implemented to restore contaminated areas using our remediation approach, and prevent future dependency and further contamination through bioaccumulation. The bioaccumulation and persistent occurrence of arsenic in aquifers provides a strigent call for action to address this issue immediately and protect public health.

The findings of this study will eventually provide policymakers, non-governmental organizations, and stakeholders with evidence-based recommendations that target fostering of sustainable solutions that address the specific needs of Sanso communities and similar communities. This study confirmed that arsenic leaching was controlled and the stabilization process is economically and environmentally friendly and sustainable for technology transfer. Generally, this work can provide a practically efficient and effective technique for the stabilization and solidification of other hazardous waste. This work can provide a promising solution for both inhabitants and consumers, Ghana Water Company Limited, Environmental protection agency and government officials in the elimination of hazardous contaminants in mechanically urbanized drinking water sources.
