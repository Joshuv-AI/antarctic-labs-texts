Brain network mapping of structural and functional alterations associated with alexithymia
Authors: Leyi Zhang, Xuhan Cui, Yangpan Ou, Yong Liu, Bing Lang, Wenbin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough numerous neuroimaging studies have reported structural and functional brain alterations in alexithymia, the findings have remained heterogeneous. Given the growing recognition that neuropsychiatric symptoms are better characterized by distributed brain networks rather than isolated regions, limited studies have examined alexithymia at the network level. We integrated findings from 43 neuroimaging publications and extracted reported locations showing structural and functional abnormalities related to alexithymia. Using a functional connectivity network mapping (FCNM) approach, these affected brain regions were mapped onto large-scale resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging datasets (n = 872) to identify structural and functional alteration networks associated with alexithymia. Cross-sample spatial correlations were performed between the identified networks and 19 neurotransmitter distribution maps from over 1200 healthy participants. The gray matter volume alteration network was primarily involved in the subcortical network (65.06%), somatomotor network (23.52%), limbic network (19.02%), and ventral attention network (14.13%). The task-induced activation alteration network was mainly involved in the ventral attention network (26.91%), subcortical network (18.06%), and somatomotor network (6.36%). These findings were robust across different methodological parameters and test–retest analyses. In the cross-sample spatial correlation analyses, both alteration networks were associated with serotonin and vesicular acetylcholine transporters. These findings elucidate the network-level neurobiological mechanisms of alexithymia, indicating that individuals with alexithymia may exhibit dysfunction at lower levels of emotional awareness. The observed brain alterations may be linked to serotonergic and vesicular acetylcholine transporter dysfunction. The identified networks may serve as potential targets for therapeutic interventions and biomarkers for predicting treatment response or disease progression.Subject terms: Diagnostic markers, Psychiatric disorders

Introduction

Alexithymia, meaning “lack of words for emotion”, is a personality trait characterized by difficulties in recognizing, understanding, and verbalizing feelings, often accompanied by reduced capacity for imagination and limited emotional experience. Alexithymia affects approximately 9% of the general population [1, 2], with higher prevalence observed in a variety of psychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia [3], major depressive disorder [4–6], and autism spectrum disorder [7, 8]. Moreover, alexithymia individuals demonstrate increased risks of self-harm, suicidal ideation, and suicidal behaviors [9, 10]. Given these widespread clinical associations and serious implications for mental health, investigating the neurobiological underpinnings of alexithymia is of considerable importance.

Recent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have provided potential neurobiological underpinnings for alexithymia by detecting functional and structural abnormalities in individuals with alexithymia compared to those with low alexithymia. However, the findings have been highly heterogeneous. For instance, Ihme et al. observed smaller gray matter volume in the left middle temporal gyrus in individuals with alexithymia compared to controls, whereas Goerlich-Dobre et al. found smaller gray matter volume in the middle cingulum gyrus, hippocampus, and thalamus [11, 12]. Borsci et al. reported smaller gray matter volume in the temporal, frontal gyrus, cuneus, and precuneus [13]. In addition, inconsistent results have been reported for the insula. Borsci et al. observed a smaller insular volume, whereas Goerlich-Dobre et al. reported a larger insular volume in individuals with alexithymia [13, 14]. Task-induced functional MRI (fMRI) studies show similar variability. Even under similar task paradigms, reported alterations differ across investigations [15–17]. Activation of the anterior cingulate gyrus, in particular, has been described as both increased and decreased [16, 18, 19] in individuals with alexithymia relative to those with low alexithymia. Collectively, these discrepancies underscore the substantial heterogeneity of structural and functional findings in alexithymia.

To address the heterogeneity of the neuroimaging results, previous coordinate-based meta-analyses have integrated the existing literature on alexithymia and identified convergent alterations across studies [20, 21]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that psychiatric symptoms are better understood as manifestations of distributed brain network dysfunctions rather than impairments in isolated regions [22]. From this network-based perspective, the heterogeneity observed in alexithymia studies may be explained by the possibility that different affected regions converge within a common network underlying alexithymia. In this context, functional connectivity network mapping (FCNM) has emerged as an innovative method that reflects the shift from region-based to network-based thinking in psychiatric disorders. FCNM maps a disease, symptom, or psychological process onto a common, symptom-specific brain network by integrating multiple sites of structural or functional impairment with large-scale functional connectome data. This approach has been effectively employed to localize a range of neuropsychiatric symptoms and disorders [23–32]. It provides a more integrative understanding of their neuropathology from a network perspective. However, applications of FCNM to alexithymia remain scarce, leaving its functional and structural network substrates largely unexplored.

Here, we aimed to localize structural and functional brain alteration networks associated with alexithymia. First, we summarized previously published literature to identify brain locations with structural (gray matter volume) and functional (task-induced activation) alterations related to alexithymia. Second, to identify the alexithymia-specific network, FCNM was conducted using the human brain connectome derived from functional MRI data of healthy controls. Finally, given evidence that dysfunction in neurotransmitter systems may contribute to the pathogenesis of alexithymia [33], we examined the spatial correlations between the structural and functional networks associated with alexithymia and the neurotransmitter distribution maps derived from over 1200 healthy participants. Study design and analysis pipeline.

We initially synthesized published literature to identify brain structural (gray matter volume) and functional (task-induced activation) alterations related to alexithymia. Based on these extracted brain locations and the large-scale CoRR discovery dataset (n = 872) as well as the CoRR retest dataset (n = 778) of resting-state fMRI data, we applied a functional connectivity network mapping (FCNM) approach to construct structural and functional alteration networks related to alexithymia. Specifically, spheres centered at each coordinate of a given contrast were created and merged to generate a contrast-specific combined seed mask. Second, based on the rs-fMRI data, we computed a seed-to–whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity (FC) map for each participant. Third, subject-level FC maps were entered into a voxel-wise one-sample t-test to identify brain regions functionally connected to each contrast seed. Fourth, the resulting group-level t maps were thresholded and binarized at p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons using a voxel-level false discovery rate (FDR) method. Finally, the binarized maps of gray matter volume and task-induced activation contrasts were overlaid to produce two network probability maps, which were thresholded at 60% to yield the alexithymia-related structural and functional alteration networks. Moreover, cross-sample association analyses were performed between the two network probability maps and neurotransmitter distribution maps derived from previous PET studies covering 19 neurotransmitters in over 1200 healthy participants. Methods

Study search and selection

A comprehensive search of the PubMed and Web of Science databases was conducted to identify all structural (gray matter volume) and functional (task-induced activation) neuroimaging studies related to alexithymia, published in English before August 2025. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420251127803). The detailed selection process of related studies is summarized in Fig. S1, and the full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Methods. In our study, we included both comparison contrasts (i.e., group differences in neuroimaging measures between individuals with alexithymia and those with low alexithymia) and correlation contrasts (i.e., associations between neuroimaging measures and alexithymia severity). The peak voxel coordinates for each contrast were collected, and Talairach coordinates were transformed into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space to ensure consistency.

Discovery and retest datasets

The discovery dataset for this study was drawn from the Consortium for Reliability and Reproducibility (CoRR, https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/CoRR/html/index.html) [34]. To limit the effects of neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration, the study included participants aged 18–60, and sites with fewer than 10 participants were omitted. The discovery sample comprised 872 healthy individuals (423 women; mean 24.55 ± 7.42 years). For test–retest reliability, we used the retest data of the same participants from the CoRR discovery cohort, which included 778 healthy individuals (378 women; mean 24.55 ± 7.45 years). Demographic details for both discovery and retest cohorts are presented in Table S1 and Fig. S2.

fMRI data preprocessing

The fMRI acquisition parameters for both the discovery and retest datasets are summarized in Table S2. Participants with poor image quality or incomplete brain coverage were excluded.

Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using RESTplus software [35] based on the SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The preprocessing pipeline consisted of the following steps: (1) discarding the initial 10 volumes; (2) slice timing correction; (3) realignment for head motion correction, with exclusion of participants showing >2 mm translation or >2° rotation along any axis; (4) spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute template via DARTEL (Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie Algebra), resampled into a 3-mm isotropic voxel; (5) removal of linear trends; (6) regression of nuisance covariates, including Friston 24-parameter head-motion parameters, cerebrospinal fluid, white matter, and global signal [25, 27]; (7) temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz); and (8) spatially smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 × 6 × 6 mm3 full width at half maximum.

Functional connectivity network mapping

The FCNM approach was applied to identify networks associated with alexithymia based on coordinates of brain locations reported in prior studies. First, peak coordinates from significant comparison or correlation contrasts were extracted from the literature. For each contrast, a 4-mm-radius sphere was created around each reported peak, and all spheres were merged to form a contrast-specific seed mask. Second, we computed a contrast seed-to-whole brain FC map for each participant; the mean time series of each seed was correlated with the time series of every voxel across the brain to create whole-brain functional connectivity maps. Correlation coefficients were converted to z scores using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. To reduce inter-site variability, FC maps were harmonized with the ComBat algorithm [36, 37]. Third, the 872 subject-level FC maps were entered into voxel-wise one-sample t-tests to identify regions showing significant positive connectivity with each seed. We restricted the analysis to positive functional connectivity because the physiological significance of negative connectivity remains controversial [26, 38, 39]. Fourth, resulting t maps were adjusted for multiple comparisons using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of p < 0.05. Finally, the corrected maps were binarized and overlaid to generate two probability networks (i.e., gray matter volume and task-induced activation), which were subsequently thresholded at 60%.

Association with canonical brain networks

To better interpret the alteration networks associated with alexithymia, we quantified the voxel-wise overlap between the gray matter volume, task-induced networks, and eight canonical brain networks. Seven cortical networks, visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, limbic, frontoparietal, and default mode, were defined according to Yeo et al. [40]. The subcortical network, encompassing the amygdala, hippocampus, basal ganglia, and thalamus, was defined using the Human Brainnetome Atlas [41].

Validation analyses

Multiple validation analyses were conducted to evaluate the reliability of our findings. First, the FCNM procedure was replicated using the retest dataset to examine result consistency. Second, to investigate the influence of seed size, FCNM analyses were performed using spheres with 2-mm and 6-mm radius. Finally, to assess the effect of threshold selection, the analyses were repeated with 55 and 65% probability cutoffs.

Spatial correlation with neurotransmitters

Receptor and transporter distribution data across brain regions were gathered from existing PET literature, covering 19 neurotransmitters across nine systems from over 1200 healthy participants [42]. Detailed information on these studies was provided in Table S3. The nine different neurotransmitter systems included: dopamine (D1, D2, and DAT), serotonin (5-HT1a, 5-HT1b, 5-5-HT2a, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HTT), glutamate (mGluR5 and NMDA), acetylcholine (α4β2, M1, and VAChT), histamine (H3), opioid receptors (MOR), norepinephrine (NET), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABAa). cannabinoid (CB1). Here, the JuSpace toolbox (https://github.com/juryxy/JuSpace) was used to assess the spatial correlation between neurotransmitter maps and the brain alteration networks associated with alexithymia [43]. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted between the unthresholded network probability maps and neurotransmitter densities across 400 cortical regions defined by the Schaefer-400 parcellation [44]. P values were obtained using spatial permutation–based null models with 5000 permutations, and multiple comparisons were controlled using the FDR correction.

Results

Studies included in the analyses

After a comprehensive literature search and screening, 43 studies met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among these, 30 task-induced studies were identified, including 18 group comparisons (320 individuals with alexithymia and 335 with low alexithymia) and 12 correlation comparisons (438 individuals). In addition, 13 structural studies on gray matter volume were included, comprising 8 group comparisons (178 individuals with alexithymia and 230 with low alexithymia) and 8 correlation comparisons (2170 individuals). Notably, one structural study contained two group comparisons (high-alexithymia vs. low-alexithymia and medium-alexithymia vs. low-alexithymia) [13], and another included three comparisons between individuals with low alexithymia and different alexithymia subtypes, based on the cognitive and affective dimensions of the Bermond–Vorst Alexithymia Questionnaire [12]. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the included studies are provided in Tables S4 and S5.

Gray matter volume alteration network associated with alexithymia

The gray matter volume alteration network associated with alexithymia included the bilateral superior temporal gyrus, insula, putamen, thalamus, hippocampus, anterior cingulate gyrus, fusiform, and middle cingulate gyrus (Fig. 2). In terms of canonical brain networks, this alteration network was primarily involved in the subcortical network (65.06%), somatomotor network (23.52%), limbic network (19.02%), and ventral attention network (14.13%) (Fig. 3).

Task-induced activation alteration network associated with alexithymia

The task-induced activation alteration network associated with alexithymia comprised brain regions including the bilateral superior temporal gyrus, middle cingulate gyrus, thalamus, putamen, insula, and supplementary motor area (Fig. 2). Regarding canonical brain networks, the task-induced activation alteration network mainly involved the ventral attention network (26.91%), the subcortical network (18.06%), and the somatomotor network (6.36%) (Fig. 3).

Validation analyses

First, performing the FCNM analysis on the retest dataset (n = 778) resulted in brain alteration networks comparable to those identified in the discovery cohort. Second, analyses using spheres with 2-mm and 6-mm radius generated networks largely consistent with those obtained using the 4 mm radius spheres. Finally, applying 55 and 65% probability thresholds generated networks comparable to the 60% threshold, with the 65% threshold yielding a more confined spatial distribution. Collectively, these findings confirm the robustness of our results across test–retest analyses and methodological variations.

Spatial correlation with neurotransmitters

Cross-region spatial correlations between neurotransmitter densities and the network probability maps of gray matter volume and task-induced activation revealed significant correlations (permutation-based P < 0.05, FDR corrected). Specifically, the gray matter volume network exhibited positive correlations with serotonin transporter (5-HTT, r = 0.37, p = 0.002), dopamine D2 receptor (D2, r = 0.47, p = 0.033), and vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT, r = 0.60, p = 0.002). The task-induced activation network was positively correlated with 5-HTT (r = 0.21, p = 0.008), VAChT (r = 0.46, p = 0.004), and metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5, r = 0.21, p = 0.046) (Fig. 4, see Table S6 for details).

Discussion

Using the FCNM method, we map alexithymia to structural and functional alteration networks by integrating abnormalities reported in previous studies and using large-scale functional connectome data. Specifically, the gray matter volume alteration network is primarily involved in the subcortical, somatomotor, limbic network, and ventral attention network. Task-induced activation alteration network is mainly involved in the ventral attention, subcortical, and somatomotor networks. Moreover, both networks were associated with specific neurotransmitter systems, including serotonin (5-HTT) and vesicular acetylcholine (VAChT).

We observed that both gray matter volume and task-induced alteration network associated with alexithymia were involved in the subcortical network, with the gray matter volume network being more pronounced. This finding aligns with previous meta-analysis reporting smaller volumes of the left amygdala, caudate, and putamen associated with higher levels of alexithymia [21], as well as reduced amygdala activation during the processing of negative emotional stimuli in individuals with alexithymia [20]. The subcortical network plays a crucial role in generating, processing, and regulating emotions and motivation by integrating interoceptive, reward-related, and threat-related information to form the foundation of emotional experience and behavioral responses. Specifically, the amygdala is considered to detection of emotional significance of external stimuli [45], while the hippocampus contributes to the contextualization and encoding of emotional memories. The thalamus serves as a sensory gateway relaying emotion-related sensory information to the cortex, and its dysfunction may disrupt emotional signal transmission, contributing to impaired emotion recognition reported by previous studies [16, 46]. Given the role in emotional processes of the subcortical network, its dysfunction may hinder the transmission of emotional signals from physiological to cognitive levels, leading to difficulties in perceiving, identifying, and expressing emotions. According to the hierarchical model of emotion proposed by Lane and Schwartz that emotional awareness could be divided into five levels: awareness of physical sensations, action tendencies, single emotions, blends of emotions, and blends of emotional experience [47]. The first two levels primarily involve automatic emotional processes that are rapid, modular, and cognitively impenetrable, with subcortical networks contributing to the generation of these automatic emotional responses. Our findings of alteration network associated with alexithymia suggested individuals with alexithymia may primarily involve dysfunction at the lower level of emotional awareness, namely, the physical or action-oriented stage, where physiological emotional signals fail to be effectively integrated into higher-order cognitive awareness.

The ventral attention network is another network involved in both the gray matter volume and task-induced alteration networks associated with alexithymia. It is primarily composed of the middle cingulate cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, supplementary motor area, and temporoparietal junction in the Yeo-7 network, largely overlapping with the classic salience network [40]. The ventral attention network serves as a critical neural bridge, detecting emotionally and interoceptively salient stimuli, redirecting attention toward them, and facilitating the transformation of bodily signals into conscious emotional awareness [48]. Dysfunction in this network may underlie the deficits observed in alexithymia, particularly in the conscious experience and awareness of emotions [49]. Within the attention network, the cingulate cortex plays a pivotal role in emotional awareness, regulation, and empathy [50–53]. Moreover, it is also essential for selecting targets from competing inputs, facilitating appropriate responses while suppressing inappropriate ones [54]. Previous studies have reported that both gray matter volume and task-related activation of the cingulate cortex are associated with alexithymia [11, 13, 14, 55]. Impairment in the cingulate cortex may therefore disrupt the allocation of attention to emotionally salient signals, contributing to difficulties in perceiving one’s own emotions. Another important node of the ventral attention network is the insula. The insula is crucial for emotional awareness since it receives information from internal bodily states and integrates it into a subjective feeling state [56, 57]. In individuals with alexithymia, both increased and decreased insula activation have been observed across different tasks compared to controls [15, 16, 18, 19, 58–60]. Dysfunction of the insula reflects a failure to integrate interoceptive bodily signals, thereby preventing the formation of coherent emotional experiences.

The somatomotor network was also involved in both gray matter volume and task-induced alteration networks. The somatomotor network is engaged in processing sensory information and coordinating motor functions. Damage in this network may reflect the tendency of individuals with alexithymia to focus on bodily sensations rather than emotional states, as supported by previous imaging studies [14, 61, 62]. Such findings suggest that individuals with alexithymia might exhibit heightened physiological arousal to affective stimuli but reduced cognitive awareness of these emotions. Moreover, the gray matter volume alteration network was localized within the limbic network, which comprises the temporal, orbitofrontal, fusiform, and parahippocampal gyrus according to the 7-Yeo network. This network is primarily involved in emotional processing. Specifically, the temporal and parahippocampal gyrus are responsible for integrating emotional memory; impairments in these regions weaken the connection between emotional experiences and memory context, making it difficult for emotions to be semantically represented and conceptualized. Abnormalities in the temporal and parahippocampal regions have been widely reported in previous studies on alexithymia during affective tasks, indicating a failure to retrieve stored memories and link visual cues with meaning [15, 63]. Moreover, damage to the fusiform gyrus may affect facial emotion recognition, thereby further weakening emotional empathy and self-awareness of emotion [18, 63]. Thus, damage within the limbic network may contribute to alexithymia by disrupting emotional memory integration and emotion recognition, ultimately impairing the ability to identify and describe one’s own feelings.

In the present study, the FCNM approach was applied to identify the gray matter volume and task-induced alteration networks associated with alexithymia by integrating reported abnormal brain locations from previous studies with large-scale functional connectivity data. Compared with conventional meta-analysis, FCNM represents a methodological advancement by addressing the heterogeneity of previous findings. Rather than assuming that isolated brain regions independently underlie a symptom, this approach localizes heterogeneous regions into a common symptom-related network, acknowledging that complex psychological traits such as alexithymia emerge from distributed and interacting neural systems. Through this network-based framework, alexithymia-related abnormalities were localized to the subcortical (more pronounced in gray matter volume), ventral attention (more pronounced in task-induced activation), and somatomotor networks. Considering the respective functions of these networks, alexithymia may reflect disrupted transmission of emotional signals from physiological to cognitive levels, impaired allocation of attention to emotionally salient stimuli, and heightened physiological reactivity to affective cues. These findings further suggest that individuals with alexithymia may exhibit dysfunction primarily at the lower levels of emotional awareness (e.g., the physical or action-oriented stage), rather than at higher-order cognitive awareness. Collectively, this study extends the understanding of alexithymia from a regional to a network-level perspective and provides novel insights into its potential neurobiological mechanisms.

By correlating the spatial distribution of various neurotransmitter systems derived from previous PET studies with the network alteration maps, we found that both the gray matter volume and task-induced alteration networks were associated with the serotonin and acetylcholine systems. The serotonin systems were previously implicated in alexithymia [33]. Specifically, 5-HTT density showed a positive correlation with both alteration networks. The 5-HTT, encoded by the SLC6A4 gene, has been genetically linked to alexithymia. For instance, Kano et al. reported that Japanese individuals with the L/L genotype exhibited significantly higher Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) scores [64], while in an Italian Caucasian cohort, the same genotype in males was positively associated with greater difficulty identifying feelings (a TAS-20 subscale) [65]. These findings align with our results, supporting a potential involvement of the serotonin system in the neural alterations underlying alexithymia. Acetylcholine systems have been implicated in emotional processing and regulation [66]. Hyperactivation of acetylcholine within developing cortical circuits has been proposed to induce aberrant neural activity patterns, thereby impairing emotional functions [67]. Moreover, various acetylcholine receptors have been shown to modulate emotional states, contributing to depression and anxiety regulation as well as exerting pro-cognitive effects [68, 69]. Given that the VAChT is essential for loading acetylcholine into synaptic vesicles, it serves as a critical regulator of cholinergic neurotransmission efficiency. Dysregulation of VAChT may therefore lead to altered cholinergic tone and abnormal activation of emotion-related cortical regions, such as the anterior cingulate cortex [70]. These findings provide a potential neurobiological basis linking acetylcholine system dysfunction.

Several limitations should be noticed. First, all included studies assessed alexithymia exclusively via self-report instruments. Given that these measures depend on individuals’ capacity to introspect and report their own emotional states, they may be inherently less reliable in participants with high levels of alexithymia. Consequently, our findings are restricted to self-reported alexithymia and may not fully capture the construct. Second, the resting-state fMRI data used to construct the alexithymia-related alteration network were from a healthy cohort. Despite evidence from earlier studies that sample selection has a limited impact on network localization [26, 71–73], FC patterns in individuals with high alexithymia might differ slightly from this healthy scaffold. Moreover, the repeated FCNM analysis was conducted using retest data from the same cohort, this limits the ability to perform independent validation and does not guarantee the generalizability of the findings to a new cohort. Finally, the FCNM analysis did not incorporate the sample size or effect size of individual studies, as there is currently no consensus on the optimal approach for incorporating these factors. Future work addressing these considerations, alongside methodological advancements, will be important.

Conclusions

In summary, this study applied the novel FCNM approach alongside large-scale human brain connectome data to map neuroanatomically heterogeneous structural and functional alterations related to alexithymia into two networks corresponding to gray matter volume and task-induced activation. Specifically, both alteration networks were localized within the subcortical, ventral attention, and somatomotor networks. These brain alterations may be linked to dysfunction in serotonin and vesicular acetylcholine transporter systems. Together, these findings provide new insights into the potential neurobiological mechanisms of alexithymia from a network-based perspective. Moreover, the identified networks may represent promising targets for therapeutic interventions, such as noninvasive neuromodulation, and could serve as prognostic biomarkers for treatment response or disease progression.
