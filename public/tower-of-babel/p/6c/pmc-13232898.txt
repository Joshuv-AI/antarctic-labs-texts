Successful treatment of refractory anaphylactic shock with angiotensin II
Authors: Katarina Barbara Bajec, Andreja Möller Petrun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRefractory anaphylaxis is a severe form of anaphylaxis, defined as anaphylaxis requiring ongoing treatment despite two appropriate doses of epinephrine. There is a lack of data about second-line vasopressor treatment in refractory anaphylaxis. We present a case report of refractory anaphylaxis after a young female received a gadolinium-based contrast medium and was successfully treated with an angiotensin II (ATII) infusion. ATII is converted from angiotensin I by the action of the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE). Since oestrogen has an inhibitory effect upon mRNA synthesis of ACE, premenopausal women have lower ACE and ATII levels, making them more prone to anaphylaxis. There are also gene polymorphisms related to lower ATII levels in patients with severe anaphylaxis. Since ATII has no effect on mastocytes and bronchospasm, it can only be used as a second-line vasopressor beside epinephrine and fluid treatment in refractory anaphylaxis.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis had a global increase in incidence and mortality in the last two decades.1,2 Refractory anaphylaxis is a new entity of severe anaphylaxis, mostly defined as a suboptimal response to two doses of epinephrine, the key drug for anaphylaxis treatment.3 Epinephrine is effective against mastocyte degranulation, angioedema, bronchospasm, and cardiovascular collapse.3 Two to three percent of all anaphylaxis cases do not respond to two appropriately administered doses of epinephrine.3 In rare cases, gadolinium-based contrast medium (GBCM) is a potential trigger of immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated hypersensitivity reactions.4 To the best of our knowledge, we report the first case of refractory anaphylaxis after the application of GBCM successfully treated with an angiotensin II (ATII) infusion.

Written consent for the publication of this case report was obtained from the patient. This work follows the CARE guidelines from the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman, in whom a tumour of the caecum was suspected, was scheduled in October 2024 for outpatient magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadoteridol (10 mmol in 20 ml), a low osmolality GBCM. She had no known allergies and had not been exposed to any contrast medium or chemotherapy previously. She was being treated with topical corticosteroids for pemphigus foliaceus but had no other comorbidities or medications. After 18 ml of the gadoteridol dose having been administered, she suddenly developed dyspnoea and pruritus. A timeline of events in the MRI suite is shown in (Fig. 1). During transportation to the ICU a continuous infusion of epinephrine was started at 0.1 mcg kg−1 min−1 and, on arrival in the ICU, norepinephrine at 0.1 mcg kg−1 min−1 was added. Additionally, she received four puffs of salbutamol, 2 mg of clemastine i.v., 100 mg of hydrocortisone i.v. and epinephrine inhalation.

A multiaccess catheter, a central venous catheter and an arterial catheter were inserted under ultrasound guidance. Aggressive ultrasound-guided fluid resuscitation was initiated; 7.5 l of crystalloids and 200 ml of 20% albumin were administered in total. The norepinephrine dose was gradually increased to 0.3 mcg kg−1 min−1, leaving epinephrine at 0.1 mcg kg−1 min−1 due to persisting tachycardia. A vasopressin infusion was added and increased to 0.03 IU min−1; however, hypotension persisted. An ATII infusion was started at 20 ng kg−1 min−1, and the dose was increased in steps of 5 ng kg−1 min−1 every 5 min to a maximum of 40 ng kg−1 min−1.

Finally, a satisfactory mean arterial pressure was reached. After haemodynamic stabilisation, the need for vasopressor support started to decline, diuresis was initiated, and lactate began to decrease. Firstly, the vasopressin was discontinued, followed by the angiotensin II, epinephrine and norepinephrine. She also received hydrocortisone 200 mg per day, which was gradually tapered over the next 3 days. Eight hours after admission, the patient was without vasoactive support and with 30% of inspired oxygen. The rash disappeared and there were no skin manifestations characteristic for pemphigus. A sedation window for neurological assessment was undertaken and the patient woke up appropriately. Due to persisting tongue oedema, which was evaluated clinically, extubation was only undertaken after 48 h. Prior to extubation, the patient was sedated with propofol for a Patient State Index around 30. The first renin level just before ATII started was 87.77 mIU l−1; after 6 h, it was 15.22 mIU l−1. The samples obtained for tryptase levels 1, 3 and 24 h after the event were 88.3 μg l−1, 49.8 μg l−1 and 9.26 μg l−1, respectively. The patient was transferred to the ward on the fifth postevent day without any sequelae or self-reported psychological distress and was very grateful for our efforts. After referral to an allergy assessment one month after the anaphylaxis, an allergy to gadoteridol was confirmed. Fortunately, a repeat MRI was not required, since the images obtained were sufficient.

Discussion

GBCM have been used for over 30 years and have an excellent safety record. Severe allergic reactions are extremely rare, occurring in up to 0.01% of all exposures.4 In our patient, the predominant clinical manifestations were angioedema, bronchospasm and cardiovascular collapse not responding to repeated doses nor to continuous infusion of epinephrine, which reflects the definition of refractory anaphylaxis.3 An additional impediment was the strong magnetic field and resultant lack of full monitoring. To facilitate the action of epinephrine, and for titratability, epinephrine was given i.v., according to ERC Resuscitation guidelines 2021 (epinephrine bolus of 20–50 mcg i.v. in specialist care) and our local protocol.5 An antihistamine and corticosteroid were also given according to local protocol. Since tryptase was highly positive, an anaphylactic reaction was very likely.6 A delayed administration of the first dose of epinephrine might have contributed to the development of refractory anaphylaxis since this is one of the known risk factors.3 Despite fluid administration and triple vasopressor therapy administered through a central venous line, an appropriate mean arterial pressure was not achieved until the ATII infusion was established. ATII is part of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), where renin and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) are limiting factors in ATII production.7 Since oestrogen has an inhibitory effect upon mRNA synthesis of ACE, premenopausal women have lower ACE and ATII levels and are more prone to anaphylaxis.1 Besides cleaving angiotensin I to ATII, ACE is also responsible for bradykinin degradation. Varney et al. found gene polymorphisms related to lower RAAS activity and ATII levels in patients with anaphylaxis.1 The main effects of ATII are vasoconstriction, reduced endothelial NO activity, release of vasopressin, increased transcription of endothelin-1, aldosterone release, induction of adrenergic stimulation, and vagal tone inhibition.1,7 Since ATII has no effect on mastocytes and bronchospasm, it can only be used as a second-line vasopressor beside epinephrine and fluid loading in refractory anaphylaxis. In our case, the negative trend of renin concentration after administering ATII might be an additional indicator of ATII efficacy in treating hypotension since renin production is triggered by hypotension, and there is a negative feedback loop between renin release and ATII concentration.7
