Expert perspectives on US regulatory approaches to large language models in healthcare
Authors: Stephen Gilbert, Tinglong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this second article of a three-article series on interviews with Dr Shantanu Nundy, an advisor in the office of the Commissioner of the US FDA on AI governance and digital health policy, we explore the challenge of regulating large language model (LLM)-enabled medical AI, particularly where it extends beyond the usual boundaries of medical devices and therefore challenges traditional regulatory frameworks.

Debate over the regulation of medical AI is wide-ranging, with commentators advancing sharply differing views. At the extremes, some have argued for a moratorium on the development and use of AI to avoid existential risks to humanity1, whereas others resist almost any regulation that might constrain innovation2. Within the regulatory community, positions are typically less polarized. Few dispute the need for risk-based regulation, or the likelihood that new approaches will be required to address emergent behaviours in increasingly capable systems, particularly as such technologies extend beyond the usual boundaries of medical devices, for example, when general-purpose AI chatbots provide health advice3,4. Disagreement instead centres on where oversight boundaries should be drawn, and how much regulatory innovation is warranted to govern these new classes of technology. The FDA has taken a highly proactive approach, meeting innovation in technology with innovation in regulation. In this interview, Dr Nundy articulates how the agency navigates the boundary between wellness tools and regulated medical devices in the age of LLMs. In particular, he presented his own perspectives on a two-dimensional framework (clinical severity crossed with tool autonomy), which offers a practical lens for thinking about which LLM-enabled applications warrant regulatory scrutiny and could inform future guidance.

Introduction to Dr Shantanu Nundy

In the first article in this three-part series5 we spoke with Dr Shantanu Nundy about the TEMPO program6. Dr Nundy is a practicing primary care physician, serial innovator, and advisor in the FDA Commissioner’s Office working on AI issues across the agency. Because he is a contractor, not a federal employee, the views expressed here are his own and should not be interpreted as official FDA policy.

The wellness-medical device interface for wearables and LLMs

We spoke with Dr Shantanu Nundy in early February 2026 amid a burst of FDA activity on digital health and AI, including the agency’s 2025 advisory committee meeting on generative-AI-enabled mental health devices, request for public comment on post-market evaluation of AI-enabled evaluation, and the January 2026 reissuance of the general wellness and CDS guidances7–9. We discussed the latest developments in LLM-enabled health technology, the implications of those guidance changes, and how these tools may be regulated going forward. The interview has been edited for clarity and annotated with references for context.

Stephen Gilbert

We’ve seen launches by the biggest players in the large language models space, of patient-facing or layperson-facing tools, including those interacting with wearables10. At the same time, some of them have also launched doctor-facing or healthcare system-facing AI platforms11. Over time, one could anticipate linking these two approaches into platforms that both face the patient and the doctor. It would make a lot of sense for patients if they could transfer information that they have within their wellness application, which may be provided by the same platform that their doctor would be using on the other side. Is this something that the FDA has been considering, and how would an interface between the wellness space and the clinical decision support system space work from a regulatory perspective?

Shantanu Nundy

The starting point is not, ‘Is this AI?’ It is, ‘What function is the product performing, and does that function fall within FDA device regulation?’ The recent updates to the CDS and general wellness guidances were meant to clarify specific areas where the FDA does not intend to regulate software as a medical device9,12. One important example is wearables that measure physiological signals such as blood pressure. Historically, the existence of the measurement itself could pull a product into device regulation. The updated guidance recognizes that some of those measurements also have low-risk wellness uses. Another example is CDS software that offers a single recommendation. In some clinical contexts, there is only one appropriate recommendation, so just because a software presents one diagnosis or treatment option should not automatically make it a regulated device. More broadly, the FDA regulates function, not specific technology categories. That is why these guidelines do not have to be specific to AI to be highly relevant to AI-enabled tools.

Shantanu Nundy

I do think the wellness and clinical worlds will increasingly converge. A patient may use a wellness tool to monitor blood pressure at home; the product may flag that the readings are persistently elevated and prompt the patient to see a clinician. At that point, it makes sense for the information to move with the patient into the clinical setting, where a clinician might use separate decision support to confirm a diagnosis and guide treatment. From a regulatory standpoint, the act of sharing information from patient to clinician has generally not been treated as a regulated medical function. The key is to disaggregate functions by risk. A broad platform may include some functions that are clearly low risk, such as step counting or information sharing, and others that are at higher risk and therefore warrant FDA review.

Tinglong Dai

If we think about examples like ChatGPT for healthcare, like Claude for Healthcare11, at what point do they cross the line to where they should be regulated as a medical device?

Shantanu Nundy

I think about this first as a physician. Two questions matter most. The first is clinical risk: how serious is the condition, and how reversible is harm if the tool is wrong? A low-stakes error in a preventive or chronic context is very different from an error in a time-sensitive, high-acuity setting. The second is autonomy: is the tool simply surfacing information, recommending an action, or taking action itself, and if it is acting, is a clinician meaningfully supervising that action? Those two dimensions together provide a practical way to think about risk. They also point to an important discipline for developers: a product cannot be ‘for everything.’ If a tool is meant to remain in a lower-risk category, to me, it needs real guardrails and clear boundaries around the questions it will and will not answer. If it is intended to operate in higher-risk contexts, then it has to be evaluated against the highest-risk function it is designed to perform.

Stephen Gilbert

The FDA’s approach to demarcating wellness and device functions makes sense, but general-purpose, non-medical large language model tools already provide detailed medical answers to user questions. Recently, Australia has introduced an approach3, and the UK has hinted at a similar approach, which is not to regulate on what the manufacturer claims something will do, but to regulate on what it actually does. Does it matter that we have incredibly powerful general tools that will answer pretty much anything, from the perspective of the layperson user?

Shantanu Nundy

I think it is worth asking whether search and information tools now need clearer regulatory guidance, because they have become a major way people seek health advice. Historically, the FDA does not look only at marketing or labelling; it also looks at intended use as reflected in design, workflows, and real-world behaviour. So, if a general-purpose application is effectively directing a person through a high-risk medical decision - for example, telling someone with possible stroke symptoms to take aspirin - that begins to look much more like a medical product. To me, this remains an unsettled area, but it is one that deserves attention as general-purpose models become more prominent in everyday health decision-making.

Tinglong Dai

In the coming years, we will almost certainly see an influx of devices with GenAI capabilities. I understand you’re saying FDA is kind of agnostic about specific technologies, but it is also true that regulating such devices is so much harder than regulating predictive AI, due to the lack of definition of a ground truth. What’s the FDA’s general position in terms of regulating generative AI devices?

Shantanu Nundy

The need for premarket review is still driven by intended use and function, not whether the underlying model is generative or deterministic. But once a product falls within device regulation, the technology absolutely matters for the evidence package. Two tools may perform the same clinical function - say, triage - but raise different review questions depending on how they work. For a traditional ML model, reviewers may focus on training data, validation, and planned updates. For an LLM-based system, the emphasis may shift toward drift, failure modes, and real-world validation. So, the FDA can be technology-agnostic at the front door while still being technology-specific in its evidence standards.

Tinglong Dai

Do you expect the different questions to be asked to be formalized in 2026, 2027, and some guidance to be issued soon?

Shantanu Nundy

I cannot speak to what formal guidance may come next, but I can say the topic has been front and centre. FDA convened the Digital Health Advisory Committee in 2024 on generative AI7 and again in 2025 on generative-AI-enabled mental health devices8, and it also issued a request for public comment on methods for measuring and evaluating AI-enabled device performance in the real world13. That tells you the agency is actively listening, learning, and trying to build the right policies and capabilities for a very fast-moving field.

Summary

This interview illuminates how the FDA approaches the regulation of LLM-enabled health technologies, not by regulating the underlying technology, but by regulating function and intended use. Dr Nundy’s distinction between the determination of whether a product is regulated (which is technology-agnostic) and the evidentiary questions asked during premarket review (which are technology-specific) is a particularly useful clarification for developers and researchers. The convergence of wellness and clinical tools, and the regulatory challenges posed by general-purpose LLMs that provide general or unsolicited medical advice, remain open frontiers. How will we look back in future decades on our current time of challenging new medical AI concepts, regulators’ approaches to them, the testing of existing frameworks and the development of new regulatory approaches? Due to the rapid advances of LLM-enabled health technologies, we may not yet be ready to set out formalized frameworks for their assessment; however, the voluntary regulatory pilot described in the first article in this three-part series5 may help define the best way to regulate – through adaptive practical experience and through looking at real-time data.
