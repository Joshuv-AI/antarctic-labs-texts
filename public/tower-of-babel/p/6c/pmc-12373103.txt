Audit of Plastic Surgery Activities in West Africa (Ivory Coast): Key Performance Indicators From 568 Consecutive Cases
Authors: Valerie Dje Bi Dje-Assi, Komla Sena Amouzou, Lanciné Kaba, Konan Christophe Allah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:In Africa, there has been limited research on audits in plastic surgery (PS). This study analyzed key performance indicators from the PS department at the University Hospital of Treichville in Abidjan, Ivory Coast.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 568 patient files collected between January 2018 and May 2019 (17 mo). The variables assessed included demographic profiles; clinic data (recruitment methods, treatment decisions); ward metrics (number of hospital days, average length of stay, average bed occupancy rate); emergency cases (conditions and surgery); elective procedures (number of postponed cases, number of revision procedures, postoperative outcomes); and outpatient wound care (number of dressings, outcomes).Results:A total of 568 patients were included in the study: 370 men (65.14%) and 198 women (34.85%), yielding a sex ratio of 1.86 to 1. The mean age was 41.78 years (range: 1–80 y). Conditions treated included skin and soft tissue defects (n = 323) and hand trauma or congenital conditions (n = 241). Surgery was performed on 406 of the 568 (71.47%) patients, including 276 elective (67.98%) and 130 emergency cases (32.02%). The average length of hospital stay was 9.9 days, with a bed occupancy rate of 40.8%. Uneventful outcomes were recorded for 368 of 383 (96.08%) patients, with 1 inpatient death. A total of 112 consultations were provided to other departments.Conclusions:The audits of our PS activities highlighted the need for structured data collection. Evaluation of sociodemographic factors, patient conditions, organizational metrics, and key performance indicators identified areas for improvement.

Takeaways

Question: What are the main key performance indicators (KPIs) for the unique plastic surgery department in Ivory Coast?

Findings: KPIs for outpatient clinics and dressings, and in hospital surgical activity (emergency and elective) were identified.

Meaning: The identified KPIs allowed us to point out the limits and areas for improvement in expanding plastic surgery services in Ivory Coast.

INTRODUCTION

Although plastic surgery (PS) has been widespread for centuries, in West Africa, the first indigenous plastic surgeon started a practice in Nigeria in 1967.1 The first PS department was established in Ghana in 1992.2 Although other PS departments were established after 1992 in Ghana and Nigeria, the shortage of PS specialists in Africa is still striking.1–4 In Ghana, the 2 PS departments train resident plastic surgeons.5,6 In other West African countries, few trained specialists, proper infrastructure, and essential supplies support the existing PS departments.1,4,6–11 The unique PS department in Senegal (in Dakar, the capital) has only been training residents since 2022. The only plastic surgeon in Togo works in the main university hospital in Lomé (the capital). The unique PS department in Ivory Coast, created in 1997 and located in the tertiary teaching hospital of Treichville in Abidjan, is one of the most recent and has not yet started training residents.

Only a few of the 44 essential packages of PS7 are currently performed in African low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Procedures routinely performed are skin graft, amputation/escharotomy, suture of skin lacerations, skin excision, and release of burn contractures. Congenital deformities, such as cleft lip and palate, are mainly treated by humanitarian missions from developed countries.1,3,4

Published audits of PS activity allow for identifying and defining areas of competence of specialists, upgrading the practice, and advocating the inclusion of the specialty in the curriculum of medical students.6,10,12–16 To our knowledge, no previous audit of PS activities in the Ivory Coast has been published. Given the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on PS departments across Africa,17 we report on the activities of the PS department in Treichville before the pandemic (January 2018 to May 2019) to set a reference point for the postpandemic period. This analysis aimed to assess data about key performance indicators (KPIs) to highlight pearls and pitfalls in expanding PS by measuring clinics’ metrics, admissions, emergencies, elective procedures, outpatient wound care, and opinions provided in other departments.

METHODS

This retrospective single-center study was performed in the Department of Plastic, Reconstructive, Aesthetic, Hand Surgery, and Burn Care at the Teaching Hospital of Treichville in Abidjan (Ivory Coast). Our analysis referred to the charts of patients followed in the department from January 2018 to May 2019 (17 mo). The study was written according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational studies.18

GENERAL VIEW OF THE DEPARTMENT

The wards had 12 beds. Wound care for outpatients was performed in a separate room. The department shared the same outpatient clinic, emergency unit, intensive care unit, and operating rooms with the general surgery department. Surgical activity was conducted 2 days a week for elective cases and daily for emergencies. The PS staff included 3 senior surgeons: 2 board-certified plastic surgeons with fellowships in hand surgery completed in Europe and 1 general surgeon. In addition, the PS staff encompassed 1 intern final year student, 7 nurses, 2 nurse assistants, and a secretary. The staff provided consultations and treated patients in other departments as well. Additionally, the education of medical students was part of the regular activity. There was no defined referral system between hospitals or within our hospital. Some patients came directly from external centers to our hospital. In contrast, although they have been seen in other hospital departments, different patients may come on their own to seek a plastic surgeon’s opinion. Additionally, given the absence of a defined social security system, many patients without insurance had to pay for their treatment.

THE STUDY

We assessed the overall activity of our department, including clinics, surgery, outpatient wound care, and consultations given in other departments based on hospital logbooks, registries of the emergency unit, and paper-based files. We did not include unusable records and patients not registered in the department’s books.

Variables retrieved from the files included sociodemographic parameters and conditions treated in general and different settings. We analyzed the KPIs in the PS department across various activities: (1) clinics: number of patients, mode of recruitment, and treatment decisions; (2) admissions: number of patients, mode of admission, patient outcome, number of hospital days, average length of hospital stay (ALOS), average bed occupancy rate (BOR), conditions treated, and mortality rate [ALOS: number of inpatient days during a period/number of discharges; BOR = (used bed days × 100)/(available beds × 365)]; (3) emergencies: number of patients, treated conditions, and procedures performed; (4) elective surgical activity: number of procedures, number of postponed cases and reasons for the postponement, number of revision procedures, and the postoperative course; (5) outpatient wound care: number of patients and dressings, and patient outcomes; and (6) opinion provided in other departments: number of patients and departments involved. The patient’s outcome was assessed based on whether they left the hospital with or without medical advice by signing a discharge form, by escape, or following death. The data were collected in accordance with ethical guidelines in line with the Declaration of Helsinki.19 The current study was approved by the ethical committee of Treichville University Hospital.

Data entry and analysis of results were performed using Microsoft Word 2013, Microsoft Excel, and Epi.info 7.2.2.6 software (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta). Qualitative variables were expressed as proportions and quantitative values as means and averages.

In the Wards

A total of 383 patients were admitted to the wards. The total number of hospital days was 2949, the ALOS was 9.9 (10 d/patient), and the BOR of the department was 40.80%. The inpatient outcome was uneventful for 368 of 383 (96.08%) patients. Only 1 patient died; thus, the mortality rate was 0.26%.

Elective Surgery

Procedures were scheduled for 276 of 406 (67.98%) elective cases. Of the 276 elective cases, 43 (15.58%) were postponed definitively or rescheduled. The reasons for postponement were patients’ unfavorable economic conditions (eg, patients did not buy the supplies [20, 46.51%]), patients not completing routine blood tests and not buying the supplies (16, 37.21%), no available operative room (4, 9.30%), and medical condition contraindicating surgery (3, 6.98%). Of these 276 elective cases, revision surgery was required for 12 (4.35%) patients due to flap revisions (defatting bulky flaps and trimming partially necrotic flaps) or complex hand amputations with secondary soft tissue necrosis. The postoperative course was uneventful in 273 of 276 (95.65%) patients; 3 of 276 (1.00%) patients had complications, including partial or total flap necrosis and wound dehiscence.

Outpatient Wound Care

We treated 400 patients in outpatient wound care and performed 5003 dressings. Patients were recruited from clinics (17 of 400, 4.25%) and inpatient wards (383 of 400, 95.75%). Of these 400 patients, 299 (74.75%) were followed up until complete healing, whereas 101 (25.25%) were lost to follow-up before complete wound healing.

Opinions Provided in Other Departments

We provided an average of 2 opinions per week in other departments: internal medicine (33 cases), orthopedics (30 cases), cardiology (20 cases), pediatric surgery (12 cases), ICU (10 cases), pediatrics (4 cases), and infectious diseases (3 cases).

DISCUSSION

This study spotlighted the clear need for structured data collection about the activities of our PS department, which is essential to provide basic information to identify KPIs. For example, our data lacked information on mortality rates in emergencies, comorbidities, or indications for dressings, making it difficult to determine the type of wound (postoperative wound, direct wound healing, or preparation for surgical coverage).

Identifying these shortcomings was a strength of our study, as this prepandemic picture will empower our team to focus on areas of expertise, detail the requirements to cover the essential packages of PS, and advocate to the competent authorities about the needs for the proper functioning of the PS department. However, our study was limited by its retrospective nature. Additionally, the absence of a precise benchmark of data collection hindered a complete pattern of data relevant to defining the KPIs.

Our sociodemographic data revealed a predominance of young and male patients. This patient population is the most exposed to trauma, as reported in the literature20 and in our unpublished hospital data. PS centers in other LMICs reported activities based on acquired diseases, such as burns and other traumatic injuries.5,11,14,16 We had a high percentage of patients with trauma (75 of 406) operated on with orthopedic surgeons, and this underscores the value of multidisciplinary collaboration for open traumatic injuries of the limbs, as also reported in other PS centers, including Nigeria,15 Mozambique,9 India,21 and Turkey.22 Given our limited resources and infrastructures, we sought to experiment with innovative and feasible approaches for trauma, for example, the “on-arrival block” (brachial block) as an advantageous first step of the protocol for patients with severely injured upper extremities.23

Our percentages of elective surgery (68%) were not comparable to other African countries, given the different settings and PS activities. For example, in Zambia, the PS department’s 16-year activity, which relied on 42 hospital beds and 2 weekly operative days, involved 5740 patients (358 patients annually).11 In Ghana (Kumasi center), the PS activity included 1964 patients operated on during 36 months (on average 655 cases/y),5 including burns and maxillofacial conditions, which were untreated in our department. We applied general anesthesia to 84% of patients, compared with 53.8% in the Kumasi center, and regional anesthesia to 5.42% of the patients, compared with 24.2% in the Kumasi center.5 In Maputo (Mozambique), plastic surgeons performed procedures under local anesthesia due to the unavailability of operating rooms.9 Some procedures, such as limited surface skin grafts, were executed under local anesthesia in the dressing room or the patient’s bedside.11 Ward24 reported and advocated the use of local anesthesia for certain PS procedures long before the discovery and use of the wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet technique that facilitates complex operative procedures in outpatient settings, thus reducing the need for a properly equipped operating room and anesthesiologists.25–27 The wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet technique, along with brachial block, spinal anesthesia, and nerve blocks, may be used as adjunctive approaches to expand practice in many centers that struggle to deliver all essential surgical packages.

In LMICs, the 44 essential PS packages are usually applied to procedures for trauma and some congenital medical conditions.7 Most of our procedures were skin grafts, soft tissue excisions, flaps, and amputations. This pattern, common in Africa,5,11 may change once the PS specialty becomes recognized, and the number of trained surgeons increases.21

The treatment of skin tumors, which we performed, requires proficiency in reconstructive surgery acquired through specific training. For example, postoperative complications leading to flap failure may benefit from salvage flap techniques due to venous congestion.28 In Africa, congenital diseases (mostly cleft lips and palates) were traditionally treated by foreign teams during short-term missions,7,29 but they may benefit more from developing local capacity that enables local teams to take the lead also in this field.30

In our setting, most treated conditions were minor and moderate deformities. Pediatric congenital hand anomalies accounted for up to 20% of our hand diseases and may benefit from collaboration between the PS and pediatric departments. This collaboration could also be advantageous for keloid and hypertrophic scars, which are highly prevalent in Africa. We performed steroid infiltrations in 15% of this patient population, a percentage lower than in other countries,12 probably due to the noninclusion of steroid infiltrations from our hospital’s billing logbooks.

Clinics were held only 3 days per week as we had to share the same clinic rooms with other surgical departments, and the limited number of PS surgeons did not allow for more extensive clinic activity. As the rooms had no specific equipment or supplies for wound dressings, many patients may have been seen in the outpatient wound care department. Additionally, the low number of patients referred by other hospital departments (23.2%) may be due to the lack of knowledge about the PS specialty, as reported in various countries.1,3,4,21 In this regard, we have planned an information campaign for our hospital’s healthcare professionals and the public, in collaboration with patient associations. Our ALOS of 10 days per patient and a BOR of 40% for scheduled and emergency surgery in our PS department ward, lower than those in other African PS centers, may be attributed to our focus on reconstructive and traumatic or nontraumatic hand and other conditions that do not require prolonged hospital stay. Unlike other investigators,5,14,31 we did not classify procedures into major, small, and mild categories.

Injuries to soft tissues, hand diseases, sequels of burns, and skin tumors required a hospital stay of less than 10 days; therefore, some cases may have fallen into the outpatient surgery setting. The limited number of PS surgeons hindered the presence of a PS department representative in the surgical emergency room. However, the number of patients treated by our team is high compared with the 32 cases in 2 years reported by Nasir et al13 (Ilorin, Nigeria). For future research, we should determine the time of admission to emergency and the time to reach the operating room as important measures of KPIs in a surgical department and indicators of the overall quality of a health system.5,11,31

Several factors influenced the outcomes of the treated conditions: delays or postponements of surgery due to socioeconomic conditions, as seen in our 37 elective cases, a recurring situation in sub-Saharan Africa, characterized by the lack of a robust social security system.7,11,32 Additionally, the unavailability of operating rooms may lead to performing surgery under local anesthesia with limited need for heavy equipment. Although collaboration with charity organizations may help reduce the number of postponed elective surgical cases, government initiatives to generalize national health insurance will be the most sustainable solution.

The indications for dressings were missing for the 400 patients treated in the outpatient dressing room; of them, 100 patients were lost to follow-up, probably due to their inability to afford the financial burden of supplies and transportation. New digital smartphone analyses to assess wound morphology33 could be explored and used for remote wound care, thus reducing the number of visits to the hospital. In our planned data collection, we should distinguish between directed healing and postoperative dressings and those prepared for surgery. The modified tissue, inflammation/infection, moisture, edge/epithelialization scoring system to predict wound healing time34 could help in scheduling wound care.

We provided 2 opinions per week to other departments (mainly internal medicine and trauma-orthopedics) for 112 patients. Most conditions addressed in these departments required wound dressing by the PS team, which is specifically trained for this task, and this confirmed the interdisciplinary nature of the PS specialty.3,4,11,31

Improving the activities of our department will require various approaches, such as planning clinics with possible dressing on-site; collecting telephone numbers for appointments21; coding all procedures (including aesthetics); tools, such as the LACE+ (ALOS, acuity of admission, comorbidities, emergency department use) index, to predict the risk of perioperative events35; recording cases more accurately; and organizing open days pointed to other departments and the general population. With this framework, we can plan future research studies using statistical analyses suitable for interpreting our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study on the activities of the PS department of the Ivory Coast emphasized the importance of structured data collection and organization for defining KPIs. Detailed descriptions of KPIs will help expand the reach of essential PS services in our country, identify and evaluate strengths and weaknesses, measure the need for improving daily service, and track progress over time. The application of KPIs can lead to greater recognition of our PS department and the range of PS activities by specialists from other hospital departments and the general population.
