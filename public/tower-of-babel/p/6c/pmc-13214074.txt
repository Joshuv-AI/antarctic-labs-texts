Dual regulation of PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitochondrial autophagy in ovarian cancer progression and drug resistance: mechanisms and therapeutic potentials
Authors: Jing Xu, Fangyuan Liu, Qiaochu Chen, Yue Li, Jaifei Liu, Fengjuan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer (OC) is a common primary female malignancy globally. It is characterized by a high rate of confirmed diagnosis at an advanced stage (around 70%), abdominal and distant metastases at initial diagnosis, and relatively lower average 5-year survival rate worldwide (< 30%). This undoubtedly greatly increases the difficulty and complexity of the treatment. The therapeutic effect of OC is primarily compromised by the mechanism of chemotherapy resistance, which is complex and has not been fully elucidated. Beyond producing negative impact on the effectiveness of existing treatment strategies, the resistance of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents may also prompt the medical community to constantly explore new treatment approaches. Therefore, in order to develop more effective treatment plans, it highlights the importance and necessity of achieving a thorough understanding of the pathogenesis of OC and the mechanism of chemotherapy resistance. Currently, mitophagy, an intracellular self-degradation process, has been documented to be pivotal in the development of OC and in chemotherapy resistance. It functions to maintain the stability of the intracellular environment through the autophagy mechanism, regulate cellular energy metabolism, and participate in immune responses, supporting its potential of inhibiting tumor growth theoretically. In particular, the PINK1/PARKIN pathway demonstrates a unique bidirectional regulatory effect in tumor growth regulation and chemotherapy resistance reversal. Its dynamic regulatory mechanism in platinum-based resistance of OC has revealed potential therapeutic targets. Accordingly, with emphasis on the bidirectional regulatory role of the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway-mediated mitophagy in inhibiting the growth of OC and reversing chemotherapy resistance, this study aimed to integrate the latest research results, and provide innovative treatment strategies and ideas for OC treatment and chemotherapy efficacy optimization.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a common primary female malignancy, which is featured by insidious lesions and inconspicuous clinical symptoms.As a result, around 70% of patients have already experienced abdominal or distant metastasis at initial diagnosis. The 5-year survival rate of patients is only 25% to 30% [1]. It is recently estimated that the newly diagnosed OC cases would reach approximately 314,000 globally in 2020, and the OC-related death would be nearly 207,000 patients [2]. In China [3], there were 61,060 new cases of OC and 32,646 deaths in 2022, with an increase of the new cases by 5,718 compared with that in 2020.Moreover, the 5-year survival rate is 89% for early-stage OC, while that is only 20% for stage IV OC [4]. At present [5], OC is generally treated by a combination therapy involving surgery, chemotherapy (first-line chemotherapy involving postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy and neoadjuvant chemotherapy [6]), radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, etc.OC still experience extremely low 5-year survival rate, despite significant progress in treatment strategies and plans [7]. Cancer cells develop resistance to chemotherapeutic agents, leading to poor chemotherapy outcomes, serving as a major issue that causes poor therapeutic outcome and low survival for OC [8]. Currently, platinum-based resistance has become a key bottleneck for treatment failure.Cisplatin (DDP) resistance in OC is discovered to have an intimate association with mitophagy, highlighting that it can be alleviated by weakening protein-mediated mitophagy [9].

Mitochondria, as the “power factory” of cells, are the key sites for biological oxidation and energy conversion in eukaryotic cells.Mitochondria can be damaged clearly or exhibit dysfunctional status through autophagy to maintain the stability of the mitochondrial network [10]. Acting as one of the subtypes of selective autophagy, mitophagy is a highly conserved degradation mechanism that can maintain cellular function [11]. Given its double-edged sword role in cancer, autophagy can both inhibit the growth of early tumors, and also promote the growth of advanced tumors [12]. Its mechanisms may be related to both ubiquitin-dependent and non-ubiquitin-dependent pathways [13]. At present, ubiquitin-dependent pathways are representative mitophagy mechanistic approach, among which the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway has garnered great attention [13]. In normal mitochondria, PINK1 may enter the mitochondria by passing smoothly through the transport complexes of the outer and inner membranes.Subsequently, PINK1 may becleavedand degraded by mitochondrial processing peptidase and progerin-associated rhombic protease.This action may prevent PINK1 from accumulating on the outer membrane of mitochondria, otherwise it may trigger autophagy in healthy mitochondria [14, 15]. However, in the context of mitochondrial dysfunction, PINK1 cannot enter the mitochondria through the transporter complex, but accumulates in large quantities on the outer mitochondrial membrane [16].Through the recruitment of massive PARKIN(an E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for attaching phosphorylated ubiquitin (p-Ub) molecules to substrate proteins [17]) to the outer mitochondrial membrane and further phosphorylation, PARKIN becomes activated and may catalyze the ubiquitination of mitochondrial proteins, leading to the formation of mitochondrial autophagosomes When it is recognized by selective autophagy receptors [18]. Ultimately, it fuses with lysosomes to form mature mitochondrial autophagic lysosomes and initiates the program of mitochondrial degradation, achieving mitophagy consequently [19] (Fig. 1).

Mitophagy is a double-edged sword.Specifically, mitochondrial dynamic dysregulation has been reported to be a key pathogenic mechanism for various diseases and pathologies [13]. Increased mitochondrial fusion is associated with chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer, cervical cancer, OC, etc [21]. In general, mitophagy maintains normal cellular metabolism in the early stage of tumor occurrence.Under this circumstance, PINK1 can pass through the cell membrane smoothly, and the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway is lowly expressed [22]. Mitochondria can initiate a quality control program and maintain the functional state of normal cells by clearing dysfunctional mitochondria, thus preventing normal cells from being affected by stress or genomic damage to suppress tumor development ultimately [22]; In the middle and late stages, cancer cells can better tolerate adverse conditions (e.g.,low nutritional levels and hypoxia) under enhanced mitophagy, which can be explained mechanistically by the classical PINK1/PARKIN signaling or non-classical pathways independent of PARKIN [23]. Tumor cells can improve their tolerance to low-nutrition and hypoxic conditions, depending on enhanced mitophagy [24]. In the context of impaired mitochondrial function, PINK1 cannot pass through the cell membrane smoothly, which may further initiate the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway to induce mitophagy, thereby accelerating tumor cell growth [23, 24]. For example, Hanahan proposed six major characteristics of cancer cells, among which the resistance to cell death was related to mitochondrial dynamics [25]; In human OC tissues, there were increase in the number and length of mitochondria, while a decrease in crest width and crest connection diameter, according to the electron microscopy analysis.This might represent an adaptive response of mitochondria to the energy supply of cancer cells [25]. In this case, different proteins involved in mitochondrial dynamics and structure were found to be changed in OC, some of which were important contributors to the occurrence, development and even chemotherapy resistance of OC [25]. Furthermore, mitophagy has been documented to have a strong relationship with the development of various tumors [26]; The deletion of the PARKIN gene, combined with long-term accumulation of toxic substances and mitophagy functiondefect, can promote the transformation of hepatocytes into hepatocellular carcinoma cells [26]. Moreover, the disappearance of the PARKIN gene located at the vulnerable site FRA6E on chromosome 6 may decrease the protein expression of PARKIN, thereby triggering the development of certain breast cancer subtypes [27]. Critically, the occurrence of OC is generally related to the down-regulation of the mitophagy mechanism [28]. The stimulation of mitophagy chemically may inhibit the spread of OC, but the autophagy response is initiated through the pressure of the endoplasmic reticulum, indicating potential differences in the sites where mitophagy is triggered [29]. Simultaneously, some common features were discovered in the studies of OC, neuroblastoma and breast cancer, manifesting as the up-regulation of N-Myc and cyclin E in these tumor cells and their progeny cells [30]. It can be inferred that N-Myc may inhibit the transcription of the PARKIN gene [31]; and cyclin E will accumulate and increase when the PARKIN protein loses its function.The PARKIN protein may indirectly regulate the cell cycle of OC.The level of mitophagy is also a regulator of the development of chemotherapy resistance in tumors. Vianello C [9] discovered that mitophagy could retard the occurrence of chemotherapy resistance in OC through inhibiting BNIP3-mediated mitophagy.Therefore, there is a need to carry out in-depth research on the mechanism of mitophagy, thereby providing fresh therapeutic targets for reversing chemotherapy resistance in OC [31]. Mechanistically, the occurrence and development of OC may be induced by deleting the mediator proteins of three key pathways regulated by PINK1/PARKIN: ①the PINK1/PARKIN-mediated ubiquitin-dependent mitophagy pathway (core pathway for damaged mitochondrial clearance [17, 32]), ②the PINK1/PARKIN-Bcl-2 apoptotic pathway (inactivates anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family to promote OC cell apoptosis [33], and③the PINK1/PARKIN-HIF-1αmetabolic reprogramming pathway (regulates glycolysis to support cancer cell survival [34],. Deletion of these pathway mediators accelerates cancer cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, which is closely related to platinum resistance [35].

Accordingly, the present review elaborates in detail on the mechanism of the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway mediating mitophagy to achieve dual regulation of growth inhibition and chemotherapy resistance reversal of OC.

Mitochondrial autophagy

Mitophagy is a type of selective autophagy that may remove damaged or redundant mitochondria from the mitochondrial library under certain stimuli (e.g.,drug stimulation, hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, etc.) during acute reactions [36]. It plays an important role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [37]. On the one hand, mitophagy can protect the normal function of cells as it acts as a participator in the quality control of mitochondria by eliminating unnecessary mitochondria [37]. On the other hand, it can induce autophagic death or promote cell apoptosis by regulating multiple pathways [10, 13]. Two major molecular regulatory mechanisms of mitophagy exist in mammals [38]. Specifically, the first one is the PARKIN-dependent pathway, which involves PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy [39]; and the other one is the PARKIN-independent pathway, in which mitosis is mainly mediated by receptor proteins such as BNIP3L/NIX, BNIP3 and FUNDC1 [39]. Among these pathways, the one dependent on PINK1/PARKIN has been the most thoroughly studied to date [32]. PINK1 is believed to play a role in a common pathway upstream of PARKIN to maintain mitochondrial quality [20]. Chen [40] found that apoptosis was caused by down-regulating COX-2 to activate PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, thereby inhibiting hepatocellular carcinoma growth.In view of its characteristics, mitophagy can be divided into four stages: (1)the mitochondrial membrane potential undergoes depolarization, and loses the membrane potential after mitochondria are damaged by acute stress stimulation, leading to the activation of the mitophagy-related proteins [41]; (2) early autophagosomes, with the participation of the adaptor protein LC3,wrap around damaged mitochondria to form mitophagosomes [41]; (3) The mitotic spindle forms by binding to lysosomes using acetylated tubulin as a scaffold [41]; and (4) mitochondria are eventually degraded in lysosomes [30]. The important proteins involved in this process include phosphatase and tensin homologous inducible kinase 1PINK1/PARKIN, NIX/BNIP3,and FUN14 domain protein 1 (FUNDC1) [32]. Among them, the mediation of three major kinetoprotein-related proteins, including mitoprotein 1 (MFN1),mitoprotein 2 (MFN2),and optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1),is essential to achieve mitochondrial fusion [32]. The degradation of MFN1 and MFN2 involved in mitochondrial fusion occurs through proteolytic ubiquitination [32]. The top-10 mutated MRGs [42] have been identified in OC, namely UBC, MFN2,MFN1,SRC, CSNK2A1,VDAC1,ATG5, MTERF3,PINK1 and RPS27A.Changes in mitochondrial morphology can reduce mitophagy and lead to apoptosis of immune cells [43]. However, autophagy can promote immune escape through the Warburg effect and ultimately facilitate tumor development [44]. Therefore, in-depth research on inhibiting mitophagy by regulating the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway is particularly important for promoting the cell apoptosis of OC and inhibiting its occurrence and development [32].

The PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway and the molecular mechanism of mitophagy

Triggering conditions for activating the PINK1/PARKIN pathway

Mitochondrial depolarization and the loss of membrane potential are robust to the localization regulation of PARKIN [16]. The recruitment of PARKIN mitochondria relies on the prolonged oxidative stress in mitochondria, chronic loss of mitochondrial fusion, and loss of mitochondrial DNA integrity [45]. However, in each case, PARKIN recruitment is associated with the absence of mitochondrial membrane voltage (ΔΨ, mitochondrial membrane potential) at the individual mitochondrial level, suggesting that the loss of mitochondrial membrane voltage may also be a signal for PARKIN recruitment in these circumstances [16]. In the comparison of depolarized bioactive cells with other cells, PARKIN was more inclined to be recruited into mitochondria that had lost their membrane potential [16]. Therefore, PARKIN can independently recruit mitochondria with impaired membrane potential, and there may be a uniquely targeted relationship between PARKIN recruitment and impaired membrane potential.Mechanism related to such recruitment is limited to mitochondria with impaired membrane potential [16]. Interestingly, the introduction of PINK1 is not sensitive to voltage, like most extracellular proteins, but the first step of PINK1 protein hydrolysis is voltage-dependent [46]. Therefore, despite at a low level in healthy mitochondria usually, PINK1 may accumulate rapidly and selectively on mitochondria that have lost their membrane potential [46]. Moreover, it may indicate that a large amount of expressed PINK1 needs to be aggregated in order to recruit and activate PARKIN [46]. PINK1 enables the recruitment of PARKIN onto mitochondria with impaired membrane potential.Under the regulation of the biological energy state of a single mitochondrion, PINK1 and PARKIN can recognize the mitochondrial network and facilitate a selective elimination of dysfunctional mitochondria [46].

Recruitment of PARKIN and ubiquitination cascade reaction

The PINK1/PARKIN pathway is currently the most thoroughly studied ubiquitin-dependent pathway [32]. Richard Youle’s team demonstrated [47] that PARKIN can clear damaged mitochondria, and the PINK1/PARKIN pathway was a key regulator for mitophagy.In general, PARKIN is responsible for attaching phosphorylated ubiquitin (p-Ub) molecules to substrate proteins, which are then recognized and degraded by UB-labeled proteases [17]. PINK1 can activate the E3 ubiquitin ligase of PARKIN by phosphorylating serine 65 in the ubiquitin-like domain of PARKIN [18]. PINK1 can activate the E3 ubiquitin ligase of PARKIN by phosphorylating serine 65 in the ubiquitin-like domain of PARKIN [18]. Following activation, PARKIN may ubiquitinate key mitochondrial proteins (e.g., MFN1,MFN2,VDAC1 or Miro1),a process that may promote the isolation of damaged mitochondria [40]. Meanwhile, the PINK1-phosphorylated ubiquitin chain can boost further PARKIN recruitment and activation, amplifying the mitophagy signal [18]. At this time, autophagy proteins p62 and OPTN can recognize mitochondrial protein polyubiquitination and interact with LC3 [48], ultimately forming a complex that can be degraded by the autophagy mechanism, thereby promoting autophagy completion [48]. (Fig. 2) Altogether, the PINK1/PARKIN regulatory system can regulate damaged mitochondrial clearance in three ways, including promoting fission by degrading fusion proteins; releasing and separating dysfunctional mitochondria from the mitochondrial network; and alternatively, through ubiquitination to mark the mitochondria that need to be degraded and form autophagosomes on the mitochondria that need to be degraded [32]. It inspires us that it is possible to regulate the clearance of damaged mitochondria by these three ways.In addition, mitochondria self-regulate through endogenous and exogenous pathways to control the quantity of proteins [33]. Ligand-receptor interactions can initiate exogenous apoptotic pathways; while endogenous pathways are modulated by changes in mitochondrial membrane permeability, leading to the release of cytochrome C and other apoptotic proteins that may trigger the process of apoptosis [33]; As one of many endogenous pathway families, Bcl-2 family B-cell lymphoma gene 2 (Bcl-2) can prevent cytochrome C from being released from mitochondria.These signals can activate the cytoplasmic signal transduction process to activate the caspase cascade reaction, which is the main executor of the apoptotic response [33]. In chemotherapy-resistant OC, the regulation of the caspase cascade can inhibit cell death to promote tumor progression [33].

Similarities and differences with other mitochondrial pathways

The receptor-type mitophagy pathway, with critical roles in this process, is different from that of the PINK1/PARKIN-dependent pathway in terms of the underlying mechanism [38]. Conversely, receptor pathways such as BNIP3/FUNDC1 are mainly triggered under conditions such as hypoxia and stress responses, leading to a direct binding to LC3 to induce mitophagy [49, 50]. The PINK1/PARKIN pathway induces mitophagy through a mechanism different from that of the receptor-type mitophagy pathway [32]. As evidenced previously, the main common feature of receptor mitophagy represented by Nix [51], BNIP3 [52] and FUNDC1 [53] is the existence of a conserved key domain, namely the LIR domain(LC3-interacting region domain), which can directly bind to other proteins [54]. Taking Nix/BNIP3 as an example, the Nix protein is capable of directly binding to the LC3 protein and recruiting it to damaged mitochondria to trigger mitophagy, which may be one of the receptors for mitophagy [54]. While BNIP3,as a homologous to Nix, is expressed in mitochondrial structure and endoplasmic reticulum environment [53]. When mitochondria are in a hypoxic state, BNIP3 may dissociate Beclin-1 from Bcl-2/Bcl-XL by competing with Beclin-1 for the binding of Bcl-2/Bcl-XL [53]. It binds Beclin-1 to mitochondria, recruits LC3 and gamma-amino butyric acid receptor associated protein (GABARAP) to mitochondria, thereby activating mitophagy [53]. Both factors can induce mitophagy by directly interacting with the LC3 protein through the LIR domain [54]. Meanwhile, BNIP3 phosphorylation can also enhance its binding to the LC3 protein and promote the completion of mitophagy [52]. In contrast, the PINK1/PARKIN pathway may also mediate the occurrence of mitophagy through the indirect binding of proteins [47, 48]. The similarity shared by the two pathways lies in that both maintain cellular homeostasis by eliminating damaged mitochondria, preventing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and apoptotic signal release, thereby regulating the number of mitochondria [37]. Secondly, as for the relationship between mitophagy and tumors, both factors can inhibit tumor proliferation and metastasis by regulating autophagy, and reverse chemotherapy resistance [55, 56]. The PINK1/PARKIN pathway is more dependent on the ubiquitination cascade, with a core role in chemotherapy resistance [57], while the receptor-based pathways (e.g., BNIP3) respond to hypoxic microenvironments and may promote metastasis by regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [53].Collectively, both are essential in achieving mitophagy, controlling mitochondrion number, and inhibiting tumor progression [39].

The dual role of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway in OC

Tumor suppressive effect (Early stage)

Mitochondria can selectively clear functionally impaired mitochondria through autophagy to stabilize intracellular environment, thereby regulating mitochondrial quality and promoting cell death or apoptosis [10]. Mitophagy is a key player in the modulation of cell death or apoptosis, which has been reported to be closely related to the development of cancer [13]. At this stage, PINK1 can be normally introduced into mitochondria and degraded, resulting in a state of low expression of this pathway [22]. By initiating the mitochondrial quality control program, it can reduce ROS production and genomic damage, and suppress the transformation of normal cells into cancer cells [22]. Mitophagy mainly regulates the expression of PARKIN, BNIP3,etc.,through tumor suppressor factors represented by P53 and oncogenes such as NF-κB, FOXO3 and HIF-1α,thereby driving tumor development and even mediating chemotherapy resistance critically [58]. PARKIN, as an important tumor suppressor gene [59], is located on chromosome 6q25-q27,and deletion often occurs at this site in various tumors such as OC, breast cancer, and bladder cancer [59]. PARKIN overexpression has been found to inhibit colorectal cancer cell proliferation, and its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity is the key to exert its anti-cancer effect [60]. PARKIN gene deletion has been detected in 33% of patients with colorectal cancer, resulting in the acceleration of adenoma development [61], further verifying its tumor suppressor function.The anti-cancer effect of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway may also be related to the regulation of apoptosis and metabolic reprogramming.It can inactivate the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 family in OC cells, promoting the release of cytochrome C by mitochondria, activating the caspase cascade reaction, and inducing cancer cell apoptosis [33]. Meanwhile, PARKIN can interact with pyruvate kinase M2,inhibit glycolysis, ubiquitinate and degrade HIF-1α,thereby inactivating its transcriptional targets, including proteins related to glycolysis, and further inhibiting the Warburg effect [10].Up-regulation of PARKIN expression can increase oxidative metabolism, enhance mitochondrial integrity, limit the Warburg effect downstream of p53,and further strengthen mitochondrial integrity, thereby explaining the tumor suppressive activity of PARKIN [62]. Furthermore, mitophagy is also pivotal in the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells [63]. In response to certain extreme conditions, such as hypoxia and starvation, mitophagy plays a role in quality regulation [24]. By reducing the overall mass of mitochondria, it can prevent the excessive production and accumulation of ROS, reduce ROS-induced DNA damage, and ensure normal cell survival under energy stress conditions [24]. At this point, the PINK1/PARKIN pathway can reduce ROS accumulation by eliminating damaged mitochondria, inhibit ROS-induced DNA damage and genomic instability, thereby delaying tumorigenesis [64]. Mitochondrial DNA damage can impair the function of mitochondrial complex I, which may generate a large amount of ROS and enhance the metastatic ability of tumor cells [65]. However, the intervention of ROS scavengers can reduce the metastatic ability of cells [65].

Concerning chemotherapy resistance, P53 inactivation may reduce the sensitivity to apoptotic responses, and hence increase the resistance to platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents [66]. The balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic in the BCL-2 family is critical in improving the sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy; The mitophagy mechanism can inhibit abnormal activation of the BCL-2 family to prevent apoptotic resistance [66]. The BCL-2 family, with anti-apoptotic property, can regulate apoptotic mitochondria by controlling outer mitochondrial membrane permeability [33]. The pro-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 associated X protein (Bax) can also regulate the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane; During its activation, Bax may translocate and insert into the mitochondrial outer membrane to disturb the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane [33]. However, Bax, even if not activated, will move to the outer mitochondrial membrane [66]. It have been proven that Mcl-1,Bcl21/Bcl-XL and Bcl-W can prevent the mitochondrial ubiquitination activity of PARKIN by disrupting or inhibiting PINK1-PARKIN interaction, thereby suppressing the initiation of mitophagy and exerting an inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation [66] (Fig. 3).

Tumor-promoting effect (middle and advanced stages)

Crucially, there may be hypoxia and nutritional deficiency within the tumor microenvironment as the tumor progresses to the middle and advanced stages [23]. In this context, there is an abnormally activated PINK1/PARKIN pathway, which may enhance the stress tolerance of cancer cells by strengthening mitophagy, eventually promoting tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance [23, 24]. At this stage, the impaired mitochondrial function may prevent PINK1 from being normally introduced into mitochondria [16]. It then accumulates in large quantities on the outer mitochondrial membrane and activates PARKIN [16]. Subsequently, the damaged mitochondria are cleared through the ubiquitination cascade reaction, providing energy support for cancer cell survival [67]. Under electron microscopy, it was noticed with adaptive changes such as increased number and length of mitochondria in OC tissues, and abnormal crest structure, which were closely related to PINK1/PARKIN pathway activation, thus meeting the energy requirements for the rapid proliferation of cancer cells [25]. Noticeably, the bidirectional interaction mechanism between tumor-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and the PINK1/PARKIN pathway is a major participator in the development of OC [68]. In the microenvironment of OC, CAFs form a “metabolic symbiosis” circuit with cancer cells by regulating PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, jointly promoting chemotherapy resistance, supporting this interaction was a potential new target for resistance reversal [68]. CAFs highly express transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) in hypoxic microenvironments, inhibit PARKIN expression in OC cells through the Smad2/3 signaling pathway, reduce mitophagy activity, and decrease DDP-induced apoptosis [60]. Cancer cells degrade mitochondrial ferritin through the PINK1/PARKIN pathway, releasing free iron into the microenvironment [69]. It, after being taken up by CAFs, may further activate the ferroptosis resistance pathway, in turn secreting IL-6 to maintain the stem cell-like characteristics of cancer cells [69]. Prior clinical evidence has demonstrated a negative correlation of the expression level of PINK1 in CAFs in platinum-resistant OC tissues with the expression of PARKIN in cancer cells, revealing a significant association with a shortened progression-free survival of patients [70].

The cancer-promoting effect of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway is also related to gene expression regulation and epigenetic modifications [31].In the studies of OC, neuroblastoma and breast cancer, some common features were discovered, manifesting as the up-regulation of N-Myc and cyclin E in these tumor cells and their progeny cells [30]. Therefore, it can be inferred that N-Myc may inhibit the transcription of the PARKIN gene [31]. The PARKIN protein may indirectly participate in the regulation of the OC cell cycle and accelerate the cell cycle process [31]. Meanwhile, the vulnerable areas of FRA6E containing PARKIN are often absent in breast cancer, with the detection of obviously down-regulated PARKIN expression in various breast cancer cell lines and primary breast cancer tissues, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion and other malignant biological behaviors [62]. While the deletion of PARKIN promoted their tumor biological behavior, suggesting its specific advantages in promoting tumor growth, in turn confirming the tumor suppressor effect of this factor [62]. At the epigenetic level, PARKIN promoter hypermethylation may lead to transcriptional silencing, reduce its expression level, and weaken its tumor suppressor effect [29], which may be related to its transcriptional silencing [71]. Moreover, in cardiomyocytes, low mitochondrial DNA replication is associated with mitophagy, possibly causing declined mitophagy, especially during starvation, a state where cellular energy is deprived [72]. Tumor-produced ROS can induce oxidative stress in related fibroblasts [73]. A large amount of ROS can accelerate DNA damage and genomic instability in malignant cells, thereby promoting the progression of cancer [73]. Secondly, these tumor-associated fibroblasts begin to reverse the Warburg effect, undergoing mitophagy and aerobic glycolysis, consequently causing tumor progression and drug resistance [73].Simultaneously, DNA methylation is the most common epigenetic modification in malignant tumors [74]. Abnormal methylation of CpG islands in OC is associated with cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, drug sensitivity and oncogene silencing [74]. Current evidence has documented that DNA demethylation can promote tumorigenesis by activating the transcription of oncogenes [71]. Demethylating agents can activate the silencing of endogenous retroviral RNA transcription, stimulate antiviral interferon signaling, and activate anti-tumor immune responses [30]. Therefore, DNA methylation may be critical in regulating gene expression during the competition between viruses and host genes [30]. Significantly, DNA demethylators can reverse the cancer-promoting effect of PARKIN by activating its expression, providing a target for epigenetic therapy [71]. Another study found [34] that in pancreatic tumors, PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy can degrade transferrin, and the accumulation of iron on mitochondria can trigger HIF-1α -dependent Warburg effect and inflammasome activation.Eventually, the loss of PINK1/PARKIN function may accelerate the occurrence of pancreatic tumors [34]. Mitophagy enables the regulation of metabolic reprogramming by inhibiting the Warburg effect.In the case of impaired key pathways or molecules mediating mitophagy (e.g.,defects in PINK1 and BNIP3),the increased aerobic glycolysis may boost the accelerated development of tumors [24].

Defects in the PINK1/PARKIN pathway and chemical resistance in OC

The core mechanism by which PINK1/PARKIN deficiency triggers platinum resistance

DDP has been used as the first-line therapeutic agent for chemotherapy of OC [75]. About 80% of patients receive chemotherapy with DDP plus paclitaxel or carboplatin plus paclitaxel [75], but 70% of advanced patients will develop resistance to DDP, leading to treatment failure and recurrence [76]. Critically, the occurrence of platinum resistance has been proven to be explained partially and significantly by defects in the PINK1/PARKIN pathway [35]. Specifically, dysfunction of this pathway can decrease the activity of mitophagy, which may hinder the effective clearance of the damaged mitochondria, induce the accumulation of ROS, and activate the DNA damage repair mechanism, thereby reducing the sensitivity of cancer cells to DDP [35]. Clinical data has mentioned that exposure of DDP-sensitive cell lines (OVCAR-3,OVCAR-4,and IGROV-1) to DDP resulted in significantly higher relative mitochondrial content and basal oxygen consumption rate than those of drug-resistant cell lines (OVCAR-5,OVCAR-8,and A2780) [77]. It indicates a strong relationship of changes in mitochondrial dynamics with DDP resistance [77]. The PINK1/PARKIN pathway may affect DDP resistance by regulating autophagy receptor p62 expression [35]. DDP may increase the quantity of LC3 II and reduce the quantity of p62 [35]. In the PINK1/PARKIN pathway, p62 is recruited into mitochondria in a PARKIN-dependent depolarization manner [48]. Therefore, DDP-induced knockdown of p62 can remarkably suppress mitophagy and enhance the drug resistance of OC cells to DDP [35]. Another study identified an E3 protein ligase, CRL4CUL4A/DDB1,whose down-regulation could increase endogenous LC3,and p62 accumulation, accompanied by escalated LC3-II levels [78];These LC3-II were then recruited into mitochondria in a PARKIN-dependent depolarization manner.The increase of p62 is essential in promoting mitophagy [78]. Knockdown of CRL4 CUL4A/DDB1 enhanced the expression of PINK1 and PARKIN in cisplatin-resistant OC cells (OCCs) and promoted autophagy in platinum-resistant OCCs cells, thereby inhibiting OCCs growth [78]. In addition, chemotherapy-induced senescence (CIS-chemotherapy-induced senescence) is also an important mechanism of platinum-based resistance [79]. Specifically, cell cycle arrest (mainly in the G1 phase) caused by chemotherapeutic agents is associated with DNA damage [79]. When there is a failure of repairing DNA damage caused by platinum-based drugs, OC cells tend to enter a senescent state rather than undergo apoptosis, known as CIS [79]. CIS-related metabolites are enriched in epithelial OC patients rich in tumor stem cells and polyploid giant cancer cells, and the latter ones can evade aging through multipolar mitosis and other non-classical cell division methods [80]. The generation of drug-resistant daughter cells explains the mechanism of some OC cells resuming growth in the short term after chemotherapy [79, 80]. Some researchers proposed that in mice with PINK1 knockout, there were significant defects in the cell cycle process, indicating the tumor-promoting property possessed by PINK1 [81]. Meanwhile, knockdown or absence of PINK1 has been found to enhance the sensitivity of OC cells to DDP, while PARKIN deficiency may stimulate the formation of drug resistance by damaging the tumor suppressor activity of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [60].

Furthermore, Previous studies have also demonstrated that cisplatin or paclitaxel induce OC cell death by enhancing mitochondrial fission and reducing Drp1 phosphorylation at serine 637 (p-Drp1Ser637),leading to the death of OC cells, while SIK2 is a key regulator of the Warburg effect in OC cells [82]. Overexpression of SIK2 in OC cells/tissues is associated with a poorer prognosis [82]. Overexpression of Drp1 can restore mitochondrial breakage caused by SIK2 knockdown in A2780 cells, while Drp1 knockdown can significantly inhibit mitochondrial breakage induced by SIK2 overexpression and promote the death of OC cells [82]. Zou [83] investigated the expression of mitochondrial dynamics related proteins in SKOV3 cells under DDP stress and found that DDP stress down-regulated Drp1, up-regulated all mitochondrial fusion proteins, including MFN1/MFN2 and OPA1,and increased mitophagy expression, which promoted the apoptosis of OC cells [83]. Normal levels of mitophagy are a defensive protective mechanism of the The body under unfavorable conditions, while excessive abnormal mitophagy may cause abnormal mitochondrial networks and energy metabolism disorders, thereby creating conditions for cancer occurrence and development due to the chaotic internal structure.Mitophagy plays a double-edged sword role in cancer, depending on the type and stage of cancer [12]. The resistance mechanism of OC cells to chemotherapeutic agents is rather complex, including but not limited to multidrug resistance (MDR), DNA damage response, cell metabolism, oxidative stress, cell cycle regulation, cancer stem cells (CSC), immunity, etc [84].

The expression of PINK1/PARKIN and epigenetic Silencing in OC tissues

The occurrence of OC is often accompanied by the down-regulation of the mitophagy mechanism [28]. Chemically induced mitophagy can inhibit the spread of OC [29]. As discovered by Wang J [72], gene mutations of MFN1,MFN2 and PINK1 participated significantly in the development of OC, all of which were risk factors affecting the prognosis of patients [72]. PTEN serves as a major tumor suppressor [60]. Its exogenous expression can up-regulate PINK1,while PARKIN deficiency may promote PTEN degradation, and activate the PI3K/AKT oncogenic pathway, forming a vicious cycle that promotes tumor progression [60].

The novel regulatory network composed of m6A modification and circular RNA can regulate the PINK1/PARKIN pathway [8]. In addition to traditional DNA methylation and miRNA regulation, the proposed novel regulatory network, m6A methyltransferase METTL3,can regulate the sponge adsorption of miR-125b by modifying circPARK2 (circular RNA derived from the PARKIN gene) [8]. It may further affect PARKIN expression, a new epigenetic aspect that is crucial for drug resistance in OC, offering a fresh perspective for regulating the PINK1/PARKIN pathway epigenetically [85]. Specifically, circPARK2 is highly expressed in platinum-resistant OC cells, and its sequence contains the m6A modification site (RRACH motif) [8]. Mettl3-mediated m6A modification can enhance the stability of circPARK2 and prevent it from being degraded by exonucidases [8]. Stable circPARK2 adsorbs miR-125b through sponges, relieving the translation inhibition of miR-125b on PARKIN mRNA, increasing PARKIN protein expression, leading to excessive mitophagy activation, and cancer cells tolerate DDP through “autophagy addiction“ [85]. Functional intervention experiments demonstrated that METTL3 knockdown or circPARK2 antisense oligonucleotide transfection could reduce PARKIN expression in platinum-resistant cells, double the apoptosis rate induced by DDP, and in the OC PDX model, reduce tumor volume [8].

Epigenetic modifications acts as a key regulator of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway [71]. Abnormal methylation of CpG islands in OC has a strong association with cell cycle regulation, apoptosis and drug sensitivity [74]. Among them, tumors with hypermethylated CpG promoter islands of SLFN11 type have increased chemotherapy resistance to platinum-based therapy [86].SLFN11 hypermethylation is an independent prognostic factor, with a tight relation to a shorter overall survival. Therefore, there may be an increase in chemotherapy resistance of OC with SLFN11 hypermethylation to platinum-based therapy, shortening the overall survival of patients [72, 86]. Despite insufficient research to directly unveil the PINK1/PARKIN pathway and DNA methylation, there is evidence suggesting that PARKIN promoter hypermethylation may be related to its transcriptional silencing [71]. As an important epigenetic regulatory factor, miRNA can regulate the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, thus affecting the activity of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway eventually regulating the progression of OC and chemotherapy resistance [85]. In addition, under energy deprivation conditions such as starvation, mitophagy genes (e.g.,PINK1,PARKIN, and OPTN) are significantly up-regulated, maintaining the survival of cancer cells by enhancing mitophagy [87]. Epigenetics, as a supplement to classical genetics, can regulate gene expression through DNA methylation, histone modification and non-coding RNA [74]. Theoretically, mitophagy can be targeted and inhibited through gene or drug pathways, thereby suppressing tumor growth and enhancing the sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents [88]. Silencing mitophagy-related genes (PARKIN, PINK1,BNIP3,Nix and FUNDC1) can inhibit the process of mitophagy and suppress tumor growth by blocking the formation of autophagosomes, inhibiting autophagosome-lysosome fusion and weakening autophagosome degradation [88].

Regulatory factors related to MDR

Approximately 70% of patients with OC will experience tumor recurrence, and the recurrent tumors often present as MDR, which is the main cause of death for patients [89]. MDR has been identified to be an important cause of chemotherapy failure and patient death.MDR is a drug resistance phenotype in which cancer cells can simultaneously develop resistance to multiple chemotherapeutic agents with different molecular targets and no obvious structural similarity [90]. The core regulatory factors of MDR include p-glycoprotein (P-gp) and MDR-associated protein, both of which can lower intracellular drug concentrations through adenosine triphosphate-dependent active drug transport functions.It may also decrease the sensitivity of cancer cells to multiple chemotherapeutic agents, weakening the efficacy of chemotherapy [91]. Overexpression of P-gp is an important feature of MDR in OC.The third-generation P-gp inhibitors, despite their entering into the research stage, have not yet been applied clinically due to issues such as cytotoxicity [91].

The association between the PINK1/PARKIN pathway and MDR is mainly achieved by regulating the tumor microenvironment and metabolic reprogramming [34]. Defects in this pathway can decrease mitophagy activity, promote ROS accumulation and HIF-1α activation, thereby up-regulating the expression of glycolycle-related genes and enhancing the tolerance of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents [34]. In addition, as a response to oxidative stress, the cell-activated transcription factor NRF2 is a major regulatory factor for antioxidants [92]. Activated NRF2 pathway may be involved in the occurrence of OC and platinum resistance [92]. Abnormal pathways can activate the NRF2 pathway, enhance the antioxidant capacity of cells, and reduce oxidative damage induced by chemotherapeutic agents [92]. Further aggravating MDR, inhibiting NRF2 is expected to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity.It has been reported that the suppression of NRF2 can increase the sensitivity of cellsto DNA damage caused by ROS, while PARP inhibitors (e.g.,olaparib) can also block DNA repair pathways [92]. Therefore, NRF2 inhibitors combined with PARP inhibitors may improve the therapeutic effect, especially in BRCA1-mutated cancer cells, with no serious side effects potentially [92]. In addition, the presence of CSC is also an important cause of MDR.Crucially, the PINK1/PARKIN pathway can influence drug resistance generation and maintenance by regulating the self-renewal and differentiation ability of CSC [78].

Strategies targeting the PINK1/PARKIN pathway to reverse drug resistance

PINK1/PARKIN agonists and their potential in cancer treatment

PINK1/PARKIN agonists can activate pathway activity, enhance mitophagy, promote apoptosis of drug-resistant cancer cells, and improve chemotherapy sensitivity, exhibiting significant clinical application potential [93]. Various potential agonists have been discovered so far.Among them, dihydroergotamine tartrate, as a keratinoid alkaloid derivative, can activate PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy and promote lung cancer cell death [93]; Sorafenib is a multi-kinase inhibitor that can stabilize PINK1 on the mitochondrial outer membrane by targeting the mitochondrial electron transport chain complex and adenosine triphosphate synthase, activate PARKIN recruitment, and enhance apoptosis in renal cancer cells and liver cancer cells [94]. The activation of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway can also promote mitophagy and apoptosis of cancer cells [32]. Currently, research on such agonists in OC is still in the experimental stage, with problems such as insufficient tumor penetration and off-target effects, despite their promising prospects in tumor treatment [95]. In the future, highly specific agonists are necessitated to be developed to enhance tumor targeting and reduce the impact on the mitochondrial function of normal cells, so as to provide possible support for clinical translation [95] (Table 1).

Epigenetic intervention

By regulating DNA methylation, histone modification and non-coding RNA expression, epigenetic intervention can restore the activity of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway and reverse chemotherapy resistance [71]. Approximately 10% of patients with epithelial OC have genetic susceptibility, with autosomal dominant inheritance of BRCA1/2 gene mutations being the main feature [96]. Intervention in epigenetic genes needs to improve the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agent treatment [97]. At present, epigenetic drugs, including the new generation of anti-cancer drugs, can be used alone or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity [98]. For example, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors(PARPis), as the first targeted drug for OC, can prevent single-stranded DNA damage repair and significantly improve the 5-year survival and platinum-based drug sensitivity of BRCA1 mutation-harbored patients [97, 98]. Ginsenoside Rg3 can reduce the mRNA and protein expression of DNA methyltransferase, promote the expression of tumor suppressor genes (e.g.,p53) through demethylation, thereby inhibiting OC cell migration and invasion, and promoting its cell apoptosis [99]. DNA demethylating agents can activate endogenous retroviral RNA transcription, stimulate antiviral interferon signals, and activate anti-tumor immune responses [30]. The therapeutic effects can be significantly enhanced in the context of combined use with chemotherapeutic agents [100]. In addition, miRNA-targeted therapy can also restore the tumor suppressor function of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway by regulating the expression of genes related to this pathway, providing a new direction for epigenetic therapy [85]. Combination therapy

Combination therapy can effectively overcome the limitations of monotherapy and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity through the synergistic action of multiple targets [92]. For instance, a combined use of NRF2 inhibitors and PARP inhibitors can increase the sensitivity of cancer cells to ROS-induced DNA damage, block the DNA repair pathway, exhibiting synergistic therapeutic effect in BRCA1-mutated cancer cells, with controllable side effects [92]. In the treatment of advanced epithelial OC, bevacizumab (VEGF-targeted monoclonal antibody) combined with olaparib (PARP inhibitor) can extend the progression-free survival of patients with negative homologous recombination repair deficiency by 2 years [101]. Epigenetic drugs used in combination with paclitaxel and platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents can significantly enhance the therapeutic effect [102]; ATR inhibitors plus PARP inhibitors can reduce the resistance of OC cells to PARP inhibitors and platinum-based drugs, especially for patients with congenital drug resistance [103]. The mitochondrial-targeted drug delivery system enhances the therapeutic efficacy of PINK1/PARKIN agonists

Liposome nanocarriers modified with mitochondrial-targeted peptides can precisely deliver PINK1/PARKIN agonists (e.g.,sorafenib derivatives) to the mitochondria of OC cells, thereby lowering the off-target effect, and reversing the drug tolerance barrier in the tumor microenvironment simultaneously [95]. The liposome surface is modified with mitochondrial-targeted peptides, which can specifically bind to the VDAC1 protein on the mitochondrial membrane [95]. The uptake efficiency of OC cells is 5.3times that of ordinary liposomes [101]. The vector simultaneously loads sorafenib derivative (SFN-2) and glucose oxidase (GOx) [104]. GOx can consume glucose in the tumor microenvironment, exacerbate hypoxia-induced PINK1 accumulation, and collaboratively activate mitophagy with SFN-2 [104]. The tumor suppression rate of this nano-formulation reached 78. 6% in the platinum-resistant OC PDX model, which was 42% higher than that of the free drug, without the observation of any significant damage to the liver or kidney function [96].

Discussion and prospect

This review systematically clarifies the dual regulation of PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy in the progression of OC and chemotherapy resistance.Mitophagy is regarded as a double-edged sword in cancer [12]. It can reduce oxidative stress by eliminating dysfunctional or redundant mitochondria to exert an inhibitory effect on cancer ([37]; while it can also protect tumor cells from apoptosis or necrosis to enhance their survival under stress, thereby promoting cancer progression ([12]. As for its regulatory mechanism: In the early stage, it possesses an anti-cancer effect by maintaining mitochondrial mass homeostasis ([22]; but it may promote tumor progression and drug resistance formation by enhancing the stress tolerance of cancer cells in the middle and late stages ([23]. Furthermore, regulating the activity of this pathway directly affects chemotherapy sensitivity and prognosis of patients with OC ([35]. Its defects can exacerbate platinum resistance through various mechanisms (e.g.,p62 expression down-regulation, epigenetic modifications, metabolic reprogramming, etc.),becoming an important obstacle to clinical treatment [35, 60].Significantly, PINK1/PARKIN agonists, epigenetic therapy and other combination regimens are expected to provide active treatment options for clinical treatment, which may contribute to the reversal of the development of chemotherapy resistance [93, 98].

At the same time, there exists heterogeneity of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway at the single-cell level and the formation of drug-resistant subpopulations [105]. In OC tissues, PINK1high/PARKINlow and PINK1low/PARKINhigh are two functional heterogeneous cell subpopulations [105]. The former subpopulation can tolerate chemotherapy by enhancing mitophagy, while the latter one may promote metastasis through metabolic reprogramming [105, 106]. Targeting subgroup-specific markers can achieve precise treatment.The proportion of the PINK1high/PARKINlow subpopulation reached 32.7% in platinum-resistant OC tissues [105]. This subpopulation highly expresses the autophagy receptor OPTN and relies on mitophagy to clear chemotherapy-induced damaged mitochondria [105]. Meanwhile, the PINK1low/PARKINhigh subgroup highly expresses the EMT transcription factor Snail, which degrades E-cadherin through PARKIN-mediated ubiquitination, promotes cell invasion, and has higher sensitivity to PARP inhibitors [106]. Tumors formed by the PINK1high/PARKINlow subgroup have a 2.8-fold increased resistance rate to DDP, and a combined use with OPTN inhibitors can restore sensitivity [104, 105].

However, among various therapeutic regimens currently available for OC, the decreased sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents remains the main cause of poor therapeutic outcomes leading to patient deaths [89]. Existing studies support that the PINK1/PARKIN signaling pathway can promote the accelerated apoptosis of OC cells by removing functionally impaired mitochondria [33]. An activation of this pathway can significantly enhance the sensitivity to DDP [35]. However, there are still many problems regarding these pathways, the mechanisms between related proteins, the mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance, and the implementation of treatment plans [107].

The core issues existing in the current research include: ① there is still a poor understanding of the regulatory mechanism of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway in OC, the protein-protein interaction network, signal crossover and off-target risk involved [32]. ② The subtypes of OC are highly heterogeneous [107]. There are differences in mitophagy activity and pathway function among different pathological types (e.g., well-differentiated plasmocytic carcinoma and clear cell carcinoma), and there is a lack of personalized treatment strategies [107]. ③ There is still a lack of targeting of the current drug delivery system [95]. The existing PINK1 kinase inhibitors have poor tumor penetration and are prone to affect the mitochondrial function of normal cells [95]. ④ Most studies are still at the stage of cell models and animal experiments, awith inadequate evidence for clinical translation [93]. Accordingly, corresponding therapeutic effect requires to be verified by further multi-center clinical trials [98].

In response to the above issues, future research directions can focus on: ① in-depth exploration of the regulatory mechanisms of PINK1/PARKIN pathway-related proteins (e.g.,p62 and OPTN), and construction of a precise and stable targeted treatment network [48]; ② multi-center clinical studies to analyze the functional differences of pathways in different subtypes of OC and development of personalized treatment plans [107]; ③ formulation of novel drug delivery systems to enhance the tumor targeting of PINK1/PARKIN agonists and inhibitors and to reduce off-target effects [95]; and ④ improvement of multi-disciplinary cooperation, acceleration of the research and development of highly specific inhibitors, optimization of combination therapies, development of dynamic monitoring technologies, and the speeding of clinical transformation [92]. Indeed, there are still limitations in the understanding of PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy in OC at present.Nevertheless and importantly, with the continuous deepening of research, this The pathway is expected to become a core target for reversing chemotherapy resistance in OC, which may provide fresh therapeutic direction for improving patient survival rates.
