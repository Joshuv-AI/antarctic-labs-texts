Navigating workplace ostracism: the potential protective power of career adaptability against job burnout
Authors: Ye Geng, Yanan Sun, Xue Li, Yuqi Wu, Xiaodan Liu, Hangyu Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe issue of job burnout among hemodialysis nurses is becoming increasingly prominent. However, existing research has mainly concentrated on organizational and individual factors, overlooking the impact of negative interpersonal interactions. Workplace ostracism may contribute to job burnout among hemodialysis nurses, though the underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. This study examined the potential mediating role of workplace loneliness and the moderating effect of career adaptability within the Conservation of Resources framework.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in November 2025 among 434 hemodialysis nurses from 30 blood purification centers in Liaoning Province, China. Data were collected using validated instruments: the Workplace Ostracism Scale, Loneliness at Work Scale, Maslach Burnout Inventory, and Career Adapt-Abilities Scale. Mediation and moderated mediation analyses were performed using PROCESS macro with 5,000 bootstrap samples.ResultsWorkplace ostracism positively predicted job burnout (β = 0.355, P < 0.001). Workplace loneliness accounted for 23.9% of this total effect; its indirect effect remained significant (β = 0.085, P < 0.001 ).The association between workplace loneliness and job burnout was moderated by career adaptability (β = -0. 115, P < 0. 001);Once career adaptability surpassed the standardized cut-off point of 0.515, the effect was no longer statistically significant.ConclusionThis study found associations between workplace ostracism, workplace loneliness, and job burnout. Workplace loneliness mediated the relationship between workplace ostracism and job burnout, and career adaptability moderated the association between workplace loneliness and job burnout. Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that at least one in four health workers suffers from anxiety, depression, or job burnout [1]. In the United States, nearly half of all registered nurses reported experiencing burnout, whereas post-pandemic survey indicated that over three-quarters of nurses in China were affected [2, 3]. Nurse burnout now poses a serious threat to healthcare systems worldwide. Job burnout strikes roughly 30% of nurses across nations and specialties, with those in lower-income countries and high-stress units bearing the heaviest burden [4, 5].

Hemodialysis (HD) nursing illustrates this problem with particular clarity. HD nurses face a battery of occupational hazards: alarm fatigue from constant machine signals [6]; hand pain at twice the rate of other healthcare staff [7]; needle-stick injuries affecting nearly a quarter of the workforce [8]; and chronic irritation of eyes and airways from low-level chemical exposure [9]. High-intensity job demands, thin resources, chronic understaffing, and weak social support push many toward the exit [10, 11]. Studies showed that 40.4% of nurses in American HD units still plan to leave, compared with 68.1% of their counterparts in south-west China [12–14].

Psychosocial risks at workplace lie at the core of job burnout [15]. Strong social support and positive interpersonal relationships function as conditional resources that buffer against psychosocial distress [16, 17]. Although previous studies have primarily examined how organizational characteristics and individual factors contribute to job burnout among HD nurses, the crucial role of negative interpersonal interactions remains insufficiently explored and warrants urgent attention [18–22]. The quality of workplace relationships shapes job burnout in powerful ways. Workplace ostracism is defined as the subjective experience of feeling ignored, isolated, or rejected by others in the workplace [23]. It is characterized by its low-intensity and ambiguous nature, which distinguishes it from more explicit forms of interpersonal mistreatment like incivility or bullying, as the perpetrator’s intent to harm is often unclear. According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals continuously exposed to resource threat environments will initiate resource defense mechanisms, resulting in the sustained depletion of psychological resources [24]. In organizational behavior research, workplace ostracism has been demonstrated to be significantly associated with core dimensions of job burnout, including emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [25–28]. Workplace ostracism disrupts the reciprocity norms governing social interaction, causing the loss of individuals’ access to conditional resources. To counteract the psychosocial threats posed by workplace ostracism, individuals adopt resource regulation strategies to resist the process of resource consumption. When the rate of resource depletion exceeds that of replenishment, individuals may manifest job burnout. Nurses who experience workplace ostracism show higher job burnout than those who do not [29, 30]. In high job demands–resources contexts, this pathway of resource depletion remains poorly understood.

Workplace ostracism blocks access to social resources. The victims face a widening gap between the relationships they expect and what they actually have [31]. This painful discrepancy is workplace loneliness-the uncomfortable feelings arising from a lack of appropriate social connections [32]. It signals the start of resource depletion, a subjective state blending cognition and feeling. Loneliness shifts as circumstances change; fresh resources can close the gap before the spiral becomes unstoppable. Nurses depend heavily on formal and informal ties for information and emotional sustenance, leaving them especially vulnerable [33]. Persistent loneliness confirms and deepens the sense of loss. Closing the gap demands extra effort, and sustained expenditure hollows out core reserves, producing emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment. Whether workplace loneliness transmits workplace ostracism’s damage to job burnout has not been examined in the nursing field.

Career adaptability reflects the readiness to cope with the predictable tasks of preparing for and participating in the work role and with the unpredictable adjustments prompted by changes in work and working conditions [34]. As a personal resource, it helps individual tap inner reserves, find alternative support and maintain well-being. It may weaken the relationship between workplace loneliness and job burnout in three ways: (1) Nurses with high career adaptability are more adept at proactively seeking new social resource connections or tapping into personal resources (e.g., self-efficacy, optimism), thereby compensating for resource losses [35]; (2) they are more inclined to perceive loneliness as a temporary and modifiable challenge, and this cognitive reappraisal alleviates the helplessness and stress induced by loneliness, attenuating its psychological impact as a stressor [36]; (3) the psychological resources embedded in career adaptability (e.g., resilience, optimism) help individuals maintain emotional stability and decelerate the depletion rate of emotional resources, thereby interrupting the rapid development of emotional exhaustion [37]. Whether career adaptability actually dampens this relationship in practice remains an open question.

This study was based on the COR theory to explore potential associations between workplace ostracism and burnout among HD nurses. The theory holds that individuals strive to protect and build valued resources; that loss outweighs gain in psychological impact; that repeated loss spins into a self-feeding spiral and that personal resources can buffer this descent [24]. Resources cover object, condition, energy and personal. Researchers have widely utilized the COR theory to elucidate the relationship between negative work events and employee well-being. Workplace ostracism attacks conditional resources, workplace loneliness propagates the loss and job burnout marks the final depletion of energy, with career adaptability slowing the acceleration from loss to collapse. Theoretical framework and research model

This study aimed to accomplish three main objectives: (1) To examine directly the relationship between workplace ostracism and job burnout among HD nurses; (2) To test the mediating role of workplace loneliness in the pathway from workplace ostracism to job burnout; (3) To explore career adaptability as a moderating factor affecting of the relationship between workplace loneliness and job burnout. The hypotheses were presented:

H1

Workplace ostracism positively predicts job burnout among HD nurses.

H2

Workplace loneliness mediates the relationship between workplace ostracism and job burnout.

H3

Career adaptability potentially negatively moderates the relationship between workplace loneliness and job burnout, such that higher levels of career adaptability weaken the strength of this association.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in November 2025 on nurses of 30 blood purification centers in Liaoning Province by convenience sampling. Path analysis was used to estimate the path coefficients among variables that determine job burnout among HD nurses. The report was written in accordance with the STROBE statement.

Sample size and subjects

Sample size estimation formula based on descriptive study measurement data: . Referring to previous literature, the score of job burnout among HD nurses in China was 38.69 ± 17.47 [38]. α = 0.05, ε = 0.05. The sample size of this study was at least 324.

The study included 434 HD nurses who held a valid registered nurse license in the People’s Republic of China and had at least six months of experience in blood purification nursing. These participants were recruited from tertiary hospitals, secondary hospitals, and private dialysis centers. Nurses temporarily off work due to illness were excluded.

Instrument tools

General information questionnaire

A researcher-designed questionnaire was used to collect information, including gender, age, nursing age, education level, and weekly working hours, to evaluate the influence of background factors on HD nurses.

Workplace ostracism

The Workplace Ostracism Scale (WOS), developed by Ferris in 2008, measures employee perception of workplace ostracism [23]. The scale consisted of 10 items. The items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) and the total scores ranging from 10 to 70. A sample item was “Others from switched region when you entered”. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the scale ranged from 0.89 to 0.96. Among Chinese nurses, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.979 [39]. Higher scores on the scale indicate a greater degree of perceived workplace ostracism. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.927.

Workplace loneliness

To measure perceived work-related loneliness, Wright developed the Loneliness at Work Scale (LAWS) in 2006 [32]. The scale consisted of 16 items divided into two dimensions: emotional deprivation (9 items) and social companionship (7 items) .The items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) and the total scores ranging from 16 to 112. A sample item was “I frequently feel forsaken by my coworkers when I am under stress at work”. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.930 for emotional deprivation and 0.870 for social companionship. Higher scores on the scale indicate greater perceived workplace loneliness. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.930.

Job burnout

Job burnout levels were assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), originally developed by Maslach in 1986 and later adapted for Chinese nurses [40, 41]. In this study, the evaluation of burnout status among HD nurses used the Chinese version of the MBI.The scale consisted of 22 items divided into three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (9 items), depersonalization (5 items) and reduced personal accomplishment (8 items). The items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) and the total scores ranging from 22 to 110. Higher scores on emotional exhaustion and depersonalization indicate greater job burnout, with cut-off values of 26 and 9. Lower scores on reduced personal accomplishment suggest higher job burnout, with a cut-off value of 34. A sample item was:“I feel burned out from my work”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.930. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of three dimensions ranged from 0.81 to 0.91. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.943.

Career adaptability

In 2012, Porfeli and Savickas constructed the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS) to measure a person’s ability to adjust themselves with social changes and remain in tune with their environments [34]. The scale consisted of 24 items divided into four dimensions: concern, curiosity, control, and confidence (6 items in each dimension). The Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not strong to 5 = very strong) and the total scores ranging from 24 to 120. A sample item was “Thinking about what my future will be like”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.92 and the Cronbach’s α coefficients of different dimensions ranged from 0.74 to 0.85. Among Chinese nurses, the Cronbach’s α coefficients of different dimensions ranged from 0.90 to 0.96 [42]. A higher score indicates a higher readiness of an individual to adjust to changes in work conditions. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.932.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection and analysis in this study consideration were held on three ethical principles: (1)No personally identifying information was requested; (2)Informed consent was obtained before participants completed the questionnaire; (3) Team members were trained on confidentiality aspect and adhere to it strictly. Data were analyzed with SPSS 27. The characteristics information was summarized as frequency and percentage. The scores were described with the average ± standard deviation. Background variables difference were examined with independent sample t-tests or one-way ANOVA, while correlation between the variables used Pearson correlations. Reliability was evaluated by internal consistency analysis. To address potential common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was performed [43]. Multiple-meditation models (PROCESS macro Models 4 and 14) were estimated with PROCESS v4.1. Indirect effects were tested with 5,000 bias-corrected bootstrap samples and 95% confidence intervals (CI); effects were considered significant if the CI did not include zero. To control Type I error due to multiple comparisons, all tests were two-tailed with α = 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The research received approval from the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of China Medical University(No.2025 − 865).

Discussion

This study found that workplace loneliness mediated the association between workplace ostracism and job burnout among HD nurses. Additionally, career adaptability moderated the relationship between workplace loneliness and job burnout, which may indicate a buffering effect against resource depletion.

Status of job burnout among HD nurses

In this study, HD nurses reported a reduced personal accomplishment score of 19.34 ± 7.85 and a depersonalization score of 12.22 ± 5.17, indicating elevated burnout in these dimensions. They also exhibited moderate overall burnout, with an emotional exhaustion score of 22.29 ± 8.42. Several factors may be associated with higher burnout levels among HD nurses: (1) An aging population in Northeast China and a growing number of end-stage renal disease patients have significantly increased the workload of dialysis nurses [44]; (2) The low public investment in health has led to an unattractive salary structure and deepened burnout. The pressure of economic transformation in northeastern China and the slower growth rate of local health fiscal revenue relative to that in eastern coastal areas also restrict investment in public hospitals [45]; (3) There is ongoing devaluation of nursing at all levels. This underestimation is not accidental but is manifested at multiple levels. The current medical payment system primarily reimburses tangible technical procedures, while nursing core values—clinical judgment, patient monitoring, and emotional support—are classified as “intangible” labor, limiting nurses’ economic compensation [46]. Hospital performance evaluation systems focus primarily on medical activities, while nursing-sensitive indicators related to patient safety and quality are excluded from resource allocation metrics, indirectly devaluing nursing’s professional contributions [47]. When media and organizational narratives consistently portray nurses as “willing self-sacrificers,” their identities as clinical experts and scientific authorities are overlooked [48]. This stereotype not only weakens public recognition of nursing professional autonomy but also directly hinders nurses from obtaining equal voices in interdisciplinary decision-making. (4) Chronic emotional stress was positively associated with emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [49]. Where organizational support is perceived as inadequate, nurses may exhibit behaviors that could contribute to patient dissatisfaction [5, 50]. Based on these observations, potential directions for future research include: (1) examination of workload optimization strategies; (2) development of appraisal systems valuing holistic care; (3) exploration of specialized certification roles; and (4) piloting of structured psychological support.

Mediating effects of workplace loneliness

This study identified workplace loneliness as a mediator in the association between workplace ostracism and job burnout. COR theory posits that when individuals face stressful situations, resource loss has a stronger psychological impact than resource gain [24]. This study found that workplace ostracism was associated with elevated workplace loneliness and job burnout. In the context of the nursing profession, which involves high emotional labor and high interpersonal interaction, emotions themselves constituted a crucial personal resource [51]. When individuals perceived workplace ostracism from colleagues or superiors, their perception of social support weakened, which in turn triggered negative emotional responses such as loneliness, anxiety and anger [52–54]. These emotional states were associated with perceived depletion of emotional resources and reduced access to interpersonal support channels, patterns consistent with the resource loss spiral described in COR theory [55]. These findings suggest that workplace loneliness may function not only as an emotional outcome but also as a mediating variable reflecting impeded resource-replenishment pathways. By embedding this mechanism within the COR framework, the study detailed how ostracism propelled nurses into a helpless and isolated state in which resource regeneration became extremely difficult. Loneliness weakened organizational belonging, reduced emotional expression and help-seeking intentions, and lowered the probability of obtaining emotional support through social interaction [32]. Prolonged failure to restore emotional resources led to emotional exhaustion, which in turn decreased work engagement and increased counterproductive work behaviors while reducing organizational citizenship behaviors [56].

Consistent with COR theory that resources are embedded in interpersonal and environmental channels, workplace ostracism may function as a key barrier blocking nurses’ emotional resource channels, whereas workplace loneliness represented the psychological rupture of these channels.These findings suggest potential targets for future intervention research: (1) inclusive team climates and peer support networks; (2) manager training for recognizing covert exclusion; and (3) emotion regulation and interpersonal skills training.

The moderating effect of career adaptability

This study examined career adaptability as a moderator and found that it attenuated the association between workplace loneliness and job burnout.Individuals with high career adaptability possessed four resources: concern, curiosity, confidence, and control. They could quickly initiate alternative resource acquisition strategies when workplace loneliness increased, thereby reducing the net loss of emotional resources. The moderating effect of career adaptability is consistent with the COR theory’s proposition that personal resources may buffer the association between resource loss and burnout. This study found that higher career adaptability was associated with a weaker relationship between workplace loneliness and job burnout, consistent with the proposition that personal resources may buffer resource loss. The result was consistent with previous research findings that the higher the career adaptability, the gentler the resource loss slope of employees facing stressors [57]. This finding suggests that career adaptability may function as a protective factor, though its effectiveness in preventive interventions requires empirical testing. A potential intervention model for future application is outlined in detail in the Supplementary Material, though its effectiveness remains to be tested.

Limitations and future research directions

This study examined potential social-psychological mechanisms linking workplace ostracism to job burnout. Several limitations inform directions for future research. (1) This study used a cross-sectional design. Although it revealed associations between variables, causal inferences could not be established. Future research might employ longitudinal designs to assess temporal relationships and test mediating mechanisms more rigorously.(2) Convenience sampling from 30 blood purification centers in Liaoning Province limits representativeness and generalizability. Findings require replication in healthcare settings with diverse cultural contexts, as HD nurses in coastal or western China may experience different workplace conditions.(3) Although this study focused on social-psychological pathways, organizational factors (e.g., leadership style) and individual factors (e.g., personality traits) were not controlled, potentially confounding observed associations. Future research might include these as covariates or moderators to elucidate these relationships more comprehensively.

Conclusions

This study found associations between workplace ostracism, workplace loneliness, and job burnout. Workplace loneliness mediated the relationship between workplace ostracism and job burnout, and career adaptability moderated the association between workplace loneliness and job burnout. These findings suggest that workplace relational factors and personal resources may warrant attention in future occupational health research.
