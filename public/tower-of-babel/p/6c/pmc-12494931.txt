Long-term outcomes in pediatric brain AVMs: beyond radiological cure
Authors: Francesco MC Lioi, Alessandro De Benedictis, Davide Luglietto, Franco Randi, Carlo Gandolfo, Giulia Lucignani, Maria Lopez Gutierrez, Carlo E Marras
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain AVMs are typically considered cured after angiographic confirmation of nidus obliteration. However, in pediatric patients, recurrence can occur many years later, underscoring the need for long-term clinical surveillance. Event-free survival (EFS) represents a complementary, exploratory metric of therapeutic success that captures time-dependent morbidity.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 46 pediatric patients with brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) treated between 2013 and 2025. Clinical data, bAVM characteristics, treatment strategies, and outcomes were systematically collected. EFS was defined as the absence of clinically significant neurological complications throughout follow-up, whether early or late, and attributable to bAVM natural history or treatment-related effects. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess EFS by radiological cure status. Long-term functional outcomes were evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).ResultsMedian age was 9.8 years; 78.3 % presented with hemorrhage. Radiological cure was achieved in 77.5 % of patients completing treatment. At final follow-up (median 56.5 months; 64 months in the EFS subset), 87 % had a favorable outcome (mRS ≤2). Clinical events occurred both early (≤30 days) and late (>30 days), with late events predominating. Over 30 % of patients with confirmed radiological cure experienced events. Long-term events were not associated with hemorrhagic onset, nor with urgent surgical treatment, suggesting they are not merely acute-phase complications. Two angiographic recurrences occurred after more than 9 years.ConclusionEvent-free survival (EFS) is a complementary metric that reflects long-term morbidity from both natural history and treatments, highlighting the persistence of events beyond radiological cure and supporting extended follow-up in pediatric AVMs.Keywords: Brain arteriovenous malformations, Pediatric neurosurgery, Event-free survival, AVM recurrence, Long-term functional outcome

Introduction

Brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) are rare in children but represent a major cause of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage in the pediatric population (Aboukaïs et al., 2017; Della Puppa and Scienza, 2016). Management is complex due to longer life expectancy, recurrence potential, and cumulative risks associated with multimodal treatment [19]. Angiographic obliteration of the nidus is widely accepted as a therapeutic success. How this radiological endpoint intersects with long-term clinical well-being warrants further scrutiny. In real-world practice, follow-up is often de-escalated once cure is confirmed angiographically (Lauzier et al., 2021). However, recurrence has been reported months or even years after treatment—even in patients previously considered cured [19]. In addition, other clinically meaningful events, both early and late, may impact functional outcomes, regardless of their timing or pathophysiology. Yet, the full range of long-term outcomes after radiological cure remains incompletely understood (Abecassis et al., 2016). In this study, we assess event-free survival (EFS)—defined as the absence of clinically significant neurological events attributable to bAVM natural history or treatment, either early (≤30 days) or late (>30 days)—in a cohort of 46 consecutive pediatric patients treated between 2013 and 2025 for brain AVMs. EFS is proposed here as a complementary, exploratory metric, not intended to replace traditional endpoints, but to capture time-dependent morbidity throughout follow-up. We specifically examine whether patients with confirmed radiological cure remain at risk of such events, and how these may influence long-term functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study included pediatric patients (<18 years) diagnosed with brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) between 2013 and 2025 consecutively treated at Bambino Gesù Children's Hospital, a tertiary referral pediatric center in Rome, Italy. Inclusion criteria were: bAVM diagnosis confirmed by digital subtraction angiography (DSA); a tailored treatment strategy defined for each patient during a dedicated multidisciplinary neurovascular case conference; availability of early postoperative imaging; and a minimum of 12 months of combined clinical and radiological follow-up. Patients with incomplete records or still under radiosurgical surveillance at the time of analysis were excluded from outcome evaluation.

Data collection

Clinical and imaging data were retrospectively collected by a primary investigator (F.L.) and independently verified by two co-investigators. AVM characteristics were assessed from pre-treatment imaging, and Spetzler-Martin and Spetzler-Ponce grades were retrospectively assigned by consensus. Emergency procedures (e.g., ICP monitoring, EVD, major surgeries) were recorded separately for outcome analysis. Elective treatments included microsurgical resection, endovascular embolization, and radiosurgery, applied alone or in combination according to lesion complexity. Surgeries were performed by a senior neurosurgeon (C.M.) using neuronavigation, intraoperative ultrasound, and ICG videoangiography to confirm nidus removal. Embolization was performed via transarterial access with NBCA (N-Butyl Cyanoacrylate), Onyx, or coils, based on angioarchitecture and treatment intent. Radiosurgery (CyberKnife or GammaKnife) was delivered at national centers according to institutional protocols, with dose planning tailored to AVM size, location, and eloquence.

Definitions and endpoints

Treatment completion was defined as the full execution of the therapeutic plan established by the multidisciplinary neurovascular team, including post-treatment imaging. Radiological cure was defined as complete AVM nidus obliteration, confirmed by postoperative DSA and follow-up MRI performed at least six months later. Residual AVM referred to persistent nidus or arteriovenous shunting on follow-up imaging. These patients, although eligible for further therapy, were considered not cured for analysis, as the initial treatment plan was not successfully completed. Recurrence was defined as new angiographic evidence of shunting in a previously cured patient. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the absence of significant clinical events throughout follow-up, whether early or late, and attributable to either AVM natural history or treatment effects. Events included rebleeding, new-onset seizures, hydrocephalus, or neuropsychological disturbances. Neuropsychological disturbances were defined as newly emerging cognitive or behavioral dysfunctions—including impairments in attention, memory, executive function, or mood regulation—that became evident beyond the acute phase and required clinical evaluation or follow-up. Although formal neuropsychological testing was not yet implemented systematically during the study period, these events were identified through structured neurologic or psychologic assessment. Their inclusion reflects a progressive integration of neuropsychological surveillance into our pediatric neurovascular practice, adapted from established protocols in pediatric neuro-oncology and other conditions. These disturbances were considered clinically relevant due to their potential long-term impact on functional independence and quality of life. Treatment-related complications were classified according to the Landriel Ibañez grading system, which accounts for severity and therapeutic implications, and were considered a subset of clinical events. Functional outcomes were assessed retrospectively using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), based on neurosurgical records and physiatric evaluations, at ∼1 month and at last follow-up. Outcomes were defined as favorable (mRS ≤2) or unfavorable (mRS 3–6). Patients were stratified by radiological cure to evaluate the impact of clinical events on long-term function.

Follow-up

Clinical and radiological follow-up was conducted at standardized intervals according to institutional protocols and adjusted for treatment modality and individual patient risk profiles. Follow-up imaging comprised both MRI and digital subtraction angiography (DSA), with modality selection determined by the multidisciplinary neurovascular team. The median follow-up duration was 56.5 months (interquartile range: 36–104.5 months), with a substantial subset of patients monitored for over 8 years, allowing assessment of both early and very late events.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for all variables. Categorical data were compared using Fisher's exact test, while paired ordinal outcomes (mRS scores) were analyzed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Event-free survival was estimated via Kaplan–Meier analysis, stratified by radiological cure status, and differences between survival curves were assessed using the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed with Jamovi software (version 2.4.8; www.jamovi.org). A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 46 consecutive pediatric patients with brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) were included (Fig. 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 9.9 ± 3.8 years (range: 2–17), with an equal gender distribution (50 % male). Most patients presented with intracranial hemorrhage (n = 36, 78.3 %), followed by incidental findings (n = 5, 10.9 %), seizures (n = 3, 6.5 %), and focal neurological deficits (n = 2, 4.3 %). One patient (2.2 %) died acutely following hemorrhagic onset. Median follow-up was 56.5 months (IQR: 36.0–104.5), with 30.4 % (n = 14) of patients monitored for over 8 years. Of the 46 patients, 43 completed treatment (40 actively treated, 3 conservative), while 3 were still undergoing radiosurgery (Table 1a).

AVM taxonomy

Of 46 bAVMs, most were located in the supratentorial compartment (n = 29, 63.0 %), followed by the posterior cranial fossa (n = 11, 24 %) which includes cerebellum and brainstem, and deep-seated regions (n = 6, 13.0 %), including basal ganglia, periventricular areas, and intraventricular locations. Of the 46 bAVMs, morphological analysis was possible in 44 cases, as 2 patients lacked sufficient preoperative imaging to allow accurate classification (Table 1b). Among the 44 classifiable cases, nidus was classified as diffuse in 25 patients (54.3 %) and compact in 18 (39.1 %), while 3 cases (6.5 %) could not be reliably categorized due to incomplete preoperative imaging. Based on early arterial-phase DSA, 28 AVMs (60.9 %) measured less than 3 cm, 14 (30.4 %) were 3–6 cm, and 2 (4.3 %) exceeded 6 cm. Eloquent brain regions were involved in 22 patients (47.8 %), spared in 22 (47.8 %), and unassessable in 2 (4.3 %) due to incomplete data. Venous drainage was superficial in 26 patients (56.5 %) and deep in 18 (39.1 %), with 2 unclassified cases. Associated aneurysms were identified in 15 patients (32.6 %). According to Spetzler–Martin grading, AVMs were distributed as follows: grade I in 13 patients (28.3 %), grade II in 11 (23.9 %), grade III in 14 (30.4 %), grade IV in 5 (10.9 %), and grade V in 1 patient (2.2 %). Based on the Spetzler–Ponce system, 24 AVMs (52.2 %) were class A, 14 (30.4 %) class B, and 6 (13.0 %) class C.

Initial clinical management

In the acute phase, 29 of 46 patients (63.0 %) required urgent invasive management for clinical deterioration, hydrocephalus, or mass effect. Invasive procedures included EVD placement (n = 13, 28.3 %), craniotomy (n = 10, 21.7 %) or ICP monitoring (n = 6, 13 %). The remaining 17 patients (37.0 %) were managed conservatively with observation, medical stabilization, and scheduled imaging. All patients regardless of acute-phase management were subsequently included in a multidisciplinary elective management plan, which could consist of either active or conservative treatment. At last follow-up, favorable outcomes (mRS ≤2) were observed in 87.0 % of patients, including 82.8 % of invasively managed and 94.1 % of those initially treated conservatively. The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.39; Table 2). Early invasive management, although required in more severe presentations, did not adversely affect long-term functional outcomes.

Treatment endpoints: cure, residual, and recurrence

Among the 46 patients included in the study, 40 completed their active treatment plan, excluding 3 patients managed conservatively and 3 others still undergoing radiosurgery at the time of final analysis. Among these 40 patients with completed active treatment, 31 (77.5 %) achieved radiological cure, defined as complete nidus obliteration on post-treatment digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and stable follow-up MRI (Table 3). Cure rates varied by treatment modality. Among those treated with a single modality (n = 27), the highest cure rate was observed in the microsurgery group, with 19 of 20 patients (95 %). Endovascular treatment alone led to cure in 2 of 4 cases (50.0 %), while radiosurgery alone was curative in 1 of 3 patients (33.3 %). Among patients treated with multimodal strategies (n = 16), the cure rate was 56.2 % (9/16). Microsurgery alone demonstrated significantly higher cure rates compared to all other treatment strategies combined (95 % vs. 52.2 %, p = 0.011, Fisher's exact test), supporting its efficacy in appropriately selected patients. Treatment allocation reflected AVM complexity: all Spetzler–Ponce class A AVMs were managed surgically, whereas classes B and C showed increasing use of multimodal or non-surgical strategies. Five patients (10.9 % of the total cohort) had residual AVMs despite completing their therapeutic plan, most commonly associated with diffuse nidus morphology, deep or eloquent locations, and higher Spetzler–Ponce grades. Recurrence occurred in 2 patients (6.5 % of those initially cured; 4.3 % of the treated cohort), both over nine years after apparent cure (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). These lesions were Spetzler–Martin grade I, with diffuse architecture and hemorrhagic onset. One was identified after rebleeding, and the other on routine surveillance. These findings reinforce the need for long-term imaging follow-up.

Clinical events: incidence, timing, severity

A total of 25 out of 46 patients (54.3 %) experienced at least one clinically significant event during follow-up. Events were classified as early (≤30 days; n = 7, 28 %) or late (>30 days; n = 18, 72 %), aligning with mRS assessments. Early events were mostly post-operative, commonly involving CSF diversion procedures for hydrocephalus (n = 6). Late events included 4 rebleeds, 3 new-onset seizures, 3 delayed hydrocephalus, and 8 neuropsychological disturbances requiring clinical management (Table 4). Among these, 16 events were identified as treatment-related complications, comprising 11 grade I and 5 grade II cases based on the Landriel-Ibáñez classification. This distribution reflects a predominantly mild and self-limiting complication profile, with no grade III or IV events observed. These findings underscore the low severity of treatment-induced morbidity in the cohort. When stratified by radiological status, 17 of 31 cured (54.8 %) and 7 of 10 non-cured patients (70.0 %) experienced clinical events, suggesting a higher vulnerability in the non-cured subgroup. Late events were also more frequent among non-cured patients (60.0 % vs. 45.2 %). However, the difference in event incidence between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.48, Fisher's exact test). Patients who experienced late clinical events had worse long-term functional outcomes compared to those with early events, with a significantly higher median mRS score (2.0 vs. 1.0; p = 0.028). Although not statistically significant, there was a trend toward increased disability (defined as mRS >2) in the late event group (OR = 4.43; p = 0.092). No significant association was found between clinical events and AVM morphology (eloquence, nidus type, venous drainage). Hemorrhagic onset showed a non-significant trend toward increased event risk (OR ≈ 3.2, p = 0.14). These findings underscore the value of event-free survival as a complementary endpoint beyond radiological cure.

Event-free survival and outcomes

The median follow-up was 64 months (IQR: 36–104.5; range: 4–174), with 25 % of patients followed for over 8 years. While 40 patients had completed their active treatment at the predefined data cut-off, one of the three patients still undergoing radiosurgery completed treatment and reached the ≥12-month clinical follow-up threshold during the extended analysis window. As a result, 41 patients were ultimately included in the final analytic cohort. At 1-month follow-up, 14 of 46 patients (30.4 %) had unfavorable functional status (mRS >2), decreasing to 6 (13 %) at last follow-up, with 87 % achieving favorable outcome (mRS ≤2). Among 37 patients with paired mRS scores, 78.4 % improved, 18.9 % remained stable, and 2.7 % worsened due to late rebleeding (Fig. 4). Kaplan–Meier analysis (n = 41, ≥12 months follow-up) showed a progressive decline in event-free survival (EFS), with a median time to first event of 36 months (IQR: 5.75–71.75, Fig. 5). While not statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.054; Fisher's p = 0.15), the trend favored greater long-term stability in cured patients. Cox regression suggested increased risk without radiological cure (HR = 2.24; p = 0.133). EFS was lowest in Spetzler–Ponce class C and in patients with hemorrhagic onset. Late events were associated with worse long-term function (median mRS 2.0 vs. 1.0; p = 0.028) and showed a non-significant trend toward disability (OR = 4.43; p = 0.092).

Discussion

Beyond radiological cure

Despite achieving radiological cure in over 75 % of treated patients, approximately one-third of these individuals experienced clinically significant events during follow-up — including seizures, neuropsychological deterioration, and even hemorrhagic recurrence more than nine years after treatment. These outcomes underscore a critical dissociation between radiological and clinical trajectories. Pediatric patients may be particularly vulnerable to long-term instability despite angiographic cure. Our dataset reinforces these concerns, being among the few with extensive long term follow-up. Similar concerns for long-term risks have been described by several studies which emphasize the need for long-term surveillance strategies (Lauzier et al., 2021, 2023; Oulasvirta et al., 2023; McDowell et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022). Accordingly, we recommend a structured imaging follow-up with DSA at 6 months and MRI at 1, 3, and 5 years, with repeat DSA reserved for cases of clinical concern or inconclusive MRI results. Young age at diagnosis has consistently emerged as a major risk factor for recurrence, likely due to vascular remodeling and ongoing developmental angiogenesis (Hak et al., 2022; Kawashima et al., 2020; Kitayama et al., 2021; Nikolaev et al., 2018). Lauzier et al. in a metanalysis of 1134 angiographically cured pediatric arteriovenous malformations observed recurrence in 4.8 % of cases (Lauzier et al., 2021). Aboukaïs et al. questioned whether reappearance of AVMs after complete resection represents true recurrence or simply reflects false-negative early imaging (Aboukaïs et al., 2017). To reliably exclude false-negative results, MRI follow-up should be performed using a standardized protocol comprising post-contrast T1-weighted imaging and dedicated angiographic sequences. Gross et al., in one of the largest pediatric AVM microsurgical series (117 children), reported that the implementation of a protocol including systematic perioperative angiography was associated with a significant reduction in recurrence rates (Gross et al., 2015). This approach, though valuable, is not feasible in all settings due to limited access to intraoperative angiography. It appears more relevant to surgical confirmation of complete resection, which can also be aided by ICG videoangiography (Della Puppa and Scienza, 2016). That said, this dissociation emergent from literature calls into question whether angiographic obliteration should continue to be considered a definitive therapeutic endpoint in children. Clinical surveillance should extend far beyond technical success (Jimenez et al., 2017).

Event burden and functional recovery

Early events in our series were predominantly attributable to the immediate postoperative period and were generally manageable with standard care. In contrast, late events were more heterogeneous and potentially more consequential. These included new-onset seizures and neuropsychological disturbances that emerged months or years after treatment, rather than in the acute or perioperative phase (Prablek et al., 2021). Although formal neuropsychological testing was not yet implemented systematically during the study period, these dysfunctions—affecting attention, mood regulation, and executive function—were identified through structured neurologic or psychologic evaluation, often requiring follow-up or pharmacologic support. Their timing and persistence suggest multifactorial origins, including treatment-related effects and disease-related impacts on the developing brain [23]. This inclusion reflects our progressive adoption of neurocognitive surveillance strategies adapted from pediatric neuro-oncology. Importantly, these delayed clinical events occurred despite high obliteration rates. Neither hemorrhagic presentation nor the need for emergency surgical management was significantly associated with their occurrence, suggesting that such events are not simply sequelae of acute-phase injury, but may represent broader vulnerabilities or late-onset treatment effects. Notably, we also found no significant association with variables of AVM complexity such as eloquence, nidus diffuseness, or deep venous drainage. Our findings align with prior studies reporting that conventional anatomical or procedural features may not adequately predict long-term outcomes. For instance, Agarwal et al. found that poor long-term functional outcome was associated with presenting mRS score rather than AVM characteristics or treatment modality (Chen et al., 2022). Similarly, in our cohort, clinical presentation and early mRS did not predict long-term disability. Neither rupture at onset nor the need for urgent intervention (e.g., decompression or CSF diversion) correlated with worse functional recovery—confirming observations by LoPresti et al. [16], who showed that emergency decompression followed by delayed resection did not compromise outcomes. These findings reinforce that early disability should not be seen as a limit to long-term recovery in children. Many of the events we observed, though diverse in origin—from treatment complications to neurodevelopmental sequelae—share the potential to impact autonomy, learning, or quality of life. This justifies their inclusion in a broader endpoint: event-free survival (EFS). Rather than implying a common biological mechanism, EFS was used here as an exploratory composite outcome, designed to capture clinically relevant events regardless of cause or timing. This approach acknowledges that in pediatric AVM care, significant morbidity may arise beyond initial obliteration—even among “cured” patients. Including events like late-onset seizures under EFS does not equate them with recurrence, but acknowledges their comparable clinical burden. Inspired by frameworks used in pediatric neuro-oncology—where relapse, late toxicities, and neurocognitive effects are jointly evaluated—EFS provides a dynamic lens for measuring long-term success. Several of these disturbances became evident only months or years after treatment, typically during school reintegration, and are likely related to a combination of AVM-related factors—such as chronic hemodynamic steal, microhemorrhages, or large-scale network disruption—and treatment effects, consistent with the “growing into deficit” model described in pediatric neuro-oncology. Although not yet widely adopted in AVM literature, similar multidimensional endpoints are emerging in pediatric cerebrovascular research (Prablek et al., 2021). We believe that EFS, despite its exploratory nature, adds value to outcome analysis by incorporating real-life functional burden in a population with high developmental potential and long life expectancy.

Recurrence, residuals, and grading systems

Treatment choices in our series were largely consistent with the Spetzler–Ponce classification, reinforcing its usefulness in initial surgical decision-making—particularly for low-grade AVMs [21,22]. However, the occurrence of significant late events even in straightforward cases reveals the limitations of this and other traditional grading systems in predicting long-term clinical trajectories. Two patients in our cohort experienced AVM recurrence more than 9 years after angiographic cure. These cases challenge the prognostic utility of binary endpoints and suggest that dynamic biological factors—such as neurodevelopment and vascular remodeling—may decouple initial angioarchitectural severity from future outcomes in pediatric patients. This underscores the need for long-term surveillance strategies, as well as outcome models that incorporate longitudinal clinical and functional data. Event-free survival (EFS) offers a potential framework to meet this need. As a composite and patient-centered endpoint, EFS integrates both radiological cure and clinically meaningful stability over time. While our angiographic cure rate of 75.6 % compares favorably with the literature, nearly one-third of cured patients experienced events impacting quality of life or function (Jimenez et al., 2017). These may include subtle but persistent deficits often missed by traditional grading scales. Although the overall event rate may seem high, we believe it reflects the granularity and clinical sensitivity of our reporting. Including neuropsychological impairments, late seizures, and even asymptomatic recurrences in the outcome analysis provides a more realistic picture of survivorship than binary endpoints alone. Capturing these elements helps clinicians and families anticipate the broader consequences of AVMs and their treatment. Our results also reaffirm that residual AVMs carry an elevated risk of long-term events. When possible, additional treatment aimed at complete shunt elimination should be pursued. Meanwhile, the two cases of very late recurrence support emerging consensus on prolonged post-cure surveillance. Non-invasive imaging (e.g., MRA, ASL-MRI) for at least 10–15 years, with DSA reserved for symptomatic or ambiguous cases, may help detect silent recurrence before neurologic compromise occurs (Kawashima et al., 2020; Lauzier et al., 2023). Finally, while we acknowledge the heterogeneity of events included in EFS, our intent was not to suggest pathophysiologic equivalence, but to reflect their cumulative clinical impact. In pediatric AVM care, decades of survivorship may magnify the effects of even “minor” complications. By embracing a multidimensional endpoint like EFS, we move toward a more holistic evaluation of therapeutic success—anchored not only in cure, but in sustained functional well-being.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the modest sample size (n = 46) limits statistical power, particularly in subgroup comparisons and Cox regression modeling. While several findings did not reach formal statistical significance—likely due to limited cohort size—the consistency and magnitude of observed trends suggest clinically meaningful differences that warrant validation in larger cohorts. Although originally developed for adults, the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was applied to assess long-term functional outcomes, in line with current pediatric AVM literature, where it is widely used in ruptured cases (Abecassis et al., 2016). The definition of clinical events encompassed a wide spectrum of neurological and neuropsychological sequelae—ranging from attentional disturbances to hemorrhagic recurrence—which, although clinically relevant, are heterogeneous in nature. Stratification by event type may offer additional granularity in future studies. The interpretation of very late recurrences—observed in only two patients—should be approached with caution; while not conclusive, these cases act as sentinel observations that highlight the need for prolonged surveillance and a broader definition of therapeutic success in pediatric AVMs. Finally, we acknowledge that this retrospective series spans a 13-year period, during which some institutional practices evolved—particularly regarding the early use of multimodal strategies and integration of neuropsychological surveillance. While core principles of treatment and imaging remained stable, such temporal variability may have influenced patient selection and event detection, representing an inherent limitation of the study.

Conclusions

Pediatric patients remain vulnerable to clinically meaningful events even after angiographic cure, including seizures, neuropsychological disturbances, and rare recurrences. Nearly one-third of patients considered cured experienced adverse events, highlighting the disconnect between radiological success and real-world functional outcomes. Event-free survival (EFS) offers a more comprehensive, patient-centered metric of long-term success. Risk stratification should move beyond anatomical grading, incorporating extended follow-up and neurofunctional assessment. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and refine follow-up protocols.
