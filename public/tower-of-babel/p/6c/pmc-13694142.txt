Normal Values for the Two-Point Discrimination Test for Fingers in the Brazilian Population: Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Mariana Ayumi Fujisaki, Vinícius Ynoe de Moraes, Fernando Araújo Pires, João Baptista Gomes dos Santos, Flávio Faloppa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo establish normative values for the two-point discrimination test in the Brazilian population.MethodsThe present cross-sectional study was conducted in an adult population without neurological conditions affecting the hands. Discrimination was assessed on the fingertip pulp, from the thumb to the little finger, using a graded metal two-point discriminator with fixed tips. Normality was determined using the Von Frey test, with an esthesiometer consisting of calibrated nylon monofilaments.Results A total of 984 hands, comprising 244 male and 248 female participants, were evaluated. The sample included 354 non-manual workers, 103 manual workers, and 35 retired participants. All fingers showed a discrimination range from 2 to 5 mm. The modes were 4 mm, 3 mm, 3 mm, 4 mm, and 4 mm for the 1 st , 2 nd , 3 rd , 4 th , and 5 th fingers, respectively. ConclusionAll fingers showed a discrimination interval between 2 and 5 mm. The mode was 4 mm for the 1st, 4th, and 5th fingers, and 3 mm for the 2nd and 3rd fingers.

Introduction

The two-point discrimination test defines the minimum distance required to distinguish between two distinct tactile stimuli. This test is widely used to assess the tactile acuity of a given cutaneous region and, consequently, its sensory function. 1 2 3

Despite being a readily accessible and low-cost test with broad clinical application, several aspects of the two-point discrimination test lack methodological standardization in the current literature. There are significant variations in application protocols, in the definition of distance intervals used, and in how results are obtained and interpreted, which compromise comparability across studies and between different healthcare settings. 4

Moberg 5 reported that he had discovered this technique 45 years earlier and that it was a major mistake not to have described it in detail at the time. The pressure required to perform the test is a key point of discussion, as it is extremely difficult to standardize the force applied by the examiner to generate sufficient tactile stimulus without causing pain and altering the test results. 5 6

There are two main approaches to determining the applied force. The first is the force sufficient for the patient to perceive the stimulus, which is subject to subjective interpretation and may lead to confusion with a painful stimulus. The second is the application of the minimum force necessary to cause a change in the color of the skin adjacent to the instrument's point of contact (a phenomenon known as blanching). 4 7

A computerized device for standardized pressure application was developed to overcome this difficulty. Although the device yielded good results, its application in clinical settings is limited. 8 Studies have also shown that changes in the intensity of the applied force may influence discrimination thresholds, indicating that stimulus pressure is an important determinant of the tactile acuity measured by the test. 7 9

However, differences in the perception of pressure applied with one or two points, rather than the tactile sensitivity to one or two points of contact, may influence the results even when the applied force is standardized across all measurements. 10 In addition, no consensus has been reached on the number of times each site should be assessed to obtain more reliable results, with recommendations ranging from 3 to 10 repetitions. 11 12

The classic reference value for the test considers < 6 mm as a normal result, 13 whereas more recent studies have expanded the spectrum, classifying results as normal (< 6 mm), fair (6–10 mm), and poor (11–15 mm). 14 Furthermore, gender and age influence normal values, 6 15 16 with performance declining in older subjects. 6 Testing frequency and repetition also affect discrimination results, facilitating the perception of two points at shorter distances. 17 Moreover, there are no studies evaluating whether regression occurs after prolonged periods without stimulation. Other potential influencing factors, such as hand dominance and occupation, have not been extensively evaluated. The present study aimed to establish the normative values of the two-point discrimination test in the Brazilian population.

Methods

The present cross-sectional study was conducted at the Hand Surgery Group, Department of Orthopedics and Traumatology, Escola Paulista de Medicina, Universidade Federal do Estado de São Paulo, São Paulo, SP, Brazil. Data collection occurred between November 2023 and May 2024. The study was approved by the ethics committee under number CAAE: 69747623.4.0000.5505. This manuscript was prepared according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, which are available at equatornetwork.com.

The population analysis included 492 healthy adult subjects (older than 20 years) without evident and/or reported conditions within the scope of hand surgery, most of whom were companions of patients attending the Hand Surgery Service at our hospital, totaling 984 hands.

The sample was divided into the following age groups: (1) 20 to 40 years, (2) 41 to 60 years, and (3) over 60 years. It was also categorized by gender (male or female) and type of occupational activity (manual vs. non-manual). The distribution included (1) 244 male subjects and (2) 248 female subjects. Regarding hand dominance and occupation, the sample consisted of 103 manual workers, 354 non-manual workers, and 35 retired subjects.

The Von Frey test was performed using an esthesiometer with calibrated nylon monofilaments for cutaneous sensitivity assessment to validate preserved neurological function ( Fig. 1 ). Only participants with green esthesiometry were eligible for the study.

Esthesiometer used for the Von Frey test.

The test was performed with the participant seated, wrists in a neutral position, hands resting dorsally on the table, and fingers in a resting position. We used a graded metal two-point discriminator with fixed tips (suitable for assessing static tactile discrimination) ( Fig. 2 ). Measurements were taken on the fingertip pulp of each finger, from the 1st to the 5th, and the discriminator was positioned longitudinally along the midline of the fingertip pulp ( Fig. 3 ).

Metal two-point discriminator.

“Blanching” phenomenon described by Moberg.

The participants were asked to close their eyes to avoid visualizing the test. Contact was applied randomly, using either one or two points to ensure simultaneous contact with the skin. Participants were asked to indicate whether they perceived one or two points.

The initial measurement was set at 10 mm, decreasing progressively by 1 mm until the participant could no longer discriminate between two contact points. 13 The pressure applied to the fingertip pulp was sufficient to produce a change in skin color at the point of contact with the discriminator, a phenomenon described by Moberg as blanching ( Fig. 4 ). The recorded value corresponded to the last distance, in millimeters (mm), at which the participant could distinguish two points before reporting only one contact point.

Measurement performed on the fingertip pulp of each finger, from the 1st to the 5th, with the discriminator positioned longitudinally along the midline of the fingertip pulp.

All data underwent statistical analyses to identify relevant correlations. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare discrimination among fingers in the overall sample, with additional stratification by age group, sex, and occupation type. Paired Student's t -tests were used to compare the same fingers between the dominant and non-dominant hands. Tukey's multiple comparison method was used for pairwise finger comparison. The significance level was set at 5%.

All fingers showed a discrimination range from 2 to 5 mm. The mode was 4 mm for the 1 st , 4 th , and 5 th fingers and 3 mm for the 2 nd and 3 rd fingers.

Among all fingers evaluated, the second finger showed shorter discrimination distances compared to the others.

Age-related variation was observed ( Table 2 ). The youngest group (20–40 years), totaling 296 subjects, showed shorter discrimination distances compared to the 124 subjects aged 41 to 60 years and the 72 subjects older than 60.

The 354 non-manual workers showed better discrimination capacity than the 103 manual workers, whereas the retired subjects, totaling 35, showed the lowest capacity ( Table 4 ). There was no significant difference in discrimination capacity between the 244 men and 248 women, and between dominant and non-dominant hands, occurred only in the fifth digit ( Tables 3 5 ).

Discussion

Considering the lack of standardization in the Brazilian population, the current study evaluated 984 non-neuropathic hands, demonstrating values from 2 to 5 mm. These values corroborate previous findings, 18 with a mode of 4 mm for the 1 st , 4 th , and 5th fingers and 3 mm for the 2 nd and 3 rd fingers. The Von Frey monofilament test was used to ensure preserved neurological function, and the study included only subjects with a green esthesiometry. 19 20

Despite the clinical exclusion criteria, the monofilament test has limitations, including variable sensitivity for early neuropathies and potential failures in detecting subclinical abnormalities. 21 Furthermore, variability in its diagnostic accuracy has been described, with the possibility of false negatives and false positives when used as a standalone screening method. 22 Therefore, the inclusion of mild, undetected sensory abnormalities cannot be completely ruled out.

The second finger showed shorter discrimination distances, indicating greater tactile sensitivity, potentially related to the higher density of receptors in distal regions and those with more frequent functional use. 23 The more richly innervated the finger, the more clearly and accurately it can perceive the difference between two contact points. 18 23

The correlation between increasing age and poorer sensory performance corroborates previous findings in the literature, which describe a progressive decline in sensitivity with aging. 16 The ability to discriminate two points tends to deteriorate over time, a phenomenon associated with the quantitative and functional reduction of cutaneous receptors, especially Meissner corpuscles and Merkel cells. 16 Classical histological studies by Cauna and Ross 24 and Ridley 25 demonstrated this age-related decrease, providing morphological and functional support for the observation of higher discrimination thresholds in older subjects.

There was no significant difference between genders. This finding is partially consistent with the literature, as a study conducted in the United States found that women had lower scores than men ( p < 0.001). 26 These authors observed higher thresholds in women, as well as better performance at the fingertips. 26 The lack of statistical significance regarding hand dominance corroborates the findings of Cheng. 14 Thus, it is suggested that dominance does not have a significant impact on two-point discrimination.

In addition, methodological variations among studies, such as applied force, instrument orientation, response criteria, and examiner training, contribute to discrepancies in the results reported in the literature. The methodological standardization adopted in the current study, including the use of the Von Frey monofilament test to exclude subclinical neuropathies, allowed the definition of more consistent and specific values for the Brazilian population, ranging from 2 to 5 mm. These findings refine and corroborate the classical literature.

The main limitation of the present study is its cross-sectional design, which prevents causal or longitudinal analyses, as well as the scarcity of recent studies on test standardization in healthy populations. Nevertheless, the classical physiological and methodological foundations remain valid. Future studies, with more diverse samples and standardized methodologies, may expand and update the proposed normative parameters.

Conclusion

Two-point discrimination in the Brazilian population with healthy hands from 2 to 5 mm, with a mode of 4 mm. The mode was 4 mm for the 1 st , 4 th , and 5 th fingers, and 3 mm for the 2nd and 3 rd fingers. Younger participants, aged 20 to 40 years, demonstrated shorter discrimination distances compared to older age groups. Non-manual workers showed better two-point discrimination capacity compared to manual workers, followed by retired subjects.
