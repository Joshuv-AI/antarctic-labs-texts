Recent highlights from the International Journal of Cardiology Heart and Vasculature: cardio-oncology
Authors: Anke Fender, Florian Bruns, Dobromir Dobrev
Published: 2025

Cardiovascular disease and cancers remain the leading causes of death worldwide. As the global population ages, and at the same time the survival of cancer patients increases due to advances in therapy, the numbers of patients afflicted by both diseases at some point in their life will also rise. We here highlight the papers published recently in this journal that advance the field of cardio-oncology. This selection of studies provides insight into how malignancies promote and exacerbate cardiovascular risk and outcome, how drug treatments for cancer impact detrimentally on the heart, and how new screening paradigms could be implemented for early detection and monitoring of cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) and toxicity.

Cardiovascular outcome on the background of cancer

The first of our spotlight papers is by Nochioka et al. [1], who examined clinical outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), with and without additive complication by pre-existing atrial fibrillation (AF) and/or a history of cancer. The multicenter hospital-based cohort study enrolled 3233 patients, of which 11 % had CAD plus AF, and around the same number had CAD plus cancer history. Nearly 3 % had the triple burden. Not surprisingly, this group had the highest risk − over 2-fold compared to CAD alone − for a composite outcome that included stroke, thrombosis and major bleeding. The presence of either AF or cancer on top of CAD led to a comparable composite outcome risk, somewhere between CAD only and CAD plus both comorbidities. If the outcome measures are considered individually, stroke seems to be the major determinant of composite outcome, while divergence between the groups in terms of systemic thrombosis or bleeding was less striking. All groups were to a large proportion on antiplatelets, nearly 90 % in the CAD only group, with the lowest proportion, 78 %, in the triple-burden group. The type of antiplatelet therapy − aspirin or others, single or dual (SAPT, DAPT) − was not specified, but this might have been relevant for cardiovascular, bleeding and tumor outcomes. The triple disease-burdened group also encompassed the highest proportion − 49 % − of patients on anticoagulants. In the CAD plus AF group, 43 % were anticoagulated, less than 10 % in each of the non-AF groups. There is some limited preclinical data linking thrombin with tumor growth and metastasis. Therefore it can be speculated that anticoagulation could impact on either cancer-related outcomes, or, given the multiple hemostasis-independent effects of coagulant proteases, also on cardiovascular outcomes. The anticoagulant types were not specified, but particularly with respect to bleeding and net clinical outcome, the direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC) show a safer bleeding profile than warfarin in patients with combined CAD and AF, which might have contributed to detected differences between groups [2]. Of note, more than half of patients with diagnosed AF did not receive anticoagulants, reflecting global trends of underuse [3,4] and possibly contributing to the high stroke incidence [5] especially in the CAD + AF + cancer group. Although colorectal and stomach cancers were the major malignancies in the present cohorts, for a substantial proportion of patients, cancer type was not defined. It is also not evident if the cancer types in the CAD plus cancer group were similarly distributed as in the CAD plus cancer plus AF group, and for how long the patients had been in effective remission. Overall, this study was not powered enough for in-depth subsgroup analysis, and such insights should be gained from larger cohorts in future studies.

Leiva et al. [6] tackled the question of heart failure (HF) outcomes in patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), a form of chronic leukaemia. Clinical and preclinicial mechanistic studies have linked MPN with HF development, but how an underlying MPN affects outcome when HF manifests is not clear. HF pathophysiology features a prominent immune and inflammatory component [7,8], so it is likely that the background neoplams might sustain remodeling and dysfunction. The authors of this study assessed patients hospitalised for HF based on MPN comorbidity yes (total 4626 patients) or no (46,250 patients, propensity score-matched). Patients with MPN at the time of HF hospitalization showed a moderately increased risk of in-hospital death as well as of cardiovascular-related and all-cause readmissions, primarily driven by thromboembolic complications. This is in keeping with the hypercoagulant state associated with disturbed regulation and function of immune cells and signals [9,10]. HF-related readmissions specifically were not different between the groups. Here, it would have been interesting to compare comedications between the groups, since numerous agents used in HF and comorbidity management might have impacted on one or more of the study outcomes [11,12]. Intriguingly, the authors noted that patients with MPN appear to be less likely to have HF with reduced ejection fraction or the classical cardiovascular risk factors [13] diabetes and CAD, suggesting a distinct HF pathophysiology in patients with MPN that warrants further study.

Osawa et al. [14] examined how cancer may affect outcome after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for aortic stenosis (AS), a highly challenging treatment scenario. While the long-term outcome after TAVR at least in low-risk patients is less favourable than surgical intervention [15], short-term outcome is generally better [16]. TAVR might therefore be suitable in patients with malignancy who present with high surgical risk and a shortened life-expectancy but data on TAVR safety and efficacy for severe AS complicated by cancer is inconclusive. The authors conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies enrolling nearly 200,000 patients undergoing TAVR, of which around 20 % had past or present cancer. Overall, this very high risk group did not show a worse short-term outcome after TAVR: in-hospital or 30-day mortality was even lower than in the patients without cancer, as were perioperative complications (mainly stroke and kidney injury). Long-term mortality over 12 months and beyond after TAVR was however higher in the cancer cohort, by 1.5-fold, and the need for pacemaker implantation was greater. The authors conclude that as in the general patient population, cancer patients do quite well in the short-term after TAVR, but long-term outcome may be lower, being dependent on cancer survival and/or cardiotoxicity due to anticancer therapies.

Treatment challenges for cardiovascular disease in the cancer setting

The combined manifestation of AF and cancer is a difficult treatment scenario, since both diseases present as highly coagulable states, requiring careful anticoagulation [17]. Li et al. [18] provided a meta-analysis of 7 observational cohort studies from the US, Denmark, Sweden, Spain and Taiwan, assessing the safety and efficacy of DOAC over warfarin in real-world patients with both cancer and AF. Overall, patients on DOAC fared better in terms of both thromboembolic risk and bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage. This finding reflects what is seen in patients without cancer [2,5], and supports the use of these agents in AF complicated by malignancy. Subgroup analysis showed that the relative risk (RR) for thromboembolism was 0.93, 0.86 and 0.77, respectively for dabigatran, rivaroxaban and apixaban, compared to warfarin. The risk of major bleeding compared to warfarin was notably lower with dabigatran (RR 0.80) and apixaban (RR 0.58), but not for rivaroxaban, which was comparable in this regard to warfarin. En face, these data suggest that in high risk patients with both cancer and AF, the factor Xa inhibitor apixaban might be the most favourable anticoagulant approach. However, it should be clarified how malignancy type and whether the cancer is active or in remission could impact on the safety and efficacy of the different DOAC.

Surgical and device-based therapies also provide treatment challenges especially with background comorbidities. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) is a less invasive alternative to mitral surgery and may be suited for e.g. obese patients [19] or others not eligible for operative treatment [20]. Khan et al. [21] report a retrospective analysis of TEER outcome specifically in patients with a history of cancer. Patients with an expectedly shortened lifespan due to active or past malignancy are typically excluded from clinicial trials for cardiovascular devices, but this insight is critical for management of these groups, who are at high surgical risk and therefore suited to undergo percutaneous valve repair. Khan et al. [21] identified 503 propensity-matched pairs of patients with and without a history of cancer undergoing TEER. It is evident that this technique is applied quite frequently in cancer patients, despite the lack of clinical data. Notably, there was no difference in outcome in terms of post-operative mortality, HF exacerbation, stroke or hospitalizations for up to 12 months, which indicates that TEER is indeed suitable to treat mitral regurgitation in cancer patients. Thus these observations fill a critical gap in knowledge as commended in the accompanying editorial by El-Andari et al. [22]. One critical editorial comment pertains to the lack of differentiation of active versus past malignancy, which would be important to clarify in subsequent work. There is also still no head-to-head comparison of TEER vs. optimal surgical management in patients with cancer history or currently active malignancy. These plus other gaps in knowledge are comprehensively discussed by the editorial authors, perhaps providing an impetus for further focused research in this context.

Cardiotoxicity and cardiovascular outcome of cancer therapy

Unrecognised and untreated hypertension remains a key and often silent cardiovascular killer and represents, together with diabetes, hypercholesterolemia and smoking, the quartet of standard modifiable cardiovascular risk factors (SMuRFs). How hypertension fits into the long-term prognosis and therapy of certain cancers is not fully understood. Dilli Babu et al. [23] provided an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised clinical trials linking with high-grade hypertension in cancer patients with the the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor inhibitor (VEGFRi) axitinib. A total of 8 trials encompassing over 2500 patients were analysed. Of these, four studies directly compared atixinib alone vs. placebo, in the other studies, the VEGFRi was combined with one of docetaxel, gemcitabine or pemetrexed-cisplatin. In all cohorts, axitinib use was associated with a higher risk of all-grade and high-grade hypertension than in the comparator groups. The authors propose that this could arise from loss of the endothelial vasodilators nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin [24], leading to diminished angiogenesis, increased arterial resistance and rarefaction. They critically discussed the implications of their analysis in light of current evidence and emphasised the need for cardiovascular profiling and risk scoring [25], specifically of pre-existing hypertension, before initiating VEGFRi therapy and during the treatment period. Loss of endothelial NO function was also identified by Hammond et al. [26] as a candidate mechanism of cardiovascular toxicity in the context of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) chemotherapy. A cross-sectional analysis of 30 cancer patients treated with 5-FU compared to 32 matched controls showed a selective loss of cutaneous microvascular reactivity to endothelium-dependent stimuli in the 5-FU group. Reactivity to the endothelium-independent trigger sodium nitroprusside was unaffected. Inhibition of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS, NOS3) suppressed the endothelium-dependent response in control subjects, but was without effect in the 5-FU group. This suggests that 5-FU diminishes microvascular function by suppressing NO-dependent regulation of vascular tone, a concept verified by the observation that 5-FU suppressed eNOS gene expression in human coronary artery endothelial cells. Given the critical involvement of NO in many other aspects of cardiac protection, remodeling and function, and the link between these and cardiometabolic diseases [27,28], such mechanisms could contribute at least in part to 5-FU cardiotoxicity.

Anthracyclines such as doxorubicin are also anticancer agents with high cardiotoxicity. Jracquemyn et al. [29] retrospectively assessed 190 pediatric cancer patients who underwent serial cardiac assessments during and after anthracycline chemotherapy (median treatment duration around 234 days). A specific aspect of this study was the association between baseline hypertension and CTRCD. Around 13 % (24/190) and 5 % (10/190) of anthracycline-treated children had hypertension stage 1 and 2, respectively, at baseline. This pre-existent hypertension did not lead to greater ventricular dysfunction or CTRCD development at 5-year follow-up, compared to children who were normotensive prior to commencing therapy, but they did show a distinct treatment response. Specifically, with prior hypertension (i) global longitudinal strain increased progressively faster, (ii) tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion showed faster progressive decreases, and (iii) left ventricular ejection fraction demonstrated slower decreases and recovered faster after chemotherapy. These observations are critically evaluated by the authors with consideration of study limitations and possible confounders. The long-term effects of anthracyclines on cardiovascular health of pediatric cancer survivors warrant further clarification. Given the emerging evidence that HF pharmacotherapy may affect cancer progression and treatment [30], future work should evaluate the effects of individual cardiovascular drugs on the different cancer types.

Roadmaps for CTRCD detection and monitoring

Belger et al. [31] provide an overview of the latest pre-clinical and clincial insights into molecular drivers of anthracycline cardiotoxicity in cancer patients. The authors discuss how cancer therapy may be aligned with the individual cardiovascular profile of the patient, and highlight certain panels of comorbidities and/or non-modifiable characteristics that promote cardiotoxicity in response to the prototypic anthracycline doxorubicin. The review also features a summary of current imaging modalities for early detection of adverse cardiac effects, as well as of novel candidate biomarkers that may help to screen for cardiotoxicity before overt cardiac injury has occurred. In line with the need for methods to detect early cardiovascular complications of chemotherapy, Mesitskaya et al. [32] evaluated remote cardio-monitoring by single-lead ECG in cancer patients with minimal baseline cardiovascular diseases. The study enrolled 162 patients over 18 years old with a first diagnosis of different types of solid tumors. After the first cycle of chemotherapy, these patients underwent monitoring during days 14–21 after chemotherapy administration. The authors report early detection of QTc prolongation in 8 patients, first-diagnosed AF detected in 11 patients and LV diastolic dysfunction in 49 patients – this equates to around 40 %! The study concludes that remote monitoring using portable ECG devices may provide a simple, convenient and low-cost approach to screen for early cardiovascular toxicity in patients on chemotherapy. This may in the long-run improve clincial outcome and survival through early detection of AF for example [33,34].

Concerning biomarkers, an insightful editorial by Talukder et al. [35] comprehensively outlined how an interdisciplinary biomarker assessemnt − incorporating the perspectives of cardiologists, oncologists and immunologists − might aid early diagnosis of immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-associated cardiotoxicity, much as is being implemented in the context of rheumatoid arthritis [36]. The same approach teaming large-scale gene-expression and machine-learning, used to identify hub genes and candidate biomarkers in HF [37], might also be of diagnostic value in the context of cardiotoxiciy in cancer treatment, while close longitudinal immune profiling may allow for timely detection of critical developments underlying cardiovascular complications [38]. However, the optimal combination and number of biomarkers needs to be validated, as more is not always more insightful [39]. The most common, severe and potentially highly lethal form of CTCRD for which a validated early detection biomarker panel would be very useful is ICI-related myocarditis (ICIRM), which requires timely detection and prompt treatment with intravenous corticosteroids. The decision to abort ICI therapy of course has implications for tumor prognosis, so a certain diagnosis is mandatory to justify this step. Talukder et al. [35] neatly guided through the mechanistic basis of ICIRM and its distinct phenotypic and functional features. The pros and cons of biomarkers associated with these aspects of ICIRM, and their candidate benefits for myocarditis screening and longitudinal monitoring were critically evaluated. Equally critical attention was given to studies that reported on the validity and usefulness of the biomarkers – alone or as a panel – and finally, the pitfalls, gaps and opportunities for this important aspect of cardio-oncology. Troponin was one of the biomarkers highlighted in the review by Talukder et al. [35]. Even very low levels, below the 99th percentile even, can independently predict mortality for example in patients with CAD [40]. Cardiac troponin is increasingly seen also as a sensitive indicator of mild asymptomatic cardiac dysfunction during cancer treatment. Many treatments for breast cancer in particular are cardiotoxic, and accordingly elevate troponin levels, but no clear guidelines exist on how to approach troponin elevation in the clinical context. Cronin et al. [41] tackled this clinical conundrum on which data remains incomplete and conflicting. In their comprehensive review of the topic, the authors critically discussed the additive role and importance of cardiac biomarkers including troponin, in patients treated for breast cancer and propose a framework to guide troponin surveillance and clincial management if levels rise during chemotherapy.

On a different note, Punzo et al. [42] systematically reviewed modern imaging approaches for the detection, diagnosis, characterization and monitoring of Cardiac ParaGangliomas (PGLs). This very rare tumor form arises from chromaffin cells of the sympathetic ganglia and is typically asymptomatic, so that diagnosis is generally incidental. The authors here comprehensively advocate standardization of multimodal imaging together with biochemical profiling in order to optimize PGL diagnosis and prognosis. The unique advantages of Cardiac Computed Tomography (CCT) and Cardiac Magnetic Resonance (CMR) for assessing and characterising tumor morphology, position and composition are detailed, as is the application of Positron Emission Tomography (PET) or Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) with specific radiotracers to distinguish the secretory phenotype and function of the tumor.

Cardio-oncology with its multiple interdisciplinary facets will likely continue to gain importance. The International Journal of Cardiology Heart and Vasculature welcomes submissions that advance clinical and fundamental understanding of this growing field.
