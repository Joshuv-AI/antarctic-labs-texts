Follow-up rescreening among women living with human immunodeficiency virus who tested positive and treated for cervical pre-cancer lesions in South Ethiopia
Authors: Bikila Lencha, Biftu Geda, Kemal Ahmed, Lara Kapps, Mark Spigt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen living with human immunodeficiency virus face a heightened risk of cervical cancer. Lack of follow-up rescreening among women with cervical lesions could result in increased risk of cancer-related morbidity and mortality.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among 191 women living with HIV who tested positive for visual inspection with acetic acid and were treated from September 2020 to September 2023. A data extraction checklist was used to collect data from October 1, 2023 to March 30, 2024. Descriptive statistics was computed and then logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between the independent variables and follow-up rescreening. The association was ascertained using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was declared at a P-value less than 0.05.ResultThe mean age of the participants was 36.88 (±7.29) years. Slightly less than one-third (60, 31.4%) of the women came back for follow-up rescreening within 3 years. Those women who were educated [AOR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.03-4.68] and treated by Cryotherapy [AOR = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.1-5.4] were more likely to come back for rescreening compared to those who were uneducated and treated by thermal ablation, respectively.ConclusionFollow-up rescreening for cervical pre-cancer remains low. Women’s educational status and treatment modality affect the follow-up rescreening. Proper counselling on post-treatment follow-up and available screening and treatment options is very crucial to improve adherence to protocol. A qualitative study is warranted to identify the challenges of follow-up rescreening.

The low rate of follow-up rescreening among high-risk groups necessitates the importance of proper implementation of the screening and treatment protocol in Ethiopia. Empowering women through education campaigns and proper counselling should be emphasized in the follow-up protocol.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) develops in the cells of the cervix.1 Human papilloma virus (HPV) is a necessary cause of cervical cancer1 and causes 5% of all cancers.2 Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is one of the common factors that increase the risk of cervical cancer3,4 and it also leads to the persistence of the HPV among those treated for the cervical lesions.5

According to the global cancer statistics, there were 662 044 cases and 348 709 deaths due to this disease worldwide, in 2022.6 Almost 94% of deaths due to cervical cancer occurred in low-and middle-income countries.7 In Africa, the continent ranked second in both incidence and mortality rates, with cervical cancer accounting for 19% of new cases and 23.1% of deaths in 2022.7 The most affected regions within Africa were Eastern, Middle, and Southern Africa, with Eastern Africa experiencing particularly high rates—an incidence of 40.1 per 100 000 women and a mortality rate of 28.6 per 100 000 women annually.8

Within Ethiopia, cervical cancer is the second most prevalent cancer among women, with 8168 cases and 5975 deaths reported in 2022, representing 10.2% of all cancer cases and 10.9% of cancer-related deaths among women.9,10 The incidence of cervical cancer in South Ethiopia has increased since 201611 and accounts for 75% of all gynecological cancers.11 Around 70% cancer cases in South Ethiopia were diagnosed lately.12

Cervical cancer is preventable if detected early in the course of the disease.13 However, for its prevention to be successful, follow-up rescreening is needed.14 Among women living with HIV (WLWH) who don’t attend follow-up screening program, the progression from pre-cancer to cancer is more likely to happen earlier than expected.15 WLWH have a lower chance of the healing of pre-cancerous lesions and a higher rate of persistence after receiving treatment.16

The 2021 updated WHO guideline recommends that all WLWH who are sexually active should undergo regular screening, irrespective of their age17 and those who were treated for pre-cancer should come back for rescreening within 6 months.17 Ethiopia developed its first cervical cancer screening and treatment guideline in 2015.18 Then it was updated in 2021.10 The former recommends follow-up rescreening at 1-year post-treatment. However, the 2021 updated guideline recommends follow-up rescreening at 6 months post-treatment for WLWH.10

The rate of adherence to follow-up rescreening ranged from 27.9% to 51.1% in Ethiopia.19–22 Similar low adherence levels were observed in Cameroon, Tanzania, and Kenya, with reported rates of 34%, 65.3%, and 61%, respectively.23–25 The primary barriers identified to follow-up adherence included lack of awareness, fear and stigma, inadequate health-seeking behavior, absence of male involvement, perceived absence of pain, gender preferences for screening, lack of reminder systems, and health system and access-related challenges.25–28

Based on a secondary analysis of data from a 4-year project in Ethiopia, 10% of women tested positive for visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) and 97% of them were treated with cryotherapy.21 However, only half of the treated women adhere to 1-year follow-up.21,22 Evidence shows that high-grade cervical lesions can recur after treatment of pre-invasive cervical lesions.29 A recent study from Ethiopia reported persistence of the lesions among 43% of the women, and HIV status was identified as a common predictor.5 However, their follow-up rescreening is not well addressed, and there is a paucity of literature in our study setting. Therefore, our study aimed to assess the magnitude of follow-up rescreening and its influencing factors among WLWH who tested positive for cervical pre-cancer lesions on baseline VIA test in West Arsi and Bale zones public health facilities of Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and design

A facility-based retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted among WLWH who were screened using VIA and found positive in public health facilities in the West Arsi and Bale zones of Ethiopia. These zones are located in Oromia, the largest region in Ethiopia. West Arsi Zone has a total of 16 districts with the city of Shashemene as its capital, located 250 km south of Addis Ababa. On the other hand, Bale Zone is located 430 km from Addis Ababa, and Bale Robe is the capital of the zone. Eleven health facilities (4 hospitals and 2 health centres) from the West Arsi Zone and two hospitals (Robe General Hospital and Madda Walabu University Referral and Teaching Hospital) from the Bale Zone were included in the current study. The study uses data available from September 2020 to September 2023.

The cervical cancer screening clinics were mainly run by trained midwives or nurses and provided health information, screening, and treatment of cervical precancerous lesions. The cervical cancer screening and treatment program was integrated in to ART clinic. VIA is available at all selected facilities. However, HPV DNA testing is conducted at the regional laboratory located within Shashemene Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (SHCSH). Cryotherapy and thermal ablation are the common treatments provided for precancer lesions based on the WHO recommendations. Loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) is only provided at SHCSH as a referral service. WLWH who were treated for cervical pre-cancer should be followed up after 6 months for rescreening as per the 2021 updated national guideline.10 However, the previous guideline recommends the follow-up rescreening to be at 1 year for WLWH who were treated for the cervical pre-cancer lesions.18 The details were explained in our previous publications.28,30

Population

The study included all WLWH on highly active anti-retroviral therapy (HAART) who tested positive for pre-cancer cervical lesion by VIA and treated at public health facilities in West Arsi and Bale Zones from September 2020 to September 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women aged 15-49 years residing in the West Arsi and Bale Zone, who were on HAART, and have tested positive for VIA and treated with either cryotherapy or thermal ablation, were included. We excluded women who have had a previous cervical cancer diagnosis, treated by LEEP, hysterectomy done, pregnant, or are within 8 weeks postpartum. Records missing essential information, such as screening date, treatment date, and appointment date, were also excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Single population proportion with the following assumptions: Adherence level (P) = 51.1%,21 confidence level = 95%, margin of error = 5% and incompleteness = 10%. Since the number of our population is smaller than the calculated sample size, we applied the population correction formula and estimated to be 201. Finally, all the records of all WLWH who have tested positive for cervical cancer using VIA from September 2020 to September 30, 2023 were taken from the registries and included in our study. There were 231 patients potentially eligible for the study. Forty patients were excluded because (13 unable to obtain their cards, 16 have appointment in the future, and 11 had incomplete data). Then, the patient chart of the women and the electronic database from each facility were reviewed for all the pertinent information (Figure 1).

Data collection methods

A structured data extraction checklist was developed from ART follow-up forms of the patients and national CC screening and treatment guidelines.10 The checklist was used for data extraction on the socio-demographic characteristics, as well as on the HIV-related clinical characteristics, behavioral, sexual, and reproductive factors of the participants. Clinically related variables, like CD4 count, viral load, WHO stage, drug adherence, body mass index, drug regimen, history of opportunistic infection, history of TB co-infection, and history of treatment failure, were obtained from patient charts using the data extraction tool. Additionally, information about cervical cancer screening, risk factors, treatment date, and appointment date was obtained from the CC intake forms attached to the chart of the patient. Patient registries were also cross-checked to validate the information obtained from the CC intake form. The tool was prepared and uploaded to the Kobo Toolbox data collection software. Data collectors, in collaboration with the data clerks in each facility, identified all the patients who have had a positive VIA test since September 2020 to September 2023. Data were collected from October 2023 to March 2024 over a period of 6 months. Once the cards of VIA-positive patients were identified, the checklist was filled from patient charts and CC intake forms. Electronic records were referred for missing information in the registries and intake form.

Data quality control

Health care providers, professionally, nurses, and midwifes, responsible for CC screening and treatment, were recruited from each hospital and appointed as data collectors. Additionally, data clerks were consulted for the electronic database to complete the missing information. First, training was provided to the data collectors on the study protocol, ethical issues, data collection tools, Kobo Toolbox and objectives of the study. They were properly instructed and trained on how to perform the data collection by using the Kobo Toolbox smartphone application. The tool was revised by the research team (B.L. and L.K.) for clarity, and necessary amendments were made. Using the Kobo Toolbox helped us to correct errors daily by discussing with the research team. All the findings were reported according to the STROBE guideline for reporting cross-sectional studies.31

Variables measurement

Our dependent variable was follow-up rescreening and categorized as adherent and non-adherent. Follow-up rescreening was defined as having any rescreening at any time within 3 years after the initial VIA-positive screening and treatment.22,32 The recurrence rate was defined as women who tested positive on rescreening follow-up after being treated for pre-cancer lesions previously.20 Rescreening outcomes were categorized into three groups: positive, negative, and unknown. Our independent variables included socio-demographic, clinical, sexual, reproductive and behavioral, and screening-related characteristics.

Data analysis and processing

Data were entered into the Kobo Toolbox and then exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Data were checked, coded, and cleaned. Continuous variables were described as mean and standard deviation and made into categorical variables. Frequency and percentage were carried out to summarize categorical data. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression were used to assess the strength of the association between the dependent variable and independent variables. Variables with a P-value of less than 0.3 were included in the model.33 The goodness of fit of the model was tested using the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test33 and it was 0.91. Adjusted odds ratio and 95% confidence interval were used to report the results. The level of statistical significance was declared at P-value of less than 0.05.

Ethical consideration

Madda Walabu University ethical review board approved the study with reference number of: IRERC/040/2023. Formal letters were written to all facilities participating in this study. Anonymity of the patients and confidentiality of the data were kept by not using any personal identifiers and by restricted data access by the investigators only. All the study’s procedures followed the principles of Declarations of Helsinki.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Records of 191 patients were used for the analysis. The mean age of the women was 36.88 (±7.29) years. The majority of the women, 95 (49.7%), were in 35-44 years group. More than one-fourth of the women, 54 (28.3%), were unable to read and write. Two-thirds of the women, 127 (66.5%), were married (Table 1).

Cervical cancer screening and treatment characteristics

The majority of the patients, 159 (83.2%), were screened using VIA; whereas 32 (16.8%) were tested by HPV DNA and triaged using the VIA test. The majority of the samples 23 (71.8%) for the HPV DNA test were self-collected by women themselves. Threefourths 143 (74.9%) of the women were treated by Cryotherapy, and the rest 48 (25.1%) were treated by thermal ablation.

Follow-up rescreening

Slightly less than one-third, 60 (31.4%) women came back to the clinic for follow-up rescreening within three years. However, only 26 (13.6%) came back to the clinic within the appointment window period according to the national protocol. Rescreening result shows that 10 (16.7%) were positive, 44 (73.3%) were negative, and 6 (10%) were unknown.

Factors associated with follow-up rescreening

In the multivariable logistic regression model, treatment modality [AOR = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.1-5.4] and educational status [AOR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.03-4.68] were significantly associated with follow-up rescreening. Those women who were treated by cryotherapy were 2.4 [AOR = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.1-5.4] times likely to adhere to follow-up rescreening compared to the women who were treated by thermo-coagulation. Those who had an education were 2.2 [AOR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.03-4.68] times likely to adhere to follow-up rescreening compared to the women who had no education (Table 4).

Discussion

The study aimed to assess the adherence to follow-up rescreening and its associated factors among WLWH who were treated for cervical pre-cancer lesions. About 3 in 10 women came back to the clinic for rescreening. Educational status and type of treatment received for pre-cancer lesion were associated with adherence to rescreening.

The national guideline urges that every effort should be made to reach and contact women who have received positive test results.10 However, over two-thirds of the women, totalling 131 (68.6%), did not attend a single post-treatment visit during the 3-year follow-up period, similar to the findings from Kenya, where adherence ranges from 22% to 39%.34 Similarly, 34% of women came back for rescreening in Cameron.24

The finding of our study was lower than the couple of studies from Addis Ababa and surroundings, which showed that half of (51.1%), and 44.7% of the women who were expected to attend a follow-up screening actually returned and underwent the rescreening.21,22 Our finding is also less than the findings from Tanzania (65.3%).25 The possible reason for the difference could be that the studies from Addis Ababa (the former was part of the 4-year donor-funded project and the latter included both HIV-positive and HIV-negative women). The study from Argentina found that 70.1% of women who tested positive for HPV had a record of a second HPV test. This may be due to the difference in the geographical location and the type of test performed. In our study, only (16.8%) were tested with HPV DNA and triaged with the VIA test. This is because the HPV DNA test is new for our study setting and we commonly rely on the VIA test.

The adherence level in our study is similar to the result of a longitudinal cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia (31.4% vs 34.8%).20 The possible reason could be similar approach followed for the study and the overlap of longitudinal follow up time (September 2020 to September 2023 vs November 2022 to April 2024). However, our finding is higher compared to the other study from Addis Ababa which reported an adherence level of 27.9%.19 The difference could be due to the time and design of the study.

Educational status is associated with adherence to post-treatment follow-up. Educated women were more likely to adhere to cervical cancer screening (CCS) post-treatment follow-up compared to uneducated women. This finding is similar to a recent review that found an association between higher educational attainment and adherence to cervical cancer screening programmes.35 This finding is also supported by a couple of studies conducted in Ethiopia.20,22 Poor knowledge about cervical cancer screening was one of the main barriers to CCS uptake in SSA.36 This highlights the importance of empowering women through education to improve the cervical cancer screening continuum of care.24 Qualitative studies also revealed the importance of knowledge to improve CC screening adherence.37

The study found an association between the type of treatment received for the pre-cancerous lesion and adherence to rescreening. Women treated with cryotherapy were more likely to return for re-screening than those treated with thermocoagulation. This finding is similar to the findings from Southwestern Uganda where loss to follow-up from rescreening in patients who were treated with thermocoagulation was high.38 Thermocoagulation has a shorter duration of treatment and lower side effects compared to Cryotherapy.10 This may lead patients to perceive that additional follow-up is unnecessary, given the minimal side effects experienced. Qualitative studies support this misconception, indicating that patients often believe “follow-up is not necessary if one feels healthy.”23,28,29

Furthermore, healthcare providers tend to prefer thermocoagulation due to its ease of operation and greater comfort for patients.10,38 Therefore, post-treatment counselling about the importance of follow-up rescreening is crucial, even in the absence of pain or discomfort. On the other hand, the study from Tanzania reported that women treated with excision procedure (LEEP) were less likely to adhere to rescreening compared to those treated with ablation (ie Cryotherapy or thermocoagulation).25 This disparity in follow-up rescreening based on treatment modality warrants further qualitative investigation.25

According to other studies conducted in Africa, including Ethiopia, knowledge of recommended screening frequency, age over 40 years, no/incorrect follow-up appointment, use of reminders, and HPV status at initial screening were associated with adherence to follow-up.22,24 However, these variables were not significantly associated in our study. This may be because some of the variables were identified by interview in addition to the chart review.

Limitations and strengths of the study

This is one of the few studies to assess post-treatment cervical cancer screening follow-up specifically among WLWH in Ethiopia. All relevant information was collected from medical records and cervical cancer screening registries. However, the study has some limitations. The sample size is not large enough to detect the difference precisely. Therefore, we have refined the definition of our outcome variable to include more observations in the two-by-two tables. The data were obtained from the files, and some relevant variables were not available in the file. We were unable to address variables that were not available on the records of the patients. Despite all these limitations, the study provides important baseline information for further follow-up studies. Readers should therefore interpret it in the light of the above limitations.

Conclusion and recommendations

Adherence to post-treatment follow-up for cervical pre-cancer is very low among WLWH in our study setting. Only slightly less than one-third of women returned for post-treatment follow-up. Educational status and the type of treatment received for the pre-cancerous lesion were significantly associated with adherence to post-treatment follow-up. Empowering women through education is very important to improve women’s adherence to post-treatment follow-up. In addition, those who were treated with thermo-coagulation should be given due attention during counselling for cervical cancer screening and treatment. Health care providers should give an adequate explanation about the different treatment options and the follow-up programme for re-screening. An appropriate explanation about appointment is critically needed.
