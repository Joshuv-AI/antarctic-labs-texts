Undifferentiated carcinomas in a large animal model of liver cancer
Authors: F Edward Boas, Olca Basturk, Zhirong Yin, Hooman Yarmohammadi, Lobna Elkhadragy, Jason Chiang, Imran Shair Mohammad, Stephen B Solomon
Published: 2026

The Oncopig is a transgenic pig with Cre‐inducible site‐specific TP53 and KRAS mutations. Cressman et al. 1 reported that they induced liver tumors in the Oncopig, and they characterized the tumors as inflammatory pseudotumors. In our experience, we also saw inflammatory tumors in 3 of 46 Oncopig liver tumors. However, in the vast majority of Oncopig liver tumors (93%; 43 of 46), we saw neoplastic cells, in addition to the inflammation. 2 Histologically confirmed metastases to peritoneum, lymph nodes, and lung were seen. These tumors appear similar to undifferentiated carcinomas in humans (see Supplemental Materials). Undifferentiated carcinomas tend to be more inflammatory than well‐differentiated carcinomas in humans. In Oncopigs, the amount of inflammation seen in the tumors might depend on the time point or inoculation technique (see Supplemental Materials), although the exact causes and mechanisms remain to be determined.

Human carcinomas can contain up to 90% non‐neoplastic cells. The non‐neoplastic cells in the tumor microenvironment include inflammatory and mesenchymal cells, and they play a key role in tumor biology. 3 In contrast, rodent tumors generated by injecting cell lines contain mostly neoplastic cells, and the response of these cell line–derived tumors to anticancer drugs is poorly correlated with the response seen in humans. 3 The prominent tumor stroma seen in the Oncopig model potentially reflects an important aspect of human cancer biology.

The rapidly growing inflammatory carcinomas in Oncopigs increase in volume by 45% between weeks 1 and 2, then start to spontaneously regress after around 2 weeks, likely due to an antitumor immune response. 4 Therefore, we typically scan and perform locoregional therapy at day 7, and then scan and necropsy at day 14. Although this rapid treatment timeline is not ideal, it allows us to compare tumor regression after successful therapies with tumor growth in untreated tumors. 2 , 5 Similar to humans, Oncopig liver tumors demonstrate a radiographic response to locoregional therapy.

The Oncopig provides an interesting data point that ties into fundamental questions about tumorigenesis. In mice, one to two mutations (Kras and p53) are sufficient to generate nonregressing adenocarcinomas (KP model). In Oncopigs, KRAS and TP53 mutations generate inflammatory carcinomas that spontaneously regress. In adult humans, at least five driver mutations are generally required to cause cancer. These data are consistent with the hypothesis that larger and longer‐living animals needed to evolve more defenses against cancer and thus require more mutations to develop cancer. 3 The Oncopig could be a good model system for further exploring this hypothesis.

All animal models have various limitations. The Oncopig model is good for evaluating tumor targeting, safety, and pharmacokinetics of intra‐arterial and percutaneous therapies. It can also be used to evaluate response to new locoregional therapies, 5 although it is unknown whether this will be predictive of outcomes in humans. The Oncopig model may not be suitable if a specific histology or mutation is required, or if longer‐term follow‐up is required. Newer Oncopig models with tailored tumor mutations might overcome some of these limitations. 6
