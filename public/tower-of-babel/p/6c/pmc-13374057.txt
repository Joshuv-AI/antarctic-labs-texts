Learning curve for the abdominal procedure in total robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy with simultaneous cervical approach: an initial experience
Authors: Takahito Sugase, Takashi Kanemura, Norihiro Matsuura, Kei Yamamoto, Yuki Ushimaru, Yasunori Masuike, Yoshitomo Yanagimoto, Kazuyoshi Yamamoto, Ryota Mori, Masatoshi Kitakaze, Masahiko Kubo, Yasunari Fukuda, Hisateru Komatsu, Masaaki Miyo, Toshinori Sueda, Yoshinori Kagawa, Kunihito Gotoh, Shogo Kobayashi, Hiroshi Miyata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) is increasingly being implemented; however, the feasibility and learning process of total RAMIE using robotic approaches for thoracic and abdominal phases remain to be fully evaluated, particularly with a dual-docking technique enabling simultaneous cervical and abdominal approaches.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 50 consecutive patients who underwent total RAMIE with retrosternal gastric conduit reconstruction and cervical anastomosis at a single institution between September 2024 and August 2025. Cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis of abdominal operative time was used to assess the learning curve. Perioperative outcomes were compared between the phases.ResultsCUSUM analysis identified three phases: phases I (patients 1–6, learning phase), II (7–18, competence phase), and III (19–50, proficiency phase). Median abdominal operative time decreased from 139 min (124–154) in phase I to 104 min (80–137) and 87 min (66–146) in phases II and III, respectively (P < 0.001). Initially, this procedure was applied to cStage I patients without abdominal nodal involvement, and was gradually expanded to advanced disease. The number of harvested abdominal lymph nodes was 15 (6–27) in phases I and II and 17 (6–26) in phase III. Postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2) occurred in 14% of patients, with no abdominal procedure-related morbidity observed.ConclusionsProcedural proficiency for the abdominal operative procedure was achieved in approximately 18 patients, after which operative efficiency improved without compromising oncologic thoroughness or patient safety. Total RAMIE with a two-team approach can be safely implemented in centers with experience.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is among the tenth most common malignancies and sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Radical esophagectomy remains the standard curative treatment for potentially resectable thoracic esophageal cancer; however, it is one of the most invasive gastrointestinal procedures [2–5]. Robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) has been increasingly adopted as a promising alternative, offering articulated instruments, three-dimensional visualization, and improved ergonomics, which facilitate precise dissection and may reduce perioperative morbidity [6–10].

Recently, thoracic esophageal cancer surgery using robotic approaches for the thoracic and abdominal regions has been reported as total RAMIE [11]. However, previous learning curve studies have largely focused on the thoracic phase or two-field lymphadenectomy, primarily in patients with esophagogastric junction carcinoma or adenocarcinoma, which are more common in Western countries [12, 13]. Several studies from high-volume Western centers have also evaluated the robotic abdominal phase during the Ivor Lewis esophagectomy, demonstrating its feasibility and safety [14, 15]. In contrast, thoracic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma predominates in many Asian countries, where three-field lymphadenectomy with cervical node dissection (the McKeown approach) is the standard surgical strategy. Given the differences in procedural components and technical demands between operative phases, particularly in the context of three-field lymphadenectomy, the learning process may not be uniform across the entire procedure. However, total RAMIE with cervical lymphadenectomy remains technically demanding and less well characterized.

More recently, it was reported that total RAMIE with a simultaneous two-team approach using a new docking method can be safely introduced. Simultaneous cervical and abdominal manipulations using the new docking method allow for total RAMIE without prolonging operation time, suggesting that it may be a valuable approach for esophageal cancer surgery [16]. However, the learning curve for total RAMIE employing this technique has not been adequately investigated, particularly in institutions newly adopting this procedure. In addition, the abdominal phase involves distinct technical steps, including gastric mobilization, suprapancreatic lymphadenectomy, and creation of the retrosternal route, and requires close coordination with the cervical team during the simultaneous two-team approach. These characteristics suggest that the learning process of the abdominal phase may differ from that of the thoracic phase and therefore merits separate evaluation. The present study focuses on the learning curve associated with the implementation of this previously reported approach [16] at a new institution.

This study aimed to evaluate the learning curve for the abdominal operative procedure in total RAMIE using a new simultaneous two-team docking method, and to assess its perioperative safety and feasibility in the treatment of thoracic esophageal cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-institution retrospective study included 50 consecutive patients who underwent robot-assisted surgery using both thoracic and abdominal approaches with retrosternal gastric tube reconstruction and cervical anastomosis after esophagectomy at the Osaka International Cancer Institute between September 2024 and August 2025. Patient medical records were reviewed to extract data on patient characteristics, surgical procedures, clinicopathological findings, and postoperative outcomes. Esophagoscopy, computed tomography, and positron emission tomography were performed to determine clinical stages of disease. The eighth edition of the TNM classification system, developed by the Union for International Cancer Control, was used to classify clinical and histopathological findings [17].

The study protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution. This study was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee of Osaka International Cancer Institute (Approval No. 18033-4.). Written informed consent for the use of clinical data was obtained from all the patients, as required by the Institutional Review Board of the Osaka International Cancer Institute.

Surgery

At our institute, the standard surgical procedure for thoracic esophageal cancer consists of transthoracic esophagectomy with three-field lymphadenectomy involving the neck, mediastinum, and upper abdomen, in accordance with the Japanese Esophageal Society guidelines [18, 19]. Gastric conduit reconstruction is routinely performed via the retrosternal route with cervical anastomosis. Cervical lymphadenectomy was omitted in patients with lower thoracic esophageal cancer when intraoperative histological examination confirmed the absence of recurrent laryngeal nerve lymph node metastasis [20–22]. Even in such patients, dissection of the left cervical paraesophageal lymph nodes was performed using a cervical approach. The specimen was extracted through an umbilical incision, and a gastric conduit was prepared. After cervical anastomosis, the gastric conduit was laparoscopically fixed to the abdominal wall to create a feeding gastrostomy (Fig. 1a, b). A four-arm technique was used for the thoracic and abdominal phases of RAMIE using the da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Thoracic approach

The patients were placed in the left lateral semi-prone position. Robotic ports were placed as follows: the first arm was placed in the 9th intercostal space (ICS) along the scapular line. The second and third arms were placed in the 7th and 5th ICS on the posterior axillary line, respectively, and the fourth arm was placed in the 3rd ICS on the midaxillary line. A 12-mm assistant port was placed in the 6th ICS at the midaxillary line. Esophagectomy with total mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed using monopolar curved scissors and the double bipolar method. All the procedures were conducted by two board-certified esophageal surgeons, each of whom had performed > 100 minimally invasive esophagectomies before this study.

Abdominal approach

Robotic surgery in the abdominal approach was used for abdominal lymphadenectomy and retrosternal tunneling, with careful avoidance of direct gastric wall manipulation to preserve blood flow. The surgeon had completed formal robotic training, obtained certification, and had performed > 100 laparoscopic abdominal surgeries for esophageal cancer. Despite this experience, all abdominal procedures in the present study were performed by a single surgeon with no prior clinical experience in robotic surgery.

Surgical techniques

The abdominal procedure was performed in a stepwise manner as follows.

Step 1: Patient positioning and port placement

The patients were placed in the supine position. A small umbilical incision was made to insert a single-port device for pneumoperitoneum. Four robotic arm ports were positioned at the right costal margin, periumbilical region, left midclavicular line, and left costal margin. A 12-mm assistant port was placed at the right costal margin along the right midclavicular line (Fig. 1a). The first and second robotic arms were deployed on the patient’s left side, and the third and fourth arms were deployed on the right side, following the previously reported dual-docking method for simultaneous cervical and abdominal procedures by Sato et al. [16].

Step 2: Exposure and greater curvature mobilization

The lesser curvature of the stomach was grasped using an organ retractor (Fig. 1c) and retracted rightward to expose the greater curvature (Fig. 1d). The gastrocolic omentum was divided approximately 3 cm from the gastroepiploic arcade using a vessel sealer to open the omental bursa.

Step 3: Division of gastric vessels and splenic-side mobilization

The left gastroepiploic artery and vein were clipped and divided. The gastrosplenic ligament was divided, including transection of the short gastric vessels (Fig. 1e). Dissection was extended toward the left diaphragmatic crus (Fig. 1f), completing mobilization along the greater curvature.

Step 4: Lesser curvature dissection and suprapancreatic lymphadenectomy

The lesser omentum was divided toward the right crus while preserving the right gastric artery. The left gastric artery was elevated using an organ retractor to improve exposure of the surgical field (Fig. 1g). Suprapancreatic lymphadenectomy was then performed along the common hepatic and splenic arteries (Fig. 1h). The left gastric artery and vein were clipped and divided.

Step 5: Hiatal dissection and esophageal mobilization

Dissection proceeded toward the esophagogastric junction, and lymph nodes around the hiatus were dissected (Fig. 1i). The resected esophagus was pulled into the abdominal cavity, and the hiatus was closed with a non-absorbable suture.

Step 6: Creation of the retrosternal route

The retrosternal route was created by dividing the round and falciform ligaments and incising the peritoneum beneath the xiphoid process (Fig. 1j). A gauze was inserted from the cervical field and pulled into the abdominal cavity to expand the retrosternal space.

Step 7: Coordination with the cervical team (simultaneous two-team approach)

During the abdominal phase, the cervical and abdominal procedures were performed simultaneously using a two-team approach as previously described [16]. The cervical team initiated lymphatic dissection in the cervical field, while the abdominal team performed gastric mobilization and lymphadenectomy after docking of the robotic system. The two teams worked in parallel during most of the abdominal phase, with overlap particularly during retrosternal route creation. Interference between the robotic arms and the cervical operative field was minimized through careful port placement, lateral deployment of the robotic arms away from the cervical field to maintain a wide working space, and coordination between the two teams. In addition, the assistant port was selectively used for cephalad dissection when robotic reach was limited.

After completion of the procedure, hemostasis was confirmed, and the robotic system was undocked.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables are presented as medians (range) and percentages, respectively. Associations between patient data and operative procedures were retrospectively analyzed using the χ2 and Mann–Whitney U tests. All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical significance was set at P of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP® Pro 16.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis was performed to evaluate changes in operative times over the chronological sequence of patients. The abdominal operative time was defined as the duration of pneumoperitoneum. CUSUM was calculated as the CUSUM of the differences between each operative time and overall mean operative time. For the first patient, CUSUM was defined as the operative time minus overall mean. For subsequent patients, CUSUM was calculated using the following equation: CUSUMₙ = CUSUMₙ₋₁ + (operative timeₙ − mean operative time), where n indicates the patient number. A CUSUM curve was plotted as a line chart using Microsoft Excel (Redmond, WA, USA). A positive slope indicated consecutive patients with above-average operative times, whereas a negative slope indicated patients with below-average operative times. The turning point of the learning curve was defined as the point at which the CUSUM curve reached its maximum value and subsequently began to decline.

Results

Patient characteristics

Fifty patients underwent total RAMIE, with a median age of 65.5 years (range, 43–80 years) and 72% males. Preoperative treatments included chemotherapy (40%), chemoradiotherapy (8%), immunotherapy (6%), and endoscopic submucosal dissection (8%); 38% of the patients received no therapy. Tumors were located in the upper (38%), middle (28%), and lower thoracic esophagus (34%) and were predominantly squamous cell carcinoma (90%). Clinical stages were stages I (36%), II (40%), III (14%), and IV (Table 1).

Learning curve analysis

Abdominal operative time decreased in the consecutive patients (Fig. 2a). CUSUM analysis of the abdominal procedure of the total RAMIE identified three phases (Fig. 2b). The slope increased from the first to the sixth patient (phase I: learning phase), remained relatively stable between the seventh and eighteenth patients (phase II: competence phase), and showed a decreasing trend from the nineteenth patient onward (phase III: mastery/proficiency phase).

In phase I/II, most of the patients had cStage I disease without preoperative therapy, whereas in phase III, 24 of the 32 patients (75%) underwent preoperative treatment. Moreover, none of the patients in phase I/II had abdominal lymph node metastasis, whereas 6 of the 32 patients (19%) in phase III presented with clinical nodal involvement (Table 2).

Surgical outcomes in each learning phase

Median abdominal procedure time decreased from 139 min (range, 124–154) in phase I to 104 min (80–137) in phase II and 87 min (66–146) in phase III (phase I/II vs. phase III, P < 0.001), with a corresponding reduction in total operative time from 505 min (490–599) and 475 min (427–527) to 435 min (336–561; P < 0.001). Initially, only left cervical paraesophageal lymph node dissections were performed without supraclavicular lymph node dissection (two-field lymphadenectomy); however, 65% of the patients in phase III included supraclavicular dissection (three-field lymphadenectomy). The total nodes harvested increased from 38 (phase I/II) to 50 (phase III), although this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.160). Median number of dissected abdominal lymph nodes was 15 (range, 6–27) in phase I/II and 17 (6–26) in phase III, with metastases detected in three and seven patients, respectively. Postoperative complications of Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2 occurred in one patient in phase I/II (cervical surgical site infection) and six in phase III, including pleural effusion (n = 2), pneumonia (n = 2), severe swallowing disorder (n = 1), and anastomotic leak (n = 1). Median postoperative hospital stay was 13 days (range, 11–19 days) in phase I/II and 12 days (range, 11–41 days) in phase III (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the learning curve for abdominal operative procedures and the short-term surgical outcomes of total RAMIE using a dual-docking method that enabled a simultaneous two-team approach. The present study builds upon the previously reported dual-docking technique for total RAMIE [16] and provides further insight into its practical implementation by focusing on the learning curve during the initial adoption phase. This study was conducted at a high-volume center, as reflected by the relatively short inclusion period for consecutive cases, which may have contributed to procedural consistency and facilitated efficient skill acquisition. CUSUM analysis of abdominal operative time revealed three distinct phases: learning (patients 1–6), competence (patients 7–18), and proficiency (patients 19–50), with a marked reduction in abdominal operative time over the course of the study. Despite progression along the learning curve, there was no significant difference in the number of abdominal lymph nodes dissected across the phases. The overall incidence of postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2) was 14%, with no abdominal procedure-related complications reported. The overall complication rate remained acceptable and within expected ranges for this procedure. These findings suggest that this surgical approach is safe and feasible. In addition, it facilitates efficient skill acquisition, even among surgeons without prior experience in robotic abdominal procedures.

In the early learning phase, the surgical team focused on optimizing port placement and refining the procedural steps to effectively implement the dual-docking method and simultaneous two-team approach. As this was the first institutional introduction of total RAMIE using this technique, several initial patients were critical for establishing workflow and team coordination. To ensure safe team flow and procedure standardization, these early patients did not include patients requiring extensive therapeutic abdominal lymphadenectomy. Longer operative times during this phase reflect the trial-and-error processes necessary to overcome technical challenges and ensure safety. Once these adjustments were made, operative efficiency improved in the subsequent competence and proficiency phases. This highlights that although an initial learning phase is essential for safe and effective adoption, total RAMIE with the new docking method can be successfully integrated into clinical practice at new centers.

Previous studies investigating the learning curve for RAMIE have primarily focused on the thoracic phase or two-field lymphadenectomy. Ramos-Fresnedo et al. reported that technical proficiency was achieved after 55 cases in robot-assisted Ivor Lewis esophagectomy [13], while Yuan et al. demonstrated that 27 cases were required to attain proficiency in robot-assisted McKeown esophagectomy [12]. In contrast, data on the abdominal phase are limited. In our series, proficiency in the abdominal component appeared to be achieved earlier, with a turning point observed after six cases, although direct comparative evidence remains limited. This rapid initial turning point likely reflects the transfer of prior laparoscopic skills to the robotic platform rather than entirely de novo learning. While familiarity with individual surgical steps is required, the most challenging aspect during the early learning phase was coordination between the cervical and abdominal teams in the simultaneous two-team approach. In particular, optimization of workflow and communication between teams required a period of adaptation. Synchronization of operative timing during overlapping steps, such as retrosternal route creation, also contributed to this learning process. Although the surgeon in this study had extensive experience in laparoscopic abdominal procedures, proficiency in the abdominal component may be acquired more rapidly than that in the thoracic phase. These findings indicate that abdominal RAMIE can be introduced safely and efficiently, even in centers with limited prior robotic experience. They may also provide valuable insights for the development of future training strategies for comprehensive RAMIE programs.

A major innovation of this study is the simultaneous two-team approach, which permits concurrent cervical and abdominal procedures without prolonging operative time. This strategy is particularly advantageous in high-volume centers, where surgical efficiency is critical. Although the exact duration of simultaneous work between the cervical and abdominal teams could not be quantified in this retrospective study, the overlap time likely varied depending on the extent of cervical lymphadenectomy. In some cases, the cervical procedure was completed earlier, whereas in others it required a longer time. Nevertheless, because the cervical and abdominal procedures were performed simultaneously, improved efficiency of the abdominal phase during the parallel workflow may have contributed to shortening the total operative time, as reflected by the decrease in median total operative time from 490 min in phases I/II to 435 min in phase III. In addition, this approach may improve operating room utilization and overall efficiency by enabling a parallel workflow, which could have favorable implications for resource use and cost-effectiveness; however, this was not formally evaluated in the present study.

Additionally, this approach may shorten the overall learning process by allowing each surgical team to focus on a clearly defined field, facilitating role specialization and minimizing intraoperative interference. From an educational perspective, the two-team method provides opportunities for stepwise training, enabling less-experienced surgeons to participate in a structured and supervised manner. Taken together, these advantages suggest that the simultaneous two-team approach may not only improve operative efficiency but also enhance training opportunities and standardization in the implementation of total RAMIE.

However, this study has several limitations. First, this was a single-institution retrospective study with a relatively small sample size and single-surgeon experience, which may limit its generalizability. Second, the outcomes reflect the experience of a single surgeon whose extensive prior laparoscopic experience may have contributed to the steep learning curve, limiting its applicability to less-experienced surgeons. Third, the outcomes may be operator- and team-dependent, and reproducibility in other centers remains uncertain. Finally, although the short-term postoperative results were favorable, long-term oncologic outcomes were not evaluated, and further studies with extended follow-up are required. Additionally, although abdominal proficiency appeared to be acquired more rapidly than thoracic proficiency, a direct comparison with thoracic learning curves was not performed.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that procedural proficiency for the abdominal operative procedure of total RAMIE can be achieved in approximately 18 patients. Total RAMIE, using a robotic docking method combined with a simultaneous two-team approach, is a safe, feasible, and efficient surgical strategy for treating thoracic esophageal cancer. This approach offers a promising option for institutions aiming to implement total RAMIE, particularly in settings where three-field lymphadenectomy is the standard practice. Future multicenter prospective studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and further refine training protocols for robotic esophageal surgery.
