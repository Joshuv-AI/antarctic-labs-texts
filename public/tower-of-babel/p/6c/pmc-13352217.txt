Comprehensive Care Goals in Myasthenia Gravis

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesGoals for comprehensive care are important in the management of individualized treatment for patients with myasthenia gravis (MG), a disease with variable presentation and degrees of severity. Yet there is limited guidance on how comprehensive care should be achieved and implemented. We present global consensus recommendations for comprehensive care of patients with MG.MethodsAn international panel of experts was formed, and a targeted literature review was conducted to inform the recommendations. A steering committee selected relevant topics and draft recommendations were developed for each topic. Formal consensus was achieved using the RAND/UCLA appropriateness method. Seventeen panelists from North America, Europe, and Asia rated statements online from 1 (“extremely inappropriate”) to 9 (“extremely appropriate”) and provided comments and suggestions for modifications. The methodologist modified statements for further rating, based on panel scores and feedback. Statements achieving agreement as appropriate by 4 rounds of rating were accepted.ResultsConsensus was achieved for 21 statements. Statement 1 defined the ongoing treatment goal: “to work toward, achieve, and sustain minimal symptoms and treatment-related adverse events, with a patient-acceptable quality of life (using validated measures)”. Subsequent statements described implementation of this goal and covered: early control of symptoms; establishing and sustaining a treatment goal; vaccination and screening for infection; family planning/pregnancy; management of fatigue and comorbidities; and management of impending crisis and crisis.DiscussionExpert consensus was achieved on a series of global recommendations for comprehensive care goals, which has implications for improved disease outcomes and health-related quality of life for patients with MG. These recommendations will require updating as treatment paradigms evolve.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a rare neurologic autoimmune disease, characterized by fluctuating, fatigable muscle weakness (“neuromuscular fatigability”).1,2 Many patients initially present with ocular symptoms, such as ptosis and diplopia, but most go on to develop generalized MG (gMG), where bulbar muscles and/or muscles of the trunk, neck and extremities are affected.1,3,4 Worsening weakness of upper airway or respiratory muscles can result in respiratory failure (myasthenic crisis).5

MG pathophysiology, manifestations, and treatment response are heterogeneous, and use of available conventional, targeted, and surgical treatment options should be individualized according to disease and patient characteristics.6,7 Although clinical improvement is generally attainable,8 some patients experience ongoing disease burden, treatment side effects, or persistently impaired quality of life (QoL).9-11

Care goals in MG are important to guide patient-centered treatment selection, monitoring, and therapeutic adjustment12,13 and to aid patient expectations and understanding of treatment.14 While guidelines for the treatment of MG are available,6,7,12,13,15,16 there is limited guidance to provide comprehensive care goals beyond treatment. There is also limited guidance on how to achieve goals for individual patients with consideration of the wider disease and comorbidity burden affecting QoL, disease stage, and risk of exacerbation or crisis. Evidence to guide practical aspects of management, such as patient education, patient assessment, need and time frame for treatment escalation, glucocorticoid tapering, and preemptive vaccination, is also limited. Shared decision making is an important part of this process and is often not formally addressed.

We therefore used an expert consensus approach to develop globally relevant recommendations to provide comprehensive care guidance for individual patients with MG, to support the broad range of clinicians internationally who manage these patients. These recommendations address the overall management of MG and do not discuss individual treatments.

Methods

Steering Committee and Expert Panel

The study sponsor (UCB) initiated the consensus process, which was then driven by the steering committee. The steering committee comprised 3 neurologists (P.N., R.M., and K.U.) who were invited by the study sponsor based on their expertise in guideline development, the strength of their clinical expertise in MG, and their broad geographic representation (the United States, Europe, and East Asia). PN was the nonvoting methodologist. An expert panel of 15 neurologists, recommended by the steering committee based on a review of their experience in the management of MG and international representation, were invited by the sponsor to participate in the consensus process. The steering committee developed the topics and were involved in drafting the initial statements. After the initial statements were drafted, the methodologist took over with adapting and generating statements based on expert panel feedback. Online meetings and materials were conducted or prepared in English. Language translation was arranged where requested through use of interpreters during the expert panel briefing call held before rating. Additional information on the steering committee and expert panel is in the Supplementary Materials (eAppendix 1: Supplemental Methods).

Literature Search

A literature search was conducted to inform the scope of the draft recommendations (eAppendix 1: Supplemental Methods). Three separate searches (limited to English language publications) for articles on MG treatment goals and related aspects, national and international management guidelines and consensus guidance in MG, and ocular MG, were conducted in Medline using PubMed (eTable 1; eFigure 1). Additional articles were sourced by screening reference lists and through desk research, resulting in a total of 108 articles for review (eFigure 1). A written summary of the scientific evidence was developed by a medical writer with direction, input, review, and revisions by the steering committee. A document defining terms used in the statements was also developed by a medical writer with steering committee review, revision, and approval. Both documents were shared with panelists before rating.

Consensus Method

This consensus process used a RAND/UCLA appropriateness method to gain collective expert judgment on the appropriateness of guidance recommendations (“statements”) relating to treatment goals in MG (eAppendix 1: Supplemental Methods). All steps, including initial meetings of the steering committee, expert panel briefing calls, rating rounds and analysis, were conducted online.

The consensus process was initiated in May 2023 and completed in July 2025. There were 4 rounds of rating with analysis and revision of statements in between rounds. Inclusion of the fourth round (a change to the intended maximum of 3 rounds) was implemented after 3 statements (including one 2-part statement) underwent minor revision post-Round 3 based on panel feedback. Fifteen expert panelists (6 from the United States, 2 from Japan, and one each from France, the United Kingdom, Spain, Canada, Germany, Norway, and Poland) and 2 steering committee members (Italy and Japan) participated in the rating of statements.

Generating Statements and Assessing Consensus

The steering committee identified 5 initial broad topics for recommendations (establishing and sustaining a treatment goal; working toward early symptom control; pregnancy/family planning; fatigue and comorbidity management; and managing impending crisis and crisis) and drafted the initial statements with support from a medical writer. Statements were shared with the expert panel for individual online rating (Supplementary Materials: eAppendix 1, eFigure 2) on a 1–9 scale, where ratings in the 1–3 range were classed as varying degrees of inappropriate, 4–6 as uncertain, and 7–9 as appropriate.17 Panelists could suggest modifications to statements and provide qualitative comments or references for the modifications (eFigure 2).

Appropriateness and agreement for each statement were determined using the RAND/UCLA method for a panel size of 14–16.17 A median score of 7–9 without disagreement was defined as appropriate. Agreement was defined as ≤4 scores outside the 3-point region (1–3, 4–6 or 7–9) containing the median. Disagreement was defined as a vote split with ≥5 participants each voting in the 1–3 and 7–9 ranges. Statements that achieved agreement as appropriate were accepted as final statements.

The methodologist adapted and/or generated new statements with editorial support from a medical writer to address panel feedback. If consensus (i.e., agreement as appropriate) was not achieved after the planned 3 rounds of individual rating, the statements were to be excluded. Statements with only minor (e.g., editorial) amends after achieving consensus underwent a simple “yes” (appropriate)/“no” (inappropriate) vote, with agreement defined as ≥80% of panelists voting “yes”.

Results

Narrative Review

Current Treatment Goals in MG

Disease control based on symptoms, signs, or functional limitations is a core element of existing treatment goals in MG, as proposed by national and international management guidance (eTable 2).6,7,12,13,15,16,18-22 The definition and level of control vary across the guidance publications,6,7,12,13,15,16,19-21 acknowledging that the ideal goal of full remission (no signs or symptoms of MG)20 can be difficult to achieve with currently available therapies. The international consensus, Japanese, French, and Israeli guidance, for example, state the goal is minimal manifestations (MM) or better.13,15,16,20 Treatment goals may also encompass the minimization of treatment-related adverse events12,21 by requiring, for example, MM with an oral prednisolone dose no greater than 5 mg/d (MM-5 mg)15,19 or side effects with Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade no greater than 1.13,20 Maintaining QoL and mental health,7,15 minimizing the disease impact on personal and professional life, and limiting disease progression16 may also form part of the treatment goal (eTable 2). Consistent with guidance recommendations, the goals of MG treatment in clinical practice are typically to relieve symptoms and functional limitations without adverse events, with acceptance that mild weakness of some muscles may persist.23 For some patients, including those with difficult-to-treat disease, improving function and QoL may be a more realistic or more patient-centered goal than achieving MM.24

Evaluating Progress Toward Treatment Goals

A plethora of patient-reported and clinician-reported measures are available to evaluate progress toward treatment goals in clinical practice, including measures for the assessment of symptoms, functioning, health-related QoL, social relationships, fatigue, and anxiety and depression.25-27 There is often a lack of clear evidence to favor one measure over another, and usage and access can vary.27 The German and Israeli guidelines recommend that clinical outcome measures (e.g., Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis, Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living [MG-ADL], and Myasthenia Gravis Quality of Life 15-item revised scores, as well as current Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America [MGFA] Disease Class) are regularly collected as part of ongoing patient assessment.7,20 A European consensus on patient assessment recommends consistent use of the MG-ADL followed by a simple question (e.g., the “patient-acceptable symptom state” [PASS]28) to evaluate patient satisfaction, with other assessments when warranted.29 Italian and Gulf region recommendations suggest that clinicians may choose the most appropriate tools to monitor progress, considering factors such as ease and time to administer and patient-specific variables.12,30 Assessment of MM status can be inconsistent, depending on how clinicians interpret the MM criteria; to help address this, a machine learning model to support uniform assessment of MM status based on clinical scores has been developed.31

A rating scale to assess the fluctuations and severity of ocular signs and symptoms (the Ocular Myasthenia Gravis Rating Scale) has been developed through an international collaboration,32 and the Myasthenia Gravis Impairment Index includes an 8-item ocular subscore as well as a 20-item generalized subscore.33 Generalization of ocular disease can be identified through routine assessment of patient history and examination.32 Treatment-related adverse events should be routinely assessed.29 Preliminary data suggest that use of the “Adverse Event Unit” (a patient‐ and physician‐weighted metric to quantify treatment adverse event burden in neurologic patients) is feasible and valid for measuring the adverse event burden during routine MG patient visits.34,35

Patient Involvement in Establishing Comprehensive Care Goals

Comprehensive care goals in MG should be tailored to and agreed with the individual patient.22 The literature suggests that patients' preferences for their role in the decision-making process, and their medical literacy, may influence the extent of their participation.36,37 However, there should be an attempt to know the patient, their lifestyle, their overall values, and any limitations from MG that they would like to overcome.14,38 In this way, patient goals and preferences can be incorporated into treatment plans, including in cases where treatment may be complex as a result of comorbidities, and the need for psychological support considered. The Nordic management guidelines, which include patient representatives among the authors, emphasize the importance of a personalized approach to treatment, and of combining drug and nondrug therapies (such as exercise and nutrition, speech therapy, and mental health support) with active user involvement.22 Importantly, care goals should be re-evaluated with the patient periodically to ensure adjustments can be considered that are meaningful to the patient.38

Treatment Selection

Because no single management approach is optimal for all patients with MG, treatment choice should be individualized according to disease characteristics, comorbidities, age, childbearing status and plans, patient preferences and occupation, and treatment availability.6,7,20 Clinicians also face financial considerations, with situations that vary across different countries including regulatory approvals, payer reimbursement, individual cost of care, and the wider cost to society.1,8,18 Most MG treatment guidelines emphasize that immune-directed therapy is required, rather than purely symptomatic treatment, and treatments such as glucocorticoids, nonsteroidal immunosuppressants and rescue therapies (intravenous immunoglobulin [IVIg] or plasma exchange [PLEX]), and thymectomy, as well as newer, targeted therapies (e.g., complement inhibitors, anti-neonatal Fc receptor [FcRn] therapy, and B-cell inhibitors) are recommended.1,7,12,13,15,22 Steroid-sparing strategies remain important, with approaches to minimize the risks associated with long-term use of glucocorticoids recommended.15,39 Communications with patients regarding treatment goals should include early discussion of the potential benefits and risks of available therapeutic options.16 Physical activity and training, occupational therapy, speech and swallowing therapy, and psychological intervention can also have an important role in the management of MG, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration in MG management.16,20,22

Importance of Early Disease Control

Evidence is accumulating for the importance of early disease control in MG, within 1–2 years of onset, to improve longer-term MG outcomes.15,20,40,41 A retrospective analysis of 199 patients with new-onset gMG (with generalized symptoms presenting within 1 year of onset) showed that early remission (defined as being continuously asymptomatic from year 1 to year 2) predicted a benign disease course through 10 years.41 A 15-year cohort study highlighted the severe prognosis of refractory gMG and suggested that intensive monitoring and effective treatment in the early phase (within 1 year) may be needed to prevent transition to refractory disease.40

Early suppression of disease activity may also be of benefit in ocular MG.42 In retrospective analyses of patients with ocular MG (MGFA Disease Class I), early intervention with immunotherapy or high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) improved the likelihood of achieving MM (or better) status or MM-5 mg, and early high-dose IVMP showed a steroid-sparing effect.42,43 Immunotherapy initiation within 12 months of symptom onset was also a prognostic factor for the resolution of myasthenic-related persistent ophthalmoparesis.44 A clinical concern in ocular MG is to prevent progression to generalized disease.45,46 A systematic review and meta-analysis of retrospective data from Western populations with ocular MG found that early use of prednisolone and other immunosuppressants reduced the risk of disease generalization by 81%.47 Tools and models to predict generalization risk have been developed in different populations.4,48-50 These have the potential to support clinical decision making, for example, by guiding use of early immunosuppressive treatment (and/or thymectomy) in patients at high risk of generalization.4,48

Adapting Treatment to Achieve and Sustain Care Goals

It is important to reassess treatment and make changes if care goals cannot be sustained.13,21 Where conventional therapies do not achieve symptom control or are poorly tolerated, newer therapies targeted to the underlying MG pathophysiology should be initiated.7,12,15,20,e1 A retrospective cohort study showed that most patients with MG can achieve MM or better within 2 years when treated with available therapies according to the international consensus recommendations, although interpretation of the findings is limited by the lack of control for confounding variables.e2 Co-ordination with (or treatment by) a specialist or unit with expertise in MG management is important for multimodal management of MG,22 including when the disease is difficult to manage.13,19

Exacerbation and Crisis

Patients with MG can be at risk of exacerbation (clinical worsening) and myasthenic crisis (worsening weakness of the upper airway or respiratory muscles resulting in respiratory failure; MGFA Disease Class V)5 throughout their disease course. It seems reasonable to advise patients on the symptoms and potential triggers for clinical worsening and crisis, which can include heat exposure, infection, some prescription or over-the-counter medications, pregnancy, and surgery.1,6,8,12 A retrospective cohort study identified disease severity at diagnosis as a predictor of exacerbation or crisis and highlighted the importance of close monitoring and prevention of infectious complications in at-risk patients.e3 Myasthenic crisis affects up to 20% of patients at least once in their lifetime.5 Rapid clinical worsening that, in the opinion of the treating physician, could lead to myasthenic crisis in the coming days and weeks is referred to as “impending crisis.”13 Rapid-acting treatment with PLEX or IVIg is used as rescue therapy for both impending crisis and crisis.12,13 More recently, case reports documenting the use of new, targeted therapies in myasthenic crisis are emerging.e4 Myasthenic crisis also requires appropriate ventilatory support.5

Generalized Fatigue

There is growing evidence that generalized fatigue, a subjective feeling of tiredness or lack of energy, is a prevalent patient-reported symptom of MG that diminishes patients' QoL.e5 The causes of generalized fatigue in MG are not fully understood. However, it has been associated with MG severity, depression, sleep debt and disturbance, lower physical activity, obesity, and female sex.e5-e8 Generalized fatigue is distinct from the core MG symptom of neuromuscular fatigability and may have different underlying pathophysiology.e9 MG-specific treatment has been shown to improve both fatigue and fatigability in parallel, although fatigue is usually more treatment-resistant and may persist despite control of muscle fatigability.e10-e12 Psychological therapy and exercise programs may also be effective in managing fatigue.e5,e6

Pregnancy

MG can present specific challenges during and after pregnancy. Where possible, pregnancies should be planned when a patient's MG is stable.e13 Women with MG can be at risk of exacerbations during pregnancy, maternal/fetal complications, and postpartum readmission for both MG exacerbations and unrelated obstetric conditions.e14-e16 Despite this, pregnancy for most women with MG is uncomplicated, although symptom deterioration is common in the postpartum period owing to hormonal changes and stress.e13,e17 MG affects delivery choices, and assisted or cesarean delivery may be more common in women with MG than in the general population.e14 All aspects of pregnancy and giving birth should be discussed regularly with women of reproductive age who have MG,e13 given that more than a third of pregnancies may be unplanned in women with MG.e18 It has been suggested that the treating neurologist should co-ordinate with obstetric services13 and other relevant specialists during the pregnancy and postpartum periods.13,21,22

Comorbidity

Comorbidity management is an integral part of long-term MG management, often involving referral or care co-ordination with other clinicians or specialists. Common comorbidities experienced by patients with MG include hypertension, depression and anxiety, and other autoimmune disorders.e19,e20 Comorbidities may be pre-existing, disease-related, or treatment-related (e.g., glucocorticoid-induced psychiatric symptoms).e19,e20 Early detection of comorbidities is important for optimal patient management,e20 although screening methods and tools may vary across centers.

Immunizations

It is suggested that the recommended immunization schedule for age and comorbidities should be completed in patients with MG and, if feasible, before the initiation of any type of immunosuppressive therapy, or as early as possible during treatment, in accordance with available guidelines.7,e21 These guidelines may include local, national (e.g., US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices guidelines), or WHO immunization standards,e22,e23 acknowledging the lack of MG-specific standards. Guidelines, immunization schedules and product information emphasize that vaccination against Neisseria meningitidis is required before starting treatment with complement inhibitors.7,15,18 Additional treatment-specific vaccinations may become a requirement in the future as other targeted therapies enter the landscape. Screening for underlying infections is recommended in anticipation of the use of immunosuppressive agents, particularly for hepatitis B and hepatitis C, and for other latent disorders such as tuberculosis and human immunodeficiency virus in special circumstances.e21,e24-e26

Outcome of the Consensus Process

A total of 21 statements reached agreement as appropriate across 6 topics: establishing and sustaining an MG treatment goal; working toward early symptom control; infection screening and vaccination; pregnancy and family planning; fatigue and comorbidity management; and managing impending crisis and crisis. Details of ratings and of each round are presented in the Supplementary Materials (eAppendix 2: Supplemental Results; eTables 3 and 4).

Discussion

This was a formal consensus exercise with the strengths and limitations inherent to such a process. There was geographical representation from 10 countries across 3 continents. Some parts of the world, including Asia ex-Japan, Africa, Latin America, and Australasia were not represented among the expert panel, potentially limiting the generalizability of the statements, including to low- and middle-income countries and resource-limited settings.

Depending on the country and institutional setting, patients with MG can be managed by health care professionals with varied training, experience, and background (i.e., Doctor of Medicine [MD] or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine [DO] and advanced practice providers [nurse practitioners]). The impetus for this document was the approval of new targeted therapies and the ongoing development of therapies based on the pathogenesis of MG. Although this document does not discuss specific treatments, the recommendations take into account the characteristics of treatments, including effectiveness, time to effect, and adverse event profile. There are no previously published guidance statements similar to this. While our treatment goal definition has the same underlying principles as previous definitions, it expands on themes such as inclusion of QoL and provides methods of operationalizing constructs from other consensus documents.

Patients or caregivers were not involved in the consensus process. When future updates are made, patient and caregiver perspectives will be incorporated in the process. Based on these consensus statements, patient–clinician discussion guides are being developed in collaboration with MG patient advocacy organizations to facilitate shared decision making.

We believe that these recommendations will help guide overall management, treatment, and referral decisions throughout the patient journey.
