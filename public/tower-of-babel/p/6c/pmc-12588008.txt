Invasive fungal infections in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: an explorative analysis from the German RABBIT register
Authors: Alina Purschke, Vera Zietemann, Peter Herzer, Cornelia Kühne, Thomas Marycz, Uta Syrbe, Adrian Richter, Anja Strangfeld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore characteristics and treatment of patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) with invasive fungal infections (IFIs) and to describe infection details and outcome.MethodsIFIs reported to the German RABBIT (Rheumatoid Arthritis: Observation of Biologic Therapy) register between May 2001 and December 2023 were analysed using a two-stage approach. First, a detailed case description, including a comparison of cases by infection outcome, was conducted using routine register data and hospital discharge letters. Second, a multivariable time-dependent Cox regression model was fitted to analyse associations with IFI development using routine register data only.ResultsAmong 22 535 patients with RA, 69 IFIs occurred (incidence rate: 0.65/1000 patient-years), with a case fatality of 35% (n=24). Causative pathogens were Pneumocystis jirovecii (35%), Candida spp (33%) and Aspergillus spp (13%).Patients with IFIs were on average 65.4 years old; 54% were female, 80% ever-smokers, 84% glucocorticoid (GC) users and 91% had any infection-risk associated comorbidity at hospitalisation. IFI development was associated with older age (HR 1.36 per 10 years (95% CI 1.05 to 1.76)), male sex (HR 1.92 (95% CI 1.14 to 3.21)), ever-smoking (HR 2.43 (95% CI 1.35 to 4.37)), cardiovascular disease (HR 3.03 (95% CI 1.80 to 5.10)), chronic lung disease (HR 2.09 (95% CI 1.21 to 3.61)), other autoimmune disease (HR 2.11 (95% CI 1.10 to 4.07)), and the use of GCs (HR 1.17 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.22)).ConclusionIn patients with RA, IFIs are rare but life-threatening. They occur particularly in patients who are predisposed to infection due to relevant comorbidities, a history of smoking or both. Except for GC use, the overall impact of targeted RA treatment on the risk of IFI appears to be less relevant than that of multimorbidity.Keywords: Arthritis, Rheumatoid; Epidemiology; Antirheumatic Agents; Glucocorticoids

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Several studies have reported on cases with invasive fungal infections (IFIs), especially Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but there is no consistent characterisation of an at-risk subgroup among patients with RA.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

By combining longitudinal data from a large German register with detailed information from discharge letters, this study provides a European perspective on IFIs in patients with RA beyond PJP alone. It highlights the role of infection risk-associated comorbidities and smoking in IFI development and indicates a limited generalisability of the host factors specified in the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) diagnostic criteria to an RA population, where cardiovascular and pulmonary comorbidities seem to be more important determinants.

Introduction

The rise in the global threat of invasive fungal infections (IFIs) in recent decades1 led the WHO to publish a fungal priority pathogens list.2 Its aim is to heighten awareness and to reduce the emergence of infections with fungi such as Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida spp, Cryptococcus spp, Histoplasma spp and Pneumocystis jirovecii. Invasive infections caused by these fungi have a high case fatality of 25–60% in treated and up to 100% in untreated non-HIV patients,1 highlighting the need for timely diagnosis and, consequently, improved treatment. Currently, fungal detection remains difficult because all available methods have drawbacks,3 4 which is why they need to be interpreted alongside clinical features and patient characteristics.

Because of the difficult diagnosis, the Infectious Diseases Group of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) developed consensus definitions5 to support case evaluation in medical research. They include the assessment of mycological methods, clinical features and patient and treatment characteristics — referred to as ‘host factors’. These host factors are established for patients with cancer, recipients of haematopoietic stem cell transplants or solid organ transplants. It is unclear whether they are also applicable to patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Patients with RA may represent an at-risk population for IFI due to the autoimmune nature of the disease, common comorbidities predisposing to infection and the frequent use of immunosuppressing or —modulating treatments like glucocorticoids (GCs), biologic (b) and targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs).6,10 Diabetes, pulmonary disease and the use of GCs, methotrexate (MTX) and other immunosuppressants were frequently found in patients with RA with IFIs.11 12 Additionally, studies focusing on Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) indicate that patients with RA with PJP are more likely to be male, older,13,16 more frequently have lymphopenia16 and are more often treated with bDMARDs.1417,19

Nevertheless, data on IFI development are still inconsistent, since most studies involve only a low number of patients, are primarily conducted in Asia or focus solely on PJP. It remains uncertain which comorbidities and DMARDs are associated with IFI development, or whether RA-related autoimmunity predisposes patients to IFI. Due to this data gap, there are no European recommendations on IFI management in patients with RA, which is why the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology has included screening for fungal infections in its research agenda on opportunistic infections in rheumatic diseases.20

The objective of this study was to explore the characteristics and treatment of patients with RA who developed an IFI within the German RABBIT cohort (Rheumatoid Arthritis: Observation of Biologic Therapy). An in-depth description of patient characteristics, treatment and courses of IFI cases is provided in a first step using hospital discharge letters in addition to data collected regularly in the register. In the second step, potential associations between selected risk factors for IFI are examined using adjusted time-to-event models.

Methods

Source population

All cases reported between May 2001 and December 2023 to the German longitudinal cohort study, RABBIT, were analysed. RABBIT enrols adult patients with RA either starting (1) a new b/tsDMARD, (2) a new conventional synthetic (cs) DMARD after at least one csDMARD failure or (3) adding a new csDMARD treatment to an existing one. Written informed consent is obtained from patients before their enrolment.

Data collection and follow-up

RABBIT collects data from nationwide rheumatological care units using standardised physician and patient questionnaires at baseline, after 3 months, after 6 months and biannually thereafter for at least 5 and up to 10 years. The data collected on a regular basis include demographics, clinical variables such as disease activity measurements, RA treatment details, comorbidities, adverse events and patient-reported outcomes. If a serious adverse event (SAE) of interest is reported, an additional standardised query is conducted to gather more information and hospital discharge letters are requested. Further details on RABBIT’s data collection process are published elsewhere.21

The following covariates were used in this study from the regularly collected data: age, sex, RA disease duration, rheumatoid factor (RF)/anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) status, disease activity measured by the disease activity score for 28 joints using the C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) and simple disease activity index, physical function measured by the Hanover functional ability questionnaire (FFbH), smoking status, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, mean GC dose over the last 6 months, current DMARD treatment and the number of previous b/tsDMARDs.

To enrich the routinely collected data for the description of IFI cases, additional information was obtained from infection-related SAE queries, hospital discharge letters or both. These data supplemented the routine register data on smoking status, comorbidities, BMI, DMARD treatment discontinuation and the course of IFI (see ‘Analysis’ for a detailed variable description).

Case definition

Fungal infections were classified as invasive if pathogens penetrated a tissue barrier and spread beyond. To determine all IFIs, adverse events were filtered for ‘fungal infectious disorders’ using the respective MedDRA High Level Group Term and plain-text searches (figure 1). Additionally, all available discharge letters for cases of serious pneumonia, bloodstream infections and central nervous system infections were screened to ensure the inclusion of causative fungi that were not coded.

Flow chart of the case selection and evaluation process.

Due to the challenging diagnosis, all cases were evaluated by a RABBIT pharmacist (AP) and the lead physician of RABBIT (AS) by reviewing diagnostic criteria extracted from hospital discharge letters and SAE queries. IFIs were then classified as proven, probable or possible based on adapted EORTC/MSG criteria.5 Adaptations were aimed at making criteria applicable to epidemiological studies without direct patient assessment. That means that the classification was based on the method, specimen type and positive result reported by the diagnosing physician without further details on sample preparation, staining procedures and other method-specific information. Additionally, since the objectives of this work were to explore patient characteristics and treatment in patients with RA, the absence of ‘host factors’ as defined by EORTC/MSG did not result in case exclusion. If no detailed diagnostic information was reported, cases were categorised as ‘diagnostic criteria missing’.

Fungal infections were excluded if (1) they were not considered invasive, such as allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, aspergilloma or other non-invasive Aspergillus infections, as well as mucosal or skin infections; (2) presumptive diagnoses were not confirmed by the reporting physician and EORTC/MSG criteria were unknown.

We identified 625 fungal infections reported to RABBIT between May 2001 and December 2023. After excluding non-invasive infections (n=555) and unconfirmed diagnoses (n=1), 69 IFIs in 68 patients were included in the descriptive analysis (figure 1). In the time-to-event analysis, only first events were analysed (n=68).

Course of infection, definition of infection outcome and mortality

For the description of cases, all additional sources, including SAE queries, hospital discharge letters and death certificates, were reviewed. Data on fungal species, diagnostic criteria (mycological and radiological methods), other detected pathogens, anti-infective treatment, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and infection outcomes were extracted.

As the primary infection outcome, fatality was calculated as the percentage of patients who died from or with infection. Additionally, 30-day and 90-day mortality were calculated as the percentage of deaths from any cause occurring within 30 or 90 days, respectively, after hospitalisation or the diagnosis of IFI, whichever occurred first. Death was either reported by the rheumatologist or identified through a standardised dropout investigation, including contact with relatives, the local registration office and the local public health office.

Analysis

Data preparation and all analyses for this study were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). All tables display continuous variables as means and SD and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, unless stated otherwise, and were generated using R package table1.22 Bar charts and the forest plot were created using R package ggplot2.23 The incidence rate was calculated as events per total patient observation time in RABBIT, with exact confidence intervals based on a Poisson distribution.

Two approaches were employed to explore characteristics and treatment of patients with RA who developed an IFI. First, as the available literature provides only sparse information, demographic and clinical data and the course of infection were described in detail using only descriptive statistics mentioned above. This was done to extensively capture the clinical presentation of patients with IFIs as well as to generate hypotheses for the second approach, a time-to-event analysis.

For the description of IFI cases, data were assessed either on the day of hospitalisation or the last visit prior to hospitalisation, except for the RF/ACPA status, which was evaluated at baseline. Age and disease duration were calculated for the day of hospitalisation. Disease activity and GC use were reported at the last visit prior to hospitalisation. Similarly, smoking status, BMI and comorbidities were obtained from the last visit but, if missing, supplemented with information from discharge letters. Additionally, adverse events reported to RABBIT and coded at the MedDRA Preferred Term level were screened for chronic comorbidities of interest. Only comorbidities that predispose to infection were analysed, defined as diabetes mellitus, chronic lung disease, chronic renal disease, liver fibrosis or cirrhosis, autoimmune disease other than RA or immunodeficiency, chronic cardiovascular disease without arterial hypertension or (a history of) malignancy. DMARD treatment was defined as current if ongoing at hospitalisation or discontinued within the last 3 months before hospitalisation. Information on previous and current DMARDs was gathered from regular RABBIT data, while details on potential discontinuation in temporal proximity to the event were obtained either from the rheumatologist or discharge medication records. The underlying reasons for this discontinuation, either hospitalisation, IFI diagnosis or coinfection, could not always be clarified.

In the time-to-event analysis, we investigated risk factors for IFI by comparing IFI cases with the remaining cohort and by using univariable and multivariable time-varying Cox proportional hazards (Cox-PH) models.24 This analysis was restricted to first events (n=68). The follow-up period started on the day of enrolment in RABBIT and ended either on the day of a first event or at censoring due to death, drop-out or the end of RABBIT observation, whichever came first. Only patients with at least one follow-up after enrolment were analysed. A 3-month risk window was applied for current and previous DMARD treatment (9 months for rituximab (RTX) due to its longer half-life) to assign the respective treatment to the event of interest. Cox-PH models24 were fitted using a robust sandwich estimator.25

The variables included in the Cox-PH models were selected a priori based on a literature review and were further informed by the descriptive analysis from approach 1 and the univariable models. The aims in selecting the variables were to choose a set of covariates that is clinically relevant and a number of covariates that is statistically feasible, in order to avoid overfitting,26 which is especially important given the rarity of events. All variables included were based solely on routine register data to ensure the same data accuracy for cases as for the remaining cohort. Age, sex, ever-smoking and the comorbidities were analysed as baseline values. Liver fibrosis/cirrhosis was not analysed due to a low observed frequency in both cases and the remaining cohort. The DAS28-CRP, FFbH, mean GC dose over the last 6 months and current and previous b/tsDMARD treatment were considered time-varying. Schoenfeld residuals27 were used to check the proportional hazards assumption. Missing values were imputed 20 times using SAS PROC MI and the fully conditional specification method. Multiple imputed data were analysed according to Rubins’ rule28 using SAS PROC MIANALYZE.

Results

Approach 1: Case description

Patient characteristics

Among all RABBIT patients, 68 developed 69 IFIs (including 1 recurrent PJP) over 106 629 patient-years (PY), corresponding to an incidence rate of 0.65 per 1000 PY (95% CI 0.50 to 0.82/1000 PY). The mean age of patients with IFIs at hospital admission was 65.4 years, with 54% of patients being female and 80% ever-smokers (table 1). 61% of patients presented with moderate to high disease activity at their last visit prior to hospitalisation. 91% of all patients had at least one infection risk-associated comorbidity; 69% had two or more.

EORTC/MSG host factors5 were identified in 51% of patients, based on the use of TNFi and abatacept as T-cell immunosuppressants, RTX as a B-cell immunosuppressant, the GC dose at the last visit and the diagnosis of neutropenia, without data on cell count or duration (online supplemental tables S4-1–S4-4). Fungal pathogens and infection outcome

The most common IFIs were Pneumocystis infections (n=24), followed by Candida infections (n=23) and Aspergillus infections (n=9). The fungal species was reported in 70% and included P. jirovecii, C. albicans, C. glabrata and A. fumigatus. In 10 cases, the fungal genus was not reported and 3 patients were infected with more than one fungus concurrently. No cases of cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis or other IFIs were observed.

All Pneumocystis and Aspergillus infections and infections with multiple fungi presented as pneumonia (table 3). Although the most common manifestation of infections with Candida or unknown fungi was also pneumonia, those infections also included cases of pulmonary sepsis, sepsis with another focus, infectious arthritis of the hip joint, meningitis or encephalitis, or fungal abscess of the throat.

Only 7 infections could be classified as proven (ie, requiring histological evidence), 26 as probable and 26 as possible (figure 1, table 3) according to the adapted EORTC/MSG criteria.5 For 10 infections, no diagnostic criteria were documented.

In 30 cases, the IFI occurred as either a coinfection or superinfection (online supplemental tables S2-1, S2-2) of a viral (n=3) or bacterial infection (n=21). Six patients experienced subsequent or concurrent infections with bacteria, viruses and fungi.

The course of IFI was serious in all cases. Although data were only available for 46 cases, 31 required ICU treatment (table 3). More than one-third of patients died from or with infection (n=24, 35%). After 30 days, 16 of 68 patients (24%) had died from any cause, with 9 additional deaths occurring within the next 60 days (36%). In one case, the patient’s vital status remained unknown, as she had dropped out of the study due to being in a coma following the infection.

Comparison of patient characteristics by infection outcome

Deceased patients were more often female, older and had a longer disease duration, a tendency to a higher disease activity and were less often current or former smokers (table 1). Notably, the high number of comorbidities seen in all patients was even higher in the subgroup of deceased patients, with all of them having at least one infection risk-associated comorbidity and almost 60% having two or more. Especially the frequency of cardiovascular diseases, chronic lung diseases, current or prior solid malignancies and chronic renal diseases was higher in patients who died than in those who recovered (figure 2).

Patients who recovered were more often treated with b/tsDMARDs, csDMARDs and, consequently, any DMARD therapy before hospitalisation than patients who died (table 2). They were also more often on combination therapy.

Approach 2: investigation of associations between IFI occurrence and clinical factors

At baseline, patients who developed an IFI during follow-up (=cases) were more often male, older, had more often seropositive RA and were more limited in their physical function, as reflected by a lower mean FFbH score (table 4). They were also more often current or former smokers and had a higher number of infection risk-associated comorbidities than the remaining cohort. Regarding comorbidities, differences were especially pronounced for cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, (a history of) solid malignancy, arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal disease and other autoimmune disease.

Discussion

This study advances the limited data on IFIs in patients with RA by analysing 69 cases using a nationwide register, complemented with hospital discharge letters. Combining the systematic nature of longitudinal cohort data with patient-level outcome data enabled both detailed case descriptions and the investigation of associations between key clinical and demographic variables and the development of IFIs.

The observed incidence rate of 0.65 IFI per 1000 PY underscores the rarity of IFIs in patients with RA. However, these infections are life-threatening, as shown by two-thirds of patients with available data being treated in the ICU, and a case fatality of 35% among all patients with IFIs, which is in line with findings from other studies.13 14 29 30 Notably, the 30-day all-cause mortality was 24%, indicating that nearly a third of patients died from or with infection after 30 days, consistent with Galmiche et al.11

Patients who developed an IFI were mainly older, with a mean age of 65 years at hospitalisation, which is similar to the age reported in other studies.1113,15 30 Results of the multivariable analysis of this study indicated that the hazard of developing an IFI increases by 32% for every 10-year increase in age. This finding aligns with age being recognised as a risk factor for serious infections in general.

In RABBIT, the frequency of males was higher in cases compared with the remaining cohort. The adjusted analysis supports the hypothesis that male sex may be a risk factor for developing an IFI. Data on sex differences reported in other studies are inconsistent, as some11 15 identified respective differences while others31,33 did not. However, as Egger et al34 reviewed, sex differences in the development of IFIs may be plausible due to genetic and hormonal influences on the antifungal immune response, as well as sex-specific health behaviours.

So far, most studies did not analyse the impact of smoking as a risk factor for IFI in patients with RA.11,1315 17 29 In this study, a high number of current or former smokers was found among cases. The multivariable Cox regression model also revealed a significant association between ever-smoking and the development of IFIs. Smoking may increase the IFI risk35 by causing immune dysregulation and tissue damage, while also contributing to comorbidity development like pulmonary fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy and major adverse cardiovascular events.36 This is consistent with the high comorbidity burden in this study.

As stated before, most patients with IFIs had multiple infection-risk associated comorbidities, while only a few had none. Generally, the frequency of comorbidities was higher than in other studies.11 14 17 30 The results of the time-to-event analysis are especially noteworthy as they underscore that comorbidities appear to play a major role in the development of IFIs. The association with pre-existing cardiovascular disease is particularly strong. Notably, other studies of patients with RA did not report on cardiovascular diseases. However, Webb et al37 reported similar frequencies of congestive heart failure in the general population with IFIs. Moreover, the link between the development of IFIs and chronic lung disease and other autoimmune diseases seems highly relevant. Xiong et al12 also identified an association between IFI occurrence and chronic lung disease in patients with connective tissue disease. Other studies15 18 33 that only investigated PJP have identified a similar association in patients with RA. The overlap to other autoimmune diseases was also reported by Galmiche et al,11 though it was more pronounced in the RABBIT cohort (online supplemental table S4-5). The considerable burden of comorbidities in this study suggests that multimorbidity, structural lung damage, cardiovascular disease and conditions that involve immune dysregulation—such as diabetes, malignancy and other autoimmune diseases—increases susceptibility to IFIs.

The association between IFIs and time-dependent disease activity (DAS28-CRP), physical function (FFbH) and the number of previous b/tsDMARDs is not statistically significant, potentially due to the limited statistical power. Nevertheless, effect sizes for disease activity and physical function are not marginal, suggesting that higher long-term disease activity may be relevant in the development of IFIs. Future research needs to elucidate if long-term disease activity only plays an indirect role by contributing to comorbidity development, or whether it also directly impairs antifungal responses through immune dysregulation caused by autoimmune disease.

Beside patient characteristics, RA treatment may contribute to the development of IFIs.8 Most studies reported that GCs are commonly used in patients with RA who develop an IFI.711,15 17 19 30 31 Consistent with these studies, 84% of patients in this study used GCs at the last visit prior to hospitalisation, with a mean prednisolone-equivalent dose of 8.6 mg/day.

In contrast to other studies,1217,19 we could not find an association between b/tsDMARD treatment in general and the development of IFIs. However, the limited number of events precluded an analysis of DMARDs by mode of action, as such an approach would require more advanced balancing techniques38 to account for channelling effects and other biases. Particularly, the increased HRs (>1) observed for RTX and abatacept in the univariable analyses (online supplemental table S7-1) may reflect channelling bias, as TNFi non-responders are more likely to receive second-line therapies, but they could also indicate a genuine effect on the risk of developing IFIs.

As expected, deceased patients with IFIs were older, had a longer disease duration, a slightly higher disease activity and more comorbidities. Whether women are truly at higher risk of death when infected, as the results suggest, or whether this finding arose by chance in a small group, remains to be determined in future studies. A seemingly paradoxical finding of this study is that deceased patients were less frequently current smokers compared with those who recovered. This may be explained by differences in age and comorbidity burden, as current smokers in this cohort were generally younger and had fewer comorbidities (online supplemental table S5-2).

This study underlines the difficulty of the IFI diagnosis. In this study, only 10% of all infections could be classified as ‘proven’ according to the EORTC/MSG guidelines; in three of seven, this was done postmortem. In addition, the most commonly used method for diagnosing fungal pneumonia was PCR analysis of lower respiratory tract specimens (online supplemental table S1-1). However, this method cannot differentiate between an active infection and colonisation, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty. Furthermore, in ten cases, the fungal genus was not identified, which reduced the precision of the case definition and limited the ability to attribute infections to specific pathogens.

The majority of patients did not have any comorbidity that was listed in the EORTC/MSG criteria as a host factor for aspergillosis, candidiasis and pneumocystosis. However, a limitation of our study is that in most cases, neutrophil and CD4+ counts related to the IFI were not available. Therefore, the proportion of patients meeting these host factors may have been underestimated. None of the patients had an allogeneic stem cell transplant, a solid organ transplant, an inherited severe immunodeficiency or acute graft-versus-host disease, and only a few had leukaemia or lymphoma. The study of IFI cases in the general population by Webb et al37 similarly reported low numbers of the conditions specified as host factors in the EORTC/MSG criteria but found similar frequencies of comorbidities that were seen in our study. This supports the idea of the EORTC/MSG criteria’s authors that host factors may need to be expanded, for example, to patients with structural lung damage, to be applicable to patients with other underlying diseases, including RA.

Similarly, the EORTC/MSG defines prolonged GC use as a host factor at ≥0.3 mg/kg for at least 3 weeks within the past 60 days.5 In contrast, only three IFI patients were given such high doses in the RABBIT cohort (online supplemental tables S4-1–S4-4). Our time-to-event analysis also supports the results of other studies,711,15 17 19 30 31 in which GC use is associated with IFI development even at lower doses. Moreover, the EORTC/MSG defines T-cell immunosuppressants as host factors for Aspergillus, Candida and Pneumocystis infections, and B-cell immunosuppressants additionally for Aspergillus.5 In this study, 42% of patients received one of the aforementioned DMARD treatments at the time of hospitalisation (online supplemental tables S4-1–4-4). However, it is uncertain which DMARDs increase susceptibility to IFIs, and it needs to be elucidated by future research.

The most common fungus in this RA cohort was P. jirovecii, consistent with Galmiche et al.11 Aspergillosis and candidiasis may also occur. The absence of other IFIs may be explained by endemic conditions but may also support the need for more advanced diagnostic methods.

This study has some limitations. Despite the considerable size of the RABBIT cohort and a higher number of IFIs compared with other studies, IFIs remain rare. The number of events is still insufficient for more advanced statistical methods, as required in observational data, for example, to investigate causal associations, including the effects of individual DMARD classes on the risk of developing IFIs. This limitation also prevented an adjusted analysis of factors associated with the infection outcome (recovered vs deceased) among IFI cases. In addition, the proportional hazards assumption appeared to be violated partly based on the Schoenfeld residuals. However, given the small number of events observed in over 20 years with irregular intervals between events, the residuals and associated smoothed coefficient paths are inevitably unstable.

Another limitation of this study is missing data. Although RABBIT has extensive monitoring processes to ensure a high quality for regular register data, hospital discharge letters are not standardised across hospitals and as such, lead to a higher number of missing data. This complicated the retrospective case validation and increased uncertainty in applying the EORTC/MSG diagnostic criteria, compared with studies involving direct patient assessment.

Further, a difference in the amount of missing data was observed between patients who recovered and those who died, as the latter often missed follow-ups before IFI diagnosis, had fewer hospital discharge letters and those available were often of lower quality. A further, non-quantifiable concern in our study is under-reporting due to the difficult diagnosis. Autopsy studies39 40 have found that 60–73% of invasive Aspergillus infections are diagnosed only postmortem. Together with the low autopsy rates in Germany,41 it is possible that IFIs might have remained undiagnosed.

Despite these limitations, RABBIT combines data from numerous German rheumatological care units and includes over 22 500 patients with RA, making it well-powered to detect rare outcomes. This study adds new knowledge to the work of Galmiche et al,11 Xiong et al12 and Webb et al37 by examining IFIs from a rheumatological perspective and providing monitored and curated data on vital status, comorbidities, smoking status, disease activity and treatment within a longitudinal cohort. In particular, it can help to extend the EORTC/MSG criteria by providing descriptions of comorbidities that have not been reported in such detail in other studies of patients with RA. Additionally, by comparing deceased and recovered patients, it can inform future studies to find factors associated with IFI outcomes, which are crucial to understand given the poor prognosis shown by the high case fatality. Lastly, this study adds evidence to existing research on PJP, primarily from Asia.1315,17 19 29 31 It also examines other invasive fungal diseases and provides data from a different geographical setting with different endemic conditions.

Conclusion

This study found that IFIs were rare yet life-threatening infections with a high case fatality in patients with RA, emphasising the need for early recognition. IFI development was associated with older age, male sex, ever-smoking, cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, other autoimmune disease and the use of GCs even at lower doses. The EORTC/MSG host factors were only partially observed in this population, but patients often had other comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases, structural lung damage, diabetes, chronic renal diseases, other autoimmune diseases and solid malignancies. These explorative results provide essential information about possible risk factors for the development of IFIs. They also suggest that, beyond the malignancy and transplant setting for which EORTC/MSG criteria were developed, an alternate set of host factors may be more appropriate for patients with RA. However, future studies are needed to draw causal conclusions.
