Association between long-term burden of sarcopenia and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity
Authors: Zuolin Lu, Yunyuan Kong, Weihao Shao, Jiawen Ke, Yachen Wang, Xiaoxia Wei, Yabing Hou, Ruitai Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia, the progressive loss of muscle mass and function, is a common condition in older adults and has been linked to both cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic respiratory diseases (CRD). However, the association between long-term changes of sarcopenia and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity remains underexplored. This study aims to investigate how changes in sarcopenia burden over time relate to cardiorespiratory multimorbidity in Chinese adults.MethodsData from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were used, including 5186 participants aged 45 and older. Sarcopenia was assessed using criteria for muscle mass, strength, and physical performance. Sarcopenia trajectories were identified using group-based trajectory modeling, and associations with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity (CVD and CRD) were assessed using multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models.ResultsThe study included 5186 participants with mean age of 58.2 ± 8.4 years. A total of 301 (5.8%) participants experienced cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Four distinct sarcopenia trajectory groups were identified: persistently low, moderate-to-low, low-to-high, and persistently high burden. Compared to the reference (persistently low group), the low-to-high trajectory of sarcopenia burden had the strongest association with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity (OR: 2.64, 95% CI: 1.73–4.04), followed by the persistently high group (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.46–2.89) and moderate-to-low group (OR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.38–2.60).ConclusionsChanges in sarcopenia burden are significantly associated with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, with a rapid increase in sarcopenia burden (low-to-high trajectory) being particularly detrimental. Monitoring sarcopenia progression and early intervention may be crucial in preventing cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Further research is needed to explore underlying mechanisms and evaluate interventions.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Cardiovascular disease, Chronic respiratory disease, Cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, Trajectory

Introduction

Cardiovascular and chronic respiratory disease (CRD) are the two leading causes of death worldwide [1]. Shared risk factors, such as smoking, ageing, poor diet, and physical inactivity, as well as related pathophysiological mechanisms, including oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, make the two frequently coexist [2,3]. Studies indicate that approximately 40% individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are comorbid with various cardiovascular disease (CVD), for example, coronary heart disease, heart failure, atria l fibrillation, and peripheral vascular disease [4]. In addition, cardiorespiratory multimorbidity is linked to worse health outcomes compared to either condition alone [5]. Patients with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity experience a poorer prognosis, including reduced quality of life, diminished exercise tolerance, and an increased risk of hospitalization [6]. Therefore, identifying key risk factors and mitigating their impact is crucial in preventing cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, reducing adverse health outcomes and improving life expectancy.

Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized skeletal muscle disorder characterized by loss of muscle mass, strength, and function [7]. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) defines sarcopenia based on three key criteria: low muscle strength (the primary criterion), low muscle mass and poor physical performance [7]. Sarcopenia is prevalent among the elderly with 5–13% in individuals aged 60–70 years and increased up to half in those over 80 years [8]. Sarcopenia has been linked to CVD through elevated inflammatory markers, insulin resistance, and reduced endothelial dysfunction [9]. In addition, sarcopenia is strongly associated with increased risk of CRD due to low-grade systemic inflammation, reduced physical activity, and malnutrition [10]. However, few studies have assessed the impact of sarcopenia and the risk of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Besides, the severity of sarcopenia tends to change over time [11]. For example, sarcopenia is commonly getting worse with aging but can also be reverted or relieved by effective interventions. Therefore, to further evaluate the association between the changes of sarcopenia burden and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity is crucial for optimizing the strategies of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity prevention and management.

The present study aims to identify the potential changes of sarcopenia status over time and further investigate their associations with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity among a large population-based cohort in China.

Methods

Study population

Participants were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which seeks to explore the social, economic, and health conditions of Chinese residents aged 45 and older [12,13]. The survey spans 450 villages, 150 counties, and 28 provinces, with four waves of data collection completed to date (Wave 1 in 2011, Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, and Wave 4 in 2018). Using probability proportional to size sampling, CHARLS randomly selected 150 counties from across the 28 provincial administrative units in China, resulting in a baseline sample of over 17,000 individuals. All survey interviewers were trained at Peking University by CHARLS staff and conducted interviews in strict accordance with the survey protocol, utilizing a Computer-Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) system. Further details about CHARLS can be found in previously published work, with data available for download at http://CHARLS.pku.edu.cn/en. The study protocol received approval from the ethics review committee at Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), Beijing, and all participants provided written informed consent.

In this study, we included 5896 participants aged 45 and older who had data available to define sarcopenia severity (including sex, age, height, weight, handgrip strength, walk speed, and chair stand test) across all three waves from 2011 to 2015. We excluded 710 participants with either pre-existing CVD or respiratory disease prior to 2015. As a result, the final analysis included 5186 participants to identify the potential trajectories of sarcopenia status and to examine their relationships with cardiorespiratory disease (Fig. 1).

Assessment of sarcopenia and its severity

Sarcopenia was defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia: 2019 Consensus Update on Sarcopenia Diagnosis and Treatment (AWGS2019) [14]. Possible sarcopenia is diagnosed when there is low muscle strength or low physical performance. Participants with age-related loss of muscle mass, plus low muscle strength and/or low physical performance are diagnosed as sarcopenia. Severe sarcopenia is specifically diagnosed when all three criteria are simultaneously met, whereas due to the limited statistical power, we did not further stratify this group into those with and without severe sarcopenia. The specific measurements and their respective cutoff values for the Asian population for each diagnostic component are detailed below.

The appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was calculated using a validated anthropometric equation among the Chinese population [15,16]:

The lowest 20% of the height-adjusted ASM (ASM/heightt2) of the study population was used as the cut-off point to define low muscle mass with <7.01 kg/m2 in men and <5.27 kg/m2 in women in 2011, <7.05 kg/m2 in men and <5.35 kg/m2 in women in 2013, and <7.05 kg/m2 in men and <5.35 kg/m2 in women in 2015 [[16], [17], [18]].

Low muscle strength is defined as handgrip strength <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women [14]. Handgrip strength in kg was measured in both hands by squeezing a dynamometer (Nantong Yuejian Physical Measurement Instrument Co., Ltd.) as hard as possible. Each individual was measured twice for both hands, and the average of the maximum available values was used.

Criteria for low physical performance are 2.5-m walk <1.0 m/s, or 5-time chair stand test ≥12 s [14]. Specifically, the chair stand test was assessed by requiring subjects to rise continuously from a chair five times while folding their arms across their chest. Those participants failing to perform the above tests were recorded as having low physical performance as well.

In our analysis, the severity of sarcopenia was scored as three levels, from non-sarcopenia (0), possible sarcopenia [1], to certain sarcopenia [2].

Assessment of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity

Multimorbidity is defined as the coexistence of two or more long-term conditions within the same individual, according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). In this study, cardiorespiratory multimorbidity was defined as the self-reported coexistence of at least one CVD and at least one CRD [4]. All available data on CVD and CRD were self-reported by the participants and collected from the 2020 follow-up survey. In CHARLS, CVD encompasses heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, and other heart conditions, as well as stroke. CRD refers to all chronic lung conditions, such as chronic bronchitis and emphysema (excluding tumors or cancer).

Assessment of covariates

Assessments of covariables were described in detail previously [11,17]. Standard questionnaires were used to record the data on the demographic information and prevalent comorbidities (self-reported) through face-to-face interviews. All the covariates included in the analysis were collected in 2011. A physical examination was conducted by trained staff for each participant. Blood pressure and pulse were measured three times while seated using a standard automated electronic device (Omron TMHEM-7112), with the average of the three readings being used for analysis. Weight and height were measured using calibrated equipment, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m²). Waist circumference was measured with a non-elastic tape. Participants’ physical activity was self-reported and categorized into three types: vigorous activity, moderate-intensity activity, and light activity/walking. For each type, participants reported the average frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day). Weekly metabolic equivalent (MET) values were then calculated by multiplying the standard MET value assigned to each activity by its reported duration and frequency, and summing across all activity types [19]. Venous blood samples were taken after an 8-h fasting period and stored at −80 °C until further analysis in the central laboratory. Fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and uric acid were assessed. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, current use of antihypertensive medication, or a self-reported history of hypertension. Dyslipidemia was identified as a total cholesterol level ≥240 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, current use of lipid-lowering medications, or a self-reported history of dyslipidemia in accordance with the 2016 Chinese Adult Dyslipidemia Prevention Guideline [20].

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables or numbers and percentages for categorical variables.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) [21] was used to determine clusters of participants who followed similar trajectories of sarcopenia burden over the given exposure period (from 2011 to 2015). The trajectories are allowed to vary between latent classes, and the number of latent classes and their sizes in the population are estimated from the data. In our analysis, sarcopenia score was considered as a dependent variable and time of each wave was considered as independent variable. Polynomials of time with up to cubic were used to identify the potential non-linearity of sarcopenia scores over time. The number of trajectory groups was limited to five. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was used to assess the fit of the model and choose the best model [21]. The model calculates a posterior probability of membership in each class for each participant. Each participant is assigned to the class for which his/her posterior probability is the highest. To ensure that all obtained classes are of clinically meaningful size, we imposed the condition that each class should include at least 5% of the participants, the relative entropy >0.8, and the average posterior probability >70% [21,22]. Then, class-specific predictions were calculated to plot the class-specific trajectory at each wave.

After determining the trajectories of sarcopenia score, Logistic regression models were used to estimate the associations between a certain trajectory group and the risk of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the trajectory groups, with the potentially most favorable trajectory as the reference. Models were adjusted for baseline age and sex (Model 1) and additionally for residential area, education, marital status, smoking status, status of alcohol intake, daily sleep duration, BMI, fasting blood glucose, uric acid, hypertension (yes/no), and dyslipidemia (yes/no) (Model 2). Subgroup analyses were performed according to age categories (<60 or ≥60), sex, residential area (urban or rural), marital status (married or other), education (middle school and lower or high school/vocational high school and above), alcohol intake (current or not), smoking status (current or not), sleep duration (<6 h, 6−8 h or >8 h), BMI (<18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30 or ≥30 kg/m2), prevalent hypertension (yes or no), and prevalent dyslipidemia (yes or no).

A complete case analysis was conducted in GBTM. Missing values for covariates in the Logistic models were imputed using the mean for each continuous variable and were assigned a missing indicator category for categorical variables. Sensitivity analysis was performed on participants with no missing values for any covariates, and the results were consistent with those obtained from the imputed data. All analyses were performed using STATA 15 (Stata Corp) and R software version 4.1.1. All P values were two‐sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The mean age of the 5186 participants was 58.2 ± 8.4 years, among whom the male accounted for 45.3%. The mean BMI was 23.8 ± 9.9 kg/m2. A total of 1171 (22.6%) participants had prevalent hypertension and 420 (8.1%) had prevalent dyslipidemia. During the study period from 2015 to 2020, 301 (5.8%) participants experienced cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Details of baseline covariates were shown in Table 1.

Trajectories of the burden of sarcopenia during 2011−2015

Potential trajectories of sarcopenia burden during the exposure period, from 2011 to 2015, were identified using latent class models. Four distinct trajectories were identified and are depicted in Fig. 2. Class 1 (Persistently low burden) included participants who consistently exhibited low sarcopenia scores throughout the follow-up period, indicating stable minimal risk. Class 2 (Low-to-high burden) comprised participants initially showing low sarcopenia scores, which increased significantly over time. Class 3 (Moderate-to-low burden) included those starting with moderate sarcopenia scores that declined over the study duration. Class 4 (Persistently high burden) represented participants with high sarcopenia scores maintained or worsening throughout the study. In this study, 43.1% of participants were assigned to Class 1, 7.6% to Class 2, 26.9% to Class 3, and 22.4% to Class 4.

Baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by sarcopenia trajectories, are presented in Table 2. In general, all baseline characteristics differed significantly across the sarcopenia trajectory groups (p-values < 0.001), reflecting substantial variation in demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Specifically, mean age varied significantly among groups, with the highest mean age (63.3 years) observed in the persistently high burden group. This group also had the highest proportion of females and rural residents. In contrast, the lowest proportion of married individuals was found in the persistently high trajectory group. Regarding lifestyle factors, smoking and alcohol consumption were both less common in the persistently high group. Clinically, hypertension was most prevalent in this group, while dyslipidemia was slightly more common in both the moderate-to-low and persistently high groups. The highest mean fasting glucose was observed in the persistently high group, whereas triglyceride levels were highest in the persistently low group. Uric acid levels were slightly lower in the persistently high group.

Discussion

The present study aimed to identify the longitudinal trajectories of sarcopenia burden within a large, population-based cohort in China and investigate their association with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Our findings revealed four distinct trajectories of sarcopenia burden during a 5-years observation period. We found that various trajectories were significantly associated with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, suggesting the importance of monitoring the long-term changes of the sarcopenia status for cardiorespiratory multimorbidity prevention and management. Of note, participants with a low-to-high burden of sarcopenia had the strongest association with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, even stronger than those with a persistently high burden of sarcopenia. This implies that a rapid deterioration in sarcopenia may serve as an important warning sign for cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Additionally, the weakest association between the moderate-to-low burden of sarcopenia and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity underscores that effective interventions for sarcopenia should be taken as early as possible to eliminate the residual risk of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity.

Sarcopenia, characterized by the loss of muscle mass, strength, and function, is a well-established geriatric syndrome associated with adverse health outcomes, including frailty, disability, and increased mortality [23]. Previous research has focused on associations between sarcopenia status at a single time point and health conditions [9,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]], but the present study adds to the literature by identifying the trajectory of sarcopenia over time, reflecting a long-term burden of sarcopenia. Our study identified four distinct trajectories of sarcopenia over a 5-year period [1]: persistently low burden [2], moderate-to-low burden [3], low-to-high burden, and [4] persistently high burden. This classification underscores the heterogeneous nature of sarcopenia progression and suggests that different individuals experience varying rates and patterns of muscle decline.

Among these trajectories, participants in the low-to-high burden group demonstrated the strongest association with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. This trajectory represents a rapid deterioration in sarcopenia over time, suggesting that a rapid decline in muscle function might serve as an important clinical alert for the onset of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that sarcopenia is not just a static condition but a dynamic process [11] that can evolve over time and may signal broader health risks. Rapid muscle decline, as seen in the low-to-high burden group, could reflect accelerated aging and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, or poor nutritional status [29], all of which contribute to the interaction between CVD and CRD and the development of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity [[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Interestingly, participants with a persistently high burden of sarcopenia showed a weaker association with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity than those with a low-to-high burden. This finding suggests that while individuals with persistently high sarcopenia may already be at high risk for cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, the acceleration of sarcopenia over time may represent an even more critical risk factor. The low-to-high trajectory might indicate a shift from a relatively stable phase of sarcopenia to a more pathological progression, which could overwhelm the body’s adaptive mechanisms, triggering the onset of multiple metabolic disorders [34], and further affecting the heart and lungs.

The relationship between sarcopenia and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity is complex and likely mediated by several interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. First, both conditions share common risk factors, including aging, physical inactivity, poor nutrition, and chronic low-grade inflammation [9,10]. These factors not only contribute to the loss of muscle mass and function but also impair cardiovascular and respiratory function, setting the stage for the development of multimorbidity. Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in both sarcopenia and cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), have been shown to promote muscle catabolism, thereby accelerating sarcopenia [35]. Simultaneously, these inflammatory markers also contribute to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness, increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases [30]. Moreover, chronic inflammation is thought to impair respiratory function by influencing airway remodeling and reducing lung compliance, thereby increasing the likelihood of respiratory conditions like COPD [33].

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have important clinical implications. Given the strong association between the low-to-high trajectory of sarcopenia and multimorbidity, targeted interventions aimed at slowing or reversing muscle decline could be pivotal in reducing the risk of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. The identification of a rapid deterioration in sarcopenia as a significant predictor of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity suggests that healthcare providers should be particularly vigilant in monitoring changes in muscle function over time, especially among older adults. In addition, the component-specific findings from our trajectory analysis—particularly the disproportionately rapid decline in muscle strength within the low-to-high sarcopenia trajectory—have important implications. While loss of muscle mass and reduced physical performance contribute to sarcopenia, our results indicate that muscle strength deterioration may be the most sensitive and actionable early indicator of accelerated progression. Because muscle strength is readily measurable, inexpensive, and strongly predictive of adverse outcomes [36], incorporating grip strength testing into routine health assessments could facilitate earlier identification of individuals at elevated risk for cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. On a broader scale, public health strategies that integrate muscle strength screening into community-based health programs and healthy aging initiatives could enable targeted, scalable interventions—such as progressive resistance training, adequate protein intake, and physical activity promotion [37]. These approaches have the potential not only to slow sarcopenia progression and maintain independence in older adults, but also to reduce the long-term burden of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases.

Limitations and future directions

While this study offers valuable insights into the trajectories of sarcopenia and their relationship with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our study relied on a population-based cohort from China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations, especially those with different ethnic, cultural, or socioeconomic backgrounds. Future research should examine sarcopenia trajectories and their associations with multimorbidity in diverse populations to determine whether these findings are consistent across different settings.

Second, the outcome measures in this study were all self-reported by participants, which may lead to recall bias. In addition, CVD or CRD were included as a group in the study, making it impossible to assess the effects of any specific diseases on the results.

Third, while we identified four distinct trajectories of sarcopenia burden, the study did not include detailed information on the specific mechanisms or factors driving these trajectories. Future studies should investigate the biological, behavioral, and environmental factors that contribute to the progression of sarcopenia.

Finally, it should be noted that due to the observational nature of our study, reverse causation cannot be completely ruled out. Although our findings demonstrate significant associations between trajectories of sarcopenia and the risk of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, it is possible that cardiorespiratory conditions could have contributed to accelerated sarcopenia progression. Therefore, causality should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies employing longer follow-up durations, repeated measurements, and ideally, prospective intervention designs are warranted to disentangle these bidirectional relationships more clearly.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that different trajectories of sarcopenia burden are significantly associated with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity, with the low-to-high burden group showing the strongest association. Rapid deterioration in sarcopenia may serve as a warning sign for the development of cardiorespiratory multimorbidity. Further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of sarcopenia progression and its dynamic interactions with cardiorespiratory multimorbidity.
