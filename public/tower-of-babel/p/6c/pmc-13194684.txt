Genetic diversity and phylogeography of Chimaera monstrosa (Linnaeus, 1758) in the Mediterranean Sea: insights from COI mitochondrial DNA analysis
Authors: Nuri Başusta, Naim Saglam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimaera monstrosa (Linnaeus, 1758) is a deep-water cartilaginous fish classified as Vulnerable globally and Near Threatened in the Mediterranean by the IUCN, yet its population genetic structure remains poorly characterised across much of its range. Here, we report the first mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) sequences of C. monstrosa from Turkish Mediterranean waters, obtained from five specimens collected as bycatch during commercial bottom trawling at 460–550 m depth in İskenderun Bay. These sequences were analysed together with 60 publicly available COI sequences from the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean using maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis and TCS haplotype network reconstruction. All Turkish specimens clustered within the Mediterranean clade and were distributed across three haplotypes (H1, H2, and H3), showing close affinity with samples from other Mediterranean localities. Across the full dataset of 65 sequences (596 bp), 30 haplotypes were identified, with high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.909) and low nucleotide diversity (π = 0.00544). AMOVA revealed strong and significant inter-basin structure (ΦST = 0.682, P < 0.001), with Mediterranean and Atlantic haplotypes forming non-overlapping regional clusters. Neutrality tests produced significantly negative Fu’s Fs (− 22.528, P < 0.001), yet the multimodal mismatch distribution does not support simple demographic expansion; these results are best treated as model-dependent summaries given the geographically structured nature of the dataset. The findings support genetic differentiation between Mediterranean and Atlantic populations of C. monstrosa and suggest that these populations merit independent consideration in future conservation assessments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45984-2.Keywords: Chimaera monstrosa, Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI), Haplotype network, Mediterranean–Atlantic differentiation, Molecular phylogeography, Population structureSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Molecular biology, Zoology

Introduction

Chimaeras (Holocephali), commonly referred to as “ghost sharks” or "rabbitfish," represent one of the most ancient lineages of jawed vertebrates, having diverged from their elasmobranch relatives over 400 million years ago. Like elasmobranchs, they possess a cartilaginous skeleton and practise internal fertilisation, yet they are distinguished by a single external gill opening — a feature more reminiscent of bony fishes than of sharks and rays. The order Chimaeriformes currently comprises around 50 described species distributed across three families, of which two genera — Hydrolagus and Chimaera — have been recorded in the Mediterranean Sea1–3. Chimaera monstrosa is a benthopelagic species that typically feeds on benthic invertebrates. Males bear a cephalic clasper (tenaculum) on the forehead, used to grasp the female during mating, in addition to the pelvic claspers characteristic of all male chondrichthyans. This species produces elongated, keratinous egg cases and is rarely taken by bottom trawls1.

C. monstrosa is reported by the IUCN to occur over a wide geographic area in the Northeast Atlantic, including the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the Mediterranean Sea, and the west coast of Africa. The species has been identified over an extensive depth range (200–1663 m) but is generally found at depths of 200–700 m4. This species is classified as Near Threatened in the Mediterranean5 and as Vulnerable globally by the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species4. C. monstrosa has been recorded at numerous localities across the Mediterranean basin, including the Aegean Sea, Sea of Marmara, Tyrrhenian Sea, Balearic Sea, and the Levantine coast6–13, as well as from Italian waters where it has been the subject of recent molecular studies14–16. Molecular and phylogeographic work has since begun to resolve the population structure of the species across the Atlantic–Mediterranean transition zone and within the basin itself15,17.

Characterising the genetic structure of C. monstrosa lineages is relevant both for understanding its evolutionary history and for informing conservation planning. Mitochondrial DNA, particularly the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene, has been widely used to assess phylogenetic relationships and genetic diversity in marine fishes15,18–21. Its combination of cross-species conservation and adequate interspecific divergence makes it well suited to resolving taxonomic ambiguities in difficult groups19,22. For Mediterranean chondrichthyans, large-scale initiatives such as the ELASMOMED DNA barcode reference library have demonstrated that COI data can resolve taxonomic issues, detecting cryptic diversity, and provide preliminary insights into phylogeographic patterns16.

Previous genetic work on C. monstrosa has revealed deep mitochondrial divergence between Atlantic and Mediterranean populations, with no shared haplotypes and negligible inferred gene flow across the Strait of Gibraltar17. Region-wide sampling has since identified distinct haplogroups and subtle structure within the Mediterranean basin15, suggesting that historical vicariance and depth-related ecological gradients have together shaped the evolutionary history of the species.

Despite progress in characterising C. monstrosa across the Mediterranean, sampling remains geographically uneven. Sequences from the Levantine Basin and the Turkish coasts are absent or poorly represented in published datasets15,16, leaving the eastern margins of the species’ range largely uncharacterized. This geographical gap is of particular concern given the increasing pressure from bycatch and habitat degradation on deep-sea communities throughout the region23,24. For deep-sea species such as C. monstrosa, logistical constraints — including low bycatch rates and restricted access to depth — have historically limited the accumulation of molecular data, making even small regional datasets a meaningful contribution to the comparative baseline23,25.

The present study provides an assessment of the genetic diversity of C. monstrosa in the eastern Mediterranean using the COI gene region. The sequences reported here constitute the first publicly available COI data for C. monstrosa from the Turkish Mediterranean coast and have been deposited in GenBank to expand the regional reference dataset. Rather than aiming to exhaustively reassess population structure at the basin scale, this work focuses on how specimens from Turkish waters integrate into the existing Atlantic-Mediterranean COI dataset and whether they conform to or deviate from previously reported haplogroup patterns15,16,25. The findings are discussed in the context of the biogeographic processes shaping chimaera distribution across the basin. In the Mediterranean, where anthropogenic pressures are intensifying across all depth zones, expanding the molecular baseline for deep-sea species such as C. monstrosa represents a necessary step toward evidence-based conservation planning24,26,27.

Materials and methods

Collection of specimens

Specimens of C. monstrosa (Fig. 1) were collected as bycatch during commercial bottom trawling operations at depths of 460–550 m in the vicinity of İskenderun Bay, Türkiye, on 10 July 2023 and 20 November 2025. Immediately upon capture, specimens were placed on ice to preserve tissue integrity prior to laboratory processing. All specimens used in this study were obtained as incidental bycatch during routine commercial bottom trawling, and no animals were captured specifically for research purposes. No experimental procedures or manipulations were performed on live animals. Sample collection complied with the relevant fisheries regulations applicable to the study area. The specimens were transported to the Fish Biology Laboratory, Faculty of Fisheries, Fırat University, for morphological examination. Total length was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a calibrated measuring board, and body weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 g. A small piece of white muscle tissue was excised from each specimen, transferred to a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube containing 70% ethanol, and stored at + 4 °C until DNA extraction. Only five specimens were available for analysis, as C. monstrosa is rarely taken as bycatch in commercial bottom trawls.

Chimaera monstrosa, caught from off Iskenderun Bay, eastern Mediterranean.

DNA isolation and amplification

Tissue samples were briefly blotted on sterile paper to remove excess moisture and rinsed with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate residual ethanol. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Tissue Kit (Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

A partial fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was amplified using two primer pairs applied sequentially across specimens. The universal pair LCO1490/HCO219828 was used for the first specimen (LCO1490: 5′-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′; HCO2198: 5′-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3′). For the four subsequently collected specimens, the fish-specific pair FishF2/FishR219 was employed instead (FishF2: 5′-TCGACTAATCATAAAGATATCGGCAC-3′; FishR2: 5′-ACTTCAGGGTGACCGAAGAATCAGAA-3′).

PCR amplifications were performed in a total volume of 25 µl, containing 1 × PCR buffer, 2.5 mM MgCl₂, 1.25 pmol of each primer, 50 µM dNTPs, 1 U Hot FIREPol Taq polymerase (Solis BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia), approximately 50 ng of template DNA, and nuclease-free water to volume. Thermal cycling comprised an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, annealing for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The annealing temperature was 51 °C for the LCO1490/HCO2198 primer pair and 52 °C for the FishF2/FishR2 pair, consistent with the original protocol described by Ward, et al.19; all other PCR conditions were identical for both primer pairs.

PCR products were resolved on a 1.5% agarose gel to confirm amplicon size and quality. Bands of the expected size were excised and purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Bidirectional sequencing was performed using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on an Applied Biosystems 3500xL Genetic Analyzer.

To contextualise the newly generated sequences within a broader geographical framework, published COI sequences of C. monstrosa were retrieved from GenBank and the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD System v5). Following a thorough check for duplicates and removal of redundant entries (see Table S1), 60 sequences of C. monstrosa were retained from public databases. Together with the newly generated sequences from Turkish waters, the C. monstrosa dataset comprised 65 sequences in total. Hydrolagus mirabilis (GenBank accession no. MW029477)29 was included as the outgroup, together with additional Chimaera species. As a result, the final dataset used for phylogenetic tree reconstruction and pairwise genetic distance analyses comprised 93 sequences and 552 bp (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis and genetic diversity analyses

Sequences were edited in BioEdit v7.2.530 and aligned using the ClustalW algorithm31 with default parameters. To verify the mitochondrial origin of the newly generated sequences and to screen for potential nuclear mitochondrial pseudogene copies (numts), all sequences were translated in silico using the NCBI ORF Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/) with the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code (Genetic Code 2); only sequences yielding an uninterrupted open reading frame free of premature stop codons and frameshift mutations were retained for subsequent analyses.

Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analysis was conducted in MEGA v1232. Prior to tree reconstruction, the substitution model was selected using the BIC and AICc criteria; the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano model33 with a discrete Gamma distribution (+ G, parameter = 0.5874, five rate categories) and a proportion of evolutionarily invariable sites ([+ I], 47.48%) provided the best fit for our dataset. The final alignment comprised 93 sequences of 552 bp after removing all positions with gaps or missing data (complete deletion option). Bootstrap support was calculated from 1,000 replicates, and initial trees were generated automatically using the Neighbour-Joining and BioNJ algorithms. The tree with the highest log-likelihood score (− 2480.26) is shown. Hydrolagus mirabilis (GenBank accession no. MW029477)29 served as the outgroup, both to root the tree and to flag potential misidentification among Chimaeriformes specimens. Codon positions included were 1st + 2nd + 3rd + non-coding sites, and branch lengths are shown in substitutions per site.

Haplotype network analysis

Haplotype relationships among C. monstrosa COI sequences were explored using a statistical parsimony network constructed with the TCS algorithm34 in PopART v1.735. The analysis was based on 65 sequences of 596 bp, comprising 60 publicly available sequences retrieved from GenBank and BOLD, together with the newly generated sequences from the Turkish Mediterranean coast. Haplotypes were colour-coded by geographic origin to facilitate interpretation of regional distribution patterns, with particular attention to the positioning of the Turkish specimens relative to previously described Mediterranean and Atlantic haplogroups 15,25.

Standard genetic diversity indices — haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (π), and the number of segregating sites (S) — were calculated for the complete dataset in DnaSP v636. Neutrality tests (Tajima’s D, Fu’s Fs, and Fu & Li’s D and F) were likewise computed in DnaSP v6 across the entire dataset to assess departures from neutral evolution and to evaluate past demographic changes. Given that only five of the 65 sequences were newly generated in this study, all indices are reported for the combined dataset and should be interpreted with appropriate caution; inferences drawn solely from the Turkish specimens are not statistically reliable given the limited sample size.

Branch diversity (Bd) and haplotype network branch diversity (HBd) were additionally calculated following Garcia, et al.37 to characterise network topology beyond standard diversity metrics. These indices combine genetic diversity (Hd) with a probabilistic measure of branch structure to enable quantitative comparisons of network complexity. Calculations were performed using the HapNetComplexity R script (https://github.com/ericgarciaresearch/Haplotype-network-branch-diversity_HBd)37 in RStudio Desktop v2026.01.1 + 40338 for Windows 10/11 (64-bit).

Results

Phylogenetic analysis

The five new sequences from the Turkish Mediterranean (GenBank accessions PP977571, PX945310–PX945313) grouped within the Mediterranean clade and showed close phylogenetic affinity with samples from Italy-Med and Israel (Fig. 2). Pairwise comparisons further indicated complete sequence identity at the COI locus with some Mediterranean samples.

Intraspecific genetic distances across Mediterranean and Atlantic datasets of C. monstrosa ranged from 0 to 0.017 (Table 1). Despite this narrow range, the phylogenetic tree recovered a clear separation between Mediterranean and Atlantic lineages: Atlantic specimens — from France-Atl, Portugal-Atl, Norway-Atl, Sweden-Atl, Ireland-Atl, and Denmark-Atl — formed distinct sub-clades with no overlap with the Mediterranean grouping.

Interspecific distances confirmed the taxonomic integrity of C. monstrosa relative to its congeners (Table 1). The closest species was C. owstoni (0.103–0.113), while the most divergent were C. argiloba (0.180–0.201) and C. phantasma (0.189–0.203). Notably low distances were observed between C. carophila and C. lignaria (0.036–0.045); the distance between C. monstrosa and C. lignaria ranged from 0.128 to 0.145.

Haplotype network and geographic distribution

The COI mtDNA haplotype network of C. monstrosa revealed two main phylogeographic clusters: one predominantly Mediterranean (H1–7, H16–30) and one predominantly Atlantic (H8–15). H3 is the most frequent haplotype in the Mediterranean, whereas H11 dominates in the Atlantic (Fig. 3). The five sequences from Türkiye were distributed among haplotypes H1 (n = 3), H2 (n = 1), and H3 (n = 1), all of which were nested within the Mediterranean cluster. Individuals from Türkiye (H1–3), Israel (H1), and Algeria-Med (H3–4) exhibit haplotypes that are also observed in Italy-Med samples (H1–3, H5–7, H16–30). Furthermore, the H3 lineage, which is present in Spain-Med, is also found in Turkish and Italian Mediterranean samples (Fig. 3).

North European populations show lower haplotype diversity than those in the Mediterranean region. These haplotypes are mainly found in Portugal-Atl (H11, 13, 14), France-Atl (H9, 10, 11), and Scandinavia (Norway-Atl: H8, 12; Sweden-Atl: H11, 12, 15; Denmark-Atl: H8; Ireland-Atl: H10, 12) (Fig. 3).

Genetic diversity and demographic history

Across the 596-bp alignment, 34 polymorphic sites were identified, corresponding to a total of 34 mutations (Eta), of which 23 were singletons. These sites defined 30 haplotypes distributed across five haplotype classes (nHc = 5). Haplotype diversity (Hd) was 0.909 ± 0.025 (variance = 0.00065), and nucleotide diversity (π) was 0.00544 (Table 2). Branch diversity (Bd = 0.7019) and haplotype network branch diversity (HBd = 0.6283) further characterise the complexity of the network topology (Table 2; Fig. S1).

Discussion

This study provides the first publicly available mitochondrial COI sequences of C. monstrosa from the Turkish Mediterranean coast, extending the empirical foundation for testing phylogeographic scenarios for this species against previously published data from the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean.

The phylogenetic analysis placed all Turkish sequences within the Mediterranean clade, where they displayed complete sequence identity or close affinity with samples from Italy-Med and Israel-Med. This placement indicates that the Turkish specimens belong to the broader Mediterranean assemblage rather than constituting a separate eastern Mediterranean lineage, consistent with previous evidence that the main phylogeographic division in C. monstrosa occurs between Mediterranean and Atlantic populations rather than among localities within the basin15. The uniformly low pairwise distances within the Mediterranean (Table 1) and the non-overlapping haplotype distributions between the two basins (Fig. 3) reinforce this view. Similar Atlantic–Mediterranean breaks have been reported across a range of marine taxa and are consistently associated with restricted connectivity across this transition zone39,40.

Although intraspecific COI distances were low overall, the AMOVA confirmed inter-basin structure (Table 2), reinforcing the view that low absolute mitochondrial divergence should not be equated with unrestricted gene flow. The available variation is geographically partitioned, with Mediterranean and Atlantic samples forming genetically separated units despite the conservative nature of the marker. Comparable patterns — modest sequence divergence coexisting with strong geographic structure — have been documented for other marine vertebrates41,42. Interspecific distances confirmed that C. owstoni is the closest congener examined (0.103–0.113), whereas C. argiloba (0.180–0.201) and C. phantasma (0.189–0.203) were the most divergent, providing a useful reference for contextualising intraspecific variation.

Several processes may have contributed to this basin-scale pattern. The Strait of Gibraltar is a well-documented partial barrier to dispersal for many marine organisms, capable of reducing connectivity sufficiently to promote lineage sorting and regional genetic differentiation without necessarily producing complete isolation39,40. Environmental contrasts between the two basins — differences in temperature, salinity, productivity, and hydrographic regime — may have reinforced this separation by influencing both dispersal dynamics and local persistence over time43,44. Historical climatic fluctuations have likely added a further layer of complexity: Pleistocene glacial cycles are known to have fragmented marine habitats, isolated populations in refugia, and intensified genetic drift across many taxa in the North Atlantic and Mediterranean45,46. Subsequent post-glacial and Holocene environmental changes may then have facilitated range expansion and partial recolonisation of suitable deep-water habitats47. The present-day mitochondrial structure of C. monstrosa may therefore reflect the combined legacy of historical isolation, climatic instability, and the persistent influence of oceanographic barriers.

Within the Mediterranean, the Turkish material does not reveal pronounced sub-regional differentiation at the COI locus. The close association of Turkish haplotypes with those from Italy-Med and Israel-Med suggests continuity with lineages already reported from neighbouring sectors of the basin. Whether this reflects shared ancestry, historical connectivity, or incomplete lineage sorting cannot be determined confidently from the present dataset.

The diversity indices calculated from the full dataset indicate substantial haplotypic variation (Table 2). Branch diversity metrics (Fig. 3 and Fig. S1) further indicated moderate network complexity relative to a star-like topology, consistent with a dataset composed of multiple regional haplotype assemblages. The combination of high haplotype diversity and low nucleotide diversity is a pattern frequently observed in populations that have accumulated several closely related lineages without substantial sequence divergence — a pattern often interpreted as compatible with relatively recent demographic processes or diversification among closely related haplotypes48. The neutrality tests also require cautious interpretation. Tajima’s D was negative but non-significant, while Fu’s Fs and Fu and Li’s D and F statistics deviated significantly from the neutral null model (Table 2). These results are broadly consistent with an excess of low-frequency variants, yet they do not point to a single unambiguous demographic explanation. The multimodal mismatch distribution does not support a sudden-expansion model in a single panmictic population; rather, in combination with the high ΦST value, it more plausibly reflects the superimposed signatures of population structure and demographic history across differentiated regional groups. Neutrality statistics should be interpreted descriptively rather than diagnostically, and single-scenario demographic inferences should be treated with caution.

More broadly, the genetic diversity and neutrality statistics reported here are model-dependent in a sense that deserves explicit acknowledgement. The standard tools of molecular population genetics were built largely within a Neutral Theory framework, and observed patterns are habitually attributed to mutation, drift, and demographic change. That framework has not gone unchallenged. Kreitman49 identified fundamental tensions between neutral predictions and empirical molecular data, and subsequent work has progressively narrowed the conditions under which neutrality can be assumed. Lynch, et al.50 showed that temporally fluctuating selection produces allele-frequency dynamics essentially indistinguishable from drift, directly complicating the interpretation of diversity indices. Song, et al.51 demonstrated that beneficial mutations are far more prevalent than the neutral theory requires, and that antagonistic pleiotropy across environments can sustain clock-like molecular evolution despite pervasive selection — a result with direct implications for demographic inference. Mitochondrial markers warrant particular caution: Lake, et al.52 documented strong purifying selection across the human mitochondrial genome at a scale that challenges assumptions of neutrality, and Horton, et al.53 showed that sequences previously dismissed as non-functional exert measurable effects on transcription factor binding and gene expression. Further inconsistencies between neutral predictions and empirical data have been described by Wu, et al.54 and Aguilar-Rodríguez, et al.55. The diversity and neutrality results presented in this study should therefore be read as descriptive summaries that characterise the dataset and frame hypotheses, not as definitive evidence for any particular evolutionary scenario.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgement. The Turkish dataset comprises only five specimens, all collected as bycatch during bottom trawl operations at depths of 460–550 m in İskenderun Bay. This small number does not reflect a deliberate sampling constraint, but rather the biological and logistical realities of working with a Near Threatened deep-water species whose bycatch is infrequent and unpredictable. Second, the study relies exclusively on the mitochondrial COI locus, which captures only maternal inheritance and provides limited resolution for evaluating fine-scale contemporary connectivity. Third, the broader comparative framework depends on sequences compiled from public repositories, which do not replace standardised sampling across the species’ full range.

Despite these constraints, the results remain relevant from both biogeographic and conservation perspectives. The consistent recovery of Mediterranean–Atlantic differentiation across phylogenetic, network, and AMOVA analyses indicates that these two basins should not be treated as a single management unit for C. monstrosa. Even when divergence at a mitochondrial marker is modest, persistent regional structure may reflect distinct evolutionary trajectories and different capacities to respond to future environmental pressures56,57. Future work should expand sampling across the eastern Mediterranean — including the Levantine Basin, Aegean, Ionian, and Adriatic Seas — and incorporate nuclear markers, SNP-based datasets, or broader genomic approaches to test whether the apparent Mediterranean coherence persists under higher-resolution analyses58. Wider geographic coverage would be particularly valuable for assessing whether subtle internal structure exists within the Mediterranean lineage and for distinguishing historical separation from ongoing demographic processes.

Conclusions

The COI sequences reported here fill a conspicuous gap in the molecular record of C. monstrosa by providing the first data from the Turkish Mediterranean coast. These specimens sit comfortably within the broader Mediterranean lineage and show no indication of a distinct eastern sub-lineage, yet the consistent recovery of strong Mediterranean–Atlantic differentiation across every analytical approach employed — phylogenetic reconstruction, haplotype networking, and AMOVA — argues that the two basins harbour functionally separate genetic units that merit independent consideration in future conservation assessments. At the same time, the small sample size, the reliance on a single mitochondrial locus, and the geographically uneven composition of the compiled dataset impose clear limits on the conclusions that can be drawn: the diversity and neutrality statistics presented here should be read as descriptive baselines rather than definitive demographic reconstructions. Resolving finer-scale structure within the Mediterranean and disentangling the relative contributions of historical isolation, oceanographic barriers, and contemporary gene flow will require broader spatial coverage — particularly from the Levantine Basin, Aegean, and Adriatic — combined with nuclear or genome-wide markers capable of capturing both sexes’ dispersal histories.
