Quantification of morphological, functional, and biochemical features of H9c2 rat cardiomyoblast retinoic acid differentiation
Authors: Nicole S York, Joel E Rivera, Mohammadreza Rahmani Manesh, K’sana Wood Lynes-Ford, Rory Smith, Leigh E Wicki-Stordeur, Laura T Arbour, Leigh Anne Swayne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell culture models enable advancement in our understanding of heart development and heart disease. The H9c2 rat ventricular cardiomyoblast cell line can be differentiated with retinoic acid and low serum, leading to morphological, molecular, and functional changes that partially resemble aspects of cardiomyoblast-to-cardiomyocyte differentiation. However, morphological, functional, and biochemical changes are rarely investigated in parallel, thereby limiting fulsome understanding of how these processes are interlinked, and to what extent these model cardiomyoblasts can be differentiated. To provide a parallel analysis as a resource for future studies, we therefore characterized H9c2 cell morphology, Ca2+ handling, and gene expression after five days (5 days-in-vitro, DIV5), and fourteen days (DIV14) of exposure to differentiation stimuli, consisting of retinoic acid and low serum. We observed statistically significant morphological changes during differentiation. We saw changes consistent with those already described in the context of cardiomyoblast differentiation. However, some of these were previously limited to qualitative observations, for example increased cell length. Notably, several of our morphological observations are completely novel, such as increases in eccentricity, perimeter length (aka cell boundary length), and in the density of actin clusters, were investigated de novo. Differentiation also resulted in the onset of spontaneous Ca2+ transients – this is the first instance, to our knowledge, that this has been characterized in the absence of pharmacological stimulation. The mean frequency and synchronicity of Ca2+ transients in differentiated H9c2 cells were much lower than those observed in primary cardiomyocytes, underscoring their relatively immature differentiation state from a functional perspective. Additionally, key cardiomyocyte cytoskeletal proteins and ion channel transcript and protein expression levels changed significantly with differentiation, including at early timepoints (DIV5 h and DIV3) which had not yet been investigated by others, in alignment with changes normally observed in cardiomyocyte development. Overall, our findings position differentiated H9c2 cells as a relatively high-throughput model for studying cardiomyoblast differentiation, while also clarifying their limitations in recapitulating fully mature cardiomyocyte phenotypes, and highlight reliable markers (e.g., Cacna1c, Myom2, cTnT, VCL, and Gja5) for experimental readouts.Keywords: H9c2, Differentiation, Retinoic acid, Morphology, Spontaneous Ca2+ transients

Introduction

The H9c2 cell line was first isolated from the lower half of a rat ventricle at embryonic day 13 [1]. Following selective passage to enrich for cardiomyoblasts, the cells ceased beating but maintained other key cardiomyoblast characteristics [1], such as their morphology, ultrastructure, and presence of specific ion channels [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The H9c2 cardiomyoblast cell line can be differentiated with low serum and retinoic acid to express some phenotypes linked to cardiomyocytes [3,5,6]. Early work showed that retinoic acid differentiation increased Cav1.2 (α1C)-based L-type Ca2+ channel expression [3], which are enriched in and important for generating the electrical and mechanical properties of cardiomyocytes [7], and cardiac troponins [5]. Several additional studies have investigated the morphological and biochemical aspects of differentiated H9c2 cells [5,6], but functional studies are rare [3,8]. Given the convenience of being an immortalized cell line, retinoic acid-differentiated H9c2 cells could be an attractive model to study aspects of cardiomyoblast biology, namely their development (Fig. 1). However, many previously described changes have not been quantified or have focused on only one or two aspects of cardiomyoblast biology at a time. To this end, we measured changes in H9c2 cell morphology, Ca2+ handling, and gene expression across retinoic acid differentiation to better enable their use as a model for cardiomyoblast biology in health and disease contexts.

Overview of morphological, functional, and molecular changes observed during H9c2 cell differentiation. Several morphological features, including increased cell area, length, eccentricity, multinucleation, and the emergence of actin clusters, increased with differentiation, particularly at DIV14 as illustrated by the model cell shape changes. Functional changes were marked by the onset of spontaneous Ca2+ transients at DIV5, represented by the lightning bolt symbol, with a rightward shift observed at DIV14. Molecular changes reflected altered gene and protein expression across differentiation, including established markers (GATA4, cTnT, Myom2, Cacna1c) and novel markers (Nkx2.5, ankyrin-B, VIM, Cx43). Shading denotes transcript/protein identity, and numbers indicate relative abundance.

H9c2 cell differentiation is associated with elongation of cell shape [3,[6], [7], [8]], but this has not been fully quantified [1,3,5,6]. Consequently, the amplitude and extent of changes in basic morphological properties associated with differentiation is unclear. To date, characterization of H9c2 cell intracellular Ca2+ handling has been limited to stimulation of undifferentiated cells [[8], [9], [10]], but the impact of differentiation on baseline H9c2 cell Ca2+ handling properties is unknown. H9c2 cell differentiation is associated with increased expression of genes encoding cardiomyocyte markers, such as cardiac troponins, and genes involved in Ca2+ handling and mitochondrial metabolism [5]. Although H9c2 cell differentiation-associated gene expression changes are better defined [5,6,8], they have rarely been compared with morphological and/or functional aspects in parallel. Furthermore, early changes (e.g., 5 h to 3 days), which could be driving various signaling processes, have not been thoroughly investigated. The amplitude and timing of gene expression changes relative to morphological and Ca2+ handling changes is rarely studied in parallel, making it difficult to identify possible connections.

To this end, we performed parallel characterization of morphology, Ca2+ handling, and gene expression changes across H9c2 cell retinoic acid differentiation, comparing mainly three time points, DIV0, DIV5, and DIV14. We used confocal and super-resolution microscopy, intracellular Ca2+ imaging, as well as RT-qPCR and Western blotting to assess these changes. We observed several morphological changes across differentiation, including increases in mean length, area, eccentricity, and multinucleation, and density of actin clusters. Differentiation resulted in the onset of spontaneous Ca2+ transients (i.e., without external stimulation), with subpopulation-level changes in transient attributes with increasing time in differentiation. We also observed multiple changes in gene expression consistent with cardiomyoblast differentiation, such as increased Myom2, Cacna1c, GATA4, and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) expression and characterized the expression of several novel genes including ankyrin-B (ANKB), connexin-43 (Gja1, Cx43), and Nkx2.5. We also included two earlier time points, DIV5 h and DIV3, to provide additional insight into the onset of differentiation and support improved characterization of early-stage changes. Finally, we compiled and summarized these changes according to DIV to enable optimal use of differentiated H9c2 cells in modeling aspects of cardiomyoblasts in health and disease contexts. Our study underscores the potential utility of differentiating H9c2 cells to model cell biological aspects of cardiomyoblast development at various stages of differentiation.

Results

Differentiated H9c2 cells are larger, multi-nucleated, and develop localized actin intensities

During cardiomyoblast differentiation, cells undergo changes in their overall shape that are key for their function [[11], [12], [13]]. While prior work commonly differentiated H9c2 cells up to DIV5 [5,6] more recent work has extended differentiation to DIV14 [8]; however, these earlier studies relied on qualitative analysis, or limited quantification of shape (particularly length, area, or multinucleation). We compared an array of gross morphological features, including eccentricity and perimeter, which, to our knowledge, had yet to be investigated by others, of undifferentiated (DIV0-), DIV5- and DIV14-differentiated H9c2 cells by analyzing confocal images of F-actin (phalloidin) and nuclei (Hoechst) (Fig. 2A) with a custom MATLAB script (available on GitHub, link in methods). Refer to Table 1 for statistical analyses. Average cell length was significantly greater at DIV14 compared to DIV0, with corresponding right shifts in the frequency distribution plot of cell length for both DIV5 and DIV14 cell populations (Fig. 2Bi, Ci). Cell area also significantly increased at DIV14 compared to both DIV0 and DIV5 cells, and the frequency distribution plot of cell area exhibited right shifts for both DIV5 and DIV14 cell populations (Fig. 2Bii, Cii). Cell eccentricity increased at DIV5 compared to DIV0, with no further change evident at DIV14 (Fig. 2Biii). Corresponding right shifts were evident in the frequency distribution plot of cell eccentricity for both DIV5 and DIV14 cell populations (Fig. 2Ciii). The proportion of multinucleated cells was also drastically increased at DIV14 compared to DIV0 (Fig. 2Biv, Civ). We found no significant changes in mean cell perimeter across differentiation, however the frequency distribution plot for cell perimeter did show some right shifting of DIV5 and DIV14 cell populations (Fig. S1). Together these data indicate that differentiated H9c2 cells are, on average, longer, larger, more eccentric (less circular), and more likely to be multinucleated than undifferentiated DIV0 cells. Moreover, these changes are more apparent at the DIV14 differentiation timepoint than at DIV5, suggesting that extending the differentiation time course further enhances cardiomyocyte-like phenotypic changes.

Notably, we observed that DIV14 differentiated H9c2 cells commonly exhibited localized actin intensities, or “clusters” of F-actin (phalloidin+) (Fig. 2Aiii) like those previously reported in other cell types [[14], [15], [16]]. We used stimulated emission depletion (STED) super-resolution microscopy to further resolve these F-actin clusters, which were circular to ellipsoid in nature (Fig. 2D). At DIV14, 61.8 % ± 5.1 of cells showed actin clusters compared to 10.8 % ± 2.0 of cells at DIV5 (unpaired t-test, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2Ei). Of the cells that exhibited actin clusters, the average number of clusters per cell increased between these time points (DIV5, 1.0 ± 0.1 clusters/cell vs. DIV14, 2.7 ± 0.3 clusters/cell; unpaired t-test, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2Eii).

Together these results suggest that retinoic acid differentiation increases cell length, cell area, eccentricity, and the proportion of multinucleated cells, and induces the formation of circular/elliptical F-actin clusters.

Differentiated H9c2 cells develop spontaneous Ca2+ transients

Intracellular Ca2+ handling changes dynamically across the development of cardiomyoblasts into cardiomyocytes [17,18]. Yet Ca2+ handling during H9c2 cell differentiation has not been thoroughly characterized, making it difficult to begin to relate changes in morphology and gene expression to functional outcomes. To this end, using live confocal imaging of Fluo4-AM [19] and previously published MATLAB scripts [20], we examined spontaneous Ca2+ transients in undifferentiated and differentiated H9c2 cells and compared DIV5 and DIV14 differentiated H9c2 cells with neonatal primary mouse cardiomyocytes. At DIV0 we did not observe readily detectable spontaneous Ca2+ transients (not shown) and therefore, DIV0 was not included in subsequent analyses. To determine the extent to which differentiation brings H9c2 cells towards cardiomyocytes' Ca2+ handling, all metrics were compared to those of primary cardiomyocytes. The mean transient (spike) amplitudes (change in fluorescence from baseline to peak) for all H9c2 cell differentiation groups were similar to those of primary cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3Bi, Table 2). However, as expected, and underscoring their relatively immature differentiation state, mean spike frequency (spikes per minute) and synchronicity (number of spikes occurring at the same time that exceed a threshold based off the mean intensity profile in all the cells in the field of view (FOV)) were significantly lower in all differentiated H9c2 cell conditions compared to primary cardiomyocytes isolated from neonatal heart (Fig. 3Bii,iii, Table 2). Distribution plots of frequency and synchronicity data displayed right shifts of the DIV14 population compared to DIV5, suggesting a further shift of DIV14 cells closer to the primary cardiomyocyte phenotype.

Together these results suggest that spontaneous Ca2+ transients develop with differentiation of H9c2 cells and are, somewhat surprisingly, comparable in amplitude to those of primary cardiomyocytes. Notably, while the longer differentiation timepoint (DIV14) does not overtly alter H9c2 cell Ca2+ transients compared to the DIV5 timepoint, there may be subpopulations of DIV14 cells that are shifted closer towards a primary cardiomyocyte-like phenotype. Nonetheless, DIV14 differentiated H9c2 cells remain relatively immature compared to primary cardiomyocytes from a functional perspective.

Differentiated H9c2 cells exhibit gene expression changes consistent with cardiomyoblast differentiation

In parallel to our assessment of changes in H9c2 cell morphology and Ca2+ handling associated with differentiation, we probed transcript (RT-qPCR) and protein (Western blotting) expression levels of genes involved in transcription, cytoskeleton, ion flux regulation, and signal transduction (see Table S1 for brief descriptions of functions and references). Many of these gene expression changes have been previously described in H9c2 cell differentiation (Rbp2, Tnnt2, Myom2, Pde4a [5]). We also looked at additional gene expression changes based on the cardiomyocyte literature (Nkx2.5, α-actinin (ACTN1)). For select transcript changes we confirmed protein expression with Western blotting (cTnT, GATA4, Nkx2.5, β-actin (ACTB), Cx43, vimentin (VIM), and in some cases used only used Western blotting (ACTN1, vinculin (VCL), ANKB). We used two types of comparisons. In the first approach, to identify increases or decreases in gene expression associated with H9c2 cell differentiation, we quantified the levels of select transcripts and proteins at DIV0, DIV5 h (5 hours), DIV3, DIV5, and DIV14 of retinoic acid differentiation. In the second approach we compared the extent to which the expression levels of select targets in differentiated H9c2 cells (DIV5 and DIV14) compare with those of primary cardiomyocytes. Summary of major changes and experimental recommendations

The use of H9c2 cells across different timepoints, DIV0–3, DIV5, and DIV14, may be useful for different studies depending on the research question. H9c2 cells at DIV0 represent the undifferentiated baseline, with smaller, mononucleated cells lacking spontaneous Ca2+ transients and with lower expression levels of several key cardiomyocyte genes (e.g., Myom2, Cacna1c). For detecting early changes to gene expression, timepoints before DIV5 may be of use (such as DIV5 h or DIV3). By DIV5, cells exhibit initial changes to cell eccentricity and trends towards cell shape changes, the emergence of spontaneous Ca2+ transients, and protein expression changes associated with mature cardiomyocytes. By DIV14, cells exhibit pronounced cardiomyocyte-like features, such as multinucleation, actin clusters, more complex Ca2+ handling, and gene profiles more closely resembling primary cardiomyocytes. H9c2 cells at DIV14 are likely the best candidate for morphological and functional outputs while DIV5 and earlier cells are beneficial for studying gene expression changes preceding the morphological changes.

Discussion

Our aim here was to characterize key morphological, functional, and molecular transitions across H9c2 differentiation, to position this model within a cardiomyoblast developmental spectrum (Fig. 1). Our findings are consistent with previous reports that H9c2 cells progress along a developmental trajectory with time in differentiation, but never fully recapitulate mature cardiomyocyte phenotypes in any of the three areas, and importantly, never develop spontaneous contractility, even at DIV14. Our parallel analyses support a context-specific use of H9c2 cells at various stages of retinoic acid differentiation for dissection of cardiomyoblast developmental signaling pathways in a high throughput manner.

Key morphological findings included increases in mean length, area, eccentricity, multinucleation, and density of actin clusters with retinoic acid differentiation of H9c2 cells. To our knowledge, we are also the first to report and characterize the onset of spontaneous Ca2+ transients with H9c2 cell differentiation. Additionally, we quantified gene expression levels of key cardiomyogenesis and/or cardiomyocyte proteins not previously assessed in the context of H9c2 cell differentiation (see Table 3, Table 4 for details of all gene expression changes and relevant citations), including ANKB, Cx43, and Nkx2.5. We also included two early analysis time points, DIV5 h and DIV3, enabling identification of early-stage changes. To facilitate implementation of our findings we also provide a helpful summary table and diagram (Fig. 1). This new information will support the optimal use of differentiated H9c2 cells to advance understanding of the mechanisms underlying cardiomyoblast differentiation with health and disease applications.

In addition to previously reported parameters such as length, area, and multinucleation, we also quantified perimeter, eccentricity, and actin cluster density. Increases in length, area, multinucleation, and density of actin clusters were most pronounced at DIV14 (Fig. 2), suggesting that DIV14 may be most useful for studying morphology-related aspects of cardiomyoblast development. Early gene expression changes related to cytoskeletal organization also supports this. Increased expression of Cacna1c, which encodes for the L-type Ca2+ channel pore, not only initiates Ca2+ signaling but also supports downstream Ca2+-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling pathways. Modulation of proteins like VCL, ACTN1, VIM, and ANKB, likely contribute to the increases in cell length, area, and eccentricity observed across differentiation. These structural changes are further accompanied by the emergence of actin clusters, revealed by super-resolution microscopy (Fig. 2 D, E), which became significantly more prevalent at DIV14. Changes in actin are key for the development of cardiomyocyte cell shape and sarcomere formation [[21], [22], [23]] and while the role of actin clusters has not been thoroughly characterized in cardiomyoblasts and/or cardiomyocytes, they are known to play important roles in regulating actin dynamics in other cell types [[24], [25], [26]]. The actin clusters observed here could represent the beginnings of key cardiomyocyte-like structures such as focal adhesions, sarcomeric precursors, or stress-related cytoskeletal features. Although we did not co-label with markers such as VCL, ACTN1, or Z-disc proteins in this study, this highlights an area for future work to classify these clusters and determine their role in cardiomyoblast development.

Spontaneous Ca2+ transients first appeared at DIV5 (Fig. 3), aligning with early increases in Ca2+-flux associated gene expression, such as Cacna1c, the gene encoding for the pore of the L-type Ca2+ channel, a key functional channel in developing cardiomyoblasts [3] as well as in mature cardiomyocytes in health and disease contexts [[27], [28], [29]]. Gene expression changes associated with differentiation are modulated by changes in internal Ca2+ levels, as well as direct transcriptional regulation by the L-type Ca2+ channel C-terminal proteolytic fragment [30,31]. Thus, the emergence of these transients may serve to influence several aspects of H9c2 cell retinoic acid differentiation [18,32,33]. Although largely-speaking, the Ca2+ transient measures of differentiated H9c2 cells were significantly different than those of primary cardiomyocytes, we observed subtle shifts in population distribution at DIV14 towards values observed in primary cardiomyocytes. Additional assays could serve to provide further precision with regards to the assessment of the functional maturation state of differentiated H9c2 cells. For example, insight into metabolic activity through measurement of ATP content, or visualization of the organization of sarcomeric markers such as ACTN1 or myomesin-2 could be context-dependent approaches, depending on the intended use of this model.

Notably, while the changes in transcript and protein expression levels are often correlated, this was not always the case. The transcripts for Gata4, Nkx2.5, Tnnt2, Vim, and Gja1 did not significantly change, whereas the levels of the proteins they encode did change, possibly due to post-transcriptional regulation, protein turnover, or other regulatory mechanisms [34]. Overall, our transcript expression level data were largely consistent with previous reports [5,8], such as the upregulation of Myom2 and Cacna1c and downregulation of Gja5. However, some genes such as Tnnt2, Actb, and Vim showed no significant change at the transcript level in our dataset. The protein levels of cTnT (encoded by Tnnt2) did increase, and the levels of VIM decreased, matching the direction of previously reported transcript level changes [5,8], suggesting some coherence in the expression changes of these key cardiomyocyte-associated genes, albeit at the translational level. Among the markers we examined here, previous studies also reported statistically significant increases in the transcript levels of Myom2 and Cacna1c, and in the protein levels of cTnT and VCL [5,6,8]. Similarly, others have also reported a statistically significant decrease in the transcript levels of Gja5 [8]. The consistency between these findings from different labs, suggests that these targets (Myom2, Cacna1c, cTnT, VCL, Gja5) could be reliable for assessment of the impact(s) of environmental or genetic intervention on H9c2 cell differentiation. Analysis of early differentiation timepoints, DIV5 h and DIV3, revealed additional new findings. Two transcripts related to ion and metabolite flux decreased early: Nkx2.5 at DIV5 h and Gja5 at DIV3 potentially suggesting an early regulation of signaling in immature cells towards differentiation, as the levels of these factors are known to be regulated throughout the cardiomyoblast development [35,36]. At the protein level, VCL increased at DIV5 h and DIV3, consistent with its key role in focal adhesion formation and stabilization, which serve as mechanosensors and signaling hubs driving cardiomyocyte differentiation [16]. On the other hand, ACTN1, VIM, and Cx43 decreased at DIV3. The reduction of VIM is expected given its association with mesenchymal cells and low abundance in mature cardiomyocytes [37], whereas the decline in ACTN1 and Cx43 may potentially reflect remodeling of cytoskeletal and junctional networks and could be of interest to follow up with microscopy studies. Notably, ANKB increased at DIV3, and further increased at DIV5. ANKB acts as a scaffold for ion channels and receptors [38,39], linking them to the cytoskeleton [40,41], and also plays other key structural and signaling roles in mature cardiomyocytes [42,43]. The increase in ANKB protein expression with time in differentiation together with the clinical associations of the ANKB p.S646F variant with cardiomyopathy [44,45], raises the possibility that ANKB could play an important role during cardiomyoblast differentiation. Other newly quantified transcripts and proteins (e.g., Myh2, Nkx2.5, Piezo1, ACTN1, Cx43) further expand on prior datasets, offering new insights into early molecular changes in H9c2 differentiation.

Thus, as outlined above, while DIV14 differentiated H9c2 cells exhibit more mature cardiomyoblast morphology, multinucleation, actin clustering, and spontaneous Ca2+ transients than observed at DIV5, they remain relatively functionally immature, exhibiting lower Ca2+ spike frequency, reduced synchronicity, and gene expression profiles than primary cardiomyocytes, underscoring that even 14 days of retinoic acid exposure is not able to fully differentiate H9c2 cells into cardiomyocytes. Despite this, our parallel assessment of differentiated H9c2 cell morphology, function, and gene expression underscores its relevance as a model system for cardiomyoblast development and disease, and provides a framework for their context-specific use.

Limitations and future directions

It is worth noting some of experimental limitations and potential uses for those considering using the H9c2 cell line. One of the challenges that arose during segmentation for morphological analysis was the close proximity of the highly confluent cells; however, this is difficult to address as appropriate cell density is a key for proper modeling of cardiomyoblast development [46,47]. To this end, we adopted an approach that identified cells with clear boundaries, followed by random selection of three for further measurement. While this strategy allows accurate segmentation, it could bias selection towards relatively undifferentiated cells that are more likely to be separate from neighbors based on our observations. For consistency, assessment of spontaneous Ca2+ transients at different DIV, imaging must be done in living cells initially plated on the same day, thereby precluding same-day comparisons between groups. An approach to account for potential differences associated with imaging groups on different days, could be to pharmacologically stimulate a maximal Ca2+ spike to normalize amplitudes. Additionally, we coated the glass required for imaging with fibronectin, which can modulate integrin signaling and may therefore produce subtle differences in gene expression [[48], [49], [50]]. Primary cardiomyocytes were generated from mixed sex neonatal hearts, whereas H9c2 cells were originally isolated from a female left ventricle. Moving forward, the use of single-heart cultures could enable sex-based analyses. Our heatmap highlights relative differences in gene expression levels between H9c2 cells at DIV5 and DIV14 and primary cardiomyocytes (Fig. S2). While additional statistical approaches could help to tease out more nuanced information, and some changes are consistent with a more mature and/or differentiated cardiomyoblast phenotype, it is ultimately clear that differentiated H9c2 cells are not phenotypically equivalent to mature cardiomyocytes. In addition to their general relative ease of use, a key notable potential benefit is the high DNA plasmid transfection efficiency (Fig. S3). This accessibility for manipulating gene expression underscores the potential to facilitate relatively high throughput screening of relevant genetic variants within the context of a cardiomyoblast model.

Conclusions

The H9c2 rat ventricular cardiomyoblast cell line has long been used to model aspects of cardiomyoblast and/or cardiomyocyte biology in health and disease contexts. Here we quantified changes in three broad areas of H9c2 cell biology across five and fourteen days of retinoic acid differentiation: morphology, spontaneous Ca2+ transients, and gene expression of select targets. As summarized in Fig. 1 we observed changes in morphology (see Table 1), Ca2+ transients (see Table 2), and gene expression changes (see Table 3, Table 4), consistent with those previously described, or in some cases for the first time reported. Our results position differentiated H9c2 cells as a relatively high-throughput model useful for investigation of cardiomyoblast morphology, Ca2+ and gene expression changes implicated in cardiomyoblast biology in health and disease contexts.

Materials and methods

H9c2 cell culture

The H9c2 rat ventricular-derived cardiomyoblast cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-1446). H9c2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, ATCC, 30–2002) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher, 12483020, reserve 10498874, lot 2240764RP), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, 15140122) at 37 °C in 5 % CO2. Cells were kept at or below 15 passages and passaged at 70–80 % confluence to prevent the loss of differential potential. Each vial was tested for mycoplasma using the universal mycoplasma detection kit (ATCC, 30-1012 K).

H9c2 cells were differentiated as previously described [3]. Briefly, cells to be collected at DIV5 were seeded at 8,333 cells/cm2 for RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and live cell Ca2+ imaging experiments, and 8,000 cells/cm2 for immunocytochemistry experiments. For cells collected at DIV14, plating cell density was halved to prevent cells from becoming confluent and detaching during the prolonged differentiation period. One day after seeding, FBS was decreased from 10 % to 1 % and media was supplemented with 1 μM all-trans retinoic acid (Sigma, R2625-50MG). All-trans retinoic acid (referred to elsewhere as retinoic acid) was reconstituted and serially diluted in DMSO (Millipore Sigma, D2438-5X10ML) to 1 mM, diluted in differentiation media to 50 μM, and then added to cells at the final concentration of 1 μM. Retinoic acid was added daily, in the dark, and cells were collected prior to treatment (DIV0), at 5 h (DIV5 h), 3 days (DIV3), 5 days (DIV5), and 14 days (DIV14) post-treatment. Sample size was chosen based from the resource Eq. [51].

One day after seeding, cells were transfected with a tetracycline inducible pCMB6-AC-GFP-rtTA plasmid (Origene, PS100125) using jetPEI (Fig. S3). 4 μL of jetPEI diluted in 150 mM NaCl was added to 2 μg of plasmid DNA also diluted in 150 mM NaCl, the mixture was incubated for 15 min and then added dropwise to the cells in DMEM containing no antibiotics. One day after transfection, media was changed to DMEM containing 1 % FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and was treated daily with 1 μM retinoic acid. To induce GFP expression, 10 ng/mL doxycycline hyclate (Sigma, D9891-1G) was added to the differentiation media.

Experimental animals

All animal procedures were approved by the University of Victoria Animal Care Committee and performed in accordance with the guidelines set by the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Male and female embryonic day (E)15.5– postnatal day (P)2 mice were used in this study. C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (#000664). Mice were housed under a 12/12-h light/dark cycle starting at 8 A.M., with food and water ad libitum; temperature was maintained between 20 °C and 25 °C and humidity at 40–65 %. Sample size was chosen based from the resource Eq. [51].

Heart tissue extraction

Embryonic day 15.5 (E15.5) and postnatal day 2 (P2) heart tissue was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. E15.5 heart tissue intended for RNA analysis was stored in RNAlater Stabilization Solution (Thermo Fisher, AM7020). Heart tissue was homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer on ice with either 100 μL TRIzol (Thermo Fisher, 15596026) per 5–10 mg tissue for RNA, or RIPA lysis buffer (9.1 mM PBS, 150 mM NaCl, 1.7 mM monobasic sodium phosphate, 9.1 mmol/L dibasic sodium phosphate, 1 % Igepal, 0.5 % w/v sodium deoxycholate, 0.1 % sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), 10 μL/mL protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, P8340-5ML)) for protein. RNA was extracted from tissue samples after incubating on ice for 5 min and then stored in TRIzol at −80 °C. Protein lysates were pushed through a 27 ½ gauge needle twice and incubated on ice for 30 min; samples were then centrifuged at 13500 xg for 20 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was also frozen at −80 °C.

Primary culture isolation

Mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated according to standard methods previously described [52]. 6–10 hearts, mixed sex, were used per each given culture, representing 1 biological replicate (N). Briefly, hearts were extracted from P1-P2 mice and transferred into phosphate-buffered saline solution (without Ca2+ or Mg2+) containing 20 mM 2,3-Butanedione monoxime (BDM) (Sigma, B0753). In a sterile cell culture hood, the hearts were washed, minced, and submerged in isolation medium consisting of Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) with 20 mM BDM and 0.025 % Trypsin (Gibco, 15090–046) for incubation overnight under gentle agitation at 4 °C. After incubation, the isolation medium was removed, and the tissue was oxygenated in Leibovitz's L-15 medium containing 10 mM BDM and 0.75 mg/mL of Collagenase/Dispase® mixture (Roche, 10269638001), then placed in a shaking incubator for 30 min at 37 °C. To release cells into suspension, the tissue fragments were gently triturated and filtered through a 100 μm nylon mesh cell strainer. Cells were centrifugated at 200 x g for 5 min to remove fibroblasts and endothelial cells. The resulting pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5 % Heat Inactivated Horse Serum (Thermo Fisher, 26050070) and plated in a cell culture dish (a pre-plating step that removes many of the remaining fibroblasts and endothelial cells). After a 2–3 h incubation, non-adherent cardiomyocytes were collected and plated on pre-coated collagen coverslips (Neuvitro, NEU-GG-12-1.5-COLLAGEN) or onto collagen from calf skin (Fisher, ICN16008410) coated 35 mm glass bottom imaging dishes at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per cm2. The following day, plating medium was replaced by DMEM containing 5 % HIHS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin for maintenance.

Immunocytochemistry

H9c2 cells were seeded onto fibronectin coated coverslips either purchased from Neuvitro (NEU-GG-12-1.5-FIBRONECTIN) or coated in lab using fibronectin bovine plasma (Sigma, F1141-1MG). Coverslips were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 5 min at DIV0, DIV5, and DIV14, followed by three washes with PBS containing Ca2+ or Mg2+. Cells were permeabilized with 0.1 % Tween-20 for 5 min, washed three times in PBS as before, blocked with 1 % BSA and 22.52 mg/mL glycine in PBS-T, and then were incubated with Hoechst (1:500) and phalloidin (1:500; 555, Invitrogen, A34055; Acti-Stain 670, Cytoskeleton, PHDN1) diluted in 1 % BSA in PBS overnight at room temperature for 1 h. Following secondary incubation, coverslips were washed three additional times, and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, P10144).

For transfected cells, fixation with 4 % paraformaldehyde was performed as previously described, followed by three washes in dPBS and mounting with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant.

Microscopy

Cells were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with HyD detectors. For DIV0 versus DIV5 comparisons, imaging was performed using a 40× oil-immersion objective set at 0.75 zoom (1.30 NA, pinhole = 65.28 μm, 3904 × 3904 (pixel size = 74.46 × 74.46 nm)). For DIV5 versus DIV14 cells, images were acquired using the 20× dry-objective (0.7 NA, pinhole = 60.69 μm, 3800 × 3800 (pixel size =148.92 nm × 148.92 nm)).

For transfected cells, images were acquired using the 40× oil-immersion objective set at 0.75 zoom (1.30 NA, pinhole = 65.08 μm, 2048 × 2048 (pixel size = 141.98 nm × 141.98 nm)). GFP was excited using the 488 nm Argon laser at 10 % intensity.

STED images were acquired using a 100× oil-immersion objective (1.40 NA, pinhole = 117.5 μm, pixel size optimized to 20 μm × 20 μm) at a zoom of 2. Phalloidin 555 was excited using the 514 Argon laser at 20 % intensity. Fluorescence was captured using the HyD detector using the 660 STED laser at 25 % 3D, 80 % intensity and 70–90 % power.

For all images presented, brightness and contrast were uniformly adjusted for presentation purposes.

Morphology analysis

To analyze morphological features of H9c2 cells, a custom MATLAB script was created: Morphology Analysis Overview A (code available at https://github.com/SwayneLab/Quantification-of-H9c2-cell-differentiation-/). Images were blinded before analysis. First, the analysis region for each cell was marked by manually drawing a polygon around it. The script processed a binarized image where cell pixels are set to one and background pixels to zero. Zeros were padded around the image to ensure accurate boundary detection at the edges. The cell boundary was identified by examining each pixel and its eight surrounding neighbors in a three x three grid, with boundary pixels identified whenever a surrounding pixel was zero. Next, a covariance matrix was created from these repositioned coordinates. An eigenvalue decomposition was performed on this matrix to find the principal directions in which the cell extended. These principal directions correspond to the longest (major) and shortest (minor) dimensions of an ellipse that could fit around the cell.

Length, area, and eccentricity were calculated by first finding the center point, or centroid, of each cell, averaging the positions of all the pixels that made up the cell. This average point helped reposition the cell's pixel distribution to the center of the coordinate system for easier analysis. Length was calculated from the square root of the largest eigenvalue (major axis) and eccentricity was calculated from the ratio of largest eigenvalue (major axis) to smallest eigenvalue (minor axis). The perimeter was calculated by counting the number of boundary pixels, and area was calculated from filling in pixel area between the boundary pixels. The number of nuclei per analyzed cell was counted manually. All plots and statistics were performed in Prism 10 for Mac OS X (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Ca2+ imaging and analysis

Isolated mouse cardiomyocytes seeded in collagen-coated 35 mm glass bottom imaging dishes (ibidi, 81218–200) were loaded with cell-permeant 4 μM Fluo-4 AM (Invitrogen, F-14201) for 35 min. H9c2 cells seeded in fibronectin-coated imaging dishes (ibidi, 81218–200) were loaded with 1.5 μM Fluo-4 AM at room temperature for 25 min. Incubation time, temperature, and loading concentrations were optimized for each cell type to prevent dye toxicity and compartmentalization. The cells were subsequently washed using HBSS with 10 mM HEPES (Stem Cell Technologies, 37150) and incubated for 20 min to allow for de-esterification of the AM esters. All samples were imaged in complete FluoroBrite™ DMEM media (Gibco™, A1896701) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in a Tokai Hit Stage Top Incubation System mounted on the Leica SP8. Fluo-4 was excited with the 488 nm argon laser line at 20 % intensity through a 20× objective (0.70 NA, pinhole =242.65 μm). Using the Leica SP8 resonant scanner, cardiomyocytes were imaged at 27.7 FPS for 1 min and H9c2 cells were imaged at 14.6 FPS for 2 min. A minimum of 2 dishes were imaged per biological replicate with 2–5 fields of view captured per dish.

Imaging files were initially processed by EZcalcium, an open-source, automated data analysis package [53]. Part of the MATLAB-based toolbox, the automated ROI detection module was run first to detect all active cells, followed by the ROI refinement module to semi-automatically eliminate dying cells and those with a poor signal/noise. Minor movement artefacts in some FOV were not deemed significant by the motion correction module. For quantification of the extracted data files, a series of MATLAB scripts based on those published [20] were employed. All data were analyzed and plotted in Prism 10 for Mac OS X (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software, Inc.), frequency distributions were created in R (version 4.4.0).

RNA extraction

RNA was isolated using TRIzol as in [54]. Briefly, RNA from H9c2 cells or primary cardiomyocytes was collected by adding 0.4 mL TRIzol per 1 × 105-1 × 107 cells. Extracted RNA was stored at −80 °C.

RNA integrity was checked using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Agilent, 5067–1511). Briefly, to minimize secondary structure, 2 μL RNA was denatured at 70 °C for 2 min. 1 μL was loaded into the primed chip containing the gel-dye mix and RNA 6000 nano marker. Once loaded, the chip was vortexed at 2400 rpm for 60 s in the IKA vortex mixer, loaded into the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer, and the total RNA (eukaryotic) run was started within 5 min. The RNA integrity number (RIN) scores were above 8.8 for H9c2 samples, 7.6 for P2 heart tissue and primary cardiomyocytes, and 1.5 for the E15.5 sample which showed both the 18S and 28S bands.

RT-qPCR

One thousand ng of RNA underwent treatment with DNase1 (Thermo Fisher, EN0521) to digest any potential double-stranded DNA isolated with the RNA. cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of the DNase1 treated RNA using the Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit using random primers (Thermo Fisher, K1652). For each RT+ reaction, an RT- reaction was also run. A GAPDH PCR reaction was run to confirm the cDNA synthesis and validate the RT- controls on a 2 % agarose gel. qPCR reactions for genes of interest were performed on a QIAquant 96 well 5 plex qPCR machine (Qiagen, 9003010). Using PrimeTime qPCR probe assays (IDT) and PrimeTime gene expression master mix (IDT, 1055772), 100 μg of RNA was run in 10 μL reactions. The probe assays used are listed in Table S2. Standard cycling conditions were run: polymerase activation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. RT- qPCR data was analyzed using geNorm to identify the most suitable normalization strategy [55]. Reference genes (Ppia, Polr2a, and Rplp0) were selected based on a prior study, which identified these as stable candidates for gene expression normalization in cardiac tissues [56]. geNorm analysis confirmed their stability across time points. Target gene expression was then normalized using the geometric mean of two reference genes, Polr2a and Ppia. Graphs were created in Prism 10 for Mac OS X (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Western blotting

Adherent cells were washed 2 times in dPBS and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (9.1 mM PBS, 150 mM NaCl, 1.7 mM monobasic sodium phosphate, 9.1 mmol/L dibasic sodium phosphate, 1 % Igepal, 0.5 % w/v sodium deoxycholate, 0.1 % sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), 10 μL/mL protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, P8340-5ML)) by scraping cells off the plate or coverslip on ice. Cell lysates were then treated as described for the mouse heart tissue. Protein content was determined using the DC protein assay (Bio-rad, 5000112). 10 μg of protein was incubated in Laemmli sample buffer (1×: 0.08 mM bromophenol blue, 0.05 M Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 0.058 M SDS, 3.33 % v/v glycerol) at room temperature under reducing conditions (5 % dithiothreitol (DTT) and 5 % β-mercaptoethanol), separated by SDS-PAGE on TGX-stain free gels (Bio-rad, 4561094) and transferred to either a 0.2 μm pore PVDF membrane (Bio-rad, 1620177) for peroxidase detection using the Syngene G:Box imager or a 0.45 μm pore LF-PVDF membrane (Sigma, IPFL00010) for fluorescent detection using the Licor Odyssey CLx Imager. For Western blots using the peroxidase-based detection, the membrane was blocked with 5 % fat free instant skim milk in TBS-T, then incubated with indicated antibodies in the blocking solution, and protein bands were visualized using the Clarity Western enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad, 1705061). For Western blots using the fluorescent-based detection, the membrane was blocked with intercept (TBS) blocking buffer (Li-cor, 927–60003), incubated with indicated antibodies in the blocking solution containing 0.1 % Tween 20 and 0.01 % SDS (secondary only). All proteins were normalized to total protein using Ponceau S Solution (0.1 % w/v in 5 % acetic acid) (Sigma, P7170-1L) or the Revert 700 Total Protein Stain (Li-cor, 926–11010) and quantified with ImageJ. The antibodies and concentrations used are listed in Table S3. Graphs were created in Prism 10 for Mac OS X (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R, GraphPad Prism 10, and MATLAB. Relevant details are described in Results and figure legends. Data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Significance thresholds were defined as follows: ****,p < 0.0001; ***,p < 0.001; **,p < 0.01; *,p < 0.05; and non-significant (ns) for p > 0.05. Assumptions of normality were visually assessed using Q–Q plots of ANOVA residuals. These plots did not show substantial deviations from normal distribution. For all experiments involving comparisons between more than two groups, a one-way ANOVA was performed. When ANOVA indicated a statistically significant difference, Tukey's post-hoc multiple comparisons test was used to identify specific group differences. For experiments involving two groups, independent t-tests were performed.
