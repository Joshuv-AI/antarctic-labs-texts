Artificial intelligence and machine learning in diagnosing and managing temporomandibular disorders: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Vaishnavi Rajaraman, Deepak Nallaswamy, Amrutha Shenoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models have recently emerged as promising tools for enhancing diagnostic accuracy.ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of AI/ML models in detecting TMDs through a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing literature.MethodsA comprehensive search of electronic databases was conducted to identify studies assessing the diagnostic performance of AI/ML models in TMD diagnosis (PROSPERO-CRD420251035080). Data extraction and quality assessment were conducted independently by two reviewers using the AXIS tool for cross-sectional and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort studies. Meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy was performed using pooled sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio, and area under the curve. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with the I2 statistic.ResultsThe systematic search identified 368 articles, of which 12 studies met inclusion criteria after screening. Risk of bias assessment showed most observational studies had low to unclear bias, while cross-sectional studies varied from moderate to high quality. Five studies were eligible for meta-analysis and they revealed that AI and machine learning models achieved a pooled sensitivity of 87.1 %(95 %CI:84.9 %–89.2 %) and specificity of 87.0 %(95 %CI:84.8 %–89.2 %) for TMD diagnosis. The diagnostic odds ratio was 45.1(95 %CI:30.5–66.8), with an area under the ROC curve of 0.96, indicating excellent diagnostic accuracy. Moderate heterogeneity I2 = 38.7 %.ConclusionAI/ML models demonstrate excellent accuracy in differentiating patients with and without TMDs, reinforcing their potential as reliable diagnostic aids in clinical and screening settings. However, variability in input features and lack of standardized model development protocols highlight the need for future research focusing on validation across diverse populations and harmonization of diagnostic criteria.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint disorders, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Diagnosis, Pain management

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) have transformed various healthcare fields by enhancing diagnostic accuracy, optimizing treatment planning, and improving patient outcomes [1,2]. In dentistry, AI applications have gained significant attention,3, 4, 5 particularly in the diagnosis and management of temporomandibular disorders (TMD). AI-driven models, including deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), support vector machines (SVMs), and decision trees, have demonstrated promising results in radiographic interpretation,6,7 occlusal analysis,8 and predictive modeling. By leveraging large datasets and pattern recognition capabilities, AI has the potential to assist in early detection, objective diagnosis, and personalized treatment strategies for TMD patients, reducing human bias and increasing clinical efficiency.

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) encompass a diverse group of conditions affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated anatomical structures.9 These disorders are characterized by a range of symptoms including pain in the jaw, face, and neck; joint sounds such as clicking, popping, or crepitus; restricted mandibular movement; deviation on opening; headaches; and occlusal disturbances. TMD can be broadly categorized into myogenous (muscle-related), arthrogenous (joint-related), or a combination of both. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving biomechanical, neuromuscular, biopsychosocial, hormonal, and genetic factors. Stress, parafunctional habits (e.g., bruxism), trauma, malocclusion, and systemic conditions such as arthritis have all been implicated in its pathogenesis.

Additionally, TMD frequently coexists with other medical conditions such as anxiety, depression, sleep disorders (e.g., insomnia or sleep apnea), fibromyalgia, and chronic widespread pain. Systemic inflammatory diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome have also been associated with TMJ dysfunction. These comorbidities highlight the biopsychosocial complexity of TMD, which not only affects orofacial structures but also has a substantial impact on overall quality of life. The presence of such multifactorial associations further complicates the diagnostic and therapeutic landscape of TMD and supports the need for advanced, integrative diagnostic technologies like AI.

The prevalence of TMD varies widely, with studies reporting that up to 60–70 % of the population may experience at least one sign of TMD during their lifetime, though only a smaller proportion seeks treatment. This variability underscores the need for objective, standardized diagnostic tools and treatment protocols. Diagnosis remains challenging due to the subjectivity of clinical assessments and variability in imaging interpretations. Traditional methods rely on manual palpation, joint auscultation, patient-reported symptoms, and imaging techniques like panoramic radiography, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).10 While these methods are widely used, they often lack standardization and reproducibility, leading to diagnostic inconsistencies among clinicians.11 Additionally, treatment approaches including occlusal splints, physiotherapy, pharmacological management, and surgery often lack strong evidence-based protocols, resulting in variable treatment success rates.12

AI/ML models offer the potential to support clinical decision-making by enhancing diagnostic consistency, enabling earlier detection of subtle pathological changes in the TMJ, and reducing subjectivity in clinical and radiographic assessments. Rather than replacing clinicians, these tools can serve as adjuncts to improve diagnostic reliability and guide individualized treatment strategies in TMD care.13 Machine learning models have also been applied in electromyographic (EMG) analysis and occlusal force assessments, allowing objective quantification of muscle activity and bite forces in TMD patients.14 AI-based algorithms have been explored for automated segmentation of TMJ structures, classification10,15 of occlusal discrepancies, and prediction of treatment outcomes.11,16 Studies have reported that deep learning models can outperform traditional diagnostic methods in detecting TMJ pathologies by identifying subtle patterns in imaging that may not be easily recognized by human observers [17,17, 18, 19].

Despite these advancements, several challenges hinder the widespread implementation of AI in TMD management. These include limited clinical validation, small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and a lack of standardized AI frameworks tailored for TMD diagnosis. Ethical concerns related to data privacy, algorithm transparency, and clinical accountability also need to be addressed before AI can be fully integrated into routine clinical practice. Further research is needed to establish robust AI models with large-scale validation studies that compare their performance against experienced clinicians. To the best of our knowledge, no prior systematic review has comprehensively evaluated the diagnostic accuracy, clinical utility, and limitations of AI and ML models specifically in the context of temporomandibular disorders. While AI-based applications have been explored in various branches of dentistry, their application in TMD diagnosis and management remains a rapidly evolving and underexplored area. This systematic review seeks to fill this gap by synthesizing current evidence and comparing AI-driven models with conventional diagnostic methods.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis were meticulously designed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines20 and adhered to the methodological framework outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.21 The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251035080), ensuring methodological transparency and accountability. The comprehensive literature search targeted full-text studies published between 1980 and 2023, and the entire review process was carried out over a one-year period, from November 1, 2023 to October 30, 2024.

Research rationale

With increasing applications of AI in dentistry, a systematic review on how machine learning models assist in TMD diagnosis (based on imaging, EMG, or patient-reported data) could highlight future trends.

Research question

The literature search was based on the question: “Are artificial intelligence and machine learning models effective in diagnosing and managing temporomandibular disorders compared to traditional methods ?”

The search strategy was prepared based on Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO). The population (P) of interest was patients diagnosed with temporomandibular disorders (TMD). The intervention (I) included the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models for the diagnosis and/or management of TMD. The comparison (C) group comprised conventional diagnostic approaches such as clinical examination, radiography, and electromyography (EMG). The outcome (O) focused on the diagnostic and therapeutic performance of AI/ML models, specifically in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and overall clinical effectiveness. The included study designs were clinical studies such as observational studies, cohort studies (prospective or retrospective), cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, and randomized controlled trials.

Inclusion criteria

(a) Studies involving patients diagnosed with Temporomandibular Disorders (TMD), (b)Studies using clinical, radiographic, or patient-reported data for TMD diagnosis and management, (c) Studies assessing Artificial Intelligence (AI) or Machine Learning (ML) models for TMD diagnosis or treatment, (d) Studies reporting on diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, predictive value, and/or clinical outcomes related to AI/ML in TMD, (e) Studies evaluating treatment effectiveness using AI-based decision support systems, (f) Original research articles (RCTs, observational studies, cross-sectional studies, etc.), (g) Published in peer-reviewed journals, conference papers.

Exclusion criteria

(a) Studies that do not focus on TMD patients (e.g., general AI applications in dentistry or unrelated disorders), (b)Studies that do not report diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or clinical outcomes, (c)Studies on animal models or cadaveric research, (d)Reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, case reports, expert opinions, letters to the editor, (e)Grey literature, unpublished theses, or preprints.

Study selection and data collection process

The finalized PubMed search strategy was systematically applied across all selected databases, including Embase, Cochrane Databases of Systematic Reviews and Google Scholar, following strict adherence to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Each identified study, titles and abstracts were carefully reviewed by two independent investigators (V.R., and A.S.) (κ value = 0.83, which indicates near-perfect agreement between the two authors) to ensure accuracy, thoroughness, and the removal of duplicates. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and adjudication by a third reviewer. Relevant data from eligible articles were extracted and compiled using Microsoft 365 Office-powered Excel for organized synthesis. Any discrepancies in study selection or data extraction were resolved through consensus among the reviewers to maintain consistency and minimize bias.

Risk of bias assessment

Two investigators (V.R. and A.S.) meticulously conducted the data extraction and screening process in strict accordance with the pre-established inclusion criteria. The methodological quality of the included cross-sectional studies was evaluated using the AXIS tool, which comprehensively assesses 20 domains relevant to potential biases and validity concerns in observational research.22 For retrospective and prospective cohort studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was employed, scoring studies across three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome, for a maximum of 9 points. Each study was independently assessed by both reviewers with responses assigned based on the closest matching criteria.23 Any discrepancies that arose during the evaluation process were resolved through consensus, and if needed, a senior reviewer (D.G.) mediated discussions to ensure uniformity and accuracy in quality appraisal.

Summary measures and synthesis of results

A meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4 to pool sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratios with 95 % CIs using a random-effects model. SPSS (v26) was used for descriptive statistics. Continuity correction (adding 0.5) was applied to 2 × 2 contingency tables with zero-cell frequencies to enable computation of effect sizes and prevent infinite odds ratios. The pooled sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and area under the curve (AUC) were derived from the included studies. Studies were assessed for quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies and AXIS for cross-sectional studies. The data were analyzed using SPSS and RevMan, and statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. TP, FP, FN, and TN values were extracted directly from the studies or calculated based on available data. In studies where TP, FP, FN, and TN values were not explicitly reported, we derived them from reported sensitivity, specificity, and sample size using back-calculation.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

In this systematic review, a structured and comprehensive search strategy was applied across three electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Databases for Systematic Reviews and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies on the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning in temporomandibular disorders. The search yielded a total of 368 articles. After removing duplicates using EndNote Basic Software (Clarivate Analytics, New York, NY), 202 unique records remained. These were then screened against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following full-text review, a total of 12 studies were selected for inclusion. The forest plot for the same was obtained (Fig. 4). The diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) was 45.1(95 % CI: 30.5–66.8), indicating a strong association between AI/ML models and accurate TMD diagnosis (Fig. 5). The findings were robust across sensitivity analyses, and no significant publication bias was detected.

Discussion

Interpretation of diagnostic accuracy

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that AI and ML models show high diagnostic accuracy in detecting and managing temporomandibular disorders (TMDs). The pooled sensitivity was 87.1 % (95 % CI: 84.9 %–89.2 %), and specificity was 87.0 % (95 % CI: 84.8 %–89.2 %), indicating strong and balanced predictive performance across the included models. These results reinforce the role of AI as a valuable adjunct for improving diagnostic consistency and early detection of TMDs. AI techniques such as convolutional neural networks and interpretable machine learning algorithms consistently outperformed conventional diagnostic approaches, particularly in analyzing imaging data and clinical parameters. The results confirm that AI can be a valuable adjunct tool in improving early and accurate diagnosis of TMDs.

Sources of heterogeneity and bias

However, some variability in diagnostic accuracy was noted, with a moderate heterogeneity value (I2 = 38.7 %). This may be attributed to differences in AI model architectures (e.g., CNNs, SVMs, decision trees), imaging modalities (CBCT, MRI, EMG), dataset sizes and labeling standards, and the reference diagnostic criteria used across studies (e.g., RDC/TMD vs. radiologist-confirmed imaging). These variations likely contributed to the statistical heterogeneity observed in our meta-analysis.

Recent technological advances in AI models for TMD diagnosis

Recent advancements in AI/ML for TMD diagnosis have demonstrated significant potential in improving diagnostic precision and clinical decision-making. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and other deep learning architectures have shown high accuracy in detecting TMJ structural abnormalities using CBCT and MRI scans, often surpassing traditional radiographic interpretation by clinicians. AI-assisted tools have been developed for automatic segmentation of the condyle and disc, which streamline the analysis of joint morphology and displacement patterns. Machine learning algorithms have also been employed in surface electromyography (sEMG) analysis to objectively assess muscle function and detect abnormal neuromuscular activity associated with myogenous TMD. Furthermore, predictive models are being designed to forecast treatment outcomes based on baseline imaging, occlusal data, and patient-reported symptoms. Examples include CNN-based MRI disc position classification, random forest models for symptom-based TMD subgrouping, and RNNs for temporal pattern analysis in pain progression. Hybrid AI systems integrating occlusal force distribution, radiomics, and clinical profiles are also emerging, reflecting a more holistic approach to TMD diagnostics. These technological advances pave the way toward precision diagnostics and individualized treatment planning in TMD care.

AI model performance and clinical implications

The high diagnostic performance of AI models in this review aligns well with trends observed in other medical imaging and diagnostic fields, where deep learning has enhanced detection rates of complex pathologies. For instance, CNN-based classification of TMJ osteoarthritis severity mirrored findings from radiology and orthopedics, where AI improves sensitivity to subtle structural changes.24 Moreover, our results support previous studies reporting the integration of multimodal data (imaging, EMG, psychosocial factors) as a means to enhance TMD diagnosis, echoing similar approaches in chronic pain and musculoskeletal disorder research [25,26].

Comparative diagnostic performance and clinical integration of AI tools

The findings of this meta-analysis highlight the strong diagnostic performance of AI and machine learning models in detecting temporomandibular disorders, aligning with trends observed in prior research. The diagnostic accuracy of conventional tools for temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) demonstrates considerable variability.11,12 In comparison, advanced imaging techniques like computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) considered the gold standards for diagnosing osteoarthritis and internal derangement, respectively have shown consistently high inter-examiner reliability.27,28 Previous studies by authors like de Dumast et al. demonstrated comprehensive phenotypic characterization of TMJ health and disease at clinical, imaging and biological levels, using novel flexible and versatile open-source tools for a web-based system that provides advanced shape statistical analysis and a neural network based classification of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis.29 A systematic review assessing clinical diagnostic tests and signs reported sensitivity ranging from 2 % to 89 % and specificity from 14 % to 97 %, depending on the specific test and the TMD subtype being evaluated.30 The AI model showed accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.84, 0.54, and 0.94, respectively, for diagnosing bony abnormality.24 Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have been explored as decision-support tools in TMD diagnosis. By leveraging diverse data sources such as diagnostic imaging, video footage, radiomic features, jaw motion analysis, electronic medical records (EMRs), and biomarkers, AI has the potential to significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy. In contrast to some previous reports where model generalizability was limited by small datasets or lack of external validation, the studies included in the present review exhibited broader applicability due to more robust design and multicenter datasets.

Strengths and limitations

This review's conclusions are tempered by several limitations. Many included studies had small sample sizes and lacked external validation, limiting the generalizability of findings. The heterogeneity in AI methodologies, input data types, and outcome measures complicated direct comparisons and meta-analysis. Additionally, potential publication bias towards positive AI results cannot be excluded. The use of RevMan 5.4 and SPSS for meta-analyses was guided by their compatibility with diagnostic accuracy data and forest plot generation. A continuity correction (adding 0.5 to zero cells) was applied to 2 × 2 tables with sparse event data to avoid infinite or undefined diagnostic odds ratios. While appropriate for rare events, this correction may slightly affect pooled estimates in small samples.

Despite the potential of AI in enhancing TMD diagnosis and management, several barriers hinder its routine clinical adoption. Ethical concerns include data privacy, algorithmic bias, and accountability in AI-assisted decisions. Regulatory issues such as the absence of clear guidelines for medical AI approval and lack of standardization across datasets also limit scalability. Additionally, the high cost of AI infrastructure, software licensing, and required hardware may be prohibitive for smaller practices or low-resource settings. Clinician-related barriers include limited awareness, insufficient training, skepticism toward AI-generated outputs, and the need for tools that integrate seamlessly into existing diagnostic workflows. Recent policy frameworks, including the WHO's guidance on ethical AI deployment in health and the FDA's action plan for AI/ML-based software as medical devices, underscore the need for transparency, ongoing validation, and clinician oversight. Aligning future AI models with these international standards will be essential for their safe and effective clinical translation31. Overcoming these barriers will require multidisciplinary collaboration, policy reform, and continued education to build clinician confidence and acceptance.

Future directions and clinical integration

Future trends in AI for TMD diagnosis include the adoption of federated learning, which allows collaborative model training across multiple clinical sites without sharing raw patient data, thereby preserving privacy while expanding model generalizability. Another promising direction is explainable AI (XAI), particularly LIME, enhances the transparency and trustworthiness of AI systems in healthcare, and SHAP being favored for its stability and mathematical guarantees thereby potentially improving patient outcomes and fostering greater acceptance of AI-driven solutions among medical professionals32. This seeks to make AI decision-making more transparent and interpretable to clinicians, fostering trust and facilitating clinical integration. Furthermore, real-time integration of AI models with electronic health records (EHRs) could enable continuous monitoring and personalized recommendations based on a patient's full medical-dental profile. These innovations are expected to significantly enhance the clinical applicability of AI tools in TMD management. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter prospective studies with standardized protocols for data collection and AI model evaluation. Emphasis on developing explainable AI algorithms will be crucial to ensure clinical transparency and trust. Ethical and regulatory frameworks must also be addressed to guide safe integration of AI into routine TMD diagnosis and management.

Conclusion

The findings of the present systematic review and meta-analysis confirm that AI and machine learning models exhibit high diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing between patients with and without temporomandibular disorders, supporting their reliability in clinical and screening contexts. These findings strengthen the evidence that AI has matured into a clinically relevant tool for TMD diagnosis. However, heterogeneity in feature selection and training data remains a critical concern, underscoring the need for standardized methodologies and validation across diverse populations to ensure consistent and generalizable outcomes.
