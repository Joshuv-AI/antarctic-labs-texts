Neuroprotective effect of mesenchymal stem cell-derived miR-143-3p on dopaminergic neurons via the TLR3/NF-κB/p53 signaling pathway
Authors: Jing Sun, Tian Wang, Chen Qiu, Tianwen Li, Peng Wang, Tatyana Matveeva, Tanner Boyd, Qingyuan Tang, Jun Xue, Lu Su, Hyemyung Seo, Yi Eve Sun, Tian-Lin Cheng, Jeffrey Schweitzer, Bin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransplantation of midbrain dopaminergic neurons (mDANs) derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) represents a promising therapeutic approach for Parkinson’s disease (PD), with several clinical trials currently in progress. However, the poor survival of grafted mDANs, in part due to inflammation triggered by surgical injury, poses significant challenges to both therapeutic efficacy and safety. In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and their derivatives have played an important role in the field of stem cell therapy due to their easy accessibility and immunomodulatory properties. However, their potential role as an adjunct in cell therapy remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the protective effects of conditioned medium (CM) derived from MSCs and the mechanisms of action by which it attenuates surgery-induced neuroinflammation and enhances the survival of transplanted mDANs.MethodsTo mimic the neuroinflammatory response caused by the surgical aspect of cell transplantation, transplantation medium alone (i.e. without cells) was injected into the striatum of male NOD SCID mice. The anti-inflammatory effects of MSC-CM in vivo were evaluated by assessing the activation of microglia and expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Next, an in vitro cell death model was established using IFN-γ and TNF-α stimulation. The protective effects of MSC-CM against IFN-γ and TNF-α-induced inflammatory injury in mDANs were assessed using immunofluorescence staining and TUNEL assay. To identify the active components and underlying mechanisms for the protective effects of MSC-CM, inhibitory treatments, miRNA sequencing, dual-luciferase reporter assay, and subsequent validation by qPCR and Western blotting were performed. Finally, MSC-CM was administered intranasally into male NOD SCID mice following mDAN transplantation to evaluate its protective effects on the grafted mDANs in vivo.ResultsMSC-CM suppressed neuroinflammation and significantly protected mDANs from inflammation-induced cell death via inhibition of TLR3/NF-κB/p53 signaling pathway in this model system. This protective effect was largely reproduced by treatment with a specific component of the MSC-CM, miR-143-3p. Overexpression of miR-143-3p further enhanced the protective effects and mechanisms of action of MSC-CM.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that MSC-CM or its component miR-143-3p exert protective effects on mDANs against inflammatory injury, primarily by inhibiting the TLR3/NFκB/p53 signaling pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05186-z.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) affects 1–2% of the population over the age of 65 but can occur in younger age groups as well. It is second in prevalence among movement disorders, and among neurodegenerative disorders [1, 2]. Its economic burden on society is escalating with the aging population. Its pathological hallmark is the selective and progressive death of midbrain dopaminergic neurons (mDANs) in the substantia nigra, leading to motor symptoms including bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity and postural disorders [3–6]. Treatment of PD via functional replacement of mDANs in the brain has a lengthy history with extensive documentation of short- and long-term outcomes. Both human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) derived from the patient’s own fibroblasts are potential sources of cells that can be differentiated into suitable midbrain dopaminergic progenitors (mDAPs) for cell replacement therapy [7–12]. However, there remain significant obstacles to the optimal implementation of stem-cell based therapies for PD. One of the greatest challenges is cell survival through the transplantation process.

Studies involving the transplantation of rat or human mDANs into the rat striatum have shown that the majority of transplanted mDANs die within 24 h post-transplantation [13]. Some cells in the graft develop into mDANs over time, but this process may take months, and the number of mature mDANs is difficult to predict and may be insufficient to give the expected therapeutic benefit [14]. The poor survival of the transplanted mDANs could be due to a range of deleterious factors such as hypoxia/ischemia, deprivation of essential trophic support, and elevated oxidative stress [15].

Inflammatory processes related to the surgical implantation may also play a role in limiting transplanted mDAN survival [16]. Studies have shown that post-transplantation, markers of lymphocytes (such as CD3 and CD8), astrocytes (GFAP), and microglia (Iba1) can be detected in the graft area, indicating that the inflammatory response induced by the transplantation procedure may be a significant contributor to the loss of mDANs [17, 18]. In addition, microglial activation and neuroinflammation are currently considered pivotal in the progression of PD itself [19, 20]. Microglial activation initiates or exacerbates neuronal loss by increasing the production of cytotoxic molecules such as superoxide, nitric oxide, various pro-inflammatory cytokines, and prostaglandins [21–24]. In an in vivo CRISPR/Cas9 screen, Kim et al. [25] identified p53 as a key gene limiting the survival of transplanted dopaminergic neurons, revealing a TNFα/p53/apoptosis pathway. Their study demonstrated that p53-mediated apoptosis is a major contributor to dopaminergic neuron loss and established a causal link between the TNFα–NFκB signaling pathway and decreased cell survival. Other studies have also shown that NF-κB contributes to 6-OHDA-induced apoptosis of nigral dopaminergic neurons through p53 [26].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a type of stem cell with multipotent differentiation potential. They are easy to obtain, exhibit strong proliferative capacity, and possess immunomodulatory activity [27]. Injection of MSCs has been shown to improve limb coordination, significantly alleviate motor deficits in an MPTP-induced mouse PD model, and protect dopaminergic neurons [28, 29]. In recent years, MSC therapy has been proposed as a treatment for PD [30], and patients exhibited certain behavioral improvements after the procedure [31]. Studies have shown that the immunomodulatory, neuroprotective, and neurogenic effects of MSCs [32, 33] are primarily mediated by the secretome released into the extracellular environment [34]. The MSC secretome is a complex mixture of bioactive molecules secreted into the MSC-conditioned medium (MSC-CM), consisting of soluble growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles, which are capable of transferring proteins and genetic materials (such as miRNAs) to other cells, offering significant therapeutic potential [35–37]. MSC-CM has been shown to increase the survival rate of dopaminergic neurons in the 6-OHDA PD cellular model [38]. Umbilical cord-MSC-exosomes suppress the activation of microglia and reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines in PD animal models [39]. miRNAs have advantages as potential therapeutic agents, including smaller molecular size, easier delivery, and lower immunogenicity. Studies have shown that miRNAs are involved in various mediated cellular activities, such as angiogenesis and anti-angiogenesis, immunomodulation, anti-apoptosis, and anti-fibrosis [40]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that MSC-derived miRNAs can suppress neuroinflammation and alleviate neurological disorders. For example, overexpression of miR-100-5p derived from trophoblast stage-derived MSCs can inhibit oxidative stress by targeting NOX4 [41]. Additionally, bone marrow-MSC-derived miR-146a, miR-133b, and miR-21 have been shown to enhance neuronal plasticity and promote cell survival [42, 43]. MSC-derived miRNA-146 regulates the inflammatory properties of microglia and reduces neuroinflammation by inhibiting TRAF6 and IRAK1 in microglia, thereby downregulating the phosphorylation of NF-κB [44]. These studies collectively demonstrate the important potential for MSC-derived miRNAs to regulate neuroinflammation and alleviate neurological diseases. However, the involvement of MSC-CM-derived microRNAs in regulating transplanted cell function remains poorly characterized.

In this study, we investigated the potential for MSC-CM to mitigate neuroinflammation and protect mDANs following cell transplantation, with a particular focus on the role of miRNAs in MSC-CM. We found that MSC-CM not only alleviated the inflammation caused by surgical injury following cell transplantation in vivo but also protected mDANs from apoptosis induced by pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α in vitro. Through miRNA sequencing analysis of MSC-CM, we found that miRNA-143-3p was highly expressed in MSC-CM. In mDANs treated with IFN-γ and TNF-α, the expression of miRNA-143-3p was decreased, whereas it was upregulated following treatment with MSC-CM. Further studies confirmed that both MSC-CM and miRNA-143-3p exert neuroprotective effects by suppressing the expression of TLR3, thereby inhibiting the phosphorylation of NF-κB and downregulating the expression of p53 and its downstream target genes, a key factor limiting the survival of terminally differentiated mDANs. Our findings suggest a protective role for MSC-CM in mDAN survival through transplantation and suggest that specific MSC-CM components, especially miR-143-3p, may represent a promising biological adjunctive enhancement to cell therapy.

Methods and materials

Animals

NOD SCID (NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/NCrCrl, Charles River, strain code 394) male mice, 8–10 weeks of age were purchased from Charles River and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated micro-isolation cages. NOD SCID mice were randomly divided into the control group and MSC-CM group, n = 6 per group. All animals were maintained on a 12-hour light–dark cycle with free access to food and water, as well as controlled ambient temperature and humidity. Animal studies were performed in accordance with current NIH guidelines and carried out under a Massachusetts General Hospital/Harvard Medical School protocol (2023N000148). The study was reported following Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Measures to ensure blinding were applied throughout various stages of the experiment to minimize bias and improve the study’s reliability. Group assignments were carried out using a randomized procedure. The researchers conducting outcome evaluations and data analyses were kept unaware of the group assignments to prevent potential bias.

Human embryonic stem cell line WA09 culture

Human embryonic stem cell line WA09 was purchased from WiCell Research Institute. WA09 cells were cultured on Matrigel (Corning, 354277) -coated dishes using Nutristem® hPSC XF Medium (Sartorius, 05-100-1 A), with daily medium changes. For passaging, the cells were washed once with DPBS and dissociated with 0.5 mM EDTA (Gibco, 15575-020) for 5 min. Y-27632 (TargetMol, T1725) was added to the Nutristem® hPSC XF Medium during passaging and replaced with Nutristem® hPSC XF Medium without Y-27632 the following day.

Generation of mDANs

As described in our previous study [45], WA09 cells were differentiated in vitro for 21 days using a spotting-based protocol to generate mDANs. On D15, cells were dissociated with Accutase (Sigma, SCR005) and replated onto dishes coated with poly-L-ornithine (Sigma, P4957), fibronectin (Sigma, F0895-5MG), and laminin (Sigma, L2020-1MG) at a density of 100,000 cells/cm². On D21, mDANs were treated with 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (Peprotech, 300-02) and 100 ng/mL TNF-α (Peprotech, 300–01 A) for 7 days to generate a cell apoptosis model. Half of the culture medium was replaced every other day, with fresh IFN-γ and TNF-α added at each medium change.

Culture of human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and processing of conditioned medium (CM)

The MSC derived from human umbilical cord were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA. MSC were cultured in α-MEM basal medium (Thermo Fisher, 32561037) supplemented with 5% UltraGRO™ (AventaCell Biomedical Corp, HPCPLCRL10) and 2U/mL heparin (MCE, HY-17567 A), with medium changed every other day.

MSCs were cultured until they reached 70–80% confluency, at which point the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were further cultured for two days. The MSC-CM was transferred into a 50 mL tube and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was carefully collected, transferred to a new 50 mL tube, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Finally, the resulting supernatant was transferred to a new 50 mL tube for subsequent use. For RNase A treatment, 1mL MSC-CM was treated with 1 µL of RNase A, and then incubated at 37 °C for 5–6 h. MSC-CM heat inactivation was performed by incubating at 95 °C for 30 min.

Generation of MSCs with miR-143-3p overexpression or knockdown and collection of CM

Lentivirus was purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). MSCs were transfected with control lentivirus, miR-143-3p overexpression lentivirus, or miR-143-3p inhibitor lentivirus, each at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. Polybrene was added at a concentration of 5 µg/mL to enhance transfection efficiency. The medium was replaced 48 h after transfection. CM was collected 48 h later for subsequent experiments.

Culture of HEK293T and processing of CM

HEK293T cells (GNHul7, Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher, 11965118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher, 10091148) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, 15140122), with medium changed every other day. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, the medium was replaced with MSC medium, and cells were cultured for an additional two days. HEK293T-CM was then collected using the same method as for MSC-CM.

Preparation of 50× concentrated MSC-CM and intranasal administration

MSC-CM was transferred to an Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter (Sigma, UFC901024) and centrifuged following the manual. The concentrated MSC-CM retained in the upper chamber was collected and stored at − 80 °C. For mouse intranasal administration, 3µL (100U in PBS) of hyaluronidase (Sigma, H3506-100MG) were dropped into each nostril using a pipette tip. Thirty minutes later, 100µL of concentrated MSC-CM was administered into each of nostril using a pipette tip. This procedure started from one day before transplantation and was repeated every day until the mice were perfused at 1 or 2 weeks post-transplantation.

TUNEL assay

Day21 WA09-dervied mDANs were treated with 100 ng/mL IFN-γ or 100 ng/mL TNF-α or with DMSO alone for 7 days and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15710). Cell apoptosis was detected with TUNEL/7-AAD assay kit (Abcam, Ab66110) used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing twice, the samples were incubated with DNA labeling solution containing TdT enzyme and Br-dUTP for 1 h in a dark, humidified 37 °C incubator. The labeled sections were washed with rinse buffer, incubated with antibody solution containing anti-BrdU-Red antibody, and stained with DAPI. The number of TUNEL⁺/7-AAD⁺ cells was measured in three randomly selected non-overlapping fields per sample for quantitative analysis per group. Each experiment was conducted independently three times.

miRNA transfection

50 nmol miRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher, 31985062). RFect Transfection Reagent (BaiDai Biotech, 11013) was diluted in Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium in a separate tube. The dilutions were mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The miRNA and the RFect dilution were then combined, mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The mixture was then added to the culture well and the culture plate was shaken gently to mix well. The hsa-miR-143-3p (UGAGAUGAAGCACUGUAGCUC) and the hsa-miR-143-3p inhibitor (ACUCUACUUCGUGACAUCGAG) were purchased from GeneScript.

miRNA sequence and analysis

The MSC-CM was subjected to quality inspection for the miRNA sequencing (Chengqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China). Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using edgeR. The significantly differentially expressed miRNAs were identified based on the following criteria: |log2 fold change | > 1 and P-value < 0.05. The target genes of miRNAs were predicted using the miRNAWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) database based on the following criteria: binding p value > 0.9, TargetScan, miRDB and miRTarBase database-linked, including 3UTR, 5UTR and CDS binding site. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway) enrichment analyses of the target genes were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.14) with default parameters, with a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Luciferase reporter assay

miRWalk analysis was used to predict the binding sites of TLR3 and miR-143-3p. Both the wild-type TLR3 wild-type (WT) plasmid containing the miR-143-3p binding site and the TLR3 mutant (MUT) plasmid identical to the miR-143-3p sequence were ligated into the luciferase reporter vector GP-miRGLO (synthesized by GenePharma, Shanghai, China). The MUT sequence, being identical to miR-143-3p and thus non-complementary, is not bound by miR-143-3p. This construct serves to verify the specificity of the interaction. The same company also synthesized miR-143-3p and its mimic negative control (NC). HEK293T cells overexpressing mimic NC or miR-143-3p were co-transfected with TLR3-WT, TLR3-MUT, or the GP-miRGLO vector. After 48 h of co-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, E1910). The results are expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase relative light units.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using 2% NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma, PPC1010). The lysate was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, 23227), followed by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. Proteins were then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, washed once with 0.3% PBST (0.3% Tween in PBS), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the membrane was washed three times with 3% PBST, incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Promega, W1015) with a Chemi-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). All antibodies information is in Supplemental Table 1.

Quantitative RT-PCR

mDANs were lysed with Trizol to extract mRNA, and the RNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). Reverse transcription was performed using the GoScript Reverse Transcriptase Mix with Oligo(dT) (Promega, A2791). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix for qPCR kit (Thermo Fisher, A25918) on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). For miRNA qPCR, following total RNA extraction, qPCR was performed using the Mir-X™ miRNA qRT-PCR TB Green® Kit (Takara, 638314). Primer sequence information is in Supplemental Tables 2 and 3.

Immunostaining

Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and washed three times with 0.3% PBST (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS). Blocking was performed at room temperature for 1 h using blocking solution containing 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) and 1% horse serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 008-000-121) in 0.3%PBST. Following the addition of primary antibodies, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with 0.3% PBST, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies and Hoechst 33258 at room temperature for 1 h. Following three additional washes with 0.3% PBST, coverslips were mounted using Fluoromount-G. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). All antibody information is contained in Supplemental Table 1.

Cell preparation for transplantation and surgical procedure

On day 21, WA09-derived mDANs were rinsed with DPBS and then digested with Accutase for 8 min. Digestion was stopped by transplantation medium consisting of Lactated Ringer’s solution, 20 ng/mL BDNF (Peprotech, 450-02), 20 ng/mL GDNF (Peprotech, 450 − 10), 10 µM Y-27632 (TargetMol, T1725), and 20 µM Boc-D-FMK (Cayman, 16118), and the cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The cells were counted using a cell counter (LUNA-FX7™, Logos Biosystems). The cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 50,000 cells/µL and kept on ice. After isoflurane (Baxter, 1001936040) anesthesia, the mouse was placed in a Kopf stereotaxic frame (Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA). For bilateral intrastriatal transplantation of mDANs (or transplantation medium injection), a volume of 2 µL was injected at the coordinates: AP + 0.5; ML ± 1.8; DV -3.2 mm. Injections were performed using a Hamilton syringe with a blunt 26G, 0.75-inch needle at 0.2 µL/min.

Brain sections, immunohistochemistry, and stereological analysis

After weighing the mice, anesthesia was induced with Ketamine 100 mg/kg (Zoetis, 10004027) and Xylazine 10 mg/kg (Bimeda, 1XYL003). The depth of anesthesia was assessed by toe pinch, tail pinch, and palpebral reflex tests. Once the animal no longer responded to these tests, the abdominal hair was shaved, and the animal was perfused with cold PBS followed by 4% PFA. The brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight and dehydrated by immersion in sucrose solutions. Coronal sections of the striatum were cut at a thickness of 30 μm. Brain sections were rinsed with 0.3% PBST, followed by treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After three rinses, sections were blocked with 1% horse serum at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were treated with primary antibodies and incubated overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, brain sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB staining kit (Vector, SK-4100). Immunohistochemistry images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). To quantify TH+ and hNuc+ cells in the grafts, the optical fractionator probe of the Stereo Investigator system (MBF Bioscience, Williston, VT) was used under a 63X oil immersion objective. A counting frame of 50 × 50 μm and a grid size of 200 × 200 μm were applied, with a section sampling interval of 1:6. Graft volume was estimated using the Cavalieri Estimator probe under a 5x objective, employing a grid spacing of 100 μm and a section sampling interval of 1:6.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. A two-tailed t-test was used to assess statistical significance. p-values are represented as follows: p > 0.05 indicates no significant difference, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

Results

MSC-CM suppresses the inflammatory response induced by surgical injury

As described in our previous study [45], WA09 cells were differentiated for 21 days to generate midbrain dopaminergic cells (mDACs) including dopaminergic progenitors (mDAPs) and mDANs, with approximately 85% being LMX1A⁺/FOXA2⁺ mDAPs, and around 15% being TH⁺ mDANs. Most of the mDANs were identified as GIRK2⁺ A9-type neurons, which are the major subtype lost in PD (Fig. S1A). We transplanted these cell populations into the striatum of NOD SCID mice. After one week, the number of surviving TH⁺ cells was markedly low, accounting for less than 1% of the total transplanted cells (Fig. S1B-C). These data indicate that a substantial loss of mDANs occurred after transplantation.

To assess the inflammatory response to the surgical injury of transplantation itself, we used the transplantation protocol but injected only cell-free transplantation medium into the striatum of NOD SCID mice (Fig. 1A). One-week post-injection, brain tissue analysis revealed significant microglial activation (Fig. 1B-C) and upregulation of MHC II expression (Fig. 1D-E). In addition, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ (Fig. 1F-G) and TNF-α (Fig. 1H-I), were markedly elevated. These findings indicate that surgical injury triggers a neuroinflammatory response in the host brain. Based on this, we hypothesized that the early loss of transplanted mDANs might be related to this neuroinflammation, which is consistent with our previous reports [16]. MSC-CM is well known for its potent anti-inflammatory properties. Therefore, we speculated that MSC-CM administration could be an effective strategy to mitigate surgical injury-induced neuroinflammation. Previous studies [46, 47] have established intranasal delivery as a possible route of administration of small molecules and peptides targeting the central nervous system. After intranasal delivery for one week, MSC-CM significantly reduced microglial (Fig. 1B-C) and MHC II (Fig. 1D-E) activation and suppressed the acute upregulation of the key pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ (Fig. 1F-G) and TNF-α (Fig. 1H-I). These results provide strong evidence supporting a potential neuroprotective role for MSC-CM in surgical injury-induced neuroinflammation.

MSC-CM protected mDANs from IFN-γ- and TNF-α-induced cell death.

To evaluate whether MSC-CM can inhibit inflammation-induced cell death in vitro, we used the pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α, which are released following surgical injury (Fig. 1F-I), to establish a cell apoptosis model. At D20, mDANs were pretreated with a 1:1 mixture of MSC-CM and mDAN culture medium, with mDAN culture medium treatment as control. Then mDANs were further exposed to the pro-inflammatory IFN-γ or TNF-α at 100 ng/mL from D21(Fig. 2A). After 7 days of treatment with IFN-γ, the number of TH⁺ cells was markedly reduced, while there was no significant difference in the number of FOXA2⁺ cells, indicating that IFN-γ primarily affects mature mDANs rather than mDAPs. Moreover, the number of Ki67⁺ proliferating cells was also significantly reduced (Fig. 2B-C). Expression of the mature neuronal marker NeuN was also significantly decreased, indicating that not only dopaminergic cells but also other neuronal subtypes underwent apoptosis upon exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 2B-C). Consistently, the apoptotic marker cleaved caspase-3 (CC3) was significantly upregulated (Fig. 2B-C). In addition, TUNEL staining revealed significant apoptosis in mDANs; similar results were obtained using 7-AAD staining (Fig. 2D, E). Similar results were observed when mDANs were treated with another pro-inflammatory cytokine, TNF-α (Fig. S2). These findings collectively indicate that the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines induces neuronal apoptosis.

We further evaluated whether MSC-CM could rescue cells from apoptosis induced by IFN-γ and TNF-α. In the IFN-γ apoptosis cell model, the results showed that MSC-CM rescued the cell death of TH⁺ cells but had no significant effect on FOXA2⁺ cells, and it also reduced the number of Ki67⁺ cells, rescued NeuN⁺ neuronal loss, and significantly downregulated CC3 expression, whereas HEK293T-CM had no such effects, indicating that these protective effects are specifically derived from MSC-CM (Fig. 2B-C). And MSC-CM treatment decreased the expression of TUNEL and 7-AAD (Fig. 2D, E). In the TNF-α apoptosis cell model, MSC-CM was also observed to alleviate apoptosis (Fig. S2). In summary, our results indicate that the acute release of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α following surgery-induced injury in vivo can lead to substantial loss of mDANs in vitro, and that this damage can be effectively alleviated by MSC-CM. In addition, MSC-CM also suppressed excessive cell proliferation.

RNA is an active component of MSC-CM in neuroprotection

Multiple studies have shown that miRNAs are involved in regulating inflammatory injury and death of mDANs. Multiple miRNAs, such as miR-124-3p, miR-155, miR-330, and miR-195, exert protective effects on dopaminergic neurons and regulate neuroinflammation [48–50]. To determine if miRNAs are an active component in MSC-CM responsible for ameliorating the effects of neuroinflammation, we treated the MSC-CM with RNase A to inactivate RNAs. TUNEL and 7-AAD staining were used to detect cell apoptosis and revealed that MSC-CM lost nearly all its neuroprotective effects on mDANs after RNase A treatments (Fig. 3A, C). Furthermore, compared to the normal MSC-CM group, the number of TH⁺ cells was significantly reduced after RNase A treatment, while the proportion of FOXA2⁺ cells remained unchanged and Ki67⁺ cells increased (Fig. 3B-C). In addition, RNase A treatment significantly reduced the number of NeuN⁺ cells and increased the number of CC3⁺ cells (Fig. 3B-C), indicating that the MSC-CM treated with RNase A was unable to suppress the initiation of apoptosis in cells, suggesting that RNAs in MSC-CM contribute to suppression of neuroinflammation and protection mDANs. This finding is consistent with the results from heat inactivation of MSC-CM, which showing a significant increase in TUNEL and 7-AAD positive cells, a reduction in TH⁺ and NeuN⁺ cell populations, no alteration in FOXA2⁺ cells, an elevation in Ki67⁺ cells, and an increase in CC3⁺ cells, compared with MSC-CM treatment without heat inactivation (Fig. 3B-C). Taken together, our results suggest that RNAs in MSC-CM contribute to suppression of neuroinflammation and protection of mDANs.

MSC-CM-derived miR-143-3p mediates the inhibition of IFN-γ and TNF-α-induced neuroinflammation.

To further identify functional miRNAs associated with MSC-CM, we performed miRNA sequencing analysis comparing MSC-CM with control CM. A total of 715 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified, among which 323 were upregulated and 392 were downregulated. The top 20 miRNAs with the highest expression levels are listed (Supplemental Table 4). Using the miRWalk, TargetScan, and miRDB databases, we collectively predicted 713 potential target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the target genes of highly expressed miRNAs in MSC-CM were mainly involved in the regulation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, regulation of cell growth, and neuronal apoptosis processes (Fig. 4A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway (KEGG pathway) analysis indicated that the highly expressed miRNAs in MSC-CM were involved in the regulation of the MAPK, mTOR, the p53 signaling pathway and other inflammation-related pathways (Fig. 4B). Among these, we focused on eight MSC-CM-derived miRNAs that are closely associated with the regulation of the NF-κB pathway. These eight differentially expressed miRNAs include miR-410-3p, miR-143-3p, miR-495-3p, miR-31-5p, miR-125-5p, miR-138-5p, miR-199-5p, and miR-199a-3p. We performed miRNA qPCR to examine the expression of the eight predicted miRNAs in mDANs following treatment with IFN-γ and TNF-α. The results showed that among the eight miRNAs, miR-143-3p was significantly downregulated after IFN-γ (Fig. 4C) or TNF-α (Fig.S3) treatment. When mDANs were treated with MSC-CM in the presence of IFN-γ or TNF-α, the expression of miR-143-3p was markedly upregulated (Fig. 4C, S3). As previous studies have shown that miR-143-3p can inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway [51], we speculated that miR-143-3p may be one of the factors responsible for the anti-neuroinflammatory and neuroprotective effects of MSC-CM on mDANs. To elucidate the role of miR-143-3p in IFN-γ-induced neuroinflammation, we assessed apoptosis in mDANs following transfection with miR-143-3p or a nonspecific miRNA control. The results showed that overexpression of miR-143-3p significantly reduced the number of TUNEL⁺ and 7-AAD⁺ cells (Fig. 4D, F) in IFN-γ-treated mDANs, while the number of TH⁺ cells increased and the proportion of FOXA2⁺ and Ki67⁺ cells remained unchanged (Fig. 4E-F). Moreover, the number of NeuN⁺ cells increased, and the number of CC3⁺ cells was also reduced (Fig. 4E-F). To further validate the role of miR-143-3p in mediating the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of MSC-CM, cells were transfected with a miR-143-3p inhibitor (a synthetic antisense oligonucleotide complementary to miR-143-3p, designed to suppress the function of endogenous miR-143-3p) following IFN-γ and MSC-CM treatment. Inhibition of miR-143-3p attenuated the effects of MSC-CM, as evidenced by a reduction in the number of TUNEL⁺ cells and an increasing trend in the number of 7-AAD⁺ cells (Fig. 4D, F). It also significantly reduced the number of TH⁺ cells, although there were no obvious changes in FOXA2 and Ki67, and it decreased the number of NeuN⁺ cells while increasing CC3⁺ expression (Fig. 4E-F). To further confirm the role of miR-143-3p, we overexpressed or knocked down miR-143-3p in MSCs and then collected their CM. Overexpression of miR-143-3p in MSCs enhanced the neuroprotective effect of MSC-CM (decreased CC3⁺, increased TH⁺ neurons), while miR-143-3p knockdown attenuated it (Fig. S4). Thus, the neuroprotective activity of MSC-CM is related to miR-143-3p expression in parent MSCs, and its abundance directly determines the efficacy of the conditioned medium. These findings indicate that MSC-CM-derived miR-143-3p markedly inhibits IFN-γ-induced neuroinflammation, thereby exerting a protective effect on mDANs.

MSC-CM or miR-143-3p inhibits inflammation through the TLR3/NF-κB /p53 pathway

To confirm direct interaction, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted. The binding sites of TLR3 and miR-143-3p were predicted using miRWalk analysis. (Fig. 5A). The 3’UTR of TLR3 mRNA, encompassing both the WT sequence and a MUT version harboring a mutation at the putative miR-143-3p binding site, was cloned into a dual-luciferase reporter vector GP-miRGLO. Following co-transfection of these constructs into 293T cells with either miR-143-3p mimics or NC, we observed that overexpression of miR-143-3p led to a marked reduction in the luciferase activity driven by the WT 3’UTR, whereas no significant effect was detected on the MUT 3’UTR (Fig. 5B). These findings collectively demonstrate a direct binding interaction between miR-143-3p and the TLR3 3’UTR. Under physiological conditions, IκB forms a complex with p65, retaining it in the cytoplasm and inhibiting its activity [52]. Upon inflammatory stimuli (such as IFN-γ or TNF-α), upregulation of TLR3 induces phosphorylation of IκB, which is subsequently ubiquitinated and degraded [53]. The released p65 is then phosphorylated and translocates into the nucleus. Inside the nucleus, p65 acts as a transcription factor to activate the expression of a series of inflammation- and apoptosis-related genes, including upregulation of p53 and its downstream pro-apoptotic targets such as p21 and PUMA(Fig. 5C), leading to dopaminergic neuronal damage [25, 54]. IFN-γ and TNF-α induce neuroinflammatory responses by activating the TLR3/NF-κB/p53 signaling pathway, which is consistent with previous studies [55]. Compared to the IFN-γ-treated group, miR-143-3p or MSC-CM treatment significantly reduced the mRNA expression level of TLR3 (Fig. 5D). Moreover, the miR-143-3p inhibitor blocked the effect of MSC-CM in suppressing TLR3 expression (Fig. 5D). We then used Western blot analysis to examine the expression of key proteins in the NF-κB/p53 signaling pathway. Phosphorylation of IκB and p65, as well as their nuclear translocation, are typical indicators of NF-κB pathway activation [56, 57]. Following IFN-γ treatment, the phosphorylation levels of IκB and p65 were markedly increased, while treatment with MSC-CM or miR-143-3p reduced the expression of phosphorylated IκB and phosphorylated p65 (Fig. 5E-G). We also observed that the NF-κB target gene p53 was upregulated after IFN-γ treatment, whereas this effect was abolished by MSC-CM or miR-143-3p (Fig. 5E, H). In addition, the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein CC3 was positively correlated with activation of the NF-κB/p53 pathway, and either MSC-CM or miR-143-3p were able to suppress CC3 expression (Fig. 5E, I). Furthermore, MSC-CM and miR-143-3p were each able to suppress the mRNA levels of p53 and its downstream effectors, p21 and PUMA (Fig. 5J-L). These results were also validated in the TNF-α-induced apoptosis model (Fig. S5A-I). The miR-143-3p inhibitor blocked the inhibitory effect of MSC-CM on the TLR3/NF-κB/p53 pathway. When cells were treated with IFN-γ and MSC-CM followed by transfection with the miR-143-3p inhibitor, there was an increase in the phosphorylation of IκB and p65, as well as elevated protein levels of p53 and CC3, and increased mRNA levels of p53 and its downstream effectors, p21 and PUMA (Fig. 5E-L).

MSC-CM enhanced the survival of transplanted mDACs

Based on the above data, we hypothesized that MSC-CM can protect transplanted mDANs from surgical injury-induced cell death. To validate this hypothesis, D21 cells were transplanted into the striatum of NOD SCID mice. Intranasal administration of concentrated MSC-CM began one day prior to mDAC transplantation and was maintained once daily for two weeks to evaluate its effect on the graft. Then the mice were sacrificed for histological analysis (Fig. 6A). Iba1 staining showed significant microglial activation after transplantation, whereas treatment with MSC-CM suppressed microglial activation (Fig. 6B-C), indicating that MSC-CM can attenuate surgery-induced inflammation. We also found that MSC-CM significantly increased both the TH+ cell number and TH+ fiber density in the graft (Fig. 6D-F), suggesting that MSC-CM protected transplanted mDANs in vivo. Furthermore, the number of NeuN⁺ cells was higher in the MSC-CM group (Fig. 6G-H), suggesting that MSC-CM may also reduce the death of other types of mature neurons. In addition, the MSC-CM–treated group showed a smaller graft volume. (Fig. 6I-J). Since MSC-CM could inhibit Ki67 in vitro (Fig. 2D-E), this may indicate both improved survival of mDANs and reduced proliferation of mDAPs.

Taken together, these findings show that MSC-CM not only suppressed surgical injury-induced inflammation and enhanced the survival and maturation of transplanted mDANs, but also promoted their axonal outgrowth. These results suggest that MSC-CM can contribute to the maintenance and functional development of grafted mDANs and holds potential for improving the efficacy of cell therapy for PD.

Discussion

A major unmet challenge in dopaminergic neuron transplantation is the low survival rate of grafted cells in vivo. Park et al. [16] reported that after transplantation of mDACs containing 15% TH⁺ neurons, only about 1% of the surviving neurons in the early post-transplantation phase were TH⁺ neurons. Further in vitro studies showed that inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β, among those resulting from surgical injury, could induce apoptosis in TH⁺ neurons. Our results are consistent with these findings. To overcome the issue of low in vivo survival, large numbers of cells are often injected to ensure sufficient recovery of behavioral function in a PD animal model. However, since most of these cells die quickly, this approach may exacerbate the immune and inflammatory responses [58, 59].

Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying cell death and developing protective strategies is urgently needed for improving the efficacy of cell therapy for Parkinson’s disease. MSCs are known for their strong anti-inflammatory effects. MSCs suppress the TLR3/NF-κB pathway in macrophages [60] and administration of MSC-CM reduces total and phosphorylated NF-κB levels in mice with LPS-induced acute lung injury [61], but their possible role in preventing transplanted cell death has remained little explored.

Our results show that treatment with MSC-CM significantly inhibits the TLR3/NFκB/p53 signaling pathway. Notably, MSC-CM reversed IFN-γ and TNF-α-induced activation of TLR3 and phosphorylation of p65, two upstream regulators of the p53 signaling cascade, and significantly reduced the expression of p53 and its pro-apoptotic transcriptional targets. This suppression of neuroinflammation improved mDAN survival. Our data suggest that an active factor in this MSC-CM protective effect is miR-143-3p. Studies have demonstrated that miR-143-3p is packaged within exosomes, a formulation that likely confers resistance to enzymatic degradation and facilitates its biological function [62]. The protective effect shown here was maintained following miR-143-3p overexpression in MSCs but was abolished upon knockdown, demonstrating that its abundance determines the functional efficacy of the CM.

We found that miR-143-3p protects mDANs by directly targeting TLR3 and then inhibiting the TLR3/NFκB/p53 pathway. Unlike MSC-CM, miR-143-3p alone does not suppress Ki67 or abnormal cell proliferation, so it cannot fully replicate the full range of MSC-CM’s effects. Therefore, we chose MSC-CM for the in vivo experiments. Intranasal delivery of concentrated MSC-CM significantly reduced inflammation caused by surgical injury, lowered the release of inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α, enhanced survival of TH⁺ mDANs, and promoted dopaminergic axonal outgrowth. These findings suggest that MSC-CM not only reduces neuroinflammation and cell death but may also improve graft integration. Interestingly, although the MSC-CM-treated grafts showed a higher proportion of TH⁺/hNuc⁺ cells, their graft volume was smaller, indicating that MSC-CM may selectively promote the survival of mature mDANs while suppressing excessive proliferation of progenitor cells within the graft due to its inhibition of Ki67 expression. This effect may not be achievable by miR-143-3p alone. Meanwhile, intranasal delivery represents an effective, non-invasive strategy for targeting drugs to the brain. It primarily relies on the olfactory and trigeminal nerve pathways to bypass the blood-brain barrier, thereby enhancing drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy [63]. Therefore, intranasal administration of MSC-CM can efficiently deliver drugs to the brain and holds promise for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease.

This study has several limitations. We mainly focused on the role of miRNAs from MSC-CM in protecting mDANs, but our data suggest that protein components from MSC-CM may also contribute to cell protection. This aspect requires further investigation to fully understand the protective mechanisms of MSC-CM. Although our findings reveal that MSC-CM and its miR-143-3p component exert protective effects on mDANs by suppressing the TLR3/NFκB/p53 axis, this may not be the sole mechanism by which miRNAs act to suppress inflammatory damage. miRNA sequencing data indicate that multiple miRNAs from MSC-CM may regulate additional inflammatory pathways such as the MAPK, mTOR, PI3K-Akt, and IL-17 signaling pathways, suggesting that other miRNAs might also contribute to suppression of neuroinflammation through additional routes.

Our study has not yet evaluated long-term behavioral recovery following MSC-CM treatment in 6-OHDA-induced PD animal models, nor have we assessed synaptic integration between grafted cells and host tissue. We also note that this study was conducted exclusively in male NOD SCID mice, although female mice may respond differently to MSC-CM treatment. Therefore, future studies may be warranted adopting a dual-sex design to comprehensively evaluate the protective effects of MSC-CM in vivo. Additionally, as opposed to MSC-CM, the in vivo role of miR-143-3p itself in suppressing neuroinflammation, improving survival of transplanted mDANs, and promoting axonal outgrowth requires further investigation.

Conclusions

This study identifies MSC-CM and its component miR-143-3p as key factors that regulate mDANs cell death by inhibiting the TLR3/NFκB/p53 axis and can significantly enhance transplanted dopaminergic graft cell survival. These findings point to a strategy for improved safety and efficacy of cell-based therapy for PD. Our results provide a solid theoretical foundation and broad prospects for the development of novel neuroprotective strategies. In the future, intranasal administration may serve as a critical translational pathway bridging neuroprotective strategies with clinical accessibility. When integrated with cell-based therapies for Parkinson’s disease, this holds the potential to provide a safer, more effective, and more patient-friendly therapeutic approach.
